## 34. Ansel's Last Card

The telegram arrived at four in the afternoon, and Mr. Ansel read it twice before setting it down on the blotter with the care of a man handling something that might still be armed.

*Counter-claim lodged. Ishikawa. Originals filed.*

Three words doing the work of a eulogy.

He sat in his office above the syndicate's lowland headquarters while the ceiling fan turned its slow, useless circles and the monsoon hammered the shutters like a creditor who would not be reasoned with. Outside, the compound lamps were coming on one by one, each a small yellow surrender to the weather. Inside, Ansel did not move for a long time. He had learned decades ago that the first response to bad news was stillness — that panic was a tax paid by men who hadn't budgeted for failure, and he had always budgeted.

But this. This was not in the ledger.

He poured himself two fingers of the good whisky — the bottle he kept for celebrations and catastrophes, which in his experience were frequently the same event viewed from different altitudes — and drank it standing at the window, watching rain sheet off the tin roof into the flooded yard.

Ishikawa. The daughter.

He should have seen it. That was the honest accounting, and Ansel prided himself on honest accounting even when the entries shamed him. He had treated the girl as an instrument — a useful one, well-maintained, obedient within tolerances — because her father had been an instrument too, and instruments did not file counter-claims. Instruments did not steal their own aircraft out from under rifle fire with a clipped wingtip and fly them through a storm front that grounded everything else in the territory. Instruments did not stand in a fish-drying shed full of river families and convert thirty years of quiet cargo runs into legal standing.

He had known her father. That was the part that sat wrong in his chest, like a stone swallowed whole. He had known the man, drunk with him twice, lent him money once against a promissory note that Ansel himself had drafted the terms of. He had watched Ulyana Ishikawa grow up across a fuel counter, year after year, paying down a debt that —

He stopped there. Not because the thought frightened him but because it was irrelevant. Sentiment was a line item that never balanced. Whatever the debt had been built on, whatever the truth of it, the girl now held paper that could unmake twenty years of the syndicate's work in the valley, and sentiment would not retrieve it.

The fan ticked. The rain came down.

Ansel returned to his desk and began, methodically, to take inventory of what remained.

The claim itself: dead, or dying. A collective counter-claim lodged at the territorial registry carried weight precisely because it was collective — you could bribe one clerk or intimidate one family, but not forty landings' worth of names without turning the whole delta into a war, and wars were expensive in ways the board would not sanction. The lawyers could delay. Delay was worth something. But delay only mattered if the underlying evidence could be made to disappear, and the evidence was no longer in a cliff fissure upriver where it could be quietly buried under a rockfall and called geology.

It was filed. Originals, certified, stamped with old Ishikawa's survey marks — marks that still carried statutory authority, the registry clerk having confirmed as much before folding like wet cardboard. Copies existed now in the heads of every settlement representative who'd attended the council. You could burn paper. You could not burn forty memories.

Unless.

Ansel turned the whisky glass slowly on the blotter, watching the lamplight fracture through it.

You could not burn memories. But memory required witnesses, and witnesses were mortal, and mortality was a scheduling problem. It had always been a scheduling problem. The aquifer data proved the water existed; the falsified surveys proved the syndicate had lied about it; together they proved intent, and intent was what turned a civil dispute into something with criminal exposure that reached — he counted on his fingers, without urgency — the board, the territorial partnerships, two ministers who took their retainers in bearer shares, and Ansel himself, whose signature appeared on more of those falsified documents than anyone's, because he had been the one polite enough to sign things other men preferred to authorize verbally.

If the claim stood, it would not stop at the water rights. Claims were doors. Once opened, people walked through them looking for other rooms.

So the arithmetic resolved itself the way arithmetic does — cleanly, inevitably, with the cold satisfaction of a sum that finally comes out even. The evidence had to go. And since the evidence lived now in three heads and forty memories and one filed document, the document would have to be discredited rather than destroyed — discredited by the deaths of the three people who could authenticate it, deaths arranged so thoroughly as accident that the filing would curdle into rumor, and rumor, given time and money, curdles into nothing at all.

Three people. One pilot, one defector, one hydrologist. All currently somewhere on the lower river, moving between settlements, riding boats they did not own, protected by families who owed them nothing except everything.

Ansel opened the drawer where he kept the maps that were not for shareholders and spread the lower delta flat, weighting the corners with brass objects: an ashtray, a paperweight shaped like a bull, the telegraph key he no longer used. The settlements strung along the distributaries like beads on wet string. The registry town sat inland, a day's run by boat, half a day by air in decent weather. The monsoon's tail was still dragging across the interior — another week of it, maybe ten days, the forecasters said, though forecasters in this country were men who read chicken entrails and charged extra for confidence.

Ten days. After that, clear skies, dry season, and a territory suddenly full of eyes.

Which meant whatever was done had to be done soon, and done from the air, because the river routes were already warming to the fugitives — he'd had two reports of landings refusing syndicate couriers, one report of a fisherman who'd been asked about "the pilot woman" and had looked at the asker the way a man looks at a crocodile sunning itself near his children. The delta was closing to him. It had probably been closing for years, quietly, the way rivers close, and he had mistaken the silence of it for consent.

Ansel had spent his career being courteous. It was not weakness; it was engineering. Courtesy was cheaper than force, scaled better than force, and left fewer stains that photographed badly. He had bought pilots with patience instead of pistols. He had ruined men with paperwork so gently they sometimes thanked him at the end. He had told himself — and mostly believed — that nothing he did was violence, that violence was what happened when cleverness failed, and his cleverness had never failed.

Until a woman with her father's compass flew a damaged wing through his storm and landed standing.

He picked up the telephone, considered it, and set it back down. The switchboard was syndicate property; every call on it was logged by a switchboard operator who was, in turn, logged. Some conversations deserved the privacy of distance.

* * *

He went out into the rain without an umbrella, because umbrellas announced intention and Ansel had decided, walking down the stairs, that for the next several days he intended to look like nothing at all. The compound was quiet. The guards at the gate knew him and saluted anyway, and he nodded to them with the mild absent courtesy they'd known for eleven years, and walked past the motor pool, past the warehouse, out the service road toward the airstrip where the charter operators kept their machines.

There were three outfits flying this stretch of the coast. Two of them he owned outright through holding companies so layered that even his own accountants needed a week to trace the ownership, which was the point of layering. The third belonged to a Dutchman named Verhoeven who flew a converted amphibian — a big, ugly, dependable machine with a hull that could take rough water and a range that covered the whole delta and back with fuel to spare.

Verhoeven was in his hangar, under a work lamp, arguing with a carburetor. He was sixty, built like his own aircraft, and he owed Ansel nothing, which was why Ansel had chosen him. Men who owed you nothing could be paid honestly, and honest payment left no levers for later regret.

"Mr. Ansel." Verhoeven didn't look up. "Heard your outfit lost a claim today. Barman at the hotel says the whole delta's drinking to it."

"The barman has a generous interpretation of events." Ansel stood in the hangar mouth, rain streaming off the roof behind him in a silver curtain. "I need a charter."

"You've got charters. You've got two companies of them."

"I need a charter that isn't mine."

That got Verhoeven's attention. He straightened up, wiped his hands on a rag that had given up being clean sometime the previous decade, and looked at Ansel with the frank appraisal of a man who had survived forty years of aviation by knowing exactly when a passenger was trouble.

"What's the cargo?"

"Passengers. Three or four. Myself possibly among them."

"Where?"

"Lower delta. Several stops. Possibly some waiting involved."

Verhoeven chewed on that. Waiting meant overnights, overnights meant per diem, per diem meant the price was going up, and both men knew the negotiation was really about something else entirely.

"And the nature of the passengers' business?"

"Recovery of company property," Ansel said. "Documents. Nothing dangerous."

"Nothing dangerous." Verhoeven hung the rag on a nail. "In my experience, Mr. Ansel, when a man needs a plane that can't be traced to him, and won't say what the trip is for, the dangerous thing isn't the cargo. It's the customer." He held up a hand before Ansel could speak. "I'm not declining. I'm pricing. Triple rate, cash, half in advance, and I choose whether we divert or turn back based on my judgment of conditions — mechanical, meteorological, or moral. That last one's non-negotiable. I've flown men to do things I didn't ask about, and I've slept fine, because I never asked. But I keep a veto. You want a machine that goes where it's pointed regardless, buy one of your own."

Ansel considered him. Rain drummed on the hangar roof. Somewhere down the strip, a generator coughed and caught.

"Agreed," he said. "Fuel it for the delta range. Wheels up tomorrow, dawn if the weather allows, noon if it doesn't."

"Dawn's optimistic."

"Dawn is necessary. There's a window closing."

"There's always a window closing. That's the whole business." Verhoeven turned back to his carburetor, then paused. "One more thing. The pilot woman everyone's talking about — Ishikawa. If she's mixed up in this, I'll tell you now: she flies that river better than anyone I've ever seen, including the men I trained. If your recovery turns into a chase, don't have me chasing her. I'll lose, and I charge for losing."

"We're not chasing anyone," Ansel said, and found, to his mild surprise, that it was almost true. Chasing implied pursuit, sport, the possibility of escape. What he had in mind was closer to correction.

* * *

Back in his office, past midnight, he laid out the operation the way he laid out everything: on paper, in pencil, erasable.

The problem decomposed neatly. First, the filed originals at the registry — but attacking the registry directly was crude and unnecessary, because a counter-claim without living claimants to prosecute it would die of neglect inside a season, tied up in procedure until the procedural calendar itself became the grave. Second, the three principals. Okonkwo was injured — the reports disagreed on severity, but Reyes's last garbled transmission from the relay station had mentioned a wound, and wounds fester in delta humidity. Sable was a defector with no local roots, no family in the territory, a woman whose disappearance would generate exactly as much investigation as her employer cared to fund, which historically had been none. And Ishikawa —

Ansel sat with Ishikawa for a long moment, pencil hovering.

She was the difficult entry. She had the plane, the skills, the father's name, and now the delta's affection, which was a currency he had never learned to counterfeit. Killing her outright would make a martyr, and martyrs filed claims of their own, posthumous ones, worse ones. No. She had to be discredited first — implicated, ideally. A pilot who stole her own chartered aircraft, who'd consorted with a syndicate fixer gone rogue, who'd burned documents in front of witnesses — wait. That last part was true. She had burned the claim ledger at the delta landing. Forty people had watched her do it.

Ansel smiled for the first time all day, a thin, private movement, like ice shifting.

She had destroyed syndicate property in public. In a territory under syndicate commercial jurisdiction, destruction of corporate assets was theft, and theft was arrestable, and arrest was transportable, and transport could be arranged to pass through water deep enough to drown any number of inconvenient truths. He wouldn't even need to fabricate much. Just amplify. Reframe the burning. Let the law do what law did best, which was serve whoever fed it.

And if reframing failed — if the delta closed around her the way it seemed determined to — then there was the older method, the one that required no courts at all. Accidents happened on rivers. Boats swamped. Planes lost engines over fog. A woman flying a wingtip-damaged aircraft through monsoon tailwinds was already halfway to a plausible crash report; he had written the preliminary version in his head before Verhoeven finished naming his price.

He wrote three names on the pad. Below them, after consideration, he wrote a fourth: *Reyes.*

Marooned, according to the fragments Okonkwo's sabotage had let slip through before the relay went dark. Alive, presumably, unless the river had finished him. Reyes was a liability with a memory — he knew about the hidden radio, the orders, the confirmation-for-capture instruction. If Reyes surfaced and talked, the accident narrative acquired holes. If Reyes surfaced and was retrieved, he could be managed. Either way, the delta search had to include him, and the finding of him had to precede anyone else's finding of him.

Ansel underlined the name twice, then crossed out both underlines, because emphasis on paper was a habit of excitable men.

He looked at what he'd built: a charter flight, a legal pretext, a retrieval list, a fallback. Clean. Almost gentle. For years he had operated in the space between what was lawful and what was deniable, and the space had been wide and comfortable and he had furnished it nicely. Now the space had narrowed to the width of an aircraft fuselage, and the politeness was mostly packaging.

He noticed that he felt no different. That was the thing he examined last, turning it over like a coin of doubtful mint: no revulsion, no thrill, just the flat competence of a man doing the next task. Perhaps corruption was not a fall at all. Perhaps it was a tide, imperceptible in any single hour, and you only learned how far out you'd drifted when you tried to stand and found the bottom gone.

The fan turned. The rain eased toward the gray hour before dawn.

Ansel tore the page off the pad, folded it once, and held a match to it over the ashtray, watching the pencil lines curl and blacken — the plan surviving perfectly in his head, which was the only place it had ever really lived. When the paper was ash he rinsed it down the basin, put on his coat, and went out into the last of the wet dark toward the airstrip, where Verhoeven's amphibian sat gleaming under the field lights like a tool someone had left out overnight.

Dawn came up bruised and clearing behind the hills. The tail of the monsoon was dying over the interior, and beyond it, for the first time in weeks, the sky was going to be empty and blue and full of sightlines.

Ansel climbed aboard, settled into the cabin with his document case on his knees, and gave the order to start engines.

"Delta first," he said. "Then the river settlements. We'll begin with the landings that know her best."

Verhoeven glanced back from the cockpit. "Meaning what?"

"Meaning," Ansel said mildly, "that the fastest way to find three people is to visit everyone who loves them."

The amphibian gathered itself, wallowed once on the flooded strip, and lifted into the clearing sky, turning south toward the delta, where a woman with a borrowed compass was about to learn that the storm behind her had been the merciful part of the weather.
