## 35. Storm Intercept

The monsoon's tail was a dying animal — still dangerous, still capable of killing you, but no longer hunting with intent. Ulyana could feel the difference through the stick. The fronts that had stacked for weeks like a wall across the sky had come apart into ragged columns, gaps between them wide enough to thread a plane through if you knew where the threads were.

She knew. She'd been threading this river since she was seventeen years old and her father let her take the Cessna up alone for the first time, his hand resting light on her shoulder for exactly as long as it took to leave the ground, then gone.

The logs rode behind her seat in the waterproof case, wrapped in oilcloth, strapped to the airframe itself because she didn't trust the cargo net anymore. Sable sat beside her with the map spread across her knees, tracing their route downriver with a finger that trembled slightly from cold or exhaustion or both. Okonkwo was two days behind them on the water, fever broken but not forgotten, traveling with the delta families who had come up to meet them. He'd insisted on going by boat. *Paper should fly,* he'd said, *but men should stay near water.* She hadn't argued. There wasn't time to argue about anything anymore.

"Registry closes at four," Sable said. "If we're not there by—"

"We'll be there." Ulyana adjusted the mixture. "Say nothing else with numbers in it."

Sable almost smiled. Almost. In three weeks of enforced stillness at the relay station they had built something between them out of card games and mended nets and silences that stopped being accidental somewhere around the second week, though neither of them had said so out loud. Saying it out loud would have made it a thing that could be lost. They were both experts in things that could be lost.

They were forty minutes out of the delta, running low over the river where the fog pooled thickest, when Sable went rigid beside her.

"Ulyana."

"I see it."

She'd seen it first, actually — pilots always see it first, that's what the hours are for — a flicker of wrongness in the cloud deck above the eastern bend. A shadow moving against shadow. Then the sun found a gap and lit it: a twin-engine high-wing aircraft, syndicate markings half-scrubbed but visible, riding the cloud tops the way a shark rides a swell.

Ansel's plane. It had to be. Chapter thirty-four of her life, arriving right on schedule.

"He's between us and the registry," Sable said. Her voice had gone flat and surveyor-calm, the voice she used when the data was bad and everyone needed to hear it anyway.

"No." Ulyana banked left, dropping toward the water until the fog swallowed her wingtips. "He's between himself and the registry. There's a difference."

* * *

Here is what her father taught her about weather, one night in a leaky hangar while rain hammered the roof like an impatient creditor: *The monsoon isn't a wall, little bird. It's a river in the sky. And rivers have currents, and currents have seams, and if you can read the seam you can go anywhere the big boats can't.*

He'd been talking about more than weather. He was always talking about more than weather. That was the whole tragedy of him, she understood now — a man who hid the truth of an aquifer inside flight logs and the truth of himself inside jokes, waiting for someone patient enough to read either.

She read the seam now. The fog off the river lay in layers, and the top layer moved faster than the bottom because the wind shear did what wind shear does, and where they slid against each other there was a corridor of clear air maybe forty meters deep, hugging the water, invisible from above. She'd flown it since girlhood. Every pilot in the lowlands knew about the surface haze and avoided it, because avoiding it was the smart thing, the textbook thing.

Textbooks were written by people who had never owed anyone anything.

Above her, Ansel's pilot — and it would be a hired pilot, some competent, expensive man who flew the way Reyes shot, adequately and without love — would be doing the textbook thing: climbing to get under the deck, then instrument work through the murk, following the river valley on gauges. Which meant he would fly the valley's centerline, because that's what the gauge told him, and the centerline was exactly where the fog lied hardest. The bends fooled instruments. The cliffs threw echoes. A man flying on needles over this river was a man reading a map drawn by someone who hated him.

Her father had hated cartographers. She'd never asked why. Now she knew: because maps were how the syndicate ate things.

"Get down," she said to Sable. "Seatbelt tight. Anything loose goes under your legs."

"What are you doing?"

"Flying home."

* * *

The cat-and-mouse lasted eleven minutes, though it felt like a season.

She ran the seam. Low, lower than was sane, wheels almost kissing the white water below the fog, engine at cruise because full throttle in fog is just a faster way to die. Behind and above, she heard him before she saw him — the changed pitch of twin engines descending, the muffled thunder of a heavy airframe entering the murk. He'd spotted her when she'd crossed a gap in the deck, a mistake, her only one, and he'd committed. Committed was the word. A man who commits to a chase in cloud over terrain he doesn't know has already made every other decision badly.

She took him through the S-bend at Kettle Narrows blind, by memory, counting seconds between cliff walls the way her father had taught her — *one-Mississippi at cruise means you're wide enough, two means you're dead* — and pulled up hard on the far side into a pocket of clear air beneath a shelf of stratus, killed her forward speed to nearly nothing, and watched.

Ten seconds. Fifteen. The fog held its breath.

Then the twin-engine came through the bend exactly where the centerline said it should be, exactly where the rocks weren't quite, and its port wingtip found the updraft off the north cliff — the same treacherous lift that had tried to kill her a hundred times and that she had finally, after years, learned to use instead of fear — and the wing rose when the pilot expected it to fall, and the aircraft yawed, and for one long moment Ulyana watched a machine worth more than everything she'd ever owned stagger through the air like a drunk leaving a bar.

It didn't crash. Not there. The pilot was good; she'd give him that, wherever he ended up. He wrestled it level, climbed, broke out above the deck into open gray sky, and she saw him do the arithmetic every frightened pilot does: *I don't know this river, I don't know this fog, I'm low on fuel because Mr. Ansel wanted a fast departure, and the woman I'm chasing has vanished into a world I cannot enter.*

He turned south. Toward the flats. Toward the one stretch of open ground in fifty miles.

Toward the shingle.

Ulyana climbed after him, slowly, openly, letting him see her — because the last cruelty of a chase is that the quarry must be visible or the hunter forgets why he's running. She stayed a kilometer back and a hundred meters up and watched him set up his approach to the gravel bar with the stiff, over-controlled precision of a man whose hands had stopped obeying him.

The shingle was long but soft at the south end. Everyone who flew this river knew it. Everyone except, apparently, whoever drew the syndicate's charts.

His mains touched fine. It was the nosewheel that found the soft gravel, and then the gear folded, and the twin-engine plowed a furrow through the stones with a shriek of aluminum that carried all the way across the water to her, and stopped, and steamed, and did not burn.

She circled once. Twice. On the third pass she saw the figures climb out — two of them, the pilot and a heavyset man in a soaked suit who stood beside the wreck with his hands on his hips, looking at the river as if it had betrayed him personally.

Mr. Ansel. Alive. Of course alive. Men like Ansel survived floods, fires, bankruptcies, and justice; it was practically their profession.

She could leave him. The thought arrived fully formed, calm as a checklist item. Fly on to the registry, file the claim, let the river keep him. Let the fog and the cold and the long walk do what she would never dirty her hands doing. No one would ever know she'd seen him. The storm would erase the tracks by morning.

Sable was watching her. She could feel it without looking — that steady, unblinking attention that had undone her more thoroughly than any interrogation.

"You're going down," Sable said. Not a question.

"He knows where Okonkwo is. He knows the delta families helped us. If I leave him here he walks south and reports everything, and the gunboat comes back with lawyers instead of soldiers next time." Ulyana heard herself say it and recognized it for what it was: true, and also not the reason. "And he owes my father an answer I can't dig out of any cache."

"That too," Sable said softly.

"That too."

She brought the Cessna down on the north end of the shingle, into the wind, and shut the engine down into a silence filled only with rain on fabric and the tick of cooling metal from the wreck.

* * *

Ansel stood in the rain looking smaller than she had ever seen him. The courtesy was gone — stripped off him somewhere between the wingtip and the gravel, along with the combed hair and the dry shoes and the whole architecture of a man who controlled outcomes. What remained was wet, middle-aged, and breathing hard.

"Mrs. Ishikawa." He said it automatically, the leash-voice, and then seemed to hear it and stop.

"Mr. Ansel." She stayed by her own wing strut. Sable stood behind her, arms crossed, saying nothing, being a fact rather than a person. "Your pilot hurt?"

"Bruised. The aircraft is—" He looked at the furrow, the folded gear, the engine cowling peeled back like a tin lid. "The aircraft is finished."

"Yes."

They regarded each other across ten meters of rain. Somewhere in the wreck, his pilot groaned and began swearing with impressive fluency, which was reassuring; injured men who swear are usually fine.

"You could have kept flying," Ansel said.

"Yes."

"Why didn't you?"

It was the question underneath every other question, and he asked it with something that wasn't menace for the first time in her experience of him. Curiosity, maybe. Or the beginning of an understanding that had caught up with him the way the fog had caught up with his pilot.

Ulyana thought about the ledger in the case behind her seat. Thought about her father's name stamped on surveys he'd never signed honestly, about a debt invented in a lowland office to put a collar on a witness, about a man who died running evidence up a river because he trusted the river more than the law. She thought about mercy, which she had always suspected was just pride wearing softer clothes, and decided that this once, it wasn't.

"Because I want to know what you'll say," she said, "when there's no one left to lie to. And because my father believed people were worth the landing. I'm going to find out if he was right about you."

Ansel looked at her for a long moment. Rain ran off his chin. Behind him the wreck ticked and settled, and his pilot climbed out onto the gravel and sat down heavily, cradling his wrist, and the river went on doing what it had always done, carrying everything away or carrying everything home, depending entirely on what you put into it.

"Your father," Ansel said, and stopped, and started again, and this time his voice had no polish left on it at all. "Your father was not what I told you he was."

Ulyana felt the words land somewhere below her ribs, in the place where grief keeps its accounts.

"I know," she said. "But I want to hear you say it. All of it. Here, in the rain, where the river can hold you to it."

Ansel looked at the water. At the wreck. At the small, soaked woman standing in front of him with a compass in her pocket that had never been hers and a debt that had never been real, and whatever he saw in her face made him exhale — long, defeated, almost relieved — like a man setting down cargo he'd been carrying so long he'd forgotten it had weight.

"All right," he said. "All right. But you should sit down for this, Mrs. Ishikawa."

"I've been sitting down for it my whole life," Ulyana said. "Stand up and talk."
