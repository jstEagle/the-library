## 36. What Ansel Confesses

The fog gave him up grudgingly.

Ulyana brought the plane down along the shingle where the river braided into three silver threads, wheels kissing gravel with a shudder she felt in her teeth. Ahead, half-sunk in the shallows like a dead bird, Ansel's aircraft lay on its back in six inches of water, one pontoon torn away, the fuselage draped in river weed. A man sat on the bank with his back against a driftwood log, soaked to the skin, hands folded in his lap as though waiting for a meeting to begin.

She cut the engine and sat for a moment, listening to the tick of cooling metal and the endless conversation of the water.

Sable leaned forward from the back seat. "I can come."

"No." Ulyana unbuckled. "This one's mine."

The gravel crunched under her boots. She stopped ten paces from him, close enough to see that his glasses were gone — lost somewhere between cloud and river — and without them his face looked unfinished, a sketch of the man who had run her life from a lowland office for eleven years.

"Mr. Ansel," she said.

"Miss Ishikawa." His voice was hoarse. He'd swallowed river, by the look of him; he coughed once, politely, into his fist. "You fly very well in weather that should ground angels."

"I grew up in it."

"Yes." He looked out at the water. "So did he."

Something moved through her chest, hot and fast, and she had to stand very still until it passed. "Don't," she said. "Don't do that. Don't make us sound like the same story."

"We are the same story," Ansel said. "That's rather what I came to tell you."

* * *

She made him move up the bank, out of the spray line, because whatever he was going to say she wanted to hear it standing in dry-ish air with the sun trying its luck through the fog. He walked slowly. She noticed, with a strange detached surprise, how old he was. In her head he had always been a voice on a radio, a signature at the bottom of a ledger, a courtesy that felt like a leash. Bodies aged. Voices didn't.

He sat down on the log again. She stayed standing.

"The debt," she said. "Start there."

Ansel folded his hands. Even now, even wrecked and dripping, he arranged himself like a man about to present figures. "Your father borrowed four thousand marks from the Provisional Mining and Riverine Company in the spring of—" he paused "—you would have been nine."

"He told me he borrowed it for engine parts."

"He told everyone that. It was a good story. I wrote it." Ansel's mouth did something that was almost a smile and failed halfway. "There was no loan, Miss Ishikawa. There was a ledger entry. We created it the week after your father flew a survey charter for Meridian's predecessor company and came back with photographs he should not have had and questions he would not stop asking."

The river filled the silence for a while.

"You fabricated it," Ulyana said. Her voice came out flat, which surprised her. She had expected rage, and rage was there, but underneath it was something colder and older, a stone she'd been carrying so long she'd mistaken it for part of her skeleton. "Eleven years. Every cargo run. Every season I flew medicine into places your maps forgot, every payment I scraped out of a margin thinner than my fuel reserves — that was all—"

"Bookkeeping," Ansel said. "Yes."

"One payment a year. Never enough to finish. The interest recalculated every spring." She could recite it. She could recite it the way other people recited prayers. "You kept it alive like a pet."

"It was never meant to be paid," Ansel said simply. "It was meant to be owed. Your father understood leverage better than most men twice his education. Once we held paper on him, he became predictable. Predictable men are safe men. And your father was not a safe man, Miss Ishikawa. He was the single most dangerous man on this river, and he didn't know it, which made him worse."

"Why?" The word cracked out of her harder than she intended. "Why him? Why go to all that theater for one bush pilot?"

Ansel looked up at her then, and for the first time since she'd known him — through contracts and courtesies and the gentle closing of traps — he looked tired. Truly tired, the way Sable had looked when they pulled her off the driftwood camp, hollowed down to the load-bearing walls.

"Because of what he carried," Ansel said. "And because of what he refused to sell."

* * *

He talked for a long time, and she let him, because every word was a door opening onto a room she'd lived beside her whole life without knowing it was there.

Her father had flown survey runs upriver before the dam failed — before the drowned villages, before the maps went blank. He'd seen the valley. He'd seen the glacier, and more importantly he'd seen what the glacier fed: springs, seeps, an aquifer running under the whole watershed like a hidden vein, the only fresh water in a region that was learning to be thirsty. The syndicate had known it too, vaguely, the way you know there's money under a floorboard. What they lacked was proof. Extent, flow, boundaries — the numbers that turn geography into property.

"Your father took bearings," Ansel said. "Old-fashioned man. Paper and pencil and that brass compass of his, and a memory like a hold full of lead. And then the dam failed, and the valley sealed itself behind ice and rumor, and everyone assumed the question was closed." He shook his head. "We never assumed that. We just couldn't get up there. So we waited. And we held our paper on the one man who'd been inside."

"So the debt was a leash."

"The debt was a leash, yes. But leashes only work on animals that want something. Your father wanted nothing. That's what nobody at the company ever understood. We offered him forgiveness — wipe the slate, a salary, a house in the lowlands, his daughter educated anywhere she liked." Ansel's voice dropped. "He said no. Do you understand? He sat in my office with four thousand marks of fiction around his neck and he said no, thank you, I'll keep flying cargo. Because the moment he signed, we'd own his silence too, and his silence was worth more than the debt."

Ulyana thought of the flight logs. Of the compass that wasn't hers, that had been pressed into her hands at a funeral with a handshake from Mr. Ansel and a murmured *our condolences, and regarding the outstanding balance*. She had spent eleven years believing her father died ashamed — a debtor, a runner, a man who flew into bad weather because he couldn't face good bookkeeping.

"How did he die?" she asked. "Tell me the truth. All of it."

Ansel was quiet for a moment. Out on the water, a heron stood in the shallows with the patience of something that had never once been in a hurry.

"There was a second expedition," he said. "Before Meridian. Smaller. Company-funded, unofficially. They went up during a calving window eleven years ago — the same window, incidentally, that opens next week. Your father flew their resupply. Against our instructions. We had ordered him grounded until he cooperated." He paused. "He filed a false manifest instead. Flew them in anyway. On the return leg he carried their data out — the aquifer survey, the first complete one. Flow rates. Boundaries. Everything we've spent a decade and a fortune trying to reacquire."

"And?"

"And the weather closed behind him, as it does. He put down at the old delta landing to wait it out." Ansel's hands, folded in his lap, tightened slightly. "There was a man waiting there for him. Not sent by me. Sent by a director who decided that data in a dead man's hold was cheaper than data in a living man's hands. The plane burned. The pilot was reported lost to weather. The report was accepted because the weather was, in fact, terrible, and because grief makes poor auditing."

Ulyana heard herself breathing. The river kept talking.

"You knew," she said. "You've known for eleven years that he was murdered, and you let me believe—"

"I let you believe nothing," Ansel said. "I let you *owe*. It was crueler and it was safer and I have never pretended to be a kind man, Miss Ishikawa, only a courteous one. But I will tell you this, and you may weigh it however you like: I argued against the director's solution. I argued that a living witness who hated us was still preferable to a martyr. I lost the argument. And then I spent eleven years managing the consequences, which included you, and which I handled—" he exhaled "—badly. Courtesy was the only tool I trusted, and courtesy is just cruelty wearing gloves."

"The logs survived," Ulyana said. "The cache. You know that now. That's why you chartered a plane into a monsoon with your own hands on the stick."

"That's why I chartered a plane." Ansel nodded toward the wreck in the shallows. "Reyes's garbled transmission reached us in fragments. Enough to know the cache existed, not enough to find it. I decided if I wanted it done quietly, I would do it myself." He gestured at his ruined clothes, the drowned aircraft, the fog. "You can see how quietly it's gone."

"Why tell me any of this?" She crouched down finally, bringing her eyes level with his, and saw up close what the loss of his glasses had done: left him looking directly at things instead of through them. "You're finished. The counter-claim is lodged. The gunboat turned back at the narrows. You could sit on this shingle until the frost takes you and say nothing, and it would cost you nothing you haven't already lost. Why confess?"

Ansel considered the question with the seriousness he'd once given contract clauses.

"Because you asked," he said. "Nobody has asked me anything in twenty years except *how much* and *when*. And because—" he stopped, restarted. "Your father, in my office, the last time I saw him alive, told me something I have thought about every day since. I offered to forgive the debt. He said: *A debt you can forgive isn't real, Mr. Ansel. Which means the only thing you've ever owned is the lie.*" He looked at her. "He was right. The lie is all I've ever owned. It seems fitting to leave it with his daughter."

* * *

The rage, when it finally came, arrived strangely — not as fire but as pressure, a flood against a door she'd braced her whole life. She stood up and walked to the waterline because she could not stand to be that close to him while her hands wanted things.

Eleven years. Eleven years of dawn runs and thin margins, of counting rivets on her airframe the way other women counted coins, of signing Ansel's courteous little receipts with a hand that shook only after the ink dried. Eleven years of believing her father had been weak — that shame ran in families like eye color, that she flew cargo into forgotten country partly to pay a debt and partly to prove she wasn't the kind of person who incurred one.

And none of it had been true. He hadn't fled. He'd *run* — run evidence, run truth, run the only complete map of the water that would keep ten thousand people alive, straight into a fire set by men who valued a claim over a life. He had died the way she'd nearly died a dozen times in the last month: carrying something heavy through weather that wanted it.

"Ulyana." Ansel's voice, careful. "What happens now is yours to decide. I'm an old man on a gravel bar with no radio and no friends within two hundred miles. Whatever you do with me will be just, or won't, and either way the river doesn't care."

She turned around. The anger was still there — it would always be there, she suspected, the way old fractures ache before rain — but sitting on top of it now, unexpected and almost unbearable, was something else. Release. The specific lightness of setting down weight you'd forgotten you were carrying, followed immediately by the vertigo of learning how much of your balance depended on it.

"My father flew this river," she said. "Not for you. Not against you. For the people on it. That's the whole story. That's the only version I'm keeping."

"A better version than mine," Ansel said.

"It's not a version. It's what happened." She looked at the wrecked plane, then back at him. "Here's what happens now. We fly out tomorrow when the fog lifts. You come with us — tied if necessary, but I don't think it'll be necessary, because you're a man who honors arrangements and I'm about to make you one. You'll testify. To the registry, to the settlements' council, to whoever asks. Everything you told me today, on the record, under your own name. The fabricated debt. The director who ordered the fire. All of it."

Ansel was silent for a long moment. Then, incredibly, he laughed — a short, rusty sound, like an engine catching on the third try.

"Blackmail," he said. "From an Ishikawa. Your father would be proud."

"He'd be furious I waited this long to learn it." She held out her hand. "Do we have an arrangement, Mr. Ansel?"

He looked at her hand. He looked, for a moment, like a man being offered exactly what he deserved, and understanding it completely. Then he took off one wet glove, gripped her hand with cold fingers, and said, "We have an arrangement."

Behind them, out on the shingle, the fog was beginning to tear. Through the gaps, far upriver, the light was changing — a high white glare on the peaks that hadn't been there yesterday, the color of ice letting go.

Ulyana felt it before she understood it, the way she always felt weather first in her hands.

Calving season had opened.
