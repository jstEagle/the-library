## 38. Calving Ends

The gorge closed on a Tuesday, though Sable only knew it was Tuesday because Ulyana kept count on the cabin wall in pencil, one stroke per day, and had reached a number that meant nothing to either of them and everything.

They heard it before they saw it. A sound like the sky clearing its throat — deep, tectonic, almost bored — and then the ice walls at the gorge mouth leaned toward each other and shrugged. Sable stood on the shingle with her hands in her pockets and watched a berg the size of the fish-drying shed rotate slowly into the channel and jam there, and behind it another, and behind those a white silence assembling itself like a door being shut by someone who intended to keep it shut until spring.

"That's it," Okonkwo said beside her. He was thinner than he'd been at the narrows, his arm still bound against his chest, but he was walking again, and he said things like *that's it* with the calm of a man who had already died once and found it negotiable. "Ten days early. The melt came fast this year."

"Or we came slow," Sable said.

"Both can be true." He turned back toward the settlement, favoring his good side. "I'm going to tell them. They'll want to plan the winter run before the river freezes hard."

She stayed on the shingle after he left. That was a habit of hers she'd never managed to break — staying places after everyone else had decided they were finished with them. She'd done it at Meridian's basecamp, standing in the ash while Reyes's voice crackled somewhere upriver about accidents. She'd done it in the cave during the monsoon, listening to rain and counting the ways she could have said what she meant instead of what was safe. She did it now, watching the last open water in the gorge go grey and then white, the river putting itself away like a tool at the end of a season.

Behind her, boots on gravel. She didn't turn around. There were only three of them left within a hundred miles, and two of them she could identify by footfall now, which was the kind of knowledge you acquired without wanting it and couldn't give back.

"The route's shut," Ulyana said. She stopped an arm's length away, close enough that Sable could smell woodsmoke and engine oil, the two smells that had become, over these weeks, indistinguishable from safety. "Okonkwo told me. Ten days early."

"I heard."

"So nobody goes south until the thaw." Ulyana said it flatly, the way she reported weather, which was how Sable knew it cost her something. "The gunboat's on the other side of that ice. Ansel's lawyers are on the other side of that ice. Everything that wants to hurt us is on the other side of that ice." A pause. "And so is everything else."

Sable turned then. Ulyana was looking at the gorge, not at her, jaw set in the particular way it set when she was holding a sentence behind her teeth and daring herself to let it out. Sable had catalogued that look weeks ago. She had a whole catalogue by now: the way Ulyana's hands went quiet when she was frightened, the way she talked to the plane like a horse, the way she checked the compass twice even when the needle hadn't moved. She had spent most of her adult life reading terrain — slope, load, fracture lines — and she read Ulyana Ishikawa the same way, and the reading had been stable for months, and neither of them had done anything about it, and both of them knew why.

"You could walk out," Sable said. "South fork, past the drowned village. It's ugly but it goes. I've done it."

"In winter?"

"People have done worse for less."

Ulyana finally looked at her. "Is that what you want? For me to go?"

"No," Sable said. It came out faster than she'd planned, unarmored, and she heard it land between them like something dropped. She made herself not take it back. "No. I don't."

* * *

The thing about lying badly, Sable thought later, was that you got a lot of practice noticing other people doing it.

She had lied to Meridian for six weeks before she walked — small lies, survey numbers nudged, a compass bearing transposed, the slow sabotage of a woman buying time she didn't know how to spend. She had lied perfectly about the things that mattered and terribly about the things that didn't, and Voss — the expedition lead, the one who'd gone deeper into the valley and never come back down — had known within days and let her keep lying because a liar who stayed was more useful than a defector who ran. She understood the architecture of dishonesty the way she understood ice: where it bore weight, where it looked solid and wasn't.

So she recognized what she and Ulyana had been building all autumn. It was a lie built out of true things. *I'll check the nets.* *I'll help with the stove.* *I'm going that way anyway.* Every errand doubled as an excuse; every excuse doubled as proximity. They circled each other the way she'd once circled the cache site, taking bearings, never closing. And the whole settlement could see it — she'd caught old Marta grinning into her tea about it twice — which meant the only people still pretending were the two of them, and the pretense had gotten so thin that honesty would have been less exposing.

That evening she found Ulyana in the shed where the plane lived, under a work lamp, with the wing panel off and her arms buried in the airframe to the elbow. The repaired wingtip — the one clipped by rifle fire at the syndicate strip — was skinned in new aluminum that didn't quite match, bright as a scar.

"If you say 'hand me the seven-sixteenth' I'm going to scream," Sable said, leaning in the doorway.

"Then scream quietly, I need the seven-sixteenth."

Sable found it in the tray and handed it over and did not scream, and Ulyana worked in silence for a while, and the lamp buzzed, and outside the wind tested the shed roof the way it had every night since the ice closed, patient, thorough, asking the same question over and over.

"My father kept this plane flying through two monsoons and a war scare," Ulyana said eventually, not looking up. "He used to say a plane doesn't care who you are. It only cares whether you pay attention." She backed out of the airframe, wrench in hand, grease to the shoulder. "I think I liked it better when I believed that. Machines don't care. People are supposed to, and that's the part I never trusted."

"And now?"

"Now I'm paying attention." She set the wrench down. She wiped her hands on a rag with the deliberation of someone stalling, and then stopped stalling, which was its own kind of courage, Sable thought — the courage of a woman who flew into gorges and stood off gunboats but had to gather herself to finish a sentence. "You asked me once, in the cave, why I never left this river country. I gave you the debt. That was true, but it was the small truth. The big one is that I never believed anywhere would have me. Invisible was safe. You can't be left by people if they never learn your name."

Sable said nothing. This was the other discipline she'd learned from ice: knowing when bearing weight means standing still.

"And then I burned a ledger in front of forty witnesses," Ulyana went on, "and filed my father's name in a territorial registry, and landed here, and your people fed me, and Marta calls me *the pilot* like it's mine, like I earned it or inherited it or whatever it is, and I keep waiting for it to feel like too much." Her voice did something small and rough at the end. "It hasn't yet. That terrifies me more than the gorge did."

"It won't feel like too much," Sable said. "That's the trick of it. Everyone tells you belonging will be heavy. It isn't. It's just present. Like weather."

Ulyana laughed — short, surprised at herself. "You're terrible at comforting people."

"I'm excellent at accurate ones."

The lamp buzzed. Somewhere across the settlement a dog barked twice and gave up, deciding the night wasn't worth the effort. Sable crossed the shed — four steps, she counted them, because counting was what she did instead of breathing — and took the rag out of Ulyana's hands and set it on the bench, and then stood there close enough that the decision was no longer anyone's alone.

"We've been circling since the cave," Sable said. "Since before the cave. Since the driftwood camp, probably, when you looked at me like I might be a trap and I wanted you to be right, because a trap would at least be honest about wanting something."

"Sable—"

"I'm done pretending the circling is accidental. Are you?"

Ulyana looked at her for a long moment, and Sable watched the calculation happen — the pilot weighing load, weather, fuel, the old arithmetic of a woman who'd spent years making sure she could always leave — and then watched the arithmetic stop, which was the most beautiful thing she'd seen all year, more beautiful than the calving, more beautiful than the valley opening.

"No," Ulyana said. "I'm not."

It was not dramatic. That was the thing Sable would remember afterward, telling it to herself in the dark like a surveyor logging coordinates: it was not dramatic. It was Ulyana's cold hands and Sable warming them, and the lamp buzzing, and the unfinished wing waiting, and neither of them caring, and the kiss landing soft and certain as a plane touching down on a strip you know by heart. Unglamorous. True. The kind of love that doesn't announce itself because it plans to stay.

When they broke apart, Ulyana rested her forehead against Sable's and exhaled, long, like a held breath from a decade finally spent.

"Marta's going to be insufferable," she said.

"Marta's going to say she called it in June."

"She did call it in June."

"Yes," Sable said, "she did."

* * *

Winter arrived properly a week later, and with it the shape of their days.

Sable learned the settlements' rhythms the way she'd once learned glacier schedules: dawn for water hauling, midmorning for the drying racks, afternoon for whatever mending the cold had broken overnight. She taught the children snow-reading — how a slope lies about itself, where the cornices build, which drifts will hold a man and which will swallow him — and discovered that teaching was just surveying with your mouth, and that she was, to her own surprise, good at it. Ulyana flew when the weather allowed, medicine and mail and a stove pipe for the upper landing, short hops now that the long routes were iced, and came home each time with frost in her eyebrows and stories she told with her whole body, expansive in a way Sable suspected she'd never been while the debt still sat on her.

They shared the cabin by the third week, without ceremony. One bed, built narrow for a smaller household, which meant they learned each other quickly — the geography of elbows, the temperature preferences irreconcilable and therefore compromised nightly, Ulyana talking in her sleep about fuel mixtures, Sable waking before dawn out of old expedition habit and lying still, listening to her breathe, doing the accounting she'd avoided all her life and finding, for once, that the books balanced.

Some nights they argued. About the logs — Ulyana still insisted copies should go south with the first thaw, to the registry, to universities, to anyone who'd hold the syndicate to account, and Sable still flinched from paper that dangerous, still dreamed sometimes of the cache fissure and the weight of it. But the arguments had changed texture. They were no longer two liars trading honesty across a gap. They were two people on the same side of a table, disagreeing about the future because they both assumed they'd be in it together. Sable caught herself losing arguments on purpose just to see Ulyana triumphant, and decided not to examine that too closely.

In February the ice on the gorge groaned one night, a long settling sound, and Ulyana sat bolt upright and Sable said, "It's just the cold tightening. It does that," and Ulyana lay back down and put her hand flat on Sable's sternum, feeling the heartbeat there like checking a pulse, and said, "I know. I just forget, some nights, that nothing is coming for us."

"Nothing is coming for us," Sable said, and was startled to find she believed it.

* * *

On the last morning of the chapter of their lives that had a gunboat in it, Sable walked out to the point above the frozen gorge with the brass compass in her pocket.

She'd taken it from the cabin dashboard without asking, which was a liberty, but liberties were permitted now; that was the whole discovery of the winter. She stood in the blue shadow of morning and opened the lid and watched the needle swing and settle, pointing — as it always pointed — toward the interior, toward the valley, toward the glacier that had locked and unlocked and locked again around everything they'd found there.

Her father's instrument, Ulyana had told her. Never hers, until it was. That was the pattern of the whole country, Sable thought: things passing hand to hand, rivers running through land no map bothered to name, evidence cached in cliff fissures waiting decades for someone with the bearings to read them. Nothing here belonged to anyone. Things belonged to whoever stayed.

Behind her, crunching footsteps. Ulyana came up the point carrying two cups of coffee, steam whipping off them sideways, and handed one over without comment, and stood shoulder to shoulder with her looking at the white line where the gorge had been.

"Spring comes early this far north?" Sable asked.

"Spring comes when it comes. We fly the winter run till then." Ulyana blew on her coffee. "Okonkwo wants the first thaw run mapped before the syndicate's lawyers can charter anything. He says next year decides everything."

"Next year decides everything," Sable agreed. "Every year does. That's the deal."

Ulyana glanced at her, that quick sidelong look that Sable had long since stopped pretending not to collect. "You're staying, then. Through next year. Through all of it."

Sable closed the compass. The needle inside went on pointing regardless — that was the nature of needles — but the lid was hers to open or shut, and she shut it, and put it back in her pocket, and turned to face the woman who had flown her out of a burning valley and into a life with no exit strategy whatsoever.

"I walked away from Meridian because I wouldn't help them take this place," she said. "I'm staying because I finally found something worth being taken by."

"That's the worst romantic thing anyone has ever said to me."

"I'm a surveyor. We work in what's available."

Ulyana laughed, and the sound went out over the ice, and somewhere under the frozen gorge the river waited, the way rivers do, patient and certain, keeping everything entrusted to it until the season turns and it can carry it home.

Downriver, at the delta landing, a courier boat was tying up with the first mail of the thaw — and among the letters, one with a lowland postmark, addressed in a hand Sable would recognize anywhere, because she'd spent six weeks forging documents beside it.

Voss was alive.
