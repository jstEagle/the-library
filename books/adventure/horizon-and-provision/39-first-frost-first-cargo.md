## 39. First Frost, First Cargo

The wing came back better than it left.

That was the thing nobody told you about river country: the menders here didn't repair, they improved. The delta families had taken the clipped wingtip off her plane in October and given it back in November with new spar caps of seasoned ash, fabric stretched drum-tight, and a coat of paint the color of the sky an hour before dawn — that deep blue-gray that wasn't quite any single color, mixed by a woman named Petra who ran the fish-drying shed and had once, apparently, painted boats before she painted anything else.

"Your father's old bird was green," Petra said, wiping her hands on a rag that had been white sometime in the last decade. "I remember it coming over the trees. Green like a beetle."

"It was," Ulyana said. Her throat did something complicated around the words, so she kept them short.

"I thought about green." Petra looked up at the wing, at the blue-gray catching the first hard light of the season. "But you're not flying his routes anymore. You're flying yours."

Ulyana had stood under the wing for a long time after that, longer than any mechanical inspection required, one palm flat against the cold fabric. The plane had a name now, stenciled small near the cockpit where only someone standing this close would see it. *Provision.* Sable had suggested it. Ulyana had pretended to think about it for three days before saying yes.

The frost had come in the night — first frost, real frost, the kind that turned the grass silver and made the strip crunch underfoot and put your breath out ahead of you like a scout reporting back. Winter run weather. Cold enough for the engine to be honest with you. Cold enough that the medicine crates needed their wool blankets, and the mail sacks rode wrapped like infants.

She walked the preflight slow, because there was no debt clock ticking behind her anymore, and slow turned out to be its own kind of pleasure. Fuel check. Oil. Control surfaces moving free, no ice in the hinges. She'd done ten thousand of these inspections half-asleep, resentful, running the checklist like a man paying off a toll. This morning she did it awake, and every item on the list felt less like a chore and more like a conversation with a machine she was finally on good terms with.

The manifest sat on a clipboard in the cockpit, and she read it twice just for the pleasure of it:

*Medicine: penicillin (12), sulfa (8), fever reducers, suture kits, insulin — COLD CHAIN, DO NOT DELAY.*
*Mail: 41 kilos, sorted by landing.*
*Freight: sewing machine parts (Kettle Landing), school primers (all landings), one violin (Ferry Bend, FRAGILE, per Marta).*
*Passenger: none.*

No syndicate crates. No sealed boxes she wasn't allowed to inspect. No fixer reading her instruments over her shoulder. No ledger number in the corner where a debt total used to live.

She had burned that page months ago, along with all the others, into the current off the delta landing. But some mornings she still caught herself reaching for the clipboard expecting to feel the weight of it — the way an amputee reaches for a hand. And some mornings, like this one, she reached and found nothing there, and the nothing felt enormous.

"You're talking to it again."

Sable came down the strip with two tin cups steaming, frost in her dark hair where her hood had slipped, walking with the easy rolling gait she'd learned from a year of riverbanks. She handed over a cup without asking what Ulyana wanted, because after eight months she knew: black tea, too strong, sweetened past reason. Surveyor's habits died hard; Ulyana's pilot habits were the same disease.

"I'm conducting a negotiation," Ulyana said. "It's asking for a new propeller."

"It got a new wing. It can want."

They drank tea shoulder to shoulder under the wing while the sun cleared the tree line and set the frost smoking off the field. Sable had the route map folded into her pocket even though she could have recited it — eleven landings between here and the head of navigation, water strips frozen now, so most of them were the scraped-grass fields the settlements kept clear for exactly this season. She navigated by memory and by landmarks nobody else would have thought to use: a leaning dead pine, a bend where the current had undercut the bank into a shape like a bitten ear, the ruins of the old dam visible for six seconds on a clear day if you knew where to look.

Her father had known where to look. That was the thing Ulyana kept learning, layer under layer, like peeling bark off wet wood. Every landmark Sable used, it seemed, her father had used first. He hadn't just run this river. He'd memorized it the way you memorize a face you love.

"Fuel's full, oil's good, cargo's lashed," Ulyana said finally. "Weather window holds till midafternoon, then another front comes down the valley."

"So we go now and stop admiring the paint."

"One more minute of admiring the paint."

Sable laughed — the real laugh, not the careful one she'd arrived with at the driftwood camp a lifetime ago, when she'd been lying badly about where Meridian had gone. That laugh had taken months to come back, like feeling returning to a frostbitten hand. Ulyana had decided somewhere over the summer that hearing it was worth more than most things she'd ever owned, which was a short list, but still.

They climbed in. Sable took the right seat with the map open on her knees out of pure ceremony — she didn't need it, and they both knew it, and they both liked it anyway. Ulyana ran the start sequence, and the engine caught on the second blade sweep, that clean winter-cold roar, and the whole airframe shivered once and settled like a dog shaking off water.

She looked down the strip. Frost smoke. Blue-gray wings. A manifest with a violin on it.

"Well," she said. "Provision. Let's go be useful."

*

The first landing was Kettle Landing, forty minutes north, a shelf of cleared ground above the river with four buildings and a wind sock that was really a shirt on a pole. Old Tomas met them at a jog, breath pluming, and took the sewing machine parts like they were ammunition.

"The treadle arm," he said. "You're sure it's the right one?"

"Marta sent the measurements herself," Sable said. "Twice. She made me read them back."

Tomas grunted, satisfied, and then stood there in the cold clearly wanting something else, and Ulyana waited him out, because waiting people out was a skill the river taught you whether you asked to learn it or not.

"There's talk," he said finally, lower. "Down from the headwaters. Trappers say the gorge iced shut early this year. Solid, they say. First time in a decade it closed before midwinter."

Something moved through Ulyana that wasn't quite fear and wasn't quite relief. The gorge shut meant the upper country sealed itself away — the glacier valley, the aquifer, everything they'd bled for last calving season — locked behind ice until spring. Locked away from Ansel, whose gunboat lay rusting somewhere below the rockslide they'd dropped on the narrows. Locked away from lawyers and survey crews and men who measured rivers in cubic meters of profit.

Locked away, too, from whatever the counter-claim process would need next year, when the ice opened and the fight started again in earnest. Nothing was ever finished in river country. It just froze and thawed and froze.

"Good," Sable said quietly, when Tomas had gone. "Let it sleep."

"For a year."

"For a year." She bumped Ulyana's shoulder with hers. "We'll be ready. Okonkwo's already redrawing the survey maps with the settlement boundaries marked. He says the collective claim is stronger if the maps show who actually lives on the water."

Okonkwo. Still thin from the fever, still favoring the leg the rockslide had nearly taken, but lucid and stubborn and teaching children hydrology in Ferry Bend because somebody had to and he'd discovered he liked it. He'd sent a note north with the last mail run: *Tell my pilot the wing looks better than the original. Do not tell her I said sentimental things.* Ulyana had read it four times.

They lifted out of Kettle Landing into air so clear it felt rude, the river unspooling below in long silver bends, and Ulyana let the plane climb a little higher than the run strictly required, just to look. This was the part she'd forgotten she loved — or maybe never been allowed to love, back when every hour aloft was an hour of debt accruing. Flying as work was survival. Flying as looking was something else. From up here the settlements weren't dots on a manifest; they were woodsmoke rising straight in the cold, gardens gone brown and sleeping, boats hauled up and flipped for winter like beetles. People. Her people now, though she still tested the word sometimes, the way you test ice with your weight before committing.

"Ferry Bend next," Sable said. "And mind the crosswind off the bluff — Marta will lecture you if you bounce it."

"Marta lectures me if I don't bounce it. It gives her something to do."

"She's knitting you mittens, you know."

"I know." Ulyana banked south, gentle, the repaired wing answering true. "She asked my glove size in September like it was a casual question. Nobody asks a casual question in this country. Everything's a plan."

"That's why your father fit here," Sable said, and then went quiet, the way she did when she'd said something that landed deeper than she'd aimed.

Ulyana flew for a while. Below, the river carried plates of frazil ice downstream, the first suggestion of the freeze to come, glittering like spilled coins. Somewhere under that water was the ash of a claim ledger, pages and pages of fabricated debt dissolving into the same current that had carried her father's boat, his evidence, his whole secret life. She missed him differently now. Less like a wound, more like a room in a house she was slowly furnishing.

"He'd have hated the color," she said at last. "The wing. He was a green man."

"He'd have lied about hating it," Sable said. "And then flown it every chance he got."

Ulyana laughed, and the laugh filled the little cabin, and the plane seemed to fly lighter for it, which was nonsense, but she believed it anyway.

*

Ferry Bend went smooth — the crosswind off the bluff was real but manageable, and Marta received her violin with both hands and an expression Ulyana suspected she showed to no one else, and lectured her about the bounce anyway, exactly as predicted. The mittens appeared from a apron pocket, gray wool, sized correctly, no discussion permitted. At the schoolhouse, Okonkwo limped out to help unload the primers and managed, with tremendous dignity, to hug her with one arm while pretending it was a logistical maneuver.

By the sixth landing the light was going gold and long, and the front Sable had called was stacking up on the southern horizon exactly where she'd put it — a bruise-colored wall with a soft gray skirt, patient as arithmetic. They had two landings left and a margin that was comfortable but not generous.

"We skip Miller's Flats and double back tomorrow?" Sable asked, pencil hovering over the map.

"No. We make Miller's." Ulyana checked the fuel gauge, then the sky, then the gauge again out of habit. "Miller's has the insulin, and Mrs. Adeyemi counts doses like a miser counts coins. She'll have planned to the day. If we're late, someone goes without, and I'm not doing that math."

Sable looked at her for a moment — that look, the one that still caught Ulyana off guard, the one that said she was seeing something she found remarkable and hadn't yet gotten used to finding it remarkable.

"What?"

"Nothing. Turn us south, pilot."

Miller's Flats was the farthest landing, a gravel bar field at the mouth of a side creek, and they came in low over the water with the front's leading edge already breathing cold down their collars, and the landing was good, and Mrs. Adeyemi was waiting on the bank with a lantern lit though it wasn't dark yet, because she'd been watching the sky since noon. She counted the insulin vials twice in the lantern light, nodded once — the highest honor she awarded — and pressed a jar of smoked fish into Ulyana's hands with the same authority.

"You'll stay? Storm's close."

"We'll outrun it to the delta."

"You'll try." But Mrs. Adeyemi was smiling, because everyone in these settlements had learned that Ulyana Ishikawa trying to outrun weather was one of the reliable phenomena of the region, like the frost and the salmon runs.

They took off into the teeth of the building wind with the light failing fast, and it should have been grim — the kind of flight she'd flown a hundred times white-knuckled and alone, cargo heavy, sky closing, the sense of being pushed through the world by forces larger than herself. Instead Sable spread the map across both their knees, unnecessary and beloved, and called the riverbends by their old names — the names from the drowned village, the names her father had written in the margins of his logs — and Ulyana flew them home through the first snow flurries of the year laughing at the sound of a river getting its language back.

The delta lights came up out of the dusk like embers raked forward. She brought Provision down onto the home strip soft as a held breath, in the blue hour, with snow starting to stick to the blue-gray wings, and cut the engine, and sat for a moment in the ringing quiet with Sable's shoulder warm against hers.

"Eleven landings," Sable said. "All delivered. Not bad for a girl with no debts."

"I have debts," Ulyana said. "Just the good kind now."

Outside, the snow thickened, and the settlement lamps burned, and somewhere under the ice the river went on keeping everything it had been given — the ashes, the names, the year — until the thaw came to ask for it back.

Tomorrow there'd be mail to sort and a storm to wait out and, come spring, a fight worth having. Tonight there was smoked fish and wool mittens and a full fuel tank, and when Sable took her hand on the walk to the shed, neither of them pretended it was for balance.

The compass rode on the dashboard behind them, needle steady, pointing at nothing they needed to follow.
