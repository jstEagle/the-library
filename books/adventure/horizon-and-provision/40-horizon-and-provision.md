## 40. Horizon and Provision

The dawn came up clean for the first time in a month, and Ulyana Ishikawa stood on the delta landing with a mug of coffee going cold in her hand and watched the light come down the river like something being poured.

Frost had come in the night — the first of the season, thin as breath on glass, silvering the reeds and the tarps over the fuel drums and the mooring lines of the fishing boats. The river ran dark beneath it, unhurried, carrying its cargo of ice-flecks south toward the sea. Somewhere upstream, behind walls of stone and ice she would not see again until spring, the gorge was closing. She could feel it the way you feel a door shut in another room: not a sound, exactly, but a change in the pressure of the world.

She drank her coffee and let herself have the moment without filling it with anything. No manifest to check. No debt schedule running in the back of her head like a second engine. No voice — Ansel's voice, courteous as a leash — asking what she intended to do next.

What she intended to do next was paint her airplane.

* * *

The Cessna sat at the edge of the strip where they'd dragged her after the registry run, one wing patched, the fuselage streaked with river mud and old smoke and the particular grime that comes from being loved hard instead of kept well. Sable was already under the port wing with a rag and a tin of solvent, working at the scorch mark where the rifle round had kissed the metal on takeoff. She'd been at it since before first light. Of course she had. Sable did everything since before first light; Ulyana suspected she'd been born slightly ahead of schedule and had been making up the difference ever since.

"You missed a spot," Ulyana said, crouching at the wingtip.

"I missed three spots. I'm saving them for you."

"Generous."

"That's what everyone says." Sable looked up. There was frost in her hair too, or maybe it was just going gray early; Ulyana had decided months ago not to ask which. "New paint's in the shed. Okonkwo carried it up from the boat an hour ago and then complained about his shoulder for twenty minutes, so we all know he's feeling better."

"How is he?"

"Bored. Which for Okonkwo is the same as healed." She wiped her hands. "He wants to fly with us today. Says he has measurements."

"Of course he does."

"He says the aquifer doesn't care about our feelings and neither should the winter run."

Ulyana laughed — actually laughed, out loud, into the cold bright air, and heard how strange and good it sounded. There had been a time, not so long ago, when laughter felt like a luxury item she hadn't budgeted for.

* * *

They painted her in the morning sun, the three of them, because that was what the settlement did now — things got done by whoever was standing there. Marta from the fish-drying shed brought brushes. Two of the Ferreira kids "helped," which meant they painted each other and one section of the tail that would forever after be slightly the wrong blue. Old Tomas, who remembered her father, sat on an overturned crate and told the story again about the pilot who used to land on this strip when it was barely a strip at all, back before the dam failed, back when the valley had a name on every map instead of none.

"He always waved," Tomas said. "Every pass. Even when he was late, even when the weather was eating him alive. He'd tip the wing at whoever was watching."

Ulyana kept her brush moving. "I don't remember him waving much."

"You weren't watching from the ground," Tomas said, and went back to his tea, and let the sentence sit there doing its work.

The new paint was the color of the river in October — a deep, cold green-blue that didn't exist in any catalog. Sable had mixed it herself from three tins of leftovers and stubbornness. On the nose, below the cockpit, Ulyana painted the registration numbers, and then, after a long moment with the brush hovering, added four small letters in white:

**H & P**

Horizon and provision. Her father's old shorthand, from the flight logs — the two things every pilot trades in, the two things the river country had never had enough of. She'd found it penciled inside the cover of the cache logs, next to the compass bearings, and she'd understood it only later, standing in the fish-drying shed while the council argued about water rights: it wasn't a motto. It was an accounting method. You flew toward the horizon, and you carried what people needed. That was the whole business. Everything else — the syndicate, the ledgers, the claims and counter-claims — was weather.

"There," Sable said, stepping back. "Now she looks like she belongs to somebody."

"She belongs to me."

"That's what I said."

* * *

Okonkwo arrived mid-morning with his instrument case and his shoulder sling and his expression of a man prepared to be patient with fools, which was his resting face and also, Ulyana had learned, his most affectionate one.

"The winter run," he said, spreading his maps on the wing. "I've redrawn the interior legs. With the counter-claim filed, the settlements upriver are entitled to scheduled service — medicine, mail, seed stock. The registry clerk confirmed it yesterday." He tapped a line he'd drawn in fresh ink, threading north through country that had been blank on every map she'd ever owned. "Six landings. Some of them are strips in name only. Some of them are meadows with opinions."

"And the syndicate?"

"Gone to ground. Ansel's lawyers filed appeals; Ansel himself hasn't been seen since the shingle." Okonkwo's eyes came up, careful. "Reyes was spotted in the lowlands last month. Working dock security, someone said. He keeps his head down."

"Good," Ulyana said, and found she meant it in the complicated way — pity without forgiveness, but also without appetite. Reyes had stolen their fuel and nearly their lives, and he had also, in the end, talked to no one who mattered, because there had been no one left to talk to. She wished him a small life, honestly earned, and thought that was probably more than he'd expected.

"And the gunboat?" Sable asked.

"Sold. Scrap price. The syndicate wrote off the whole upper river as unrecoverable." Okonkwo allowed himself the ghost of a smile. "Arithmetic, in the end, was on our side. It just needed better inputs."

* * *

At noon the wind came around out of the north, clean and dry, the kind of wind that made flying feel less like a gamble and more like an appointment. Ulyana walked the plane twice, checking every rivet and control surface the way her father had taught her — *the airplane tells you everything, little bird, if you ask politely and check twice* — and then she climbed into the cockpit to set up for departure.

That was when she took out the compass.

It lived now in a soft leather pouch in her breast pocket, where once it had lived in a locked drawer in Mr. Ansel's office, held against her father's debt like collateral, like a hostage. Brass case worn smooth at the edges by forty years of thumbs. Her father's thumb first, then hers. Inside the lid, the engraving she'd finally been able to read under proper light, the day after the ledger burned: **FOR THE RIVER'S SAKE — E.I.**

Elias Ishikawa. A witness, not a debtor. A man who'd flown evidence through monsoons and died doing it, and whose daughter had spent eleven years paying a debt that had been invented to keep her small.

Ulyana set the compass on the dashboard, in the little bracket her father must have machined for it decades ago — she'd found the screw holes the week before, and understood, and said nothing to anyone, because some inheritances you install quietly. The needle swung, wobbled, settled. Steady. Pointing not north but a few degrees east of it, along a bearing that meant nothing to a stranger and everything to her: the heading to the first landing of the winter run, a meadow-with-opinions above the drowned village where her father had once bought fuel from a woman who kept goats.

The needle didn't tremble. It just pointed, the way good instruments do, indifferent to what you've done and interested only in where you're going.

Sable's head appeared at the cockpit door. "Passenger manifest says one hydrologist, one ex-surveyor, six crates of medicine, two of mail, and one box of Marta's smoked fish that she swears is for her sister but we both know is a bribe for the teacher at Landing Three."

"A bribe for the teacher. Noted and approved."

"And the navigator asks whether the captain intends to wave this time."

Ulyana looked at her. Sable leaned in the doorway with the frost-light on her face and the patience of someone who had learned, in a glacier valley and a cave camp and a hundred miles of bad river, exactly how long a person can wait for an answer and still call it patience.

"Yeah," Ulyana said. "I intend to wave."

* * *

They went out over the delta at half past noon, wheels leaving the strip in front of half the settlement, because in river country a departure was public business and nobody apologized for watching. Ulyana tipped the wing at the fish-drying shed. She tipped it at Tomas on his crate. She tipped it at the Ferreira kids, who were jumping off the mooring float in defiance of both season and parents, and at Marta, and at the whole glittering sprawl of water and reed and roof that had, without ever announcing it, become hers.

Not owned. Belonged-to. There was a difference, and she had paid eleven years and one burned ledger to learn it.

Okonkwo rode in back with his maps and his shoulder sling, narrating terrain features to no one. Sable rode beside her in the right seat, navigating with a folded paper chart and occasional glances at the compass on the dash, though she didn't need either; she knew this country now the way Ulyana knew fog. Between them, unspoken, sat the whole architecture of the winter: the six landings, the cold months, the room above the drying shed that Sable had already claimed and half-filled with survey notes, the fact that neither of them had said anything about spring because spring was very far away and neither of them was going anywhere.

The river unspooled below, silvering north. The mountains came up out of the haze — the interior, the blank place, the country the maps forgot. Except the maps didn't forget anymore. There was a new sheet in the territorial registry with Ulyana's father's survey stamps on it and Okonkwo's aquifer contours and Sable's glacier margins, and six settlements printed on it in proper type, named, visible, real. The blankness had been a lie all along. So had the debt. So had the idea, which Ulyana had carried like ballast since she was nineteen years old, that she was a woman passing through other people's lives, invisible, provisional, owed to somewhere else.

She banked east, onto her father's bearing, and the compass needle swung with her and steadied, pointing the way.

"First landing's forty minutes," Sable said. "Wind favors the meadow. And the teacher likes her fish smoked, not dried, so don't let Marta tell you different."

"Copy," Ulyana said.

Ahead, the horizon ran clean and cold and far, and the provision was in the crates behind her seats, and the needle was steady, and she was not flying away from anything at all.

She tipped the wing anyway — at the river, at the mountains, at the drowned village and the living ones, at the man who'd taught her to check twice and the woman who'd taught her to wait — and flew on into the country that finally had her name on it, staying.
