## 1. Chapter 1

The tide was out so far that morning the river seemed embarrassed by it.

Tamsin Almeida stood at the wheel of the Marguerite with one boot up on the rail and a mug of coffee going cold in her hand, watching the water pull back from the cliffs below her father's land like a curtain drawn on something it had been hiding for years. Ten years she'd run this stretch of the Rivergate, and she'd never seen the channel floor bare itself past the third marker buoy. The old-timers at the fuel dock had been talking about it all week — lowest tide in a decade, they said, moon and wind and luck all pulling the same direction at once. Tamsin didn't put much stock in luck, but she put a great deal in what the river chose to show you when it wasn't busy being a river.

And this morning it was showing her a cave.

It sat low in the cliff face beneath the Almeida property, a black mouth maybe eight feet above the exposed rock shelf, half-hidden behind a skirt of kelp and old netting that had snagged there sometime before either of them were born. She'd heard stories about it, of course. Every kid in Rivergate had. Her father used to tell one version over dinner — some smuggler's cache, some shipwrecked crew's last harbor — always changing the details, always ending the same way: *and nobody who went in came out richer.* He'd said it with a smile, but he'd also never once taken her down to see it, which for her father was its own kind of answer.

The Marguerite idled beneath the cliffs, her engine ticking as it cooled, and Tamsin drank her coffee and let the boat drift just close enough to look. The cave mouth gaped, dark and patient. Water still pooled inside it somewhere — she could hear it, a hollow breathing sound, like the cliff itself was asleep.

"Nothing down there but wet rock and disappointment," she told the river, because talking to the river was cheaper than talking to a therapist and only slightly less effective.

But she marked the spot on her chart anyway. Pencil, small x, no note. Some habits you inherited along with the property.

* * *

She was backing into her slip at the Almeida dock an hour later, sun fully up now and burning off the river mist, when she saw him standing at the end of the planks like a man waiting for a ferry that had been cancelled years ago.

Rafe.

Five years, and the first thing she noticed was that he'd gotten his hair cut short, which somehow made him look more like their father than the long hair ever had. Same lean build, same way of standing with his weight on his heels like he expected the ground to be pulled out from under him. He'd traded whatever city clothes he'd left in for a canvas jacket that had seen actual weather, and there was a duffel at his feet, which meant he'd come planning to stay at least one night, which meant he wanted something.

Tamsin brought the Marguerite in slow, taking her time with the lines, giving herself the full length of the dock to decide how she felt about any of this. The answer, when she arrived at it, was: complicated, and mostly tired.

"You're blocking my mooring," she called, because civility was easier when it started with logistics.

"I'm standing next to your mooring," Rafe said. "There's a difference. Legally speaking."

"Hm. Five years away and you come back knowing legal terms. That's ominous."

He almost smiled. She caught it and killed it with a look, out of principle.

She tied off, stepped onto the dock, and for a moment they just looked at each other across four feet of weathered cedar, two people doing arithmetic on everything that had gone unsaid since the funeral. He looked older around the eyes. She supposed she did too. The river took its toll in small installments and never sent a bill.

"Tamsin."

"Rafe."

"You look good."

"You look like someone who slept on a bus." She picked up his duffel before he could protest, mostly so she'd have something to do with her hands. "Come on. Coffee's fresh, if you count reheating as fresh."

"That's how Dad always—"

"Yeah," she said. "I know how he always did it."

That shut him up, which hadn't been her intention exactly, but she didn't take it back either. They walked up the dock path together, single file, because the path was narrow and because walking side by side would have required a different kind of conversation than either of them had packed for.

* * *

The kitchen smelled like diesel and cinnamon, which is what happened when you lived on a working boat and baked when you couldn't sleep. Rafe sat at the table where their father's chair used to be, and Tamsin noticed him notice it, and neither of them mentioned it. She set down two mugs and sat across from him instead of beside him, which was also a choice.

"So," she said. "You wrote a postcard from somewhere in the interior three years ago. A mountain, I think. Or possibly a very ambitious hill."

"It was a mountain."

"It was blurry."

"The postal service out there is one man on a bicycle, Tam. Cut him some slack."

"And then nothing. For two more years." She wrapped her hands around her mug. "So you'll forgive me if I skip the part where I ask how you've been and get right to it. Why are you here, Rafe?"

He turned the mug a slow half-circle on the table, lining up the handle with the wood grain, buying time the way their father used to line up his tools. When he finally looked up, he had the expression of a man who'd rehearsed a speech on a bus and was now discovering buses were poor rehearsal venues.

"I got a letter," he said. "Three weeks ago. From a law office in the capital. About Dad's estate."

"There's no estate. There's a house that leans, a dock that complains, and a boat I already own. The lawyer confirmed it all last year. Probate closed."

"This wasn't probate." He reached into his jacket and produced an envelope, heavy cream paper, official-looking, and laid it on the table between them like a card played face-up. "This was a private firm. They said Dad had holdings they represented. Unregistered ones. They wouldn't say what over the phone. They wanted me to come in person."

"And you came here instead."

"You're the one who stayed. You're the one who knows the property, the water, all of it." He shrugged, and the shrug had five years of distance folded inside it. "Also, the letter was addressed to both of us. I checked. Your name's right there under mine. They just couldn't find you, because apparently you don't exist according to the postal service."

"I exist fine. I'm just hard to find on purpose." She looked at the envelope without touching it. "Holdings. What kind of holdings does a riverboat captain have that need a private law firm?"

"That's what I came to find out."

Tamsin drank her coffee and thought about the black mouth of the cave, the way the water had peeled back from it like the river clearing its throat. She thought about her father changing the endings of his stories, and never once taking her down the cliff path, and the year he started locking the boathouse, which she'd always assumed was about the outboard motor.

"There's a tide," she said slowly, "the lowest in ten years. It pulled back far enough this morning to show something under the cliffs. On our land."

Rafe went very still, in the particular way of a man whose instincts are older than his manners. "Show me something like what?"

"A door," she said. "One we were never allowed to open."

* * *

They walked the cliff path in the late morning, the two of them and forty feet of careful silence between them, the ground dropping away on their left in gray terraces of rock and salt grass. The path was their father's work — cut steps, a rusted iron rail in the steep sections — and it struck Tamsin, with her boots finding each worn step without thinking, that he had maintained this path for thirty years for a destination he never spoke of.

The tide was coming back in, but slowly. The rock shelf below the cliffs lay exposed and gleaming, ribbed with channels, littered with the usual gifts: crab shells, bleached driftwood, a tire from some decade best not investigated. And above the tideline, the cave mouth waited, bigger up close than it had looked from the water, tall enough to walk into upright.

Rafe stopped at the bottom of the steps and just looked at it.

"How long has that been there?" he asked.

"Always. Underwater, mostly. Dad used to tell stories about it." She hopped down onto the shelf, testing the rock with her heel. "None of them ended well. That was sort of his signature move."

"He was a cheerful man."

"He was a careful man dressed up as a cheerful one. There's a difference, and it took me thirty years to spot it."

Inside the cave mouth the air was cold and mineral, tasting of stone and old salt. Their footsteps echoed wrong — too flat, too close, as though the rock swallowed sound rather than returning it. Twenty feet in, the daylight gave out, and Tamsin clicked on the flashlight she'd had the sense to bring, the beam swinging across walls slick with moisture.

Then the light caught something that wasn't rock.

A mark, chiseled into the wall at shoulder height. A circle with a line through it, and beside it a string of numbers, shallow but deliberate, the cuts still sharp after however many years.

Rafe's breath went out of him slowly. "Those are survey marks."

"I know what they are."

"Dad was a riverboat captain."

"Dad was a lot of things he didn't put on his tax forms." Tamsin moved the light deeper. More marks followed, spaced like breadcrumbs, leading inward. The floor sloped up gently, above the reach of even the meanest tide, and the air grew drier, and at the back of the cave, where the beam finally settled, there was a shape that resolved, as they approached, into an iron strongbox, rusted orange at the seams, sitting on a flat shelf of stone like it had been set down yesterday by someone meaning to come right back.

Neither of them moved for a moment. Somewhere behind them, far off, the sea breathed against the cliff, patient as a creditor.

"You said the lawyers wanted you to come in person," Tamsin said quietly. "What exactly did they call these holdings?"

Rafe didn't answer right away. He crouched by the box, ran his thumb along the rust, and when he spoke his voice had changed — lower, quicker, the voice of a man reading terms he'd already decided to accept.

"They used one word," he said. "Concession."
