## 2. Chapter 2

**TAMSIN**

The cave breathed.

That was the first thing Tamsin noticed as she picked her way down the wet shingle below the cliffs — a slow, tidal exhale from the dark mouth in the rock, the kind of sound that made you feel like you'd interrupted something private. The lowest tide in ten years had pulled the sea back like a sheet being drawn off furniture nobody expected to look at again, and there it was: her father's cave, or at least a cave on her father's land, which amounted to the same thing now that he was eleven months gone.

"You're sure this is legal," Rafe said behind her. He said it lightly, but she knew him well enough — five years of silence hadn't erased her ability to hear when he was stalling.

"It's our land."

"Our land is above the high-water mark. That—" He pointed at the cave's throat, black and glistening. "—is currently the ocean's living room, and we are tracking mud on its carpet."

"Then we'll apologize to the ocean." She ducked under the lip of rock before he could find another objection. Cowardice by way of geology.

Inside, the light changed. It came down in one long shaft through a crack in the cliff face, silver and dusty, and it fell across something that stopped her mid-step.

Survey marks. Chiseled into the wall in her father's unmistakable hand — the same cramped, stubborn capitals he'd used on every chart in the Marguerite's wheelhouse. *A.A. — 1974.* And beside it, painted in faded orange that had gone the color of old blood: an arrow pointing deeper in.

Rafe came up beside her. She felt him go still the way you feel weather change.

"He knew about this place," Rafe said quietly.

"Papa knew about everything on this coast. Knew it better than the charts did." She kept her voice level. It took effort. Eleven months, and still the sight of his handwriting could knock the wind out of her like a boom swung low.

They followed the arrow. The floor sloped up out of the waterline into a chamber wide enough to stand in, and there, wedged against the back wall where the shaft of light couldn't quite reach, sat a strongbox.

It was rusted the color of a fox's coat, half-buried in gravel and dried kelp, with a padlock so corroded it looked less like security than decoration. Somebody had dragged it here decades ago and left it for the sea to keep.

"Well," Rafe said. His voice had gone strange — bright and careful at once, like a man whistling past a drop-off. "Hello, gorgeous."

"Don't talk to it."

"I'm establishing rapport." He knelt, worked his fingers under the lid's edge, and hauled. The lock gave with a crack that echoed off the walls, and both of them flinched like children caught at something.

Inside: not gold. Not coins. A ledger, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, and beneath it a bundle of survey instruments, a brass plumb line green with age, and — absurdly, heartbreakingly — a tin of peppermints fused into a single geological formation.

Tamsin laughed before she could stop herself, and the laugh came out wrong, wet at the edges.

"He always had those," she managed. "In the wheelhouse. Said sugar kept a pilot honest because it gave your hands something to do besides worry."

Rafe didn't say anything. When she glanced over, he was holding the tin very carefully, turning it over, and his jaw was doing the thing it did when he was losing an argument with himself.

She unwrapped the ledger.

* * *

**RAFE**

The ledger smelled of salt and iron and time. Rafe held it while Tamsin angled the lantern they'd brought — because of course she'd brought a lantern, because Tamsin prepared for everything except forgiveness — and the first page nearly stopped his heart.

*HORIZON CONCESSION. Salvage rights, all wrecks and recoveries between Gull Point and the Rivergate Narrows. Filed under seal.*

Filed under seal. Their father's name signed as witness. Dated forty-one years ago.

"Do you understand what this is?" he heard himself say. His voice sounded like someone else's, someone younger and hungrier. "This isn't a claim, Tam. This is a concession. Grandfathered. If it was never closed out — if there's no release on record — then every salvage right on twelve miles of this coast is sitting in a rusted box waiting for somebody to pick it up."

"Somebody being us."

"Somebody being *us,* yes." He turned pages. Wreck locations. Depths. Dates. Their father's neat columns of figures, and margin notes in that same hand — *masts visible at spring low*, *chain bed intact*, *worth a diver's season*. Decades of knowledge, compiled patiently, secretly, on land Rafe had spent five years pretending didn't exist.

He looked up, ready to build the argument brick by brick, and found Tamsin watching him with an expression he recognized from childhood: the look she'd worn whenever he'd come home with some scheme, some found money, some too-good deal. Not anger. Worse. Disappointment with a head start.

"You want to sell it," she said.

"I want to *file* it. What happens after is arithmetic." He heard how fast he was talking and made himself slow down. "Tam. You run one boat up one river. I've got debts you don't even want to hear itemized. This ledger is worth more than everything either of us will earn in twenty years, and Papa — Papa *left* it to us. Buried it in our own backyard. That's not an accident. That's an inheritance."

"That's a trap with our name on it."

"How?"

"Because Papa hid it, Rafe. Under a rock, under the sea, under a lock. Men don't bury fortunes. Men bury things they're afraid of." She took the ledger from him gently, which was somehow worse than if she'd snatched it. "And because whoever he was afraid of might still be around."

"The syndicate's been defunct since the nineties."

"Paper doesn't die just because the men do. Paper gets bought." She set the ledger on the strongbox lid and stood looking at it, arms crossed, silhouetted against the silver shaft of light. "You know what I keep thinking about? The funeral."

Rafe went cold. "Don't."

"Forty people came. Half of them he'd have called strangers. And not one person — not *one* — mentioned this coast, this land, the Concession, any of it. Everybody talked about the river. Like the last thirty years of his life were the only ones that counted." Her voice stayed steady; her hands didn't. "Whatever this was, he walked away from it. Maybe we should let sleeping wrecks lie."

"Or maybe he walked away from it because Mama died and he couldn't bear the sight of anything he'd built with her, and he meant to come back and never did, and he left this here for exactly this day, for exactly us." Rafe heard the plea in his own voice and hated it. "You can't read grief backwards, Tam. You can't decide what a dead man meant."

"No," she agreed softly. "But I can decide what a live one does."

The cave breathed again, that long tidal sigh, and for a moment neither of them moved. The ledger lay open between them like a border crossing.

"We take it back to the boat," Tamsin said finally. "We look at it properly. In daylight. With coffee." She fixed him with a look. "And Rafe — we decide together. Both of us, or neither. That's the only way I carry this thing another step."

"Together," he said. And found, to his surprise, that he meant it.

* * *

**TAMSIN**

They carried the strongbox up the shingle together, taking turns, arguing cheerfully about grip technique the way siblings argue when neither wants to argue about the real thing. The tide had begun its long crawl back in; already the cave's mouth wore a lace collar of foam, and Tamsin hurried them along with the particular urgency of a woman who had seen what this coast did to the careless.

At the top of the beach, Rafe set his end down and straightened, breathing hard, and looked out at the Marguerite riding at her mooring — small and white and stubborn against the gray water.

"She needs paint," he said.

"She needs a hundred things. She gets two of them a year, same as me."

He smiled. It was the first real smile she'd gotten from him in five years, crooked and familiar, and it landed somewhere under her ribs and stayed there, warm and inconvenient.

Then he frowned. "Tam. There's a boat."

She followed his gaze. Far out, near the channel marker, a launch sat with its engine off — too far to make out faces, too deliberate in its positioning to be fishing. Nobody fished the slack water off Gull Point. Every soul on this river knew it was dead water.

"Probably nothing," Rafe said.

"Probably." But her father's voice was in her head suddenly, clear as the day he'd taught her to read water: *Nothing on this river is ever nothing, girl. The river just hides its intentions better than most.*

She looked down at the strongbox between them, at the ledger inside with its sealed name — *Horizon Concession* — and felt the shape of something closing around them, patient and slow as the tide coming back in.

"Help me get her aboard," she said. "Quickly."

Behind them, soft as breath, the sea slid back into the cave and swallowed the light.
