## 3. Chapter 3

The stranger arrived on the morning tide, which Rafe thought was fitting. Everything in Rivergate came and went on the water's schedule — the fish, the freight, the gossip, the grief. You learned early that the river gave nothing freely, and took payment whether you agreed to terms or not.

Rafe was on the dock repairing a cleat his sister had been complaining about since before he'd left, five years of rust and rot he was trying to make up for in lag bolts, when the launch came around the point. It was a fine boat. Too fine. Polished mahogany, twin engines idling with the smug purr of money that had never been rained on, and at the wheel a man in a coat that cost more than the Marguerite's annual haul.

Two more men sat in the stern, doing an excellent impression of furniture.

"Morning," the man called up, all teeth and warmth. "Corliss. I'm looking for Tamsin Almeida."

"She's not here." She was, actually — up at the house going through their father's papers again, but Rafe had learned caution the hard way, mostly by lacking it.

"Then I'll look for you instead. Rafe Almeida, isn't it? The prodigal." Corliss tossed a line to the dock without waiting to be asked, and one of the furniture men caught it and made fast with the ease of long practice. "I hear you've been home almost two weeks now. Reconciling. Very touching. Families should reconcile."

"We manage."

"I'm sure you do." Corliss climbed up onto the planks like a man boarding his own property, glanced at the cleat Rafe had just replaced, and said, "Nice work. You'll want stainless next time. Salt eats everything here eventually." He said it pleasantly, the way a man says *I know what you have and how long it will last.*

Rafe set down the wrench slowly. "What is it you want, Mr. Corliss?"

"What everyone wants. A conversation." He smiled. "Preferably indoors. Preferably with coffee. I've been on the water since four."

* * *

Tamsin met them in the kitchen of the old house, arms crossed, wearing the expression she'd perfected somewhere around age fourteen — the one that could curdle cream at forty paces. She didn't offer chairs. Corliss took one anyway, settling into their father's spot at the head of the table as if he'd been coming here for years, and Rafe watched his sister's jaw tighten at the sight.

"You've been in the cave," Corliss said. Not a question.

"The tide's been out," Tamsin said. "People walk the flats."

"People do." He opened a leather case on the table — slow, deliberate, a magician laying out his props. "Let me save us all some time. Twenty-two years ago, a salvage operation called the Horizon Concession filed a claim on the waters and seabed off this property. Wreck of the *Cormorant*, cargo manifest valued — conservatively — at a sum I won't say aloud in your father's kitchen because it feels rude." He smiled again. "The claim lapsed. Never closed, never renewed. Dead paper, technically. Except dead paper has a way of standing up when someone needs it to."

"And you're the someone," Tamsin said.

"My syndicate holds the successor interest. We intend to file to revive it. There are procedures. There are also—" he tapped the case "—alternatives."

Rafe leaned against the counter where he could see both the door and the water. Old habits. "Which is where we come in."

"Which is where you come in." Corliss produced a document, unfolded it, turned it to face them. "A quiet sale. Your father's littoral rights, your cooperation, your signatures, and a number with enough zeroes to buy a new boat, a new life, and a very long vacation from each other. Everyone walks away rich. Nobody goes to court."

The silence stretched. Rafe could hear the tide breathing under the dock, could hear Tamsin not breathing at all.

"And if we don't sign?" she asked.

Corliss sighed, genuinely regretful, or a very good imitation of it. "Then we file, we win, and you get nothing but legal bills. That's the honest version. But here's the part I suspect you don't know yet, and I confess I'm curious whether you've found it." He reached into the case again and drew out a photostat, yellowed, creased from decades of folding. "Because your father knew about the Concession. Knew about it for years. And three weeks before he drowned, he went to the land office in Rivergate himself and filed this."

Tamsin took it. Her hands were steady; her voice wasn't quite. "A counter-claim."

"A counter-claim," Corliss agreed. "Contesting the Concession's boundaries, citing survey marks on the cliff face — which, I understand, the recent low tide has so helpfully exposed. If that filing is confirmed before the window closes, our revived claim dies in its cradle. If it isn't confirmed..." He spread his hands. "Paper war. Years of it. And my syndicate has deeper pockets than a riverboat captain and her brother."

"When does the window close?" Rafe asked.

Corliss looked at him with something almost like respect. "When the pass snows in. First heavy snow closes the Rivergate range, closes the land office's circuit season with it. The assessor rides out one last time before winter. After that, nothing moves until spring — and by spring, our filing will be sitting on top of yours like a stone on a seedling." He stood, buttoned his coat. "Three weeks, give or take the weather's mood."

He left the photostat on the table. He left a card, too, weighted with a coin so it wouldn't blow away — a small theatrical touch Rafe suspected he practiced in mirrors.

"Consider the offer," Corliss said from the doorway. "Truly consider it. Your father didn't, and we both know how that ended." Then he was gone, down the steps, across the dock, and the launch pulled away leaving a wake that rocked the Marguerite against her fenders like a dog shifting in its sleep.

* * *

They didn't speak for a long while after the engine noise faded. Tamsin sat at the table turning the photostat over and over, and Rafe put the kettle on because neither of them would think to otherwise and because his hands wanted occupation.

"He knew," Tamsin said finally. "Dad knew they'd come."

"He filed a counter-claim three weeks before he died. Yes. He knew."

"No — I mean—" She pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "I mean he knew they were *coming*. Already. That night. Why else file then? Why else keep the ledger in the strongbox instead of the bank?" She looked up at Rafe, and there was something in her face he hadn't seen since they were children huddled under blankets during a storm, listening to the wind try the shutters. Fear, and fury at being afraid. "Everyone says he drowned drunk. Everyone always said it. But Dad stopped drinking after Mom. You told me that yourself, once, before you—"

"Before I left," Rafe finished quietly.

"Before you left."

The kettle began its low climb toward whistling. Rafe took it off early. Some sounds you didn't need right now.

"Corliss knew about the cave," he said. "Knew about the survey marks. Knew about us reconciling, which means he's had eyes on this dock since I stepped off the bus. That's not a man making an offer, Tam. That's a man checking whether the lock works before he decides whether to pick it or break the door."

"So we sell."

"Do you want to sell?"

She was quiet a moment. Outside, the light was starting to go gold and long, the way it did in October, the way it had the day they'd buried their father with half the town watching and Rafe conspicuously absent. "No," she said. "But I don't want to lose everything either. And I don't trust a man who compliments a cleat."

Despite everything, Rafe laughed — a short, surprised sound. "That's the thing that broke you? The cleat?"

"It's the *principle* of the cleat."

He sat down across from her, the photostat between them like a third person at the table. Their father's signature stared up at them, cramped and slanting, the same hand that had written tide tables on the kitchen chalkboard and *back by dark* on notes left under magnets.

"Three weeks," Rafe said. "Snow closes the pass, the window shuts, and whatever Dad started dies with the season. Unless we carry it through. The ledger, the strongbox, the original survey notes — over the range to the land office before the assessor's last ride."

"That's eight days' hard walking in good weather."

"It's October. Weather won't be good."

Tamsin looked at him for a long moment, and Rafe recognized the calculation behind her eyes because he was running the same one: the weight of the boxes, the height of the pass, the men in the stern of a polished launch who were very good at catching lines. The trap wasn't the offer. The trap was the calendar — Corliss hadn't needed to threaten anything at all. He'd only needed to tell them the truth, and let the clock do the squeezing.

"He left us a map and a deadline," Tamsin said softly. "Typical Dad. Couldn't just leave the boat."

"He left us a fight," Rafe corrected. "Whether we take it is ours."

Tamsin slid the photostat into her shirt pocket, over her heart, like a talisman, and stood. When she spoke again her voice had changed — lower, steadier, the captain's voice, the one that could be heard over engines and gales and five years of silence.

"Then we'd better start tonight. Corliss's watchers will expect grief and indecision. Let's give them a day of it — and load the Marguerite in the dark."

Rafe nodded, already reaching for the lamp to kill it before full dark fell, and outside, far down the channel, a second launch — one he hadn't seen arrive — slipped quietly behind the point and dropped anchor where it could watch the dock.

They weren't alone anymore. They hadn't been for weeks.
