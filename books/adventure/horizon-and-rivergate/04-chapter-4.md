## 4. Chapter 4

The ledger smelled of salt and mildew and, faintly, of pipe smoke — a ghost smell that stopped Tamsin's hand every time she turned a page. Their father had smoked his last pipe the year Rafe left home, but the scent had soaked into everything he'd owned: his charts, his oilskins, the cushions on the Marguerite's wheelhouse bench. Five years gone and he still managed to fill a room.

She sat at the galley table with a lamp turned low and the ledger open to its final third, where the handwriting changed. The early entries were in their father's careful surveyor's hand — numbers squared off like fence posts, notes in the margins about tide tables and rock strata. But somewhere around the spring of his last year, the letters began to lean. To hurry. As if the man writing them kept looking over his shoulder.

Rafe sat across from her, pretending not to watch. He'd been pretending for two hours. He'd made coffee twice, drunk half of one cup, and rearranged the tools in the galley drawer with the focused intensity of a man who found screwdrivers more emotionally manageable than conversation.

"You're hovering," Tamsin said.

"I'm standing."

"You're hovering while standing. It's a skill."

He sat down. Progress.

The problem was the handwriting itself. Their father had written fast when he was agitated, and he'd written agitated for months before he drowned — they'd both known it, both said nothing at the time, because saying something would have meant asking questions neither wanted answered. Now the agitation was all that was left of him, slanting across the pages in ink that had faded to the color of old bruises.

Tamsin held the page close to the lamp. *Concession permit renewed 19—* no. Crossed out. Underneath, pressed so hard the pen had nearly torn the paper: *renewal is FALSE. Corliss knows. Checked the registry myself.*

"Corliss," she said quietly.

"The man who sent us flowers and a threat in the same envelope." Rafe leaned forward. "Read it again."

"*Renewal is false. Corliss knows.*" She traced the words without touching them. "Dad checked the land registry himself. The Horizon Concession's permits expired decades ago. They've been salvaging under our cliffs on paper that doesn't exist."

"So the fortune isn't even theirs." Rafe laughed, but there was no humor in it — just a kind of bitter vertigo. "We've been circling a treasure chest with a broken lock, and the lock was broken by the people guarding it."

"There's more." She turned the page. The next entry was dated eleven days before the drowning. The handwriting had deteriorated further, letters collapsing into each other like tired men. *Filed counter-claim today. Clerk Voss witnessed. If anything happens to me, the claim stands until the window closes. Window closes with the pass. Tell my children—*

The sentence stopped. Not trailed off. Stopped, mid-thought, as if he'd been interrupted, or as if he'd realized there were some things you couldn't put on paper for strangers to read.

*Tell my children.*

Tamsin became aware that her eyes were burning. She didn't let them do anything about it.

"He knew," Rafe said. His voice had gone flat and careful, the way it went when he was holding something heavy very still. "Tamsin. Read the last entry."

She turned the page. It was the final one. Dated the day before the drowning — the night the Marguerite had come back to the dock empty, the night a harbor patrolman had knocked on their door with his hat in his hands.

The entry was four lines long. It took her three tries to read it aloud.

*"Boat came past the point after dark. No lights. Two men asked about the cave at the Anchorline. I told them nothing. If I go out tomorrow and don't come back, it wasn't the river."*

The lamp hissed softly. Somewhere outside, water lapped against the hull, patient as ever.

"It wasn't the river," Tamsin repeated. Her voice sounded strange to her, borrowed. "Everyone said — the coroner said — Dad went out in weather he shouldn't have. Everyone said he'd been drinking."

"Dad hadn't touched a drop in ten years," Rafe said. "I used to watch him pour it into the ficus. Mom thought the plant loved him."

Despite everything, something cracked open in her chest that might have been a laugh. "The ficus. God. It was enormous."

"It was a tree, Tam. It reached the ceiling."

And then, because grief is an unsteady floor, they were both laughing — short, awful laughter that broke apart almost immediately and left them sitting in the lamplight with wet faces, holding the same terrible sentence between them.

*It wasn't the river.*

"He wrote it down," Tamsin said finally. "The night before. He wrote it down and he went out anyway, because he thought—" She stopped. Started again. "Because he thought writing it down would be enough. That whoever came for him wouldn't dare."

"Or he couldn't run," Rafe said. "He had a claim to protect. A family. He stayed because staying was the only move he had." He looked at her across the table, and for a moment five years of silence fell away entirely, and he was sixteen again, furious and frightened, the night he'd stormed out over a thing neither of them could now fully remember. "We have moves he didn't, Tam. We have the ledger. We have the strongbox. We have three weeks."

"Corliss has watchers on the dock," she said. "Has since yesterday. Man in a gray cap who reads the same newspaper without turning the page. And a launch that does slow circuits past the point at dusk, official-like, running lights like a patrol boat that never patrols."

"So we don't leave at dusk."

"No," Tamsin said. "We leave at two in the morning, on the ebb, through the Narrows, and we're forty miles downstream before their newspaper reader finishes his coffee."

* * *

Loading a boat in darkness is an art, and Tamsin had been practicing it her whole life. Everything had its order: heavy low, light high, nothing that clinks near anything that matters. The strongbox went into the forward bilge, wrapped in an oilskin and buried under coiled line. The ledger went inside her coat, against her ribs, where it rode like a second heartbeat.

Rafe worked beside her in silence, passing gear hand to hand, and it struck her how easily their rhythms fit again — how twenty years of childhood on this deck lived underneath five years of estrangement, patient as ballast. He cast off the stern line without being told which end. He fendered the piling exactly the way their father had taught them both.

"You remembered," she said.

"I remember everything about this boat," he said. "That was sort of the problem."

She let that sit. There would be time — on the gravel bars, in the mountain cold — for the whole ugly accounting of why he'd left and why she'd let him. Tonight they had a river to run.

At 1:40 a.m., the gray-cap man was visible even in the dark, a shape on the bench by the fuel office, newspaper folded on his knee. Tamsin watched him through the wheelhouse glass and felt fear turn over inside her, the way a keel turns — and then settle, and drive.

Fear was fuel. Their father had known that too. *If I go out tomorrow and don't come back, it wasn't the river.* He'd been afraid, and he'd gone out anyway, and she could do no less.

"Engine room's quiet," Rafe reported, dropping into the mate's seat. "I oiled everything that squeaks. Which, in fairness, was most things."

"This boat does not squeak."

"This boat sings opera. I've muffled the soprano section."

Tamsin eased the Marguerite off the dock on idle, letting the ebb take them, no wake, no lights beyond the minimum the law allowed. The gray-cap man did not look up. Behind them the cliffs of their father's property slid past, black against blacker sky, the sea cave hidden somewhere below the tideline, keeping its secrets the way it always had.

Then the point, and the open river, and the Narrows ahead — a mile of channel barely wider than the Marguerite herself, where the ebb ran hard over rock shelves and the current spoke in a language only pilots understood.

"Take the wheel?" she offered.

"You're the captain."

"I'm offering."

"And I'm declining, on the grounds that I value my life." But he moved to stand beside her, close enough to share the compass light, and put his hand on the rail above hers. "Call your marks. Like when we were kids."

Tamsin almost smiled. "You'll get them wrong on purpose."

"I got them wrong once. Eleven years ago. You've never let me forget."

"Because we nearly lost the rudder."

"Because I was twelve."

"Excuses," she said, and brought the bow around toward the Narrows, and the river took them.

The current seized the hull and the world narrowed to black water and white foam-lines and the small red eye of the compass. Tamsin called her marks low and steady — *midchannel rock abeam, steer for the shadow, ease her left* — and Rafe echoed them back, and for a few wild minutes they were children again at their father's elbows, shouting the river into order while he laughed from the wheel.

Halfway through, a light appeared behind them.

Not a house light. Not a star. A running light, sweeping around the far bend below the point, moving fast — the dusk-patrol launch, up at two in the morning, coming on at speed with its searchlight dark.

"They know," Rafe said.

"They suspected. Now they know." Tamsin's mouth had gone dry, but her hands were steady. Fear, sharpened into purpose. "Hold on. We're going to be impolite about this."

She gave the Marguerite everything she had. The old engine roared — the soprano section entirely unmuffled now — and the boat surged down the throat of the Narrows, spray coming green over the rails, the ledger a hot weight against Tamsin's ribs. Behind them the light grew, patient and certain, a syndicate that had waited thirty years and murdered for less.

But the river was theirs tonight. Their father's marks, their father's boat, their father's stubborn blood in both their veins.

They shot out of the Narrows into the broad dark lower reach, forty miles of open water between them and the river town where Odile's mule train waited, and Tamsin set their course and watched the pursuing light fall slowly, slowly astern.

"We made it," Rafe breathed.

Tamsin didn't answer right away. She was watching the light behind them — watching it stop chasing, hang motionless at the Narrows' mouth, and begin, deliberately, to signal. Three flashes. Pause. Three flashes.

"That's not giving up," she said. "That's calling ahead."

The light blinked its message into the dark, and somewhere downriver, in the town where they meant to buy their passage over the mountains, someone was reading it.
