## 5. Chapter 5

The launch came out of the dusk like a rumor with an engine.

I heard it before I saw it — a low, greedy throb of horsepower that had no business on this stretch of river after dark. I was at the stern of the Marguerite, pretending to check the wake light, and Tamsin was at the wheel doing that thing she does where her whole body goes still and quiet, like a heron deciding whether the fish is worth the strike.

"Rafe," she said.

"I hear it."

"That's Corliss's boat. Or his money's boat, which is the same thing." She eased the throttle forward a notch, casual as anything, as if we were simply enjoying the evening air. "Don't turn around. He'll read it in your shoulders."

"He can read my shoulders?"

"Tamsin Almeida," she said, "can read anybody's shoulders."

The launch swung wide behind us, then began to close. Through the dark I caught the shape of it — low, black-hulled, running without lights, which told me everything about its intentions. Honest boats run lit. Thieves run dark.

"They want us to stop," I said.

"They want us to *volunteer* to stop. There's a difference, and it lives in courtrooms." Her eyes flicked to the water ahead, and something in her face changed — not fear, exactly. More like a woman greeting an old friend she'd sworn off. "Hold on to something. And whatever happens, don't ask questions until we're through."

"Through what—"

The river bent left around a bluff, and instead of following the bend, Tamsin turned hard right, straight at a wall of reeds and shadow that my eyes insisted was solid land.

"Tamsin—"

"*Don't ask questions.*"

We went into the reeds. I braced for the crunch of hull on gravel and got instead a sudden hush, the engine dropping to a murmur, the Marguerite sliding down a channel so narrow the cattails brushed both rails at once. The launch's engine roared behind us, confused, then committed, then — a long grinding shriek of metal on sandbar, followed by shouting, followed by a silence that sounded expensive.

Tamsin killed our engine and let us drift.

For a while neither of us spoke. The channel opened just enough to show stars, and somewhere ahead, water moving over gravel made a sound like slow applause.

"The Sow's Ear," she said finally, with grim satisfaction. "Dad called it that. Said it was a silk purse if you knew the thread count." She glanced back toward the sounds of the grounded launch, now faint and furious in the distance. "That bar shifts every spring flood. There's maybe four people alive who know where it isn't."

"And you're one of them."

"I'm one of them." She let out a breath. "He taught me. Not you."

There it was. Five years of silence, and we'd managed three days together before it surfaced again. I leaned against the rail and watched the stars do their indifferent work overhead.

"No," I said. "Not me."

* * *

We grounded on a gravel bar an hour later, because the Sow's Ear only gives passage at certain tides and certain tempers, and we'd missed both by minutes. Tamsin ran the Marguerite up onto the shingle with the gentlest touch I've ever felt from a boat that size, dropped anchor, and announced that we were now prisoners of the moon.

"Six hours," she said, building a small driftwood fire with the efficiency of someone who'd done it ten thousand times. "Maybe seven. Water comes back, we go on."

"So we wait."

"We wait." She handed me a tin cup of coffee that tasted like it had been brewed during a war. "Drink that. It'll put hair on whatever you've got."

I sat across the fire from her. The ledger was wrapped in oilcloth under my arm, and I set it between us like a third person at the table. Our father's handwriting crawled across its cover in faded ink: *H.C. — accounts & claims.* Horatio Cade Almeida, who signed everything with his initials because he said full names were for gravestones and land offices.

Which, I thought, looking at my sister's firelit face, was about the size of it.

"You weren't at the funeral," she said.

Not a question. She'd been saving it, I realized, since the dock. Maybe since before the dock. Maybe since the day I walked out five years ago and let the river take my messages unopened.

"No," I said.

"Three hundred people came. Three hundred. Half the county." She poked the fire harder than the fire deserved. "Men who hadn't spoken to him in twenty years. Women he'd charmed at the grange dances. The whole Rivergate Volunteer Fire Brigade, in uniform, standing in the rain like he'd been their captain." A short, bitter laugh. "And his son couldn't cross a county line."

"It wasn't the county line that stopped me."

"Then what?"

I looked at the fire. It seemed easier than looking at her. "The last time I saw him, we fought. You know that. You were there for the start of it and gone for the end — you'd taken the Marguerite up to Kestrel Landing."

"I know how it started."

"Do you know how it ended?" I waited. She didn't answer, so I did. "He told me I was a tourist in my own family. That I'd come back from the city with soft hands and city ideas, and that the claim — the Concession, all of it — would go to whoever had the stomach for it. And I said fine, keep it, keep all of it, I never wanted your buried treasure anyway. And he said—" My voice did something I didn't authorize. I cleared my throat. "He said, 'You're just like your mother, Rafe. She walked out too.'"

The fire popped. Downriver, an owl asked its one question and got no answer.

"He didn't mean it," Tamsin said quietly.

"He meant it enough to say it."

"Dad said a hundred things he didn't mean. Dad said the Marguerite was ugly. Dad said Odile Pradier couldn't find her way out of a paper bag." She almost smiled. "He said *you* were the disappointment, once, when you were nine and you cried over a gull with a broken wing and he told you the river doesn't keep pets. Then I found him at midnight in the boathouse, splinting the wing with lath strips and swearing me to secrecy."

I stared at her. "He never told me that."

"He wouldn't have. That was the whole point of Dad." She drew her knees up, wrapped her arms around them. "Everything true about him lived sideways. You had to catch it out of the corner of your eye or not at all."

"Why didn't you come after me?" It came out more accusation than question, and I regretted it even as it landed. "Five years, Tamsin. One postcard. You knew where I was."

"Because you knew where *I* was too." Her voice stayed level, but her knuckles went white around her own knees. "And because every time I picked up a pen, I heard him saying I was cleaning up after you. Because that's what I did, Rafe. My whole life. You'd fight with him and leave, and I'd stay and mend the nets and listen to him pretend he wasn't waiting for your letters. Somebody had to be the one who stayed, and I hated it, and I hated you a little for making it be me, and then he drowned, and there was nobody left to be angry at except—" She stopped. Looked at the river. "Except everybody."

The tide was turning; I could feel it before I could see it, a subtle change in the voice of the water against the shingle. Neither of us moved.

"I should have come to the funeral," I said.

"Yes."

"I'm not going to dress it up. I was a coward. I thought if I didn't stand there, it wouldn't be finished. Some part of me believed he could still—" I shook my head. "That's not grief, is it. That's arithmetic."

"That's family," Tamsin said. "Nobody in this family ever met a feeling they couldn't postpone."

Despite everything — despite the cold coming off the water and the men hunting us somewhere downstream and the ledger sitting between us like a loaded thing — I laughed. Actually laughed, and heard her laugh too, ragged and reluctant, two people discovering the same terrible joke from opposite ends.

She reached over and took the coffee tin from my hands, drank from it herself, and handed it back. An old ritual. A truce older than the war.

"When the water's back," she said, "we run for Harrow Bend and put this boat up. We go over the pass on foot with Odile. And Rafe—" She looked at me straight, firelight in her eyes, and for a second she was sixteen again and daring me to jump off the cliff at Miller's Point. "Whatever we find at the end of that ledger, we decide it together. No more leaving. Either of us."

"Together," I said.

She nodded once, satisfied, and stood to check the anchor. And that was when she went still — that heron-stillness — staring downriver at the far bank, where a match flared briefly in the dark. Then another. Two points of light, patient as ticks.

"Rafe," she murmured. "How attached are you to sleeping tonight? Because somebody just found our smoke."
