## 6. Chapter 6

The town was called Ferrier's Landing, which was generous, since the ferry had rotted to ribs a decade ago and the landing was mostly mud. But it had a stable, a dry-goods store, and a saloon with a stove that actually worked, and after three days on the river with Corliss's watchers somewhere behind us, that was luxury enough.

We ran the *Marguerite* in at dawn, fog still lying on the water like something asleep. I tied her off at the old pilings and put my hand flat against her wheelhouse door.

"You're sure about this," Rafe said. It wasn't quite a question. He'd learned to phrase things as not-questions around me, which was either progress or surrender.

"She's too slow for the pass country and too loud to hide." I said it evenly, but my hand stayed where it was. "I've left her before."

"Not for five years you haven't."

"No," I agreed. "Not for five years."

The plan was simple enough to say out loud: forty miles of river road to the trailhead at Cold Saddle, then over the Rivergate range before the snows closed it — six days if the weather held, nine if it didn't, and we had maybe eight. The ledger rode inside my coat now, wrapped in oilcloth, warm against my ribs like a second heartbeat. The strongbox stayed aboard the *Marguerite*, chained under the floorboards, because there are limits to what a person will carry up a mountain, and because part of me — a part I didn't examine too closely — wanted something of hers waiting when we came back down. If we came back down.

* * *

Odile Marchand found us before we found her, which should have been my first warning about how this town worked.

She was drinking coffee at the back table of the saloon at eight in the morning, a woman built like a fence post, sixty if she was a day, with a face weathered down to essentials and eyes that had clearly already priced us, weighed us, and found us wanting but possibly profitable. A mule train guide who'd been running Cold Saddle for thirty years, according to the stableman, "when she feels like it, and God help you when she doesn't."

"Almeida," she said, when Rafe gave our names. Not a question either. "River people. Your father used to ship freight through here. Good payer. Terrible tipper."

"You knew him?" Rafe asked, and I heard the hunger under it, the same hunger I'd been swallowing all week.

"Everyone knew Elias Almeida. He sat where you're sitting." She turned her cup a quarter turn on the saucer, precise as a surveyor. "Now. You want the pass, and you want it quiet, and you want it fast. That's three wants, and two of them fight each other. Six mules, two riders, my boy Tomas cooking, and Henri on lead. Four hundred dollars, half up front, and you don't ask me to hurry the mules past their sense."

"That's steep," Rafe said.

"That's cheap," Odile said, "for what you're actually buying, which is your lives. Sign or don't."

Rafe looked at me. I looked at the money belt. We signed.

It was only later — much later, when I'd had time to count things — that I noticed she'd never once asked what we were carrying. And that everyone else in that room had been very carefully not looking at us at all.

* * *

By noon I understood why.

The general store smelled of coffee, kerosene, and molasses, and the man behind the counter — a soft-bodied fellow named Pruett with a smile like a folded receipt — went from cheerful to careful the moment Rafe mentioned we were heading over the pass.

"Cold Saddle's closed early some years," he said, wrapping our beans with great attention.

"It's October the ninth," I said.

"Mountains keep their own calendar." He slid the parcel across. "You folks be careful now."

Outside, an old man mending a net by the dock watched us pass without moving his hands. Two children stopped playing to stare, then ran off toward the saloon, quick as birds flushed from cover. And across the street, in the window of the telegraph office, a man stood watching us with the particular stillness of someone whose job is watching — a lean, neat figure I recognized from three days ago, standing on the bank at Miller's Bend, doing nothing at all.

Corliss's man. Or one of them. Here first, settled in, comfortable.

"He bought the town," Rafe said quietly. We were walking, not stopping, both of us suddenly very interested in the scenery. "That's what this is. You don't need muscle in a place like this. You buy the ferry rights, the credit at the store, the telegraph. Make us strangers everywhere."

"He didn't buy Odile," I said. "She set her own price and didn't blink."

"People can be bought without knowing they're sold," Rafe said, and there was something bitter and personal in it, and I chose — deliberately, with effort — not to ask what that meant.

* * *

The saloon that evening was crowded in the way small-town saloons get crowded in October: trappers flush with early pelts, a crew off the dredge barge, the usual furniture of men who live where the work is. Warm light, fiddle tuning up in the corner, the stove ticking. Under any other circumstances it would have been pleasant.

Under these circumstances, every laugh sounded rehearsed.

Tomas, Odile's cook — a big, gentle-seeming young man with flour on his forearms — brought us stew and told us about his sister in Kelso until Rafe relaxed by degrees. Henri, the lead packer, sat alone at the end of the bar with his back to the wall, drinking steadily and saying nothing, which I might have read as shyness except that his eyes kept doing arithmetic: us, the door, the stairs, the back window. Twice.

And at the corner table, four men who hadn't been in the room an hour ago were playing cards with the loose, loud friendliness of men performing friendship. One of them wore a canvas coat with a rip at the shoulder that I had seen before — seen it on a man poling a skiff through the reeds below our father's cliffs, the morning after we found the cave.

"Tamsin." Rafe's voice was low, barely above the fiddle. "Corner table. Don't look."

"I'm not looking."

"The one dealing. He dealt three hands without shuffling."

I laughed at something Tomas said, warm and easy, and under the laugh said, "Back exit?"

"Through the kitchen, probably. Odile—"

"—is at the stable with the mules, which is either the safest place in town or the worst, depending on who owns her loyalty, and we won't know which until it costs us." I spooned up stew I couldn't taste. "So we smile, we eat, we go upstairs, and tomorrow we're on the trail where there's nowhere for anyone to hide."

"And tonight?"

"Tonight we take turns sleeping."

He nodded, and for a moment — just a moment — something passed between us that felt like the old thing, the childhood thing, the way we used to read each other across a deck without words. Then the card players roared at some joke, and it was gone.

* * *

It was near midnight, my turn awake, sitting by the window of the rented room with the ledger a hard shape against my ribs even through the coat, when I saw the light.

A lantern, moving along the river path toward the stable. It paused. It swung twice — deliberate, measured, a signal, not a wanderer's sway — and went dark.

Ten minutes later a second light answered from the direction of the telegraph office.

I woke Rafe with a hand over his mouth, which he took remarkably well for a man who'd spent five years away from people who loved him. I pointed. He watched the dark stable yard for a long time.

"Henri's bunk is empty," he whispered.

"Maybe he's walking."

"Nobody walks at midnight in October for pleasure."

"Then maybe he's meeting someone. Or maybe—" I stopped, because saying it made it real. "Or maybe he's the one Corliss owns, and tomorrow he takes us up the mountain and there's a party waiting at Cold Saddle with better boots than ours."

Rafe sat on the edge of the bed, elbows on knees, head down, thinking the way our father used to think — completely, silently, annoyingly. When he looked up, his eyes had gone calm in a way that frightened me more than the lantern had.

"Then we don't wait for tomorrow," he said. "We go to Odile now. Tonight. We tell her everything — the cave, the ledger, Corliss, all of it — and we watch her face while we do it. She's the only one in this town whose price we know, because we paid it. If she's honest, we've lost nothing. If she's not—"

"Then we find out in a stable yard at midnight with no witnesses."

"Yes," Rafe said. "Exactly like Father would have done it."

And that, God help us, was the argument that convinced me.

* * *

The stable was warm and dark and smelled of hay and mule, and Odile was awake, sitting on an overturned feed bucket by the lantern with a rifle across her knees, mending a harness strap, as though midnight visitors were a scheduled service.

"Took you long enough," she said, without looking up. "Sit down before somebody sees you standing in the doorway like a pair of churchwardens."

"Henri's gone," Rafe said.

"Mm." She bit her thread. "Gone an hour. Went out the back and down toward the water, quiet as a cat that knows it shouldn't be out." She finally raised her eyes to us, and they were tired and clear and absolutely unafraid. "Now. You two have been circling something since you walked into my saloon wearing guilt like Sunday coats. So say it plain, because I don't ride for people who lie to me, and I especially don't ride for people who let me lie for them blind."

So I said it plain. The cave. The marks. The ledger. The Horizon Concession, and Corliss, and the filing window, and the counter-claim our father filed before he drowned. I watched her face while I did it, exactly as Rafe promised, and what I saw was not surprise. What I saw was a woman rearranging numbers behind her eyes and arriving at a total she'd suspected for days.

"Elias Almeida's ledger," she said at last. "In your coat right now."

"Yes."

"And the syndicate man has been in my town three weeks, buying drinks and opinions, asking after any party heading for Cold Saddle." She set down the harness. "Three weeks, and he never once asked me, because he knew what I'd charge him and knew he couldn't afford honest. So he went cheaper." She jerked her chin toward the dark corral. "Henri's got a wife in debt and a brother-in-law who works for Pruett at the store. That's how it's done, girl. Nobody sells a whole man. They sell the piece that's already cracked."

"So he'll lead us into them," Rafe said. "On the trail."

"Probably at the bridge over the Kettle, where it's narrow." Odile stood, unhurried, and took down a second lantern. "Which is why Henri will wake up tomorrow morning with a fever I diagnosed personally, and Tomas will run lead instead, and we'll leave an hour before dawn while the card players in the saloon are sleeping off the whiskey I sold them myself." She smiled then, briefly, like a crack of sun through cloud. "Four hundred dollars buys your lives, I told you. I wasn't haggling."

I let out a breath I felt I'd been holding since the cave. "Why?" I asked her. "Why help us? You knew Father. Everyone keeps telling me that. But that's not a reason."

Odile trimmed the lantern wick with her thumb, and for a moment she looked older, and sadder, and further away.

"Your father carried my sister's coffin down out of Cold Saddle in a spring flood, on his own back, when no boat could reach the trailhead and no one else would try," she said. "He never spoke of it after. Never once let me thank him proper." She picked up the lantern. "Some debts sit thirty years waiting for the heirs to come collect them. Now saddle up, Almeida. We've got a mountain to cross and a liar to outrun — and come daylight, I want to see how fast your brother can lie about a fever."

Behind us, out in the black yard, a lantern swung twice toward the water, and nobody answered it.
