## 7. Chapter 7

The mountain did not announce itself. It just kept getting closer, the way a debt does.

Rafe Almeida had climbed things before — fences, mostly, and once the water tower behind the cannery in Rivergate when he was nineteen and stupid and trying to impress a girl who'd already left town. But this was different. This was the Rivergate range proper, the pass their father used to point at from the deck of the Marguerite and say, *That's where sensible people turn around.* The trail had given up pretending to be a trail two days ago, and now it was a suggestion — a line of cairns Odile's mules followed with more conviction than Rafe felt.

Odile Marchand led from the front, small and weathered as a river stone, calling corrections over her shoulder in a voice that carried without effort. "Left of the scree, monsieur Rafe. The right side looks friendly and will kill you."

"Everything on this mountain works that way," Rafe said.

"Yes," she said pleasantly. "It is honest that way. Better than people."

He glanced back at Perrin.

Perrin was one of Odile's four hired men, a broad-shouldered, soft-spoken fellow who'd joined the train at the last river town and hadn't said ten words since. He handled his mule beautifully. He checked the lashings on the cargo — including the oilskin-wrapped ledger strapped to Tamsin's pack animal — more often than anyone else. Rafe had told himself for three days that this was diligence. Now, watching Perrin's hands linger on the knots a beat too long, he told himself other things too, none of them comfortable.

Tamsin rode ahead of him, hunched into her father's old canvas coat, and when she caught him looking she raised an eyebrow. She always knew. Five years apart and she could still read him across a mountainside like a tide chart.

*Later,* he mouthed, and she frowned but let it go.

The rope bridge came into view near midday, slung across a gorge where the trail dropped away into a gray-green roar of meltwater. It was maybe forty feet of planks and hand-lines, ancient and sun-bleached, swaying gently in the wind like something asleep. Odile stopped the train and walked out onto it herself, testing each anchor with both hands, then stood at the far end and looked back with an expression Rafe would think about for a long time afterward.

"It has been cut and re-tied," she said. "Recently. Badly. On purpose."

The train went quiet except for the mules, who were stamping and blowing in a way that suggested they'd known before any of them.

"One of you," Odile said, not loudly, walking slowly along the line of men, "has been paid to lose us this crossing. I do not know which. So we will all stand here while I look at your faces, and if nobody confesses, we go back down and take the long way, which costs everyone two days and possibly our lives when the snow comes." She paused. "Or someone confesses, and I only break his nose."

Nobody confessed. Perrin looked at the gorge with an expression of mild professional interest.

"We don't have two days," Tamsin said quietly. "Corliss's party is better equipped than we are. They'll have taken the low route around the shoulder — they're probably a day behind us now instead of three."

"So we cross a sabotaged bridge," Rafe said.

"So we cross a sabotaged bridge," Odile agreed, "after I re-tie every knot myself. Which takes an hour. Monsieur Rafe — you have strong hands?"

"I've been told they're my best feature."

"Don't flatter yourself. Come hold rope."

They worked together in the wind, Odile untying and Rafe holding tension, and it was strange how quickly trust could be built out of shared labor — stranger still how much he liked this woman he'd known four days. She talked while she worked, about her father who'd run this pass for thirty years, about the year the avalanche took the winter camp and only her mother's stubbornness brought anyone home. "The mountain gives you exactly what you bring to it," she said, cinching a knot. "Bring fear, get fear. Bring care—" she patted the new line "—get across."

Rafe was anchoring the far-side hand-line when he saw it: Perrin, at the rear of the train, easing toward the lead mule. The one carrying the ledger. Not the bridge anchors — those were fixed now, checked twice. But the cargo lashings, the ones that held everything they'd bled for, were still being transferred to fresh packs for the crossing.

"Perrin," Rafe called, casual as he could make it. "Give me a hand with this line."

Perrin hesitated half a second too long, then came. His hands were steady. His eyes weren't.

They crossed in singles, mules first, then the men, then Tamsin, who crossed the swaying gap with her jaw set like she was daring the gorge to try something. Rafe went last with Odile. Halfway across, the wind gusted, the whole bridge swung, and for one lurching moment Rafe hung over forty feet of white water with nothing but knotted hemp between him and a very short, very loud life. Odile laughed at him. He decided he deserved it.

They made the far side by early afternoon, and that was when the mountain collected its price anyway.

It happened fast, the way disasters do — no drama leading up, just a crack of splitting wood as the near-side anchor post, rotten inside where no one could see, gave way under the strain of the last mule's pull. The animal screamed. The bridge whipped sideways. And Odile, who had gone back alone to free the tangled lead line off the broken post, went over the edge with it.

Rafe didn't remember deciding. Later, piecing it together, he understood that some part of him had been braced for exactly this ever since Odile said *on purpose* — that he'd spent the whole crossing watching the anchors instead of the view, and when the post went he was already moving, already dropping to his belly on the remaining planks with his arm out over the void.

His hand closed on her wrist. Her weight hit the line like a falling tree. The fraying hand-line burned through his grip and he slid a foot, two feet, the gorge roaring below, and he heard Tamsin screaming his name and Perrin shouting something and none of it mattered because there was only the rope and the woman and the simple arithmetic of holding on.

"You will not let go," Odile said. She wasn't begging. She was instructing, calm as a harbor pilot, even hanging over certain death. "You will not let go, monsieur Rafe."

"Wasn't planning on it," he gritted.

Perrin appeared beside him — actually appeared, dropping flat, taking the line above Rafe's hands, hauling with the strength of a man who'd done this work all his life. Between them they walked the load back inch by inch until Odile's boots found the planks and she pulled herself up, soaked to the waist, hair whipping, breathing hard.

She looked at Rafe for a long moment. Then she looked at Perrin.

Then she hit Perrin so hard in the face that he sat down on the bridge and didn't get up quickly.

"The money Corliss paid you," she said, shaking out her fist, "will not cover what I would have done to you if Rafe had missed. Get off my mountain. Walk. If I see you again I will not be this generous."

Perrin went, cradling his jaw, and nobody watched him go except Rafe, who watched all the way until the trees took him, feeling something ugly and familiar turn over in his chest. Because he'd known. Three days of knowing and saying nothing, telling himself *later*, waiting for proof, for certainty, for the perfect moment — and the cost of his caution had nearly been Odile's life.

Tamsin found him sitting on a rock after the train moved on. She handed him tea from the dented pot and didn't say anything, which was her way of saying everything.

"I knew," he said finally. "About Perrin. I was almost sure. And I waited, because I wanted to be wrong, because accusing the wrong man on this mountain gets everyone frozen out of their own camp, and—" He stopped. "Dad used to wait like that. Read a current three times before committing. And the one time it mattered, the night he drowned, he must have—"

"Hey." Tamsin sat down beside him. "You caught her. That's the entry in the book. You can't audit yourself on the things that didn't happen."

"No," Rafe said. "But I can pay for the ones that did. Two days, Tam. Re-tying, recovering, resting the mules after that scare. Two days we don't have, and the weather's already—" He nodded east, where the peaks had spent the afternoon putting on cloud like a man dressing for a funeral.

Tamsin followed his gaze. Above them, the pass they needed — a thin notch in the ridge line, still hours of climbing away — had begun to disappear into gray.

"Then we walk faster," she said, "and colder."

* * *

They made camp that night in a hollow below the tree line, the last trees they would see, and the cold arrived like a creditor. Odile doubled the watch. The remaining three men — Bex, Halloran, and quiet young Tomas, who couldn't have been more than twenty — looked at Rafe differently now. Not warmly, exactly. But when Bex handed him the first pour of coffee without being asked, Rafe understood he'd bought something on that bridge that couldn't be bought any other way, and that it might be the only currency the next few days would accept.

He slept badly and woke before dawn to the sound of Odile standing at the edge of camp, looking up.

Snow. Not falling yet — but sitting on the high ridges like a held breath, and the wind had turned, and it smelled the way cold smells before it commits.

"Two days lost," Odile said quietly, when he joined her. "And the sky has noticed us." She glanced back down the way they'd come, where the valley lay dark and patient. "Your Monsieur Corliss took the low road. He has not lost two days. If he pushes, he could be behind us by tomorrow night."

"Then we're racing."

"We are always racing, monsieur Rafe. The trick is to race something slower than you." She shouldered her pack and looked up at the notch in the ridge, white now, sharp against a bruised sky. "We reach the high shelf today or we do not reach it at all. Dress warm. Say goodbye to the easy part."

Above them, the first flakes came down — small, dry, almost gentle, like the mountain testing whether they were paying attention.
