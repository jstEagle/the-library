## 8. Chapter 8

The mountain lied to us about the weather.

Odile had promised us a day and a half of clear sky — "a window," she called it, tapping the clouds the way a doctor taps a chest — and we'd climbed through that window like thieves through a casement. For thirty hours the range had been almost kind: granite shoulders dusted white, mules complaining in their patient way, Rafe humming some half-remembered shanty through chapped lips just to hear something besides wind.

Then, an hour past the saddle below the high col, the sky closed like a book.

It didn't snow so much as arrive. One moment I could see Odile ahead, small and certain against the slope; the next she was a gray suggestion, and the next after that she was gone entirely, and there was only the rope at my waist pulling taut with each of her steps, my one thread of proof that the world still contained anything but white.

"Rope team!" Odile's voice came back to me flattened by the wind, stripped of everything but its bones. "Tighten up! Count your people!"

I counted. "Four!" Me, Rafe behind me, then the two packers, Baptiste and young Halloran, and the mules strung between. Five souls and six animals, all of us breathing steam into a whiteness that swallowed it whole.

"Three!" Rafe's hand found my shoulder from behind, squeezed once. In five days on the mountain he'd learned the language of her hands again — the squeeze that meant *here*, the tap that meant *look*, the grip that meant *don't say it, I already know*. We'd spent twenty years learning it the first time and five years forgetting it, and apparently the forgetting was the shallow part.

We moved. That was the whole of the strategy now: move, and count, and move.

* * *

We made the shelter rocks — a leaning slab of granite with a hollow beneath, big enough for four people if two of them were willing to be very good friends with a mule — shortly before dark, which by then was a distinction without much difference. Odile got a stove hissing in the hollow's mouth while Baptiste saw to the animals. I sat with my back to stone and the ledger inside my coat, inside its oilcloth, inside my arms, and tried not to think about how much it weighed. Not the paper. The paper weighed nothing. It was everything else it carried that made my arms ache.

"You should eat." Rafe crouched beside me with two cups of something that smelled like pine tar and ambition. He pressed one into my hands. "Odile calls it tea. She also calls the mule 'the gentleman,' so her standards are flexible."

I drank it. It was terrible. It was the best thing I'd ever tasted, or my body had simply stopped taking requests.

"Rafe." I kept my voice under the wind. "If it comes to it tomorrow — if Corliss catches us —"

"Don't."

"Let me finish. The ledger goes in the fire before it goes in his hands. Promise me you'll do it. You're faster than me."

He looked at me for a long moment, steam rising off his cup, and I watched him weigh it — the fortune he'd wanted, the brotherhood he'd only just gotten back, the arithmetic between them. Five years ago I'd have bet on the fortune. Five years ago I'd have been right.

"I promise," he said. "But it won't come to that."

It came to that.

* * *

They overtook us mid-morning, out of the whiteout like wolves out of fog — six figures on snowshoes, roped tight, moving with the unhurried confidence of men whose equipment cost more than our entire expedition. Corliss led them himself. Of course he did. A man doesn't chase a fortune across a mountain range and send an assistant for the last mile.

"Tamsin Almeida!" His voice carried strangely in the flat air, close and far at once. "A word!"

"A word," Rafe muttered at my shoulder, "with six rifles in it."

They weren't quite rifles — short carbines, slung but visible, which was its own kind of message. Corliss stopped ten feet away, snow crusting his eyebrows, and even half-frosted he managed to look like a bank manager. Behind him his men spread out with drilled ease, and I noted — filed away — that two of them were looking at their leader sideways, the way crew look at a captain who's stopped making sense.

"You're done, Captain," Corliss said. Almost gently. "The pass closes tonight. Your guide knows it. Give me the ledger and the box, take my offer as written — passage down, cash, no hard feelings — and everyone walks off this mountain as friends."

"And if we don't?" I asked, though I knew. I'd known since the river.

Corliss sighed, and drew his carbine, and held it low and casual as a walking stick. "Then this becomes a different conversation. One where I stop pretending I have time for manners."

The wind screamed through the gap behind us. Somewhere under my coat, against my ribs, my father's handwriting waited — *they will come for it, they always come* — and I thought: *he wrote that standing on a riverbank at night, and here I am, and they came.*

"The ledger stays," I said.

"Tamsin—" Rafe started.

"No." I pulled the oilcloth bundle halfway out, holding it where every man on that slope could see it. "You want to know what's in here? A dead man's numbers. Salvage tonnage from forty years ago. Permit filings. And one line, Mr. Corliss, in my father's own hand, saying he knew someone meant him harm the night he drowned. That's what you've chased across a mountain. Paper and a ghost."

Something flickered across Corliss's face — not guilt. Impatience. That was somehow worse.

"The ghost is irrelevant," he said. "The claim is real. Hand it over."

And that was when the mountain decided it had heard enough.

* * *

The ground gave no warning. Whiteout country never does — the snow bridges the crevasses the way a liar bridges a story, smoothly, until you're standing on nothing.

The crack came from under the mules. A shriek of harness, a lurch, and the lead animal — the gentleman himself — went down into white that wasn't ground, dragging the rope line, and the whole world became a single taut cord of panic. Baptiste shouted. Halloran threw himself flat. And the strongbox, lashed to the second mule's pannier, tore free and slid — slow, almost dignified, absurdly graceful — toward the blue-black seam opening in the snow.

I don't remember deciding. I remember being on my belly at the lip of it, arm buried to the shoulder, fingers closing on the box's cold handle just as its weight took me. The crevasse breathed up at me, cold and enormous, and my boots scrabbled for purchase on ice that offered none, and the ledger — God help me, the ledger — was grinding between my chest and the snow, oilcloth tearing, and somewhere very far away Rafe was shouting my name in a voice I'd last heard at our father's funeral.

"Hold!" Odile. Her hand closed on my ankle, then Baptiste's on hers, a chain of strangers and family with me at the bottom of it, hanging over a hole in the world.

The box swung. My shoulder screamed. Below me, forty feet down, the mule had landed on a shelf and stood there trembling, and I understood with terrible clarity that the same shelf would catch the strongbox in about three seconds, and that I did not have the strength to lift it, and that letting go meant watching forty years of my father's life fall into the dark.

"Let it go!" Corliss, of all people, on his knees at the edge, carbine abandoned in the snow. "Almeida — let it GO, you'll pull them all in—"

"Then get on the rope!" Rafe was there suddenly, beside me, his hands over mine on the handle, and his eyes were wild and wet and furious. "Together. On three. Or we lose it AND her, and I swear to God, Corliss, if you're standing there doing nothing—"

Corliss got on the rope.

I'll say that for him until the day I die. He dropped his weapon in the snow, wrapped the line around his forearm, and hauled with the rest of us — his men too, all but one, who stood apart with his carbine half-raised and then slowly lowered it, because there are limits even to hired loyalty, and a woman dangling over a crevasse exceeds most of them.

The box came up. Slowly, in agonies, an inch at a breath, my father's rusted iron rising out of the blue dark like something surfacing from a drowning — and the moment it cleared the lip we all fell backward in a heap of rope and steam and shaking limbs, and the strongbox sat among us in the snow, dented, dripping meltwater, heavier than any object has a right to be.

For a long moment nobody spoke. Six syndicate men, four of ours, one mule still bawling on its shelf, and the wind filling the silence for all of us.

Then one of Corliss's men — the big one, the one who'd lowered his rifle — said quietly, "Boss. Enough."

Corliss turned on him. "Enough? Do you know what's—"

"We nearly died," the man said. "For a *box*. She nearly died. You put us on a rope next to the people we came to rob, in a whiteout, above a hole." He looked around at his fellows, and none of them met his eye, and none of them disagreed. "How much does the syndicate pay a widow's pension, boss? Asking for myself."

Corliss opened his mouth. Closed it. And I watched something happen in his face that I'd seen once on a man going down with his boat — the moment the water wins and the fighting stops being strategy and becomes something older and sadder. He looked at the strongbox. At me. At the whiteout swallowing the pass behind us, the pass that would close tonight, that was closing now.

"The weather's turning," he said finally, to no one, to the mountain. "Everyone knows the weather's turning."

"It turned yesterday," Odile said dryly, coiling rope. "You were just too busy to notice."

* * *

We parted ways at the saddle an hour later — them down the western chute, us over the col, both parties moving now less against each other than against the sky, which had begun dropping snow in fat, silent, ominous flakes, the kind that mean business. Before he went, Corliss paused beside me. Up close his eyes were bloodshot and haunted, and he looked ten years older than the man who'd boarded the Marguerite's deck five weeks ago.

"You'll never make the land office in time," he said. Not cruelly. Almost pleading. "Turn back. Take my offer. Please."

"No," I said.

He nodded, as if I'd confirmed something he'd suspected all along, and turned into the snow.

We climbed. The storm built around us like a wall being raised brick by brick, and by late afternoon Odile called a halt at a shallow overhang, and we dug in, and the temperature began its long slide toward the killing kind. Rafe sat pressed against my side for warmth, the strongbox between his knees, his arms around it.

"We should open it," he said quietly. "While we still can. See what he kept in there."

I looked at the box — scarred, salt-eaten, rescued twice today from the dark. Inside it, whatever it was, my father had carried it long enough and far enough, and so would we.

"Tomorrow," I said. "Tonight we live."

But sleep wouldn't come, and near midnight, in the blue blackness of the overhang, I heard Rafe's breath change — heard the small, sharp intake of a man reading something by matchlight that he cannot believe — and when I rolled over, he was staring at the strongbox lid in his hands, at the hairline seam along its edge that the crevasse's fall had cracked wide.

"Tamsin," he whispered. "It's open."
