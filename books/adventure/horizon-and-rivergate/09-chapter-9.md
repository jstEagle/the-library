## 9. Chapter 9

**Tamsin**

The mountain had stopped pretending to be a place and become a condition, like a fever you walked through.

Tamsin could no longer feel her left boot as anything other than a rumor attached to her leg. Ahead of her, Rafe broke trail with the stubborn, plodding rhythm of a man who had learned somewhere in the last eight days that complaining cost calories. Behind them, Odile's mules — two of the original five; she'd stopped asking about the others — picked their way down the switchbacks with more dignity than any of the humans involved.

"Tell me again," Rafe said, not turning around, "why we didn't take the boat."

"Because Corliss bought every dock between here and the valley," Tamsin said. "And because you said, and I quote, 'how hard can a pass be.'"

"I said that in June."

"You said it in a tavern in June, which is where all bad decisions are born."

He laughed — a short, foggy breath of it — and kept walking. Eight days ago that laugh would have been impossible. Five years ago it would have been unimaginable. The mountain was stripping them down to essentials: breath, warmth, each other. She supposed their father would have found that funny.

The ledger rode against her chest inside her coat, wrapped in oilcloth, warmed by her own failing body heat. The strongbox was gone — swallowed by the crevasse on the second day of the storm, along with Corliss's dignity and most of his men's enthusiasm — but the ledger remained, and the ledger was everything. Their father's hand. His counter-claim. The proof that the Horizon Concession had been rot from its first signature.

The land office closed for winter at sundown tomorrow. The pass had cost them two days on the rope bridge, one day buried in snow, and Odile's patience, which was renewable but expensive.

They crested a shoulder of rock and the valley opened below them — a long green-brown throat of river country, smoke rising from the land office town like a promise. So close. Close enough to hurt.

Then Tamsin saw the figures on the slope behind them.

Four of them, strung out, moving fast. No mules. No camp gear. Just men with ropes and axes and one purpose, descending the mountain like water finding a crack.

"Rafe."

He turned. Squinted. "Corliss."

"He's got nothing left to haul," Tamsin said. "That makes him faster than us and stupider than God."

Odile came up beside them, breathing steam, and looked at the figures for a long moment. "He has lost his strongbox, his permits mean nothing now, and his men have been asking themselves for three days why they are freezing on a mountain for a man who lies." She shrugged. "But grief makes men climb. Come. We go down fast and we do not look back."

Tamsin looked back anyway. One of the four figures raised an arm — not a weapon, just a gesture, wild and human, *wait* — and then they were moving again, all of them, everyone on the mountain pulled downhill toward the same small window of paper and law.

* * *

**Rafe**

Rafe had spent five years learning how to leave things, and the mountain was teaching him the opposite curriculum at a punishing tuition.

By mid-afternoon the descent had gone from difficult to dishonest — the kind of slope that looked walkable and wasn't, ice under new snow, the whole face of it tilted toward a drop that ended in a river gorge. Odile led. Tamsin followed, one hand always on the ledger through her coat, touching it the way other people touched rosaries. Rafe took the rear, which meant he saw Corliss's party close the gap.

Three hundred yards. Two hundred. He could hear them now — boots, breath, the creak of frozen rope. And he could hear something else underneath: arguing. A younger voice saying *enough, Mr. Corliss, it's over, the box is gone.* And Corliss answering in a voice scraped down to wire: *the ledger. The ledger is the claim.*

"They're coming straight down the fall line," Rafe called forward. "They're going to cut across our switchback."

"Let them," Odile said. "Ice does not care whose fault it is."

It happened exactly the way ice doesn't care.

Rafe heard the crack before he understood it — a sound like a rifle, clean and final — and then the slope itself moved, a shelf of wind-loaded snow letting go above the traverse, and Corliss came down with it. Not sliding. Falling, the way furniture falls, arms pinwheeling, past the switchback, past the edge of the shelf, onto a tongue of blue ice that jutted out over the gorge, and then rolling once, twice, and stopping at the very lip of it, fingers hooked into a seam of rock, legs dangling over three hundred feet of nothing.

His men stopped at the break line. Stared down. Did not move.

Rafe was already moving.

"Tamsin!" he shouted, and heard her shout something back — his name, or *no*, or both — but his body had made the decision without consulting him, the way it had when he'd caught Odile on the bridge, the way it apparently always would. He traversed down through the debris, driving his axe with everything the last week had taught him, and reached the ice tongue where Corliss hung against the sky.

Up close, the syndicate man looked smaller than he had in Tamsin's cabin with his muscle and his offer. His gloves were torn. His face was gray. His eyes found Rafe's and there was nothing in them but arithmetic — a man calculating whether his own weight was worth another man's risk.

"Let go of my arm," Corliss rasped, "and this ends cleanly."

"Noted," Rafe said, and reached down, and took hold of his wrist.

* * *

**Tamsin**

From above, Tamsin watched her brother lie flat on the ice with his heels hooked on rock and his hands locked around the wrist of the man who had hunted them across a mountain range, and she understood that some inheritance skips the paperwork entirely.

Their father had died in the river, and the river had never told her why, and she had spent five years building a life with a hole in it shaped like that question. She had watched Rafe spend the same five years building a life with a hole shaped like the family itself. And here was Corliss, dangling over the same gorge their father's river fed into, and here was Rafe holding on.

She got down to them as fast as the slope allowed, Odile behind her, and together they hauled — Rafe lifting, Tamsin pulling Corliss's collar, Odile anchoring them both with a rope and a stream of curses in three languages — until the syndicate man came over the lip and collapsed on solid ground, shaking, weeping without noise, a middle-aged man in a ruined coat learning what the bottom of a gorge smells like.

His men arrived at the edge and stood there, uncertain.

"Go down," Corliss said hoarsely, waving at them. "Go down to the town. It's finished."

They went. Nobody argued. Even obsession, Tamsin thought, runs out of volunteers.

Corliss sat in the snow with his ruined hands in his lap and stared at the valley below, at the thread of river, at the smoke of the town. When he finally spoke, his voice had changed — emptied out, like a house after the auction.

"You want to know how I knew your father filed a counter-claim," he said. "You've been wondering. Everyone wonders how I knew."

Rafe went very still beside her.

"Because I wrote the original permit," Corliss said. "Thirty years ago. I was a clerk in the land office and the Concession's founder paid me to backdate a survey that was never done. Your father's cave, your father's water — none of it was ever theirs. I signed it into being." He laughed, a terrible dry sound. "I built the fortune on a forgery, and then I spent thirty years climbing the ladder it bought me, and then your father — a riverboat captain with ink-stained fingers and no lawyer — found the flaw in my signature and filed against me. One honest piece of paper. Do you understand? One. After everything, the thing that could end me was written by a man who kept his accounts in pencil because he trusted people to correct them fairly."

Tamsin's throat closed. Her father's ledger, against her ribs. His careful, hopeful handwriting.

"The night he drowned," Corliss went on, staring at the river, "I hadn't sent anyone. I want you to know that. I'd sent letters, threats, offers. But the men I hired to frighten him—" He stopped. Started again. "They were supposed to scare him off the filing. There was a storm. There was a boat. I have spent every night since telling myself I didn't kill him, and every morning knowing I did."

The wind moved over the shelf. Below them the valley waited, patient as paper.

"So burn it or file it," Corliss said. "Either way, I'll sign whatever confession you want. The mountain already took everything else. It may as well take the truth too."

* * *

**Rafe**

Rafe sat down in the snow next to the man he'd saved, because his legs had informed him the conversation would happen sitting or not at all.

He thought about the ledger. About the fortune folded inside it like a seed — salvage rights, water rights, a number with so many zeroes it stopped meaning money and started meaning power. Everything their father had bled for. Everything Corliss had lied for. Everything the mountain had tried to keep.

He looked at Tamsin. She was looking at the valley, at the land office smoke, and her expression was one he recognized from the gravel bar, from the cave, from every hard quiet moment since the tide opened the cliff: the look of a woman deciding what a thing was worth.

"We should get off the ice," Rafe said. "All of us. Confessions keep. Frostbite doesn't."

Corliss blinked at him. "You're not going to make me beg?"

"Oh, I'm going to enjoy watching you walk into that land office and say all of that again in front of a clerk," Rafe said. "But begging costs heat, and we're on a budget."

Odile snorted. Tamsin almost smiled — almost, the way the sun almost comes out — and then she stood, and pulled Corliss up by the collar the way they'd hauled him off the lip, and pointed down the mountain.

"Walk," she said. "You've got until sundown tomorrow to tell the truth in a room with a stamp in it. Consider it the first honest work you've done in thirty years."

They went down together — the siblings, the guide, the broken man — four small figures on a white slope, descending toward the river, toward the town, toward the last hours of a window that had cost them everything except the only thing that mattered.

And in Tamsin's coat, against her heart, the ledger waited to learn what it would become.
