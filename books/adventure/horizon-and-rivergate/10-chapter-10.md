## 10. Chapter 10

The land office in Rivergate smelled of pipe smoke, lamp oil, and forty years of other people's paperwork. Tamsin stood at the counter with snowmelt dripping off her boots into a puddle she was trying not to feel guilty about, and watched the clerk — a narrow man named Prewitt with spectacles that kept sliding down his nose — read the counter-claim their father had drafted the week before he died.

"You understand," Prewitt said, for the third time, "that this filing voids the Horizon Concession. It does not *transfer* it. You'd be extinguishing the claim entirely. Salvage rights on that stretch of coast, the cave, everything below the high-tide line — it all reverts to unclaimed water."

"That's the idea," Rafe said.

Tamsin glanced at her brother. He looked terrible. They both did. Nine days on the mountain had carved them down to essentials: chapped faces, cracked lips, hands still scabbed where the rope had burned through Odile's harness and Rafe had caught her one-handed over the drop. His coat was borrowed from a trapper and three sizes too big. He'd lost his hat somewhere above the ice shelf and hadn't mentioned it since.

But his voice was steady, and when he said *that's the idea*, he almost smiled.

Prewitt set the paper down. "There's a fortune in that cave, son. I've seen men walk four hundred miles in worse weather than you just walked to claim half as much."

"We're not most men," Tamsin said. "We're Almeidas. We mostly walk to get home."

Something flickered across the clerk's face — recognition, maybe, or memory. "You're Elias's kids."

It wasn't a question. Everyone in Rivergate knew Elias Almeida, or had known him, or knew the story of how he'd gone into the river one October night and stayed there. Tamsin braced for the usual look — pity, curiosity, the small hungry gleam of a man about to ask something none of her business.

Instead Prewitt just nodded slowly, stamped the counter-claim twice, and slid it into a drawer that clanged shut like a cell door.

"Filed," he said. "Concession's void as of this hour. The syndicate can appeal if they like, but without the original permit—" he tapped the drawer, where Corliss's confession was already written up in an affidavit Tamsin had dictated with blue fingers two days ago— "they've got nothing but embarrassment. And Mr. Corliss, from what your guide tells me, has more embarrassment than he knows what to do with."

Down the street, they could hear him. The syndicate party had come down off the pass behind them, hollow-eyed and frostbitten, and Corliss was holding forth in the hotel lobby about sabotage, about theft, about *his* rights, while his own men sat around the stove pointedly not looking at him. Odile had described it that morning over breakfast with the particular relish of a woman who charged by the day and considered drama a free bonus.

"He keeps saying the mountain cheated him," Odile had said, buttering toast. "I told him the mountain doesn't cheat. The mountain just is. Like a river." She'd looked at Tamsin then, and raised her coffee an inch. "Like some people."

Rafe signed the last page. Prewitt countersigned. A rubber stamp came down — *RECEIVED, RIVERGATE DISTRICT LAND OFFICE* — and that was it.

Forty years of their father's obsession, a fortune in salvage, a syndicate's greed, nine days of cold and fear and a crevasse that had nearly swallowed the strongbox whole: concluded with a stamp and a clang.

Outside, the light was doing that thing it did in late autumn on the coast — low and gold and generous, as if the sun felt bad about the winter coming and was overcompensating. Snow lay thin on the rooftops. The pass behind them was already white to the shoulders; it would close within the week, maybe tomorrow. They'd made it with hours to spare, exactly as planned, which Tamsin suspected meant their father would have found something to criticize about the margin.

Rafe stood on the office steps and breathed out a long plume of steam.

"I keep waiting to feel different," he said.

"Rich?"

"Relieved. Vindicated. Something." He turned the ledger over in his hands — their father's ledger, salt-stained and swollen, recovered from the strongbox before the crevasse took the box itself. "He spent thirty years on this. And we just handed it to a man with sliding glasses and let him put it in a drawer."

"It's not in a drawer," Tamsin said. "It's in a fire."

She'd decided that morning, somewhere on the last mile of descent, watching the valley open up below her with its woodsmoke threads and its bright cold river. She hadn't told Rafe yet. She took the ledger from him now, weighing it.

"Come on," she said. "One more errand."

* * *

They burned it on the Marguerite's stern, anchored mid-channel off the old property, because Tamsin could not think of a better place and Rafe, when she explained, didn't even argue. That alone told her how far they'd come since a wary reunion on a dock five chapters — five years — ago.

The tide was coming in. Below the cliffs, the sea cave had swallowed itself again, the way it did every high water, keeping its survey marks and its rusted secrets under a fathom of green. Somewhere in there was whatever remained of the strongbox's contents — papers, mostly, and a brass compass their father had never used because he trusted the river more than any needle. Let the cave have it. Caves were better at keeping things than land offices were.

Rafe built the fire in the galley stove's ash pan and carried it out carefully, the way you carry anything that matters, and set it on the stern boards between them.

"You're sure?" he asked.

"No," Tamsin said honestly. "But I'm sure about the alternative. Every time I imagine reading that ledger cover to cover — every entry, every grudge, every number he chased instead of coming to my wedding—" she stopped, surprised the words came out so easily, "—I feel the same way I felt swimming after his body. Cold, and busy, and wrong. He wrote all this down so someone would finish it. I don't want to finish it. I want it done."

Rafe was quiet a moment. Then he laughed — short, startled, real.

"You know what he'd say? 'Wasteful girl.'" He fed the first page to the flames. "And then he'd watch it burn and say nothing else, because he'd know you were right."

Page by page, the ledger went. The handwriting their father had fought to decipher on the trail — cramped, slanting, getting drunker toward the end and then abruptly sober again in the final entries, the ones that had named the night of his death before it happened — curled and blackened and lifted off the pan in flakes of grey. Ash rose and drifted astern, and the current took it, and the river did what the river always did with everything it was given, which was carry it somewhere else without comment.

Tamsin found she was crying, a little, and that it didn't hurt. Grief, she thought, was mostly just love with nowhere to go. For five years hers had backed up like a flooded bilge. Now it had somewhere to go: into the fire, into the air, into the water her father had loved more than any fortune and died inside of, and she would never know all of why, and she was going to have to make peace with that, and — watching the ash scatter on the ebb — she thought maybe she already was.

"The name stays," Rafe said suddenly.

She looked at him.

"Almeida. On the boat, on the claim records, on whatever we do next. He wanted us to be rich. Fine. We're not. But we're still his kids, and this is still his boat, and I spent five years pretending otherwise because it hurt too much to be his anything." He wiped his nose with the back of his hand, inelegant, unashamed. "I'm done pretending."

"Marguerite Almeida," Tamsin said, testing it. The boat had been their mother's name; the family had been their father's. Both, painted on the transom, the way it should always have been. "It'll need repainting anyway. The M's been peeling since spring."

"I'll do it. If you'll teach me the channel past Widow's Rock. Properly. Not the way you taught me when I was twelve and you were showing off."

"I was fourteen and I was magnificent."

"You grounded Dad's skiff."

"And he never let me forget it, which is why I learned it properly, which is why we're alive, which is my point." She stuck out her hand, absurdly formal, and he shook it, and then neither of them let go for a second longer than a handshake requires.

A throat cleared theatrically from the bow.

Odile stood at the rail with her bedroll over one shoulder and a contract — her contract, drawn up in pencil on the back of a mule-train invoice — held out like a summons. Behind her on the dock, her mules stood looking deeply relieved to be unemployed.

"I heard the word *crew*," she said. "I am here to negotiate. My terms are as follows: room, board, and you stop calling me *the guide*. I have a name. It is Odile. It is a good name. Also I cannot swim, which on a riverboat is a character flaw we will address through denial."

"Those are outrageous terms," Tamsin said.

"They are the terms."

"Done."

Odile came aboard the way she did everything, decisively and slightly too fast, and immediately began inspecting the rigging with the critical eye of a woman who had crossed mountains and was not impressed by rope work. Rafe went to help her, arguing cheerfully about knots within the minute, and Tamsin turned back to the stern.

The fire had burned down to a soft orange glow in the ash pan. The last of the ledger was gone. She scooped the pan up and tipped it over the side, and the wind took the final grey breath of it out across the water, downstream, toward the sea, toward the cave, toward everywhere her father had ever wanted to go and never quite managed.

"Rest now," she said quietly. "We've got the river."

The Marguerite swung gently on her anchor as the tide peaked and began to turn. Downstream, the channel waited — Widow's Rock, the shallows where they'd grounded the syndicate launch, the long green run home. Upstream, snow was closing the pass over everything they'd survived, sealing it away like the cave sealed its secrets, until spring, until someday, until it was only a story they told each other in the wheelhouse on long nights.

Rafe appeared beside her at the rail. "So," he said. "Captain. Where to?"

Tamsin looked at the water, and the water looked back, patient and silver and full of the afternoon.

"Home," she said, and weighed anchor.
