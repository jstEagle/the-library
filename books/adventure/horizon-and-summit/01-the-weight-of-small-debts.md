## 1. The Weight of Small Debts

The tide went out at Greywater like a man clearing his throat before saying something unpleasant, and Jonah Vane was on the flats before it finished.

She worked the mudline where the pilings of the old fish cannery leaned into the channel, boots sinking to the ankle in silt the color of old bruises. The cannery had burned forty years ago and the fire had done her a favor: copper pipe, brass fittings, a whole skeleton of salvageable metal slowly giving itself up to the sea. Most salvagers worked the easy stuff near shore. Jonah worked the stuff that required knowing exactly how long she could hold her breath and exactly how much a bent section of two-inch copper would bring from Ferro's yard on the north side of town. She knew both numbers to the decimal.

Four minutes, give or take. Eleven coppers a pound, less if Ferro was drinking.

She came up with an armful of pipe, spat brackish water, and checked the sky out of habit. Grey. Grey with a chance of greyer. Somewhere beyond that ceiling of cloud, out past the harbor bar and the shoals and the drowned country nobody mapped anymore, the Meridian lay on the bottom with everything it had ever carried. But that was a story for people with ships, and Jonah had a skiff with a patched hull and a sail she'd sewn herself using thread meant for leather.

One thing at a time. That was the whole discipline of staying alive when you were nobody: one thing at a time, and never let anyone learn your name twice.

* * *

Ferro's scrapyard smelled of rust and burnt coffee, and Ferro himself sat behind his scale like a toad on a stone, reading a shipping notice he'd already read three times.

"Eleven," Jonah said, dumping the copper on the scale pan.

"Nine."

"It's clean. Stripped it myself."

"Market's soft."

"The market for copper is never soft, Ferro. You melt it down and it doesn't care what year it is." She kept her voice flat, bored, the voice of someone who could walk away. Walking away was a bluff she'd never once been able to afford, but Ferro didn't know that, and the entire economy of Greywater ran on what people didn't know.

He sucked his teeth and paid her eleven. Sixty-two coppers total, which bought bread, lamp oil, and nothing else. Her rent on the room above the chandlery was four days past due, and Mrs. Halloway had started leaving notes under the door instead of knocking, which was how kindness announced its exit in a town like this.

"You hear about the fleet?" Ferro said as she counted coins into her palm. He said it casually, but his eyes did something careful. "Meridian Salvage Company. Their buyers hit town yesterday. Paying cash for charts. Any charts. Old ones, wrong ones, they don't care. Buying them up and burning them in a barrel on the dock."

Jonah kept counting. "Why would anyone pay for charts just to burn them?"

"Why does a rich man do anything? Because he can." Ferro shrugged, but he was watching her now, and she felt the watchfulness like weather. "They're asking questions too. Who works the flats. Who dives. Whether anybody's found anything unusual lately."

"I find bottle glass and dead men's pipes," Jonah said. "Unusual enough?"

"For them, maybe." He slid the coins across. "Word of advice, Vane. Whatever you've got stashed, don't be stashing it this week."

"I've got nothing stashed. I've got sixty-two coppers and a bad attitude."

"Then you're richer than most." But he laughed without any humor in it, and she left with the feeling of being weighed behind her, like cargo.

* * *

The fleet's enforcers wore grey coats and moved through Greywater in pairs, which was one pair more than the town's constable could afford. Jonah counted three of them by midday, going shop to shop along the waterfront with a ledger book and a smile that had been manufactured somewhere far away and shipped here in crates.

She watched them from the loft window above the chandlery while she ate half the loaf and saved the rest. Below, they'd set up their burning barrel right on the dock, just as Ferro said, and a small crowd had gathered the way crowds gather around any fire, hungry for warmth they weren't going to get. Old charts went in. Paper curled black. A clerk in a good coat recorded each purchase in his ledger, and the enforcers paid in silver, real silver, for things worth nothing except to someone looking for a specific stretch of coast.

That was the part that sat wrong in Jonah's chest, the way a swallowed fishbone sits wrong. Nobody burned worthless paper. You burned paper when it pointed somewhere you didn't want pointed.

She thought about the chart rolled in oilcloth under her floorboard. Not a treasure chart—she wasn't a fool, whatever Ferro thought—but a working survey of the outer shoals she'd traded a winter of diving for, back when she still believed a winter of diving was worth something. It covered water within a day's sail of the Meridian's last known position. If the fleet was buying every chart on the coast, hers was worth silver to them and freedom to her, and only one of those prices came with men in grey coats learning your face.

Stay invisible. That was the rule she'd built her whole life inside, brick by brick, after her father's name became the kind of name that got doors closed and ledgers audited. Vane. Even now, ten years on, the name had a smell to it in certain rooms. Disgraced archaeologist, they said, as if disgrace were a substance you could dig up. He'd chased something out past the shoals too, near the end. He'd come back with nothing and died with less, and Jonah had spent her whole adult life making sure no one connected the girl on the mudflats to the man in the scandal sheets.

It was exhausting, being no one. It took constant work, like bailing a boat. You could never stop, never rest, never once stand up straight and say *here I am*, because the moment you were visible you were countable, and the moment you were countable you were owed or owing, and debts were how people like her got ground down into the mud she made her living from.

A knock at the chandlery door below. Then voices. Then Mrs. Halloway's voice going thin and polite, the way it went when she was frightened and pretending to be helpful.

Jonah went very still on her cot, listening.

"—just routine," one of the grey coats was saying. "We're compensating holders of coastal surveys. Fair market rates. We understand there's a young woman rents the loft. Dives the flats. We'd like a word."

"She keeps odd hours," Mrs. Halloway said. "I couldn't say when she's in."

Odd hours. God bless the woman; she'd have her back rent forgiven if Jonah lived through the week.

Footsteps on the stairs anyway. They always climbed anyway; politeness was just reconnaissance with better manners. Jonah rolled the chart tighter into its oilcloth, dropped it into her dive bag with the knife and the wax-sealed matches, and went out the window onto the chandlery roof, moving quiet as gull-shadow, because the roof knew her weight by now and forgave her for it.

From the ridge tile she watched the street. Two grey coats below, waiting. One on the corner by the net-mender's, pretending to read a bill of lading. Three. They'd posted three on a woman who salvaged bottle glass.

Her heart was doing arithmetic it didn't like the answer to.

* * *

She went out over the backs of the buildings, down through the tannery yard where the smell covered all sins, and came out on the mudflats at low water with the light dying orange behind the harbor bar. The skiff was pulled up among the salt-killed reeds where she'd left it, hull sound, sail dry. She could be gone on the morning tide. North, south, anywhere the fleet's ledger didn't reach. Start over in some other grey town with another false first name and another rented loft, bail the boat again, stay afloat again, stay no one again.

Sixty-two coppers wouldn't cover the crossing. Fifty-five after food. The math was a wall she kept walking into.

That was when she saw the man lying among the reeds.

She almost stepped over him—a bundle of wet wool, that's what he looked like, the tide's leavings. But bundles of wool don't breathe in ragged pulls, and they don't open their eyes when your boot scuffs close, and they don't say, in a voice scraped down to the boards:

"Vane. You're a Vane."

Every instinct she owned told her to keep walking. Instinct had kept her alive ten years. But the man's hand came out of the reeds and caught her ankle with the grip of a drowning sailor, and in his other hand, held up between two fingers like a card sharp showing his deck, was a folded square of paper gone soft and brown at the creases.

Hand-drawn. She could see that even in the failing light. Ink lines, depth marks, a coastline she almost recognized.

"I've been looking for you three days," the man said, and coughed, and the cough sounded like the end of something. "I'm dying, girl, so do me the courtesy of hearing the price before you run."

Jonah stood in the cold with the fleet's fires burning on the dock behind her and the tide starting to turn, and looked down at the map, and understood—with the particular dread of someone whose luck has finally stopped being merely bad—that she was already caught.

"My name's Crane," the man whispered. "Elias Crane. And what I'm selling isn't the map."
