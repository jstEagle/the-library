## 2. A Map for a Promise

The man was dying in Jonah's doorway when she came back from the breakwater with a coil of copper wire and half a loaf of black bread.

She saw him first as a shape slumped against the frame of her shack, one shoulder propped on the doorpost like he'd been holding it up for hours and had finally run out of the strength to pretend otherwise. Greywater's fog did that—turned people into suggestions of themselves. But this shape was breathing wrong. Wet, rattling breaths, each one a negotiation.

"I'm looking," the man said, "for the Vane girl."

Jonah stopped walking. The copper wire went still in her hands.

There were maybe forty people in Greywater who knew her name, and most of them only knew it because they'd tried to cheat her at scrap-weight and lost. Nobody came looking for the Vane girl. That was the entire architecture of her life: nobody came looking.

"You've got the wrong shack," she said.

"No." He coughed, and the cough went on too long, and when it ended there was blood on his knuckles where he'd pressed them to his mouth. "No, I don't. Your father had your mother's jaw. I sailed with him once, off the Sable Reach. You have it too."

The bread was getting cold under her arm. She stood in the mud of her own front path and felt something old and ugly turn over in her chest—the place where her father lived, the sealed room she never opened. *I sailed with him once.* Everyone who said that wanted something. It had been true when she was nine and it was true now.

"Whatever he owes you," she said, "I don't carry his debts."

"It's not that kind of visit." He tried to stand straighter and failed, sliding down the doorpost until he sat in the mud, legs out, like a man who had simply decided the ground was as good a place as any to conduct business. Up close, in the thin grey light, she could read him properly: sixty-some years carved down by salt and hard weather, hands like braided rope, a prospector's canvas coat gone soft as cloth from decades of rain. His eyes were the worst part. They were clear. Whatever was killing him hadn't touched the part of him that thought.

"Then what kind is it?"

"The kind you'll want to hear sitting down."

"I'm fine standing."

He almost smiled. "So was I, once."

* * *

She gave him water because refusing water to a dying man was the kind of thing that followed you. She gave him the bread because he looked like he hadn't eaten in two days, and he ate it slowly, deliberately, the way people eat when they're rationing their last meals without admitting it. Then he sat in her single chair with his coat dripping onto her floorboards and introduced himself as Elias Crane, and waited, with a patience she found deeply irritating, for her to give her name back.

"Jonah," she said finally. Just that. Let him make of it what he liked.

"Jonah Vane." He said the whole thing anyway, and she hated how it sounded in someone else's mouth—like a fact, like a thing that existed in records somewhere. "I need you to take something to Corvess."

"I don't go to Corvess."

"I know. That's why I walked three days past two other towns to find you."

"Why me? You don't know me."

"I know enough." He set the water cup down with exaggerated care, as if his hands might betray him if he rushed. "I know you salvage. I know you're good at it, because I watched you work the breakwater wreck last spring from the ridge, and you found the copper line everyone else missed for six years. I know the fleet's enforcers have been through Greywater twice this month buying charts, and I know they didn't buy yours, which means either you don't have one worth selling or you're smart enough not to sell it. And I know—" he paused, and the pause cost him; another wet rattle of breath— "that a Vane would understand what it means when somebody buries the truth underwater and calls it salvage."

Jonah put her back against the wall. It was an old habit, keeping the door in view.

"What truth?"

"That's the trade," Crane said. "Not the answer. The errand."

He reached inside his coat, moving slow, telegraphing every motion so she wouldn't spook, and drew out an oilskin packet the size of a prayer book. It was tied with waxed cord, knotted twice. Even before he opened it she knew what it was. She'd grown up around charts the way other children grew up around lullabies. The oilskin, the double knot, the smell of tallow and salt that rose off it when he loosened the cord—that was a working map, kept close, kept secret, kept alive.

He spread it on his knee. Hand-drawn, dense with soundings and bearings in a cramped, certain hand. Depth marks ran down the page in columns. A coastline she half-recognized curved along the top edge, and off it, maybe nine miles, marked with an X that wasn't an X at all but a small careful circle, was a notation: *Meridian.*

Her mouth went dry.

Everyone on this coast knew the name. The Meridian was the fleet's founding legend—the great salvage ship lost with all hands thirty-odd years ago, the disaster that had made the owners rich twice over, once in insurance and once in the salvage rights that let them strip every wreck on the coast without competition. There were songs about it in the taverns. There was a monument in Corvess harbor. The fleet's captains wore a meridian line stitched into their collars like a holy symbol.

And here it was on a dead man's map, circled, with soundings beside it that no chart-seller in Greywater had been willing to part with.

"This is a hanging offense to own," she said quietly. "Kessler's people are buying up every sheet of paper on the coast. If they find out you have this—"

"They won't find out. Because soon there'll be nothing left to find." He tapped his own chest, below the collarbone, where the cough had been living. "It's in my lungs. Doctor in Harrow's Bend gave me a month if I'm careful, and I have never once in my life been careful. So I'm spending what's left on this."

"On what? What's at the Meridian?"

"The truth." He said it flatly, without drama, which somehow made it worse. "Thirty-four years ago the Meridian didn't sink. She was sunk. Her crew didn't drown—they were drowned. And the men who did it built a fleet on top of the grave, and they've spent three decades buying up every chart, every logbook, every witness, and feeding them to the sea. I found proof. Years ago. I've been sitting on it because I was a coward and a coward with a fortune doesn't rock boats." His voice cracked on the last word, and he covered it with a cough, and when he surfaced again his eyes were wet and furious at being wet. "I'm done being careful. But I can't deliver it myself, and I can't trust anyone the fleet can buy, and I can't trust anyone who wants the fortune more than they want it finished."

"So you want a stranger."

"I want someone invisible," Crane said. "That's a compliment, girl, whether you hear it as one or not. The fleet owns every loud person on this coast. What they don't own is the ones nobody sees."

Jonah looked at the map. The little circle. Nine miles of open water between it and everything safe.

"What exactly am I delivering?" she asked. "To whom?"

"My daughter. Maren Crane. She lives in Corvess, on Tallow Street, above the chandler's shop. She thinks I died hating her, and I let her think it because I was ashamed, and that shame is the second thing I'm shipping in this packet." He folded the oilskin closed over his knuckles, gripping it like a rail. "You bring her the map and what comes out of that wreck, and you tell her—I'll write it down—you tell her exactly what I say, word for word, even if it makes me look like what I was."

"And what comes out of the wreck is...?"

"Proof. Documents. What kind, I'll not say, because saying it aloud makes it real, and real things get people killed before they're ready." He met her eyes. "You'll know it when you hold it. Anyone would."

Outside, the fog was thickening toward evening. Somewhere down the lane a dog barked twice and quit. Jonah became aware, distantly, of how quiet her shack was, how empty, how perfectly suited to a life in which nobody asked anything of anybody. One bad week from ruin, yes—but ruin was simple. Ruin was just hunger with better posture. This—this was a man handing her a live coal and calling it an inheritance.

"I want payment," she said. "For the passage, the diving, the risk. Half of whatever the proof sells for."

Crane shook his head, slow, unsurprised. "It doesn't sell. That's the whole point of it. It goes to Maren, and Maren takes it where it does the most damage. That's the errand. All of it or none of it."

"Then find someone else."

"There is no one else." For the first time his voice lifted, and underneath the gentleness there was iron. "I've got a month, maybe less, and Kessler's ships are already sweeping the coast, and every day I wait is a day closer to this map going into the sea with me. You're not my first choice, Jonah Vane. You're my last one. Last choices are usually the right ones."

She turned away from him and looked out the window at nothing. The tide was coming in over the flats, filling the channels she'd worked that morning, covering her tracks the way it always did, the way she liked. Invisible. Safe. One bad week from ruin and free of every debt except the ones she chose.

*Sworn word.* Her father had used that phrase. Sworn word, given on open water, binding as chain. She'd sworn one herself, once, at fourteen, to a boy who taught her to dive—and she'd kept it six years after he stopped deserving it, because that was what a sworn word was. It didn't care what you learned later. It held.

"If I say yes," she said, without turning around, "it's a real oath. Not a favor. Not a kindness. If I swear it, I do it, even if it costs me everything I've spent ten years building, which is nothing, which is the joke, but it's mine."

"I'd expect nothing less from a Vane."

"Don't flatter me. Flattery makes me suspicious."

"Good," said Elias Crane. "Stay suspicious. Stay suspicious all the way to Corvess."

She made him write the message to Maren that night while she cooked the rest of the bread into something hot, mostly so her hands would have work and wouldn't shake where he could see them. He wrote in the same cramped certain hand as the map, four lines, folding it into the oilskin with the chart. When he finished he looked smaller, emptied, like the packet had been holding him upright and now it was hers.

"Say it," he told her. "All of it. I want to hear the words."

She said them. *I, Jonah Vane, swear to carry what you give me to Maren Crane of Corvess, and to tell her the truth of it, whatever it is, whatever it costs.* The shack was very quiet after. The tide breathed against the pilings outside.

Crane slept in her chair because he couldn't walk further, and sometime before dawn the wet rattle in his chest slowed, and slowed, and stopped, and Jonah sat across from him in the dark with the oilskin packet in her lap and understood, with a clarity that felt almost like drowning, exactly what she'd done.

She'd given her word too easily. Again. To a stranger. For a truth no one would name, to a woman she'd never met, against the most dangerous fleet on the coast—and the only man who could explain any of it was cooling in her chair with his secrets folded shut in waxed cord.

She unwrapped the packet one more time, by candle stub, telling herself she was checking the map for damage.

At the bottom corner of the chart, in handwriting suddenly hurried, someone had added a note she'd missed in the dark of the afternoon:

*Six tides. After the sixth, the air pocket floods until spring. Move fast, and trust no one flying the meridian line.*

Six tides. She counted back through the almanac in her head, lips moving, and felt the floor tilt under her.

She had five and a half days to learn to be visible.
