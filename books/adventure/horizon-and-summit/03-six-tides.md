## 3. Six Tides

The map was drawn on sailcloth, the kind of thing a man carries against his chest for thirty years until it wears soft as skin. Jonah spread it across her worktable in the loft above the chandlery, weighted the corners with four brass fittings she'd pulled off a drowned dinghy, and told herself to breathe.

Elias Crane had died at dawn, coughing blood into a rag he'd tried to hide, and his last act on this earth had been to grip her wrist hard enough to leave marks and say, *Six tides. You count them from tonight's low. After that it's gone till spring.*

She'd promised. That was the problem with promises — they were easy to make when a man was dying and hard to live inside afterward. She'd said yes because saying no to a dying man felt like a kind of murder, and now she owned a map to a wreck everyone on this coast had heard of and no one had ever found, and a debt to a daughter in Corvess whose face she couldn't picture.

*The truth of what they buried there.* He hadn't said what. He'd run out of time before he ran out of words.

Jonah bent over the map and worked.

Crane had been a prospector, not a cartographer, which meant the map was honest but ugly. The coastline was recognizable if you tilted your head — Greywater's hook of breakwater, then south along the shingle coast to a place marked only with three short strokes and the word *TEETH*. A reef. She knew it; every salvager knew it. The Teeth ate hulls and gave back nothing but splinters, and the charts warned off it in language usually reserved for plague ports.

Beyond the Teeth, Crane had drawn a cross and beneath it, in a hand gone shaky: *Meridian. 14 fathoms. Air pocket — forward hold, below the waterline, sealed bulkhead. Floods with the spring neap. Six tides.*

Fourteen fathoms. Eighty-four feet. Deep enough that free-diving it was suicide, deep enough that you needed pumped air or a diving bell or a death wish. Her father had used all three. The thought arrived uninvited, the way thoughts of him always did, and she shoved it back down where it lived.

The air pocket was the prize. A sealed compartment on a wreck that old meant whatever was inside might still be dry. Dry paper. Dry ledgers. Dry *truth*, whatever that meant. And six tides before the spring neap pushed the sea through whatever seam Crane had found and drowned it for the season.

She counted on her fingers like a girl doing sums. Tonight's low tide was tide one. Then tomorrow's pair, then the next day's pair, then the final high water on the third day. Six tides. Roughly sixty hours. She looked out the loft's salt-fogged window at Greywater harbor, at the masts swaying against a sky the color of old pewter, and did the other arithmetic — the one that mattered.

She didn't own a boat.

* * *

She'd known about Kessler before Elias Crane died. Everyone in Greywater knew about Kessler; you'd have to be deaf and drunk not to. For two weeks the salvage fleet's tenders had been working the waterfront, buying charts. Not hiring divers, not leasing salvage rights — *buying charts*, every coastal survey, every fisherman's private depth-notes, every scrap of inked vellum that showed the shingle coast south of Greywater. Paying triple what they were worth and burning what they bought in a brazier on the tender's stern, right there in front of the sellers, so there could be no mistake about what happened to information once the fleet owned it.

Jonah had sold them two of her own charts the week before Crane found her. It had hurt to do it — those were good charts, earned over years of cold, patient work — but hurting was cheaper than being noticed, and being noticed by the fleet was how people stopped turning up. She'd watched the chart-seller feed her coastline into the flames and thought: *that's fine, I have it memorized, that's the point of me, everything important lives in my head where nobody can buy it.*

Now the most important thing she'd ever carried was inked on sailcloth in a dead man's hand, and the fleet was buying every map on the coast, and those two facts were not strangers to each other.

She sat down and made herself think it through, because panic was a luxury and luxuries got you killed.

Why would a salvage fleet burn charts? Fleets wanted wrecks. Wrecks were money. You didn't destroy information about possible wrecks unless you were afraid of someone else reaching one first — or unless you were afraid of what was *on* one. And Crane hadn't said treasure. He'd said *truth*. Truth wasn't something you burned charts to protect. Truth was something you burned charts to bury.

And buried things, in Jonah's experience, had a way of having her father's fingerprints on them.

She hated herself for the thought even as she followed it. Aldous Vane had spent the last decade of his life chasing exactly this kind of story — massacres dressed up as maritime accidents, cargoes that existed only on paper, crews written off as lost with weather when the weather had nothing to do with it. He'd ruined himself on those stories, ruined their name, ruined *her*, and died somewhere far from home with his notes scattered to whoever would carry them. If the Meridian held something worth a fleet-wide cover-up, then somewhere in the pattern, cold and patient as a current, her father would be waiting.

She wasn't going. That was the first conclusion, and it lasted about an hour.

Because here was the rest of the arithmetic: Crane had come to *her*. Out of every scavenger, smuggler, and diver on the Greywater waterfront, the dying man had picked the daughter of Aldous Vane, and that meant either he'd known who she was — in which case others might know too — or he hadn't, in which case the promise was hers alone and no one was coming for it. Both possibilities ended the same way. Either she was already visible, or she was about to make herself so, and the comfortable middle ground she'd built her whole life on — unseen, unclaimed, unbothered — was flooding out from under her like a tide she couldn't hold back.

Six tides. She had six tides to stay invisible, and then the sea itself would take the choice away.

* * *

On the second morning she went down to the harbor to test the wind, and saw the fleet's flagship come around the breakwater.

She knew the ship from gossip if not from sight: the *Corvess Star*, black-hulled, three-masted, rigged for hauling salvage gear instead of cargo. It took the harbor mouth with the arrogant ease of a thing that had never been refused anywhere, and dropped anchor in the deep channel like it owned the mud under it. A longboat came ashore within the hour. Men in fleet gray moved through the market with a list, asking questions — she watched them from behind a stack of crab pots, watched a fisherman shake his head, watched another one think about it too long before shaking his head.

They were looking for someone. She couldn't hear the questions from the crab pots, but she could read the shape of them in the fishermen's faces, and the shape was: *a woman, alone, recently came into something.*

Elias Crane had died in a rooming house with thin walls. Dying men talked. Doctors were paid. Landlords were paid better. Jonah stood among the crab pots with her heart going like a hammer on a stuck door and understood that the window wasn't six tides anymore. The window was however long it took a landlord's memory to turn into coin.

Fear came first — a cold flood of it, the animal kind, the kind that said *run, hide, go to ground, be small.* She let it move through her and kept standing there, because underneath the fear, arriving late but arriving solid, was something else. Something with edges.

Resolve, maybe. Or spite. With her it had never mattered much which.

They wanted her invisible? Fine. Invisible was a skill. Invisible people could walk past fleet enforcers with a crate on their shoulder and a bored expression. Invisible people could take a boat in broad daylight, because nobody robbed a skiff in front of witnesses and nobody remembered a woman carrying rope.

She went to work.

* * *

The skiff belonged to Deacon Hale, who was not a friend but was the closest thing the word allowed in Greywater: a fellow scavenger who'd split finds with her twice and owed her once. His skiff, the *Petrel*, was moored in the outer basin, fourteen feet of patched cedar with a stubby mast and a centerboard that jammed if you swore at it wrong. Deacon was ashore drinking off a bad month, which was most months.

Stealing from Deacon felt different from stealing from anyone else, so she didn't steal. She left payment — every coin she had, wrapped in oilcloth and tucked under the thwart, along with a note in her careful, unremarkable hand: *Borrowing the Petrel. Back in five days or don't expect her. I'm sorry. J.V.*

Then she signed it, and stared at the letters a moment, because she hadn't signed anything in years. Initials weren't a name. Initials were a compromise between existing and not. She folded the note away under the thwart where he'd find it too late to stop her, and told herself the full name could wait until she had something worth putting it to.

Loading took an hour and cost her everything else. Pump, air hose, the old surface-supply helmet she'd rebuilt piece by piece over three winters — a relic design, heavy as sin, but it didn't need a support ship or a bell or a second diver, just a hand-cranked air pump and someone topside turning it. Rope. Lead weights. Two days of food, a lantern, a knife, the strongbox tools she'd never expected to use again. The map went against her chest in an oilskin wrap, where Crane had carried it, where it seemed to want to be.

She cast off from the outer basin at slack water, in the flat gray hour when the fishing fleet was ashore and the harbor watch was eating. The *Corvess Star* sat fat and black in the channel, and Jonah brought the *Petrel* out along the northern arm, close under the breakwater stones, where the flagship's boats couldn't see without coming around. Nobody shouted. Nobody turned. The world performed its favorite trick and declined to notice her, and for once she was grateful for it.

Past the breakwater the open water took the little skiff into its long gray hands, and Greywater shrank behind her to a smudge of smoke and masts. Somewhere back there, men with a list were asking about a woman who'd come into something. Ahead of her lay the Teeth, and fourteen fathoms of dark water, and a sealed room that had kept its secret through forty years of tides.

Six tides, Crane had said. She'd already spent one getting ready. Five left, and the sea didn't negotiate.

Jonah Vane put the tiller over, trimmed the little sail, and ran south along the shingle coast with the wind freshening at her back — toward the reef, toward the wreck, toward whatever truth was worth killing a coastline of maps to keep drowned. Behind her, far astern and closing, a black sail rounded the breakwater and turned south.

The *Corvess Star* had weighed anchor.
