## 4. The Man Who Sold Charts

The flagship *Vigil* rode at anchor off Greywater like a knife laid flat on the water, and Ada Kessler stood at her stern rail watching the port town burn its lamps against the dusk. She had been watching for three days. Not the town—she had no interest in the town—but the small boats that came and went from it, the salvage skiffs, the crab trawlers, the lean gray shapes of men who made their living picking over other people's drownings.

Somewhere in that sprawl of rotting piers and salt-bleached houses was a map.

She turned from the rail when she heard the boots behind her. Her first mate, Dree Halloway, moved the way Kessler liked her people to move: without wasted motion, without noise, without questions until they were asked for.

"The chart-seller's aboard," Dree said. "The one from the Anchorline stall."

"Name?"

"Ovid Marsh. He's nervous."

"They're all nervous." Kessler pulled on her coat. It was good wool, dark blue, cut close—the only extravagance she permitted herself, because a captain who looked poor invited pirates, and a captain who looked rich invited thieves, and she had learned long ago that the trick was to look like neither. She looked like a woman who kept accounts. That was what she wanted them to see. "Bring him to the chart room. And Dree—no one else in the room. I'll call if I need you."

Dree hesitated a half-second. "You're sure?"

"I'm sure."

The chart room sat amidships, below the waterline, windowless by design. Kessler had ordered the portholes sealed her second week in command, and when the owners' man had asked why, she'd told him it reduced glare on the tables. The truth was simpler and she kept it folded away with everything else she didn't look at directly: she did not like seeing the sea while she worked. The sea out there held things. She preferred to work over paper, where everything drowned was flat and named and could be pinned down with a brass weight.

Ovid Marsh was already sweating when she entered. He was a narrow man, sixty or thereabouts, with ink-stained fingers and the particular stoop of someone who had spent thirty years bent over copying tables. He'd set up his stall at the Anchorline market every season for as long as she could remember, selling coastal charts of varying honesty, and he had never once been worth her attention before this week.

"Captain Kessler." He said it like a man greeting a judge.

"Sit down, Ovid." She took the chair across the table from him rather than standing over him. Standing over people made them stupid; she needed him precise. On the table between them she laid three coins—a silver mark, a copper bit, and a gold sovereign—and left them there, untouched, like an open question. "I'm going to ask you some things, and you're going to answer them exactly, because I already know most of the answers and I'm only here to find out where my information is wrong."

"I haven't done anything—"

"You've sold charts your whole life. Some honest, some not. This isn't about that." She let the silence do a little work. Ovid's eyes flicked to the gold coin and back up. Good. Greed was reliable. Fear was too, but greed lasted longer. "Three days ago I bought every coastal chart in Greywater. Every one. Forty-one charts, four hundred marks, and my buyers were told to purchase quietly and pay well. Do you know why?"

He shook his head.

"Because two days after we started buying, someone came to your stall asking after deep-water coordinates. East of the Serrat shoals. Past the shipping lanes." She watched his face. "Tell me what you told them."

Ovid's hands flattened on the table. "Nothing. I told them nothing, because I don't—I don't sell that far out. Nobody does. There's nothing out there but the Meridian, and everyone knows the Meridian's picked clean, and anyway the fleet has exclusive claim, so even if I had—"

"Even if you had what?"

His throat worked. "A copy. Of a chart. Someone brought me one, years back, asked me to verify the soundings. I kept the copy. Habit. Verifiers keep copies, it's—it's professional practice—"

"Who brought it?"

"A prospector." Ovid's voice dropped, as though the name itself might be dangerous. "Elias Crane."

Kessler felt something cold settle behind her sternum, and she noted it, catalogued it, put it aside. Crane. The name had surfaced twice in the last month—once in a tavern rumor out of Corvess, once in a report from a fleet tender that Crane had been seen asking after old crew manifests. A dying man asking after dead men. She had sent word to have him found. Apparently she'd been slow.

"And the copy you kept," she said. "Where is it?"

"Gone." Ovid said it fast, the way a man says the thing he's been dreading saying all day. "Stolen. Two nights ago. My stall was—" He stopped himself, recalculated, and Kessler saw the recalculation happen, saw him decide that lying to her was more dangerous than the truth. "It wasn't broken into. Nothing else was taken. Just the copy, and just after someone had come round asking me about east-of-Serrat soundings. A young woman. Local salvager. Goes by—"

"Go on."

"Vane." The word landed in the chart room and seemed to echo off the sealed walls. "Jonah Vane."

Kessler sat very still. There was a discipline to stillness; she had learned it young, in rooms much worse than this one, and it had saved her life more times than she could count. Stillness now bought her three seconds to feel the whole shape of the thing.

*A Vane.*

Of course. Of course it would be a Vane. The sea had a sense of humor and it was cruel, and somewhere beneath forty fathoms of black water, Aldous Vane was laughing at her.

She became aware that Ovid was still talking, filling the silence the way frightened men did, offering her everything now because he understood, finally, what kind of attention he'd attracted. "—the daughter, I think. The archaeologist's girl. She grew up around here, before—well. Before. She salvages scrap mostly, off the breakwater, keeps to herself. If she's got Crane's chart, then Crane gave it to her, which means Crane trusted her, which means—"

"Which means nothing yet." Kessler stood. She pushed the silver mark across the table to him. "For your time. You'll stay aboard tonight. Tomorrow you'll sail with us as far as the Serrat light, and you'll earn the copper by telling my navigator everything you remember about those soundings. Every number. Every correction Crane ever asked you to verify."

"And the gold?"

She paused at the door. "The gold is for the day you tell me something true that I don't already know. Most men never collect it."

She left him with the coins and climbed two decks into the evening air, where Dree was waiting with the particular patience of a woman who had learned not to ask questions on deck.

"Set a watch on the breakwater district," Kessler said. "Every pier, every skiff slip. If Jonah Vane puts to sea, I want to know before her anchor's wet. And send a tender to the Serrat light station—if she's smart she'll run the shoals at night, and the light keeper sees everything."

"And if she's already gone?"

Kessler looked south, past the harbor mouth, to where the water went from green to gray to that deep unlit blue that sailors didn't name because naming it felt like an invitation. Six tides, Crane's old notes had said, before the wreck's air pocket flooded for the season. She had read those notes eleven years ago, in a room in Corvess, sitting across from men who owned half the coast, and she had watched them decide what to do about it, and she had said yes, and she had been saying yes ever since, one order at a time, each one smaller than the last and each one landing heavier.

"She's not gone," Kessler said. "Not yet. She's one bad week from ruin—that's what the harbor records say, and harbor records don't lie about money. A woman like that doesn't run for open water on hope. She runs when she's certain. Give her a day to be certain."

Dree nodded and moved off, and Kessler stayed at the rail a moment longer.

Below her feet, in the hold, the *Vigil* carried diving gear for forty men, dredging chains, a powder magazine, and a strongbox welded shut under her own seal that she had never once been permitted to open. The owners called it contingency cargo. Eleven years ago she had believed there was such a thing as contingency cargo. Now she knew it was just a word for the part of a job you agreed not to think about.

The Meridian hadn't sunk. That was the thing no chart recorded, the thing the fleet's claim papers danced around in careful legal language, the thing she had been sent out here to make sure stayed unrecorded. Ships sank. The *Meridian* had been *ended*—holed below her own waterline by men who boarded her at night with the crew bound in the hold, and the men who paid for it had names that appeared on harbor trusts and shipping boards and charity galas in Corvess, and one of those names had signed Kessler's commission, and she had taken the signature because the alternative had been explained to her very clearly, and she had been younger then, and afraid, and the fear had calcified into competence, and the competence had become a career.

And now a prospector was dead, and a thief's daughter had a map, and somewhere out on that gray water the past was floating toward the surface whether Kessler caught it or not.

She thought about Jonah Vane—what little there was to think. A scrap salvager. Invisible by trade. Kessler had been invisible once too; that was the joke of it, the ugly private joke she carried like ballast. The difference was that Kessler had clawed her way up by making herself useful to powerful men, and invisibility had curdled into complicity, and now here she was, hunting a stranger across open water to bury a massacre, and calling it salvage.

*Find the chart,* the owners had said. *Recover any documents. Handle the bearer discreetly.* Discreetly. Eleven years ago that word had meant something specific and she had pretended otherwise, and there were two graves on the Serrat coast that she visited in her sleep and never once awake.

She would not add a third. Whatever else she was, whatever rot had grown through her, she had kept that line drawn for eleven years and she meant to keep it. Find the woman. Take the papers. Send her home alive and ruined and silent. That was the plan. That was always the plan.

But plans assumed the other person behaved like a person, and Jonah Vane had grown up on Aldous Vane's stories, and Aldous Vane had never once in his life behaved like anything except a man determined to drown himself in the truth.

Kessler went below, lit the lamp over her own charts, and drew a line from Greywater to the Serrat shoals with a steady hand. Then she sat looking at it, listening to the hull creak around her like a held breath.

Six tides. The clock was running, and for the first time in eleven years, Ada Kessler was afraid of what she might find when it stopped.

* * *

The tender reported back near midnight.

"Not at her lodgings," the young officer said, dripping rainwater onto Kessler's chart table. "Landlady says she settled her debt three days ago—paid it all, sudden-like—and hasn't been seen since yesterday morning. But the harbormaster's log shows a skiff signed out. Stolen, likely. Small craft, late tide."

Kessler studied the log entry. The departure time was circled in the harbormaster's careful hand, and beside it, in the margin, someone had written a single word in pencil: *east.*

She felt the cold thing behind her sternum shift, unfold, take weight.

East. Past the lanes. To the Serrat shoals, where the water was forty fathoms down and black as a closed eye, and where a wreck that was not a wreck waited with its dead and its documents and its one sealed compartment that Kessler had spent eleven years pretending not to dream about.

"Signal the engine room," she said. "Weigh anchor. All ships make way for Serrat."

The officer hesitated. "In this weather, Captain? The barometer's falling fast. Storm by morning, maybe worse."

Kessler rolled up the chart. Outside, the first wind of it was already worrying the rigging, plucking at the halyards like fingers testing knots.

"Yes," she said. "Worse. Get us underway. We're racing a tide and a storm, and I intend to win both."

Above her, the sky over Greywater had gone the color of wet slate, and somewhere out in that gathering dark, a stolen skiff was running east with a dead man's map and a living woman's promise, straight into the teeth of everything Kessler had spent a decade keeping underwater.

She touched the rail once, lightly, the way a gambler touches a losing streak for luck, and followed her orders into the storm.
