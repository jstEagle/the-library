## 5. Stormwater

The storm came out of the west like something with a grudge.

Jonah saw it building for an hour—a bruise-colored wall stacking up over the water, swallowing the horizon whole—and she did the math the way she did all her math: fast, cold, and with no room for hope. The Meridian's coordinates lay a full day's sail northeast. The storm would arrive in half that. There was no outrunning it, only choosing what to be caught in.

She chose open water. A skiff caught against a lee shore in weather like this became kindling; at least out here she'd have sea room to run before it.

The first hour was manageable. The second was not.

Rain came sideways, hard enough to sting, and the wind found every seam in the old sail and screamed through it. Jonah hauled the tiller into her hip and let the boat run, watching the swells grow from gray hills to moving mountains, each one lifting the skiff up its face and then dropping it into the trough like something discarded. She'd been on rough water her whole life. This was different. This was the sea deciding.

Somewhere in the third hour—she'd lost track of time, lost track of everything except the next wave—the sail split.

Not gradually. One moment it was canvas, the next it was a rag flapping itself to pieces, and the skiff slewed broadside and took a wave over the gunwale that filled her boots and knocked her off the tiller. She crawled back, bailed with her cupped hands because the bucket had gone overboard, and understood, with a clarity that felt almost calm, that she was going to die out here, six tides short of the wreck, holding a dead man's map inside a waxed oilskin pouch strapped against her ribs.

*No,* she thought. *Not like this.*

She couldn't have said where the island came from. Later she'd remember it as a darker smudge in an already dark world, a shape she steered for on instinct alone, running before the wind with the broken sail dragging behind her like a wounded wing. The shoal took the keel with a grinding shriek that went up through her spine, and then the skiff was slewing, tipping, and Jonah was in the water.

It hit her like a fist. Cold enough to stop her heart if she let it, so she didn't let it—she swam, one-armed, the other hand locked on the oilskin strap, kicking toward the black shape of land while the storm tried to peel her away and add her to its inventory. Her feet found rock. She found herself crawling, then walking, then falling onto wet sand as the skiff came apart behind her with a sound like a door slamming forever.

She lay there a long time, cheek against the grit, rain hammering her back, and laughed once—a single cracked bark of a sound that the wind took and scattered.

One bad week from ruin. Now she was one bad night from nothing at all.

* * *

Dawn came gray and mean, the storm blown through but leaving wreckage of its mood behind: a churned sea still heaving, wrack strewn along the tideline, the sky scoured down to bare bone. Jonah sat against a leaning slab of granite and took inventory.

Map: dry, against her ribs. Knife: still in her belt. Everything else—food, water, the tarp, the coil of line, the little brass sextant her father had left her and she'd never once forgiven herself for keeping—gone with the skiff, which was now a scatter of planks decorating the beach like the sea's own commentary on her plans.

The island wasn't much. A quarter mile of rock and coarse grass, a stunted wind-bent pine, a tidal pool full of stranded fish she could eat raw if it came to that. It would come to that. She had maybe two days of strength in her, four tides of margin, and a wreck that wouldn't wait.

She was prying limpets off the rocks with her knife when she heard the cough.

Not a storm sound. A human sound—wet, ragged, coming from the far side of the pine. Jonah went still, knife low, and moved toward it the way she moved toward everything: quietly, ready to be disappointed or worse.

A man lay in the grass beneath the tree, half in a salvaged tarp of his own, and even before she saw the faded fleet insignia stitched on his jacket—a blue meridian line over crossed hooks—her stomach dropped.

He was older than her by fifteen years, maybe twenty, with a beard going to iron and hands that looked like they'd been rebuilt from spare parts. His eyes opened when her shadow crossed him, and he didn't reach for a weapon, which either meant he had none or he was too smart to try.

"Easy," he rasped. "If I could stand, I'd have robbed you already."

"You're fleet."

"I'm *ex*-fleet." He said it like spitting something out. "Eleven months ex. Before that, nine years in." He pushed himself up on one elbow, wincing. "Tull."

"Didn't ask."

"You were about to." He looked her over with a fisherman's frankness—not her body, her *condition*, the shivering, the salt-cracked lips. "You came off that skiff last night. I watched it break from right here. Thought you were dead until you started swearing at the tide."

"I don't swear at tides."

"No," he agreed. "You argue with them. Different thing." He nodded at the oilskin pouch, still strapped to her. "That what you saved instead of your own gear?"

"That's none of yours."

"Everything on this rock is none of mine. Doesn't stop a man from looking." He coughed again, deep and ugly, and when he caught his breath there was something new in his face—not friendliness, nothing so soft, but a kind of grim attention. "You're headed northeast. Storm drove you here, but you were pointed northeast before it. I've been watching that heading for eleven months, wondering who else knew."

Jonah's grip shifted on the knife. "Knew what?"

"The Meridian." He said the name flatly, without reverence, the way you'd name a grave. "And don't waste breath denying it. Nobody sails into open water in storm season for anything smaller than a fortune, and nobody sails *that* heading for anything smaller than the Meridian." He held up one ruined hand before she could speak. "I'm not asking what's there. I know what's there. I helped look for it for three years and never found it, and then I stopped wanting to find it, and then I got off Kessler's ship any way a man can get off—which is to say badly, at night, in a stolen dory, with a bullet through my shoulder as a parting gift."

He turned, gingerly, and showed her the scar through the torn shoulder of his jacket. Old. Ugly. Honest.

"So here's my situation," Tull said. "I've got a dory hidden in the cove on the south side—seaworthy, slow, and ugly. You've got a heading and, I'm guessing, coordinates. And we've both got Captain Ada Kessler hunting the same water, because she doesn't stop hunting. Ever. Nine years, I never saw her quit on anything, and I once watched her spend four months chasing a rumor of a rumor." His eyes were steady on hers. "You can't get there without me. I can't get there without you. That's the whole arithmetic."

"And your price?"

"A share. Small. Ten percent of whatever you pull off that wreck, weighed after, ashore, no arguments." He shrugged like it cost him nothing, though she could see it cost him plenty. "I spent nine years making other men rich off water I bled into. I'm done working for promises. Cash or cargo, weighed in front of me."

Jonah crouched down to his level, knife still loose in her hand, and studied him the way she studied salvage: for the flaw, the crack, the thing everyone else overlooked. Deserters lied. Deserters sold people. Deserters were exactly the kind of risk she'd spent her whole life refusing to take, because taking risks on people was how you ended up like her father—trusting, believed, drowned.

But the tide clock in her chest was ticking. Two days of strength. Four tides of margin. A wreck that flooded for the season, and a captain who never quit.

"If you sell me to Kessler," she said, "you'll get amnesty and a purse."

"I'll get a purse and nine more years of being owned." Something moved behind his eyes then, something old and bitter and very tired. "You want to know why I really deserted? It wasn't the work. It was a night three years back when we pulled a hull off a shoal near Corvess, and Kessler ordered the crew manifest burned with the rest of it. Just paper. Just names. And I stood there and watched it burn, and I told myself it was just paper." He looked out at the heaving gray water. "It wasn't just paper. Nothing ever is."

The wind moved through the bent pine above them, and Jonah felt the trap close around her with a click she recognized: the promise-shaped trap, the Elias Crane trap, the trap of needing someone. Trusting anyone was a debt taken out against a future she couldn't see.

But Elias had trusted *her*, and she hadn't been able to refuse him, and maybe that was the whole rotten lesson—that some debts you chose, and some chose you, and the only real decision was whether you paid them straight or squirmed out.

"Ten percent," she said. "Weighed after, ashore. But you follow my orders on the water, and if Kessler shows up, we run—no heroics, no settling scores. You're a deserter, not an avenger."

Something that was almost a smile moved under the iron beard. "Deal."

They shook on it, his hand swallowing hers, calluses catching on calluses.

* * *

The dory was exactly as advertised: seaworthy, slow, and ugly, a stub-nosed workboat with patched planking and a smell of old fish that no amount of salt air would ever drown. But she floated, and she had a mast, a serviceable jib, a bailer, and a small iron stove with a tin of tea, and by noon they were standing out of the southern cove with the island shrinking astern and the sea still rolling heavy from the storm.

Tull handled the boat with the unthinking competence of a man who'd been born aboard something. He set the course from her coordinates without writing them down, repeating them twice under his breath and then nodding like he'd swallowed them whole.

"Tide's with us till evening," he said. "We make the outer shoals by tomorrow's dawn slack if the weather holds. Which it won't, but it'll hold enough." He glanced at her. "Kessler's flagship, the *Reprisal*, patrols a grid off the Verren banks. We cross the edge of it tonight, in the dark, running dark. No lamp, no noise past moonset."

"You've done this before."

"Once." He didn't elaborate, and she didn't ask.

They ran through the afternoon with the jib eased and the hull humming, and Jonah sat in the stern with the oilskin pouch in her lap and let herself feel, for the first time since Greywater, something adjacent to hope. It was a dangerous feeling. Hope was the most expensive thing on the water; she'd seen it bankrupt better people than her. But the wreck was a day away, the map was dry against her ribs, and for ten percent of whatever waited in the hold, she had a crew.

Near dusk, Tull handed her tea from the stove and settled across from her, studying her face in the failing light with that same frank fisherman's attention.

"Question," he said. "And you don't have to answer. But I've been turning it over all day." He wrapped both hands around his own tin. "Your name. When you gave it, you gave it like a woman handing over counterfeit coin. Like you expected me to bite it and check the edges." He sipped. "Vane. That a common name, out this coast?"

The tea went cold in her mouth.

"It's common enough," she said.

"There was an archaeologist by that name," Tull said, conversational, terrible. "Years back. Big reputation, bigger fall. Disappeared on an expedition, and the fleet's owners bought up every word of the inquiry before the ink dried. Kessler had his file on her desk the whole time I served. Kept it like scripture." He looked at her, and his voice gentled into something almost kind. "I'm not asking. I said that. But if you're sailing to the Meridian carrying that man's compass and that man's stubbornness, then Kessler isn't just hunting treasure anymore. She's finishing something. And you should know that before we cross her water tonight."

Jonah stared out at the darkening east, where the Meridian waited under six tides' worth of water—five, now—and said nothing at all.

Above them, the first stars came out, and somewhere beyond the horizon, running dark on the same sea, Ada Kessler was doing the same arithmetic she was.
