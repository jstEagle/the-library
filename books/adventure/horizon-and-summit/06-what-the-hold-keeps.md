## 6. What the Hold Keeps

The Meridian announced herself before Jonah saw her.

Tull's boat—*the Gannet*, a converted pilot cutter with an engine that coughed like a consumptive—came around the shoal at slack water, and the sound reached them first: a low, rhythmic knocking through the hull, transmitted up through the keel and into the soles of their boots. Metal on metal. The wreck shifting against the reef as the tide breathed in and out of her.

"She's been down thirty years," Tull said, cutting the engine. "And she still talks."

Jonah stood at the bow with the map unrolled across her knees, though she didn't need it anymore. She'd memorized it in the first hour out of Greywater, every penciled contour, every annotation in Elias Crane's failing hand. The Meridian lay on her starboard side in forty feet of water, wedged into a shelf of coral and basalt where the reef had caught her like a fist. Crane had marked the outer hull breach with an X and one word beside it: *careful.*

"Forty feet," Tull said, reading her face. "That's two dives if you want time down there. Three if you're being smart."

"I'm not being smart."

"No," he agreed. "I didn't think so."

They anchored in the lee of the shoal, and while Tull rigged the lines and checked his tanks—the man had a deserter's paranoia about equipment, checking every valve twice, three times—Jonah sat on the gunwale and looked down through the water. From above, on a day like this, with the sun high and the sea flat, you could see the shape of her. A long dark suggestion beneath the blue-green, like something sleeping under a sheet.

Her father had taught her to dive in a flooded quarry when she was nine years old. He'd held her hand the whole way down and told her that fear was just information arriving too fast, that a diver's job was to slow it down, sort it, make it useful. He'd said it in that voice he used for lessons, warm and certain, and she'd believed him then, before she learned that the same man could hold your hand underwater and let go of everything else.

She pushed the memory down where she kept it and started checking her own gear.

* * *

The water took her the way it always did—all at once, cold closing over her head, the world going green and silent. She equalized, hung for a moment in the bright upper layer where the light came down in columns, and then kicked for the bottom.

The Meridian grew out of the dark the way wrecks do: first a shadow, then a shape, then suddenly all of it, too much of it, a hundred and sixty feet of steel lying on her side with her masts broken off and her rail furred white with coral. Fish moved through her in silver rivers. Anemones had colonized the hawseholes and turned them into gardens. Thirty years of the ocean doing what the ocean does—taking a thing built by people and slowly, patiently making it into something else.

Jonah swam the length of the outer hull, following Crane's map from memory. The breach was where he'd said it would be—a tear in the plating below the former waterline, edges rolled outward, big enough to admit a swimmer if the swimmer didn't think about it too hard. She did think about it, briefly, and then stopped thinking, because thinking was the enemy down here. Her father's voice again, uninvited: *fear is information.* Fine. Information received. She went in.

Inside was darker and quieter, the kind of quiet that pressed on the ears. Her lamp cut a cone through water gone murky with disturbed silt, and by its light she saw the corridor of the Meridian's lower deck turned ninety degrees sideways, doors hanging open along what had been the ceiling, a steward's cart still lashed to a bulkhead with its dishes fused into the wood by three decades of salt. She moved through it slowly, counting bulkheads the way the map instructed, past cabins with their bunks still made, past a child's shoe standing upright in the silt like a small monument.

Crane's X put the strongbox in the purser's office, third door past the galley. Jonah found the galley by its great iron stove, found the third door, and found the office half-collapsed under a section of overhead plating. The strongbox was exactly where a dying prospector had promised it would be: bolted to the deck, which was now the wall, which meant it stood upright at head height like a door to nowhere, its brass fittings gone green, its lock crusted but intact.

She'd brought tools for this. She'd brought tools because Elias Crane, in his last lucid hour, had described the box in detail—the make, the mechanism, the trick of the secondary latch—and she'd understood, even then, that a man who knew a strongbox that well had opened it himself. Had seen what was inside. Had been carrying that knowledge alone for however many years, letting it eat him alive.

The lock fought her. Salt had welded the tumblers, and she worked the picks by feel more than sight, her breath rasping loud in her own ears, bubbles rising in silver strings toward a ceiling that was also a floor. Twice the pick slipped. Twice she made herself stop, breathe, start again. *Slow it down. Sort it. Make it useful.*

On the third try, the lock gave with a click she felt in her teeth.

The lid swung open on darkness. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, were bundles of paper.

She knew before she touched them. Some part of her knew from the moment she'd read Crane's map and seen the careful architectural precision of the lettering, the surveyor's habit of annotating depths and bearings in margins. But knowing and holding are different things, and when she pulled the first bundle free and unwrapped it in the lamplight, and saw the handwriting—*that* handwriting, the slanted, impatient script that had filled the margins of every book in the house she'd grown up in—her regulator nearly fell out of her mouth.

Edmund Vane. Her father. His expedition notes, sealed in oilcloth, sealed in a strongbox, at the bottom of the sea.

* * *

She should have surfaced. Every rule she lived by said surface, regroup, breathe air that wasn't filtered through a tank. Instead she found the driest corner of the purser's office—an air pocket, shallow, stale, tasting of rust and old rope—and broke the surface into it, dragging off her mask, and held the bundle over her head with both hands like something rescued from a fire.

The pocket was barely bigger than a coffin stood on end. Above her, the inverted deck. Around her, black water waiting to take it all back. She got the lamp braced on a pipe fitting and unwrapped the notes properly, page by page, and her father's voice rose off them so completely that for a few minutes she forgot where she was.

*Day 11. The Meridian is not where the charts say she is. Either the charts are wrong or someone wanted her lost in the wrong place. I know which bet I'd make.*

*Day 14. Found the crew manifest discrepancy. Forty-one souls signed on at Corvess. The fleet's official record lists twenty-six lost. Fifteen men unaccounted for, and the owners' petition lists them as deserters. Deserters. From a ship that sank under them.*

*Day 19. Met a survivor today. Old, half-mad, living in a hut on the shoal islands. He was there. He says the fifteen were in the after hold when the flooding started, and the after hold was sealed—from outside. He says the fleet's own boats were on the water and did not come for them. He gave me this account and asked me to make them count for something. I intend to.*

*Day 22. Someone searched my cabin. They took nothing, which tells me they already know what I have. That is worse.*

The last entry was different. Shorter. The handwriting had changed—not shakier, exactly, but compressed, written fast, written by a man who understood he was running out of room on the page and possibly everywhere else.

*I have sent copies ahead by three routes. If you are reading this, I am dead, and the original is in the only place they will never willingly look: the wreck itself. They buried these men twice—once in the hold, once in the record. Let the sea keep the truth until someone comes who will carry it up.*

*To whoever finds this: I'm sorry. I'm sorry I wasn't braver sooner. Tell my daughter—*

The entry stopped there. No instruction. No apology finished. Just *tell my daughter*, and then nothing, the sentence drowned mid-breath, and Jonah sat in the dark air pocket with the pages trembling in her hands and thirty years of anger coming apart in her chest like wet paper.

He'd been here. He'd found this. He'd died for it—she'd always known he died badly, disgraced, alone, the family name a curse she'd spent her whole life scrubbing herself free of—but she hadn't known *this*. That the disgrace was the price. That the cowardice she'd never forgiven him for had been the other side of a courage she'd never given him credit for. That he'd chosen the truth over the fortune, over his reputation, over coming home to her, and had died in the choosing, and had left the last words he ever wrote for her unfinished because death doesn't wait for drafts.

She laughed, once, a short ugly sound that bounced around the pocket. The inheritance she'd wanted was money. The inheritance she'd gotten was a promise with no bottom to it.

Below her, through the deck plates, the knocking continued. The wreck breathing. Counting tides.

And then, faintly, carried down through forty feet of water and hull steel, another sound joined it. An engine. Not the Gannet's consumptive cough—something bigger, deeper, running at low revs the way a ship runs when it's being careful, when it doesn't want to be heard until it's already here.

Jonah killed her lamp and listened, her father's last unfinished sentence pressed against her chest, and counted the seconds between the engine's pulses, and thought: *two ships' lengths away, maybe less, and getting closer.*
