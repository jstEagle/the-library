## 7. The Price of the Ledger

Tull found the second compartment by accident, which was the only way he'd ever found anything worth having.

He'd gone back down alone while Jonah slept in the skiff's shadow on the Meridian's tilted deck, her breathing shallow and salt-rasped, one hand curled around the strongbox like a child around a stolen loaf. He'd told himself he was keeping watch. He'd told himself a lot of things in eleven years aboard Kessler's ships, and the telling had gotten so easy it barely counted as lying anymore.

The wreck groaned around him. The *Meridian* lay on her port side in forty feet of green water, and even through his mask Tull could feel her listening—the long creak of a hull that had been dying for thirty years and hadn't finished yet. Six tides until the air pocket flooded for the season. Five now. The math sat in his chest like swallowed ballast.

The strongbox itself was up in the hold where Jonah had left it, wedged under a collapsed shelf, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark. She'd pried it open that afternoon with a salvage bar and gone white as drowned meat at what she found inside—her father's notes, pages and pages of them, written in a hand Tull didn't recognize but whose name he did. Vane. The disgraced one. The ghost story every diver on the coast told with a drink in him.

Jonah had read three pages and stopped like she'd hit glass.

So when she finally slept, Tull took the box out into the light.

Not to steal it. He wanted that on the record, whatever record there was, wherever records went when you died on someone else's ship. He told himself he just wanted to look. There was a difference between looking and stealing. Eleven years on Kessler's crew had taught him exactly how thin that difference was, and how much weight it would bear.

The box was old naval issue, brass corners, a maker's stamp half eaten by corrosion. He turned it over in the failing light and ran his thumb along the bottom seam—and felt it catch. A lip. A false floor, maybe an inch deep, running the full footprint of the case.

His heart did something stupid and hopeful.

He worked the seam with the salvage bar's flat edge, careful, the way you worked anything you couldn't afford to break. The panel gave with a soft click that sounded enormous in the flooded quiet. Inside, wrapped in waxed linen that had kept the water out for three decades, was a book.

A ledger. Bound in grey leather, stamped with no name, its pages dense with columns—dates, figures, signatures. Tull knew ledgers. He'd spent six years in the fleet's pay office before Kessler moved him to the dive crews, and he knew what a real ledger looked like versus what a fake one looked like, and this one had the specific boring authority of the genuine article. Cargo manifests. Insurance filings. Crew rosters for the *Meridian*'s final voyage.

And then, two-thirds in, the columns changed.

They became payments. Dated over four months after the wreck everyone called an accident. Line items with codes instead of names—but codes were nothing, codes were children's puzzles, because at the bottom of each page were initials and countersignatures, and Tull had spent enough years in the fleet's offices to know three of those sets of initials the way you know your own scars.

Harrowgate. Vestry. Ondine Shipping.

The owners of the salvage fleet. The men whose flags flew over every harbor from Greywater to the Corvess roads. Paying out, month after month, in sums that only made sense as one kind of payment: silence. And beneath the payments, in a clerk's cramped hand, a single annotation that repeated like a refrain:

*Meridian survivors settled. No further claims.*

Survivors. Plural. Settled.

Tull sat very still on the tilted deck plates and let the wreck creak around him.

Everyone knew the story. The *Meridian* had gone down in a storm off the Serrano shoals, cargo lost, most of her crew lost with her, a tragedy that had launched the salvage fleet that grew fat pulling her holds apart for a decade. Everyone knew it the way everyone knows things—secondhand, unexamined, convenient. Tull had been nineteen when he signed onto the fleet, hungry and harborless, and he'd never once asked what happened to the crew who *didn't* drown. You didn't ask questions on Kessler's ships. Asking questions was how you ended up on night watch alone in bad water.

But he'd seen things. Small things, filed away the way you file splinters. The way the fleet's early charts marked certain coves RESTRICTED with no explanation. The way old crewmen went quiet when the *Meridian* came up in conversation, not grieving-quiet but guilty-quiet. The way Kessler herself, cold as ballast, once drank herself silent on the anniversary of the sinking and stared at the water like it owed her something.

Thirty-one names. That was what the roster said. Thirty-one men on the final crew, and the official count of the lost was nine.

Twenty-two men had walked off that wreck alive. And then they had walked into payments, or graves, and nobody had ever asked where.

Tull closed the ledger.

His hands were shaking, which annoyed him. He was forty-three years old, he'd held his breath beside drowning men, he'd cut a fouled line off a corpse at ninety feet without his pulse moving. But this—a book, paper and ink, the least dangerous thing on this whole coast—this made his hands shake.

Because he knew what it was worth. Not the truth. Truth was free; he'd watched it wash up on beaches for years and nobody bent to pick it up. He meant the object. Paper naming Harrowgate and Vestry, countersigned, dated—that was worth more than the gold everyone assumed was buried in this hull. That was worth empires. That was worth exactly one thing to one person:

Amnesty.

Kessler's offer had come through the usual channels, which meant a note in a dead-drop buoy, unsigned, unmistakable: *Any deserter returning with information regarding the Vane woman or her salvage will be received without charges. Full reinstatement. Full pardon.*

Full pardon. Tull turned the words over. Eleven years of service, and his desertion—six months ago, a lifeboat, a night, a decision made at gunpoint of conscience he still couldn't fully explain—had made him a hunted thing. They'd put his description in every port office from here to Corvess. *Tull Marren. Fleet tattoo, left forearm. Do not engage; report.* He'd scraped at the tattoo with a knife the first week and stopped when he understood he was only punishing the wrong party.

If he brought Kessler this ledger—or better, told her where it was and let her do the burning—he walked back aboard the flagship with clean papers. A bunk. Wages. A name that opened doors instead of closing them. He'd survive the winter. He'd survive a lot of winters.

And Jonah Vane would go into the water.

Not fast. Kessler wasn't fast about people she needed quiet. But the girl had a map in her head and a father's name on her back and a strongbox she thought she understood, and none of those things survived contact with the fleet. Tull had watched Kessler erase problems for a decade. She was good at it. She'd been *trained* at it, though trained by whom was a question he'd never let himself finish.

He looked at the ledger again. At the annotation. *No further claims.*

Twenty-two men. Somebody's fathers. Somebody's sons. Men who'd survived a storm and been murdered by arithmetic on some beach or in some warehouse, their deaths entered in a column and initialed by men who signed church pews on Sundays.

And Jonah Vane's father had found this. Had come out here years ago, alone, broke, disgraced, and found it—and died for it, somehow, somewhere, in a way that had made his daughter hate him right up until the hour she learned he'd been telling the truth all along.

Tull thought about the girl sleeping on deck. She'd given him a share. That was the whole of their contract—one third, agreed in the rain on a shoal island, sealed with nothing but her word and his. He'd laughed at her for it, privately. Nobody sealed anything with a word anymore. He'd planned, in the honest dark of the first night, at least three ways to take the whole find and leave her swimming.

He hadn't done it. He'd told himself it was because she was useful, because she knew the map, because sentiment was just profit deferred. He'd been telling himself things for eleven years.

The tide was turning. He could feel it in the wreck's voice, the groan shifting pitch, the water beginning its slow argument with the hull. Two tides left after this one. Maybe less, if the weather turned, and the weather always turned.

He wrapped the ledger back in its linen. Then he sat with it on his knees for a long time, doing the arithmetic survival demanded, the same arithmetic he'd done every day since he was nineteen: what things cost, what things paid, what a man could afford.

Here is what amnesty cost: a girl who kept her word to a dying stranger. Here is what it paid: a bunk, a wage, a few more winters of being the kind of man who could sleep on a fleet mattress because he'd never once asked what the fleet slept on underneath.

Here is what the other road cost: everything, probably. Kessler didn't forgive and didn't forget and had ships enough to make the whole coast a closed fist. If he stayed with Jonah, he was choosing drowning over hanging, and the sea at least had the decency to be quick.

Here is what it paid: twenty-two names, remembered. One promise kept—not his promise, hers, but promises were contagious; that was the trap of them. And a ledger that reached Corvess, that reached a court, that reached the men who signed the columns, and made them answer for *no further claims.*

Tull Marren had deserted the fleet once already, in the rain, over a thing he'd never told anyone: a diver named Cass, sent down on a bad gauge on a bad day because the schedule mattered more than the man, and the sound Cass made on the radio, and the way the incident report had read *equipment failure, no fault assigned,* and the way Tull had signed it anyway because signing was easier than drowning.

He'd signed it anyway. That was the rot in him. He knew it was there. He'd carried it six months like a stone in his boot.

He stood up, joints cracking, and tucked the ledger inside his coat against his chest, where he could feel it with every breath like a second heartbeat.

Then he heard the skiff's hull knock against the wreck, and boots on deck plating, and Jonah Vane's voice, sharp with the particular fear of a woman who has woken alone next to open water:

"Tull?"

He climbed toward the light. Above him the sky was going the color of pewter, and far out past the shoals—too far to be mistaken, too slow to be fishing boats—three sails were standing in toward the *Meridian*, riding the new tide like they'd booked passage on it.

Kessler had found the coordinates.

Tull took the stairs two at a time, ledger against his heart, and made his choice with his feet before his head could vote again.
