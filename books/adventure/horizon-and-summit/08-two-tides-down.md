## 8. Two Tides Down

The lantern died at slack water, and that was how Jonah knew the tide had turned.

She'd been counting. Six hours in the flooded hold of the Meridian, hauled up on a shelf of collapsed decking with her father's strongbox open across her knees, and every hour she'd marked it against the slow breath of the sea outside—the groan of the hull as the pressure shifted, the way the water in the companionway climbed and fell like something sleeping. Now the breathing had stopped. Which meant the water would start rising again soon, and this time it wouldn't stop until the air pocket was gone.

Tull sat across from her with his back against a bulkhead, working a whetstone along a knife he hadn't needed yet. He'd been doing that for an hour. Not sharpening—listening.

"Boat," he said.

Jonah heard it a second later. Oars, muffled by steel and seawater, but there. More than one set.

"They found us." She closed the strongbox. Her father's notes were folded inside her shirt now, oilcloth-wrapped, warm from her own skin. "How?"

Tull didn't answer right away. The whetstone stopped moving.

"Jonah."

The way he said her name made her stomach drop before he said anything else. She'd known him four days. Four days was enough to learn how a man held his knife, how he took his coffee, how he lied about small things so you'd believe him about large ones. She'd told herself she'd learned all of it.

"How long have they been offering amnesty?" she asked quietly.

He looked up. In the dark she couldn't read his face, only the pale smudge of it. "Since I jumped ship. Two years. Full pardon, half share of whatever's recovered, passage anywhere they run to." A pause. "They renewed the offer last month. Posted it on every port board from Greywater south."

"And when you saw the map—when you recognized those coordinates—you knew what I had. You knew what they'd pay."

"I knew," Tull said.

The oars came again, closer. Somewhere above them, through two decks of drowned ship, Kessler's people were feeling their way down the outer hull, looking for the breach Jonah had used.

"So this is where you tell me you're sorry," Jonah said. Her voice came out flat and cold, and she was grateful for it, because underneath the flatness something was cracking. "Or where you put the knife to work."

"I turned them down."

She laughed—one short, ugly sound. "That's a convenient thing to say with their boats at your door."

"It's the truth, and you can believe it or drown alone." He set the knife down deliberately, out of his own reach, and slid it across the deck toward her. "I sent them a message three days ago. From the shoal island, before we ever sailed. Told them I had the map, told them where we'd be, and told them my price wasn't amnesty. It was Kessler's head on a plate and the Meridian's crew named in open court. They didn't answer. So either they're here to arrest me, or they're here because Kessler decided a deserter who makes demands is worth more dead than pardoned." His jaw tightened. "Either way, I'm in this hole with you, and the tide's coming in, and I'd rather spend what's left proving it than arguing about it."

Jonah picked up the knife. Weighed it. Thought about the man who had pulled her out of a storm without being asked, who had stood watch so she could sleep, who had flinched—actually flinched—when she'd first spoken the names from the ledger aloud.

She believed him. That was the worst part. She wanted not to, and she did anyway.

"The inner hold," she said. "There's an air pocket past the watertight door. My father's notes describe it. It'll buy us another hour after this one floods."

"Then we move now."

* * *

They went down through black water holding a single line, hand over hand, into the Meridian's spine. The inner hold had been sealed since the sinking—Jonah felt the difference the moment they surfaced inside it, the air thick and old, tasting of rust and forty years of nothing. Their lantern threw a trembling circle across cargo cradles, a shattered crane arm, the ghost-shapes of crates lashed down for a crossing that never ended.

And across all of it, painted in tar over the inner wall where no passing ship would ever see it: names. Dozens of them. The crew had written themselves onto their own tomb.

Jonah's chest hurt. She thought of the ledger in the strongbox, the neat columns of payment and signature, men paid to make sure none of these names ever reached shore.

Then the water in the doorway behind them shivered, and boots sounded on the deck above, and there was no more time for grief.

Kessler came down the ladder herself.

Jonah knew her from the port gossip and from Tull's hatred—a tall woman, grey at the temples, moving with the unhurried economy of someone who had never once needed to hurry. Two divers flanked her, harpoon guns slung. She surveyed the air pocket, the names on the wall, Jonah and Tull standing hip-deep in the rising water, and she did not look surprised by any of it.

"Jonah Vane," she said. "Your father would be proud. He also died in this room."

The words landed like a hook under the ribs. Jonah kept her face still. "You knew him."

"I read his file." Kessler stepped closer, water swirling around her boots. "Elias Vane. Brilliant, careless, and convinced the world owed him the truth. He found this wreck eleven years ago. He surfaced with documents. Three weeks later his boat burned at anchor with him aboard." She let that sit. "You've read the notes by now. You know how the story ends for people who carry paper instead of coin."

"I know how it ends for people who sell," Jonah said. "You're still carrying yours."

Something moved behind Kessler's eyes—not anger. Something older and more tired. "Here is my offer, and I will make it once. The ledger and the notes. In exchange: the salvage rights signed over to you, legal, notarized, worth more money than your father ever dreamed of. A new name if you want one. Passage anywhere. You walk out of this hold rich and free and no one—no one—ever connects Jonah Vane to Elias Vane again. You disappear upward, into comfort. Isn't that what you've always wanted? To be left alone?"

The water was at Jonah's waist now. Cold, patient, climbing.

And God help her, for three full seconds she wanted it. She stood in her father's grave and let herself imagine it: a room of her own with a lock on the door, food she hadn't salvaged, mornings that belonged to nobody. All she had to do was hand over the names of forty dead men and let them stay dead a little longer.

Her hand went to her shirt, to the oilcloth packet.

"No," she said.

Kessler exhaled slowly. "Your father hesitated too. Right about there." She nodded at Jonah's hand. "It cost him everything."

"He chose wrong, then," Jonah said. "Not the choice. The timing. He tried to sell the truth to people who could afford to bury it. I'm not selling it at all." She lifted her chin. "There's a girl in Corvess named Maren Crane. Everything I have goes to her. Every page. And by the time your owners hear my name, it'll be in front of a magistrate, and there won't be enough money left in your fleet to buy it back."

For a moment the only sound was the water rising, a soft obscene whisper against the hull.

"You can't shoot us both and call it salvage," Tull said quietly. "Two bodies in a sealed hold, Kessler. Even your owners aren't that good."

Kessler looked at him. Then at Jonah. Then—at last, and this was the thing Jonah would remember for the rest of her life—she looked at the names on the wall. Her gaze traveled down them slowly, like a woman reading a debt she'd spent forty years pretending wasn't hers.

"Harlow," she read aloud. "Imani Harlow. Cook's mate." Her voice cracked, just barely, on the second word. She cleared her throat. "My first berth was a Meridian-class hull. I was nineteen."

"Captain—" one of the divers started.

"Get topside," Kessler said. "Both of you. That's an order."

They went. The ladder rang. And then it was the three of them, and the water at Jonah's sternum, and the lantern guttering as the air pocket thinned.

Kessler unslung her harpoon gun and dropped it into the water. It sank without a sound.

"I'm going to lie to my owners," she said. "I'm going to tell them the hold flooded before we reached you. They'll believe it, because they want to believe the sea does their work for them." She was already backing toward the ladder, water climbing her thighs. "But understand me, Vane. If you surface, if you speak, if that paper reaches a courtroom—they will come for you the way they came for your father. There is no outswimming them. There is only outrunning them, forever."

"Then I'd better start now," Jonah said.

Kessler paused at the ladder, half-submerged, and for one unguarded instant she looked less like a hunter than like a drowning woman watching someone else find a rope. "Tell them the cook's mate's name," she said. "When you testify. Tell them Imani Harlow. She deserved better than all of us."

Then she was gone, and the dark swallowed the ladder, and the water kept rising.

* * *

They had maybe ten minutes of air left, and Jonah spent five of them reading.

She stood chest-deep beside the wall of names with the lantern held high and the oilcloth packet open, and she read her father's final page aloud while Tull treaded water beside her, keeping the light steady when her hands shook.

"—and so I understand at last what I came here believing I already knew," his voice said, in her voice, in the drowned dark. "That the truth is not a thing you own. I planned to trade it, the way I traded everything else—my marriage, my daughter's childhood, my name. I sat in this hold with the ledger in my hands and calculated its price, and the number was enormous, and I have never in my life been so poor as I was in that moment.

"If anyone finds this: I am not asking forgiveness. I am leaving instructions. Take it to someone who cannot be bought. Take it to someone who has nothing to lose, because I lost everything worth keeping and it bought the truth exactly nothing.

"Jonah, if it's you—if you're reading this, then you found me after all, and I don't deserve that, and I'm proud of you anyway. Don't be me. Don't wait until the water's at your chin to decide what you are.

"The tide's turning. I can hear it. I choose the truth. God forgive me for taking so long."

The lantern hissed. The air pocket was the size of a coffin now.

Jonah folded the pages against her chest, wrapped the oilcloth tight, and looked at Tull. He was treading water, watching her, waiting.

"Together on the line," he said. "One breath each. We go on the exhale."

Above them, faint through forty feet of black water, the hull groaned—and somewhere far off, muffled by steel and sea, a bell began to ring. Not Kessler's ships. Something bigger, coming fast through the dark toward the Meridian's position, ringing and ringing like a summons.

Tull's head snapped up. "That's a revenue cutter."

The water sealed over the last of the air pocket, and Jonah kicked for the line, her father's words pressed to her heart, the whole wreck groaning around them like a thing finally ready to talk.
