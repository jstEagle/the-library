## 10. Above Water

The tide took something from her in the drowning room, and she never learned what to call it.

Jonah surfaced at Greywater three days later, coughing brine into a fisherman's nets, and the first thing she did was laugh. It came out wrong—cracked, half water—and the fisherman's wife wrapped her in wool and said nothing, which was the kindest thing anyone had done for Jonah Vane in eleven years.

Tull was already gone. He'd rowed off before dawn with his share of nothing, pressing a coin into her palm at the dock like an apology. "For the skiff I got you killed," he'd said. "Buy a better one." She'd watched him go until the fog ate him, and she hadn't decided yet whether she was angry. Maybe that was what trust cost: you never got to finish the accounting.

She had the ledger sewn into her coat lining, against her ribs, where it had ridden through two storms, one drowning, and a magistrate's indifferent stare at the Greywater customs house. She had her father's notes wrapped in oilcloth under her arm. She had a compass that no longer pointed anywhere useful, because its owner had stopped needing directions.

And she had a promise, still wet, still owed.

* * *

Corvess smelled of coal smoke and cut flowers, a city that had never once thought about the sea except as scenery. Jonah walked its avenues in borrowed boots, salt-stained and hollow-eyed, past shop windows where her reflection kept surprising her—a woman with a face, visible, unmistakable, walking in daylight among people who could see her.

The Crane house stood on Alder Street behind a wall of ivy gone feral. The knocker was brass, shaped like a meridian line. Of course it was.

Maren Crane opened the door herself. She was younger than Jonah expected, or maybe just worn differently—young in the face, old around the eyes, the way people get when they've spent years waiting for news they're afraid to name.

"You're not a courier," Maren said.

"No."

"Then you're the reason there hasn't been one." Her voice didn't shake. "Come in before the neighbors decide you're interesting."

Inside: maps on every wall. Charts of coasts Elias Crane had walked and drowned beside, framed like saints' icons. A cold hearth. A chair by the window facing the door, positioned for watching arrivals. Jonah recognized the furniture of a life spent waiting.

"My father," Maren said. "Tell me."

So Jonah told her. All of it—the dying prospector in the Greywater alley, the map pressed into her hands, the promise sworn over a man's last breath. The Meridian. The flooded hold. The strongbox, and what was inside it besides her father's handwriting. The ledger, and the names in it, and what those names had ordered done to a crew of forty-one people who had trusted their owners with their lives.

When she reached the part about the massacre—the order given, the boats turned away, the forty-one left to drown while the fleet's owners salvaged the cargo and burned the records—Maren sat very still, the way her father must have taught her, the way sailors sit out weather they can't fight.

"He knew," Maren said finally. "All these years, he knew, and he couldn't prove it, and it ate him alive. That's why he went looking. That's why—" She stopped. Started again. "You said there were notes. Another man's notes."

Jonah unwrapped the oilcloth and set her father's expedition journal on the table between them.

Edmund Vane. The disgraced archaeologist. The liar, the fraud, the man who had abandoned his daughter to scandal and vanished into other people's histories. He had come to this same wreck six years ago. He had found the same truth. He had written it down in his small, precise hand, page after page, and somewhere between the wreck and home, someone had caught him, and taken everything but what he'd hidden inside the strongbox, and ended him quietly enough that the world called it exposure and fever.

Jonah had spent eleven years refusing to grieve him. She read his final page aloud in Maren's cold parlor, and grief arrived anyway, late and total, like a tide that doesn't care about your schedule.

* * *

The trial lasted four months and consumed the fortune whole.

That was the joke nobody laughed at: the salvage rights to the Meridian, the cargo manifest, the recovered bullion claims—all of it, every coin of the fortune that could have bought Jonah a hundred lifetimes of invisibility, dissolved into lawyers. Court costs. Depositions. Expert witnesses. The owners' families sold townhouses and yachts to pay for their defense, and lost anyway, because a ledger is a stubborn kind of ghost and forty-one names do not stay quiet when someone finally reads them aloud.

Ada Kessler testified under immunity. Jonah watched her from the gallery: the cold competence intact, the rot beneath it showing only when the prosecutor read the dates aloud and Kessler's hands, folded on the rail, went white at the knuckles. She had let Jonah swim out of the drowning room. She had chosen to drown with the secret rather than kill the one witness who understood her. The court called it cowardice. Jonah knew better, and never said so, and hated herself a little for the mercy of that silence.

The fleet was impounded at anchor, all nine ships, their hulls going green in the grey water off Corvess while auctioneers circled like gulls. The owners went to prison in ones and twos, staggered across the calendar like a slow tide going out.

Elias Crane's name was cleared in week three. Maren read the verdict standing, alone, in a courtroom full of strangers, and did not cry until afterward, in the corridor, gripping Jonah's arm hard enough to bruise.

"He sent you," she said. "Of all the wrecks on that coast, of all the thieves and salvagers—he found you."

"I wasn't hard to find," Jonah said. "I was just good at pretending otherwise."

* * *

Spring came to Greywater the way it always did: late, skeptical, smelling of thawed mud and diesel.

Jonah stood at the harbormaster's window with a salvage claim form in front of her and a pen that belonged to the office, chewed at the cap, ink unreliable. The clerk waited with the patience of a man paid by the hour.

The form asked for a name. It had always asked for a name. For eleven years Jonah had signed *J. Fairweather*, her mother's surname, a shell she wore over the wound of being a Vane—or she'd signed nothing at all, worked the margins, salvaged what others overlooked, stayed small enough that the world's attention slid off her like water off oiled canvas.

Her father had been visible once. It had destroyed him—or she had believed so, for eleven years, because believing it was easier than admitting he'd been right about anything. He had died visible, holding a truth too heavy to carry quietly. And his daughter had inherited the truth, and the compass, and the choice, and had learned in a flooding room forty feet under the sea that some things are worth surfacing for.

The fortune was gone. The fleet was broken. Kessler was somewhere east, testifying again, paying her own debt in the only currency she had left. Tull was, presumably, alive somewhere, spending his apology coin.

Jonah wrote *Salvage of the brigantine* *Meridian*, auxiliary materials, outer shoal field—one compass, one sealed strongbox, contents documented. Then, in the space marked *Claimant*, she set the pen down and looked at it for a long moment.

The clerk shifted. Outside, a gull screamed at another gull about something neither would ever surrender.

She signed:

**Jonah Vane.**

Full name. Both words. Ink blotting slightly at the tail of the *e*, like a wave settling.

"There," the clerk said, stamping it. "Congratulations. You officially exist."

"Something like that," Jonah said, and took her copy, and walked out into the light.

The harbor lay open before her, grey and glittering, full of wrecks nobody had claimed and truths nobody had told. Somewhere out there was work worth doing, and for the first time in her life, she intended to do it under her own flag, in her own name, visible from any distance the horizon allowed.

She touched the compass in her pocket—her father's, needle steady now, pointing the only direction that had ever mattered—and headed for the water.
