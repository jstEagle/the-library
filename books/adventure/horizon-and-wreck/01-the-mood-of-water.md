## 1. The Mood of Water

The Vell River has three moods, and I have learned to read all of them the way other people read faces.

There's the morning mood, when the water lies flat and gray as pewter and gives up nothing—no current worth naming, no secrets, just a long sullen silence from Millbrook down to the Split. There's the afternoon mood, when the wind comes off the eastern bluffs and chops the surface into small angry arguments. And then there's the mood it's in right now, at half past four on an October afternoon with winter breathing down its neck: the low-water mood, the lying mood, the one that shows you a smooth brown skin over sandbars that would take the Kestrel's hull apart like a child pulling wings off a fly.

I stood at the wheel with one boot hooked on the rail brace and watched the water lie to me.

"Bar's moved," I said.

Behind me, in the pilothouse doorway, my deckhand—a boy called Tuck who was less a hand than a mouth attached to an appetite—squinted at the river like it owed him money. "Looks fine to me."

"It always looks fine to you."

"That's 'cause I got faith, Captain."

"You've got a hole where sense should be." I nodded at the water ahead, maybe two hundred yards out, where the current made a long lazy S around what looked like open channel. "See how it bends left there? Water doesn't bend for nothing. There's a bar sitting under that smooth stretch, new since the spring flood, and the river's going around it because the river's not stupid. We are, if we follow the pretty part."

Tuck squinted harder. "So we go where it's ugly?"

"We go where the water's honest." I swung the wheel and brought us left, toward the choppy seam where the current broke white over something shallow, then eased into the deeper cut beyond it. The Kestrel leaned, grumbled through her old timbers, and slid through the gap with six feet of river under her keel instead of eighteen inches. Behind us, the pretty water rippled innocently over a sandbar that would have grounded us until the spring thaw and cost me everything I had left, which wasn't much.

"That's witchcraft," Tuck said.

"That's twenty years of listening." I didn't say the rest of it, which was: *and you'd better learn it fast, boy, because if I go down, nobody else is going to teach you.* Some things you keep behind your teeth.

The Vell runs better than two hundred miles from the high country down to Vellport, and in twenty years I had run every mile of it in every season except the dead of winter, when she froze solid enough that even fools stayed ashore. I knew her snags by name. I knew which cutbanks were eating themselves alive and which bars grew a foot every flood and which eddies held drowned men's gear until the ice took it under for good. Other captains bought charts. Charts are rumors printed on paper. I had the river herself, and she and I had an arrangement: I paid attention, and she let me live.

It was, I reflected, the longest relationship of my life, and the only one that had never lied to me about anything important.

* * *

We made the landing at Corben's Bend just before dark, running firewood and salt pork and a crate of patent medicine that was probably colored sugar and probably killed fewer people than the whiskey sold legally two doors from the dock. Corben himself came down the plank road with his ledger under his arm, a fat man moving with the careful dignity of someone who has decided not to run anymore.

"Voss," he said.

"Just Elodie," I said. "Captain Elodie."

He gave me the look he always gave me—the one that said he knew perfectly well there was more to my name than that, and that he was too sensible to say so. "Elodie, then. You're carrying for Harrow freight this month?"

The word went into me like a fishhook. I kept my face still. Twenty years of practice keeping your face still means nobody can tell when your stomach drops, and I recommend the practice to anyone planning a dishonest life.

"I don't carry for the Syndicate," I said. "You know that."

"The Syndicate doesn't care what you carry, girl. They care that boats on their river pay their tolls." He opened his ledger and turned it toward me, and there it was, printed neat as scripture: *Harrow Consolidated Toll Schedule, effective this season.* "New rates. They're posting them at every dock between here and Vellport. My cousin saw the toll boat at Split Rock yesterday, flying the black-and-gold."

"They don't own the river."

"No," Corben agreed mildly. "They own the docks, the warehouses, the insurance offices, the assayer's office, and the territorial judge. The river they're still working on." He licked his thumb and turned a page. "Your account stands at forty-one dollars against next month's mooring. Cash, as usual?"

"Cash, as usual."

I counted it out into his soft palm and watched him write *paid* beside my name—or beside the name I give people, which is the same thing for practical purposes. That's the whole architecture of my life, if you want to know it: cash, never credit; first names, never last; no debts written down anywhere except the one debt I couldn't pay, which sat in a lockbox in Vellport with the Harrow seal on it and grew interest like a tumor.

Forty-one dollars. It used to be that kind of sum meant nothing to me. Now it was a week of my life, counted out in silver and sweat, and I handed it over gladly because every dollar that passed through my hands and out again without leaving a paper trail was another dollar the Syndicate couldn't find.

"Word of advice," Corben said, closing the ledger. He lowered his voice, which for Corben meant he stopped shouting. "The toll boat working the lower Vell this season is captained by a man named Dain. Corvus Dain. He collects for the Harrows, and he collects past-due accounts personally." His eyes came up and rested on mine, mild and terrible. "Past-due accounts being his specialty."

"I don't have any past-due accounts."

"No," Corben said. "Of course not. Nobody ever does, until they do." He hefted his ledger. "Firewood's stacked on the north bank. Don't be here at dawn; the toll boat makes its morning run through the Bend, and I'd hate for them to see your face and start wondering why a woman with no last name pays everything in cash and carries nothing for anyone."

"I'm invisible," I said. "That's the whole point of me."

Corben looked at me for a long moment with something almost like pity. "Nobody's invisible on a river, girl. A river remembers everybody." Then he turned and went back up the plank road, fat and slow and wiser than he let on, and I stood on my own deck feeling the hook twist.

* * *

We ran through the dark that night, which I don't usually do, but I wanted the Bend behind me before morning and sleep is a thing I've never been good at anyway. Tuck took the first watch below and I took the wheel, and the Vell ran black and glassy under a half moon, and I did what I always do on night runs, which is talk to her.

Not out loud. Mostly.

People think a riverwoman talks to her boat out of loneliness, and they're wrong. It's out of professional courtesy. The Kestrel is sixty-two feet of pre-war hull with a sternwheel that shimmies above eight knots and a boiler older than Tuck, and she has opinions. She told me all night long, in the language of vibration and drag and the particular note her wheel blades make when they slap water that's shallower than I want it to be, and I listened, and between the two of us we came down the middle miles of the Vell in the dark without touching bottom once.

Around midnight I let myself think about the toll boat.

Here's what you need to understand about the Harrow Syndicate, and I'll keep it short because thinking about them too long is like picking at a wound: they started as a freight company thirty years ago and became a government without elections. They own the shipping on four rivers. They set the rates, they collect the tolls, they hold the paper on half the towns along the Vell, and when somebody can't pay, they don't send lawyers. Lawyers leave records. They send collectors.

And I owe them.

I won't tell you how much, because saying a number makes it real, and I have spent five years keeping that number unreal. I'll tell you instead what it costs me: every cargo I take gets weighed against whether it draws attention. Every port I make, I calculate first whether a Harrow man might be watching. I pay cash. I use one name. I have no family listed anywhere, no property, no history a clerk could pull. Five years ago I built myself a life shaped exactly like a shadow, and it has worked, and I hate it, and I am very good at it.

The moon went behind cloud and the river went truly dark, and that's when I saw the lights.

Two lanterns, low on the water, coming upriver around Harker's Point—and under them, fainter, the shape of a boat moving against the current with the steady arrogant pace of a vessel that has never once worried about sandbars, because sandbars are a problem for people who don't own the dredging contracts. I knew her before I could read her lines. Shallow-draft side-wheeler, black hull, gold scrollwork at the bow. The *Magpie*. Harrow toll boat, working the night shift, which meant Dain was hunting after dark now, which meant something had changed.

I put the wheel over easy and slid the Kestrel toward the near bank, into the shadow of the willows, and shut her down to a whisper.

"Captain?" Tuck, in the doorway, rubbing sleep off his face. "What—"

"Toll boat." I kept my eyes on the lights. "Get below. If anyone boards, you're the only soul aboard and you're running her downriver to Corben's for your uncle."

"But you—"

"Am not aboard. Am I clear?"

He wasn't, entirely, but he went, because Tuck is a coward in the useful way—he believes whatever I tell him to believe, immediately and completely, and sometimes that saves lives.

The *Magpie* came on. I stood in the willow shadow with the wheel under my hands and my heart doing a slow heavy knock against my ribs, and I watched her pass, close enough that I could see the man at her rail with a lantern raised, sweeping the bank. Not searching for anything in particular. Just sweeping. Reminding the river who owned it.

Then, as she drew level—maybe eighty yards of black water between us—the lantern stopped.

Swung back.

Held.

I didn't breathe. I stood in the dark under the willows with my hand on the wheel and I thought, with a strange detached clarity: *this is how it ends, not with a bang but with a toll collector's curiosity,* and I was already calculating the channel behind me, the cut through the shoals at Miller's Eddy that nobody but me had ever run at night, the long desperate chance of outrunning a boat with a newer boiler—

The lantern dipped. A voice carried across the water, bored and young: "Nothin' there. Just willows."

The light swung away. The *Magpie* held her course upriver, her wheel beating the water in that unhurried proprietary rhythm, and I stood there counting my heartbeats until she was gone around the point and the sound of her had faded into the ordinary night noises of the Vell.

Then I sat down on the deck plates, because my legs had opinions of their own.

Here's the thing I learned in those ninety seconds, or rather the thing I already knew and got reminded of: fear doesn't make you careful forever. Fear wears out. Fear is a muscle, and I have been flexing mine for five years, and lately—lately when the lantern swings my way, some tired corner of me thinks, *let them come, let it be over, let me stop counting dollars and names and exits.*

That thought frightened me worse than the lantern did.

A person who stops being afraid of the Harrows is a person about to make a mistake, and mistakes on the Vell don't get second chances any more than sandbars do. So I got up, and I lit the lamp, and I took the wheel again, and I told myself the story I always tell myself: *you are invisible, you are careful, you are fine.*

The river ran dark and honest under the keel, and I lied to it all the way to dawn.

* * *

Dawn found us at the mouth of Miller's Creek, tying up at a fueling stage that consisted of a rickety platform, a woodpile, and an old man named Hobbes who charged fair prices and asked no questions, which made him the closest thing to a friend my lifestyle permitted.

"Ran the night, did you?" he said, watching me stack cordwood with the economy of long habit.

"Couldn't sleep."

"Hm." He handed down a chunk of bread and hard cheese wrapped in cloth, unasked, the way he did every visit, and pretended it was nothing, the way I did. "River's dropping fast. Another week, the low-water bars'll be bad as they get. You'll want to lay up somewhere warm before the ice."

"I'll lay up when the work lays off."

"That's what your type always says." He spat expertly over the rail. "Your type generally freezes, drowns, or marries. Pick one."

"I pick none."

"Suit yourself." But he said it comfortably, without heat, and I took my bread and cheese and my firewood and cast off, and the Kestrel turned her bow downriver toward Vellport and the lockbox with the seal on it, because it was nearly the first of the month, and the first of the month was payment day, and payment day was the whole reason I existed.

That was the plan, anyway. Run down to Vellport, deliver the patent medicine and the salt pork, pay the installment, vanish upriver again before anyone at the Harrow office could look at my face twice. Simple. Routine. The kind of run I could do asleep, and sometimes nearly had.

I was standing at the wheel an hour later, coming down through the wide slow water above Split Rock, when Tuck came up from below at a jog and grabbed the rail, pointing upstream.

"Captain. Smoke."

I saw it before he finished speaking—a smudge against the far bank, where the reeds grew thick along the shallows below the old ferry crossing. Not campfire smoke. Too thin, too steady, too deliberate. A signal fire, or the remains of one, tended by somebody who wanted to be found.

Or a trap. On the Vell, those are often the same thing.

I should have steered wide. Any sensible captain would have steered wide—kept to the channel, kept to the schedule, kept to the shadow-shaped life that had kept me alive for five years. The reeds were a mile off my course and none of my business, and the smart move, the safe move, the *me* move, was to watch the smoke until it fell astern and tell myself the river washes everything downstream eventually and none of it is yours to carry.

I looked at the smoke a long moment.

Then I swore, quietly and comprehensively, and put the wheel over.

The Kestrel sighed and leaned toward the reeds, and I remember thinking—not knowing, just thinking—that the river was about to stop being the only thing in my life that never lied to me.
