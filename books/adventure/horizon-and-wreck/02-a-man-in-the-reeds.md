## 2. A Man in the Reeds

The trouble with being good at something is that people expect you to keep doing it.

I'd been running the Vell for eleven years, and in that time I had learned exactly three things worth knowing: the river lies, the river remembers, and the river always wins eventually. Everything else—navigation, commerce, survival—was just the fine print on those three clauses.

So when I saw the heron standing wrong at Miller's Bend, I knew before I knew why.

Herons are honest birds. They stand where the fishing is good and they stand still, statues with opinions, and if you've watched them long enough you can read the water through them. This one was standing in water too shallow for fish, one foot lifted like it had second thoughts about the whole arrangement, and its head was cocked toward the reed bank instead of down at the current. Something had disturbed it. Something was still disturbing it.

I could have kept going. Let me be clear about that, because later—much later—I would lie awake running through all the versions of me who kept going. The Kestrel was light on fuel, I was behind schedule, and the stretch below Miller's Bend was notorious for snags after the spring floods. Any reasonable woman would have noted the heron, admired the heron, and let the heron mind its own business while she minded hers.

But there's a thing the river does to you, if you let it. It teaches you to notice. And once you've noticed, you can't unnotice, and pretending you didn't is just a slower way of carrying the weight.

I brought her in slow, backing the paddle wheel against the current until the Kestrel drifted stern-first toward the reeds, quiet as gossip. The morning fog hadn't burned off yet. It lay on the water in gray ropes, and the reeds came up out of it like the ribs of some drowned cathedral.

"Hello?" I said, because I'm an optimist in the way that drowning men are swimmers.

The reeds said nothing. The reeds are worse liars than the river, which is saying something—they always rustle when they're hiding something, like a child whistling past a broken window.

I found him ten feet in, tangled in the cattails with his boots caught in the root mat, face-down in six inches of water. He'd have been fully under except the roots held his hips up, so he lay at an angle like a plank someone had dropped off a dock. His right hand was above the waterline, clenched around something dark. His left arm trailed out behind him, and from the shoulder down it was the wrong color—the deep bruised purple of a man who has bled into himself instead of out.

I checked the bank first. Habit. A man shot and dumped in the reeds is a message, and messages usually come with a sender who lingers to see them delivered. The fog gave me forty feet of gray nothing and no movement in it. Good enough. Good enough is the best this river ever offers.

Getting him aboard was ugly. He was bigger than he looked—all that soaked wool and canvas—and dead weight is a liar's weight; it shifts and grabs and argues. I got an arm under his chest and hauled, and my back filed a formal complaint, and we went over the gunwale together in a tangle of limbs and river stink. He landed on my deck like cargo nobody claimed.

Up close he was worse. Sixty, maybe, or fifty lived hard enough to pass for sixty. Gray stubble, a face gone hollow at the cheeks, hands like knotted rope. And the wound—a hole high in his left side, punched through and through by the look of it, the exit stain blooming across his shirt like ink dropped in milk. Not fresh. A day old, maybe more. Which meant he'd been in the water or crawling along that bank since yesterday, keeping himself alive on spite and whatever the river gave him to drink.

Spite's a powerful medicine. I've dosed myself with it often enough to know.

I did what could be done, which wasn't much. Got the wet clothes off him, wrapped him in my spare blanket, propped him against the wheelhouse bulkhead where the stove's heat reached. My medical kit is a joke I tell myself—whiskey, a needle, thread boiled twice, a tin of carbolic salve going cloudy at the edges. I cleaned the wounds because leaving them dirty felt like a decision I couldn't live with, and sewed the entry side because it was bleeding again now that gravity had changed its opinion of him. He never woke for any of it. That should have worried me more than it did. I told myself it was exhaustion. I've always been generous with myself that way.

His right hand never opened. Even unconscious, even half-drowned, he held that dark thing against his chest like a mother holds a baby, and when I tried to ease it loose his fingers only tightened, and a sound came out of him—not words, just a low animal refusal, a sound like a door being barred from inside.

Fine. Keep your secrets. Everyone on this river has them, and most of them aren't worth the paper they're written on.

* * *

He woke near dusk, and the first thing he did was try to kill me.

To be fair, he was in no condition to succeed. He came up off the deck with a sound like tearing canvas, grabbed for my throat, and got about halfway there before the wound remembered itself and folded him double. I caught him under the arms and lowered him back down, which took longer than you'd think, because a dying man fighting you has a kind of desperate strength that healthy men should study.

"Easy," I said. "You're on the Kestrel. You're safe."

"Liar," he said, clear as a bell, and then his eyes rolled back and he was gone again.

Well. At least he was lucid when he insulted me.

I made coffee and sat with it while the fog came back in off the water, thicker now, folding the Kestrel into a private gray world with just me and my uninvited guest and the river talking to itself outside the hull. I thought about the arithmetic of the situation, because arithmetic is how I keep from thinking about anything else.

A man shot through the body and left in the reeds is a man somebody wanted dead. Men who want other men dead tend to check their work. Sooner or later—probably sooner, probably downstream, because the current goes where everything on this river goes—somebody was going to come looking for a body, and they were going to find mine sitting next to it, warm and identifiable and full of inconvenient opinions.

Every rule I had, every rule I'd built my life on like pilings under a house, said: turn him off at the next landing. Leave him at some mission hospital with a fake name and a coin in his hand and walk away clean. The river gives you problems; the landings give you places to put them down. That was the whole system. That was the architecture.

I looked at him lying there under my blanket, breathing in that shallow, hitching way that tells you the machine is running on fumes, and I heard myself say, out loud, to nobody, "Damn it."

Because here's the thing about rules. They work fine right up until they don't, and then you find out what you're actually made of, and I had spent eleven years not wanting to know the answer to that question. Anonymity isn't cowardice, I'd always told myself. It's craft. Nobody can hurt a woman who doesn't exist. No last name spoken aloud, debts paid in cash, favors owed to no one, history scrubbed off me like mud off a hull. It had kept me alive through years when being known would have gotten me killed.

But a man doesn't crawl out of a river holding onto something with both hands unless the something matters more than the dying. And I knew that look. I'd worn it myself, once, years ago, on a night I don't talk about, when I chose the door marked *survive* and let another door close behind me forever.

I wasn't going to make him die alone in the reeds. Whatever else I was, whatever I'd become to stay safe, I wasn't going to do that.

* * *

He surfaced properly near midnight, and this time when his eyes opened they stayed open, and they found me sitting by the stove, and they did the slow careful arithmetic of a man deciding whether the person in front of him is a threat, a rescuer, or a bill collector.

"Thirsty," he rasped.

I gave him water, small sips, my hand behind his head. He drank like a man who'd been negotiating with death over the price of it.

"You're on my boat," I said. "The Kestrel. I pulled you out of the reeds at Miller's Bend. You've got a bullet hole in you that a doctor would charge you a month's wages to look at, and I'm not a doctor, so between us we're making do."

His eyes moved around the cabin—the stove, the chart rack, the brass compass on its gimbal, the coffee pot—and came back to me. "Voss," he said.

It wasn't a question, but it landed like one.

"I didn't give you a name."

"Boats talk." The ghost of something crossed his face—not quite a smile, but the ground where a smile used to grow. "Dockmaster at Corben's Landing. Two seasons back. Said the Kestrel's master paid cash, tipped fair, and signed the ledger 'E. Voss' in handwriting like she was signing away a crime."

I set the cup down harder than necessary. "People remember too much on this river."

"People remember what matters," he said, and then coughed, and the cough went on too long, and when it finished there was blood on his lip, bright and thin, and we both pretended not to see it. "Tomas," he said, when he had breath again. "Reyes."

"Tomas Reyes." I tried the name out loud and found it fit nowhere in my memory. "Should I know you?"

"No." And then, quieter, watching me: "Not yet."

There are conversations that feel like card games, where every word is played face-up but you both keep counting the deck. This was becoming one. He knew my name. I didn't know his game. And somewhere downstream there was at least one bullet with his name on it and possibly more bullets with room on them for mine.

"Who shot you?" I asked.

"The question," he said, "is who didn't."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only honest one I've got tonight." His eyes were fever-bright but steady. "Ask me tomorrow. If I'm still—" He stopped. Started over, choosing the words like footholds. "You pulled me out. Most wouldn't have. Most would've seen the blood and the reeds and done their sums and kept steaming."

"I nearly did."

"I know. I saw the boat go past." He almost smiled then, for real, and it transformed him briefly into somebody younger, somebody who'd once laughed easily in rooms I'll never see. "Came back, though. That's the part I bet on."

"You bet on me coming back? From inside a reed bed?"

"From inside a reed bed, yes." His hand moved, slow as a crane cable, to the bundle under the blanket. The dark thing. Still clutched. Still barred. "Had to be somebody who'd come back. Had to be a riverwoman. Had to be you."

"Why me?"

"Because you run the Vell like you were poured out of it, and because you pay cash, and because a woman who signs a false name on every ledger in the territory is a woman who understands what it costs to be hunted." He paused, gathering. "And because you were the only boat on this stretch of water this week that the Harrows haven't bought."

The name went through the cabin like cold air through a cracked window. Harrow. The Syndicate. Toll boats and collectors and men in good coats with bad eyes, and a debt I carried like ballast, quiet and permanent, that I paid down in cash and never spoke of. Eleven years of careful, and this half-drowned stranger says the word *Harrow* to me in my own cabin like he's reading it off my manifest.

"How do you know what the Harrows have and haven't bought?"

"Because I've spent four months learning exactly what they buy," he said, "and what they burn." His grip shifted on the bundle. "Captain Voss. Elodie. I need to ask you for something, and I need you to hear all of it before you say no, because you're going to want to say no, and I understand why, and I'm asking anyway."

"Don't." I stood up. "Whatever it is, don't. I fished you out. I stitched you. Come dawn I'll take you as far as Millbrook and put you in front of a real doctor, and that's the whole of my charity for the season. I don't take packages. I don't carry messages. I don't get involved. Involvement is how women like me end up—"

"—invisible," he said, "or dead," and the word landed between us on the deck planks like a dropped tool. "I know. I've been invisible twenty years. Ask me how that worked out."

Outside, the river muttered against the hull. Somewhere upstream a night bird called, three notes, like a question asked twice and then regretted.

"Tomorrow," I said finally. "Sleep. If you're alive tomorrow, you can tell me whatever it is, and I promise nothing except that I'll listen. That's the biggest promise I make strangers."

He nodded, slow, and his eyes were already going, fever pulling him back under. But at the last edge of consciousness his hand found the bundle again and pressed it flat against his chest, and he murmured something I only caught half of—something about a daughter, something about *before the snow*—and then he was gone, breathing shallow, holding on.

I banked the stove and sat in the dark with the coffee pot going cold and did the arithmetic again, and this time the numbers wouldn't sit still. Because I'd seen his hand, and I'd seen what he held, and oilcloth wrapped tight around something flat and stiff doesn't hide what shape it is. Papers. Maybe a map. Maybe money. Maybe a warrant with my name already on it.

Doesn't matter, I told myself. Tomorrow you put him ashore at Millbrook and you steam away and you never think about Tomas Reyes again.

The river slid past in the dark, patient as a creditor, and I didn't believe a word of it.
