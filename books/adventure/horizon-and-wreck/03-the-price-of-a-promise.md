## 3. The Price of a Promise

He woke at dusk, and the first thing he did was try to bargain with me.

I'd spent the afternoon doing what I do best, which is pretending nothing unusual is happening. I ran the Kestrel downriver past Gull's Bend at a pace that would look lazy to anyone watching and was in fact threaded through three sandbars like a needle through cloth. I cooked rice. I checked my lines. I did not look at the man lying in my bunk more often than every ten minutes, which by my standards was practically constant attention.

The river gave me a gray evening, low clouds dragging their bellies on the water, and I used it—no lanterns lit until full dark, no smoke from the stove until I'd banked it twice. If Harrow men were poling the banks looking for a bleeding prospector, they'd see nothing but another boatwoman minding her own poor business.

When I finally brought broth to the stern cabin, Tomas Reyes's eyes were open and fixed on me with an intensity that should have warned me. Dying men get that look sometimes. Like they've found something worth spending their last coins on, and I was the only shop still open.

"You pulled me out," he said. His voice was gravel dragged through water. "Why?"

"You were blocking my channel." I set the bowl down. "Figured the fish deserved better."

A ghost of a smile moved under his beard. He tried to sit up, failed, tried again, and I put a hand on his chest—not gently—and pushed him back down. The wound in his side had soaked fresh through the padding. I'd sewn it as neat as I could with sail thread and river whiskey for disinfectant, but a bullet through the gut is not a project. It's a verdict.

"Lie still or die loud," I said. "Your choice."

"They'll come for this." His hand went to the oilskin packet strapped against his ribs, fingers working at the knot like a man praying with his hands. "The men who shot me. They'll come for it, and then they'll come for you, because you've seen my face."

"I've seen lots of faces. Faces are cheap."

"Not mine." He coughed, wet and long, and when he finished there was blood on his lip and something bright and terrible in his eyes. "Not anymore. Captain—I need you to listen. I don't have the time to be polite about it, so I'm going to be plain instead."

I should have gone back up top right then. That's the honest truth of it. Every instinct I've sharpened over fifteen years on the Vell told me to climb the ladder, raise the wheel, and let the man talk to the ceiling, because the moment you sit down and listen to a dying stranger, you've bought stock in him, and stock in other people is the one commodity that always loses value.

I sat down.

* * *

"My name is Tomas Reyes." He said it like he was laying cards on a table, one at a time. "Eleven years ago I walked out of Coldharbor with nothing but a bad reputation and a good pair of boots. I had a daughter there. Wren. She was six when I left. She'll be seventeen now, if the mountains have been kind, and they haven't been kind to anybody I know."

"I'm not a delivery service."

"No." He grinned, and the grin cost him; I watched the pain take him around the shoulders like a debt collector. "No, you're not. You're the woman who reads the Vell like a psalter. Don't look surprised. I've watched you run the Widow's Teeth at night. I've watched you take the Kestrel through channels that aren't channels. I've been on this river two months, captain, hiding in reeds and culverts, and a man learns things when he's hiding. I learned your boat. I learned your habits. And I learned that when the Harrow toll boat hails somebody, you're already three bends away."

"So you've been stalking me. Wonderful. That makes you either a customer or a corpse, and we've established which."

"Both, probably." He laughed, which turned into the cough again, and I held the cup to his mouth while he drank, hating myself for how naturally my hands did it. "Listen. In my pack—the oilskin. There's a map. Not a map of the river. A map of the high country above Coldharbor, the Serpentine Range, where the old silver claims were staked before the war. One vein in particular. My vein. I found it twenty years ago and I lost everything trying to hold it, and now I'm going to lose the rest, and the only thing I care about—" his hand closed over the packet, knuckles white, "—is that it doesn't die with me. And that my girl knows her father didn't die a thief."

"The passes close in what, five weeks?"

"Four. Maybe less. Early snows this year. The birds are flying low."

"The birds are always flying low when a man wants something done fast."

"Captain." He caught my wrist. His grip was astonishing for a gut-shot man, hot and dry and desperate. "I'm not asking you to go yourself. I'm asking you to carry it. Put it in the hands of Wren Reyes in Coldharbor before the snow seals the pass. That's all. One promise. One boat ride north."

One boat ride north. Up the Vell, past the toll stations, past the Syndicate locks, into the teeth of whatever was hunting him—all to deliver a dead man's parcel to a town I'd spent a decade avoiding because Harrow eyes are thick there.

"I don't make promises," I said. "Promises are anchors. I deal in fares and freight, cash on the barrelhead, and everybody walks away even."

"Then name your price."

"There isn't one."

"Silver." The word came out of him soft and shining. "Enough silver to buy the Kestrel a sister. Enough to pay off every debt you're pretending you don't have. I know what you owe them, Elodie Voss."

My hand was around his collar before I knew I'd moved. "Don't say that name."

"Which one? Elodie? Or Voss?" He wasn't afraid of me. That was the worst part. Dying men can't afford fear; it spends too much breath. "I told you. Two months in the reeds. I heard a lock-keeper call you Voss in Millbrook, and I watched you flinch like he'd struck you. A woman who flinches at her own name is a woman carrying weight. I carried the same kind for eleven years."

I let go of him. I went to the porthole and looked at the black water and the blacker sky and hated every single thing about the evening.

"How much do you actually owe them?" he asked quietly.

"That's not your business."

"No. But it could be your way out. That vein is real. I staked it, I sampled it, I hid the workings so well that eleven years of Harrow surveyors have walked over it like children playing at pirates. It's worth more than your debt. It's worth more than their whole toll franchise, maybe. Sell it. Trade it. Use it to buy your name back."

"And hand the Harrows the thing they shot you for? They'd pay me in lead and take the map off my body."

"Not if you sell it right. Not if—" Another coughing fit took him, longer this time, and when it passed he lay gray as driftwood, breathing in shallow sips. When he spoke again his voice had dropped to almost nothing, and somehow that was worse than the shouting. "Not if you keep the promise first. That's the order of it, captain. Wren first. Silver after. Do it out of order and it all turns to ash. I've seen it turn to ash."

"Why her?" I said. "Why does a map need a daughter?"

"Because it's hers." His eyes found mine and held them, and there was no fever-glaze in them at all, just a terrible clear patience. "Because I stole nothing and everyone in Coldharbor believes I did, and she grew up wearing that belief like a coat she couldn't take off. Because when I left I told her I'd send for her, and I never did, and a promise broken to a child is a debt that compounds. This is the interest, captain. This is me paying it. Through you, since I won't live to do it myself."

"I could just throw the packet in the river."

"You could." He nodded slowly. "And I'd die anyway, and you'd spend the rest of your life hearing my voice every time you passed a bend you could've turned at. I know that voice. I've lived with it eleven years."

* * *

I went topside after that and stood at the wheel while the Kestrel slid through the dark, letting the current argue its case. The river is honest in a way people aren't: it goes where the grade tells it, no matter what it feels about the destination. I've always envied it that.

Here's what I knew, laid out flat like charts on a table:

I knew the Harrows wanted that packet badly enough to shoot a man and leave him breathing, which meant they wanted him talking, which meant they thought he hadn't given it up yet. Sooner or later they'd trace him to my boat. Reeds hold footprints. Lock-keepers sell gossip cheaper than they sell passage.

I knew a dying man had spoken my full name aloud in my own cabin, and that the sound of it was still rattling around in my skull like a coin in a tin cup.

And I knew—if I'm going to be honest, and this chapter of my life seems determined to make me honest—that the money was real. Not the fantasy of it. The arithmetic. My debt to the Syndicate was a stone tied to my ankle, and I'd been swimming with it for six years, telling myself I liked the exercise. A vein like Reyes described wouldn't just clear it. It would end the game entirely. No more dodging toll boats. No more paying cash and giving no name. No more being nobody, which sounds like freedom until you notice nobody has nobody.

That's the trap they never warn you about, the one shaped exactly like salvation.

I went below near midnight with a lamp and a knife, meaning to settle it the simple way. Cut the packet off him, open it, look at what I was actually considering. Knowledge first. Sentiment later, if ever.

Tomas Reyes was asleep—or past sleep into something shallower, his breath whistling through the wound-seam, his face slack and yellow in the lamplight. Sixty years old, maybe. Hands like tree roots. Somewhere north of here a seventeen-year-old girl had those same hands, or would, and didn't know her father was alive until two months ago and dying now, and had spent eleven years believing the worst of him because the worst was easier to believe than abandoned.

I stood there with the knife for a long time.

Then I cut the packet free, and I didn't open it. I weighed it in my hand instead. It was lighter than a man's whole life and heavier than anything else on my boat, and I set it on the shelf beside the chart case, where I could see it from the wheel.

"I'm not promising," I told the sleeping man, because apparently I'd become someone who talks to unconscious strangers. "Hear me? I'm keeping the packet dry and the girl's name written down, and that's commerce, not compassion. If the Harrows board me, I'll feed it to the river and wish you luck in the next world."

His breath whistled on. Whether he heard me, I couldn't say.

* * *

Dawn came up the color of pewter, and with it came the fever.

I'd seen it coming the night before—the heat radiating off him like a stove, the mumbling—but by morning it had teeth. He called out names I didn't know. He called out Wren's name most of all, in a voice pitched to carry across a valley, and once, horribly, he called out *I'm sorry* in the same carrying voice, over and over, like a bell with a cracked clapper.

I did what I could, which was nothing much. Cool cloths. Broth he wouldn't take. Whiskey he would. I'm a fair hand at sewing canvas and a poor one at sewing men, and the seam in his side had started to weep a thin pink fluid that smelled wrong. River medicine says: when the wound smells wrong, the river's already decided.

Near noon he surfaced enough to know me, and he grabbed my sleeve with both hands.

"You'll take it to her." Not a question anymore. The fever had burned the bargaining clean out of him and left only the bone of it. "Swear it. Swear on your boat. Men swear on gods and mothers, but a boatwoman swears on her hull—that's the oath that holds."

"Tomas—"

"Swear it, and I can go. I've been holding on out of spite, captain. Spite is heavy. I'd like to set it down."

And God help me, I opened my mouth to refuse him again—to tell him I'd deliver the packet as freight, paid and receipted, but that promises were beyond my line—and what came out instead, in a voice I barely recognized, was:

"I swear it on the hull."

He exhaled then, long and slow, like a rope paying out, and something in his face unclenched that had been clenched for eleven years. "Good," he whispered. "Good. She likes honey in her tea. She bites her nails when she's figuring sums. Tell her—" and here the fever took him again, mid-sentence, dragging him under, and he never got to finish the sentence, and I have spent every hour since wondering how it ended.

* * *

By late afternoon I knew two things with river certainty.

First: Tomas Reyes would not see another dawn. The wound had turned, the fever owned him, and no doctor between here and Vellport would do anything but charge me for the confirmation.

Second: I had roughly four hours of daylight left to make a decision I'd been avoiding for two days, because the Harrow toll station at Stonechuck Lock lay four miles downstream, and lock-keepers telegraph ahead, and somewhere behind me were men with rifles who knew a prospector had been carrying a packet and had reason to believe a riverboat woman had fished him out of the reeds.

I stood at the wheel with the current pushing me toward Stonechuck and the promise pulling me north, and I understood that the two directions had just become the same direction. There was no delivering anything to Coldharbor without running the Syndicate's gauntlet first. There was no staying invisible with a dying man in my bunk and a dead man's word on my tongue.

I'd made a promise on my hull. I'd sworn the one oath that holds, to a man I'd known thirty-six hours, against every rule I'd built my life on.

Below deck, the fever-talk had stopped. I went down fast, heart hammering, and found him quiet, eyes half-open, watching me with that awful patience.

"Stonechuck," he rasped. "They'll ask at Stonechuck."

"I know."

"Tell them I died on your deck. It's nearly true. Let them waste a day dredging the channel for my body." A flicker of the old grin. "Give me to the river one more time, captain. It's the only inheritance I've got left to give."

I sat down beside him and took his root-hand in both of mine, because there was no one left to see and no point pretending otherwise.

"Hold on till dark," I said. "Then we'll run it together."

Outside, the light was failing over the Vell, and four miles downstream a lock-keeper was lighting his lamp, and I was about to learn exactly how expensive a promise could get.
