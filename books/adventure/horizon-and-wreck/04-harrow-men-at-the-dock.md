## 4. Harrow Men at the Dock

The Harrow Syndicate has a genius for bad timing. They never come when you're ready, only when you're tired, or broke, or — in my case that particular evening — kneeling in the bilge of your own boat with your arms elbow-deep in river water and a dying man's blood drying under your fingernails.

I heard the footsteps on the dock before I saw them. That's a thing the Vell teaches you if you listen long enough: every kind of walker has a rhythm. Fishermen shuffle. Pilgrims drag. Children run in bursts like sputtering engines. And Harrow men walk like the dock owes them money — heel-first, deliberate, spaced out so you know there are two of them before you ever turn around.

I wiped my hands on a rag that was already beyond saving and climbed up into the cabin light.

Tomas Reyes lay in the back berth, fevered down to something barely human, breathing in that wet, torn way that told me the bullet had gone where bullets shouldn't. I'd done what I could with needle and whiskey, which is to say I'd performed surgery the way a drunk performs a eulogy — sincerely, and without much hope. The oilskin packet sat wedged under the floorboard beneath the stove, wrapped in an old shirt that had once been white.

Two men stood at the head of my gangplank. The tall one wore a gray coat cut too fine for the mud of Millbrook's lower docks, and the short one wore a gun worn the way other men wear rosaries — constantly, and with genuine feeling.

"Evening," said the tall one. He had the voice of a man who'd practiced being pleasant in a mirror. "Captain Voss?"

And there it was. My name. Not *captain*, not *hey, you*, not the half-dozen false handles I keep stacked like spare fenders. My actual, ledger-listed, debt-carrying name.

"That depends who's asking," I said, "and what it costs."

"Harrow Syndicate." He produced a tin badge the way you'd produce a warrant, which it wasn't. Badges are for men who need permission. The Harrows have never needed permission from anyone but each other. "Corvus Dain. This is Merrick. We're looking for a thief."

"Aren't we all," I said. "This being a river town, you've got your pick. Start at the fish market and work outward."

Dain smiled. It was a good smile, professionally maintained, the kind that gets oiled every Sunday. "A specific thief. Man named Reyes. Tomas Reyes. Prospector. He may be injured." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat on a table. "He'll be carrying a packet. Oilskin. You seen him?"

Now, here's the thing about lying. People think it's about the words. It isn't. Words are the cheapest part. Lying is about your hands, your breathing, the angle of your shoulders, the two-second delay before you answer that makes the lie feel like memory instead of invention. I learned this from a man named Callum Breck, who lied to me beautifully for three years, so I had a very thorough education.

I let my eyes flick toward the river — just a flick, the involuntary glance of a woman doing arithmetic — and then back to Dain.

"Reyes," I said, tasting it. "Prospector. Gray beard? Half his left ear gone?"

Merrick shifted. That was interest. Good.

"He came through two nights back," I went on, "wanted passage down to Vellport. Couldn't pay. I don't run charity boats." I shrugged, and let the shrug carry the whole sermon: the world is hard, the river is harder, everyone pays or swims. "Last I saw him he was walking the towpath south, arguing with a mule that wasn't there. Fever, maybe. Or the usual prospector madness."

"Which way's south?" Merrick asked. His voice was gravel soaked in oil.

"Toward Vellport, like I said. You could ask at the toll bridge. They log foot traffic." I said it helpfully. Helpfulness is the best disguise a lie can wear; nobody searches a gift horse's mouth.

Dain studied me for a long moment. I let him. I even yawned, which was only half theater — I'd been up since before dawn hauling a bleeding stranger out of the reeds, and my body was filing formal complaints.

"You're Elodie Voss," he said again, not a question this time. "You owe the Syndicate four hundred and twelve dollars."

My stomach did something slow and cold, like a stone dropped through lake ice. "That number sounds high."

"The number is the number. Interest compounds weekly." He said it the way a priest says *ashes to ashes*. "We hear things, Captain. We hear you've been running quiet runs, no manifests, cash only. We hear you know the Vell better than our own pilots do. A woman in your position should be careful about the company she keeps."

"I keep company with catfish and sandbars," I said. "Neither of them gossips."

"No," Dain agreed. "But harbormasters do. And doctors. And ferry clerks." He stepped one boot onto my deck — onto *my* deck, which is a violation older than law — and looked around the pilothouse with the unhurried appetite of a man appraising livestock. "Nice boat. Paid off?"

"She's mine," I said, which was true in the way that a hostage is free to walk around the room.

"Mm." He touched the rail, ran a thumb along the grain. Then, casually, terribly: "Mind if we look below?"

Every instinct I own stood up and screamed at once. Below deck, ten feet from where his boot rested, a man was dying with a hole in him, and under the stove was a packet that might as well have been lit with signal flares.

"Below deck," I repeated, "is where I keep my privy, my mold, and my late husband's collection of obscene woodcarvings. You're welcome to all three, but I charge a viewing fee, and I doubt the Syndicate reimburses."

Something moved behind Dain's pleasant face. Not anger. Recalculation. Men like him don't get angry at resistance; they price it.

"How much?" he asked.

"For the privy, nothing. For the carvings, five dollars. They're art."

He laughed — one dry note, like a coin dropping on a counter — and stepped back off my deck. "You're funny, Captain Voss. I like funny people. They make the work go faster." He tipped two fingers to his hat brim. "If Reyes comes back through — if he's hurt, if he needs help, anything — you'll send word to the Harrow office. There's a finder's fee. Generous."

"And if I don't?"

"Then the four hundred and twelve becomes five hundred by Thursday, and we stop being polite about collections." He smiled again, that mirror-practiced smile. "Pleasure doing business."

They walked away heel-first, in rhythm, like the dock owed them money and they intended to collect.

* * *

I gave them ten minutes, then twenty, watching from the dark of the pilothouse as their lanterns shrank up the levee road toward town. Then I went below and put my ear to the back berth.

Tomas was still breathing. Barely. Each breath came with a sound like a boot pulling out of deep mud. His eyes were open a slit, fixed on me, and there was awareness in them now — the fever had burned off enough delirium to leave the worst part behind: understanding.

"They came," he rasped.

"They came."

"You told them—"

"South, toward Vellport, arguing with a ghost mule." I sat on the edge of the berth. Up close he smelled of river rot and blood and the particular sweetness of a body starting to fail. "You want to tell me why the Harrow Syndicate sends two collectors after a prospector with empty pockets? Because in my experience, they don't spend shoe leather on men who owe them nothing. And you don't owe them nothing, do you, Tomas Reyes. You owe them something they can't put a number on."

His mouth worked. Water, I gave him. He drank like it was a sacrament.

"It's mine," he whispered. "What they want. It's mine, and I found it fair and square, and they—" A cough took him, long and tearing, and when it released him he was smaller somehow, folded into himself like wet paper. "Promise me. The girl. Coldharbor. Wren."

"We're not doing this again tonight."

"Promise." His hand came up — trembling, yellow-nailed, absurdly strong — and closed around my wrist. "You lie good, captain. I watched you do it. Lie to them all you want. Just don't lie to me."

I have been lied to by experts. I have been lied to so well I built a life inside the lie and called it freedom. So believe me when I say I know exactly what it means when a dying man asks you for the one thing you're afraid to give.

"I'm not promising anything," I said quietly, "because promises are how people like us get killed. But the packet stays dry, and I don't hand anything to men in gray coats. That much I can say without choking on it."

It wasn't enough for him. I could see it wasn't. But the fever pulled him back under before he could argue, and I sat there a while longer than I needed to, listening to that terrible breathing, wondering when exactly I'd become the sort of person who hid fugitives under her stove.

* * *

I didn't sleep. Instead I did what I always do with trouble: I made tea and took inventory.

Trouble inventory, that night: One, the Harrows knew Reyes's name and mine, which meant someone upstream had talked — a ferry clerk, a dockhand, some pair of eyes I'd never see coming. Two, Dain hadn't searched the boat, which meant either he believed me or he was saving the search for when he could do it properly, with more men and less courtesy. Three — and this was the one I kept circling back to, the splinter in the mind — he'd quoted my debt to the dollar. Four hundred and twelve. Not "around four hundred." Not "your outstanding balance." Four hundred and twelve dollars, current through Tuesday's interest.

The Harrows keep books. Of course they keep books. But knowing a number that precisely means someone opened the book recently. Someone looked at my page. Which meant I wasn't background noise anymore, one of a hundred small debtors floating down the Vell. I was a page. Pages get turned.

I stood in the pilothouse with my tea going cold and looked at the black water sliding past, and I thought about running. It's the honest thing to admit. I thought about casting off right then, lights doused, running the Kestrel downriver through channels only I know, and vanishing into the delta country until spring. I've done it before. Invisibility is a skill like any other, and I am very, very good at it.

But there was a man in my berth whose lungs were filling up, and a packet under my stove that two armed men wanted badly enough to walk the docks at night, and somewhere north a girl named Wren was presumably going about her life with no idea that her father was dying on a stranger's boat with her name in his mouth.

Running would mean leaving all of it behind. Including, I noticed, the man himself. And I found — sitting there in the dark, furious at myself for noticing — that I couldn't quite make the arithmetic work anymore. The numbers wouldn't line up. Every column ended with a dying prospector and a promise I hadn't made.

"Damn it, Tomas," I said to the empty pilothouse. "You couldn't have washed ashore on somebody else's boat."

The river said nothing. Rivers never do. That's why I liked them.

* * *

Near midnight, I heard boots on the dock again and my heart went into my throat — but it was only Old Marta, the net-mender, shuffling home drunk and singing to herself, bless her. I watched her pass and understood something with a clarity that felt almost physical:

They'd be back. Maybe tomorrow, maybe in a week, but they'd be back, and next time Dain wouldn't stand at the top of my gangplank making conversation. Next time there'd be four of them, or six, and Merrick's gun would come off his hip, and the search would happen whether I charged a viewing fee or not. Men like Corvus Dain don't lose interest. They lose patience, which is worse.

So I had until then. A day, maybe two, possibly less. Enough time to move a dying man, or move a packet, or move myself — but not enough time to do all three well, and not nearly enough time to figure out which one I was willing to abandon.

I checked Tomas's bandages. The bleeding had slowed, which I chose to read as hope rather than arithmetic. Under the stove, the oilskin packet waited, patient as a debt.

And far up the levee road, past the last of the dock lanterns, a gray coat caught the moonlight — standing still, at the end of the pier, looking back at my boat.

Just standing. Just looking.

Then it turned, and was gone, and I stood in the dark of the Kestrel counting the hours until dawn, when everything would change whether I was ready or not.
