## 6. Burning the Ledgers

I have burned a great many things in my life, and I want it on the record that I am good at it.

Ledgers, mostly. Not mine—mine are immaculate, kept in a cipher of my own devising that would take a Harrow accountant a decade to crack, and even then he'd only find what I wanted him to find. Other people's ledgers. A man in Gilt Row once paid me forty dollars to burn his gambling debts, and I did it with such ceremony he wept. A boarding house landlady in Sallow Bend paid me to burn her late husband's letters, all of them addressed to a woman who was not her, and I did that too, and she made me coffee afterward like I'd done her a kindness instead of a crime.

Fire is honest work, if you do it right. Fire doesn't keep copies. Fire doesn't testify. Fire takes a thing with weight and history and consequence and turns it into heat and a smell, and then the wind takes the smell, and then there's nothing, and nothing is the most valuable commodity on the Vell River. I deal in nothing the way other captains deal in lumber.

So when Tomas Reyes died at dawn with his hand still curled around an oilskin packet, and I pried that packet loose from fingers gone stiff as mooring line, and carried it aft to my cabin, and set it on my desk beside the lamp—I already knew how this story went. I'd burn it. That was always the ending. The map goes into the stove, the stove eats it, and by the time the Kestrel rounds Harker's Bend there is no map, no promise, no dead prospector's daughter waiting in Coldharbor for a delivery that will never come. There is only Elodie, captain of nothing in particular, invisible again, safe again.

That was the plan. I want you to understand that I had a plan, because what actually happened makes me look like a woman without one.

* * *

The stove in my cabin is a squat iron thing I salvaged off a wrecked packet boat years ago, and it has eaten everything I've ever asked it to eat. I fed it kindling first, got a proper bed of coals going, because if you're going to destroy something you should destroy it thoroughly. Half-burned evidence is worse than evidence. Any Harrow man who found a corner of that oilskin with a legible landmark on it wouldn't need the rest—he'd just need patience and a gunboat, and the Harrows have both in surplus.

I sat down across from the stove with the packet on my knees and gave myself a moment to be sensible about it.

The oilskin was wrapped twice and tied with waxed cord, the knots the kind a man ties when he expects other people to be looking for them—complicated, deliberate, the work of someone who had time to think while bleeding. I cut the cord with my belt knife rather than untie it, which felt appropriately final, and unrolled the oilskin, and there it was.

The map.

It wasn't what I expected. I don't know what I expected—treasure maps in the storybooks are all flourishes and sea monsters, X marks the spot, here be dragons. This was a working document. It was drawn on the back of a surveyor's linen sheet, the old paper yellowed and soft at the folds, and it was dense. Rivers drawn in blue ink with depths marked in fathoms. Ridgelines in brown. And everywhere, crammed into every margin, notes in a small, cramped, patient hand—the handwriting of a man who wrote slowly because he knew his writing might be the only part of him that survived.

I recognized some of the landmarks before I understood that I recognized them. That's the thing about knowing a river the way I know the Vell—you stop noticing that you know it. But there it was: the Split Widow, drawn exactly as she splits, two channels around a gravel island shaped like a drowned horse's head. The leaning pine at Miller's Eddy, marked with its lean angle, fifteen degrees east, which is correct, because I've steered past that tree four hundred times and it leans fifteen degrees east and a little more every spring. He'd drawn the river from Coldharbor to the headwaters, and then he'd drawn what came after the river—the high country, the passes, the glacier shelf—and those parts were keyed to things I didn't know. A cairn "where the stone sings." A notch in the skyline "like a bitten ear." Bearings taken from a saddle I couldn't place.

And in the lower corner, in that same patient hand, a single line:

*For Wren. She'll know why the river matters.*

I read that line maybe six times. Then I stood up, walked to the stove, opened the door, and stood there like an idiot with the map in my hands while the heat rolled out against my face.

Here is what I told myself, standing there: *You owe him nothing. You never took the bargain. He offered, you refused, he died anyway, and a refusal is a wall, not a door.* All true. Here is what else I told myself: *A dead man's map is a dead man's problem. You didn't shoot him. You pulled him out of the water, which is more than the river would have done, and the river doesn't owe anybody anything either.* Also true. And here is the third thing, the quiet one, the one I told myself last and lowest, almost under my breath: *If they find it on your boat, they will kill you for it.*

That one did most of the persuading. Fear is a better arsonist than principle. I've built a whole life on that observation.

I held the map over the open stove door. The heat came up and moved the paper's edge, just barely, a flutter, like the map was breathing. Like it was deciding something.

And I thought about the Harrow men on my dock yesterday, the tall one with the pleasant voice asking about "a thief with a packet," and how his eyes had gone over my boat the way a butcher looks at a carcass—not hostile, just inventory. I thought about how they'd be back, because collectors always come back; a debt isn't a visit, it's a weather system. I thought about the number. Four hundred and twelve dollars, plus interest accruing at a rate the Harrows had invented specifically for people like me, compounding monthly, so that paying it was like bailing a boat with a hole in the hull. Six years I'd been bailing. Six years since I signed my name to a manifest I shouldn't have signed, for cargo I shouldn't have carried, for a captain whose name I still can't say out loud without tasting copper.

And then I thought the thought. The bad one. The one I'm not proud of, and I'm telling you about it anyway because this chapter is called honesty whether I like it or not.

I thought: *This map is worth money.*

Not to me. To someone. To the Harrows, who wanted it badly enough to put men on the water and bullets in a prospector's back. To any of a dozen mining concerns who'd pay real coin for a proven vein. Silver in these mountains wasn't rumor anymore—it was arithmetic—and a map like this, in the right room, with the right handshake—

Four hundred and twelve dollars. Plus interest. Plus the six years of looking over my shoulder, the tolls paid, the routes bent, the cargoes I'd refused and the ones I hadn't. One envelope of paper, handed to Corvus Dain or whatever gray suit he answered to, and I walk away clean. No debt. No Syndicate. No more calculating every passenger's eyes for the glint of an informer. I could be a captain again instead of a fugitive with a boat.

The fire was right there. The money was right there. Both exits from the same burning room.

I closed the stove door.

* * *

I want to be clear about what happened next, because it wasn't heroism. I've read enough of myself in other people's stories to know how this gets romanticized later, so let me ruin it now: I did not keep the map out of loyalty to a dead man I'd known for less than a day. I did not keep it for the daughter, whose face I couldn't have picked out of a crowd, whose existence was a name on a scrap of delirium. I did not keep it because of some grand feeling about promises.

I kept it because I couldn't make my hands let go.

That's the truth, stripped of varnish. I stood at that stove with the map extended over the coals and my arms simply declined the instruction. Some part of me—the part that has been running calculations since I was nineteen years old, the part that survived the trial and the debt and the man who left me holding the bill—some part looked at the fire and said, *not yet,* and my body obeyed it the way a crew obeys a captain, instantly and without discussion.

I sat back down. I re-rolled the map along its original creases, careful as folding a flag. I put it back in the oilskin. And then I spent a long time—longer than I'll admit—looking for a hiding place.

Under the floorboard where I keep my cash? First place anyone looks. Inside the mattress? I've searched enough stowaways' beds to know mattresses are furniture, not vaults. In the coal bin? Damp, and coal dust tells stories. I ended up doing something I'd never done before: I hid it inside another lie. There's a chart tube above my berth, sealed, labeled in faded ink *VELL RIVER — LOWER REACHES — SURVEY 1871 — SUPERSEDED.* Nobody steals a superseded survey. It's the paper equivalent of a dead man, and dead men don't get robbed. I slid the oilskin in among the dusty charts, resealed the cap, and hung it back on its peg, and it looked so innocent up there that I almost believed it myself.

Then I went up on deck, because my cabin had gotten too small to hold me and my conscience both.

* * *

Morning was coming up the color of tin. Mist on the water, the good thick kind that hides a boat from anything slower than a telegraph. We were tied up at a private mooring below Harker's Bend—a snag of willows and rotted pilings I use when I don't want to be a person, just a shape among shapes. Tomas lay in the wheelhouse under a tarp, because the ground was frozen too hard to dig and the river was no place for him yet. I'd need to bury him somewhere with soil, somewhere off the Syndicate's sightlines, and I'd need to decide that soon, because a body keeps like fish and no better.

I stood at the rail and drank coffee that had gone bitter and thought about compromises.

Here's a thing nobody tells you about compromise: it doesn't arrive all at once, wearing a black hat. It arrives in installments. First installment: I hauled a drowning stranger aboard against every rule I live by. Second: I listened to his dying offer instead of throwing his packet straight in the current. Third: I kept the map through one night. Each one small. Each one reasonable. Each one a plank laid over the gap between the woman I pretend to be and the woman who, I was beginning to suspect, had been waiting under the pretense this whole time like water under ice.

Because here's what the sensible version of me—the version that had already written the eulogy and the excuse—kept circling back to: I could still sell it tomorrow. Nothing was decided. The map in the superseded survey tube was a door left unlocked, not a door walked through. Sell it to the Harrows, clear the debt, done. Or sell it to the assayer's office in Vellport—they paid finder's fees on proven claims, legitimate, aboveboard—or auction it quietly to one of the mining companies that weren't Syndicate-owned, of which there were perhaps two, both of whom hated the Harrows with the pure heat of competition.

Every road from that map led through money. Every one except the road Tomas had asked me to take, which led through Coldharbor, through winter, through a girl who'd learn her father died before he reached her, carrying a promise from a stranger who'd never promised anything.

The sensible roads all ran downhill. That's how you recognize them.

"Captain?"

Marta, my deckhand, at the top of the companionway, wrapped in two coats and a scarf she'd knitted herself out of three uglier scarves. Marta had been with me two years and asked no questions, which is why I kept her, and noticed everything, which is why I sometimes regretted it.

"We cast off within the hour," I said. "Millbrook by dark."

"The stove was going early," she said. Neutral as a tide table. "Cold night."

"Cold night," I agreed.

She nodded and went forward to check the lines, and I stood there understanding that she knew exactly what I'd been doing at that stove at five in the morning, and had decided, in her wordless way, that it was none of her business. That's the economy we run on, Marta and I. Silence for silence. It works. It's also, I realized with a small internal lurch, exactly the arrangement I had with the Harrows, scaled down to human size. Everybody keeping everybody's secrets until somebody decides the secret is worth more than the silence.

I tipped the cold coffee over the rail and watched the mist take it.

* * *

We cast off at seven with the mist still holding, and I took her down the middle channel past the Split Widow because the mist made the widow's gravel bar invisible and I didn't need to see it—I could feel it, the way you feel your own teeth. Marta worked the stern line. The engine talked its low talk to itself. Somewhere behind us, upstream, the Harrow toll boat would be tying up at Harker's landing, and some clerk would be writing *Kestrel — Voss — passage noted, toll unpaid, follow up,* and I felt that entry like a hand between my shoulder blades.

Millbrook by dark. Millbrook meant a surveyor named Augustin Ferro, if half of what I'd heard was true and a quarter of what I'd heard was survivable—a man who used to draw these mountains for a living before the mountains drew blood on his reputation. I needed eyes that could read the high-country notations, the bitten-ear notch and the singing stone, because I could read water the way priests read scripture but up there the map turned foreign on me, and a map you can't fully read is just paper with ambitions.

Which meant, I realized somewhere around the second bend, that I had already decided. Not with the dramatics of the stove—no fire, no vows—but with the quiet treachery of a course laid in. You don't hire a surveyor to help you burn a map. You hire a surveyor to help you read one.

I told myself I was only gathering information. Selling price versus delivery risk, a captain weighing cargo. I told myself the door was still unlocked.

The mist burned off around noon, and the sun came through hard and white on the water, and ahead of us the mountains stood up along the horizon with their shoulders already dusted—the first snow up there, weeks ahead of schedule, the passes thinking about closing early this year. I looked at them longer than a sensible woman looks at mountains.

In the chart tube above my berth, a dead man's handwriting waited patiently in the dark, addressed to a girl I'd never met, and I had not burned it, and I was not going to, and the part of me that had survived six years by wanting nothing had just, without ceremony or consent, started wanting something.

Millbrook by dark. God help me, I hoped the surveyor was home.
