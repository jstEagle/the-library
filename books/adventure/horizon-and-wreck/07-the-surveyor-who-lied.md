## 7. The Surveyor Who Lied

There are three kinds of men who end up in a river town like Millbrook: the ones passing through, the ones hiding out, and the ones too stubborn to admit they're already dead. I had spent six years perfecting the second category and was well on my way to the third.

My office—if you could call a rented room above the feed store an office—smelled of lamp oil, mildew, and the particular despair of paper that hadn't been touched in a season. On the desk lay the ruins of my trade: a brass alidade with a cracked level vial, a set of dividers gone rusty at the hinge, and a surveyor's chain coiled in its canvas bag like a snake that had given up on ambition. Once, those instruments had mapped forty miles of the Serrano Range for the Territorial Land Office. Once, my signature had carried weight. Now it carried about as much as a drunk's promise, which is to say I still signed things, but nobody believed them.

I was drinking coffee that had achieved a chemical state somewhere between tar and regret when I heard her boots on the stairs.

I want to be precise about this, because precision was once my religion: I heard the boots *before* I heard anything else. Two steps up, then a pause—the pause of a person reading the name painted on my door, or what remained of it. A. FERRO, SURVEYING & LAND ASSESSMENT, though the paint had flaked so that it read more honestly as A. FERRO, SURVEYING & LAND ASS. There are mornings I consider fixing it. There are other mornings I think it's the most truthful sign in the territory.

The knock came. Three raps, unhurried.

"Come in," I said, and did not get up, because getting up suggested hope, and hope was a currency I'd stopped carrying after the bankruptcy.

She came in the way weather comes in—not fast, but total. A woman perhaps thirty, dark-haired, with the kind of face that doesn't bother being pretty because it's busy doing something more useful. River-tanned hands. Eyes that went around my room the way a customs man goes through your luggage: everything noted, nothing commented on. She wore a boat coat with the salt faded out of it and moved like someone who had learned, somewhere expensive, exactly how much space she was entitled to take up. The answer, from the way she stood just inside the door, was: as little as possible.

"Augustin Ferro?" she said.

"That depends," I said, "on who's asking and what they've heard."

"Heard you were the best surveyor in the Serranos." Her voice was low and even, a voice built for saying things once. "Also heard you were run out of Vellport for falsifying a claim report."

"Well," I said. "At least you've heard both halves."

"I'm looking for the first half."

"And I'm looking for a reason to believe you'll find it interesting. Most people who climb these stairs want the second half. They want to know if the stories are true." I leaned back until the chair complained. "They usually are. That's the trouble with me. I'm honest about my dishonesty. It's the only honesty I've got left."

Something flickered across her face—not amusement exactly, but the place where amusement keeps its coat checked. "Elodie Voss," she said. "I captain the Kestrel. Out of Vellport."

"I know the Kestrel. Or I know *of* her. Forty-foot stern-wheeler, shallow draft, patched boiler. You run cargo and passengers and whatever else pays, and you do it under a name short enough that nobody asks about the rest." I watched her take that. "Don't look surprised. I surveyed the landings on the upper Vell before you were running her. I know every dock between here and the divide, including the ones that aren't supposed to exist."

That landed. I saw it land—a small settling of her weight, a recalibration behind the eyes. Good. It's useful when a client understands early that they've hired more than a pair of legs to carry instruments uphill.

"You've been up in the high country," she said. Not a question.

"Six years ago. Three seasons of it. The whole eastern wall of the range, from the treeline to the ice." I turned my coffee cup a quarter-turn on the desk, a habit I'd developed to keep my hands from doing anything more revealing. "Why does a riverboat captain care about the high country? Last I checked, boats don't climb."

For a long moment she didn't answer, and I want to tell you that I read her silence like a contour map—and I did, mostly—but there was one line in it I misread, and that misreading cost me something later, so let me at least record it accurately here. I thought her silence was calculation. It was partly calculation. But underneath it was grief, and grief is the one terrain feature no surveyor ever learns to map properly, because it moves.

"I've come into possession of some documents," she said finally. "Land documents. Survey notes. Old ones, and old-fashioned in their notation—bearings taken off landmarks rather than instrument stations. I can read maybe half of it. The half I can't read is the half that matters."

"Show me."

"No."

I raised an eyebrow. She met it without blinking.

"Not yet," she said. "First I need to know you can be bought. Properly bought. Not rented."

"There's a difference?"

"A rented man sells his morning. A bought man sells his silence. I need the second kind, Ferro, and I need to know the price before I show you a single line of ink." She stepped closer to the desk, and now I caught the detail I'd missed when she came in: the way her left hand stayed near her coat pocket, casual as breathing, the way a card player's hand stays near her chips. "Because the people who don't want those documents read will pay more than I can. That's the shape of it. So before you see them, I want your number, and I want to know whether it holds against theirs."

Now, in fairness to myself, I should say that everything in me—the part that had been ruined, the part that had learned caution the way a dog learns a fence—told me to name an impossible figure and let her walk. Six years of quiet. Six years of being nobody, drinking bad coffee above a feed store, alive by virtue of being beneath everyone's interest. Every scar I owned said: *turn her away.*

But there was another thing in me, older than the scars, and it sat up when she said *survey notes* the way a hound sits up at a whistle. Because I knew—I knew before I saw a single page—what kind of notes a dying prospector writes when he can't trust instruments or witnesses. I'd invented half those tricks myself, back when I taught field methods to green surveyors. Bearings off split rocks and lightning-scarred pines. Elevations keyed to bird nests and avalanche scars. The private shorthand of men who expected to die before anyone else read their work.

And I knew, too, whose work it might be. There weren't many men mapping the eastern wall six years ago. There were three of us, and one of us was dead, and one of us was me, and the third—

Well. Let's not get ahead of the story.

"Two hundred dollars," I said. "Half now, half on delivery of whatever it is you actually want delivered. Plus expenses. Plus—" I held up a finger, "—one condition of my own."

Her eyes narrowed a fraction. "Which is?"

"When we're done, whatever happens, you tell me the truth about where those notes came from. Not the version you give the harbormaster. The real one. I've spent six years eating other people's lies, Captain Voss. I'm not going to eat mine blind."

She studied me for a long moment, and I had the strange sensation of being surveyed myself—of standing on a hillside while someone ran a level line across me and found me wanting in several elevations. Then she nodded, once, and pulled a folded oilskin packet from inside her coat, and set it on the desk between us with the care of a woman laying down a live grenade.

"Open it," she said.

* * *

I opened it the way I open all documents now: slowly, and with a certain professional dread.

Inside was a single sheet of good rag paper, folded twice, drawn in iron-gall ink gone brown at the edges. And I will confess something here that I have never said aloud: when I unfolded it and saw the hand, my chest did something complicated and painful, like a chain snapping taut after years of slack.

I knew the hand. Of course I knew the hand. T.R., block letters, the R with the leg kicked out like it was trying to leave the alphabet. Tomas Reyes had lettered his field books the same way, back when he was a claim agent's assistant learning surveying off borrowed manuals, and I had teased him about that R for two entire seasons. He'd kick it harder every year out of spite.

The map itself was beautiful in the way that desperate things are beautiful. No grid. No instrument stations. Instead: a river of small annotations climbing the eastern wall. *Split rock with birch, bearing 44° true.* *Cairn below the black couloir, three stones only.* *Where the glacier tongue breaks, follow the medial moraine to the horn.* Distances in paces, corrected for slope in a margin note. Elevations guessed from boiling points—he'd carried a thermometer and known the trick, which meant somebody had taught him, and I had a sick suspicion who.

And at the top right corner, in smaller letters, almost apologetic: a notation I recognized because I had *invented* it. A triangle with a dot, meaning *verified by sight but not by chain* — and beside it, a second mark I didn't recognize, a little hook like a fisherman's curve, repeated four times down the sheet.

"What's this?" I asked, tapping the hook.

Voss leaned in. Close enough that I could smell woodsmoke and river water on her. "You tell me. That's why you're expensive."

I traced it without touching the paper. Four occurrences, each at a point where the route changed character—treeline to scree, scree to moraine, moraine to ice. "It's a warning symbol," I said slowly. "Private notation. In the old Spanish field books it means *peligro*—danger, go carefully. But four times, at each transition..." I sat back. "Captain, whoever drew this wasn't marking danger. He was marking *sequence*. These are the four places where a man carrying heavy silver would be visible from below. He's telling whoever follows him: at each of these points, assume you are seen."

Silence. Outside, a dray cart rattled past the window, and somewhere below a feed-store clerk shouted at a mule with genuine feeling.

"You recognize the ground," Voss said. It wasn't a question either. She was having a day full of statements dressed as questions.

"I could draw that route from memory," I said, "and I haven't looked at it in six years. That's the Blackspire wall. The vein, if there is one, sits in a quartz dike above the second moraine—everyone always assumed the Reyes showing was on the western spur, which is why everyone who went looking died on the western spur." I heard my own voice go flat and careful, the voice I used for delivering bad news about property lines. "The route on this map is real. It's also a killer. The scree field alone has taken four men I know of. And the glacier corridor at the top only opens during calving season, when the meltwater undercuts the seracs—for a few weeks, maybe less. Miss the window and the pass seals until spring. Get caught in it when it closes early, and you seal with it."

"So it can be done."

"It can be *attempted*. Those are different verbs, Captain, and I've watched men confuse them at altitude." I folded the map along its original creases, precisely, because handling another man's desperation roughly felt like a sin. "Who drew this?"

"You know who drew this."

"Tomas Reyes," I said. The name came out of me like a splinter coming out sideways. "Yes. I know who drew this. I taught him half the notation myself."

And there it was—the thing I'd been circling since she walked in, the thing I'd hoped wouldn't surface, surfacing. Voss went very still. Stillness, in her, was clearly the loudest thing she did.

"You *taught* him," she said.

"Field methods. Years ago, when he was working claims out of Coldharbor and needed to read and write surveys properly. He was a quick study and a stubborn one. He wanted to understand every line on his own claim papers, because—" I stopped. Because he'd been cheated before, and knew it, and swore it would never happen again. That was what he'd told me, over a fire, with the particular bitterness of a man holding a document he couldn't argue with. "Because he didn't trust the men who wrote them," I finished.

"And do you know why he might feel that way?"

"Captain." I put the map down and looked at her straight, because there are limits to how long a liar can stand in a room with a truth. "You came here because you need a surveyor who knows the Blackspire wall. Fine. I'm the best one available, and I include the dead in that assessment. But you should know what you're buying, so let me save us both some months of discovery." I took a breath, and it tasted like six years of bad coffee. "Three seasons I surveyed that range for the Land Office. On the last one, I filed a report on the Reyes claim. My report said the showing was worthless—that the assays were salted, that the quartz was barren. It was a lie. I wrote it because I was paid to write it, and I was paid by people whose names I'd rather not say in a room with thin walls, but you already know whose they are, or you wouldn't be hiding that packet in your coat like it bites."

Voss didn't move. Didn't blink. Just watched me with those customs-officer eyes while I committed suicide in front of her.

"The claim wasn't barren," I said. "I knew it wasn't. I'd seen the samples myself. But the Harrow Syndicate wanted the ground, and they wanted it quietly, and a disgraced surveyor with debts is a cheap instrument. So I signed, the claim was voided, Reyes lost everything, and I took my money and my shame to Vellport, where I discovered that shame spends poorly." I spread my hands. "That's the merchandise, Captain. A man who sold a prospector his life's work for less than you're offering me now. If you still want to hire me, I'll earn every dollar, because I owe that mountain a debt I can't survey my way out of. But decide with your eyes open."

I expected anger. I'd earned anger; I've had practice receiving it. What I got instead was worse. She looked at me for a long moment, and then she laughed—one short, unlovely bark of a laugh, with no humor in it anywhere.

"You know what I was going to do?" she said. "I was going to lie to you. Somewhere in the next hour, I had a story ready about where the map came from—a clean story, a safe story. And now I don't need it, because you handed me the ugliest thing in your pockets before I asked." She shook her head slowly. "Do you know how rare that is, Ferro? Most people show you their best face and make you dig for the rest. You just dug for yourself."

"Consider it a professional courtesy among gravediggers."

"Don't joke yet." She picked up the packet and held it a moment, weighing it—or weighing something else entirely. When she spoke again, her voice had dropped into a register I would learn to dread and rely on in equal measure, the register she used for the true things. "Tomas Reyes is dead. He died on my boat three days ago, shot twice and half-drowned, and the last thing he asked me was to carry that map to his daughter in Coldharbor before winter closes the passes. The Harrows want it. They killed him for it, or they'll wish they had, and either way they'll kill for it again. I'm going up that wall because I promised a dying man, and because I'm beginning to suspect there's more buried up there than silver." She tucked the packet away. "So. Two hundred dollars, half now. Do we have a bargain, Mr. Ferro, or do you want to go back to your coffee?"

I looked at the coffee. It looked back, tar-colored and faithful. Six years of it stretched behind me, and six more ahead, safe and gray and empty as an unsurveyed flat.

"Half now," I said, "and I keep the alidade. And Captain—when we meet the Harrows up there, and we will, because men like Corvus Dain don't send one boat when they can send three—you'll want a man along who knows exactly how those people buy liars." I smiled, and it felt like an old injury reopening. "Nobody alive knows better than I do."

We shook on it. Her grip was dry and hard and brief, a grip that closed accounts.

She was at the door when I called after her—the question that had been rattling in me since I saw that kicked-out R, the one I already suspected the answer to and dreaded anyway.

"The daughter," I said. "Reyes's daughter. Does she know? About any of it?"

Voss paused on the landing, half-turned, lamplight catching the edge of her face.

"No," she said. "And that, Mr. Ferro, is the next problem."

Then her boots went down the stairs, two at a time, and I stood in my mildewed room holding money I hadn't earned yet, looking at a map I couldn't stop seeing, and understood that I had just purchased a ticket on a journey with no return fare—and that, for the first time in six years, I didn't mind.
