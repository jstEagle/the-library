## 8. Wren

I learned to hide in plain sight before I learned to braid my own hair. Coldharbor teaches you that early—how to be furniture, how to be weather, how to be a girl nobody bothers to look at twice because looking costs something and everyone in a mining town knows the price of everything.

So when I slipped aboard the *Kestrel* at the Millbrook dock, in the blue hour before dawn, with a canvas sack of bread ends, a tin of sardines, and my mother's good shawl wrapped over my hair, I wasn't frightened. I was *practiced*.

The trick to stowing away on a riverboat is not to hide at all. Hiding is for people who expect to be found. The trick is to belong so thoroughly that no one's eye catches on you. I carried a crate of dried apples up the gangplank like I'd been hired to do it, set it down where crates go, and then simply didn't stop existing loudly enough for anyone to notice. By the time the deckhand whistled the lines loose, I was folded into the shadow behind the woodpile on the lower deck, small as a knot in the timber, listening to the river take us away from Coldharbor.

Away from Coldharbor. I said it to myself like a prayer, or maybe a dare.

Three days ago I had been nobody's daughter, which is a thing you can be even when your father is alive—you just don't know it yet. I had been Wren Reyes, seventeen, employed at the Coldharbor telegraph office, sleeping in the loft above Mrs. Ansel's boarding house, eating her terrible stew and pretending not to count the years since my father walked out of town with his head down and his hat pulled low while half of Main Street watched him go.

They said he stole a claim. That was the story, anyway—the one that got told in the saloon and the assay office and finally, gently, by my mother on her deathbed, as if she were handing me a stone she'd carried too long: *Your father took what wasn't his, Wren. Don't go looking for him.* She died believing it. I'd grown up inside that belief like a seed inside a shell.

And then a letter came.

Not to me—that was the strange part, the part that made me believe it. It came to the telegraph office, addressed to *T. Reyes, c/o Coldharbor Exchange*, which is an address that hasn't existed in nine years. The clerk almost threw it out. But I sorted the dead letters, and dead letters are my business the way other girls' business is courting or gossip, and I opened it because that's what you do with letters nobody claims. You read them. Somebody has to.

It was from a prospector named Hollis, up past the divide, who had shared a campfire with a man called Tomas Reyes two months back. Old man, bad lungs, talking about a daughter he'd lost the right to. Talking about making things right before the mountain finished what the silver started. The letter was meant to find Tomas if he came through. Instead it found me, and I stood there in the telegraph office holding proof that my father was alive, and that he was dying, and that somewhere between those two facts he had been *looking for me*—

I read it forty times. I can recite it still. *He spoke of a girl named Wren who could name every bird on the Vell by its call. He asked whether the passes would hold till spring. He seemed a man trying to settle accounts.*

Settle accounts. My father, settling accounts. The man who left without leaving so much as a forwarding direction, who let his daughter grow up under the weight of a thief's name—he wanted to settle accounts.

So I wrote back to Hollis. And then, because I am my father's daughter whether I like it or not, I did something stupider than writing back. I waited for a boat going upriver, and I got on it without asking.

Because here is what I knew, from nine years of sorting other people's messages: news travels faster than men do. If Tomas Reyes was working the high country past the divide, and he was coming down—and Hollis's letter suggested he was—then he'd come down by water eventually. Rivers are roads that only run one way, and all roads out of those mountains end at the Vell. If I went upriver far enough, I'd meet him coming down. Simple geometry. A girl who reads wires all day learns to think in lines and intersections.

What I did not know—I want that written down somewhere, carved into something, so that future generations understand exactly how much I did not know—was that the man I was chasing might already have been fished out of the reeds by a riverboat captain with careful eyes and a debt she didn't talk about. What I did not know was that the account my father wanted to settle had been settled already, in the dark, by men whose names I'd learn later and hate forever.

But I'm getting ahead of myself. A stowaway shouldn't get ahead of herself. A stowaway's whole art is patience.

* * *

The first day, I nearly died of boredom, which I understand now is the traditional stowaway's death.

Behind the woodpile there was a gap between the hull planking and a stack of tarped cordwood, just wide enough for a girl of my particular stubbornness, and I made a home of it. I ate bread ends. I watched light move across the deck boards through the gap like a slow gold tide. I listened to the boat.

You can learn a whole vessel by ear. The *Kestrel* talked to herself constantly—a shallow-draft sternwheeler, maybe ninety feet, older than me but kept lovingly, the way you keep a body that has to keep working. Her engine had a knock on the third stroke, regular as a heartbeat, which meant either a worn bearing the captain hadn't fixed or couldn't afford to. The paddle wheel slapped the water in a rhythm I fell asleep to and woke to. And above it all, the voice of the captain herself, giving orders in a low, flat tone that never rose and never apologized.

Elodie, they called her. Just Elodie. Even the deckhands who'd worked with her a season said it like it was complete, like a boat needs only one name and a woman can too. I filed that away. In the telegraph office I'd learned that people who shorten their own names are usually hiding the long one, and people hiding a long name are usually hiding a longer story.

She interested me. That was the trouble with being a watcher—it's hard to stop, even when watching is the most dangerous thing you could do.

On the second morning, pressed to the gap in the planking, I saw her come down the deck steps with a mug of coffee she wasn't drinking, just holding, and stand at the rail for a long time looking north toward the mountains. There was something in her face I recognized, though I couldn't have named it then. It was the look my mother used to wear doing sums late at night—the look of a person adding up numbers that won't come out right no matter how many times you carry the one.

Then a man came up behind her—one of the deckhands, young, freckled—and said something I couldn't catch, and she turned, and the look was gone so fast and so completely that I understood I had seen something private, and that this woman kept her privates locked down tighter than the Coldharbor assay office. I liked her immediately and trusted her not at all, which I've since learned is the correct way to feel about most competent people.

That afternoon, disaster. Not mine—his.

A cat. Or rather, the ship's mouser, a gray torn with a seaman's contempt for everyone aboard, decided that the warm space behind the woodpile belonged to him, and informed me of this opinion by walking directly onto my chest at four in the afternoon while I slept.

I woke with a scream in my throat and swallowed it, barely, which is a feat I recommend to no one. The cat looked at me with yellow eyes full of grievance. Somewhere above, boots crossed the deck. I lay rigid, one hand on the cat's spine—because the one rule of cats and stowaways alike is that sudden movement gets you thrown off boats—and listened to the boots stop directly overhead.

"You hear that?" a voice said.

"Hear what?"

"Thought somethin' moved."

"Rats," the second voice said. "Boat's full of 'em."

The first voice laughed. "Cap'n'd skin us if we said that loud. She keeps a clean ship."

They moved on. The cat and I regarded each other for a long moment, two creatures discovered by neither, and I gave him a piece of sardine as tribute, and we had an accord after that. His name, I learned from overhearing, was Admiral. He accepted the title with the gravity it deserved.

* * *

It was the third night that I heard the conversation that changed everything, and I want to be precise about it, because precision is the only inheritance my father ever gave me that I knew about at the time.

We had tied up at a woodyard below the narrows—no town, just a stump field and a stack of cordwood and a lamplit shack where a man sold fuel to passing boats. The crew went ashore to stretch their legs. The boat went quiet. And I heard voices from the cabin below mine—not shouting, nothing dramatic, just two women talking in the low register people use when they think the world is asleep.

One was Elodie. The other I hadn't heard before, an older voice, roughened, with an accent from downriver—the cook, maybe, or someone else who lived aboard.

"—still feverish," the older voice said. "Off and on. Talks when it's high."

"And?" Elodie. Flat, careful.

"Says a name. Wren. Over and over." A pause. "Like a prayer with the wrong religion."

My heart did something I felt in my ears.

"Wren," Elodie repeated. No inflection at all. That was the thing about her voice—even saying an unfamiliar name, she gave away nothing, a coin with both faces worn smooth.

"Wren," the old woman agreed. "You know it?"

"No."

A lie. I knew it was a lie, and I'll tell you how: because Elodie said it too fast. People who don't know a word repeat it slow, tasting it. People who are deciding whether to admit they know it say it quick, to get it out of their mouths before it means anything. I'd spent three years listening to operators lie about delivery times; I knew the acoustics of a lie the way a musician knows a false note.

The old woman must have known it too, because she said, gently, "Elodie. What did you pull out of the reeds at Miller's Bend?"

Silence. Long enough that I counted the paddle wheel's idle slaps against the current—eleven of them.

"A man," Elodie said finally. "Shot twice. Half-drowned. Delirious." Another silence, shorter. "He wouldn't have lasted the night if I'd left him."

"And?"

"And nothing. He's alive. He's below. And when he's well enough to tell me who shot him, I'll decide what comes next." A pause, and then, quieter, in a voice that had lost its flatness entirely: "He asked me for something. A promise. I haven't given it yet."

"What kind of promise?"

"I don't know yet. He can't finish the sentence."

I sat in the dark behind the woodpile with the Admiral purring against my ribs, and I shook.

Because there was only one reason a shot, half-drowned, delirious man pulled from the reeds would be saying my name. Only one Wren that mattered to any man on this river. My father was alive. My father was *aboard*. Forty feet below my folded knees, feverish, shot, saying my name like a prayer with the wrong religion—and I had spent three days lying behind a woodpile ten feet from him, eating sardines, naming birds by their calls in my head to pass the time.

I don't remember deciding to cry. I remember the tears arriving like they'd been waiting days for permission, hot and silent, and the Admiral butting his head against my chin, confused by the salt.

He was alive. He was alive and he'd been *looking for me*, just like Hollis's letter said, and he'd been shot for it—shot by whoever, for whatever, and the captain knew more than she was saying, and none of it mattered, none of it mattered at all, because my father was forty feet below me and breathing.

I made my decision right there, in the dark, with river-smell in my nose and my father's name hanging in the air above me. I was done hiding. Whatever came of it—whatever trouble, whatever truth—I was going to walk down into that cabin and say *Papa, it's Wren, I got your letter, I came.*

I just needed to wait until the boat was quiet. Until the watch changed. Until the moment was right.

I want you to hold onto that. *Until the moment was right.* Because I had spent three days being patient, and patience had become a habit, and habits are how the river kills people. It doesn't kill the reckless. It kills the ones who wait one hour too long for conditions to improve.

If I had gone down that ladder that night—if I had been one hour less patient—things might have gone differently. Maybe not better. I've had nine years and one very long winter to consider it, and I honestly cannot say better. But differently. He would have heard my voice before the end instead of after it. Someone would have held his hand who loved him, instead of a stranger who owed him nothing.

Instead, I waited for the watch to change. And in that hour, the *Kestrel* cast off from the woodyard and slid out into the current, heading upriver into the narrows, carrying me and my secret and my father's fever deeper into country I'd only ever seen from the wrong end of a telegraph wire.

* * *

Near dawn, unable to bear the waiting anymore, I climbed out of my nest behind the woodpile and made it as far as the companionway ladder before the Admiral betrayed me.

Not maliciously. Cats don't betray; they simply refuse to participate in your fictions. He followed me, announcing himself with the low conversational yowl of a cat who believes all conversations should include him, and the sound rang down the stairwell into the cabin like a bell.

Footsteps. Then Elodie was standing at the bottom of the ladder, lantern raised, looking up at me.

I had imagined this moment, obviously. Everyone who stows away imagines the discovery. In my imagination I was calm and charming and had a perfectly reasonable explanation prepared. In reality, I was seventeen, unwashed, red-eyed from crying, clutching a canvas sack containing bread ends and a tin of sardines, with a cat winding around my ankles, and what I actually said was:

"I can pay for my passage. I have eleven cents."

Elodie looked at me for a long moment. The lantern light moved across her face and showed me what daylight hadn't: tiredness, deep and structural, the kind that isn't from any one night. And underneath the tiredness, something else—something that flickered when she looked at me, quick as a fish turning, there and gone.

"Eleven cents," she said.

"And a tin of sardines. Unopened."

"The Admiral will be pleased. He's been complaining about the quality of his provisions." She stepped aside from the ladder. "Come down, then. Since you're paying."

I came down. The cabin was close and dim and smelled of lamp oil and river and, underneath it, faintly, of sickness. There was a bunk along the starboard side, curtained, and my whole body turned toward it like a compass needle before I could stop myself.

Elodie saw. Of course she saw. She was a woman who noticed everything and admitted nothing, and I watched her decide something behind her eyes, some weighing I wouldn't understand for weeks.

"You know why you're really here," she said. Not a question.

"My father," I said. "Tomas Reyes. He's in that bunk. You pulled him out of the river at Miller's Bend, and he's been saying my name, and I heard you last night, and I—" My voice cracked and I hated it and pushed through. "I got a letter. From a prospector named Hollis. He said my father was alive and looking for me. So I came to find him. And I found him. I found him first, before I even knew it was him."

Something moved through Elodie's face then, slow as weather. Not surprise—she'd known, or guessed, the moment she'd seen me. Something more like grief arriving at a scheduled stop.

"Wren," she said.

And the way she said it—not flat this time, not quick, but slow, and heavy, and careful, the way you handle something that might be the last of its kind—told me everything before she said another word.

The curtain around the bunk hung still. The engine knocked on its third stroke. The river ran on beneath us, indifferent, carrying us all somewhere at once.

"He's sleeping," Elodie said. "Sit down. There's coffee."

And I sat, and took the mug, and did not drink it, and stared at that curtain, and understood—with the terrible clarity that only arrives after it's too late to be useful—that the captain had said *he's sleeping* the way people say it when they mean something larger, and that the flicker I'd seen in her face when she first looked at me was pity, and that the eleven cents in my pocket were never going to cover what this passage was going to cost.

The curtain didn't move. The bunk made no sound at all.

"Papa?" I said, to the stillness. Just once. Quietly.

Nothing answered but the river.
