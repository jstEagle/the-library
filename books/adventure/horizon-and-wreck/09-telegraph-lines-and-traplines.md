## 9. Telegraph Lines and Traplines

I'd been a stowaway for three days before anyone asked my name, and I'd have happily gone another three if it meant keeping my hands busy instead of my mouth. That was the trick of it, I was learning: on the Kestrel, nobody cared who you were so long as you bailed when bailing was needed and didn't ask why the captain slept with a revolver under her pillow and a packet under her mattress.

I'd seen the packet. I wasn't proud of snooping, but I was proud of how well I did it.

Wren Reyes, that's me. Daughter of a dead man I didn't know was dead, carrying a name I'd spent half my life trying to scrub off. In Coldharbor, "Reyes" got you a certain look—the look people give a dog that bit somebody once. I'd left on the night boat with eleven dollars and my father's old brass compass, and I'd told myself I was going to find him and make him explain. Explain the leaving. Explain the eleven years of silence. Explain why a man walks out of a town that hates him and never once sends word to the girl who took his name like a bruise.

Then I'd overheard two men in a telegraph office talking about "the Reyes packet," and the whole shape of my errand changed.

But I'm getting ahead of myself. First there was the matter of the captain catching me at it.

* * *

The girl was folding linens in the passageway outside my cabin when I came up from the engine well, and she did it with the particular innocence of somebody who has just finished doing something else entirely. I've run enough passengers to know the difference between a person working and a person performing work. Her elbows were wrong. Nobody folds linens with their elbows that tense.

"Find anything interesting down there?" I asked.

"Spiders," she said, not missing a beat. "Big ones."

"Vell River spiders. Famous for it." I leaned against the rail and let the silence do its work. She lasted four seconds, which was longer than most.

"You knew," she said. "You knew who I was when you pulled me off that barge at Millbrook."

"I knew you'd paid your passage in a story about an aunt in Vellport that you couldn't look at me while telling. That's usually enough."

"My name's Wren." She said it like a dare. "Wren Reyes."

There it was. The name landed in my chest like a stone dropped down a well, and I waited for the splash. Reyes. Tomas Reyes, shot twice and half-drowned in the reeds, trading me a map for a promise with his last good breath. And here came his daughter, seventeen if she was a day, with his same stubborn jaw and none of his gray, asking me to confirm she existed.

"I know," I said.

Her face did something complicated. "You know. You *know*? You've known this whole time, and you let me stack firewood and—"

"Let you? You stowed aboard my boat, girl. Everything after that is charity."

"Did you know my father?"

The river slapped the hull. Somewhere aft, Ferro was arguing with the mule we'd acquired, which was like arguing with a wall that could kick. I looked at this girl who had his eyes and thought: not yet. Not on a open deck with the wind carrying. Some truths you have to sit down for.

"I knew him three days," I said. "Come to the pilothouse at supper and I'll tell you what I can."

"And the packet?" she said, and I stopped with my hand on the door. "The one the men in Coldharbor were asking after. The *Reyes* packet."

I turned around slowly. "What men in Coldharbor."

* * *

Here's what I should have done: told her everything right then, on the spot, mess and all. Here's what I actually did: I made her wait until supper, because I'm a coward with a schedule, and then I made Ferro sit in on it because I wanted a witness in case she threw something.

The pilothouse at dusk, the Vell gone the color of hammered tin. I put coffee in front of her and told it straight. The reeds. The bullets. The oilskin packet he wouldn't let go of even when I pried his fingers loose to check the wounds. The promise he'd asked for and I'd refused, and the way refusing a dying man sits in you afterward like a swallowed hook.

"He said carry it to his daughter in Coldharbor," I finished. "Before the passes closed. I told him I didn't carry promises. He died anyway, and I kept it anyway, and I've been sailing it upriver ever since like a fool with a lit fuse."

Wren didn't throw anything. She sat very still with both hands around her cup, and when she finally spoke her voice had gone flat and careful, the way voices go when they're holding a roof up.

"He was alive. Three days ago he was alive, and I was eleven dollars away from him."

"He knew where you were," Ferro said quietly. "That's why he sent it to Coldharbor."

"He left Coldharbor." Her jaw worked. "Everybody in that town made sure I knew he left *us*—left me and my mother. Mother died two winters back. Nobody cried but me, and even I was mostly crying mad." She looked up. "So why would he send his treasure to a town that ran him out?"

"Because it wasn't treasure he was sending," I said. "Or not only. But go on—you said men in Coldharbor. Tell me that part first."

She set the cup down. "Two days before I ran. I was at the telegraph office—my aunt's landlady works the counter there, and I was picking up a wire for her, which is how I learned to read other people's wires, which is how I learned most things I know. These two came in off the north road. Boots wrong for the country, coats too good, the kind of men who tip like they're paying you to forget them. One asks the clerk if there's been traffic on the pass line. The clerk says the pass line's been down since the rockslide in September, and the man says, *it'll be up before the season closes, the Syndicate's paying to keep it up.*"

Ferro and I exchanged a look. The pass telegraph line was Harrow property in everything but the deed. Of course they'd keep it open. A line that only carries your own traffic isn't a line, it's a throat.

"The other one," Wren went on, "was reading off a list. Not a wire—a list, like he'd memorized it. He says, *the Reyes packet comes through the pass or it doesn't come through at all. Dain wants it taken at the narrows, before the glacier road. Nobody goes up that ice carrying anything we don't already know about.*" She shrugged, a small terrible shrug. "I didn't know what the Reyes packet was. I thought maybe it was money he'd owed somebody. It had our name on it, so I figured it was one more thing my father had done wrong that I'd get handed the bill for. So I took the eleven dollars out of my aunt's tin and I got on the boat, because I figured if the bill was coming, I'd rather meet it standing up."

The pilothouse was quiet enough to hear the river thinking.

"They're not chasing him," Ferro said at last, slow, the way he said things when the ground under a survey was shifting. "They're not chasing a thief who ran. They're waiting for a *packet*. They knew it was coming. They knew the route." He looked at me. "Elodie. How did they know Tomas was moving it *now*?"

I didn't have an answer. That was the problem. I'd spent two weeks assuming the Harrows were a dog on my trail because a dying man had bled on my deck and I'd been fool enough to be seen near him. But a dog chases what it sees. These men had been standing in a telegraph office in Coldharbor two days before Tomas Reyes ever washed into my reeds, talking about his packet like it was freight on a schedule.

"They knew before he was shot," I said. "Or—" and the thought arrived cold, the way the true ones do. "Or the shooting wasn't them catching him. It was them *herding* him."

"Herding him where?"

"Toward the pass." I stood and looked out at the dark coming down on the water. "You don't rob a man in town, where there's a marshal and witnesses. You let him run. You let him load up his proof and head for the one road out of the mountains, and you stand at the narrows with your hand out. Tomas wasn't fleeing them. He was being *delivered* to them, and the only thing that spoiled it was a sandbar and me."

Wren had gone white. "He was shot in the back," she said. "You said. Running away."

"Running toward," I said. "Same thing, from where they stood."

* * *

We argued until the lamp burned low, and I'll give the girl this: she argued like her father must have, all elbows and no retreat.

She wanted to go back downriver. I told her the Harrows owned the downriver. She wanted to wire the marshals. Ferro pointed out that the only working line to the marshals ran through Harrow hands, and that a wire is just a confession you deliver yourself. She wanted to burn the packet, and I confess I let her say it all the way through before I said no, because part of me needed to hear it said out loud by somebody with clean hands.

"It's the reason he's dead," she said. "It's the reason they were in that office saying our name. Burn it and we're nobody again. We're safe."

"Safe," I said, "is a town where your father's name is mud and the men who made it mud drink free at the saloon. Safe is what the Harrows sell you after they've taken everything worth keeping." I pulled the oilskin from my coat and laid it on the chart table between us, and I watched her look at it—her father's handwriting, his careful riverman's script, the landmarks he'd drawn from memory of a claim he'd been accused of stealing. "This isn't a treasure map, Wren. I've had two weeks to look at it and I'm a riverwoman, not a fool. A man doesn't get shot twice over silver he hasn't dug. He gets shot over what the silver can *prove*. Whatever your father hid up that mountain, the Harrows need it to stay hidden more than they need it found. That makes it worth more than its weight in ore. And it makes us the only three people alive who can carry it where it needs to go."

"Which is where?" Ferro asked. "You've read the high-country notations. You've said yourself they're half cipher."

"Which is why I hired you," I said. "And why we go up. Through the pass, before it closes, along the exact road they're watching—because it's the only road there is, and because I'd rather meet Dain's men in daylight on ground I've studied than hide down here until winter makes the decision for us."

"They'll be at the narrows," Wren said.

"They'll be at the narrows *waiting for a packet to come to them*," I said. "So we don't come to them. Ferro, is there another way off the pass road above the treeline? Old survey tracks, anything?"

He was quiet a long moment, turning his cup. When Ferro goes quiet like that, it's because he's weighing how much of the truth a room can hold, and I've learned to let him pour it at his own speed.

"There's a trapline," he said finally. "Old one, pre-Syndicate. A trapper named Osk used to run it along the east wall—high, mean, no road, just cut blazes and goat paths. I mapped it in my first season, before—" He stopped. Before the disgrace, he meant. Before the Harrows bought his signature and spent it like a coin. "It rejoins the pass road above the narrows. If the blazes are still standing, it would put us behind whoever's waiting."

"Can mules take it?"

"One mule can. Slowly. The other mule will have opinions."

"Then we go by trapline." I looked at Wren. "You can still turn around at Coldharbor. Nobody would think less—"

"Stop," she said, and for a second she sounded so much like her father that the lamp flame seemed to lean. "You're going to tell me the rest of it. All of it, not the version for company. What was in the packet when you opened it. What he said at the end, word for word. And then I'm coming with you the whole way, and you don't get a vote." She put her hand flat on the oilskin, on his handwriting, and her voice cracked only a little. "They used our name in that office like it belonged to them. I'm taking it back up the mountain myself."

Ferro looked at me over her head, and I read him easy enough: *this is how it starts, the ones you can't send back.*

"All right," I said. "Word for word, then. But we sail through the night and you take the wheel at dawn. If you're crew, you're crew all the way down."

* * *

Later, alone in the pilothouse with the river running black under the running lights, I let myself think the thing I hadn't said at the table: the Harrows hadn't been hunting me at all. All my careful anonymity, my cash debts and unsaid last name, my whole architecture of being no one—and it had never mattered, because I was never the quarry. I was a sandbar in the current. The packet was the current. And now I'd tied myself to it, and to a girl with her father's jaw and a surveyor with his father's guilt, and the Syndicate's whole attention was about to come up that river like spring flood.

Dain would have men at the narrows. Dain always had men.

I took out the map and held it to the lamp, and for the first time I didn't look at the silver markings. I looked at the margins—Tomas's tiny notations, the landmarks, the trapline blaze he'd drawn near the east wall with a little mark beside it that I'd taken for a smudge.

It wasn't a smudge. It was a blaze, cut in his own hand, years old.

He'd known the trapline too. He'd drawn us the back way before he ever died.

"Well," I said to the empty river, to the dead man, to the dark, "you could have led with that."

The Kestrel ran on through the night, upstream, toward the pass and whatever was waiting at the narrows—and I went below to wake the girl, because at dawn she had the wheel, and I meant to teach her every sandbar between here and Coldharbor. If we were going to run the Harrows' road, we'd do it as crew. All the way down.
