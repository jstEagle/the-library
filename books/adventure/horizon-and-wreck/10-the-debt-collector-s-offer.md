## 10. The Debt Collector's Offer

The lock at Stonefall was the kind of place where a riverboat either waited politely or died trying not to.

It sat in a granite throat of the Vell where the river dropped twelve feet in a quarter mile, and the only way through was the lock itself—a stone box with wooden gates at either end and a winch system older than my grandmother's grudges. You couldn't run it. You couldn't bluff it. You tied off, you waited your turn, and while you waited, anyone who wanted to find you could find you.

Which is why I'd planned to shoot it at dawn on a Tuesday, when the queue was short and the lockkeeper was still hungover enough to be generous.

Instead we arrived at dusk on a Monday behind a grain barge and two timber rafts, which meant four hours minimum, which meant nightfall at the lock, which meant—when I saw the lanterns swinging along the towpath from downstream—company.

"Elodie." Ferro stood at the rail beside me, hands jammed in his coat pockets like a man pretending he wasn't cold or afraid. "That's—"

"I see them."

Three men. No, four—one hanging back by the path's bend, keeping the road. They walked the way Harrow men walked, which is to say unhurried, because hurry implies doubt, and the Syndicate had never doubted anything in its life except its own mortality, and it spent every waking hour making sure that mortality stayed theoretical.

"They'll want to board," Ferro said.

"They'll want to do more than board."

Behind me, below decks, Wren was showing the girl how to bank the stove coals for the night. I heard her laugh at something. It was a good laugh. It was going to be a problem.

I turned to Ferro. "When they come aboard, you're my hired surveyor and nothing else. You've never seen a map. You don't know what a Reyes is. If they ask about the packet, you look stupid. Can you manage that?"

"I've been managing stupid for eleven years," he said. "It's practically a trade."

* * *

Corvus Dain came up the gangplank alone.

I want to be fair about this, because fairness was a currency I rarely spent and Dain made me spend it now: he was not what the word *enforcer* promised. Enforcers were supposed to be knuckles and necks, men built like dock cranes who solved problems by sitting on them. Dain was lean and gray at the temples, with a clerk's neat hands and a voice pitched low enough that you leaned in to hear it, and leaning in was the whole trick. He wore a good coat. He carried no visible weapon, which meant he carried one somewhere, which meant he expected to need it, which meant he'd already decided how tonight would go and was merely waiting for me to catch up to his version of events.

"Captain Voss," he said.

Nobody used my last name. That was the point of the last name—it lived on paper, in ledgers, in the Syndicate's files, and nowhere else. Hearing it out loud felt like someone reading my mail in front of me.

"Mister Dain." I didn't offer a hand. He didn't offer one either. We were polite the way two knives in the same drawer are polite. "You're a long way from Vellport."

"The river goes where the river goes." He glanced past me at the boat—the Kestrel, patched and smoke-stained and worth less than the debt she carried—and something in his face did arithmetic. "May I come aboard properly? This wind has opinions."

"It does." I stepped aside. Stupid. Necessary. Both things can be true.

He settled into the deck chair like he owned it, like he'd owned it for years, like the Kestrel was already half his through some lien I hadn't read carefully enough. Maybe it was. That was the thing about owing the Harrows money: everything you touched got their fingerprints on it eventually.

"You're running upstream," he observed. "Toward Coldharbor, if I know your habits. And I do know your habits, Captain. That's rather my profession."

"My habits are boring."

"Your habits are *expensive*. There's a difference." He produced a tin of peppermints from his coat pocket, offered me one. I declined. He took one himself, unhurried, and I understood the performance completely—he was telling me he had all night, and the lock had all night, and the mountain pass would wait, but my window wouldn't. "Let's talk about Tomas Reyes."

There it was. My heart did something unprofessional against my ribs, and I kept my face doing nothing at all, which is the hardest work there is.

"Don't know him," I said.

"No," Dain agreed pleasantly. "Of course you don't. You don't know the prospector who was seen at Millbrook buying passage on a riverboat matching this one's description. You don't know the man who was shot twice and went into the Vell below Widow's Reach three days ago. And you certainly don't know what he was carrying." He sucked his peppermint. "Because if you knew any of those things, Captain, you'd have sent word to the nearest Syndicate office, being the sensible, debt-conscious woman you are. Instead you're steaming toward the mountains in late autumn with a disgraced surveyor and—" his eyes flicked toward the cabin hatch, and I felt the temperature of the conversation drop ten degrees, "—a stowaway."

"She's crew," I said. "She works for food."

"How industrious. How very *found-family* of you." He said it lightly, mockingly, but the words landed harder than he probably intended, because found family was exactly the disease I'd caught and exactly the thing Dain could use against me. People without attachments are hard to threaten. I'd built my whole life on that principle, and here I was, attached in four directions, all of them flammable.

"What do you want, Dain?"

"Ah. Business." He sat forward, and the lamplight caught his eyes, and for just a moment the clerk fell away and I saw the actual animal underneath—the one that had collected debts from men who then stopped existing. "Here is what I want. Here is what I am *authorized* to want, which is better than wanting, because authorization means backing, and backing means it will actually happen."

He let the pause stretch. Men like Dain think silence is pressure. Sometimes it is. Mostly it's just noise with the volume turned down.

"Tomas Reyes took something from the Harrow Syndicate. A document. An oilskin packet, hand-sized, possibly wrapped in canvas, possibly containing a map." His voice stayed level, conversational, a man discussing freight rates. "That document is Syndicate property. It was purchased and paid for, and the seller reneged on delivery, and the seller has since become unavailable. The debt has transferred, as debts do, to whoever holds the collateral." He smiled thinly. "You are currently holding the collateral, Captain. Whether you know it or not."

"I don't have anything of his."

"I believe you."

He believed me the way a trap believes the bait isn't bait.

"And here is my offer." He reached into his coat again, and every muscle in my body went tight under the skin, and what he drew out was not a gun but a folded paper—a ledger page, official, stamped with the Harrow seal in wax gone slightly soft from riding next to his body heat. He laid it on the table between us and smoothed it flat with two fingers, an oddly tender gesture, like tucking in a child. "This is your account. Principal, interest, penalties, the fees for the year you hid in the delta marshes thinking we'd lost your scent—we hadn't; we simply found other things more profitable to chase. Four hundred and sixty-two dollars. Which, on your margins, is roughly nine years of hauling grain and lying about your draft depth."

I looked at the number. I'd known the number for years—I could recite it in my sleep, and sometimes did, in the bad dreams—but seeing it written down in black ink with a red line drawn beneath it, the line that meant *closed*, did something to my chest I wasn't prepared for.

Nine years. Nine years of sandbars and toll boats and paying cash under false names and never once saying my own name out loud on a dock. Nine years of being a smudge on the river, invisible, weightless, free the way a ghost is free.

And he was holding the eraser.

"Forgiven," Dain said. "All of it. Tonight. You walk away clean. Not clean-ish. Clean. The ledger burned, the collectors recalled, your name struck from our books so thoroughly that five years from now even men whose profession is remembering won't remember you." He tapped the paper once. "In exchange for the packet. Unopened, ideally. Though I confess the Syndicate's preference on that point has softened considerably."

"And if I say I don't have it?"

"Then I take back my generous offer, and we return to the traditional arrangement, which involves interest compounding monthly and my colleagues boarding vessels at inconvenient hours." He spread his hands, reasonable, almost warm. "Captain, I'm not your enemy. Your enemy is arithmetic. I'm merely the man arithmetic sends."

* * *

I stalled.

I want to tell you I stalled brilliantly—that I spun him a tale so smooth he left doubting his own mother—but the truth is uglier and more useful: I stalled the way drowning people stall, grabbing whatever floats.

I told him the truth, or a shape of it. That a dying man had come aboard my boat and I'd pulled him out of the reeds because that's what riverfolk do, or used to, before the Syndicate taught us better. That he'd raved. That he'd died. That whatever he'd been carrying had gone into the river with the blood, or been buried with him in the shallows above Millbrook, marked by a cairn any honest man could dig up—which was true, actually, because I had buried Tomas Reyes properly, and I had put a cairn on him, and I watched Dain file that detail away with his clerk's hands and knew I'd just handed him a digging assignment.

Dain listened to all of it without blinking. Then he said, "Show me the grave."

"Two days downstream."

"Mm." He rose, buttoned his coat, and picked up his ledger page—my ledger page, my life in numbers—and held it up between two fingers. "Then I'll keep this where you can see it. Consider it motivation. We'll speak again when you've had time to consider how much nine years weighs against one piece of oilskin."

"That's it? You're just going to sit at the lock until I change my mind?"

"Captain." He paused at the gangplank, and the wind coming off the water flattened his gray hair, and for a moment he looked tired—not enforcer-tired, but the deep, structural tiredness of a man who had done this a hundred times and knew exactly how every one of them ended. "Do you know what happens to the people who hold Harrow collateral? Eventually, everyone brings it to us. Everyone. The brave ones take longer. The clever ones cost us more. But the mountain takes the proud ones, and the river takes the fast ones, and winter takes the rest, and what's left walks into our offices holding whatever they stole and asking what took them so long." He stepped down onto the planking. "I'd rather you were clever. Clever ones negotiate. But I'll take proud, if proud is what's on offer."

He walked away down the towpath, lantern light swallowing him by degrees, and I stood at the rail of my own boat feeling like the passenger.

* * *

Ferro found me at the wheel ten minutes later. I'd taken the watch without deciding to; my hands needed a job that wasn't shaking.

"He offered to wipe the debt," I said, before he could ask. "All of it. Four hundred and sixty-two dollars. Gone. One packet."

Ferro was quiet a long moment. Below us, the lock gates groaned open for the timber rafts, and the water level began its slow fall, and the whole boat shuddered like something waking.

"And?" he said finally.

"And nothing. I told him I didn't have it."

"Do you?"

"Ferro."

"I'm asking as a man who's spent eleven years learning what lies cost," he said gently. "Not as crew. As a colleague."

I looked at the dark water sliding past the hull, at the far bank where the mountains had started to show themselves—black shoulders against a blacker sky, snow already on the high creases, the pass up there closing a little more every night whether I was ready or not.

"Yes," I said. "I have it."

"And you're going to keep it."

"It's not mine to sell." My voice came out lower than I intended. "It's Wren's. Or it's supposed to go to Wren. Tomas bought a promise with it, and the man was shot twice and drowned half to death and the first thing he did with his last hours was make me swear. You don't get to put a price on that. Even a price that erases nine years."

"No," Ferro agreed. "You don't." He leaned on the rail beside me, shoulder nearly touching mine, and after a while he said, "For what it's worth, I'd have taken the deal. Eleven years ago, I'd have taken it before he finished the sentence. That's how the Harrows got their hooks in me—small forgivenesses, each one reasonable, each one a link." He shrugged. "So I suppose I'm saying: don't listen to me."

Despite everything, despite the collector and the ledger and the four men on the towpath and the number that lived in my chest like a stone, I laughed. Short and ugly, but real.

"Dain said the proud ones die on the mountain," I said.

"He would know. He's buried several." Ferro pushed off the rail. "Get some sleep, Captain. Whatever he decides we are—collateral holders, thieves, proud fools—we'll need to be rested ones."

* * *

I didn't sleep. I went below instead and stood outside the cabin where Wren slept, listening to her breathe, and I thought about the deal the way you think about a knife on a table—aware of it, estimating distances.

Four hundred and sixty-two dollars. One packet. And the packet wasn't even mine; that was the joke of it. Dain was offering to buy stolen goods from a woman who'd sworn to deliver them, using money she owed him for a crime she'd committed for a man who'd left her holding the rope. Every piece of it was rotten, and the rottenness was the temptation, because rotten deals are the only kind that ever get offered to people like me. Clean offers go to clean people.

Wren stirred in her bunk and murmured something—her father's name, maybe, or half of it—and rolled over, and I stood in the dark hating Corvus Dain with a purity I hadn't managed in years, because he'd made me do the arithmetic. He'd put the number next to the promise and forced me to look at both at once, and no matter which one I chose, I'd carry the sight of the other.

Above me, boots crossed the deck. Too many boots, and too quiet for crew.

I was up the ladder before I'd finished deciding to move, and what I saw in the lock's lantern light froze the blood in my arms mid-swing: four shapes at the mooring lines, moving with the unhurried competence of men who'd boarded a hundred boats, and Dain standing back on the towpath with his hands clasped behind him, watching, patient as arithmetic.

They weren't waiting for morning. They weren't waiting for my answer.

They were taking the inventory now.

"Mr. Ferro!" I shouted it down the hatch, already reaching for the winch lever, already doing the math I'd sworn I'd never do—current, gate timing, the twelve-foot drop, the darkness, the girl asleep below. "Wake the crew! We're shooting the lock!"

The first Harrow man's boot hit my deck.
