## 11. Sandbar Justice

I want it on the record that I did not stow away on the *Kestrel* to die in a set of rapids with a name like a graveyard joke.

The Widow's Teeth. That's what the rivermen called the stretch below Grayling Bend, where the Vell pinched itself between two shelves of black granite and got so angry about it that the water stood up in white fangs and bit at anything foolish enough to come through after dark. Every pilot I'd ever overheard in Coldharbor talked about the Teeth the way sailors talk about storms—reverently, and only ever as something they had *avoided*.

So naturally, three nights out of Millbrook, I woke at moonset to Elodie Voss standing at the wheel in the dark, saying nothing, listening to everything.

* * *

Here's what I knew by then, which wasn't much. I knew the captain had a dead man's map sewn into her coat lining—Ferro had told me that much before I told him I already knew, and we'd had an awkward moment of two liars discovering they were both terrible at it. I knew she owed the Harrow Syndicate a debt she never named aloud, and that the name she never spoke was Corvus Dain. And I knew my father was alive somewhere ahead of me, because if he weren't, this woman with river-gray eyes would have said so. She was a lot of things, Captain Voss, but she couldn't lie with her whole face. Only most of it.

I'd been sleeping in the pilothouse corner, wrapped in a tarpaulin that smelled of tar and engine oil, because Elodie claimed the cabin berth was for paying passengers and I claimed I hadn't asked. We'd reached a kind of armed truce about it. She fed me. I pretended not to notice.

That night I woke not to a sound but to the absence of one. The engine had gone quiet. On a riverboat, silence is louder than any bell.

"What is it?" I whispered.

Elodie held up one hand without turning her head. In the starlight her face was all planes and shadow, and her eyes were fixed on the water behind us—or rather, on a darkness behind us that was slightly darker than the rest of the dark, and moving against the current.

"There's a boat back there," I said.

"There's a boat back there," she agreed softly. "No lights. Running quiet. Been matching our drift for half an hour."

"Could be fishermen."

"Fishermen light lamps." Her voice was flat as a ledger line. "And fishermen don't kill their engine when the current's carrying them toward a bend."

Ferro came up the companionway then, moving like a man who'd spent years learning which floorboards announced him. He'd gotten better at it since joining us; disgrace teaches you things. He looked at the stern, then at Elodie's face, and whatever he saw there made him go still.

"Dain?"

"Or Dain's dogs." She turned the wheel a few spokes, gentle as adjusting a pillow under a sleeper's head. The *Kestrel* began to slide leftward, toward the north bank, where the willows hung low and black. "They've been waiting for us to anchor. Nobody runs the Vell at night—not past Grayling. They think we'll tie up at the Bend like sensible people."

"And instead?" Ferro asked.

"Instead we're going to do something stupid," Elodie said. "Wren. Go wake the boiler."

* * *

I want to say I was brave about it. What I actually was, was cold. My hands shook so badly getting the firebox door open that I scorched my knuckle twice, and I stood there feeding split pine into the throat of the boiler while the pressure gauge climbed and my mind kept offering helpful commentary, like: *you ran away from Coldharbor to find your father, and instead you're going to be boiled alive in a tin kettle by men who work for the people who—*

No. I didn't know yet what the Harrows had done to Papa. I only knew the shape of the fear, not its name. The shape was enough.

By the time I scrambled back up to the pilothouse, the pursuing boat had closed to maybe two hundred yards—a long, low skiff with six or seven shapes in it, and now, betraying them, a single hooded lantern being unshuttered at the bow. A voice carried across the water, pleasant and carrying, the voice of a man who enjoyed his own sound.

"*Kestrel!* Heave to! Syndicate inspection!"

"Inspection," Ferro muttered. "At midnight. With no papers shown."

"They showed their papers," Elodie said. "That's a Harrow gun in the bow, if I know their boats." She threw the throttle open and the *Kestrel* shuddered, gathered herself, and leapt forward like a horse feeling spurs for the first time in a week.

The lantern behind us flared brighter. A shot cracked across the water—high, wide, a warning—and then a second one that was neither.

It hit the rail four feet from where Ferro stood and took a splinter out of the wood the size of my thumb.

"Down!" Elodie barked, and shoved us both below the wheel housing, and put the *Kestrel*'s nose straight at the Widow's Teeth.

* * *

You have to understand what that meant. The Teeth weren't just rapids. They were a gate. The river came down off the high country through a gorge no wider than a wagon road, and the granite shelves on either side had shed boulders into the channel for ten thousand years, so that the water threaded between stones the way a needle threads between your ribs. By daylight, with a pilot who knew every fang by its Christian name, it was dangerous. At night, at speed, with a syndicate skiff shooting at your stern—

"It can't be done," Ferro said. He wasn't arguing. He was stating it the way you'd state that fire burns. "Elodie. It cannot be done at night."

"Everything can be done once," Elodie said, and her hands never stopped moving on the wheel, small corrections, constant, like a woman braiding something invisible. "I've run the Teeth eleven times. Daylight, yes. But I know them by ear, Augustin. Water talks. You just have to listen better than you look."

"You're going to navigate by *sound*—"

"I'm going to navigate by sound, memory, and a considerable amount of luck I haven't earned yet. Wren."

"Yes." My voice cracked on it. I hated that.

"The boiler. Keep her hot but don't let her scream—if the gauge goes into the red band, you crack the relief valve yourself, understand? If she blows in that gorge we're all paste on the rocks."

"Understood." I was already moving.

"And Wren—" I stopped. She was looking at me over her shoulder, and in the starlight her expression was doing something complicated, something that on another face might have been apology. "Whatever happens in the next ten minutes, hold onto something that's bolted down."

"That's not reassuring."

"It's not meant to be. It's meant to be true."

* * *

The gorge swallowed us.

I have been on rivers my whole life—Coldharbor sits where the Vell comes down mean from the mountains, and I grew up watching log drives and hearing drowned men talked about in the past tense—but I had never heard anything like the sound the water made inside those walls. It wasn't noise. It was weather. It came up through the hull and into my teeth and rearranged something in my chest. The *Kestrel* went from running to flying to falling, over and over, and each time she fell I felt the river decide, briefly, whether to let her rise again.

I was in the engine room, both hands on the throttle linkage, watching the gauge needle tremble at the top edge of the safe band. Steam screamed somewhere. The firebox door banged on its hinge. Through the engine room's aft port I could see, absurdly, the skiff behind us—they'd followed us in. Of course they had. Men who chase money into hell don't stop at the gate.

Then the first fang hit us.

I never saw it. I felt it—a blow along the port side that picked the whole boat up and threw her sideways, and the world became a tilted smear of black rock and white water and the sound of Ferro shouting something from above that the river ate mid-word. The gauge needle slammed into the red. I did what she'd told me. I hauled the relief valve lever down with both hands and the steam shrieked out of us like something dying, and the boiler groaned and settled, and the *Kestrel* fell into the trough between waves and rose again, listing hard to starboard, shipping water over the rails in cold gray sheets.

We came around the second bend of the gorge sideways.

I found out later that Elodie called it "the parlor," a pocket of slack water between two fangs where the current doubled back on itself, and that no pilot had ever taken it at speed because taking it at speed was suicide. She took it because the alternative was a submerged shelf directly ahead that her ears had heard a half-second before her eyes could confirm it. So we spun. The whole boat spun, a slow horrible pirouette, and everything not bolted down went across the deck like furniture in a flood.

Including Augustin Ferro.

* * *

I came up out of the engine room in time to see him go.

He'd been braced at the starboard rail—the wrong rail, the outside of the turn—and when the *Kestrel* swung he simply ceased to have anything to hold. One moment he was a tall stooped silhouette against the stars; the next he was over the side, and the river had him, and the river does not give things back. I watched his head go under once, twice, his arms making the flat useless slaps of a man who has forgotten how to swim in the space of a heartbeat, and I heard myself screaming his name, and I heard Elodie screaming something else—"the line! WREN, THE LINE!"—and my body understood before my mind did.

There was a coil of lead line at the base of the pilothouse. Riverwomen keep it there the way other people keep a Bible. I grabbed it, thumbed the loop open, and swung it—once, twice, building the circle wider than I'd ever thrown anything in my life, wider than sense—and let it fly.

Here is the thing nobody tells you about terror: it makes you precise. All those summers on the Coldharbor docks, skipping stones, throwing mooring lines to my father's friends for coppers, all of it lived in my arm without asking my permission. The coil went out over the black water, opened like a flower, and dropped across Ferro's shoulder just as he went under the third time.

He caught it. God help him, he caught it, and the line snapped taut, and I threw myself backward against the cleat with the rope burning a groove into my palms, and I screamed for Elodie, and the *Kestrel* came around out of the parlor with her deck awash and dragged him out of the river the way you drag a drowning dog, bumping and thrashing and swearing—I could hear him swearing even before he could breathe, which struck me, even then, as deeply characteristic.

We hauled him over the rail together. He lay on the deck coughing up half the Vell, gray-faced, sixty years old and shaking like a leaf, and I knelt on his chest with the rope still in my blistered hands and shouted at him, stupidly, furiously:

"Don't you *dare* die! You still owe me an apology for 1889!"

He looked up at me with streaming eyes and started to laugh, which turned into more coughing, which turned into something that might have been crying if either of us ever acknowledged such a thing. "Noted," he wheezed. "*Noted.*"

Behind us, in the gorge, there was a sound like the end of the world—a long grinding crunch, a human scream cut short, and then the river's indifferent roar closing over it. I looked back. The skiff's lantern bobbed once against the black water, guttered, and went out.

Nobody said anything for a long time.

* * *

We anchored at dawn in a slough below the gorge, among reeds and sleeping herons, and the silence afterward was so complete it rang.

Elodie came down to the deck where Ferro sat wrapped in every blanket the boat owned, drinking coffee laced with the brandy she pretended she didn't carry. His hands were still trembling around the cup. Mine were worse—rope burns across both palms, weeping clear fluid, and I noticed Elodie noticing them.

She didn't say thank you. I understood later that she almost never would; gratitude, for Elodie Voss, was a debt, and debts were the currency that had ruined her life. Instead she sat down across from us, took my hands without asking, and began wrapping them in strips of clean linen with the brisk tenderness of someone who had bandaged herself alone too many times.

"You threw that line forty feet, in the dark, to a moving target," she said, not looking up. "On a spinning deck."

"My father taught me to throw a mooring line before I could write my name."

"Mm." She tied off the last knot, precise as a surgeon. "Your father," she said, and stopped. Something moved behind her eyes and was locked away again, quick as a fish going under. "Your father taught you well."

That was the closest she ever came, in those days, to telling me the truth. I didn't know it yet. I sat there with my bandaged hands and my heart full of a fear I couldn't name, watching the mist burn off the slough, and I thought: *Papa is somewhere ahead of me, past these mountains, and these two broken people are the only bridge to him I have.*

Ferro raised his cup in a salute that spilled half of it. "To the Widow's Teeth," he croaked. "May she collect from richer men than us."

"To the Widow's Teeth," Elodie echoed dryly.

I drank to it too, from the same cup, and it was the worst coffee of my life, and I remember thinking—with a clarity that frightened me—that I would follow these two idiots anywhere.

Which was fortunate. Because when we raised anchor that afternoon, Elodie pointed the *Kestrel*'s nose upriver toward the smoke haze on the northern horizon, and said four words that turned my blood to ice water:

"Coldharbor by Thursday. Wren—we need to talk about what your neighbors are saying about your father."
