## 12. Coldharbor

I was born in Coldharbor in the spring, which the town would tell you is the only good thing that ever happened there in any season.

I'd forgotten how it smelled. That sounds like a small thing to forget about the place that made you, but smell is memory's sneakiest courier, and the first breath of it off the water hit me like a hand on the chest: spruce pitch and coal smoke and the sour-sweet rot of the tannery at the river's edge, where my father used to hold his sleeve over his nose and say, *Wren, if you ever get rich, buy the tannery just so you can burn it down.* I'd laughed every time. I'd believed he'd get rich. Children believe their fathers are giants; it takes years to learn they're only men standing close.

Now the Kestrel slid past the tannery with its cold fires and its sagging roof, and nobody had burned it down, and my father was dead on a stranger's boat, and I was coming home as a stowaway turned passenger, which is a promotion of sorts, though it didn't feel like one.

"You're quiet," Elodie said.

"I'm always quiet."

"You're quiet like a kettle," she said. "Something's building."

She stood at the wheel with her hands light on the spokes, reading the current the way other people read letters—fluently, without moving her lips. Three days upriver from Millbrook and she still hadn't asked me more than five questions total, which for a captain harboring a runaway girl was either remarkable restraint or remarkable strategy. I hadn't decided which. I hadn't decided anything since I'd told them what I overheard in the Coldharbor telegraph office, except that going back was better than running blind, and even that decision felt less like courage than like a dog returning to a kick.

The town came into view around the last bend the way it always did: all at once, unimpressively. A crescent of gray rooftops under the black shoulder of the Reyes Range. The mine headframes on the far slopes, two of them standing, three of them skeletons. The long dock with its pilings furred green at the waterline. The Coldharbor Hotel with its sign missing the H, so it advertised itself as the COLD ARBOR HOTEL, which Ferro said was at least honest advertising.

"There it is," Ferro said behind me, too gently.

I didn't turn around. "There it is."

* * *

We tied up at the north end of the dock, away from the Harrow barge that sat at the south end like a fat black tick. Two men in gray coats lounged on its deck pretending not to watch us, which meant they were watching us very carefully. Elodie noticed. Elodie noticed everything; it was her whole personality, as far as I could tell, along with sarcasm and an inability to say a kind sentence straight.

"Rules," she said quietly, once the gangplank was down. "We're a survey outfit hiring local labor. Ferro's the boss because he looks like a boss who's been cheated by life, which is authentic. I'm his pilot. You're—" She looked at me. "What do you want to be?"

"Nobody," I said. "Nobody from nowhere."

"That's my job," she said. "Pick another."

I wanted to say *Tomas Reyes's daughter*, which was the truest thing left in me, but the truth was exactly what I'd come home to test, so instead I said, "Clerk. Outfits keep clerks."

"Clerk," she agreed. "Clerks ask questions. Clerks are nosy by trade. Go be nosy."

Ferro handed me a notebook and a pencil like props in a play we were all pretending wasn't one. His hands shook—not fear, I'd learned by then, just the tremor of a man who drank his breakfast and regretted it by lunch—and when our fingers touched on the notebook he said, low, "Whatever you hear today, remember: towns lie about their dead. It's cheaper than telling the truth."

"What does that mean?"

"It means I've been the lie in a few towns myself." He smiled, and it was the worst smile I'd ever seen, and we went ashore.

* * *

Coldharbor has one street worth naming—Ledger Street, because everything in mining country is named after money—and I walked it like a ghost walking her own funeral procession. Nothing had changed, which is the cruelest thing a hometown can do. The same crooked boardwalk. The same dog asleep in front of the assay office, some descendant or reincarnation of the same dog. The same bell rope hanging outside the church, frayed where boys had swung on it, where I had swung on it, while Papa pretended not to see and then bought me a cinnamon twist at Maren's bakery to seal the conspiracy.

Maren's bakery was still there. Maren herself was behind the counter, grayer, wider, squinting at me through the window glass with the dawning expression of a woman watching a ghost order bread.

"Wren?" she said. "Little Wren Reyes?"

"Hello, Mrs. Maren."

She came around the counter fast and took my face in her floury hands, and for one whole moment I was nine years old and safe, and I nearly wept into the cinnamon rolls. Then her eyes went past my shoulder to the window, to the empty street, and back to me, and her face did something complicated.

"You're alone," she said.

"I'm working with a survey crew."

"Alone," she repeated, meaning something else entirely, and I understood that she was asking after my father without asking, the way people do in small towns, where direct questions are considered a form of undressing.

"He's dead," I said. Because it was true, and because I wanted to see what her face did next.

What it did was nothing. That was the terrible part. No shock, no grief, no gasp of *oh, the poor man*. Just a slow nod, like a debt finally collected, and a glance toward the door to make sure no one else had heard.

"When?" she asked.

"Recently. On the river."

"And before that? Where was he these—" she counted, lips moving— "nine years?"

"I don't know," I said. "That's why I'm here. Nobody would tell me anything in the telegraph office. They hung up on me."

Mrs. Maren wiped her hands on her apron, twice, three times, buying time. Behind her the oven ticked and breathed. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard creaked.

"Wren," she said, "you should talk to Odile Prue. She kept the boarding house your father stayed in. And—" the hesitation was a wall, and she walked right up to it and stopped— "and you should know that folks here will have things to say. Things that aren't kind. Your father left this town under a cloud, child, and clouds around here don't rain out. They just sit."

"What cloud?"

She looked at me with real pity, which is worse than anger, and said, "Ask Odile. I sell bread. I don't sell that."

* * *

Odile Prue's boarding house leaned against the hillside above the assay office, and Odile herself answered the door already dressed for company, which told me Maren had sent word ahead by the simple expedient of shouting across the street. She was a small hard woman with eyes like nail heads, and she looked me up and down and said, "You have his chin."

"Thank you?"

"It wasn't a compliment. Come in."

The parlor smelled of lamp oil and dried lavender doing a losing battle against both. Photographs covered one wall in mismatched frames—the boarding house gallery of everyone Odile had ever fed—and she went straight to the third row, second from the left, and took down a frame and put it in my hands.

My father. Younger than I'd ever known him, maybe twenty-five, standing on the steps of the Coldharbor assay office with his arm around a man I didn't recognize, both of them holding up claim stakes crossed like swords. He was laughing. I had never once seen my father laugh like that—open, unguarded, his whole face lit like a struck match. The man beside him had a mustache and a silver watch chain and a smile that didn't reach anywhere near his eyes.

"Who's this?" I asked.

"Harrow," Odile said. "Corvus Dain's cousin, back when the Harrows were just another outfit with more ambition than sense. That was taken the day your father and that man staked the Larkspur claim together. Silver vein, high in the pass country. Biggest strike this range ever whispered about."

"Larkspur," I said. The word landed somewhere in my ribs. My father used to sing a song about larkspur flowers, silly and tuneless, and I'd always thought it was about flowers.

"The claim was never worked," Odile said. She sat down across from me with the finality of a judge. "Because three weeks after that photograph, the claim records burned in the assay office fire, and when the smoke cleared, the Larkspur was registered to the Harrow Syndicate, and Tomas Reyes was accused of selling it to them and skipping town with the money."

The parlor got very quiet. The stove ticked. Outside, someone's cart rattled down Ledger Street, unhurried, a town going about its afternoon on top of my grave.

"That's a lie," I said.

"Probably," Odile said.

I blinked. Of all the responses in the world, I hadn't budgeted for agreement.

"Excuse me?"

"Probably a lie," she repeated. "But here's what I know, girl, and I'll give it to you straight because your father ate at my table for six years and never once shorted me, and never once let another man be shorted either, and I've watched enough men to know what that costs a person. I know Tomas Reyes was the honestest fool this town ever produced. I also know he signed a paper confessing to the sale rather than stand trial. I know he left in the night owing half the town money, including me, eleven dollars, which I have resented with great artistry for nine years. And I know that the fire that burned those records started in a room only Harrow men had keys to." She folded her hands. "So. Probably a lie. But it's the lie everybody here believes, because believing it is easier than believing the alternative."

"Which is?"

"Which is that the Harrows stole the richest claim in the mountains off a prospector with no family and no lawyer, and that your father ran rather than hang for it, and that he let every soul in this town—including you, wherever you were—think him a thief, because a thief gets sneered at and a dead man gets buried." Her nail-head eyes held mine. "Which story keeps a daughter safer, do you think? The one where her pa's a coward and a cheat, or the one where the most powerful syndicate in the territory wants him in the ground?"

I couldn't answer. My hands were shaking around the photograph frame, and I set it down on the table with a click that sounded enormous.

"He had a map," I heard myself say. "He—he found something. In the high country. He was bringing it to me."

Odile went still in a way that made the whole room go still with her.

"A map," she said. "To the Larkspur."

"I didn't say that."

"You didn't have to. Child, listen to me, and then forget I said it if you've any sense. There were others looking. Men came through last month asking after Tomas Reyes, showing his likeness around, saying he owed the Syndicate money. Polite men. Men whose politeness had teeth in it. And now you come up the river on a strange boat with a surveyor and a woman who ties up like she's dodging tolls, and you're carrying a map, and the Harrow barge is sitting at the south dock like it's waiting for something." She leaned forward. "Whatever your father gave you, it isn't a map. It's a confession. The Larkspur records didn't all burn. Everyone always assumed the originals went up with the rest—but Tomas Reyes was the one man alive who knew every landmark on that vein by heart, because he walked it for two years before anyone else laid eyes on it. If he drew it out, he drew out the truth. And the Harrows can't have a piece of paper walking around that proves what they did."

The room tilted, very slightly, the way a boat tilts. Nine years of a story rearranged themselves inside me—the drunk, the drifter, the man who never wrote, never visited, never explained—and underneath the ugly shape of him I suddenly saw scaffolding: a man holding up a lie the way you hold up a roof beam, with your own back, for years, so the house doesn't fall on the people inside.

"Why didn't he tell me?" I said. It came out smaller than I meant it to. "All those years. Why let me think—"

"Because the truth needs you brave," Odile said, "and children shouldn't have to be brave. Cowardice was the kindest thing he ever did for you, and I expect it cost him more than the mountain ever did." She stood, brisk again, the interview over. "Eleven dollars," she added. "When this is settled, I want my eleven dollars."

It was so absurd, so perfectly, stubbornly human, that I laughed—a cracked, wet sound—and she almost smiled, and then she walked me to the door and did the last thing I expected. She gripped my wrist, hard, with those small hard hands.

"They'll be watching the docks," she said. "And the telegraph office. Don't sleep on this boat tonight if you can help it, and don't tell the pilot woman more than you trust her with, and if a polite man asks you questions, remember that politeness has teeth." She opened the door onto the gray afternoon. "Your father had that chin too. Stubborn as a mule and twice as likely to get shot at."

* * *

I walked back down Ledger Street through a town that had become unfamiliar in the space of an hour. Same crooked boardwalk. Same sleeping dog. But now every window felt like an eye, and every friendly nod felt like a ledger entry, and I understood that I had spent seventeen years living inside a story the whole town had agreed to tell, and I had never once been told I was a character in it.

Elodie was waiting at the gangplank, arms crossed, watching the Harrow barge with the flat attention of a hawk watching a snake.

"Well, clerk?" she said. "Learn anything?"

I looked at her—at this sharp, closed, sarcastic woman who'd hauled a dying stranger aboard and lied to collectors and run rapids in the dark and never once asked me for anything but honesty—and I decided, in that moment, with the certainty of a girl whose whole world had just been remodeled, to bet on her.

"I learned my father didn't steal anything," I said. "I learned the Harrows did. And I learned that the map you're carrying isn't a treasure map, Captain. It might be the only proof in the world of what they took from him."

For the first time since I'd known her, Elodie Voss looked genuinely surprised. Then her eyes cut south, to the barge, to the gray coats, to the polite men whose patience was a kind of teeth.

"Below decks," she said. "Now. We're leaving within the hour—and Wren? Whatever else that map is, I suspect we just became the most valuable cargo on this river."
