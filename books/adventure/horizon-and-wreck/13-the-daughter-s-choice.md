## 13. The Daughter's Choice

I had been on the *Kestrel* for six days before I understood that the boat was not a boat.

Oh, it floated. It had a wheel and a boiler and a name painted on the stern in flaking gold leaf, and Captain Voss—Elodie, she'd finally allowed, though never "Captain Elodie," which apparently made her itch—handled it like a pianist handles a keyboard, all touch and no noise. But a boat is a thing that goes somewhere on purpose. The *Kestrel* went places the way smoke goes places: sideways, evasively, with an air of having meant to do that all along.

I learned this from Augustin Ferro, who was the only person aboard who talked to me like I was a person rather than a stowaway awaiting sentencing. Ferro was a long, folded-up man with ink-stained cuffs and the posture of someone who had spent years apologizing to furniture. He sat across from me at supper on the sixth night, stirring beans he wasn't eating, and said:

"You know she hasn't told you yet."

"Told me what?"

He looked at me over his spectacles with an expression I would come to know very well—the look of a man deciding how much truth a situation can hold without capsizing. "Ask her about Coldharbor," he said. "Ask her why we're going."

"We're going because I'm going home. She was kind enough to give me passage." I said it lightly. I had gotten good at saying things lightly; it ran in my family, along with other inheritances I didn't yet know about.

Ferro set down his spoon. "Miss Reyes. Your father hired this boat."

The river slapped against the hull. Somewhere upstream a loon called, that sound like laughter played backwards.

"He didn't hire anything," I said. "My father's been dead for—" And then I stopped, because I heard it, the seam in my own sentence, the place where the story I'd been telling myself since Coldharbor didn't close. *Dead for three years.* Said with such confidence. Based on what? A letter that stopped coming? A neighbor's shrug? The way grief fills in facts the way water fills a hole, without asking permission?

Ferro watched me arrive there. He didn't help. That was the first decent thing he did for me, though it didn't feel decent at the time.

"Where is she?" I asked.

"Pilot house. Where else."

* * *

I climbed the ladder two rungs at a time, which was one more than the captain considered polite, and found her exactly where Ferro said: at the wheel, hat low, eyes doing that thing they did—reading the water the way Ferro read maps, every ripple a word, every shadow a footnote. The Vell ran amber in the evening light, wide and slow here, deceptively gentle. Everything about that river was deceptive. So was everything about her, I was starting to suspect.

"You knew," I said.

She didn't turn. "Knew what?"

"Don't. Don't do the thing where you answer questions with questions until people forget what they asked. Ferro told me my father hired this boat. Hired. Past tense. Which means you knew him, which means when you fished a half-drowned telegraph girl out of your cargo hold and didn't put her off at the next landing, you weren't being charitable. You were being careful. There's a difference, and I've had six days to learn it."

Now she turned. The wheel creaked under her hands. Up close, in the last of the light, I could see the tiredness under her composure—not the tiredness of a long day but of a long year, maybe several, the kind that settles into a face like silt.

"Sit down, Wren."

"I'll stand."

"Suit yourself. You'll want to be sitting."

* * *

She told it plainly. That was the worst part. No softening, no circling, none of the elaborate cushioning adults build around bad news for children. She told it the way she read the river: straight through, marking every hazard.

Tomas Reyes, half-drowned in the reeds below Miller's Bend, shot twice, delirious with fever. Hauled aboard against her better judgment—her words, and she did not dress them up. An oilskin packet clutched to his chest like a second heart. A promise demanded: carry the packet to Wren Reyes in Coldharbor before the passes closed. Her refusal. His dying anyway. Two Harrow collectors walking her deck with their boots too clean for a working boat, asking after a thief with a packet. Her lying smoothly, and knowing even as she lied that smooth lies buy time, not safety. And then dawn, and Tomas Reyes dead in her bunk, and the packet opened, and a map inside drawn in a hand she now knew belonged to my father.

"He died on this boat," I said. My voice came out strange. Flat. Like someone else's voice heard through a wall.

"Three weeks ago. Below Miller's Bend."

"And you let me come aboard. You let me eat your food, sleep in your berth, scrub your deck—you let me talk about him. I told you about him. I told you about the summers, the pan in the creek, the way he whistled when he thought nobody was listening—and you knew. You knew he was dead and you let me keep talking."

"Yes," she said.

That was all. Yes. No excuse offered, no hand extended, no apology arranged into something palatable. Just yes, delivered with those flat dark eyes that gave back nothing except the exact weight of what she'd done.

"I was going to tell you," she added, after a moment. "In Coldharbor. At his grave, if there was one. Or somewhere with solid ground under your feet. Not on my boat, where you couldn't walk away from me."

"You don't get to decide when I find out my father died."

"No," she agreed. "I don't. I decided anyway. That's who I am. Now you know."

I hit her.

I want to be honest about this, because everything afterward depended on it: I hit her, a full-armed swing that caught her cheekbone and spun her half around, and she took it, and she did not hit me back, and she did not even steady herself on the wheel, and when she turned back to face me there was blood at the corner of her mouth and absolutely no surprise in her expression. As if she'd been standing in that pilot house for three weeks waiting for exactly that blow, holding the door open for it.

"Good," she said, and spat red over the rail. "Now we can talk."

* * *

We talked until the stars came out and the river went black glass, and I will not pretend I remember all of it coherently. Grief does that—it takes the memory of a night and shakes it like a rug, so what falls out is not events but weights. The weight of her voice saying *he asked for you by name*. The weight of learning he'd been shot running from Harrow men, that he'd died with my name in his mouth and a stranger's blanket over him. The weight of the map itself, which she finally brought up from her cabin—a rolled oilskin she handled like it might go off, unrolling it on the chart table while I stood there shaking.

It was his handwriting. That undid me more than the death had. Death is abstract; handwriting is not. There were the little hooks he put on his letters, the way he crossed his sevens, the marginal notes in pencil—*water high here in June, don't trust the scree*—the same notes he used to make on grocery lists, on the backs of envelopes, on the slate by our door. *Back by Friday. Feed the cat or don't, she's your problem now.*

He'd written *don't trust the scree* and *feed the cat or don't* in the same hand, and one of those sentences outlived him, and it was the wrong one.

"Why didn't you sell it?" I asked, when I could speak again. "You owe these people money. Ferro told me that much. This map could clear your debt. One promise to a dying man, and you could have walked away free."

Something moved behind her face—quick, gone, like a fish turning under the surface.

"I tried to burn it," she said.

"And?"

"It wouldn't take." She said it as if the map had opinions, and maybe, in the end, it did. "Paper burns fine. Promises don't. I stood there with it over the stove and I couldn't make my hand let go. Because if I burned it, I broke my word to a dead man, and if I sold it, I broke it worse. So I'm carrying it to you instead, which clears nothing, pays nothing, and puts me square in the Harrows' sights. In case you're keeping score of what your father cost me."

"That's not fair."

"No," she said. "It isn't. Fairness is for people who can afford it."

* * *

I went below after that. I don't know for how long. I remember the bunk, and Ferro's coat laid over me at some point without comment, and the engine's rhythm underneath everything like a heartbeat that wasn't mine but kept mine going anyway. I remember thinking, with a clarity that felt almost cruel: *everyone on this boat knows more about my father than I do.*

The neighbors in Coldharbor had said he left in disgrace. Stole a claim, they said. Ran out on debts, they said. For three years I had built my life on top of that story the way you build a cabin on permafrost—carefully, pretending the ground was solid. And now the ground had shifted, and everything I'd built was listing, and the man who'd actually known Tomas Reyes at the end was a riverboat captain with blood at the corner of her mouth who owed her life to a syndicate and her honesty to no one.

I came back up near midnight. She was still at the wheel. Of course she was. I began to suspect the woman slept standing up, like a horse, or possibly not at all, like a rumor.

"The men who shot him," I said. "Harrow men. Are they still looking?"

"They're always still looking."

"For the map."

"For the map," she said, "for whoever carries the map, and lately, I'd guess, for a seventeen-year-old girl who ran away from Coldharbor the same week a dead prospector's packet surfaced. You're not invisible, Wren. None of us are. We're just temporarily unobserved."

I looked at the black river sliding past, and I thought about my father's hands—I could see them so clearly, scarred knuckles, dirt in the creases that never fully washed out—and I thought about the fact that he had spent his last days trying to reach me. Not the silver. Me. The silver was the cargo; I was the destination. Some stubborn, buried part of me had known that since Ferro said the word *hired*, and it had been waiting, patient as a seed under snow, for the rest of me to catch up.

"I'm coming with you," I said.

She didn't laugh, which I respected. "You're going home. That was the arrangement."

"The arrangement was between you and a dying man. I wasn't party to it." I stepped closer, into the lamplight, so she'd have to see my face and not just hear my voice. "You think I want to go back to Coldharbor and sit in a house full of neighbors who call him a thief? You think I want the version of him that town keeps in its mouth? He carried this map through bullets to get it to me. Whatever's at the end of it—silver, or proof, or just the truth of what happened to him—that's the only inheritance I've got, and I am not letting it sit in your cabin while I dust my mother's chairs."

"It's dangerous."

"So was the river the night you pulled him out. He went anyway."

"That's exactly my point," she said sharply, and then caught herself, and I saw it land on her—what she'd just admitted, that she'd been arguing with herself about this for three weeks, that some part of her had already cast me in this journey whether she liked it or not.

"Elodie." I used her name deliberately, the whole of it, no title, and watched her jaw tighten. "I don't care about the silver. Let the Harrows have the silver, let them choke on it. I'm coming to find out who my father really was. The man who taught me to read water signs on our creek without ever telling me why. The man the town says was a thief and the Harrows thought was worth shooting. Those can't both be true, and I won't know which one is real unless I follow his map to the end. You can drop me at Coldharbor and deliver a packet to an empty house, or you can take me with you and keep your promise to a living Reyes. Your choice. But I'm going either way—on your boat or on the next one, and the next one won't have Ferro to patch my blisters."

For a long moment she said nothing. The wheel ticked under her hands. Out on the water, moonlight lay across the current like a spilled ribbon, and somewhere far downstream—downstream, always downstream, that was the direction her whole life pointed—I imagined the Harrow boats with their clean boots, patient as winter.

"You argue like him," she said at last, and her voice had changed, gone quieter, rougher at the edges. "He cornered me the same way. Politely. With logic. Like a man building a fence around you one post at a time and then acting surprised you're enclosed."

"Is that a yes?"

"It's a yes," she said, "and I already hate it." She turned the wheel a few degrees, easy, unconscious, correcting for a current only she could feel. "Conditions. You learn the boat. You learn the river. When I say run, you run, and when I say hide, you hide, and you don't play hero—not once, not ever. Heroics are how your father ended up in my reeds."

"And if the mountain says otherwise?"

"Then the mountain and I will discuss it," she said, "and the mountain will lose."

I should have laughed. Maybe I did, a little, wetly, in the dark where she couldn't see. Because I believed her. That was the terrible, wonderful thing—I believed her completely, this stranger who'd lied to me by omission for six days and taken my punch like a debt she'd been expecting to pay. Somewhere between Miller's Bend and midnight, without my noticing, the *Kestrel* had stopped being a boat I was hiding on and become a boat I belonged to, and its impossible captain had stopped being my father's creditor and become something I didn't have a word for yet.

Below deck, Ferro was awake—I could see his lamp burning—and when I climbed down he looked up from his charts with the question already on his face.

"Well?" he said.

"She said yes."

Ferro nodded slowly, and then, to my astonishment, he smiled—a real one, crooked and tired and relieved, the smile of a man watching a debt of his own begin, at long last, to come due. He pulled a chart toward him, weighted its corners with his cup and his spectacles case, and tapped one finger on the high country north of Coldharbor, where the contour lines crowded together like frightened sheep.

"Then you'd best look at this," he said. "Because I've seen the route your father marked, and there's a storm coming down early this year—and the pass we need may already be closing."
