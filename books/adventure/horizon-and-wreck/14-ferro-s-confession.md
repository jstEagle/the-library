## 14. Ferro's Confession

There is a particular kind of silence a man carries when he knows something that will destroy him if spoken and eat him alive if he doesn't. I had been carrying mine for eleven years, and it had grown teeth.

I was on the roof of the *Kestrel*'s cabin, ostensibly checking the caulking, actually hiding from the girl. Wren Reyes sat below on the cargo hatch with her father's old prospecting pan across her knees, turning it over the way you'd turn over a letter from someone you loved who had wronged you. Every time she looked at me with those gray Reyes eyes—Tomas's eyes, God help me—I felt the silence shift its weight in my chest like ballast gone bad.

Elodie Voss stood at the wheel pretending she wasn't watching me pretend to work. She was good at pretending. Better than me. That should have been my first warning about her, and it wasn't, which tells you how far gone I already was.

"Your seam's leaking," she called up to me.

"My seam is fine."

"Your seam is weeping like a widow. Come down before you fall through and drown in four feet of cargo."

I came down. It seemed safer than arguing with a woman who ran rapids for a living and lied to Syndicate collectors the way other people said good morning.

* * *

Let me tell you how a man ends up disgraced, drunk, and hiring himself out by the season on a river he once mapped from source to delta. It doesn't happen all at once. That's the lie men tell themselves—that there was a moment, a single catastrophic choice, a crossroads with a signpost. There isn't. There's just a slope so gentle you don't notice you're walking downhill until you look back one morning and can't see the top anymore.

I was thirty-one years old and the best surveyor in the northern ranges. Not good—*best*. I could read terrain the way Elodie reads water, which is probably why she hired me without asking too many questions. Two liars recognize each other the way dogs do. We'd been circling that truth since Millbrook, polite as dancers.

The Harrows came to me in the autumn of the year Tomas Reyes found his vein. A man named Ostergaard, soft-spoken, well-dressed, smelling of pipe smoke and money. He didn't threaten me. He didn't need to. He made an offer wrapped in a compliment: the Reyes claim assessment, the final assay report that would determine whether the claim held or reverted. The numbers were extraordinary. Too extraordinary. A vein that rich would redraw every map in the territory, and the Harrows did not intend to let one prospector with a mule and a dream redraw anything.

"You'll find," Ostergaard said, "that the samples are not what they appear. Ore of that quality degrades at depth. Everyone knows this. Your report need only reflect what everyone knows."

And I—here is the part I have never said aloud, not to the licensing board that struck my name, not to the priests whose doors I stopped darkening, not to anyone—I *looked at the samples myself first.* I assayed them twice in my own camp because I couldn't believe them. And they were real. The vein was real. Tomas Reyes had found exactly what he said he'd found, and it went down into the mountain like the root of a tree.

Then I wrote the report saying it didn't.

They paid me eight hundred dollars. I told myself it was for my silence about other things, about debts I owed, about a brother-in-law whose gambling the Harrows held over me like a knife at a throat. All true. None of it mattered. A man can be coerced and still be guilty; the two conditions are not mutually exclusive, though we spend our whole lives pretending otherwise.

Tomas Reyes lost his claim on the strength of my signature. He lost his reputation—he was accused of salting the samples, of fraud, of worse. He left Coldharbor in disgrace while the Harrows quietly filed their own paperwork on land everyone now agreed was worthless, and sat on it for eleven years waiting for the world to forget, which the world obligingly did.

Everyone except the man in the reeds.

* * *

"You're doing it again," Elodie said.

We were in the cabin that evening, lamplight low, the river muttering against the hull. Wren had turned in early—or pretended to; I heard her breathing change when she thought we weren't listening, which meant she was listening.

"Doing what?"

"That thing where your face goes somewhere else and comes back looking like it lost a fight." She poured two fingers of whiskey into a tin cup and set it in front of me without ceremony. "Drink it or don't. But stop brooding where the girl can see you. She watches you like you're a book she's trying to read upside down."

"She shouldn't."

"No," Elodie agreed. "She shouldn't. But she does. Children of absent fathers do that—they go looking for the shape of the man in every older man they meet." She said it flatly, but I caught the flinch under it, the one she kept filed away with everything else. "So either be worth finding or get off the boat at the next landing."

I drank the whiskey. It was terrible. Everything on the *Kestrel* was terrible except the captain's nerve, and I was beginning to suspect even that was a loaner.

"I knew him," I said.

Elodie went still. She had a way of going still that most people achieve only in death. "Knew who?"

"Reyes. Tomas Reyes." The name came out of me like a splinter that had worked too deep to pull clean. "Eleven years ago. I surveyed his claim."

The lamp flame leaned in the draft from the door. Somewhere above us, the rigging ticked against the mast. Elodie didn't say anything, which from her was a kind of mercy—she understood, better than anyone I've known, that a confession needs room the way a birth does, and crowding it kills it.

"I signed the report that took his mountain away from him," I said. "The Harrows paid me to call the vein barren. It wasn't. It was the richest strike in the territory, maybe the century. I held the ore in my hands and wrote down that it was dirt." I turned the empty cup in circles on the table. "He spent eleven years being called a liar and a thief because I wanted eight hundred dollars and a clean conscience I could pretend I still had."

"And now he's dead in your bunk," Elodie said quietly, "and his daughter is asleep ten feet from you."

"Yes."

"Well." She refilled my cup. Her hand was steady; mine wasn't. "That's a hell of a thing to carry up a glacier."

"I'm not telling you so you'll absolve me. I'm telling you because—" I stopped. Because why? Because the map in her strongbox described terrain I knew like my own handwriting? Because every landmark on it—the split horn, the hanging glacier, the blue notch—I had walked with a transit and a chain and a dying man's hope, and I had buried all of it under a lie for money? Because the girl with her father's eyes was going to stand on that mountain someday soon and I could not let her learn what I was from anyone but me?

"Because she deserves to hear it from a man who's sorry," I finished, "not from a Harrow man who's gloating."

Elodie studied me for a long moment. In the lamplight her face was all angles and river-logic, a woman built out of current and consequence. "You understand she might hate you."

"I'm counting on it. Hate is honest. It's the only currency I've got left that isn't counterfeit."

Something moved behind her eyes—not softness exactly, but recognition. "Ferro. When you tell her—and you will tell her, because if you don't I will—you tell her all of it. Not the version where you were pressured. The version where you had the samples in your hand."

I looked at her. "You know."

"I know you recognized the landmarks the night I showed you the map. You touched the paper like it was a grave marker." She stood, taking the bottle with her. "Get some sleep, surveyor. Confessions keep. Mountains don't wait."

* * *

It took me three more days to work up to it, which will tell you everything about courage that the storybooks leave out. Courage isn't the absence of fear. Courage is fear that has finally run out of excuses.

The evening I chose was ordinary, which felt right. Ordinary crimes deserve ordinary gallows. We were tied up below the Narrows, woodsmoke on the water, Wren cleaning fish on the deck with the quick competence of someone raised poor and practical. Elodie was below with the engine, cursing it in a language that was half mechanical and half theological.

"Wren."

She looked up. Fish blood on her hands, hair escaping its braid, seventeen years old and carrying a grief she hadn't been given the full weight of yet. "Mr. Ferro."

"Augustin," I said. "If you're going to hate me, you may as well hate me by my Christian name."

Her brow creased. "Why would I hate you?"

I sat down on the hatch cover across from her, knees complaining, heart worse. And I told her.

All of it. The way Elodie had ordered—no varnish, no mitigating circumstances dressed up as virtues. The autumn, the offer, the assays I ran myself because I hoped the samples were salted and they weren't. The report. The signature. The eight hundred dollars, which I confessed I had spent within a year and could not account for a single dollar of it, which is somehow the most shameful part. The hearing where I sat in the gallery and watched Tomas Reyes stand alone in front of the mining board and swear on his life that the vein was real, and watched them laugh at him, and said nothing.

"He looked at me," I said. "In that room. After. He looked right at me, and I think—I've told myself for eleven years he didn't know. But he knew. Prospectors know rock, and I think he knew men, and he looked at me the way you look at weather coming in." My voice cracked on the last of it and I let it. A confession that doesn't cost anything isn't one. "I destroyed your father, Wren. Not with a gun. With a pen. And then I hid on rivers for a decade because pens are easier to hide behind than guns."

The fish lay forgotten, bleeding gently on the planks between us. Wren hadn't moved. Her face had gone through shock and arrived somewhere past it, a country I recognized from the inside.

"The map," she said finally. Her voice was very quiet. "The map Elodie's carrying. You knew the landmarks."

"I surveyed that mountain. Every contour on that page passed through my hands before it reached yours." I swallowed. "Which means I'm the only man alive besides your father who can read it perfectly. Which means the Harrows will want me dead almost as much as they want the map. Which means—"

"Stop." She held up a hand, fish blood and all. Her eyes were wet but nothing fell, and I thought: *Reyes stubbornness. It's in the blood. I helped steal the proof of it and here it is anyway, indestructible.* "Did you know he was alive? When you signed on with Captain Voss. Did you know?"

"No. I swear it on whatever's left of my soul. I learned his name from Elodie on the dock at Millbrook, and I nearly walked off the boat then. I should have."

"Why didn't you?"

Because I'm a coward. Because the river was warm and the shore was cold. Because guilt is a debt and I've never paid a debt in my life, that's rather the theme. I opened my mouth to give her one of these and what came out instead was the truth:

"Because I saw you look at the river the way he looked at that room full of men who laughed at him. Like you'd been laughed at your whole life and had decided to outlast all of them. And I thought—if his daughter is that, then maybe something survived what I did. Maybe not everything I broke stayed broken." I wiped my face with a shaking hand. "It was selfish. Everything I do is selfish. But I stayed because I wanted to see it."

Wren was silent a long time. The river talked to itself against the pilings. Below us, Elodie had gone suspiciously quiet by the engine—listening, certainly, and I found I didn't mind. Let the whole territory hear it. Nail it to the pilot house.

"You're coming the whole way," Wren said at last. It wasn't a question.

"If you'll have me. If you won't, I'm coming anyway, and you can push me off at the glacier."

"Don't tempt me." But something in her face shifted—a degree, no more, the ice moving a finger's width off the bank. "My father taught me things when I was small. Panning tricks, reading scree, which stars mean weather. I never knew why a man who'd supposedly salted a claim would teach his daughter to prospect like she was born to it." She looked down at her stained hands. "Now I know. He was keeping the truth alive somewhere the Harrows couldn't burn it. He put it in *me*."

"Yes," I said. "That's exactly what he did."

"And you're going to help me prove it. On the mountain. In front of whoever's left to see."

"With my last breath, if it comes to that. Though I'd prefer it didn't. I bruise easily and I complain constantly."

The ghost of a smile crossed her face and vanished, and it was the most expensive thing I'd ever been given. She picked up her knife and went back to the fish, and our conversation was over, and I sat there feeling lighter and heavier at once, the way a man feels after amputation.

* * *

Elodie found me at the rail near midnight. She handed me coffee instead of whiskey, which from her was a sacrament.

"She didn't throw you overboard."

"Not yet. The night's young."

"She'll come around. Reyeses hold grudges like they hold ore—deep, and worth digging for eventually." She leaned on the rail beside me, watching the black water slide past. Then, without looking at me: "Ostergaard. The man who bought your report. Is he still with the Syndicate?"

The cold that went through me had nothing to do with the river air. "Senior. Very senior. Why?"

"Because the collector who boarded my boat in chapter one of this disaster—Dain's people—used his name. Twice. Like a password. Like a *warning*." She turned her gray eyes on me. "If Ostergaard is running this retrieval personally, Ferro, then this isn't about a map anymore. It's about cleanup. Eleven-year-old cleanup. And you're not a witness they've overlooked."

"I'm a witness they've been meaning to get to," I said. "I've always assumed so. It's why I drink."

"Well." Elodie pushed off the rail and headed for the pilot house, then paused. "Drink less. From here upriver, I need every man aboard sober, armed, and honest." A thin smile. "Two out of three will do."

Ahead of us, far up the black channel, the signal lamps of the Narrows lock blinked green—then, briefly, red. Green. Red. A pattern no lock keeper uses.

Elodie saw it the same instant I did. I heard her breath change.

"Somebody's signaling our arrival," she said softly, "to somebody who's already there."

The red lamp blinked again, patient as a held breath, and the *Kestrel* carried us straight toward it.
