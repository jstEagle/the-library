## 15. The Pass Closes Early

The first sign that the mountain intended to ruin my season was a smell.

Most people think weather announces itself with clouds, or wind, or some theatrical darkening of the sky. But I've spent enough years on the Vell to know better. Weather comes in through the nose first—a green, mineral tang riding down off the peaks like a rumor, the smell of snow that hasn't fallen yet but has already made up its mind. My father called it *the mountain clearing its throat*. He said it right before the storm that took our boat and half our savings, so you can imagine how much comfort the phrase gives me now.

I smelled it on the third morning out of Coldharbor, standing at the wheel while the Kestrel shouldered her way upriver against a current gone sluggish and gray. Ferro was on the roof deck behind me, squinting at the range through a brass spyglass he polished more often than he used it. Wren was below, ostensibly organizing provisions and actually reading one of Ferro's old survey journals like it was a romance novel.

"Elodie," Ferro said, and the fact that he used my name instead of *Captain* told me everything before he said another word. "What do you know about the Kettle Pass road?"

"Enough to know I don't want to be on it." I eased us around a deadfall snagging the current, all of this by feel now—the water had gone the flat, sullen quiet it gets when the air pressure drops and the river stops bothering to lie about what's coming. "Why?"

"Because there's a wall of cloud sitting on the divide that shouldn't be there until mid-month. Maybe later." He lowered the spyglass. His hands were steady. His voice wasn't, quite. "That's an early winter, Captain. That's weeks early."

I looked north. The mountains had been visible for two days—white-capped, indifferent, the kind of scenery that makes passengers say *oh, how beautiful* and makes boat captains calculate fuel. Now there was a bruise-colored mass crouched over the high country, and even from thirty miles off I could see it was moving the way weather moves when it isn't wasting time.

"How early?" Wren asked from the companionway. She'd come up without either of us hearing her, which was becoming a habit I'd have to break her of if she planned to survive me.

Ferro did the arithmetic with his eyes fixed on the cloud. "If it holds pace? The pass could seal inside four days."

*Four days.* We were two days' hard travel from the trailhead at Stonebridge Landing, and we hadn't bought so much as a sack of flour yet, because provisioning in Coldharbor had felt too much like announcing ourselves, and I'd wanted distance between us and Dain's people before we started buying mule feed under assumed names. Caution is a virtue right up until it's a coffin nail.

"Then we make Stonebridge tomorrow," I said, "and buy out the outfitter tonight, and be on the trail the morning after."

"Captain." Ferro's voice had gone careful, the way it did when he was about to tell me something he'd already rehearsed. That was the trouble with surveyors—they rehearse bad news the way priests rehearse funerals. "Even if we beat the storm to the pass, we won't beat it through it. The Kettle road crosses three open faces above the treeline. One snowfall up there doesn't close the route—it buries it. Avalanches slide all winter. Nobody goes over until thaw. Nobody."

The wheel was warm under my hands, or maybe that was just me. "Then we go around."

"You can't go around a mountain range, Elodie."

"You can go *through* it," I said, "if you're stupid in the right direction. Isn't that what your profession calls a corridor?"

Ferro went very still, the way men do when they realize a conversation they've been dreading has arrived ahead of schedule. Wren looked between us like a spectator at a card game where she didn't know the stakes but could smell blood.

"The glacier," she said.

Both of us turned. She shrugged, unrepentant. "My father's letters. He wrote about it once—said the only honest road through the Veragans was the one the ice built. Said the meltwater opens a channel every summer during calving season, and if you're quick, you can walk the corridor end to end." Her voice caught slightly on *my father*, the way it still did, and I pretended not to notice because pretending not to notice is a kindness I've learned to give. "He said it was open maybe three weeks. Four if the summer ran hot."

"That was July," Ferro said gently. "Wren, it's nearly October. Calving season is done. The corridor will be closing, not opening—"

"It opens twice," Wren said, and there was something new in her voice, something stubborn and bright that I recognized because I'd spent my whole life mistaking it for recklessness in myself. "The autumn calving. When the glacier grinds against the valley wall, the meltwater backs up and reopens the low channel for a few weeks before freeze. Papa called it the *second breath*. He said most people don't even know it happens because nobody's fool enough to be up there in October."

She said it with a teenager's certainty, the absolute conviction of someone quoting scripture they've never tested. And Ferro opened his mouth to dismantle her—and then closed it again, because he was looking at the map.

Not Tomas's map. That stayed wrapped in oilskin inside my coat, against my ribs, where it had lived long enough to feel like a second heartbeat. Ferro was looking at his own survey plates, the ones he'd carried out of these ranges eight years ago along with his reputation. He spread one across the chart table, weighted the corners with a tin cup, a belaying pin, Wren's abandoned journal, and my patience.

"There is a second breath," he admitted. "It's not in any guidebook because the window's too short and the approach is too ugly. Meltwater channels reopen along the glacier's eastern margin—here, and here—" his finger traced a line I couldn't read but understood anyway, the way you understand a scar on someone else's body, "—and if the autumn calving runs true, you get eighteen days. Twenty if you're lucky. Then the channels freeze from both ends toward the middle, and whatever's inside them when that happens stays inside them until June."

"Eighteen days," I repeated.

"At best. And that assumes the calving starts on schedule, which—" he tapped the spyglass against the cloud wall on the horizon, "—nothing about this season suggests."

* * *

We tied up that night at a place called Halfway Eddy, which is named with the honesty of all river landmarks: it was halfway to nowhere in particular, a shelf of gravel and dead cottonwoods where the current took a rest and so did we. I built a fire small enough to hide with my body. Wren cooked beans and salt pork with a competence that surprised me—I kept forgetting she'd run a boardinghouse kitchen in Coldharbor since she was twelve, feeding miners who paid late and complained early. There's a particular toughness in people who've fed strangers their whole childhood. It looks like softness right up until it doesn't.

Ferro sat apart, working his way through his survey plates by firelight, muttering elevations to himself like a man doing penance in numbers. I left him to it. A man counting his sins needs privacy even when the sins are actually contour lines.

Wren came and sat beside me on the gravel, close enough that our shoulders touched, which she'd started doing around Coldharbor and which I hadn't found a way to stop without making it a thing. On the river you learn to read silences. Hers had changed over the past week—from the wary silence of a stowaway waiting to be put ashore, to something else. Something that leaned.

"If the pass is buried," she said, "and the glacier window is short—"

"Then we have a very narrow door and a great many reasons to hurry through it."

"And Dain?"

I fed a stick to the fire and watched it catch. "Dain's not stupid. Whatever he knows about the season, he learned it from the same sky we're looking at. If the Kettle road is dead, he knows the glacier's the only way through—and he knows we know it too. Which means either he's already sent men up the eastern approach, or he's coming himself."

"He'd come himself," Wren said. Not a question.

"For something worth this much trouble? Yes." I thought of Corvus Dain's face at the lock, that terrible patient courtesy, the way he'd offered to erase my debt like a man offering to forgive a loan he fully intended to collect with interest elsewhere. "Men like Dain don't delegate what matters. They delegate the dying and attend the winning."

Wren pulled her knees up and rested her chin on them. Firelight made her look younger and older at once, the way fire does. "My father walked that glacier," she said quietly. "Alone, probably. With nothing but that map and whatever he could carry." She glanced at me sideways. "You've seen it. The map. You won't show me, but you've seen it."

"I have."

"Is it beautiful?"

The question caught me sideways. I'd expected *is it real*, or *how much is it worth*, or any of the questions a sensible person would ask about a document that had gotten a man shot and was currently keeping me company against my ribs like a tumor with ambitions. Instead: *is it beautiful.*

"Yes," I said. Because it was. I'd studied it by lamplight the night after Tomas died, and what struck me wasn't the silver markings—it was the hand. Careful, patient, loving almost, every landmark annotated in a cramped script that had clearly been drawn from memory and grief and hope in equal parts. A man had spent years carrying that terrain inside his head and set it down on paper like a letter to someone he missed. "It's the most honest thing I've ever held that could ruin me."

Wren nodded slowly, as if that answered something she'd actually been asking. Maybe it did.

* * *

The storm reached us at dawn, or rather its outriders did—the wind swung hard out of the north and the temperature fell so fast the river steamed, actual ghost-white vapor rising off the black water while frost feathered the railings. By the time we cast off, the Kestrel wore a skin of white and my breath came out in flags.

We made Stonebridge Landing by midafternoon, running hard, and I'll grant the river this much: it seemed to understand urgency. The current cooperated. The bars behaved. Even the Widow's Teeth, that stretch of broken water I'd once run at night to lose Dain's boarders and aged myself a decade doing it, lay unusually tame, as if the river had decided we'd suffered enough and was extending professional courtesy.

Stonebridge was less courteous. It was a mud-flat town of forty souls, an outfitter's store, a mule trader, and a saloon where the whiskey was served at temperatures suggesting personal grudges. And the moment we stepped onto the dock, I knew we had a problem, because the outfitter's porch held three mules already saddled and packed, and a man was nailing a board across the store's window shutters, and everyone in town moved with the brisk, grim economy of people preparing to be somewhere else.

"They're closing up," Wren said. "The whole landing."

"Storm protocol," Ferro said grimly. "Mountain towns batten down early or they bury their dead late. We've got maybe an hour before the outfitter locks his doors and joins the general retreat downhill."

An hour. To provision three people for a crossing that might take twenty days through terrain that ate surveyors for breakfast. I did the math and didn't like any of the sums.

"Split up," I said. "Ferro—food, as much dried and dense as he'll sell, and rope, and don't haggle, pay what he asks and smile about it. Wren—mules. The trader's corral's behind the saloon. Get four, sound ones, and don't let him charm you into six, we can't feed six." I was already moving toward the store. "And neither of you mentions where we're going. Not to anyone. If asked, we're hunting parties going up after elk before the snow."

"What are you getting?" Wren asked.

I patted my coat, where the oilskin packet pressed familiar and warm. "A conversation."

The outfitter was a broad, gray man named Hask, and he was in the middle of arguing with his own wife about whether to load the wagon tonight or at first light, which meant he was distracted, which meant I had perhaps ninety seconds of his full attention and needed to spend them well.

"Hask," I said, setting a coin on his counter—not a bribe, exactly, just a coin, the universal punctuation mark. "Quick questions, straight answers, and then I'm out of your hair."

He looked at the coin, then at me, and something in my face must have told him I wasn't selling anything, because he stopped packing and gave me his attention like a man paying a debt. "Ask."

"The eastern glacier approach. The Veragan corridor. Who's been through here recently asking about it?"

His face did something complicated. "Two parties this month. First was a survey crew, official-looking, federal papers, three men with good boots and bad manners. Bought cold-weather gear, lots of rope, asked about the calving window." He wiped his hands on his apron. "Second party came four days back. One man. Paid cash for everything, wouldn't give a name, had a look about him like a knife that's learned to sit still."

Corvus Dain. Alone, or claiming to be. Ahead of us, or pretending to be.

"Which way did they go?" I asked. "Eastern approach or the Kettle road?"

Hask's wife appeared in the doorway behind him, arms full of blankets, and gave me a look that said *my husband answers no more questions today*. But Hask answered anyway, leaning close, lowering his voice like the walls had ears and grudges.

"Neither," he said. "Both parties went east. But here's the thing, riverwoman, and I'll give it to you free because you'll need it more than I need your coin." He nodded north, toward the cloud wall that had swallowed the peaks entirely now, the storm arriving like a verdict. "That front's coming down the eastern valleys first. Always does. The eastern approach will be under snow inside two days, and the glacier mouth with it. Anyone up there right now—" he shrugged, a small, final motion, "—is either already through, or already trapped, or already dead. Your choice which you'd rather be."

I walked out of the store into wind that had teeth now, real teeth, the first dry stinging grains of snow already hissing past the eaves. Down at the corral I could see Wren talking hard at the mule trader, gesturing, winning. Ferro was emerging from the store's side door under a small mountain of canvas sacks. Everything proceeding according to plan, which is how I knew the plan was wrong.

Because Hask had given me the piece I'd been missing, the piece that rearranged the whole board: we weren't racing Dain to the glacier anymore. We might already be racing Dain's *ghosts*—men who'd gone in days ago, ahead of the storm, ahead of us, possibly ahead of the calving itself. Men who would be trapped in the ice corridors when the freeze came, unless the second breath opened wide and opened soon.

Or—worse thought, the one that climbed my spine like cold water—unless someone was up there *waiting* for the corridor to open, positioned at the far exit, because they'd figured out what I was only beginning to suspect: that whatever Tomas Reyes had hidden in those mountains was worth more than silver, and worth killing for, and that sooner or later a riverwoman with a promise to keep would come walking up the ice to fetch it.

The first true snowflakes came down fat and slow, catching in Wren's hair as she led four mules across the mud, triumphant and snow-dusted, holding up the lead rope like a trophy.

"Got us four beauties," she called. "The trader threw in bells. Ferro says we leave at first light, but Elodie—look at the sky—we might not have a first light worth the name."

I looked at the sky. The sky looked back, white and enormous and utterly without mercy, and somewhere above it, on a glacier I'd never seen, a door was either opening or closing, and I no longer knew which side of it we'd find Corvus Dain on—or how many of his knives were already inside.

"We leave tonight," I said.
