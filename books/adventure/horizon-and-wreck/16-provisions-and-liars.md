## 16. Provisions and Liars

*Elodie*

There is an art to buying things you don't want anyone to know you're buying, and like most arts, it looks boring from the outside.

I'd learned it the hard way, back when my name was still worth something and I had money enough to be careless about who saw me spend it. The trick isn't disguise. Disguise draws the eye. The trick is tedium. You want to be the third most forgettable person in any room—the woman buying rope, of all things, rope, and isn't that a dull life she must lead. Nobody follows the rope woman home.

So on our second morning in Coldharbor, while Wren went off to do whatever it was she did with her mornings—disappear, mostly, and come back smelling of other people's gossip—I walked into Granger & Sons Outfitters wearing my plainest coat and my dullest expression, and asked to see their selection of tarps.

Granger's occupied the whole ground floor of a stone building near the freight dock, and it was the kind of store that sold everything a body might need to die comfortably in the mountains: canvas and rope, stoves and lamp oil, dried apples by the fifty-pound sack, mule shoes, bear bells, and a wall of rifles behind glass that Granger himself polished with a cloth he kept tucked in his apron like a bishop's stole.

And leaning against the counter at the far end, pretending to examine a display of fishhooks, was a man I recognized.

He wasn't one of Dain's regulars—I'd have known him. But he had the look. Harrow men shared a certain posture, a way of standing that said *I am relaxed* the way a drawn bow says *I am at rest*. This one wore a freight clerk's coat two sizes too clean for actual freight work, and his boots were city boots, and he hadn't bought anything in the twenty minutes I'd been pretending to compare tarps.

I picked up a roll of oilcloth, held it to the light, frowned at it as though oilcloth were a subject requiring serious study, and drifted toward the dry goods.

"Help you find something?" The clerk was young, freckled, with the eager air of a man whose commission depended on selling someone a second stove.

"Just looking," I said, which is the sacred password of every liar in retail.

"We've got a sale on lamp oil."

"Do you." I let my gaze wander past his shoulder, casual as weather, and confirmed what I already suspected: the fishhook man had shifted position so he could see the door *and* me. Not the door and the room. Me.

Well then.

Here's what I knew: Dain had made his offer at the lock, and I'd stalled him, and men like Corvus Dain don't mistake stalling for thinking. He'd know by now that I meant to run the pass whether he liked it or not, which meant he'd stop trying to buy the map and start trying to take it. Which meant eyes on the outfitter, because there was exactly one outfitter in Coldharbor, and nobody climbs a glacier in street clothes.

Which meant I needed three days of provisions bought by nobody in particular, spread across nobody in particular's hands.

"I'll take the oil," I told the clerk. "Two cans. And those dried apples."

"Five pounds or ten?"

"Ten." A pause, calculated. "My husband eats them like they're going out of season."

The lie came out smooth and warm and utterly false, and the clerk smiled at the picture of it—a husband, an appetite, a domestic little life—and wrote up my order without once wondering why a woman buying apples for her husband paid in coin counted out exact to the cent, or why she never gave a name at all.

Small lies are load-bearing beams. Build enough of them and nobody notices the house is empty.

*Wren*

Papa used to say that every town has two maps: the one printed on paper, and the one written in people's mouths. He taught me to read the second kind before I could read the first. "Ask the wrong question straight," he'd say, "and you get a locked door. Ask it crooked, and doors open themselves."

I was eleven when he said that, and I thought he meant it was fun. It took me until this week to understand he'd been teaching me how to survive him.

Granger & Sons smelled of lamp oil and leather and the particular dust that accumulates around men who talk about gear more than they use it. I came in through the front like any customer's daughter sent on an errand—which, technically, I was—and found the clerk, a freckled boy about my age with ink on his fingers and a name tag that said **OSKAR**.

Oskar looked like he'd been waiting his whole life for someone interesting to walk in. I have been told I can seem interesting when I put my mind to it. Papa called it "the Reyes charm," which Mama always said was generous, since it was just her charm with his stubbornness bolted on.

"I'm supposed to get rope," I announced, "but honestly I don't know the first thing about rope, and my uncle will know if I buy the wrong kind, because he always knows."

"That's—" Oskar brightened visibly. "That depends what it's for. Climbing? Towing? You don't tow with climbing rope, people think you can—"

"Heavens, no towing. It's for the high country. Up past the pass." I said it lightly, the way you'd mention a picnic. "Uncle's got some fool idea about going up before winter shuts everything."

Something flickered across Oskar's face—not suspicion. Recognition. The look of a boy who'd heard a thing recently and was dying to repeat it.

"The glacier corridor?" he said, lowering his voice like we were sharing a secret instead of discussing geography. "Nobody goes the corridor this late."

"Why not?"

"Because it's only open during calving, and calving's nearly done, and—" He glanced down the counter, where a man in a too-clean coat was examining fishhooks with the intensity of a scholar. Oskar's voice dropped further. "Look, I probably shouldn't say. But there was a fellow in yesterday asking after guides for the corridor, offering stupid money, and Mr. Granger told him flat out no, because the last party that went up in late season—" He stopped. Swallowed. "They found one of them in the spring. Part of one, anyway. The ice does things."

"The ice does things," I repeated.

"Meltwater opens the corridor from underneath. There's places where the whole floor's a roof over running water, and you can't tell by looking, and then—" He made a gesture, brief and final, like a man closing a ledger. "Mr. Granger won't sell full expedition kit to anybody headed for the corridor anymore. Liability. His lawyer made him. So if your uncle's serious, he'll need to piece it together, and he'll want crevasse bars, and those are in the back, and I'm not supposed to sell them to—"

He caught himself. Looked at me. Looked at my face, which I kept open and honest and just a little overwhelmed, seventeen and out of her depth, and whatever he saw there made him decide I was not the enemy.

"I'm not supposed to sell them to strangers," he finished quietly. "But you're not a stranger. You're rope girl."

"I'm rope girl," I agreed solemnly.

He sold me sixty feet of good line, four iron crevasse bars, and—for free, wrapped in sacking under the rope, because Oskar had a conscience and possibly also a crush—a hand-inked sketch of the corridor approach that some dead guide's widow had left with Granger years ago. "Copy it and give it back," he whispered. "And if anyone asks, I was rude to you."

"You were terrible," I said. "I almost cried."

He grinned like I'd handed him a medal.

What I didn't tell him—what I carried out of that store folded against my ribs like a coal—was the other thing he'd said, almost in passing, while wrapping the bars: *there was a fellow in yesterday asking after guides.* Singular. Yesterday. While we'd been moored twenty yards away, believing ourselves invisible.

We weren't invisible. We hadn't been invisible since Millbrook, maybe since ever, and the man studying fishhooks at the end of the counter was proof. But knowing the shape of a trap is half of walking out of one, and Papa's voice in my head was very calm about it: *Now you know where the teeth are, little bird. Fly accordingly.*

*Ferro*

I have spent thirty years being paid to observe terrain, and I will confess that in all that time I observed people badly, which is how a man ends up signing his name to a lie for money and calling it surveying. But even I could read the situation at Granger & Sons clearly enough, and what it said was: *you are being watched, and the watcher has not yet decided what you are.*

So I gave him something to decide.

I entered the store loudly, in my shabbiest coat, smelling agreeably of the tavern, and I made a small spectacle of myself haggling over a camp stove I had no intention of purchasing. This is a service I provide. In my drinking years I learned that a drunk is the most trustworthy creature in any room precisely because everyone dismisses him; a watched door sees nothing but the fool staggering through it.

The watcher—clean coat, city boots, fishhooks—watched me the way cats watch a rolling barrel: with contempt and a readiness to pounce if it should stop rolling. Good. Let him catalogue the drunk. Let him write in whatever report he'd write: *an old surveyor, ruined, harmless.*

Harmless. If he only knew. I have been many things, but harmless was never among them; harmlessness requires a clean conscience, and mine has been dirty since the year I took Harrow silver and erased a man named Tomas Reyes off the face of the map.

I found Elodie at the dry goods counter, counting out coins for dried apples, and I leaned beside her with the practiced sway of a man balancing against his own bad decisions.

"Your stowaway," I murmured, "has acquired intelligence."

"She's good at that."

"There's a man by the hooks. Harrow. He's been here since before I arrived."

"I know." She counted another coin. Her voice was level as a spirit gauge. "He followed me yesterday too. From the chandler's."

"And you came back anyway."

"I came back anyway, because we need the gear, and because a watcher who's watching me isn't watching the boat." She turned, finally, and looked at me, and there was something in her face I was slow to name because I'd seen it so rarely directed at myself: trust. Qualified, provisional, heavily armed trust—but trust. "Wren got the route notes?"

"Presumably. She's talking to the clerk now. The boy appears to be in love."

"Everyone's in love with her within ten minutes. It's unsettling." A ghost of a smile. Then it faded, and she said, quieter: "Ferro. When we go up—if it comes to a choice between the map and the girl—"

"Don't." I said it harder than I meant to. "Don't ask me to price that. I priced a man once. I know what the arithmetic costs."

She studied me for a long moment, this disgraced old liar she'd pulled out of a Millbrook gutter because he could read contour lines, and whatever she was weighing, she weighed it fast. Captains do.

"It wasn't a question," she said. "It was an answer. I wanted to hear yours matched."

"It matches."

The bell over the door rang, and Wren came out of the back with a coil of rope over her shoulder and the radiant, slightly wicked expression of a girl who has gotten away with something magnificent. Behind the counter, the freckled clerk watched her go with his heart visibly in his throat. At the fishhooks, the Harrow man straightened, having concluded—God bless his limited imagination—that today's haul consisted of one drunk, one housewife, and one errand-running child.

We converged on the door like three unrelated accidents.

Outside, in the cold, Wren unwrapped the sacking just enough to show us the crevasse bars and the folded paper beneath. "The ice opens from underneath," she said softly. "There's water under the corridor, and you can't see it. And Dain's man was here yesterday asking for guides."

Elodie took the sketch, read it once, and folded it away inside her coat with the map.

"Then we buy the rest tonight," she said, "from three different stores, in three different names, and we're on the trail before his report reaches Dain's desk." She looked up at the mountains, white and indifferent, where the pass was already closing and the glacier waited with its blue throat full of meltwater. "Ladies and gentlemen. The rope woman, the rope girl, and the drunk. Best crew this river never knew it had."

And the terrible thing—the thing I set down here because no one else will—is that standing in the cold outside Granger & Sons, broke and hunted and bound for a corridor of killing ice, I was happier than I had been in thirty years of being respectable.

Liars make the best family. We already know all each other's worst stories. Everything after that is just the truth, arriving late.
