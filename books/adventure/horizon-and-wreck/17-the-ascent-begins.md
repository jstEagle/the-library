## 17. The Ascent Begins

I have been many kinds of liar in my life: the polite kind at dinner parties, the professional kind on survey documents, the desperate kind in front of a licensing board. But I had never before been a man packing mules at dawn while pretending he wasn't afraid of the mountain he was about to climb.

The Kestrel sat moored at the landing below Coldharbor like a patient animal that knew it was being left behind. Elodie had spent an hour the previous evening talking to her—actually talking, low and apologetic, hand flat against the pilothouse door as though swearing fidelity to a jealous wife. I pretended not to overhear. A man who lies for a living develops a talent for not overhearing things.

"Three weeks," she'd said to the boat. "Maybe four if the weather's kind."

The weather would not be kind. Weather is never kind; it merely varies its cruelty. I said nothing about this, because I have learned which truths are useful and which are simply heavy luggage.

We had three mules. Their names were Biscuit, Grudge, and—by Wren's insistence over Elodie's objections—Captain Voss the Second. The original Captain Voss declined to comment on this honor, or possibly threatened us with a boathook; it was difficult to tell with her.

* * *

The treeline trail left the river behind almost immediately, switchbacking up through lodgepole pine so dense the morning light came down in coins. I led Biscuit because Biscuit tolerated me, which after forty years of professional relationships struck me as the most honest friendship available.

Elodie walked point. She climbed the way she steered—reading the ground three steps ahead, adjusting without appearing to decide anything. I watched her once stop, crouch, and press two fingers into a patch of mud beside the trail, then rise and say nothing, and I understood she had learned something from that mud that she considered none of our business.

Wren Reyes walked in the middle, which was where the mules wanted her anyway. She had her father's map copied onto oilcloth folded inside her jacket—I had seen her check it four times before we'd gone a mile—and she carried herself with the particular stiffness of a girl performing confidence she hadn't yet grown.

"You're limping," she informed me around midday.

"I am walking with dignity."

"Your dignity has a hitch in it."

"The dignity is old," I admitted. "It took a bullet's worth of damage recently." This was true in a way she didn't yet know the full shape of, and I let it stay vague. There would be time enough on this mountain for confessions. Mountains are excellent listeners and terrible gossips; they keep everything you tell them frozen.

We made perhaps nine miles before the light went long and amber through the trees, and Elodie called a halt at a shelf of granite above a stream—the kind of campsite a surveyor notices and a riverwoman found by instinct. She'd already chosen it before I'd thought to look.

"Water's good," she said. "Fire ring's already here. Somebody used this spot before us."

"Prospectors, probably," Wren said, and then went quiet, because everything now came back to prospectors, to one prospector specifically, to the man whose blood was dried somewhere behind us on a dock in Millbrook and whose daughter was standing here folding a map with hands that shook only slightly.

I built the fire. It is the one domestic skill I own outright, learned during three miserable years mapping the northern ranges when the alternative to a good fire was dying stupidly. Elodie saw how I stacked it and gave me a look of mild surprise, as if I were a dog that had unexpectedly performed arithmetic.

"Surveyors freeze professionally," I told her. "It's in the bylaws."

"Is there a bylaw about mules eating the rope?"

There was indeed about to be such a bylaw. Captain Voss the Second had developed a taste for the lead line and was working through it with the methodical dedication of a syndicate auditor. Wren rescued what remained while scolding the animal in terms that would have gotten her arrested in any decent town, and Elodie laughed—a short, startled sound, like a woman surprised by her own voice.

I filed that laugh away. You learn to collect rare specimens.

* * *

Supper was beans and salt pork and the last of the Millbrook bread, toasted on sticks because Wren insisted bread was inedible untoasted and none of us had the energy to argue with a seventeen-year-old holding a sharpened stick. The fire cracked and settled. Above the black comb of the trees, the first stars came out hard and cold, the way they do at altitude, as if the sky were closer here and less forgiving.

"My father hated beans," Wren said suddenly, to the fire more than to us.

Elodie and I exchanged the glance of people deciding whether to step onto ice.

"He said—" She stopped. Started again. "He used to make them when I was small. Before he left. He'd put in too much pepper on purpose and say, *a man should suffer a little for his supper, it builds character.* And I'd eat them anyway because they were his." She turned the stick. "I haven't eaten beans since he went. Isn't that foolish? Eight years of avoiding a vegetable out of spite."

"It's not spite," I said. "It's grief wearing practical clothes."

She looked at me across the fire with an expression I couldn't fully read—part suspicion, part the hunger of someone who has just realized another person might understand something she thought was hers alone.

"How do you know?"

"Because I haven't drawn a map in six years," I said, "and I used to draw them the way other men breathe."

The fire popped. Somewhere downslope, Biscuit snorted at nothing.

"That's different," Wren said finally. "You *chose* to stop."

"Did I?" I smiled, and it cost me. "Child, every coward's decision looks like a choice from the inside. It's only from a distance you can see the fence."

Elodie said nothing, but she looked at me a moment longer than comfort allowed, and I understood that she had recognized the fence too. We were, the two of us, connoisseurs of enclosures.

* * *

Later, when Wren had rolled into her bedroll with the sudden total collapse of the young, Elodie and I kept the fire company. She was cleaning her revolver with the unhurried thoroughness of ritual, and I was pretending to study tomorrow's route by firelight, which mostly meant studying her.

"Ask it," she said, without looking up.

"Ask what?"

"Whatever question you've been chewing since Millbrook. You get a crease right there—" she indicated her own forehead, mirroring mine, "—when you're chewing something."

I considered lying. It is my native tongue. But the fire was warm, the girl was asleep, and the mountain above us was going to demand honesty from all of us soon enough. Practice seemed wise.

"Why did you take me on? Truly. You needed a map read. You could have hired any half-literate trapper for less coin and no history."

She finished with the cylinder, snapped it back into the frame, and set the revolver down with the care of a priest handling relics.

"Ferro. When you looked at Tomas's map in my cabin, your hands shook. Not from fear. From *recognition*. You knew that country the way I know the Vell—every bend of it living in your body." She fed a stick to the fire. "I need somebody whose body remembers. Mine remembers rivers. Yours remembers rock."

"And the history?"

"The history's why you'll work harder than a trapper would." Her eyes came up, dark and level. "People with something to prove make the best crew. People with something to hide make the worst. I'm gambling you're the first kind."

"And if you're wrong?"

"Then we'll both find out at altitude, and it'll be too late to do anything about it, so I'd rather not spend the night worrying." She lay back on her bedroll, hands behind her head. "Go to sleep, surveyor. The mountain doesn't wait for insomniacs."

But I lay awake a while longer, watching sparks climb, thinking about fences and the strange, terrifying possibility that I had already stepped through one without noticing—that somewhere between Millbrook and this fire, these two people had stopped being passengers in my story and become something else entirely.

I did not have a word for it yet. I would not find one until much later, and by then it would be too late to pretend I hadn't meant it.

* * *

Morning came gray and sharp, breath hanging, frost furring the mules' coats. We broke camp with the efficiency of people who had done it exactly once and were already proud of themselves. Wren doused the fire with stream water and stamped the coals, and when Elodie raised an eyebrow at her thoroughness, she said, "Papa's rule. Leave a camp like you were never there."

"Papa sounds like he'd have liked this crew," Elodie said quietly.

Wren didn't answer, but she stood a moment looking at the dead fire ring, and I saw her mouth move around words she wasn't ready to say aloud. Something about her father being here, in the rules he'd taught her, in the tricks she'd discover she knew. Inheritance is a strange cargo. It rides in places no mule can carry.

We took the trail upward. Behind us, far below and invisible now, the Vell ran silver through its valley, and the Kestrel waited at her mooring like a promise. Ahead, the trees thinned, and through the gaps we could see the high country waiting—gray shoulders, white crowns, and somewhere up there, a vein of silver and a box of truths, guarded by ice and, I suspected, by men.

Because I had noticed what Elodie noticed and said nothing about: boot prints beside the trail, two days old at least, heading up.

Somebody else was climbing toward the pass.

And they had not been traveling light.
