## 18. Treeline

The trees gave out the way a conversation does when someone has died—gradually, then all at once.

One hour we were walking through pine so thick the mules had to be talked through it in a low voice, and the next we were standing at a ragged edge where the world simply stopped bothering. Beyond that line there was rock, and snow in the rock's shadows, and sky doing something enormous overhead. The treeline. Ferro had told us about it back in Millbrook like a man warning us about a dog he knew personally. "Above it," he'd said, "nothing forgives you."

I'd been above treelines before. I'd been above them in summer, with a full crew and a schedule I controlled, on runs where the worst thing waiting for me was a client who wanted his ore weighed twice. This was different. This was October pretending to be December, and the worst thing waiting for me was named Corvus Dain, or possibly just the mountain itself, which to be fair was the more reliable killer of the two.

"Camp here or push?" Ferro asked. He'd been asking it every hour since dawn, in the tone of a man who already knew the answer and was only checking whether I knew it too.

I looked up. The trail climbed away from us in switchbacks toward a saddle maybe six hundred feet higher, and beyond the saddle I could see nothing but more of the same gray-and-white argument between stone and weather. The light was still good. That was the whole case for pushing. The case against was everything else—the mules were tired, Wren's boots were giving her blisters she hadn't mentioned because she thought mentioning them would count as complaining, and Ferro had gone quiet in a way I didn't love. Quiet men at altitude are either thinking or failing, and you want to know which before dark.

"We camp here," I said. "Last wood we'll see. We burn what we can carry tomorrow and after that we're cooking on dung and stubbornness."

Wren dropped her pack with the gratitude of the young, who have not yet learned to hide relief behind complaints. She was seventeen and made of wire and opinions, and in the four days since Coldharbor she had not once asked me how much farther, which I respected more than I said aloud. Her father had taught her to read water and trail both, apparently, though from what she'd told me he'd disguised the lessons as games. A childhood of secret curriculum. It explained a lot about her. It explained some things about him, too, though Tomas Reyes had been dead eleven days now and I was still deciding what I thought of him.

We got a fire going while the light lasted. Ferro split kindling with the economy of a man who'd done fieldwork for twenty years and hated every season of it, and Wren rigged the canvas over a lean of granite, and I did what captains do, which is stand around looking useful while other people work. Then we ate beans and hard bread and drank coffee that tasted like pine smoke and regret, and nobody talked much, because at altitude talk costs breath and breath is currency.

It was Wren who saw the cairn.

"There," she said, pointing past the fire, upslope and east, where a shelf of granite jutted out over the drop. "Is that a marker? Prospectors stack those, don't they?"

Prospectors do. So do surveyors. So do men who want to be found by their own people and not by anyone else, and the difference between those stacks tells you things if you know how to look. This one was wrong. I could see it was wrong from thirty feet, before I could even say why—a little too neat, a little too deliberate, stones chosen rather than gathered. And it was alone. Nobody builds a single cairn on a shelf unless they're marking something they expect to come back for.

"Don't touch it," I said, which was stupid, because she hadn't moved.

Ferro stood up. He'd seen it too, and whatever he saw in it had taken the color out of his face, or maybe that was just the altitude and the cold and the fact that he was fifty-three years old and climbing a mountain in October for reasons he kept not quite explaining. We left the fire and crossed the shelf together, the three of us, leaving the mules hobbled below, and I remember thinking, as my boots crunched on the first real snow of the day, that I should have told one of them to stay back. I remember thinking it and not doing it, because I wanted witnesses. Whatever was under that cairn, I wanted witnesses.

The top stones came off easy. The ones beneath were frosted together, and beneath those—

Well. Beneath those was a man.

* * *

He'd been dead a long time. Weeks, maybe longer—the mountain keeps its dead better than any cellar, cold and dry, and this one had been tucked into the shelf's hollow like something put away carefully. His coat was good wool, city wool, the kind of coat you buy in Vellport with money you didn't earn with your hands. His boots were city boots. His face had gone the color of old candle wax and had settled into an expression I've seen on drowned men and frozen men alike, which is not pain exactly and not surprise exactly, but the particular disappointment of a man who has just understood, finally and completely, that he has made a mistake.

On his breast, pinned through the wool, was a badge. Brass, octagonal, stamped with the harrow-and-bar of the Syndicate. Even tarnished, even furred with frost, it caught the last of the light and threw it back at us like a small yellow accusation.

Nobody said anything for a while. The wind said plenty. It came up the valley in long shoves, flattening the grass tufts below the treeline and combing snow off the rocks above, and it made a sound through the pass like a door being tried somewhere far off, over and over, patient, certain it would get in eventually.

"He's one of Dain's," Wren said at last. Not a question.

"One of somebody's." I crouched down, careful not to touch anything yet. "Harrow badge doesn't automatically mean Dain. The Syndicate has fingers in forty pies between here and the coast."

"Dain sent men after the packet," Wren said. "I heard them in Coldharbor. Intercept the Reyes packet at the pass, that's what the one man said. At the pass." She looked up the trail, toward the saddle, and I watched her do the same arithmetic I was doing. "This isn't the pass. This is three days short of it. Why would an interceptor stop here?"

Because he wasn't intercepting anymore. Because the mountain had collected its toll. But Wren's question was sharper than that and we both knew it, so I said it out loud for her.

"Because he wasn't coming up behind us. He went ahead. Weeks ago. Before your father ever handed me that map." I turned the thought over and found it heavier than expected. "Dain's known about the vein longer than we have. Maybe since before Reyes ran. Maybe that's why Reyes ran."

Ferro had gone very still. Ferro had been going very still at intervals all trip, each time the Syndicate came up, and I'd filed it under the general guilt of a man carrying secrets, which described everyone in our party including me. But this stillness had a different texture. This was the stillness of recognition.

"You know him," I said.

"I—" Ferro took off his hat, which is a thing men do for the dead even when the dead can't see, and held it against his chest. "No. I don't think so. But the coat—I sold coats like that, once, outfitting survey parties out of Millbrook. Syndicate contract crews. They wore badges on the outside going in and took them off before they reached anywhere interesting." He crouched beside me, keeping his hands to himself the way I was keeping mine. "Look at his pack straps. Cut, not unbuckled."

I looked. He was right. Both shoulder straps had been sliced through, clean, with a knife—and the pack itself was gone. Whatever the man had been carrying, someone had taken it off him deliberately, and then built a cairn over him, and walked away.

"That's not the mountain killing him and taking his gear," I said slowly. "That's a partner. Or a boss. Someone who found him dead—or dying—and stripped the pack and covered him up so nobody would ask questions until spring." I sat back on my heels. "Or so nobody would find him until spring. Except the cairn's visible. You can see it from the trail. Whoever built it wanted it found—by the right people. Marked, not hidden."

"A message," Wren said.

"A filing system," I said. "Syndicate men don't leave messages. They leave records. That cairn says: agent deployed, agent lost, position logged. Somebody upstream knows exactly where their man lies, and when the thaw comes, or when the account needs settling, they'll come collect him." I stood up. My knees cracked in the cold like small applause. "Which means somebody upstream also knows whether he found what he was looking for before he died."

"And if he did?" Wren's voice had gone flat and careful, the way it went flat and careful whenever the conversation touched her father. "If he found the vein weeks ago, why would Dain still be chasing us?"

"Because knowing where a thing is and being able to prove where a thing is are different animals," Ferro said. "A claim needs survey marks, bearings, witness posts. If the agent died before he could fix the location properly—if he had the map's landmarks but not the map's key—" He stopped himself. I watched him stop himself, actually watched the sentence die in his mouth, and I thought: there it is again. There's the thing he's not saying.

But the light was going and I had a camp to secure and a corpse to decide about, so I let it die.

* * *

Here is what I decided, and here is why, and you can judge me for both.

We couldn't bury him. The ground was iron; we'd have needed blasting powder and a week. We couldn't carry him; we had three backs and two bad mules and a window of weather measured in days, not weeks. We could take the badge, and we did—Wren pried it off the coat with her knife tip and wrapped it in a rag, and I told myself it was evidence, which it was, but I'll admit it was also a trophy, and trophies are a younger person's instinct than mine, and I let her keep it anyway because she needed to hold something solid from that shelf and I understood why.

And we could read him. A dead man's pockets are a ledger, if you're willing to do the accounting. I'm willing. I've been poor enough times to have no scruples left about it.

In his coat we found: a folding knife, good steel, which Wren claimed and I allowed because hers was rusting; a tin of matches, half spent; a folded paper that had gone soft as cloth with damp and refreezing, on which someone had written figures in pencil—distances, I thought, or supplies, the columns of a man planning a long stay—and a name written across the top in a hand too careful to be the dead man's own. *R. Calloway.* Under it, smaller: *recovered goods — hold for C.D.*

C.D. Corvus Dain. Hold for Corvus Dain.

"Well," I said, and found I didn't have a second sentence.

"He was carrying something back," Wren said. "Recovered goods. And someone cut the pack off him and took it." She looked at me, and in the blue half-light her face was all cheekbone and fury, and she looked so much like her father right then that I had to make myself keep talking. "Dain has it. Whatever it is, Dain has it now."

"Maybe." I folded the paper into my coat, inside pocket, over the heart, where I kept the map and the debt and every other thing I owned that could ruin me. "Or whoever cut the straps never delivered. Men who strip their partners' packs on a mountainside aren't always punctual about the paperwork." I looked west, where the sky had gone the color of a bruise going green at the edges, which is a color I have learned to hate. "Either way, the arithmetic hasn't changed. Dain's people have been on this trail since before we started. Some of them are dead. The rest are either ahead of us, behind us, or sitting wherever that pack got delivered, counting what's in it."

"Cheerful," Ferro said.

"I'm a riverboat captain. Cheerful is for passengers."

We went back down to the fire. I won't pretend I didn't look over my shoulder on the way, twice, at a slope that held nothing but shadow and scree, and I won't pretend the looking helped. The mountain had shown us its first bill of the trip—one brass badge, one disappointed face, one set of cut straps—and the invoice was clear: it had already eaten one Harrow man and kept the change, and it did not care whose side you were on. What the mountain cared about, as far as I could tell, was volume. It wanted more of us. It was simply waiting to see who it would get first, us or the men behind us.

We ate the rest of the beans. We fed the fire everything burnable that wasn't load-bearing. Wren took first watch, because she insisted and because I'd learned in four days that arguing with her cost more energy than it saved, and Ferro rolled into his bedroll with his back to the flames and his hat over his eyes, and I sat up a while longer doing the thing I do instead of sleeping, which is listening.

The river teaches you listening. On the Kestrel, at night, you learn to hear the difference between water moving and water gathering, between a sandbar shifting and a snag rolling, between the sound of a river being ordinary and the sound of a river about to hand you something. Mountains speak the same language, it turns out. Rockfall has a voice. Wind through a pass has a voice. And somewhere below us in the dark—faint, intermittent, unmistakable to anyone who'd spent ten years reading water in all its forms—there was another voice entirely.

Boots on gravel. More than one pair. Moving slow, moving careful, the way men move when they're following a trail they only half trust.

I didn't wake the others. Not yet. I banked the fire low, checked the mules, and sat with my knife open in my lap and my ears doing the work my eyes couldn't, and I counted the footsteps the way I'd count the strokes of a paddle wheel, and I thought about R. Calloway lying under his neat pile of stones with his pack straps cut, and I thought: somebody came back for him. Or somebody came to check the filing.

Either way, we wouldn't be alone on this trail in the morning.

The wind tried the door again, far off, patient. Below us, in the dark, the footsteps kept coming.
