## 19. The Scree Field

Here is the thing nobody tells you about mountains: they are not still. I grew up in Coldharbor believing the peaks were the patient ones, the fixed points, the things you could trust when everything else moved. Rivers shift. People lie. But a mountain, I thought, was a promise kept for ten thousand years.

The mountain disabused me of that notion somewhere around the fourth hour of the rockslide, when the slope beneath my boots turned out to be a lie the glacier told and the mountain agreed to hold for a while, until it didn't.

We'd been climbing since before dawn. Ferro was moving better than he had any right to—two days past the fever, stubborn as a mule with a grudge—and Elodie had us on a switchback trail above the treeline, where the world narrowed to grey stone, white sky, and the small human sounds of mules complaining about everything. I was on lead rope with the second mule, Petunia, who was named by Elodie in a fit of irony and who hated me personally.

The rock came down without warning. That's the part I keep returning to, the part that makes my hands go cold even now. No rumble of thunder first, no warning shout. One moment the slope was a slope, and the next it was a river of stone, and the world made a sound I can only describe as the mountain clearing its throat.

I heard Elodie scream my name. I heard Ferro shout something—probably a surveyor's curse, probably coordinates, the man swore in latitudes. Then Petunia bolted, I went with her, and the slope took us sideways off the trail into a chute of scree that carried us down like a river carries a leaf, except rivers don't hit you with boulders the size of sleeping cows.

I remember three things from the fall. One: the taste of dust, chalky and old, like the mountain's own breath. Two: Petunia screaming, an actual scream, a sound I will hear at the end of my life. Three: my father's voice, absurdly, in my head, calm as Sunday: *Don't fight the slide, Wrenny. Swim it.*

I didn't fight it. I swam it—arms up, feet first, riding the stone the way I'd once ridden the flood-current behind the Coldharbor mill—and when the slide spat me out at the bottom into a fan of rubble the size of a church, I was bruised in places I didn't know I owned, bleeding from one ear, and entirely, catastrophically alone.

The trail was gone. The switchback above was a smear of fresh rubble. And somewhere up there, on the other side of a hundred tons of newly rearranged mountain, were Elodie and Ferro and the other two mules and everything we owned except the pack that had landed next to me, which contained a canvas tarp, a coil of rope, half the food, and—by the grace of whatever gods look after mule packers—one of the two rifles.

I sat down on a rock and did the arithmetic of my situation, which came to zero.

* * *

The first hour I spent shouting. I climbed as far up the slide fan as the loose stone allowed and hollered until my voice went raw, and the mountain gave back my own voice in a dozen mocking echoes, and nothing else. If they were shouting back, I couldn't hear them over the wind that funneled down the chute like the valley exhaling. If they were hurt, I couldn't help them. If they were looking for me, they'd be looking along the trail, because that's what you do—you follow the trail—and the trail was a graveyard of stone.

That was when the panic came, the real kind, the kind that lives in the body and not the mind. My hands started shaking. My breath went short and fast. I was seventeen years old, alone on a mountain, and every story I'd ever heard about people who got separated from their party in the high country ended the same way, with a search party finding a boot in spring.

I sat on my rock and I cried for a while. I'm not ashamed of it. Well. I'm a little ashamed of it. But I cried, and then I did the thing my father taught me, the thing I'd thought was just a game, the thing I'd resented him for at the time because it seemed like one more way he was strange and secretive and never explained himself.

He used to make me play a game he called *Stranded*. I was maybe eight, nine years old. He'd walk me out past the town limits, into the scree fields below the old mine tailings, and he'd say: *You're stranded, Wrenny. Show me what you do first.*

And I'd say something childish—*I'd call for help*—and he'd say, *No one's coming. What do you do first?*

*I'd look for water.*

*No one's coming and it's getting dark. What do you do first?*

And eventually, through about a hundred repetitions of this game, over years, the answer he was fishing for always came out: *I'd stop.* Stop moving. Stop panicking. Sit down, breathe, and let the fear pass through you like weather, because a frightened person on a mountain makes decisions with their legs instead of their head, and legs are idiots.

I stopped. I breathed. I ate a strip of dried venison, because my father's second rule was *eat while you can, sleep while you can, and never save anything for a version of yourself that might not exist*—which I'd thought, at nine, was just his way of justifying eating dessert first.

Then I took stock, out loud, because the silence was worse.

"One rifle. One tarp. Rope, maybe sixty feet. Half the food, which is a third of what we need for four people for two more days, but I'm one person, so it's—" I did the sums. "Six days. If I'm careful. Which I won't be, because I'm seventeen and stupid, but let's pretend."

Water: I could hear it. That was the one mercy of scree country—meltwater runs under everything, and if you listen, the mountain tells you where. My father's third rule: *follow your ears down, not your eyes down.* Eyes look for streams and find dry channels. Ears find the water that's actually there.

I found a seep coming out from under a boulder, cold enough to hurt my teeth, and I drank until my stomach sloshed, and then I filled the one canteen on my belt, and I sat back down and made myself think about the fourth rule, which was the one I'd never understood until that exact moment on that exact mountain.

*The mountain is a map,* he used to say, crouched in the tailings with a stick, drawing in the gravel. *Every stone is a word. Most people walk through the mountains like they're walking through a crowd—shoulders down, eyes on their boots. You read it, Wrenny. You read the mountain the way your mother reads faces.*

My mother read faces because she was a barmaid and drunks lie. My father read mountains because—well. Because of everything I didn't know yet. Everything I was only starting to understand, up there on that scree fan with my ear bleeding and the afternoon going long.

Because here's what I figured out, sitting there: he hadn't been teaching me games. He'd been teaching me survival. A prospector's daughter in a hard town, a girl whose father was gone half the year and whose enemies—I know now, I know what he was running from—might someday come looking. He was arming me. Quietly, patiently, disguised as play, he had spent my whole childhood loading a weapon into my head, and he'd never once told me it was a weapon, because weapons get noticed, and the whole point was that no one, least of all me, should know I was armed.

I sat on that rock and I missed him so hard it felt like the slide had hit me again.

* * *

Night comes fast and mean above the treeline. By the time the sun dropped behind the western ridge, the temperature had fallen like it had somewhere better to be, and I understood that my real enemy wasn't the mountain anymore. It was the dark, and the cold that came riding on it.

I built my camp the way he'd taught me, in the *Stranded* game, rule five: *never sleep where the mountain sleeps.* Meaning: not in a drainage, not at the base of a slope, not anywhere the water or the stone might travel. I found a shelf of solid bedrock halfway up a low rise, backed by a boulder the size of a barn—windbreak, heat reflector, and, if the mountain had any more opinions, a shield. I dragged stones into a horseshoe. I pitched the tarp low, half-buried the edges in rubble, and lined the floor with every dry piece of brush I could pull from the lee sides of rocks, because rule six was *the ground takes your heat faster than the air does, so never let your body touch the ground directly if you can help it.*

I got a fire going—small, shielded, the kind of fire my father called a *rabbit fire*, big enough to warm your hands and small enough that it wouldn't announce me to the whole valley. Because that was rule seven, the one that had always seemed paranoid and now seemed like the wisest thing anyone had ever said: *heat draws eyes. In the high country, the thing that finds your fire is never glad to see you.*

I sat with my back against the barn-sized boulder, my hands around a tin cup of snowmelt, and I watched the stars come out over the pass we were trying to reach, and I talked to my father. Not praying, exactly. Arguing.

"You knew," I said. "Somehow you knew I'd end up out here alone one day, and you didn't say a word, you just—made me learn it. Why couldn't you have just *told* me? Why all the games? Why did you have to be so—" My voice cracked on the word I'd been using about him since Coldharbor, since the neighbors with their lowered voices: *strange. Secretive. Disgraced.* "Why couldn't you just be *honest*?"

The mountain, naturally, said nothing. Mountains are terrible conversationalists. But the wind moved through the scree below me, and the stones ticked and settled against each other, and I swear it sounded almost like an answer—*because honest gets people killed, Wrenny, and alive is the first honesty*—and I wiped my face on my sleeve and decided the cold was making me hear things.

I kept watch in shifts against nobody, dozing with the rifle across my knees, waking at every tick of settling stone. Somewhere in the small hours I heard, or dreamed I heard, a whistle from far up the slope—three notes, rising. Elodie's signal, the one we'd agreed on back when we still believed separation was a thing that happened to other expeditions. I whistled back until my lips split. The wind ate it. Either she heard me or she didn't, and either way there was nothing to do but what my father would have done, which was survive the night and let the morning do the finding.

* * *

Morning came grey and hard, with a wind that had opinions about my face. I broke camp, buried my fire, and stood at the base of the slide fan trying to decide which way was *forward* when forward had been erased.

That was when I used the last rule. The big one. The one I'd never once managed to make work in the tailings below Coldharbor, the one I'd decided was probably just something fathers said.

*Read the mountain.*

He'd taught me to read talus. Fresh slide stone is clean—unweathered, sharp-edged, no lichen, the color of a broken bone against the grey-brown of old stone. Old slides settle; new slides sit proud and raw. And a slide, like a river, has a logic: it tells you where it came from, and it tells you where it went, and if you read it right, it tells you where the *stable* ground is, because the mountain, in knocking everything down, also shows you everything that held.

I read the fan for an hour. I found the old trail's continuation on the far side, buried but traceable—a cut in the slope where the switchback resumed, marked by a blaze on a stunted pine that had survived the slide by the width of a prayer. And I found something else, something that stopped my breath: mule tracks. Two days old, maybe less, pressed into the soft rubble at the fan's edge, heading along the base of the slope—*around*, not over. Someone had come down off the trail after the slide and gone around the fan, the smart way, the way a riverwoman would think, hugging the low ground.

Elodie. It had to be Elodie. Ferro would have gone straight up and broken his neck; Elodie would read the slope the way I read a river's surface, all the hidden currents of it.

They were ahead of me. Or beside me. Or behind me. But they were *moving*, and so, therefore, was I.

I shouldered the pack, checked the rifle, and gave Petunia—one moment of silence for Petunia, who I choose to believe is eating someone's garden in paradise—a final thought. Then I started along the base of the fan, following the mule prints, reading as I went: the clean stone, the settled stone, the seep-water channels, the wind's habit of this valley. The mountain had stopped being an enemy and started being a text, and I was, for the first time in my life, fluent.

It was almost noon when I topped the low shoulder below the switchback and saw, maybe half a mile ahead on the trail, two figures and two mules, moving slow, stopping to search, doubling back. My chest did something complicated. I filled my lungs to whistle the three rising notes—

And then I saw the third figure.

He was well off the trail, below and to the east, moving the way you move when you don't want to be seen: in the shadows of the rocks, stopping when they stopped, a dark shape with a long gun and the patient, terrible economy of a man who has hunted things before. He wasn't following our trail. He was following *me*—or he had been, along the base of the fan, and had lost me when I climbed, and now he was cutting the angle toward Elodie and Ferro on the trail, and there was no version of the arithmetic where they saw him in time.

I knew that walk. I'd seen it on the docks in Coldharbor, on the men who'd come asking questions about my father with smiles that didn't reach anywhere near their eyes. Harrow men. The Syndicate hadn't stayed behind on the river. It had climbed.

The whistle died in my throat. My father's voice, calm as Sunday, in my head one more time: *Heat draws eyes, Wrenny. And the thing that finds you in the high country is never glad to see you.*

I got low behind the boulder, checked the rifle, and started to think like a prospector's daughter—because it turned out that's exactly what I was, and exactly what he'd always known.
