## 20. Dain on the Trail

He came out of the treeline the way weather comes—no announcement, just a change in the pressure of things.

I'd felt it for an hour before I saw him. The mules had gone quiet in that particular way animals go quiet when something with teeth is nearby, and Ferro kept glancing up-slope without knowing why, and Wren hummed to herself while she cooked, which she only did when she was nervous and trying not to be. A crew learns each other's tells fast or dies slow. Ours were all pointing at the same direction of compass.

I was checking the lashings on the second mule when he stepped into camp.

Corvus Dain. Alone. No rifle slung, hands visible, hat brim low enough to be polite and high enough to show me his eyes. He looked like a man who had walked three days through scree and cold on purpose, which was somehow worse than if he'd stumbled after us half-dead.

"Captain Voss." He said my whole name like he was laying it on a table between us. Nobody on this mountain called me Voss except him and the dead.

"Ferro," I said, without turning my head, "the stove."

Ferro moved. Wren didn't—she was already on her feet with a frying pan in one hand and murder in her face, and I put out an arm before she could do something we'd both regret. Dain watched her with something that might have been professional appreciation.

"That's the daughter," he said. Not a question.

"She's the cook," I said. "And you're trespassing on my camp."

"Your camp." He smiled a little. "On Harrow survey land, inside a Harrow concession, carrying Harrow property."

"The mountain doesn't belong to anybody. That's rather the point of mountains."

He laughed—a short, dry sound, like a lock turning—and then he did the thing I hated most about him, which was sit down. He settled onto a rock across the fire as if he'd been invited, unhurried, dusting snow off his knees. A man who sits down in your camp is telling you he's not afraid of what's in it. A man who comes alone is telling you something else entirely: *I don't need men for what happens next.*

"I'm going to make you an offer," he said. "The same one I made at the lock, but better. And then I'm going to leave, and you're going to have until the pass closes to decide."

"We decided at the lock."

"You stalled at the lock." He held up two fingers. "Two days' travel behind you, Captain. Two days, and I caught you alone. Now imagine what six men could do, or twelve, or what happens when word reaches the far side of the range before you do. You're walking toward a door that only opens once a year, and I know exactly when it opens."

Wren's grip tightened on the pan. Ferro had drifted to stand near the mules, casual as a man admiring tack, and I knew he was doing arithmetic too—one of him, one of me, one girl, against whatever Dain hadn't brought.

"Say your offer," I said.

"The debt is forgiven. All of it. Every coin, every favor, every name you signed in blood down in Vellport—struck from the ledger. You walk away clean. First time in six years, Elodie Voss owes nobody nothing." He let that sit. He was good at letting things sit; it was most of his trade. "And the girl goes free. Unmolested, uncomplicated, back down to Coldharbor whenever she likes, with her father's good name intact in every paper that matters. The Harrows have no interest in her. Never did."

"And in exchange."

"The packet."

Just that. The packet. As if it were a tin of tea.

I looked at the fire instead of at him, because looking at him while I thought was how he won. Six years of debt. Six years of running cargo I didn't ask about, docking in ports where my name got me waved through or waved off depending on whose palm was greased, sleeping with a knife under my pillow and a ledger in my head that never balanced in my favor. One oilskin packet, and I was a woman with no past and no leash.

It would be so easy. That was the horror of it—not the danger, the ease. Dain wasn't threatening me. He was offering me my life back at a price someone else had already paid.

Tomas Reyes had paid it, bleeding out on my deck with my hand pressed over a hole in him, trading his life's work for a promise I'd made to a dying man because it cost me nothing to promise the dying.

Except now it didn't cost nothing. Now it cost everything, and the arithmetic had changed, and arithmetic was supposed to be my best subject.

"How do I know the girl walks away?" I asked. "Words are cheap on a mountain."

Dain reached slowly into his coat—Ferro tensed, Wren stepped forward—and drew out a folded paper, official-looking, and tossed it onto the ground between us. I didn't pick it up. He nodded at it anyway.

"A signed release. Harrow seal. It says the Syndicate has no claim on Wren Reyes, no interest in her person or property, and holds no grievance against her father's estate. It's real, Captain. I don't waste real paper."

"It's real," I agreed, "right up until it isn't. I've seen Harrow seals on bills of lading full of nothing but river water."

"You've seen what we allow you to see." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, firelight catching the flat of his face. "Elodie. May I call you Elodie? We've done business six years; we're practically married."

"Don't."

"Elodie, then. Think about what you're actually carrying. A dead man's drawing on oiled cloth. Landmarks and guesses. Even if there's silver up there—and I'll grant there might be—it's rock in the ground until somebody digs it out, and digging takes men and money and years, none of which you have. What you have is a piece of cloth that makes the most powerful syndicate on the Vell want you dead. That's not treasure. That's a target with a map printed on the back of it."

"He's lying," Wren said suddenly. Her voice cracked on it. "My father found that vein. He *found* it, and they took it from him—"

"Wren." Quiet. She stopped, shaking.

Dain turned to her, and here's the thing about Corvus Dain—he was genuinely gentle when it served him. "Miss Reyes. I understand you loved your father. But fathers lie to their daughters more often than strangers do. Ask your new captain sometime about the man who taught her the river. Ask her what he told *her*."

That landed where he wanted it to land. I felt it go in.

"The offer stands until the pass closes," he said, standing, brushing off his coat. "After that, the offer changes shape. I'd think on it tonight, if I were you. Sleep on it. That's all I ask." He picked his way toward the treeline, then paused, half-turned, silhouetted against the last grey light coming off the ice fields above us.

"One more thing," he said. "A courtesy. So you know I deal straight."

I waited.

"The old man," Dain said. "Reyes. When my collectors boarded your boat at Millbrook dock, they weren't hunting a thief. They were finishing a job. He was shot on the water two days before you pulled him out of the reeds. Shot by Harrow men, on Harrow orders, for taking what was ours." He adjusted his hat. "You didn't save him, Captain. You just delayed the paperwork."

And he walked into the trees, and the dark took him, and the mountain went on being quiet around us like nothing had happened at all.

* * *

Nobody spoke for a long time.

The fire popped. Somewhere above us, wind moved through the high passes with a sound like a long exhale, the glacier breathing in its sleep. Wren stood frozen with the frying pan still in her hand, and I watched the understanding move through her the way frost moves through a window—slow, then all at once, then everywhere.

"They shot him," she said. Her voice came out very small. "They shot my father. In the water. And he—" She looked at me, and her eyes were wet and furious and seventeen years old, and I would have traded the map, the boat, the whole rotten river to unmake what was on her face. "He was swimming. He was shot and he swam, and he crawled into the reeds, and he held on—for *days*—because he was waiting for a boat. Because he knew someone would come down the river eventually, and he was going to give them—give *me*—"

"Wren—"

"He died on your floor." Not an accusation. Worse. A fact, laid down gently, like a stone on a cairn. "You knew. This whole time. Since Coldharbor. You knew how he died and you didn't tell me."

"I told you he was dead."

"You told me he was *dead*. That's not the same thing and you know it's not the same thing." She set the pan down very carefully, the way people do when the alternative is throwing it. "Why? Why wouldn't you tell me?"

Because the truth was a weight and I'd been carrying other people's weights since I was younger than her. Because grief is heavy enough without murder in it. Because I'd told myself she needed to be strong for the climb and grief makes people stupid on scree. All true. None of them the truth.

"Because I promised him I'd deliver the packet," I said finally. "Not the grief. Just the packet. I figured the rest was mine to carry."

"Well, stop." She wiped her face with the back of her glove, hard, twice. "Stop carrying my parts. I'm not freight, Captain."

"No," I said. "You're not."

Ferro cleared his throat from the mule line. "For what it's worth, the release paper is likely genuine. Which means it's also worthless. A document is only as good as the organization behind it, and theirs dissolves the moment it becomes inconvenient." He said it mildly, the way he said everything, but his eyes were on me, and they asked the question Wren was too angry to ask: *What did he offer you, and how close did you come?*

Close. That was the answer. Close enough that I could still feel the shape of it in my chest, the clean empty space where six years of debt used to live. I'd stood at that fire and priced a dead man's promise against my own freedom, and the price had almost seemed fair.

Almost.

Until Dain opened his mouth and did me the single greatest kindness of my life, which was tell me the truth.

"He killed him," Wren said again, quieter now, sitting down heavily on a crate. "And then he sat at our fire and offered to forgive *your* debt for it. Like it was money. Like my father was a line in a ledger."

"That's exactly what he thinks it is," I said. "Everything's a line in a ledger. That's the whole religion."

She looked up at me. "So what do we do?"

I crouched by the fire and fed it a stick, and thought about the question honestly, because she deserved honesty even if I'd been rationing it out to her like hardtack.

Here's what I knew. Dain had come alone, which meant either he was confident or he was desperate, and Corvus Dain was never confident without reason. He'd shown me a real document, which meant the Syndicate wanted this handled quietly—paper, not bullets, at least not yet. He'd told me they'd murdered Tomas, which no careful man does unless he wants something badly enough to spend leverage buying it. And he'd given me until the pass closed, which meant somewhere behind us or ahead of us, the rest of his arithmetic was moving.

And here's what I felt, which is different from knowing, and on the Vell River I learned to trust feeling over knowledge more often than not.

I felt done. Done with owing. Done with the ledger. Six years I'd told myself the debt was just rope around my ankle, harmless if I didn't pull it, survivable if I stayed small and quick and unnamed. And then a dying man in my cabin had handed me a map and a promise, and a girl had stowed away on my boat, and a liar with a surveyor's conscience had signed on for penance, and somewhere between the reeds and the treeline I'd stopped being a woman alone on the water and started being something I didn't have a word for yet.

Dain thought he'd cornered me with an offer. He'd actually cornered me with a confession. He'd told me the Harrows murdered Tomas Reyes in cold water, and he'd told me in front of Tomas's daughter, and there was no version of any world after that where I handed them the packet.

"We keep climbing," I said.

"And the offer?"

"The offer died the moment he opened his mouth." I looked at Wren. "Your father trusted the wrong river and it cost him. I nearly did the same tonight. Difference is, I got a warning shot across the bow first."

Ferro sat down across the fire from me, and for a moment the three of us just breathed, and the mountain breathed around us, and I noticed—I don't know when it happened, but I noticed—that nobody suggested splitting up. Nobody said *this is too much, turn back.* Wren was wiping her eyes and feeding the fire. Ferro was cleaning his spectacles with the hem of his coat. And I was sitting among them thinking that Corvus Dain had made one mistake tonight, and it wasn't showing me the paper.

It was teaching me what the packet was worth to *them*. Men who kill for a piece of oiled cloth don't fear the silver on it. They fear what's written next to the silver.

"Tomas's landmarks," I said slowly. "Ferro. When you read them at the last camp. The claim markers, the boundary stones. Did anything strike you as odd?"

Ferro put his spectacles back on and looked at me for a long moment, and I watched the old surveyor's mind turn over behind them, patient as tide.

"Odd," he repeated. "Yes. You could say that." He reached for his satchel. "There was a notation on the eastern boundary that shouldn't be there. On a silver claim. And now that our friend has confirmed the Harrows killed the man who drew it—Captain, I think I need to show you why a prospector would risk his life marking something on a map that has nothing to do with silver at all."

He unfolded the map by firelight, and the wind came down off the glacier, and somewhere below us in the dark, the mountain shifted its weight.
