## 21. Blood on the Moraine

I have been shot before. I want that on the record, in my own hand, because afterward there may be no one left to write it down: Augustin Ferro, surveyor, liar, and—on at least one occasion now—a man who has stood between a bullet and a woman who deserved better than either of them.

The first time was a hunting accident involving my uncle and a bottle of plum brandy, and it taught me that being shot feels less like a wound than like a correction. The world revises itself around you. The mountain tilts. Your body becomes a rumor you hear secondhand.

This time was no accident.

* * *

We had come down off the scree field at first light, Wren limping but alive—which is a longer story, and hers to tell—and Elodie counting our remaining supplies with the grim arithmetic of a woman doing her taxes on a sinking ship. Three mules. Half the flour gone to the rockslide. Ferro's ribs bruised from where he'd gone down hard, though I had told no one about the blood I'd coughed into my sleeve that morning. A man must keep some accounts private.

The moraine spread below us like a river that had died standing up: a mile of gray stone dumped by a glacier that had lost interest in it centuries ago. No cover taller than your boot. No water. No shade. Just rock upon rock upon rock, and somewhere beyond its northern edge, the ice.

Elodie hated it on sight. "Open ground," she said, spitting into the wind. "Every gun within a mile can see us."

"Then we cross it fast," Wren said.

We did not cross it fast. That was my fault, or the mule's, depending on how generous history intends to be. The lead animal—Biscuit, named by Wren for reasons never adequately explained—stepped into a hollow between stones and came up lame, favoring his near foreleg with the theatrical despair of his kind. Elodie swore in three languages, two of which I suspect she invented herself, and we stopped to look at the foot.

That is when the rocks spoke.

Not the rocks themselves. Corvus Dain, standing up out of the gray of the moraine as if he had been quarried along with it, and behind him the shapes of six men rising from cover they had held, patient as lichen, since before dawn.

* * *

Here is what I remember of the next ninety seconds, laid out in the order my mind has agreed to keep them, though I know some pieces are missing and others may simply be inventions—memory being a surveyor too, and an dishonest one.

Dain's voice first. Unhurried. He had the tone of a man reading terms aloud in a bank parlor rather than shouting across a field of stones with a rifle in his hands. "Captain Voss. Last offer. It improves every time I make it, you'll notice. Debt forgiven. The girl walks away untouched. You hand over the packet and we all go home to warm beds."

"And if I say no?" Elodie called back. Her voice carried flat and clear across the moraine, the voice she used on rivers, the one that doesn't tremble because trembling costs speed.

"Then I stop making offers," Dain said, "and start making examples."

I remember thinking, with a clarity that still shames me: *he will shoot her first.* Not Wren, whose testimony he needed dead more than living. Not me, whom he considered furniture. Elodie—the one variable he could not predict, the woman who ran rapids in the dark and lied to collectors with a smile. Kill the captain and the crew scatters. Simple doctrine. I have read enough military reports to recognize doctrine when it aims a rifle at someone I—

Well. At someone.

I remember moving. I don't remember deciding to move. There is a difference, and the difference is everything anyone means when they talk about courage, or foolishness; the words are interchangeable and both are lies. What I did was step sideways, into the line between Elodie and the muzzle flash I saw bloom against the gray like a gray flower opening.

The world corrected itself.

* * *

Pain arrived late, as pain does when it travels any real distance. First there was only the impact—a fist the size of a cathedral door, striking me high on the left side, spinning me down onto the stones. Then cold. Then, arriving like a debt collector after a decent interval, the burning.

I lay on my back on the moraine and looked up at a sky so pale and wide it seemed embarrassed to be witnessing this. Somewhere above me voices were shouting. Rifle fire cracked and rolled among the boulders, echoing strangely, so that it sounded as though the whole valley were shooting at us and us alone.

"Ferro." Elodie's face appeared above mine, upside down from my perspective, which struck me as funny. Everything struck me as funny. I believe that is a known symptom. "Ferro, look at me."

"I am looking at you," I said. My voice came out wrong, thin and distant, a telegraph message from a station half-destroyed. "You're upside down."

"You're the one lying down." Her hands were on my chest, pressing, and I learned something new about pain, which is that it has registers, like an organ, and she found all of them at once. I may have made a sound. I prefer to think I didn't.

"It went through," she said, mostly to herself, tearing open my coat. "Through and through. Left shoulder. Missed the—" She stopped. She didn't finish the sentence, but I finished it for her, silently: *missed the lung*. Though judging by the wet weight in my chest, it had missed by a margin a surveyor would call negligible.

"Wren!" Elodie shouted. "The kit! Now!"

And then, absurdly, the battle continued around us while I received medical attention, which is not how battles are supposed to work. I heard Elodie drag me by the collar behind a boulder the size of a chapel, grunting, swearing, refusing help. I heard Wren arrive at a run and drop to her knees beside me, and I heard her breath catch when she saw the blood—there was, I gathered, a great deal of it. Blood looks smaller on the person losing it than on the stones receiving it. The stones were getting most of it.

"He took the bullet," Wren said. Not a question. She said it the way people say things they will need to repeat later, to themselves, in the dark.

"He moved," Elodie said, and there was something raw under the flatness. "Idiot moved."

"In fairness," I managed, "it was very quick. Barely a decision at all."

"Shut up, Ferro."

"Yes, Captain."

Wren's hands were on me then, competent and shaking at once, packing the wounds with cloth from the kit while Elodie reloaded and returned fire over the top of our boulder. I remember thinking that the two of them made a strange kind of surgeon—one holding me together, one holding the world away—and that I would have trusted them with anything, including, apparently, my life, which was the transaction currently underway.

* * *

Fading now. I want to be honest about that, since honesty is the one currency I've been short of all my life. There are gaps in what follows, and where the gaps are, I will tell you, and where I've filled them with guesswork, I won't pretend otherwise.

Gap.

I come back to the sound of Wren's voice, close to my ear, saying my name over and over like a word in a language she was trying to learn. "Augustin. Augustin. Stay with me. Augustin." Nobody had used my given name in eleven years. It fit poorly, like a coat returned from storage. But I opened my eyes for her.

She had blood to her elbows. Mine. She looked younger than seventeen and older than the mountains, both at once, and she said, "You saved her. Why? You barely know her."

"Knew a man once," I said. Each word cost postage. "Stood by. Watched a lie ruin someone. Told myself it was survival." I coughed, and the cough tasted of copper. "Survival is cheap, Wren. It's the only thing that is."

She pressed her forehead against my good shoulder, just for a moment, just long enough. Then Elodie was shouting something about the flank, and Wren was gone, and I was alone with the sky again, which had turned the color of old pewter and begun, with terrible indifference, to snow.

* * *

What happened next I reconstruct partly from later accounts, since I spent much of it unconscious or wishing to be. Here is the version I have assembled:

Elodie Voss, wounded nowhere but everywhere in spirit, fought six Harrow men across a quarter mile of open moraine with a rifle, a pistol, and a vocabulary that would blister paint. She used the boulder field the way she used sandbars—reading the terrain faster than the men who thought they knew it, always seeming to be where they weren't, firing from angles that shouldn't have existed. Wren, meanwhile, discovered that a girl raised on a prospector's knee knows things soldiers don't: which stones ring hollow, which slopes are load-bearing lies, which hollows hold a man's weight and which give way beneath it. Twice she lured Harrow men onto ground that betrayed them. One of them broke a leg badly enough to scream the rest of the engagement, which I'm told did wonders for his colleagues' morale.

And Biscuit—the lame mule, poor doomed Biscuit—panicked during the exchange and bolted north, pack and all, directly through the Harrow line. Two men dove after him. Whether they caught him or he dragged them, I couldn't say. But I am told that in the confusion, Dain made his choice: cut losses, take what the mountain handed him. When the smoke cleared—or the snow, rather, which was falling thickly enough to serve—he was gone, along with two of his men, one screaming on a makeshift litter, and one mule carrying a third of our flour, the tent canvas, and, Elodie reported with a bitterness I could taste from my boulder, the coffee.

"The coffee," she repeated, kneeling beside me, checking the packing on my shoulder with hands that finally allowed themselves to shake. "He took the coffee, Ferro. We nearly die on a rock pile and the man robs us of *coffee*."

"There are worse crimes," I said.

"Not many." She looked at me then, really looked, and whatever she saw made her jaw tighten. "You should have let him shoot me."

"A captain's judgment," I said, "is not infallible."

"I'm serious."

"So am I. I've watched brave women die for less than pride." I tried to shrug with the wrong shoulder and immediately regretted the entire concept of shoulders. "Besides. You owe me now. Debts are my area of expertise. I collect."

Something crossed her face—too fast to name, gone before it landed. She tied off the bandage, hauled me upright with Wren's help, and got my arm across her neck as though I weighed nothing, which flattered me until I realized I weighed almost exactly nothing anymore.

"We move," she said. "Now. Before he regroups, before the snow buries the trail, before—" She stopped herself. Looked north, toward the ice, invisible behind the white curtain. "Before the window closes."

* * *

They carried me. I want that written plainly, because a man of my vanity would otherwise spend the rest of his life pretending he walked. Elodie took one side and Wren the other, and between them they bore me across the moraine in a stumbling three-legged procession while the snow thickened and the light failed and my blood marked the stones behind us like a trail even a Harrow man could follow.

At some point Wren began talking to me—not asking questions, just talking, a low steady stream of words meant to keep some channel of me open. She talked about Coldharbor in spring. About her father teaching her to read stars by naming the ones he'd made up himself. About whether mules could be taught to swim, and if Biscuit had swum, and if mules went to heaven or merely to better pastures. I answered perhaps one word in ten. She didn't seem to need the answers. She needed the sound of a conversation that refused to end.

Somewhere in the gray middle of that march, I understood something I had spent forty years and one false survey report avoiding: that this—being carried, being kept alive by stubbornness and talk and shared weight—was the thing I had lied to everyone, including myself, in order not to deserve. And here it was anyway. Given freely. By a runaway and a riverwoman, on a field of stones, in the snow.

It is a terrible thing, grace. It arrives without paperwork.

* * *

Night. Or what passes for night in that latitude—that blue-gray dusk that never fully commits. They propped me against the leeward wall of a boulder and built a fire small enough to hide, and Elodie rationed out the last of the food that wasn't riding north on a mule with a thief. Wren changed my dressings. The wound had stopped bleeding properly, which Elodie announced like a verdict, though her eyes said the trial wasn't over.

"Fever's coming," she told Wren quietly, thinking I was asleep. I was not asleep. I was eavesdropping, which is the traditional occupation of the dying and the disgraced. "If it turns bad, he'll need a doctor. The nearest one is—"

"Vellport," Wren said. "Harrow territory."

"Or Coldharbor. Behind us. Through Dain."

Silence, except the wind and the fire.

"Then we go forward," Wren said. "We were going forward anyway."

I slept then, truly slept, and dreamed of maps. In the dream every map I had ever drawn unrolled itself across the moraine, and every false line I had ever inked glowed red, and Elodie walked along the lines correcting them with a pencil stub, unhurried, humming, until the whole country was true.

I woke to her hand on my forehead, cool and careful, and to her voice saying the words that pulled the future tight around us like a noose of clean white rope:

"Storm's coming early. The pass is closing. Whatever we're going to do—we do it tomorrow."
