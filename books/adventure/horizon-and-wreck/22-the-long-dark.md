## 22. The Long Dark

The storm came down on us like something with a grudge.

We'd made maybe four miles past the moraine before the sky turned the color of wet slate and the wind arrived with opinions. Elodie took one look at the ridgeline, said a word I won't repeat in mixed company, and pointed at a black seam in the rock face above the trail.

"Cave," she said. "We're not arguing about it."

We were not arguing about it. I'd have argued with a bear about it, if the bear offered shelter. That's what four days on a mountain does to you. It strips you down to the parts that want to live, and the parts that want to live are not proud.

The cave wasn't much. A hollow in the granite, maybe ten feet deep, with a ceiling low enough that Ferro had to stoop and a floor that sloped toward the entrance like it was trying to evict us. But it was out of the wind, and the wind was the enemy now—not the Harrows, not the map, not the whole rotten Syndicate. Just the wind, and what came with it.

We got Ferro inside first. He'd been walking on his own since the moraine, which I will say for him, but by the time we reached the cave mouth he was leaning on my shoulder and pretending he wasn't. He was good at pretending. He'd had years of practice, I suppose, being the kind of man who lies for a living and then lies about the lying.

"Cozy," he managed, as we lowered him against the back wall.

"Liar," I said, and was surprised to find it came out almost fond.

* * *

Here is what we had, on the evening the storm sealed us in:

One oilcloth tarp. A spirit stove with maybe a cup and a half of fuel. Food for three people for two days, if the three people were sparrows. A quarter pound of tea. Elodie's compass, her knife, and a coil of rope that had already saved my life once on the scree and looked tired of the work. Ferro's surveying kit, which weighed more than he did and was worth less, right now, than a single dry match.

And Ferro himself, burning.

It started slow, the way bad things do. By midnight of the first day he was shivering under both blankets and the tarp, and his skin, when I touched his forehead to check—because I'd been checking, because somebody had to—was hot as a stove plate.

"Fever," I said to Elodie, unnecessarily. She'd been a river captain long enough to know what sick looked like.

"I know."

"From the wound?"

"From the wound, from the cold, from the walking, from being sixty years old and shot." She was feeding the stove a careful pinch of fuel. "From being Augustin Ferro, which would put fever in a healthy man."

Ferro, from under the blankets, in a voice thick with delirium: "I heard that."

"Good," said Elodie. "Stay awake. Sleeping's the enemy tonight."

He didn't stay awake. That was the beginning of the long dark, properly speaking—not the storm, which was only weather, but the watching, the two of us taking turns, the hours measured not in clock time but in the sound of a man's breathing.

* * *

Elodie took first watch. I took second. That was the arrangement, and it held for about six hours before the arrangement stopped mattering, because neither of us could sleep anyway.

I want to describe what a storm sounds like from inside a mountain, because I'd never heard it before and I don't think I'll forget it. It doesn't howl, exactly. It *presses*. The wind comes down the valley and hits the rock face and has nowhere to go, so it climbs over itself, and the whole night sounds like the sea—like the Vell in flood, which I knew from childhood, from the summers before my father left. The rock hums. Little stones rattle down the slope outside and tick against the entrance like fingers.

I sat with Ferro's head in my lap and counted his breaths, because Elodie said to, because a man with fever who breathes too fast or too slow is telling you something, and the mountain doesn't translate.

Around what I judged was two in the morning, Elodie crawled over from the stove and sat beside me without a word. She poured tea—weak tea, tea that had been brewed four times and had given up all ambition—and handed me a tin cup.

"Drink," she said. "You're no use to him dehydrated."

I drank. It was terrible. It was the best thing I'd ever tasted.

"He's going to be all right," I said.

"Probably."

"That's not comforting."

"I'm not a comforting person, Wren. I'm a person who keeps you alive." She looked at Ferro—at the gray in his stubble, the sweat on his brow, the way his hands moved slightly, sorting invisible papers. "I've known men die of less than this. And I've known men survive worse. The difference is usually whether somebody stayed up counting their breaths."

"So we count."

"So we count."

* * *

The second day, the storm didn't stop. The third day, I stopped expecting it to.

I'll tell you what I learned about Elodie Voss in that cave, because I'd spent two weeks on her boat and in her company and I knew her the way you know a locked door—by its shape, not its contents. The cave opened the door. Not all the way. But a crack.

It started, as most things with her did, sideways.

"Your father," she said, on the second evening, while I changed Ferro's bandage. The wound in his shoulder had gone angry-red at the edges, and the smell of it was sweet and wrong. I'd cleaned it with the last of our whiskey, which Ferro had protested with a speech about waste that dissolved into nonsense halfway through. "He taught you the prospecting tricks. The ones that kept you alive on the scree."

"Some of them."

"Which ones?"

I told her. About reading the slope before you cross it—where the stones sit loose and where they've knitted together over years. About the trick of lying flat when the ground starts to move, because a rolling rock takes the standing man and skips the prone one. About listening for the mountain's *voice*, which is what Papa called the deep groan the rock makes before it lets go.

"He taught me those the way other fathers teach baseball," I said. "I thought everyone's father did it. I thought it was just—things you know. Like which berries are safe." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "I didn't know he was training me. For what, I wonder. For this? He couldn't have known this."

"Parents teach what they're afraid of," Elodie said. She said it flat, like a fact about river depths. "My father taught me to read water because he was afraid of drowning. I learned the sandbars of the Vell because I was afraid of being found."

"Found by who?"

She was quiet long enough that I thought she wouldn't answer. The stove hissed. Ferro muttered something about contour lines and turned his face to the wall.

"By everyone," she said finally. "That's the short version. The long version's got a man in it, and a smuggling run, and a debt I've been paying for nine years, and I'm not telling it in a cave with a dying man listening, because he'll remember it and hold it over me at cards."

"Ferro doesn't play cards."

"Ferro cheats at everything, Wren. Cards are just the honest version."

And that was it, that night. A crack, not an opening. But I lay there after she took over the watch, with my father's fever-heat radiating through the blanket into my back, and I thought: she's afraid of being found. And she's spent her whole life on a river, which is the one place that erases you. Footprints in water.

I understood it better than I wanted to. I'd run from Coldharbor the same way, just slower.

* * *

The third night was the worst night.

Ferro's fever spiked past anything I had words for. He stopped knowing where he was. He talked—to people who weren't there, to my father, once, which stopped my heart and restarted it, to a woman he called Marta, softly, in a language I didn't know, apologizing for something in a voice that made me look away, because some griefs you don't eavesdrop on even when they're happening two feet from you.

Elodie held him down through the worst of it, her hands on his shoulders, and I'll tell you something I didn't expect to see: she was gentle. This woman who ran rapids in the dark and lied to Syndicate collectors like breathing, and she held a sick old man like he was something that could break.

"Talk to him," she said to me, over her shoulder. "He's past hearing, but talk anyway. It gives him something to swim toward."

So I talked. I told him about Coldharbor, about the winter I was nine when Papa came home with a silver dollar and let me hold it all evening, about the way he whistled when he thought no one was listening—badly, always off-key, like the tune was a rumor he'd heard secondhand. I told him things I hadn't said out loud since he died, and some things I'd never said at all, like that I was angry at him, furious, for leaving, for the disgrace, for making me spend seventeen years thinking I knew what kind of man he was and then dying in a stranger's boat with a map in his fist.

"I don't know who you were," I said to Ferro, because it was true of both of them and I was too tired to separate the sentences. "None of us knows who anybody was. We just get the pieces."

Elodie's hand found my shoulder in the dark. She didn't say anything. She didn't have to. That was the thing I was learning about her—that her silences weren't walls, they were just rooms she hadn't furnished yet.

* * *

Near dawn on the fourth day, the fever broke.

It broke the way fevers do, all at once, a tide going out. Ferro's breathing slowed and deepened, and the heat came off him in waves, and by the time gray light found the cave mouth he was sleeping like an actual person instead of a man being dragged through his own nightmares.

Elodie and I crawled out onto the ledge to look at the sky.

The storm was dying. The clouds were tearing apart in rags, and through the tears we could see the high peaks, white and brutal and lit up gold at the edges, and the glacier country beyond them, the blue distance where we still had to go. The air after a storm has a taste. Clean and mineral, like the mountain had been scoured down to its bones and didn't mind showing them.

"We've got maybe a day of clear weather," Elodie said. "Maybe two. Then the next system comes through, and after that, the pass closes for real."

"Ferro can't walk tomorrow."

"Ferro can walk the day after, or we leave him with half the food and a gun, and I don't say that because I want to." She said it looking straight at me, because that was Elodie—she'd hand you the ugly arithmetic and let you do it yourself. "I'm telling you now so you can be angry at me in advance. It saves time."

"I'm not angry."

"You will be."

"Maybe." I hugged my knees and looked at the peaks. "Elodie. Can I ask you something and you answer it straight, no river-captain dodging?"

"I make no promises."

"Why did you take the promise? Tomas's promise. You didn't know him. You didn't know me. You owed the Harrows, you were hunted, and every smart part of you said drop the map in the river and keep your head down. I've watched you for two weeks. Keeping your head down is your whole religion. So why?"

The wind had dropped to almost nothing. Down the valley, somewhere, a chunk of ice calved off something high and far, and the sound rolled over us like distant thunder.

"Because he was dying," she said, "and he asked. And I have a—" She stopped. Started over. "When I was younger, someone asked me for something, and I gave it, and it ruined my life. And for nine years I've been telling myself the lesson was: never promise anyone anything. Stay invisible. Pay cash. No last name." She turned the compass over in her hands, the brass case catching the early light. "But that's not the lesson. The lesson was: be careful *who*. Not never. I just—" A short, dry laugh. "I got the lesson wrong for nine years. Tomas Reyes was the first person who asked me for the right thing."

"And what's that?"

"To be worth something to somebody." She stood, and brushed the frost off her coat, and looked down at me with that expression she had, the one that was half wry and half a locked door. "Get some sleep, Wren. We move when he can move."

* * *

Ferro woke at noon, lucid for the first time in two days, and demanded tea with the imperiousness of a man who had recently been near death and intended to exploit it.

"Two days," he croaked, when we told him where he'd been. "I missed two days?"

"You missed the storm, a very moving speech from Wren about your character, and my confession that I cheat at cards."

"I didn't miss the cards, then. I know how you cheat." He tried to sit up, failed, tried again, and I put my shoulder under his arm and helped, and he weighed nothing, nothing, this man who'd carried surveying gear over these same ranges thirty years ago. He looked at the cave mouth, at the torn sky, at the white silence beyond. "The window," he said. "The calving season. How long?"

"Days," Elodie said. "Not many."

He nodded, slowly, and I watched him do the same arithmetic she'd done, and then something else, something older—the look of a man measuring the distance between what he owes and what he has left to pay it with.

"Then we go tomorrow," Ferro said. "Fever or no fever. I've spent thirty years not finishing things, Captain. I'll not spend a thirty-first."

Elodie looked at him a long moment. Then at me. Then out at the glacier country, where the light was already starting to change, the gold going thin at the edges of the peaks, the mountain winding itself up for whatever it meant to do next.

"Tomorrow, then," she said. And then, quieter, almost to herself, in the voice of a woman reading water she didn't trust: "God help us, tomorrow."

That night, packing by stove-light, I found it at the bottom of Ferro's kit: a folded paper, soft with age, that I recognized before I opened it. My father's handwriting. A letter, never sent, addressed to *my Wren*, dated the spring he left Coldharbor.

I didn't read it. Not yet. I held it a long time, feeling the mountain breathe outside, and then I put it in my coat, against my heart, where the weight of it could keep me company through whatever came tomorrow.
