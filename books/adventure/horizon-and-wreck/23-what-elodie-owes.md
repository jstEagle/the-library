## 23. What Elodie Owes

The storm did not break. That was the thing about mountain storms nobody tells you on the river—on the Vell, weather is a negotiation, and you can usually talk your way out of it. Up here, weather is a verdict.

Four days now, or five. I'd stopped counting honestly somewhere around the second morning, when Ferro's fever climbed high enough that time went soft at the edges, like a riverbank after flood. The cave was narrow and dry-ish, which by mountain standards made it a palace: a slot in the granite above the moraine, mouth half-curtained with drift, big enough for three people, two mules, and a diminishing inventory of food that I had stopped looking at directly because looking made it smaller.

Ferro lay against my bedroll near the back, shivering under every blanket we owned, his breath coming in a wet whistle that I liked less each hour. The bullet had gone through the meat of his shoulder—clean, the way bullets go when they're lucky—and clean should have meant simple. But he'd bled a lot getting down off the moraine, and then we'd carried him through cold that gets into a wound like water into rope, and now the fever had him.

Wren sat by the fire ring, feeding it sticks one at a time, rationing even the fire. She'd gotten good at that. Four days ago she'd been a girl who'd never built anything but arguments; now she could lay a fire in wet wood, splint a forearm, stretch three days of biscuit into six. She'd learned it all fast and without complaint, which was more than I could say for myself at seventeen, or twenty-seven, or possibly ever.

He moaned once, low, and she was up before I'd finished setting down the tin cup. Cool cloth to his forehead. Water, a spoonful at a time, because he'd vomited the last full dose. She did it all without asking me what to do, and something about that—the competence of her, the ease of it—made my chest hurt in a way that had nothing to do with altitude.

"You should sleep," she said, not looking at me. "I'll take the watch."

"I slept."

"You slept an hour. You snore like a man being murdered."

"That's slander."

"It's testimony," she said. "Sworn."

I almost smiled. Almost. Then Ferro said something in his fever-voice—a name, maybe, or just a sound shaped like one—and the smile died where it stood, and Wren looked at me across the fire with those gray Reyes eyes, Tomas's eyes, patient as deep water, and said:

"Tell me."

"Tell you what?"

"Whatever it is you keep not saying." She folded the cloth, wrung it, laid it back across his brow. "You've been carrying something since before the scree field. Since Coldharbor, maybe. It sits on you like a pack you've forgotten how to take off." She settled back on her heels. "We're not going anywhere tomorrow. Or the day after. And I've told you everything I have—Coldharbor, the telegraph office, all of it, everything ugly included. You know my whole story now. Every bad piece."

"I do."

"So." She turned her hands palm-up over the fire, an old woman's gesture on a girl of seventeen. "Fair trade. What Elodie owes."

The fire popped. Outside, the wind moved snow across the cave mouth in long white breaths, and somewhere far below us, I imagined, the Kestrel sat moored and empty at Coldharbor dock, dreaming river dreams without me.

I found I wanted to tell her. That surprised me. For eleven years I had kept my story the way you keep ballast—low, dark, never discussed—and here was this girl, this stranger who wasn't a stranger anymore, asking for it like it was hers by right. Maybe it was. She'd paid for passage in ways no passenger ever had.

"All right," I said. "But you're not going to like me much after."

"I've hauled you out of a rockslide," she said. "My opinion's already load-bearing. Try me."

* * *

"My name isn't Elodie Voss."

Wren blinked. "It isn't?"

"It is now. Eleven years now. But I was born Elodie Marchetti. My father ran barges out of Vellport—grain, mostly, and salt going up. He taught me the river before he taught me letters. Said letters were optional but the current wasn't." I fed a stick to the fire, watched it catch. "He drowned when I was fourteen. Spring flood, snagged towline, the usual stupid way. After that it was me and the boat, and the boat was enough. For a while."

"And Voss?"

"The man I loved." Saying it out loud felt like lifting something heavy off a shelf I hadn't opened in years—dust and weight and a strange lightness underneath. "Dorian Voss. He captained for the Harrow Syndicate. Freight, they called it. Freight runs upriver to the mining camps, sealed crates, no manifests. I knew what it was. Don't let me pretend otherwise—I always knew. Silver ore off the books, and worse sometimes, and I told myself it didn't touch me because I only piloted, I only read the water, I only got the cargo where it needed to go." I laughed, short and joyless. "That's the oldest lie on any river. *I only steer.*"

Wren said nothing. She had her father's gift for silence, I was learning. Tomas had used his on me too, that night in the reeds, letting me talk myself into saving him.

"Dorian was—" I searched for it. "Charming is too small a word. Dorian was weather. He came into a room and the room rearranged itself around him. He had this laugh, and this way of looking at you like you were the only competent person in a world of fools, and God help me, I believed him. Believed him about the routes, about the plan, about us. We were going to run our own line someday, he said. The Voss-Marchetti Line. He had it all drawn up. He used to sketch the letterhead on napkins in dockside bars." My voice did something embarrassing on the last word, and I took a drink of tea to cover it. "He drew that letterhead for two years."

"What happened?" Wren asked quietly.

"A job. The big one. Forty crates of raw silver coming down from the upper camps, no manifest, no paper, straight through the Vellport customs reach. The Syndicate wanted it moved during the assayer's inspection week, when every official eye in the territory was pointed at paperwork instead of water. Dorian planned it. I flew it." I stared into the fire. "There's a channel below Grayling Rock that only opens at spring melt—you have to run it at night, by feel, by the sound the water makes off the rock. Nobody runs Grayling at night. I did it. Because he asked. Because he said I was the only one alive who could."

"And?"

"And the marshals were waiting on the other side." The words came flat now, worn smooth from years of turning them over alone in the dark. "Not for Dorian. For the pilot. They'd been tipped—someone inside the Syndicate bought their way clear, and the someone wasn't Dorian. He came through the channel ten minutes behind me, saw the lanterns on the dock, and turned around. Turned around, Wren. Ran back upstream and let them take me. Let them take *me*, with forty crates in my hold and his name on none of the papers, because he'd been careful that way. Careful for himself. Everything signed so it would land on me."

"He left you," Wren said. Not a question.

"He left me to eleven years of it. The Syndicate doesn't forgive losses—it finances them. My fine became a debt. The debt grew interest. Interest grew collectors. And the collectors grew teeth." I held up my hands, palms out, the way I did when I was lying to toll boats, except this time there was nothing to hide behind. "Every run since, every sandbar I've threaded, every cash payment in every dockside office—that's what it's been. Paying for loving a man who sold me to save his own skin. That's what Elodie owes. Not money. Money would be easy. It's the believing. I owe eleven years of interest on believing him."

The fire cracked. Ferro muttered and turned, and Wren smoothed his blanket without breaking eye contact with me, which I admired and resented in equal measure.

"Why are you telling me this now?" she asked.

"Because you asked."

"No." She shook her head slowly. "People don't answer questions like that just because they're asked. I've watched you dodge harder ones with a smile and a change of subject. Why now?"

I looked at her—at the soot on her cheek, the frost-burned knuckles, the letter from her father pressed safe inside her coat, the one I still hadn't given her, the one I was still, God forgive me, rationing like the biscuits—and I told the truth.

"Because Dain offered me my debt forgiven. On the trail. For the map. And I nearly said yes." Her face didn't move, so I kept going, faster, the confession picking up speed downhill like everything else on this mountain. "I stood there doing arithmetic with a dead man's map—your father's map—counting what my freedom was worth against what it would cost you, and the numbers were close, Wren. Closer than I'll ever be comfortable admitting. And then Dain said a thing that made the deal die, and I walked away, and I've been walking away from it ever since. But I haven't told you. I let you think I refused out of principle. Out of loyalty to you."

"Weren't you loyal? In the end?"

"In the end. But the end isn't the whole road." I set down the cup. "You deserve to know the whole road. If you're going to trust me with your father's silver, with your life on this ice, you should know exactly what kind of woman you're trusting. One who spent eleven years buying invisibility because visibility is what ruined her. One who nearly traded your inheritance for her own clean ledger. One who—" I stopped. Started again. "One who is afraid, most of the time, that the river is the only thing that's ever been honest with her."

* * *

For a long moment the only sounds were the wind and Ferro's ragged breathing and the small industrious crackle of our rationed fire.

Then Wren said, "My father left because of shame."

I looked up.

"Coldharbor talks. You heard them at the tavern—thief, claim-jumper, the man who sold out his partners. I grew up inside that story. Every year it got bigger and I got smaller inside it." She poked the fire, deliberate, composing herself the way she composed telegraph messages, I thought—in short clean bursts. "And now I'm sitting in a cave with a woman who's been carrying a false story for eleven years, and I realize—" Her voice caught, and she pushed through it. "I realize I know exactly what that does to a person. What it costs. How heavy it gets, and how you get so used to the weight you forget it's not part of your body."

"Wren—"

"Let me finish." She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, briskly, annoyed at them. "You nearly traded the map. Fine. You told me. Do you know what most people do? Most people carry the secret until it kills them or gets someone else killed, and then everyone says *if only we'd known*. You're telling me while we can still do something about it. While I can still decide—with open eyes—if I trust you." She looked at me straight on. "I do, by the way. Trust you. Not because you're good. Because you're honest about not being good, and that's rarer."

Something in my chest that had been clenched for eleven years loosened—not all the way, but enough that I noticed the difference, the way you notice a headache leaving.

"Dorian found me last year," I said. The words came out before I'd decided to spend them. "After the trial, after the debt, he vanished. Last winter he sent a letter. No apology. A business proposition. Said the old route through Grayling was open again if I wanted in." I smiled at the fire. "Eleven years, and the letter still smelled of the same cologne. I burned it on the stove and then I sat up all night wondering why that felt like losing something."

"Because it was yours first," Wren said simply. "The dream. The letterhead. He stole it along with everything else, and burning his letter couldn't unsteal it."

I stared at her. Seventeen years old, four days out of a rockslide, and she'd put in one sentence what I'd failed to put together in a decade of nights.

"You should write things down," I managed. "When we're done with this mountain. People should have to read that."

"I'll put it in the company ledger," she said, and the corner of her mouth lifted. "*Reyes-Voss Line.* You know, if we live, that name's going to need explaining someday."

"If we live," I said, "I'll explain it."

Ferro stirred then, properly this time—surfacing from the fever rather than thrashing beneath it—and his eyes found the firelight, then Wren, then me. "Are you two," he rasped, "having a moment without me?"

"Go back to sleep," Wren said.

"Couldn't help overhearing. Walls like paper in this cave." He tried to prop himself up, failed, settled for looking dignified lying down, which was a Ferro specialty. "Voss-Marchetti. I remember that name from the old freight schedules. Everyone thought the pilot died at Grayling. There was a memorial notice in the *Vellport Courier*. Very tasteful. Lilies."

"They buried a version of me," I said. "I let them. It seemed convenient at the time."

"And now?" Ferro's fever-bright eyes were suddenly, uncomfortably sharp. "Still convenient?"

I thought about the Kestrel, empty at her mooring. About the compass in my pocket that pointed wherever I refused to go. About a girl who trusted me not because I was good but because I said otherwise out loud.

"No," I said. "I don't suppose it is anymore."

* * *

Wren took first watch. I lay down beside Ferro's blankets, meaning to sleep, and instead listened to the storm work the mountainside and thought about ledgers—the Harrows', mine, my father's, the great invisible book where everything we owe gets written down whether we sign for it or not.

Somewhere past midnight, Wren shook me awake. Not for the watch.

"There's a light," she whispered. "Downslope. Moving."

I crawled to the cave mouth, careful of the drift, and looked out into the white roar. Far below, threading the black shoulder of the ridge, a lantern swung—steady, unhurried, following our own backtrail like a man reading a book he'd written.

One light. One man, walking alone through a blizzard toward us, either a fool or someone who already knew exactly where we were.

I knew which one Corvus Dain was.

"Get the pistol," I said quietly. "Wake Ferro. Whatever he promised us before—he's come to collect."
