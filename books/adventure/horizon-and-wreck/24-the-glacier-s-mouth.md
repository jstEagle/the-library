## 24. The Glacier's Mouth

We smelled the glacier before we saw it. That sounds like poetry, but I mean it as a practical matter: the last mile of the corridor trail carried a wind off the ice that tasted like iron and old stone and something almost sweet, the way a struck flint smells sweet right before it bites you. Ferro stopped at the head of the switchbacks with his hand on a boulder and his chest heaving, and he said, "Listen," and I said, "I don't hear anything," and he said, "That's what I mean."

He was right. The mountain had gone quiet in the wrong way. No birds. No meltwater chuckling under the scree. Just a silence stretched tight as a drumhead, and underneath it—so low I felt it in my back teeth rather than heard it—a groan, long and slow, like a ship working against its moorings in a swell.

"That's the ice moving," Ferro said.

"I know what moving ice sounds like," I said. "I've spent my life on water that freezes."

"You've spent your life on a river," he said gently, "which is a puddle with ambitions. This is different."

Wren, ahead of us with the lead mule, turned around and put her hands on her hips. She'd been doing more of that lately—standing still, facing us, taking up space like she'd only just discovered the ground would hold her weight. "Are you two going to stand there arguing about acoustics all morning, or are we going to look at it?"

So we looked at it.

* * *

The trail crested a shoulder of black rock and dropped away, and there it was.

The corridor ran between two walls of mountain so sheer they might have been cut with a knife, and filling the floor of it, from wall to wall, lay the glacier—a river of ice three hundred feet deep, gray-white on top where the rock dust had settled into it, but blue underneath, blue the way nothing on this earth is blue except deep water and deep grief. It ran for miles into the pass, curving out of sight around a spur, and above us the peaks held their snow like white hair combed over a skull.

And it was *moving*. You could see it if you watched long enough—the way you can see the hour hand of a clock move if you're stubborn about it—but mostly you could hear it. That groan again, deeper now, rolling up the valley floor and into our boots. Somewhere far up-corridor, something boomed, a single flat concussion like a door slammed by a giant, and the echo came back off the walls twice before it died.

"The calving season," Wren said, and her voice had gone quiet in a way I hadn't heard since Coldharbor. "My father wrote about it. In one of his letters. He said—" She stopped. Swallowed. Started again. "He said the mountain opens its mouth for three weeks a year, and everything you love has to go through while it's yawning."

Ferro took off his hat, which was not a habit of his, and stood bareheaded in the cold wind like a man entering a church. "Your father had a poet in him nobody gave him credit for."

"Everybody gave him credit for exactly the wrong things," Wren said, and started down the trail.

I stayed a moment longer at the overlook. I've run the Widow's Teeth in flood, and I've held a boat steady in a current that wanted to fold it in half, and I thought I understood what moving water could do when it decided to stop being scenery. But this was water that had forgotten it was water. This was a river with ten thousand tons of patience, and every few hours it remembered, briefly, catastrophically, what it used to be—and shrugged off a piece of itself the size of a courthouse.

The boom came again, closer. The rock under my feet shivered.

"All right," I told the glacier, quietly, because talking to things is a habit I pretend I don't have. "You open your mouth. We'll walk through it. But we keep our appointments short."

It didn't answer. Mountains never do. That's the whole trouble with them.

* * *

The descent to the ice took most of the afternoon, because the trail did that thing mountain trails do where every hundred feet of drop costs you four hundred feet of walking. By the time we reached the lateral moraine—the long gray spine of rubble riding the glacier's edge like a scar—we'd been on our feet eleven hours, and Ferro's fever, which had broken in the cave and stayed broken for two blessed days, was threatening to come back like a debt collector.

"We camp here tonight," I said, dropping my pack onto a flat shelf of stone. "Not on the ice. Ice at night is a liar—it looks solid and it's thinking."

"It's always thinking," Ferro said, sitting down harder than he meant to. "That's the whole problem with glaciers. They're patient and they hold grudges."

Wren got a fire going in a hollow behind the moraine, small and screened, because even here we weren't sure we were alone. Three days past the scree field, and none of us had quite stopped listening for Dain. He'd limped off across the ice field after the standoff on the moraine with a bullet through his shoulder and my best mule under him, and men like Corvus Dain don't die of shoulder wounds—they get *worse*. Somewhere behind us or beside us or ahead of us, he was making his own arrangements, and the thought of it sat in my stomach like a swallowed hook.

But that evening, for an hour, we let ourselves forget him.

Wren had found a tin of dried apples in the bottom of her pack, hoarded since Millbrook, and she divided them three ways with the solemnity of a woman distributing communion. Ferro produced a stub of pencil and, on the back of one of his old survey sheets, sketched the corridor from memory—the map in his head, which was better than any map on paper, because he'd walked this country twenty years ago when it was young and so was he.

"The route runs up the medial moraine," he said, tapping the pencil line. "The ridge of debris down the middle. Stay off the blue ice wherever you can—it's slicker than a lawyer's conscience and it hides crevasses under snow bridges. The moraine's slower going but it's honest. And there's a landmark about six miles up." His pencil paused. "A serac. A tower of ice left standing when the glacier calved around it, years back. Tomas Reyes called it the Steeple. It's on his map."

"It's on his map," Wren repeated softly.

Everything was on his map. I'd been carrying the thing against my ribs for weeks now, and every landmark Ferro named—the Steeple, the Black Ramp, the Squeeze—I could feel it confirming itself in the oilskin, like the map and the mountain were having a conversation and we were only allowed to listen.

"How far to the cache?" I asked.

Ferro didn't look up. "Past the Steeple, past the Blue Cathedral—that's a stretch where the ice is clean and the light comes through it like stained glass—then up where the glacier bends west against the south wall. Two days' hard walking if the weather holds. Three if it doesn't."

"And how long does the weather have?" I asked, because somebody had to.

He set the pencil down. That was answer enough, but he gave me words too, because Ferro had learned somewhere along this trail that I needed words, that silences made me invent worse things than the truth. "Calving season opened early this year. Meltwater's already running under the ice—you can hear it, that hollow sound, like a barrel with a loose stave. Every day it runs faster, it eats the bridges wider. We have maybe twelve days before the corridor turns into a shooting gallery. After that, nobody crosses until next summer."

"Twelve days," Wren said.

"Ten, if I'm honest," Ferro said. "I'm usually honest at night. It's daytime I lie."

I looked up the valley, where the glacier curved away into the blue shadow of the pass, and I did the arithmetic I'd been doing since the moraine: two days up, a day at the cache, three days down if Ferro's legs held and five if they didn't, weather margins, mule margins, luck margins. It fit. It fit the way a boat fits a lock—with inches to spare and no room for error, and everybody holding their breath.

"We leave at first light," I said. "We make distance while the ice is frozen hard in the morning cold. We're off it by noon, every day, camped on rock. Nobody argues with the schedule."

"Nobody argues with the schedule," Wren agreed, and there was something in her face—not fear exactly, and not excitement either, but the look of a woman standing at the edge of her father's whole life. Everything he'd lost was up that glacier. Everything he'd been accused of. Everything he'd crawled half a territory to give her.

She wanted it the way I'd once wanted invisibility: completely, and without knowing yet what it would cost.

* * *

I took first watch, because I always take first watch, because sleep comes easier to me at dawn than midnight and because watching the dark is a kind of work I'm good at. The fire burned down to a red eye behind its screen of stones. Ferro slept flat on his back with his breath whistling faintly through the healing hole in his side. Wren slept curled toward the fire like a comma, one hand open on the ground, and even asleep she looked like someone ready to grab something.

The glacier talked all night.

I don't know how else to put it. It groaned, it cracked, it boomed, and once—around the hour when the cold is deepest—it made a sound I will hear until I die: a long rising shriek, high and almost musical, like a violin bow drawn across the whole length of the valley, ending in a crack that rolled away eastward and came back changed. I sat with my rifle across my knees and listened to a river sing in a language older than names, and I thought about Tomas Reyes dying in my cabin with my hand around his, trading me the only thing he had left for a promise I hadn't wanted to make.

*You'll know when you see it,* he'd told me, delirious, gripping. *The silver was never the point. The point is what they buried with it.*

At the time I'd taken it for the ramblings of a man whose blood was leaving his brain. Sitting alone under the stars with a singing glacier in front of me, I wasn't so sure anymore. There was something in the way the map was drawn—those landmarks keyed to a riverwoman's eye, the depths and distances marked like soundings—that had always felt less like a treasure chart and more like testimony. Like a man writing down evidence in the only code he trusted: the language of water.

Tomorrow we'd start finding out.

* * *

First light came up the color of pewter, and the temperature fell instead of rose, which Ferro said was a gift—cold mornings meant hard ice and safe bridges. We broke camp fast and quiet, loaded the two remaining mules (Dain had cost us one, and I still grieved that animal more than I let show), and stepped down off the moraine onto the glacier itself.

The first step is the one I'll remember. My boot went through six inches of loose rock dust and then met the ice beneath, and the ice rang. Not creaked—*rang*, a deep bell-note that traveled out through the glacier in every direction, and a quarter mile up-corridor something answered it, another note, lower, and then the whole mass of it hummed for a moment like a struck string.

"What was that?" Wren whispered.

"The glacier saying good morning," Ferro said. "Or complaining about us. With ice it's hard to tell the difference."

We went up the medial moraine as planned, that long gray causeway of broken stone riding the glacier's spine, and it was strange country—rock piled on rock, some of it balanced in attitudes that defied sense, carried down from the peaks decades ago and riding the ice like passengers who didn't know the boat was moving. Between the ridges of rubble, the blue ice showed through, and where it was clean the light went *into* it and didn't come back right. I've seen green glass in church windows that was less alive.

By midmorning we could hear the meltwater properly for the first time—a rushing, gurgling presence under our feet, everywhere and nowhere, the glacier's blood running in its veins. And threaded through it, every twenty minutes or so, the booms. Closer each time. Up-valley, the walls of the pass narrowed, and somewhere in that narrowing the ice was letting go of itself in pieces the size of churches, and each calving sent a tremor up through our boot soles and sent the mules' ears flat back.

It was terrible. It was beautiful. I have run rivers my whole life and I have never felt so thoroughly, so *physically* aware of being a small warm temporary thing crossing the palm of something enormous and indifferent. And I confess—and I'd deny it under oath—that part of me loved it. Part of me has always loved the places that could kill me, because they're the only places honest enough to say what they think of you.

We made eight miles by noon and climbed off onto the eastern moraine with the sun high and the ice below us starting to sweat. From the camp ledge we could see up-corridor, and there it stood, six miles distant and impossibly clear in the thin air:

The Steeple. A tower of ice, two hundred feet of it, standing alone on the glacier's back where the flow had parted around it and calved away on both sides, leaving it stranded like the last pillar of a drowned cathedral. Even at that distance you could see it leaning, very slightly, very patiently, the way old trees lean before they fall.

"Tomas marked it with a cross and the word *halfway*," Wren said, reading from memory, her eyes bright. "Halfway. We're halfway."

"We're halfway to the halfway," Ferro said, easing himself down onto a rock with the care of a man whose body had become a negotiation. "Don't spend the word yet."

I was coiling the mules' leads when I saw it—or thought I saw it. Far up-corridor, near the base of the Steeple, a flicker. White against white, there and gone. A ptarmigan flushing, probably. Sunlight off a facet of ice. My eyes playing the tricks tired eyes play.

Except that Wren had gone still beside me, binoculars—Tomas's binoculars, salvaged from his kit—rising slowly to her face.

"Elodie," she said.

"Don't say it."

"There's smoke. Thin. Behind the Steeple, in its lee." She lowered the glasses. Her jaw was set the way mine goes set, and I recognized the expression because I wear it myself: the look of a woman deciding whether to tell the truth now or later, and knowing both choices cost. "Somebody's camped on the glacier. Somebody who knows how to build a fire the ice won't tattle on."

Behind the Steeple. On our route. Between us and everything Tomas Reyes had died carrying.

I looked at the tower of ice leaning over that hidden camp, and then at the meltwater shining in the crevasses, and I thought: *the mountain opens its mouth for three weeks.*

Maybe it would close it on somebody else first.

"Break's over," I said. "We make the Steeple by dark tomorrow—and we find out who's camping in my father-in-law's parish before they find out about us."

Wren blinked. "*Your what?*"

"Figure of speech," I said, and swung up the lead rope, and did not look back at either of their faces, because I had a reputation to maintain and a glacier to cross, and only ten days to do both.
