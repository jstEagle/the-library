## 25. Calving

I have spent my professional life measuring things—elevations, distances, the precise angle at which a mountain will forgive you and the slightly smaller angle at which it will not. I can tell you to the foot how far a man falls in three seconds. I can tell you that the ice beneath our boots on the morning of the fourth day of the crossing was moving, on average, four inches per day toward the valley floor, which sounds like nothing until you understand that four inches per day is the speed at which a glacier chews through everything you thought was permanent.

What I could not tell you—and this is the confession of a surveyor, whose entire trade is telling people where they are—is where we were.

The map had carried us true through the moraine and up the ice corridor's throat, past walls of blue so deep they made the sky look like a cheap imitation. Elodie led with the compass and Wren read the landmarks her father had inked twenty years ago—a split horn of rock, a serac shaped like a kneeling monk—and between the two of them they navigated better than I ever did with a transit and a government salary. But maps are arguments with time, and time was winning. The glacier had shifted since Tomas Reyes walked it. The crevasse he'd marked as passable on the east flank had widened into a gulf you could have dropped my old reputation into without hearing it land.

We were roped together, forty feet apart, which meant that whatever happened to one of us happened, in some degree, to all of us. I was in the middle position, which Elodie assigned me not out of courtesy but arithmetic: I was the weakest link, and the middle of a rope is where the weak link does the least damage.

"You're sulking," Wren said, catching up to me as we crossed a snow bridge that groaned like a ship's hull.

"I'm conserving energy."

"You're sulking *economically*." She grinned at me, and I confess the girl had a way of grinning that made even the glacier seem less hostile. She'd taken to carrying her father's prospecting pick slung across her back like a sword, and she wore it better than most men wear swords.

Elodie held up a fist. We stopped. Ahead, the corridor opened into a broad basin of ice, and beyond the basin—the far wall, the exit, the whole reason for this madness—was perhaps two miles distant. Two miles of glacier standing between us and the descent, and the descent between us and everything that mattered.

"Listen," Elodie said.

I listened. At first there was only wind and the small wet sounds of meltwater running somewhere below us, inside the ice, in its dark veins. Then I heard what she heard: a deep, rhythmic knocking, like a fist against a door. Like many fists against many doors.

"The calving season," I said. "It's starting."

"It started," Elodie said. "That's the face of the glacier up-ice. When the meltwater gets under the terminus—" She didn't finish. She didn't need to. A glacier in calving season sheds its edges the way a dog sheds in summer, except the fur is house-sized and comes down at the speed of gravity.

We crossed the basin quickly after that, or as quickly as rope and prudence allowed. The light was doing something extraordinary—I remember that even with everything that came after, I remember the light. The sun had climbed high enough to pour down into the corridor, and the ice drank it and turned it blue, every shade from the palest winter morning to a depth of cobalt that seemed less like color than like temperature. Walking through it felt like walking through the inside of a bell that hadn't been rung yet.

Then the glacier rang itself.

The sound arrived before the understanding of it—a crack, then a roar, then a roar behind the roar, the sound of several thousand tons of ice letting go of the face we'd left behind us. I turned in time to see it: a serac the size of a chapel shearing off the upper glacier and falling, rotating slowly, almost gently, almost daintily, until it struck the lower ice and exploded into a white thunderhead that rolled across the basin toward us.

"Run!" Elodie shouted, and we ran, all pretense of surveyor's pace abandoned, three figures on a rope stumbling through blue light while the world came apart behind us.

The blast of displaced air caught us before the debris did. It picked Wren off her feet entirely; I saw her go airborne, saw the rope snap taut, and then the rope hauled me backward off my own feet and I went down hard on the ice, sliding, my bad lungs filling with cold, and Elodie—small, wiry, immovable Elodie—had thrown herself flat and wrapped the rope twice around her forearm and became an anchor, the way she must once have anchored a riverboat against a current that wanted her dead.

The powder cloud washed over us like a wave over a breakwater. For perhaps ten seconds I existed inside a white scream, unable to see my own hands, breathing ice crystals, certain with a surveyor's calm certainty that this was the manner of my death and noting, distantly, that it was at least geologically interesting.

Then the cloud thinned, and settled, and the world reassembled itself around three prone figures coughing in the blue silence.

"Everyone attached?" Elodie's voice, hoarse.

Wren, thirty feet up-rope, raised a mittened hand. "Attached. Furious. Attached."

I tried to say something witty about near-death experiences and found I couldn't spare the breath for it. That was new. That was the fever, or what remained of it, banking its fires low but never quite out. Four days in a cave with Wren Voss feeding me melted snow and opinions had kept me alive; it had not made me well.

Elodie got us up and got us moving, and it was another hour before we understood what the calving had actually cost us.

The route bent around a shoulder of ice toward the exit ramp Tomas's map described—a long, shallow slope where the glacier abutted the rock of the mountainside, the one stretch of the crossing where a man didn't need to trust the ice under him because the stone was right there beside him. Except the calving had changed the geometry. Where the map showed a continuous ramp, the fall of the great serac had shattered the surface, and the shattering had opened the earth.

Or opened the ice, rather. The crevasse ran across our path like a sentence neither of us wanted to read to the end. It was thirty feet wide at its narrowest, and it did not end where we could see. It ran off into the blue haze in both directions, a crack in the world, and down inside it—when Wren lay flat and peered over the lip with the fearless stupidity of the young—we could hear water. Meltwater, moving fast, at the bottom of a shaft that went down farther than my eye wanted to follow.

"That wasn't there," Wren said. "Dad's notes say the ramp was solid. He wrote 'solid as a church floor.'"

"Glaciers don't keep churches," Elodie said. "They keep appointments." She was already pacing along the lip, reading the ice the way she read water, looking for the seam, the flaw, the place where the argument could be won. "There's a bridge. There's always a bridge. Snow bridges form over these things every spring. Find me one that hasn't rotted through."

We found one. Of course we found one—it was the only one, which in the grammar of mountains means it was the one we were going to use regardless of its condition. It spanned the crevasse maybe eighty yards down-glacier: an arch of packed snow, perhaps six feet wide, sagging in the middle like an old hammock. Beneath its skin we could see the darker shadow of hollow space. Above us, the sun climbed. The sun was the enemy now, more than Dain, more than the Harrows, more than the debt I owed to my own conscience. Every degree of warmth was meltwater finding the bridge's bones and thinning them.

"We cross one at a time," Elodie said. "Rope stays on. If it goes, the other two anchor and pray."

"And if the anchoring fails?" Wren asked.

"Then we all learn something about grief," Elodie said, "in alphabetical order."

She went first, naturally. She crossed with the light, quick steps of a woman who had spent her life trusting surfaces that lied—river ice, deck plating, other people's promises. Halfway across the bridge flexed and gave a soft whump, and I felt my heart stop like a watch dropped in water, but she flowed over the worst of the sag and stood on the far side, waving us on with a hand that betrayed no tremor at all.

"Wren next."

"No." Wren planted herself. "Ferro goes next. I go last. If the bridge holds for him it'll hold for me, and if it doesn't hold for him—" she met my eyes, and there was no mercy in them, only honesty, which is sometimes crueler, "—then I'd rather be on this side holding the rope when it happens. You're lighter than me. It's math."

It was math. It was also the girl deciding, with seventeen years of certainty, that she was willing to watch me die rather than die uselessly behind me, and I found I had no argument that could stand against it. So I went.

I will describe the crossing honestly, because I have spent too much of my life describing things dishonestly to waste the habit now that it matters. The bridge was soft underfoot. Not rotten-soft—not yet—but yielding, the way a plank yields when the nails have begun to whisper their resignations. Each step compressed the snow with a sound like a slow exhalation. Halfway across, directly over the deepest part of the gap, the surface sank two inches under my boot and stayed sunk, and through the soles of my boots I felt the bridge's answer travel up my legs: *I am thinner than I look. I am thinner every minute.*

Below me, through a seam in the snow, I could see it: the blue dark going down and down, and at the bottom of it a thread of white water running, hurrying, indifferent. Somewhere in that dark were the bones of winters. I thought, absurdly, of the false report I had signed in a warm office in Millbrook, the one that ruined Tomas Reyes, and how easy it had been—how *light*, how weightless, like stepping onto a bridge that looks solid. Everything costs. The ice was teaching me the exchange rate.

My breath was short. My legs, which had carried me through surveying seasons across three ranges, were trembling by the tenth step, and I knew—professionally, precisely—that the tremor was not fear. Fear I could budget for. This was the fever's residue, the cave's four days, the body presenting its invoice. Twenty feet remained. Fifteen. The bridge sighed under me. Ten.

"Ferro." Wren's voice, tight. "You've stopped walking."

"I'm admiring the view."

"Walk, you ridiculous old man."

I walked. Five steps, four, three—and on the third step from the edge the bridge spoke again, louder, a cracking report that I felt through my knees, and something behind me *shifted*, and I threw myself forward the last distance with none of Elodie's grace and all of a drowning man's conviction, and my hands hit solid snow on the far side, and I lay there gasping like a landed fish while Wren crossed behind me at a dead run, light as a bird, because youth is its own engineering.

She landed beside me. Elodie cut the tension in the rope and looked at the bridge, and we all looked at the bridge, and the bridge looked back at us with its sagging middle and its thinning bones.

"Can it hold again?" Wren asked. "For the return trip?"

Nobody answered. We all knew the answer. The return trip would be a different question asked of a different bridge, if any bridge existed at all, and by then the sun would have had another day to work its patient arithmetic.

* * *

We should have kept moving. I want that recorded plainly: we should have kept moving, and the fact that we did not was my fault, because ninety minutes later my legs staged their full rebellion and I sat down on the ice at the base of the exit ramp and informed my colleagues, with what dignity I could salvage, that I required a rest of indeterminate length.

Elodie crouched in front of me and studied my face with those river-gray eyes that missed nothing and forgave selectively. "How bad?"

"Tired," I said.

"Liar," she said, without heat. "Try again."

"Bad," I amended. "Not dying-bad. Climbing-bad. There's a difference and I'm the only one here qualified to distinguish."

She dug in the pack and put a strip of dried meat in my hand and a look on her face that said *eat or I'll feed you by force*. Wren scrambled up the ramp ahead of us to scout, her father's pick glinting on her back, and for a moment it was just the two of us in the enormous blue quiet, and Elodie Voss said something I did not expect.

"When you can't go on," she said, "you say so. No heroics. I've buried enough brave men to know they're heavy."

"That may be the least romantic thing anyone has ever said to me."

"I'm not trying to romance you, Augustin. I'm trying to get you off this ice." She stood, offered her hand, and pulled me upright with a strength that always surprised people who hadn't watched her handle a boat. "Ten more minutes' rest. Then the ramp, then the descent, then trees, then a doctor who'll bill us criminally. In that order."

But the glacier had one more appointment to keep.

We were halfway up the ramp when the sound came again—the deep knocking, closer now, urgent—and we turned as a unit, three heads swiveling like compass needles, and looked back down the corridor we had crossed.

The far face of the glacier was going. All of it. Not a serac this time but a whole section of the terminus, hundreds of feet of ice front surrendering at once, and the sound was not a roar anymore but something older, tectonic, the voice of the mountain range clearing its throat. The wave of it rolled up the corridor and broke over us as wind and powder, and when it cleared, the light had changed—the sun came through a new gap in the corridor walls at a new angle, and the whole blue cathedral of the ice lit up from within like a lantern.

And below us, where the basin had been, the glacier's surface had *moved*. The shockwave had done its work. We could see it from the ramp: cracks radiating outward from the collapse zone, and one of them—a fresh, raw-lipped fissure—running straight toward the base of our ramp, widening as we watched, eating the distance between itself and the route down.

Elodie measured it the way she measured everything, in seconds. "When that crack reaches the ramp, the ramp goes into the crevasse field. After that, the only way down is the icefall on the north flank, and nobody survives the icefall."

"How long?" Wren asked.

"At the rate it's moving? An hour. Maybe less." Elodie turned to me, and I saw her do the thing she did—weigh a man's weakness against a mountain's patience, and hate both sides of the scale. "Augustin. Can you make the descent in an hour?"

I looked down the ramp, two miles of broken ice glittering in the new light, and then at the crack crawling toward us with the unhurried confidence of a thing that has never once been late. My legs answered before I could: a fine, honest tremor, the body's truthful testimony.

I thought of a false signature on a government form. I thought of a cave and a girl reading to me through the night. I thought of the word *penance* and how cheap it sounded in parlors and how expensive it felt at eight thousand feet.

"Yes," I said. And because I am a surveyor, and a liar, and lately trying to be neither, I added the truth: "Ask me again in half a mile."

Elodie took the lead. Wren took my elbow, gently, without comment, the way you steady something you intend to keep. Behind us the glacier knocked at its own door, and the crack ran on toward the ramp, and we went down into the blue light as fast as three roped lives could carry us—while somewhere below, faint but unmistakable, carried up the corridor on the wind, came a sound that stopped all three of us mid-stride.

Voices. Human voices, shouting orders, down-ice, between us and the trees.

Dain had not waited for the high route after all.
