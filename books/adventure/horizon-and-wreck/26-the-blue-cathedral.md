## 26. The Blue Cathedral

I want to say I noticed the light first, but that would be a lie, and my father raised me—briefly, and badly, but sincerely—to be honest about the things that mattered.

What I noticed first was the cold. Not the ordinary cold of the trail, which is a thing you argue with, a thing you can bully with movement and wool and stubbornness. This was a different animal entirely. The moment we stepped through the gap in the ice wall—a gap no wider than a barn door, breathing out air so still it felt held—it dropped ten degrees and went quiet in a way that made my ears ring. Elodie went in first, because she always did, her knife out for no reason she could have defended. Ferro followed, leaning on his mule's lead rope like it was the last honest railing in the world.

I went in last, because somebody had to watch our backs, and because I had learned on this mountain that going last was its own kind of prayer.

Then I looked up, and honesty took over whether I wanted it or not.

The corridor opened above us into a vault of blue. Not the blue of sky, which is thin and apologetic, and not the blue of river water, which is really just a mirror wearing a costume. This was blue the way iron is heavy—as a fundamental property, baked into the substance of the thing. Sunlight came down through forty feet of ancient glacier and arrived at us changed: filtered, deepened, patient. It fell in long slanted columns where the ice had melted and refrozen in ridges, and it pooled on the floor in bright coins, and every sound we made—the crunch of crampons, Ferro's ragged breathing, the creak of the mule's harness—came back to us from the walls with a kind of reverence, as if the cathedral itself were considering each noise before returning it.

"Well," said Ferro, who had surveyed half these ranges before anyone thought to ruin him, and who therefore had no business sounding surprised. He sounded surprised anyway. "Well."

"That's two wells," Elodie said. "Anybody want to try for words?"

I didn't. I stood there with snow crusted in my eyelashes and my father's compass warm in my pocket—heavier than any compass has a right to be, the way a promise is heavier than paper—and I let the blue have me.

Here is what nobody tells you about grief: it is loud. It talks all night. It has opinions about everything you do and commentary on everything you fail to do. My father had been dead for months by then, and I had carried his death up a mountain inside me like a coal I couldn't put down and couldn't hold, and it had talked the whole way. *You didn't know him. You didn't look hard enough when he was alive. You're doing this for silver and calling it love.* On the moraine, after the rockslide, alone under a shelf of stone with nothing but his old tricks to keep me breathing, the voice had been loudest of all.

But inside the ice, the voice stopped. Or maybe the ice just answered it. Because standing there in that impossible light, I understood something I hadn't been able to understand anywhere else: my father had *seen* this. Tomas Reyes, disgraced, accused, run out of Coldharbor with his name dragged through the mud of a claim he'd never stolen—he had walked into this cathedral with whatever companions he had, and he had looked up, and this exact blue had fallen into his eyes. And he had come out the other side and spent years building a map so precise, so lovingly keyed to landmarks only a riverwoman could read, that reading it felt like hearing him talk.

He hadn't mapped a silver vein. He'd mapped the way home.

"Your face," Elodie said to me, "is doing something embarrassing."

"So is yours."

"My face does not do things. My face is a professional instrument." But she was smiling when she said it—that crooked, reluctant smile she rationed out like hardtack—and in the blue light it made her look ten years younger and about forty pounds lighter, as if the debt she carried were a physical thing the glacier had agreed to hold for a while.

* * *

We made camp there, which Ferro called madness and then, twenty minutes later, called obvious. The corridor narrowed ahead—we could see it pinching toward a bend maybe half a mile on—and behind us the entrance gap had already begun to weep meltwater in long glassy threads. Calving season was a door swinging open, and doors swing shut. We had perhaps three hours of good light left, and the ice floor was the first flat, dry-ish ground we'd seen since the moraine, and Ferro's fever had broken in the night but had left him walking like a man negotiating with his own legs.

So we camped in a cathedral, which is the sort of sentence I intend to say at every opportunity for the rest of my life.

Elodie built a ring of stones and burned our little alcohol stove inside it, low flame, because fire inside a glacier is either warmth or suicide depending entirely on your discipline. She cooked the last of the decent tea—she'd been hoarding it since Coldharbor, doling it out in thimbles, and now she made three full cups without being asked, which in Elodie Voss's private currency was roughly equivalent to a sonnet. Ferro sat against the mule's pack with his map case open on his knees, though he wasn't reading it. He was just looking at it, the way you look at a letter from someone you wronged.

I should explain what the last week had done to us, because it matters to what happened next.

Ferro had confessed—really confessed, kneeling-in-the-snow confessed—that he was the surveyor who signed the false report that destroyed my father. That the Harrows paid him, and that he'd told himself a hundred comfortable lies about it, and that every one of those lies had a man's name at the bottom written in his handwriting. He'd offered it to me like a man offering his own head. And I had taken it, not gracefully—I'm seventeen, grace is a few years off yet—but I'd taken it, because hating him had turned out to be like drinking seawater: it filled me up and helped nothing.

And Elodie had told us about her debt. All of it. The smuggling run, the man she'd loved who captained it, the way he'd stepped off the boat in Vellport and let her take the fall, and the five years since, running the Kestrel through Harrow waters with her name kept small on purpose, invisible, unclaimed. She'd told it flat and fast, the way you pull a splinter, and then she'd gone and checked the moorings like nothing had happened, and Ferro and I had looked at each other across the fire and understood that we were now three people who knew the worst about each other and had stayed anyway.

There's a word for that. I've been looking for it since the cave. *Family* is too big and too small at once. Maybe the word doesn't exist yet and someone has to invent it, and maybe that's a job for people with more sleep.

* * *

The laughing started, as these things do, over something stupid.

Ferro had produced, from some inner pocket he guarded like scripture, a stub of pencil and the corner of a survey sheet, and he announced—with the gravity of a man presenting credentials to a king—that he intended to formally document the interior of the glacier, because "no one has ever surveyed this corridor, and if we die in here, I refuse to be a blank space on someone else's map."

"You are the only person alive who would rather die annotated," Elodie said.

"Annotation is immortality, Captain. Ask any dead man with a named pass."

"You named a pass after yourself?"

"I named a *saddle* after myself, and it was struck from the record during the disgrace, thank you for reopening that wound."

He began sketching, tongue between his teeth, and I watched over his shoulder as his pencil moved—quick, sure, beautiful. Whatever else Augustin Ferro was, whatever he'd sold and what it cost, the man could draw country the way other men sing. He got the vault of the ceiling in four lines. He got the light columns in six. Then he paused, frowned at the page, and added a small stick figure with a braid, labeled *WREN, TAKING UP SPACE.*

"That's libel," I said.

"That's cartography. Maps record what's true."

"I am a delicate and economical presence."

"You snore," Elodie said, without looking up from the stove. "Like a landslide. Small rocks first, then the whole slope goes."

"I do not snore."

"Ferro?"

"Oh, catastrophically," Ferro said. "I timed it in the cave. Four days. I have data."

"You *timed* it?"

"Somebody had to stay awake listening for Dain, and your snoring provided an excellent baseline against which all other noises could be measured as non-threatening. A bear could have walked into that cave and I'd have said, 'No, that's just Wren with a cold.'"

And I laughed.

It came up out of me before I could decide anything about it—a real laugh, ugly and loud, the kind that doubles you over, and it echoed off the blue walls and came back multiplied, so that the whole cathedral seemed to be laughing with us, forty feet of ancient ice chuckling like a grandmother. And Elodie laughed too—started with a snort she tried to disguise as a cough, failed, gave up—and Ferro laughed until he had to press a hand to his ribs where the healing was still tender, wincing and laughing at once, which is possibly the most human sound I have ever heard.

I want to be careful with this part, because it would be easy to make it small. Three tired people giggling over tea in a hole in a glacier. But I had not laughed—not like that, not with my whole chest—since before the telegraph office in Coldharbor, since before the overheard conversation that sent me running, since before I learned my father was alive and then, weeks later, that he wasn't. Grief had been sitting on me like a wet coat for the better part of a year, and somewhere between the fourth cup of hoarded tea and Ferro's slanderous stick figure, the coat came off, and the blue light got in, and for maybe ten minutes I was just a girl in an impossible place with two people who knew the worst about her and had stayed.

That's joy earned. It doesn't feel like fireworks. It feels like setting down weight.

* * *

Later, when the light columns had gone long and amber and the temperature outside the walls began its evening plunge, Elodie did something I keep turning over like a stone in my pocket.

She took out the oilskin packet. My father's map. She'd carried it against her skin since Millbrook—I'd seen the shape of it under her shirt often enough to stop wondering—and she unfolded it now on her knee, there in the stove-light and the dying blue, and smoothed it flat.

"Come here," she said.

I came. Ferro leaned in too, and for a while none of us spoke. The map was my father's hand all over it—his cramped, stubborn capitals, his little marginal notes, *beware cornice after warm wind*, *water sings here, listen*. Landmarks keyed to rivers and scree and the habits of meltwater, because he'd known, somehow, that the person carrying it would think in water.

"He drew this in the dark years," Elodie said quietly. "After Coldharbor. After everything. Look at the margins—every note is a warning, except this one." Her finger stopped at the top edge, where in tiny letters my father had written: *FOR WREN. SHE'LL KNOW THE WAY.*

"I don't know the way," I said. My voice cracked on it. "I've been following all trip."

"You knew the tricks on the scree. Nobody taught you those."

"He did. When I was little. I thought they were games."

"They were games," Ferro said. "They were also a will. He was leaving you everything he had, the only way he could, without saying the word inheritance out loud where the wrong ears might hear it." He tapped the margin note gently. "This line isn't directions, girl. It's a bet. He bet you'd come find him, someday, even if it took him dying to point you uphill."

I looked at the note for a long time. *SHE'LL KNOW THE WAY.*

"Okay," I said finally, and it came out steadier than I expected. "Okay. Then I want it in the record—" I nodded at Ferro's survey sheet, at my stick figure with the braid— "that when we get to the cache, I read the landmarks. Me. He wrote it for me, and I'm done letting other people carry him."

Elodie looked at me for a moment, and then she folded the map along its old creases and handed it to me. The whole thing. Out of her shirt, out of her keeping, into my hands.

"You'll give it back when we're through the ice," I said.

"No," she said. "I won't. It was never mine. I was just the ferry." She almost smiled. "Turns out I deliver things. It's the one skill I've got."

Ferro wrote something on the survey sheet. I didn't see what. Years later he showed me: beneath the stick figure with the braid, he'd added a second label, smaller, in his careful surveyor's hand. *HOLDER OF RECORD.*

* * *

We slept in shifts, because discipline is the rent you pay for camping in a cathedral. I had the middle watch, alone with the blue gone to black-blue and the stars invisible beyond forty feet of ice, and I sat by the banked stove and listened to the glacier work. It groans at night. Deep, slow groans, miles long, the sound of a living thing shifting in its sleep, and underneath them a finer music—trickles, ticks, the pop of air bubbles released from their thousand-year imprisonment. My father had heard all of it. Somewhere in that sound, I thought, was the reason he'd come back. Not the silver. Silver you can find anywhere if you're willing to be cruel enough. But this—this room of blue silence where a person could hear themselves think for the first time in years—this you could build a life around.

I woke Elodie for her watch, and instead of lying down, I sat beside her a while.

"What happens at the far side?" I asked.

She was quiet long enough that I thought she wouldn't answer. "Depends on how much Dain knows about calving season," she said at last. "If he's smart—and he is, don't let the cheap coat fool you—he's not waiting at the exit. He's waiting below it, where the ground stops trying to kill him. Which means we come out of the ice rested and rich and straight into the only flat ground for thirty miles." She fed a twig to the stove. "But that's tomorrow's weather."

"And today's?"

"Today's," she said, "is the best day I've had in five years. Don't tell anyone. I have a reputation."

"Your reputation is safe. Nobody believes anything a Voss says."

Something crossed her face at that—my name for her, casual, tossed off—and she looked at me with an expression I couldn't read, somewhere between pain and gratitude, and said, "No. I suppose they don't."

I slept after that, deeply, without the coal of grief talking, and dreamed of nothing at all, which is the finest luxury I know.

* * *

Morning came gray-green through the ice, and we broke camp fast, because the entrance threads behind us had thickened overnight from strings to ropes of meltwater, and Ferro pronounced the door "closing on schedule, which is to say, too soon." We moved up the corridor in single file, me in front with the map open in my mittened hands, reading my father's landmarks against the living ice: the twin pillars he'd marked, still standing; the pressure ridge shaped, he claimed, like a sleeping dog, which Ferro disputed and I defended on grounds of filial loyalty.

The corridor bent. The blue deepened. And around the bend, the light changed—went white and hard and horizontal—and I heard Elodie suck in a breath behind me, and Ferro say, softly, "Ah," in the voice of a man seeing an old enemy.

The exit stood fifty yards ahead: a tall gash of daylight opening onto a slope of dirty snow running down toward the treeless flats. Open sky. Open ground. The end of the cathedral.

And planted in the snow outside, small with distance but unmistakable, a line of dark figures, motionless, waiting for the light to bring us to them.

Seven, I counted. Maybe eight.

Beside me, very quietly, Elodie began taking off her mittens.
