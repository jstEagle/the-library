## 27. Ambush at the Far Side

There is a particular quality of silence that lives at the mouth of a glacier. It isn't the absence of sound—it's the presence of something waiting. The ice groans and ticks and settles like an old house at night, and underneath all of it there's a held breath, as if the mountain itself knows what's coming and has decided not to warn you.

I'd been listening to that silence for two days when I finally admitted what it was telling me.

We came down out of the blue cathedral in the late afternoon, Wren first, then Ferro leaning on my shoulder more than either of us pretended, then me with the mule rope wrapped twice around my fist. The glacier spilled us out onto a gravel fan where three valleys met, and the light came slantwise through the pass behind us, gold and long, the kind of light that makes everything look kinder than it is.

The trail ahead ran along a river of gray meltwater, braided and shallow, easy walking for maybe four miles before it dropped into the treeline. Four miles of easy walking. I remember thinking that was the first mercy this mountain had shown us.

I remember thinking that, and then I stopped thinking anything at all for a moment, because I saw the cairn.

It was wrong. That's all I can tell you. A cairn on this stretch of trail should be waist-high and casual, stones stacked by passing prospectors with nothing better to do with their hands. This one was chest-high and deliberate, and the stones were too clean—quarried-looking, fitted, the kind of stacking a man does when he wants cover and doesn't care who knows it. Nobody builds a wall in the wilderness unless they expect to be shot at from behind it.

"Ferro," I said quietly. "How's your eyes?"

"Better than my legs." He followed my chin. I watched him go still in that particular way surveyors go still—not fear exactly, but the professional recognition of a bad grade. "That's not natural placement. Someone's been moving rock."

"How long?"

He studied the shadows along the cairn's base, the way the gravel had scoured against the downstream side. "A day. Maybe two."

Two days. Which meant they hadn't come up behind us through the ice. They'd come around—the low route, the long way, through the pass we'd been denied by the storm. While we were crawling across a crevasse field praying to whatever god manages glaciers, Corvus Dain had been strolling a groomed trail with dry boots and time to spare.

"They're ahead of us," Wren said. She said it flatly, the way she'd said *the ice is rotten* two days earlier, and I felt the same cold slide down my spine, because she was right both times and being right out here had a way of costing people.

I counted what I could see: the cairn, and beyond it a fold in the gravel bar where the ground dipped just enough to hide a kneeling man. Probably more in the boulder field to the east. Dain didn't travel with less than six if he could help it; he considered numbers a form of manners.

Six against three, and one of my three could barely stand.

"We go back," Ferro said. "Through the corridor. Wait them out."

"The corridor closes at dark. You know what calving sounds like at night—you want to be inside the cathedral when it starts?" I shook my head before he could argue. "And even if we lived, we'd come out here eventually. He'd just wait longer. Men like Dain are patient the way rivers are patient. They don't mind the wait because the water always comes down."

Wren had gone quiet in the way she went quiet when she was working a problem her father had left rattling around in her head. Her eyes moved along the riverbank, the gravel bars, the boulder field, reading it the way Ferro read grades and I read channels. Then she said, "There's only one place they can let us pass without shooting each other at forty feet. The narrows, where the trail pinches between the water and that rock face. If they wanted us dead, they'd have already fired."

She was right, and I hated how right she was, because it meant Dain wanted something from us alive. And there was only one thing left we carried that he wanted.

I looked back the way we'd come. The glacier's mouth stood open behind us, blue and ancient and utterly indifferent, thundering softly to itself like a sleeping thing. Ahead, the trap. Behind, the ice. And somewhere in my coat, wrapped in oilskin against my ribs, a dead man's map that had cost everyone who touched it something they couldn't get back.

"Well," I said, "I've never once gone around a sandbar when I could run it instead. Let's meet him."

* * *

We walked down that trail the way you walk to your own funeral—slowly, hands visible, every step placed like a word you can't take back. I put Ferro in the middle with the mules, mostly because he'd have argued his way to the front otherwise and he couldn't outrun a determined snail. Wren walked beside me. She'd taken the pistol from the pack without asking, and I decided that today was not the day to teach her about asking.

The narrows came up on us the way narrows do—all at once, no ceremony. The rock face rose gray and sheer on our left, the meltwater chattered shallow and bright on our right, and between them maybe thirty feet of trail, and standing in the middle of those thirty feet with his hands clasped behind his back like a man admiring scenery, was Corvus Dain.

He looked exactly as I remembered him, which felt almost insulting. The mountain should mark a man. It marked Ferro twenty pounds lighter and grayer at the temples; it marked Wren with a burn scar along her forearm she didn't talk about. But Dain stood there in his good coat with his boots clean, and I understood suddenly that he'd never once been cold in his life. Cold was for other people. Cold was the weather he sent men into.

"Captain Voss." His voice carried easily in the narrow air. "You look terrible."

"You should see the other glacier."

Something flickered at the corners of his mouth—not quite a smile. Dain rationed his smiles the way misers rationed coin, and I'd never seen him spend a whole one. "Where are the rest of my men?" I asked.

"Resting. Eating hot food. Drinking coffee I paid for." He gestured vaguely eastward, toward the boulders, and I caught the glint of a rifle barrel in the shadow between two rocks, and another further up the talus slope, and I stopped counting after that because counting only tells you how much trouble you're in and I already knew. "I don't waste men on women who walk toward me with their hands empty. It's poor hospitality."

"Your hospitality's noted. Now say what you came to say, because the light's going and I don't camp in narrows."

"Direct. Good. I've always respected that about you." He took a step closer, boots crunching on the gravel, and lowered his voice as though the mountains might repeat him. "Here is the arithmetic, Captain. You owe the Syndicate eleven hundred dollars. Your boat is collateral. Your routes are collateral. Everything you've spent five years building by staying small and quiet and invisible—that's all collateral, and I hold the paper. Against that, you carry a piece of oiled paper worth—" he paused, savoring it, "—more than your boat, your routes, and your life multiplied together. I'm offering a trade that any sane woman would make before I finished the sentence."

"And yet you keep talking."

"Because you're not sane, Captain Voss. That's the interesting part." He tilted his head, studying me the way he probably studied ledgers. "You had every chance to sell that map. Millbrook. Coldharbor. Any dock between here and the coast. Instead you hired a broken surveyor and dragged a grieving girl up a glacier in calving season. That's not greed. Greed I understand. Greed I can price." His eyes narrowed. "So tell me what you think you're carrying, and I'll tell you whether it's worth what you're paying."

For a moment I almost told him the truth—that I didn't fully know, that Tomas Reyes had pressed the packet into my hands with blood on his lips and a daughter's name in his mouth, and that I'd been carrying it ever since like a stone I couldn't put down because putting it down would mean admitting what it weighed. But telling Corvus Dain the truth is like showing a card sharp your hand because he asked nicely.

"I'm carrying a promise," I said. "You wouldn't recognize the currency."

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "But I recognize leverage. And here's mine." He turned his head slightly, toward the boulder field, and raised his voice just enough. "Bring her out."

My heart did something ugly in my chest. My hand found the pistol grip before my brain caught up—and then the boulders gave up their secret, and it wasn't a hostage at all.

It was a mule.

Dain's man led her out by the halter, and I knew her instantly—the dun with the white sock, the one Dain's rider had cut from our string on the moraine ten days ago, the one carrying half our food and most of our ammunition. She looked thin and sullen and entirely unrepentant, which is the natural condition of mules.

"She wandered into our camp on the low route," Dain said. "Along with her load. Very considerate animal." He walked over and lifted the corner of the pack canvas so I could see what was left: hardtack gone to crumbs, a tin of tea, and—my stomach dropped—a bundle of papers that I recognized as Ferro's field notes. Every measurement he'd taken since Millbrook. Every notation translating Tomas's landmarks into survey coordinates.

"You see the problem," Dain said mildly. "Even if I never find your map, Captain—even if you burned it tomorrow—I have enough here to reconstruct the route. My surveyors are better than yours. So the question isn't whether the Harrow Syndicate reaches that silver. The question is whether you and the girl are alive when we do." He let that sit in the cold air a moment. "Eleven hundred dollars against your life and hers. I've watched you do harder math."

Behind me, I heard Ferro shift his weight, and heard Wren breathe once, slow and deliberate, the way she breathed before she did something reckless.

And I'll be honest with you, because there's no one left to lie to: for about three seconds, I did the math. Eleven hundred dollars. Five years of running cash payments downriver and flinching at every Harrow flag on every toll boat. Five years of being nobody, of answering to *captain* and nothing else, of keeping my last name folded away like contraband. All of it, gone, in exchange for a scrap of oilskin I'd promised a dying man I'd deliver. The deal was clean. The deal was *sane*. Dain had built his whole life on finding the sane angle in other people's desperation, and he'd found mine, and he was standing in it, smiling his rationed smile.

Then Wren spoke.

"He shot my father."

Her voice wasn't loud. That was the terrible part. It was quiet and clear and it went through the narrows like the meltwater goes through the gravel—finding every gap, filling everything.

"Tomas Reyes," she said, stepping forward beside me, "was shot twice in the back and left in the reeds of the Vell River to drown. I've spent a month learning who my father was, Mr. Dain, and every road leads back to men like you. So you can keep your arithmetic." She lifted her chin, and I saw Tomas in her face so plainly it hurt. "We're not selling."

Dain looked at her for a long moment. Something moved behind his eyes—not remorse; I don't believe he owned any—but recalculation. He'd priced me correctly and priced her wrong, and men like him hate discovering an unpriced variable.

"That's unfortunate," he said.

"It really is," I agreed, and drew.

I got my pistol out and up and fired past his shoulder into the cairn behind him, not because I missed but because I wanted the echo—I wanted the whole narrows to understand that the negotiation was over. The crack of it rolled up the rock face and came back doubled, and under it I heard the boulder field answer with rifle fire, three shots, wild, pocking the gravel at our feet.

"Run!" I shoved Wren toward the rock face, grabbed Ferro's collar, and the world dissolved into noise and gravel dust and the flat bright panic of being shot at on open ground.

We made the talus slope in maybe eight seconds, which felt like eight years. Bullets whined off stone above us. Ferro stumbled and I hauled him and swore and hauled again. Behind us Dain was shouting orders—not firing himself, I noticed; Dain never dirtied his own hands—and the dun mule screamed somewhere and I felt a stab of pure stupid grief for a stubborn animal that had never done anything worse than eat our supplies.

We scrambled up through the boulders as the light died, climbing when we should have been thinking, until the gunfire fell behind us and the only sound was our own breathing and the distant, patient thunder of the glacier.

I put us against a rock shelf with our backs to stone and counted heads. Wren, breathing hard, pistol still in her fist, knuckles white. Ferro, gray-faced, sliding down the rock to sit. Me, with a graze along my forearm I hadn't even felt happen, blood making a warm stripe down to my wrist.

Below us, lanterns were lighting up in the narrows. Six or seven points of flame, settling in for the night, arranged in a neat arc across the only way down.

"There's no route around them," Ferro said hoarsely. He'd gotten his map case out and was squinting at it by the last violet light. "The valley walls close up ahead. There's the river gorge, but no one fords that in the dark."

"Then we don't go around," Wren said.

I looked at her. She was watching the lanterns with an expression I recognized from the glacier—from the crevasse field, from the calving, from every moment on this mountain when the sensible thing was impossible and she'd reached for her father's voice instead. She was watching the lanterns the way a prospector watches a stream bed.

"Dain thinks we're trapped because he's thinking like a man on a trail," she said. "But we're not on a trail. We're on my father's mountain." She turned to me, and in the dying light her eyes were bright and absolutely certain. "Elodie. How do you feel about avalanches?"

Below us, the lanterns burned in their patient arc, and above us the snowfields of the high cirque hung silent in the dark, loaded and waiting, and I understood that the next move belonged to a seventeen-year-old girl and a dead man's knowledge, and that Corvus Dain had made the same mistake everyone makes about the Reyes family.

He thought they were finished.

They were just getting started.
