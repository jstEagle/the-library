## 28. The Map Is Not the Treasure

There is a particular quality to being cornered on ice, and I don't recommend it. On a riverboat you can always run—upstream, downstream, into the reeds, over the bar where no one dares follow. On a mountain you can climb or descend. But on a glacier's tongue, with the ice falling away behind you and six rifles arranged in front of you like a jury that has already voted, there is nowhere in the world left to be.

Dain had chosen his ground the way he chose everything: carefully, and without mercy.

The glacier's exit was a moraine fan spilling out of the corridor onto grey gravel, maybe two hundred yards of open ground between the blue throat of the ice and the treeline below. Dain's men stood spread across it in a loose crescent, mules grazing behind them—our mule among them, the one they'd stolen off Ferro's line, looking fat and traitorous. Corvus Dain himself stood front and center, hands loose at his sides, coat buttoned against the wind, wearing the expression of a man who has done this arithmetic before and always likes the answer.

"Captain Voss." He said my full name deliberately, the way a man lays a card face-up. "You look terrible."

"You should see the other glacier," I said.

Behind me, Wren's breath came fast and shallow. Ferro leaned against an ice block, grey as old porridge, one hand pressed under his ribs where the bullet bruise had gone bad. Three weeks of mountain, four days in a cave, a calving that had nearly swallowed us—and now this, waiting at the door like a bill come due.

I counted again, because counting was all I had. Six men. Five rifles visible, one slung. Dain carried a pistol, which told me he expected this to end in conversation rather than shooting, or else he was arrogant enough to think it didn't matter which.

"Here's how today goes," Dain said. "You give me the packet. You give me the girl's silence—I'll take her word for it, she seems the honest type, more's the pity. And you walk down this mountain owing me nothing. Not one coin. Not one favor. Free, Captain. Do you know what free sounds like after nine years?"

I did. That was the filthy trick of it. I knew exactly what it sounded like—the Kestrel light in the water, no ledger in the drawer, no name whispered at docks, no reason to flinch when a man in a good coat said hello. Nine years I'd have traded a limb for that word. *Free.*

And Tomas Reyes had put the price of it in an oilskin packet that had ridden against my ribs from the Vell to the top of the world.

"The map," Dain said, holding out his hand.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about dying men: they talk in circles, and if you're patient—or trapped, which amounts to the same thing—you start to hear the shape underneath the circling.

Tomas Reyes had spent three days bleeding in my cabin, and I had spent those three days telling myself I was only keeping him alive out of stubbornness. He talked the whole time. About the river, about silver, about a daughter who read books upside down when she thought no one could see her eyes moving. And about the claim. Always about the claim.

*"They didn't just take it from me," he said, near the end, fever climbing. "That's what nobody understands. Taking silver is just theft. Any fool can steal. What they did was make it so I never existed. You understand? A man files a claim, the claim gets recorded, the record says Reyes. They bought the paper, bought the surveyor, bought the clerk, and suddenly the record says Harrow and I'm a thief in every town that ever heard my name. They didn't rob me, Captain. They rewrote me."*

I'd nodded along the way you nod at a dying man, filing it under grief. Fever-talk. A ruined prospector's bitterness.

But I was a riverwoman, and rivers teach you one lesson above all others: water goes where the ground tells it to. Landmarks aren't decoration. When a man draws a map, every mark he makes is a mark he needed to find his way back—and Tomas had been a careful man, careful the way only someone robbed blind becomes careful.

The map was keyed to landmarks only a riverwoman could read—that's what I'd thought made it clever. The notch in the ridge shaped like a heron's beak. The creek that ran backwards at flood. The split rock like a keel. I'd read them the way I read sandbars, and I'd found them, and we were here, and none of that explained why a man hiding a silver vein would also hide—

Why would he also hide—

Oh.

Oh, you beautiful, stubborn, murdered old man.

"They didn't just want the silver," I said aloud.

Dain's head tilted a fraction. "What?"

I wasn't talking to him. I was talking to Tomas, wherever dead prospectors go when their maps finally get read right. My hand went to my coat, to the oilskin packet, and I didn't pull it out—Dain's eyes tracked the motion anyway, hungry—but I pressed it through the cloth like a pulse.

"He didn't bury a treasure," I said. "He buried a *case file.*"

Ferro lifted his head. Wren stopped breathing.

"Think about it," I said, and my voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect, because the whole thing was assembling itself in my head with the terrible click of a lock turning. "A man gets robbed of a claim by forgery. He can't prove it because the proof—the forged papers, the false survey, the bought clerk's receipts—all of it sits in Harrow offices where he'll never touch it again. So what does a careful man do?" I looked at Ferro. "You surveyed that range. You signed the report. What did the Harrows do with the originals?"

Ferro's face did something complicated. "They kept them. They keep everything—it's how syndicates work. Paper is power. The false survey in my hand matched the 'original' in their vault, and the real claim filing—" He stopped. Swallowed. "The real claim filing would have shown Reyes's name first. They took it. I watched the clerk take it."

"And a man like Tomas doesn't forget where anything is," I said. "He spent years finding a vein, and while he worked, he collected. Every document he could get—copies, originals, whatever fell off a wagon or out of a pocket. The forged transfer. The false survey with your signature, Ferro, or a copy of it. Maybe letters. Maybe the receipts for what they paid you." Ferro flinched like I'd slapped the wound. "He hid it all in the same cache. Same hole. Because who robs a grave full of rocks?"

"A man who thinks there's silver in it," Wren whispered.

"A man who thinks there's silver in it," I agreed. "The silver was the bait, Wren. For everyone—for the Harrows, for every greedy son of a bitch who ever heard the rumor of a lost vein. Let them come. Let them dig up silver and crow about it. But the landmarks—" I finally pulled the packet out, held it flat on my palm without opening it. "The landmarks don't lead to the vein. Or not only. There's a second set. A second reading. I've been reading this map like a riverwoman hunting channel markers, and every marker has two meanings, and I was too busy seeing silver to ask why a dying man kept saying *they rewrote me* like it was the whole point."

Dain had gone very still. Stillness, on Corvus Dain, was worse than shouting.

"That's a charming story," he said.

"It's not a story. It's why you're standing on a glacier instead of sending boys." I turned to look at him straight on, and let him see that I saw him. "Six men. A week ahead of us on the low route. A stolen mule. Corvus, the Harrow Syndicate does not spend this much blood chasing a silver map. Silver maps are a dime a dozen in every saloon from here to Vellport. Half the madmen in the territory carry one. You're here because somewhere in Coldharbor or Millbrook, somebody read Tomas Reyes's last letter, or searched his room, or got drunk next to the wrong friend—and learned that the old man had been *collecting*. And the Syndicate woke up in a cold sweat, because if those papers ever reach an assayer or a marshal or a courtroom, the whole beautiful lie comes down. The claims. The tolls. The debts." I smiled, and it wasn't a nice smile. "Debts like mine."

The wind moved along the ice. Somewhere deep in the glacier, something boomed—a calving far back in the corridor, the mountain clearing its throat.

"Give me the packet," Dain said quietly.

"There it is," I said. "No counter-argument. No *you're imagining things, Captain.* Just give me the packet." I shook my head slowly. "Nine years I've wondered what kind of machine I owed my life to. Now I know. A machine that murders an old man in the reeds and calls it pest control. That's what I've been paying interest to."

"Elodie." Dain used my first name for the first time on this mountain, soft as a knife going in. "I'm offering you clean. Whatever papers the old man gathered, they're twenty years stale and worth nothing but trouble. Silver is real. Silver buys doctors for your surveyor, who looks like he's losing a fight with his own blood. Silver buys you a new boat and a new name in a new territory. Papers buy you a shallow grave beside him." He gestured, almost gently, at the ice behind us. "You've seen what this mountain does to people who linger."

"I've seen what your Syndicate does to people who talk," I said. "I find I prefer the mountain. At least the mountain is honest about wanting me dead."

Wren made a sound beside me—not quite a laugh, not quite a sob. Ferro pushed himself upright off the ice block, swaying, and planted his feet like a man deciding to be a wall.

"Last chance, Captain," Dain said. All the gentleness drained out of his voice, and what was underneath was just arithmetic. "Six rifles. One gassed surveyor, one grieving girl, one woman with a gashed arm. You cannot win this."

"No," I agreed. "Almost certainly not."

I looked at the packet in my hand. Twenty years of a dead man's patience. A daughter's name cleared. A surveyor's sin confessed twice over. A debt of mine that had felt, for nine years, like weather—like something natural, unavoidable, mine. It wasn't weather. It was paper. It had always been paper, and paper could burn, or paper could testify.

Tomas hadn't given me a treasure. He'd given me a verdict, wrapped in oilskin, waiting twenty years for a jury.

"Wren," I said quietly, without taking my eyes off Dain. "Your father taught you the glacier. How long since the last big calving?"

Her breath caught. I heard her mind working behind me, quick as her father's must have been. "Two hours. The meltwater's peaking. Another boom should come before dark."

"And when it comes—"

"The seracs above the exit shelf let go first. Papa said the whole face sheds like a dog in spring. Anyone standing on the lower moraine when it goes—" She stopped.

"—is having a very bad afternoon," I finished.

Dain heard it too. To his credit—he was many rotten things, but never stupid—his eyes flicked upward, toward the blue walls and the hanging ice, doing his own arithmetic now, and for the first time since the crescent of rifles closed around us, the numbers didn't come out the way he liked.

"Captain," he said, and there was something new in his voice, thin and cold. "Whatever you're thinking—"

"Corvus," I said, "you should know something about me. Everyone on this mountain knows it by now. I run rapids in the dark. I read water other people won't look at. And I have spent nine years learning exactly one skill." I raised the oilskin packet high enough for every rifle on that moraine to see it, and I let myself grin like a woman with nothing left to lose, which was, at last, the simple truth.

"I am very, very good at knowing when the ice is about to go."

Deep in the glacier, something groaned—a long, tearing note, like a ship's hull giving up. Blue dust sifted from the seracs above the exit. Dain's men shifted, rifles wavering, looking up.

"Now, Wren," I said. "*Now.*"
