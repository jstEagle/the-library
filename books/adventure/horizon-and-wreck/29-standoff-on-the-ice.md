## 29. Standoff on the Ice

My father taught me three things about glaciers, and he taught me all of them before I was ten, which means I spent my childhood learning survival skills disguised as games and didn't know it until the day I needed them.

The first: ice is never still. It groans, it shifts, it breathes. A glacier is a river that forgot how to hurry.

The second: when the ice speaks, listen. The pops and cracks aren't noise—they're sentences. *That shelf is tired. That wall is leaning. Stay off that tongue of blue.*

The third—and this one he said with his hand flat on a frozen pond behind our house in Coldharbor, his breath smoking, me bundled up so thick I could barely bend my arms—*the mountain doesn't care who's right, Wrennie. It only cares who's listening.*

I hadn't thought about that day in years. I thought about it now, standing at the mouth of the glacier corridor with six Harrow men between us and the way out, because it turned out to be the most useful thing anyone ever taught me.

* * *

It started quiet, which should have been the first warning. Glaciers are loud places. When they go quiet, they're holding their breath.

We'd come down out of the blue cathedral laughing—I want to remember that we were laughing, Ferro making some terrible joke about surveyors and mules, Elodie actually smiling, that rare crooked thing she did like she was paying for it by the minute—and then the light changed at the corridor's exit and Elodie's arm came across my chest like a gate dropping.

Six men. Rifles slung casual, the way men carry them when they don't expect to need them but enjoy the option. And in front, standing on the ice with his boots planted like he owned the whole white world, Corvus Dain.

He looked worse than the last time I'd seen him. The moraine had given him something to think about—a healing cut along his jaw, a stiffness in his left side he was trying not to favor. But his voice was as smooth as ever, and that was the frightening part. Men who look beaten get desperate. Men who sound calm while looking beaten have already decided how this ends.

"Captain Voss." He spread his hands. "You've made excellent time. I'd congratulate you, but we both know you had help."

Elodie said nothing. She stood with her weight even on both feet, her gashed arm held close, her face doing that thing it does where it becomes a door with nothing behind it. I'd learned to read her over the past weeks—the way her jaw set when she was calculating, the way she went very still when she was afraid. She was both right now.

"You're outnumbered," Dain went on, conversational, like we were discussing freight rates. "You're exhausted. Your surveyor can barely stand—I can see it from here. And your route down closes with the snows, which are—" he tilted his head, listening to the wind coming off the ice, "—close. I'm offering you the same bargain I always have. The packet. In exchange, your debt dies, the girl walks away untouched, and everyone sees spring."

"The girl," I said.

His eyes flicked to me. Something almost warm crossed them, which was worse than cruelty. "Miss Reyes. You've been a surprise. Your father would be proud—he also had a talent for being where he shouldn't."

"Don't," I said, and my voice cracked on it, and I hated that. "Don't talk about him. You don't get to know his name."

"I knew him better than you did," Dain said mildly. "I knew him the year you thought he'd abandoned you. Ask your captain what the Harrows do to men who find things in mountains. Or don't—it changes nothing today. Captain. The map."

And here is what I noticed, standing there with my heart going like a paddle wheel: Elodie's hand wasn't anywhere near the oilskin packet inside her coat. It was near her knife, yes, but her fingers weren't reaching. They were *counting*. Little movements against her thigh. One, two, three, four, five, six.

She was counting them the way Papa used to count the cracks in an ice sheet before crossing. Not measuring the danger. Measuring the *material*.

* * *

Negotiation, as far as I could tell from watching Elodie work, was mostly lying with good posture.

"I don't have the map," she said.

Dain sighed. "Captain."

"I burned it." She shrugged with her good shoulder. "Weeks ago. You're standing on a glacier guarding ash."

"I watched a dying man give it to you through a spyglass in Millbrook. I've chased it across two hundred miles of river and rock. Try again."

"Then I buried it. Marked spot. You'll never find it without me, and I'll die before I walk you to it, so we're at a charming impasse."

"You'll die anyway if this goes wrong," Dain said. No heat in it. That was the thing about him—no heat anywhere, just arithmetic. "But consider the girl. Consider the surveyor. Ferro." He turned his head slightly. "How's the lung, Augustin? Still whistling?"

Ferro, gray-faced and swaying beside me, managed something magnificent. He spat on the ice. It was a weak spit—all he had—but the *intent* carried.

"There it is," Dain said softly. "The famous Ferro pride. It cost you everything once. Interesting hobby."

Elodie took one step forward, and every rifle came up, and she stopped with her palms open like she was showing us all a trick. "Here's my counter-offer," she said. "You let us walk. In exchange, I don't tell the federal assayer about the documents."

A pause. The first crack in him. Small—one muscle beside his eye—but I saw it, and I saw that Elodie saw it.

"What documents," Dain said.

"The ones cached with the silver." She said it lightly, tossing it, but I felt the ground shift under all of us. "Tomas Reyes wasn't just a prospector, Corvus. He was a careful man. He kept records. Claim filings. Signatures. Dates. Proof of who really stole the Reyes claim, and who signed the false survey, and who ordered the men who shot him." She smiled, and it was the coldest thing I'd ever seen her do. "Silver spends. Paper hangs."

"That's a story."

"It's a location. Want to hear it? It starts with the map you're so eager to burn."

And Dain—here's the thing I'll tell the marshals later, under oath, when they ask me how it happened—Dain *believed* her. Not because she was convincing, though she was. Because he wanted it to be true. Because a man who has spent years burying bodies sleeps badly, and any document that might surface is a splinter in the mind. I watched him do the math on six rifles versus one piece of paper, and the paper won.

"Show me the cache," he said. "All of us go together. If the documents exist, we discuss terms. If they don't—"

"They do."

"—then we discuss terms," he finished, as if she hadn't spoken. "Walk."

* * *

So we walked. Six rifles behind us, the ice singing under our boots, Ferro breathing in wet whistles at my shoulder. I got one arm under him and half-carried him, and he whispered, "Your father's cache is real? There are documents?"

"There's a box," I whispered back. "Papa talked about keeping records. I thought it was just—him being him. Careful about receipts." My throat closed. "He kept receipts for his own murder, Ferro. He planned for it."

"He planned for you," Ferro said. "There's a difference."

Ahead, Elodie led us off the main corridor onto a side branch of blue ice that narrowed and rose, walls climbing on either side until we walked in a slot of shadow and refracted light. I knew where she was taking us—or rather, I realized with a cold drop in my stomach, I knew where the *map* said the cache was, and I knew what the map's margin notes said about this branch, because I'd studied them every night by candlelight while the others slept.

*Mind the serac. Old ice, rotten base. Loud in warm afternoons.*

It was afternoon. It had been unseasonably warm for three days.

And suddenly I understood why Elodie had chosen this route, and my stomach dropped a second time, because she wasn't leading us to the cache at all. Not yet. She was leading us to the loudest place on the glacier, and she was buying time, and she was trusting me—

No. She was *waiting* for me. I saw it in the glance she threw back over her shoulder, quick as a card sharp. She couldn't say it with six rifles at our backs. But she'd counted them earlier, and she'd chosen this route, and there was exactly one person among us who'd been raised on a dead man's ice-lore, and it wasn't her.

*Listen,* Papa had said, hand flat on the pond. *When the ice speaks, listen.*

I started listening.

* * *

The serac loomed ahead—a tower of ancient ice the size of a church, leaning maybe ten degrees off true, its base swallowed in shadow where meltwater had been quietly eating all summer. The Harrow men bunched up beneath it without a second glance, because to men from the low country all ice looks solid, and Dain came up beside Elodie and said, "Where."

"Past the tower. The cache sits in a hollow behind it—Tomas keyed it to the serac itself. Said no avalanche would touch it." Which was probably even true. Papa trusted that tower the way you trust a mean dog you've known for years.

Dain waved two men forward to scout around the base, and that was when I heard it.

Under the wind. Under the boot-scrape and the jingle of harness. A sound like a knuckle cracking, deep underground, long and slow.

Then another. Closer.

The ice was talking, and what it was saying was: *tired, tired, leaning, tired.*

I caught Elodie's eye. She gave me the smallest nod in the history of nods.

"Papa used to sing to me," I said, loudly, to no one, my voice bouncing off the blue walls. "Working songs. Prospectors' songs. Did you know that? He said the mountain liked music."

"Wren," Elodie said, a warning note.

"He said everything up here has a voice if you listen." I crouched, laid my palm flat against the ice the way he'd laid his against the pond, and the cold bit straight through my glove into the bones of my hand. "This old boy's been holding his breath all summer. All that meltwater underneath, eating away at his feet. He's tired." I looked up at Dain. "Do you hear it? The popping? Like knuckles?"

One of the Harrow men laughed nervously. Dain didn't laugh. He was watching me the way you watch something you can't classify, and I understood suddenly that this was the advantage nobody had given me credit for: Corvus Dain had prepared for a captain, a liar, and a grieving girl. He had not prepared for Tomas Reyes's daughter.

"Get up," he said.

"In a minute." I pressed my palm harder against the ice. Beneath us, faint but unmistakable, water ran—a rushing whisper, the glacier's hidden blood. All I had to do was add one more voice to the conversation. "Papa taught me a song for moments like this. He said the mountain answers if you ask right."

I opened my mouth and sang the first thing that came, which was not a prospecting song at all but the silly river shanty Elodie hummed on the Kestrel when she thought no one was listening, loud and clear and completely tuneless, and while every head turned toward the crazy girl singing at the ice—

I pulled the piton hammer from my pack and struck the base of the serac as hard as I could.

Once. Twice. Three times, ringing blows, right where the rotten ice met the old.

The glacier answered.

* * *

Later, people will ask me what a controlled calving sounds like, and I will never find words big enough. It begins as a click, then a groan, then a sound like the whole sky tearing along a seam, and then the world simply *moves*. The serac leaned. The lean became a fall. Ten thousand tons of blue ice came down in a slow, terrible, graceful collapse, and the shockwave of it ran through the glacier like a drumbeat, and everywhere along the corridor, weakened shelves and cornices answered their brother's call.

The Harrow men scattered. That was the point—that was all the point needed to be. Two dove one way, two another, one simply dropped flat and covered his head, and the sixth turned and ran back down the corridor screaming about the icefall behind them, which was indeed calving now, great slabs shrugging off the walls in thunderclouds of powder snow.

Dain didn't scatter. Dain grabbed me.

His hand closed on my collar and hauled me backward off my feet, and I heard Elodie shout my name, and then his voice was in my ear, close and cold: "The map, or I break her arm, Captain. Right here. Count of three."

The world went narrow. His grip. The powder snow blinding us. Elodie coming through the white with her knife already out, moving faster than I'd ever seen her move, and Ferro—Ferro, who could barely stand, swinging his walking iron like a scythe and missing entirely, going down hard on the ice.

"One," Dain said.

I did the only thing my father would have approved of. I went limp, and then I didn't. I dropped my whole weight, twisted inside his grip the way he'd taught me to twist out of a snarl of rope, and drove my elbow back into the wound on his side—the one the moraine had given him, the stiffness he'd been hiding all day.

He grunted. His grip loosened one inch.

An inch was enough for Elodie. Her knife flashed—not at his throat, at his *wrist*, a short ugly slash meant to make him let go, and he did, shoving me away as he swung at her, and the blade caught her along the forearm instead, opening a red line over the older gash, and she made no sound at all, which frightened me more than screaming would have.

Then the ice finished the argument.

A crevasse mouth yawned open ten feet from us with a crack like a rifle, and the ground tilted, and Dain—off-balance, bleeding, blinded by powder—staggered, caught himself on his knees at the new edge, and looked down into blue dark. Behind him, his men were shouting for him, retreating, the corridor filling with rolling fog of snow-dust. Ahead of him, Elodie stood between Dain and me with her bloody knife and her bloody arm and an expression I will carry to my grave: not rage. Arithmetic. She was deciding what he was worth.

"Go," she said.

"This isn't—"

"Go, Corvus. The mountain's awake, and it knows your name now."

He went. I'll say that for him—he knew a lost hand when the deck collapsed on it. He scrambled up, clutched his slashed wrist, and limped into the white after his men, and the last I saw of Corvus Dain was his silhouette dissolving into the snow-fog like ink into water.

* * *

Silence after, except the glacier settling, groaning, complaining about all the excitement. Powder snow sifted down on us like flour. Ferro sat in it, wheezing and laughing at the same time, which sounded dreadful and looked wonderful. Elodie stood with blood dripping off her fingertips, pink dots on blue ice.

"You sang my shanty," she said.

"It seemed appropriate."

"You struck a serac with a piton hammer."

"You counted six men and picked a rotten route and waited for me to catch up," I said. "We're even."

She looked at her arm, flexed the hand, winced. "Not quite. This needs binding, and the cache is still behind that pile of rubble, and we've maybe lost an hour we didn't have." But she was smiling—the crooked smile, the expensive one—and this time I think she paid gladly.

Ferro struggled upright between us, put one arm over each of our shoulders, and said, "For the record, I helped."

"You fell down," I said.

"Strategically."

We stood there a moment, the three of us, in the falling powder, and I felt something settle in my chest that I didn't have a name for yet. My father's lessons in my hands. His killer running from his daughter's song. Whatever happened next—whatever waited at the cache, whatever Dain tried tomorrow—I was not the girl who'd stowed away on the Kestrel anymore.

Elodie bound her arm with strips of shirt, grim and quick, then nodded up the slope to where the serac's wreckage had torn open the hillside of ice.

"Come on," she said. "Let's go see what your father left us."

And behind us, deep in the glacier, something shifted again—a long, low groan, like the mountain clearing its throat to speak.
