## 30. The Cache

My father taught me to read a river when I was six years old, though I didn't know that's what he was doing. He'd hold me on his hip at the edge of Coldharbor Creek and say, *See how the water wrinkles there, little bird? That's a rock pretending it isn't.* Everything he ever showed me was a lesson wearing the costume of a game, and I spent eleven years thinking he'd abandoned us with nothing but games in his pockets.

He was standing beside me now—had been standing beside me for three days, if you counted the map, and I did—and he was showing me one last thing.

*Where the glacier shrugs its shoulder,* the final notation read, in his cramped left-slanting hand, *look for the stone that does not belong.*

The glacier's shoulder was exactly what it looked like: a vast blue-white slope shouldering up against the mountain's flank, groaning like an old man getting out of a chair. Elodie stood at my left with her bandaged arm held close, gray-faced but upright, scanning the ice field the way she scanned water—for the lie underneath. Ferro sat on a supply crate behind us, fever-bright and stubborn, holding a surveyor's glass he kept raising and lowering like a man checking whether the world was still there.

"Stone that doesn't belong," Ferro murmured. "In a moraine? That's like asking for a grain of sand that doesn't belong."

"He wasn't talking about the moraine." I heard myself say it before I understood it, the way you sometimes hear your father's voice come out of your own mouth. "Look at the shoulder. Where the meltwater cuts through—the calving channel. Every spring the ice moves, everything shifts. Except—" I pointed. "Except that."

Halfway down the slope, wedged where two converging flows of ice ground against each other like millstones, sat a boulder the size of a wagon. It was dark granite in a country of pale limestone. It had no business being there. It had been placed there—or rather, the ice around it had been encouraged, years ago, by a man with a shovel and a grudge and a genius for hiding things, so that the boulder would ride the glacier's slow conveyor and never quite arrive anywhere.

Elodie was quiet a long moment. "Your father," she said finally, "was either the smartest man on this river or the most paranoid."

"Why not both?" I said, and started down.

* * *

Getting to the boulder took the better part of two hours, because glaciers do not permit anything to be simple. We roped together—Ferro insisted, slurring the knots into place with hands that shook, and neither Elodie nor I argued, which tells you how frightened we both were of him falling. The ice talked the whole way. Not the storybook crackle but deep structural complaints, booms that came up through our boots like something enormous turning over in its sleep below us. Twice we detoured around crevasses whose far edges we couldn't see, blue-black slots breathing cold air, and each time Elodie probed ahead with an ice axe and made jokes in a voice pitched just slightly too high, and each time I pretended not to notice.

The boulder, up close, was even more wrong than it had looked from above. Its base had been chocked with smaller stones, packed deliberately, and the packed stones had frozen into a skirt of clear ice—ice that had melted and refrozen around them season after season, like candle wax, like a seal someone meant to be broken only once.

"We'll need fire," Elodie said. "And time."

"We don't have much of either."

"No," she agreed. "We never do."

We built the fire in a ring of stones Ferro selected with the last of his professional rigor—he actually giggled, a small unhinged sound, when he found a flat limestone slab and declared it *structurally adequate*. The flames licked at the ice skirt and water began to run, threading silver down the granite, and I crouched there feeding sticks to the fire with my bare hands going numb, and I thought: *somewhere on the other side of this mountain, Corvus Dain is doing something, and whatever it is, it is faster than melting ice.*

I didn't say it out loud. Some things you keep in your chest like a coal, to warm your hands on later when you need to be afraid properly.

* * *

The ice gave way at dusk.

Not all at once—it let go in plates, cracking off the boulder's base like shell off a boiled egg, until Elodie could wedge her axe blade into the gap and lever, and something inside the cairn shifted with a hollow, deliberate clank. A sound with intention in it. A sound a man builds.

We cleared the loose stone with our hands. Beneath the boulder's overhang, dry as a church, lay a cache: a canvas-wrapped bundle the size of a flour sack, and beside it an iron box, black with old grease, its lock a simple padlock that had waited twenty years and did not look tired of waiting.

Nobody moved for a second. The glacier boomed somewhere beneath us, patient as God.

"You do it," Elodie said quietly. "It's yours."

"It's his."

"Same thing."

I knelt. My fingers weren't shaking, which surprised me; I'd expected them to. The canvas came away stiff and crackling, and inside was ore—silver ore, real silver, galena shot through with wires of native metal so bright they looked wet even after twenty years in the dark. Enough of it to matter. Enough to buy back a reputation, a ranch, a life. Enough to kill for; men already had.

But it was the box I wanted.

The padlock surrendered to Ferro's knife blade worked in the shackle—he apologized to the ghost of Tomas Reyes for the vandalism, which under other circumstances would have made me laugh. The lid came up with a sigh of cold air, and inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone brittle as autumn leaves, were papers.

I lifted them out one at a time, and the mountain went very quiet around me, or maybe that was just my own blood stopping in my ears.

The first was a claim filing. *Reyes, T., Coldharbor District,* dated the spring before I was born. My father's signature at the bottom—his real signature, the one I knew from the flyleaves of his books, the lean *R* with the tail that hooked like a fishhook. The claim description matched, coordinate for coordinate, the landmarks on the map Elodie had carried out of a dying man's hands.

The second document was a survey report. I recognized the letterhead because I'd seen it framed in a bank office in Millbrook: *Ferro & Associates, Territorial Surveyors.* And I recognized the finding, because I'd heard it read aloud in a Coldharbor courtroom when I was nine years old, from a copy, while my mother sat beside me with her jaw set like a woman biting through a nail: *the described parcel shows no evidence of mineralization of commercial quantity.*

A false report. Signed, dated, stamped. The document that had unmade my father's life, sitting here in his cache, kept by him like a wound he couldn't stop touching.

"Ferro," I said. My voice came out strange.

"I know," he said. He was looking at the paper upside down across the firelight, and his face had gone the color of the ice. "That's mine. That's my hand. God forgive me, Wren, that's my signature."

"I know," I said. "We'll get to that."

Because the third document was better than either of them. It was a letter, unsigned but on Harrow Syndicate stationery—a letter instructing an unnamed recipient to *secure the Reyes parcel through the standard instruments* and further directing that *the surveyor's fee be drawn from general funds per our arrangement.* Below it, a ledger page. Real numbers. Payments. One line item marked *R. parcel — acquisition*, and another, dated four months later, marked *R. matter — closed*, with a sum attached that no honest closing ever required.

They hadn't just stolen his claim. They'd paid for it. Itemized it. Filed it between hay purchases and toll receipts like it was a fence post.

And the fourth document—

The fourth was a deposition, sworn and witnessed, in a clerk's careful hand. The deponent was a man named Aldous Merrick, and according to the header he had been a Harrow freight agent, and according to his own words he had driven a wagon to the Reyes claim on a night in October with two other men, and one of those men had been younger then, leaner, with a scar just beginning on his jaw, and his name had been Corvus Dain. They had been sent to persuade Tomas Reyes to sign a quitclaim. Tomas Reyes had declined. There had been a struggle. Shots. The deponent stated that Mr. Dain had fired the second and third shots, and that Tomas Reyes had fallen into the river, and that Mr. Dain had said, and the deposition recorded it verbatim, italicized in the clerk's careful hand:

*"Let the current finish the paperwork."*

He hadn't died that night. That was the thing that broke me open, kneeling on the ice with my father's enemies' handwriting in my hands. He'd crawled out. He'd dragged himself into the reeds half-dead and he'd lived—lived rough and hidden and estranged for twenty years, living like a hunted animal because these men had turned a piece of paper into a verdict and sent a killer to be the sheriff of it. And when death finally did catch him, shot in the back on the Vell River, his last act on earth had been to press this packet into the hands of a stranger and ask her to carry it home to me.

I don't remember deciding to cry. I remember the papers getting wet, and Elodie's hand—her good one—coming down gently over mine, moving them clear.

"Careful," she said softly. "These are worth more than the silver now."

"I know what they're worth."

"I don't think you do. Not yet." She crouched beside me, close enough that I could see the exhaustion carved into her face and something else underneath it, something almost like awe. "Wren. This isn't vindication. Vindication is a word for courtrooms. This is a hammer. You're holding the thing that breaks the Harrow Syndicate—not your father's claim, every claim. Every family they've done this to. There are other ledgers like this one, and this proves the pattern, and patterns are what juries hang men with." She paused. "Dain knows that better than anyone alive. Which means between here and Vellport, he is going to come at us with everything he has left, because for him this isn't about silver anymore either. It's about the rope."

Ferro laughed—one short, awful bark. "Twenty years I've carried that report," he said. "I told myself it was a mistake, an error of youth, a misread assay. It wasn't. I sat in their office and they put the number in front of me and I picked up the pen." He looked at the fire, not at us. "Put it in the box, Wren. All of it. Let it testify. I'll testify too, when the time comes, if they let a perjurer speak."

"They'll let you speak," Elodie said. "That's what testimony is for. The truth doesn't care how late it arrives. It just cares that it does."

I wrapped the documents back in their oilcloth, gently, the way you'd wrap something sleeping. Silver in the canvas bundle. Truth in the iron box. My father had cached them together, and I understood now that he'd always meant them together—that the vein was never the treasure, that the treasure was the proof, that a man who'd lost everything to a lie would spend twenty years preserving not the fortune but the *receipt*. He hadn't buried his vindication in the mountains. He'd buried theirs. Their guilt, notarized and filed and waiting.

"Little bird," I whispered, testing the phrase in his voice, keeping it. "You clever, stubborn, impossible man."

The glacier answered with a boom so deep I felt it in my teeth, and down-slope, a serac the size of a church shed a shoulder of itself and fell in thunder and powder. Calving season, opening its throat. Our road off this ice was open and would not stay open.

We loaded the mules by firelight. The silver went into panniers; the iron box went into my own pack, against my spine, where I could feel it with every step like a second heartbeat. Elodie checked the ropes twice. Ferro walked—insisted on walking, swaying, muttering coordinates to himself like a rosary.

We were a quarter mile along the corridor, the blue walls rising on either side of us, dusk going to true dark behind, when Elodie stopped so suddenly I walked into her pack.

She was looking back the way we'd come, down the long throat of the glacier toward the low route, toward the tree line, toward the world.

"What?" I whispered.

"Lantern light," she said. "Far side of the moraine. More than one." She turned, and in the last of the light her eyes were the color of the crevasse shadows. "He didn't take the low route to get ahead of us, Wren. He took it to wait. And now he knows we found it."

Behind us, faint across the ice, a lantern swung. Then another. Then, thin and carrying over the glacier's groaning, a voice I knew—the voice from the lock, from the trail, from my nightmares—calling out cheerfully, horribly reasonable, the way a man calls to neighbors across a fence:

*"Captain Voss! We should talk about the paperwork!"*

My father's killer wanted to negotiate. And the iron box rode cold and heavy against my spine, full of every reason he never would.
