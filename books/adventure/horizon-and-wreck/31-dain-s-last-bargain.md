## 31. Dain's Last Bargain

He came back at dusk, the way wounded things do—alone, limping, and too proud to admit the mountain had already beaten him.

I saw him first from the cache site, where we'd spent the better part of an hour doing something I never expected to do in my life: weeping over paperwork. Wren held the iron box against her chest like a child she'd just been handed. Ferro sat on a boulder with his arm strapped across his ribs, gray as old ice but breathing, which after yesterday was more than I'd dared hope for. The silver lay in its seam behind us, glittering in the low light like it didn't know what trouble it had caused.

And down at the edge of the moraine, small against all that white and blue, Corvus Dain picked his way toward us one careful step at a time.

"He's alone," Wren said.

"I can see that."

"Should I—"

"No." I checked the pistol in my belt out of habit more than confidence. Three bullets left, maybe four if the cold hadn't swollen the cartridges shut. "Let him come to us. Men like Dain are most dangerous when they've got ground to cover."

Ferro grunted. "Men like Dain are most dangerous always. But especially when they're bleeding."

He was bleeding. As he closed the distance I could see it now—the dark stain along his left side, the way his right leg dragged a half-beat behind the left, the frost on his beard where sweat had frozen and thawed and frozen again. He'd lost the mule somewhere, or abandoned it, or eaten it; there was no pack on his shoulders, nothing but the man himself and whatever he carried in his coat. He stopped twenty feet away, which told me two things: he still had enough sense to stay out of pistol range, and he wanted us to see his hands.

They were empty. Raised slightly, palms out, the universal language of *I am not here to kill you today*.

Which, from Corvus Dain, meant he was here to buy something instead.

"Captain Voss," he said. His voice carried flat across the ice, stripped of all its usual silk. That was new. So was the exhaustion underneath it. "You look well."

"You look like something the glacier chewed up and thought better of swallowing," I said. "What happened to your six men?"

"Gone." He said it simply. No embellishment, no blame laid at our feet. "The calving scattered them. Two went into a crevasse before I could shout a warning. The others ran for the low route and God help them on it. I've been walking since yesterday morning." He paused, and something moved behind his eyes—something I recognized, because I'd seen it in mirrors during bad years. "I'm done, Captain. Do you understand? Not beaten. Done. There's a difference, and you of all people know it."

I did know it. Beaten men come back for revenge. Done men come back for terms.

Wren shifted beside me, and I felt her anger like heat off a stove. This was the man whose Syndicate had murdered her father, whose forged papers had turned Tomas Reyes into a thief in his own daughter's memory. She had every right to put a bullet in him and the mountain would have thanked her for the compost.

But I raised one hand slightly, and she held. She was learning that from me, God help us both—the patience before the strike. Though in fairness, she'd already taught me a thing or two about striking.

"What are your terms?" I asked.

Dain lowered his hands slowly. "First, water. If you're going to refuse me, refuse me to my face, not while I'm dying of thirst in front of you. It's undignified."

It was also, I noticed, a test. Or a trap. With Dain it was always both. But Ferro was watching me with that surveyor's squint that meant *I've measured this man and here are the numbers*, and Wren was watching me with something rawer, and I found—to my considerable annoyance—that I didn't want to be the kind of woman who let a man die of thirst while she decided his fate. Not because he deserved mercy. Because I deserved not to become the thing I was refusing.

"Wren," I said. "The flask."

She looked at me like I'd asked her to hand him a knife. Which, in a sense, I had—trust being the sharpest thing anyone passes across open ground. But she unslung the flask and set it on a rock halfway between us, then stepped back, and Dain came forward those ten feet like a man crossing a river he knew was treacherous, and drank, and I watched his throat work and thought: *he is forty-some years old and this is the first honest drink anyone has given him in a decade.*

Maybe I was wrong about that. Maybe I was sentimental. The altitude does that to a person.

"Thank you," he said, and here was the strangest part: he sounded like he meant it.

"The terms," I said again.

He capped the flask, set it back on the rock—another courtesy, another signal—and straightened as much as his ruined side allowed.

"The Harrow Syndicate is finished. You know it, I know it, and by spring every clerk in Vellport will know it once your box of papers reaches the assayer. When it falls, everything under it falls. Contracts, debts, ledgers—" his eyes found mine, and there it was, the hook, baited with the only thing that had ever mattered to me, "—including yours, Captain. Your debt dies with the Syndicate. Unless someone buys it first."

"And you're selling."

"I'm *transferring*. There's a difference, and you of all people—" he almost smiled, "—know it. I hold paper on you, Elodie Voss. Eleven hundred dollars, accrued interest, signed in blood-adjacent ink six years ago. While I hold it, I can sell it to whoever crawls out of the Syndicate's wreckage with money and grudges. And there will be such men. There are always such men. But if you give me the map—if you let me walk out of these mountains with the location of that vein—I'll burn your paper on the ice in front of witnesses. You'll be free. Truly free. No debt, no Syndicate, no Dain. The river, whole and yours, forever."

The wind moved through the corridor behind him, and the glacier spoke its long slow language of groan and settle, and I stood there and let myself feel the full weight of what he was offering, because refusing a temptation honestly requires first admitting you're tempted.

Eleven hundred dollars. Six years of running sandbars at night, of paying cash and giving no name, of sleeping with the Kestrel's engine ticking under me like a second heartbeat because it was the only thing in the world that had never lied to me or left me. Six years bought back for the price of a dead man's map—a map that had already given up its greater secret, the documents in the iron box, the proof that would break the Harrows and clear Tomas Reyes's name. The silver itself I didn't need. The map, once the claim was registered in Wren's name, would be worth exactly nothing to anyone else.

Except Dain didn't fully know that. He knew about the documents—we'd shown them, or rather Wren had brandished them at his men during the ambush like a torch in a dark room—but he might not have understood what it meant: that the map's value died the moment those papers reached Vellport. He was offering to buy a fish that had already jumped back in the river.

Or he wasn't. Or he knew exactly what he was asking for, and what he wanted wasn't the vein at all but simply *out*—a bargaining chip of his own, something to trade the marshals for leniency, or the Syndicate's rivals for a place in whatever rose from its ashes. A drowning man doesn't grab for gold. He grabs for anything that floats.

"Ferro," I said quietly. "How far to the low route from here?"

"Two days, if his leg holds. Four if it doesn't. Five, and he doesn't make it at all." Ferro said it without cruelty, the way he read elevations off a contour line. Just numbers.

"Dain." I stepped forward, until the space between us was close enough to smell the blood and frost on him. "I'm going to tell you something true, and you can believe it or not, but I've earned the right to say it and you've earned the right to hear it. Six years ago a man I loved put his crime in my name and sailed away, and I have spent every day since learning the difference between freedom and flight. I thought the debt was the cage. It wasn't. The cage was believing I deserved it." I watched his face do something complicated. "You're offering to unlock a door I've already walked through. The paper you hold isn't worth anything anymore—not because the Syndicate is falling, but because I stopped paying it rent in my head somewhere around the Widow's Teeth. So no. I won't give you the map. I wouldn't give you the map if it were worth a fortune, and I won't insult both of us by pretending that's the reason."

His jaw worked. "Then you'll watch me die on this ice."

"That depends entirely on you."

"It depends on *you*." And there it was—the last of the silk gone, just the man underneath, scared and hurt and furious at being both. "You have food. Shelter. A route down. I have a hole in my side and a leg that's turning colors I don't have names for. Mercy, Captain. One night by your fire. That's all I'm asking. Then I'll walk south and you'll never see me again, and you can tell yourself whatever story makes the cold easier."

Behind me, Wren made a sound—not quite a word. I turned my head enough to catch her expression, and what I saw there nearly broke the resolve I hadn't yet finished building. Not hatred. Worse than hatred: the question. *Are we the kind of people who leave a man to freeze?* Her father hadn't been. Whatever else Tomas Reyes had been—thief in the town's ledger, stranger to his own daughter—he'd been the kind of man who taught a seven-year-old how to read a snow bridge, which is the act of a man who expects the world to be survivable for other people.

And I thought about the corpse at treeline. The Harrow agent the mountain had taken before we ever arrived. How long he'd lasted alone. What his last hours must have sounded like.

"Dain," I said, "if I bring you to my fire, what happens?"

"Nothing happens. I sleep, I eat, I go."

"If you're lying, I'll know. I've spent six years reading liars on this river, and you're the easiest read I've ever had. Your eyes go to exits when you talk. They're doing it now."

Something flickered across his face—almost respect, almost shame. "Habit," he said. "Occupational."

"So is mine." I looked past him, down the moraine, to where the glacier's tongue met the scree, and I saw what I'd been half-noticing since he walked up: the ice there was rotten. Late-season melt had undercut it, and the surface was laced with the thin gray webbing of refrozen slush—candle ice, Ferro called it, the kind that looks solid and gives way like a lie. Between us and the low route, that rotten tongue stretched a quarter mile, sagging in the middle where meltwater ran beneath.

"You want to leave," I said. "Leave."

"Captain—"

"There's your road. Straight south, down the moraine, across the ice tongue, two days to the pass. Nobody's stopping you. Nobody's shooting at you. The mountain gets a vote same as the rest of us, and I'm not going to cheat it on your behalf." I heard my own voice come out level, almost gentle, and realized with a small cold shock that I meant every word—not as punishment, not even as justice, but as the plainest truth available. "You came here to make a bargain. Here's the last one on the table: your life for your own two legs and whatever luck you've got left. It's a better deal than you gave Tomas Reyes."

That landed. I watched it land, watched him flinch the way men flinch when a name they've tried to bury gets spoken aloud in cold air.

"He sold me a map," Dain said quietly. "For a promise. Same as he sold you."

"Yes."

"And you kept it."

"I'm keeping it right now."

He stood there a long moment. The light was failing, that high-altitude dusk that comes down like a curtain, and the temperature was already falling through the soles of my boots. Somewhere deep in the glacier, something boomed—a calving, distant, the ice shedding weight it couldn't carry. Wren's hand found my sleeve. Ferro rose stiffly to stand at my shoulder, presenting the united front of three people who had, against every law of nature and narrative, become a crew.

"I could have taken it from you," Dain said. "At any point. At the lock, at the rapids, on the moraine. I had the men. I had the guns."

"But you didn't understand what you were taking. That was always the difference." I shrugged. "You thought it was a treasure map. It was a confession. You can't steal a confession, Dain. It has to be given."

He laughed then—one short, cracked sound, more cough than humor—and pressed a hand to his wounded side, and I saw fresh blood between his fingers, black in the dying light.

"For what it's worth," he said, "the offer was real. The burning of the paper. I'd have done it."

"I know," I said. "That's the saddest part."

He turned. Slowly, favoring the leg, he started down the moraine toward the ice tongue, and none of us moved to stop him, and none of us called after him, and the silence he left behind was enormous.

We watched him reach the tongue's edge. We watched him test it—one step, two, the candle ice crackling faintly under his boots like distant applause. Halfway across, he stopped and looked back. Too far to see his face clearly. He raised one hand—not a wave, not a salute. Something older than either. A traveler's acknowledgment: *you saw me.*

Then he took the next step, and the ice gave a sound I will hear for the rest of my life—not a crack, not a boom, but a long wet sigh, the sound of something deciding—and the surface tilted beneath him, and he dropped to one knee, and for one suspended moment the whole rotten tongue held, trembling, a quarter mile of bad conscience stretched over black water.

"Go!" Wren shouted, before I could stop her. "Left! Toward the rock! *Go!*"

And Dain—Corvus Dain, who had hunted us across a territory, who had offered me my freedom priced in a dead man's blood—looked toward the sound of her voice, and obeyed it. He crawled. Hand over hand, knee over knee, dragging the bad leg, he crawled across the last hundred yards of failing ice while it sighed and settled around him, and Wren stood at the moraine's edge shouting directions in her father's cadence—*weight wide, move slow, trust the dark patches least*—and I understood, watching her, that this was the inheritance. Not the silver. Not the claim. This: the reflex to save a man you had every reason to hate, because Tomas Reyes had built it into her before she could walk.

He reached the rock at the far side. He hauled himself onto stone. He knelt there a moment, head bowed, and whether he was praying or vomiting or laughing I couldn't say and didn't try.

Then he stood, and limped south, and the dark took him.

* * *

We didn't talk about it that night.

We made camp in the lee of the cache site, and Ferro drank the willow-bark tea Wren brewed and pronounced himself, in the dry voice he used for good news, "unlikely to die before Vellport, though I reserve the right to complain extensively en route." Wren inventoried what remained of our food with the grim precision of a girl who'd grown up counting tins in a Coldharbor pantry, and announced we had four days' rations for three people and two days' descent ahead of us, "so nobody gets seconds, and Ferro gets thirds, because he's the only one of us leaking."

I took first watch, because I always take first watch, and because I needed the solitude the way other people need liquor.

The stars came out hard and close. The glacier muttered to itself below. And I sat with the Kestrel's old compass in my palm—my father's compass, the one that pointed wherever I refused to go—and I thought about Corvus Dain crawling across rotten ice toward a chance he hadn't earned, guided by the shouted instructions of a girl whose father he'd helped destroy.

Mercy, I thought, is a strange currency. It doesn't balance any ledger I know. You spend it and you don't get it back, and the account never shows why you bothered. But standing there in the cold with two sleeping people behind me who would have followed me into a crevasse and a dead man's promise finally, *finally* within reach of being kept, I felt richer than I had any right to.

The mountain had voted. I'd let it. Whether that made me wise or merely tired, I supposed the descent would tell me.

Because the descent was coming. The sky in the northwest had gone the particular bruised color Ferro had been eyeing all evening, and when he'd said "two days" he'd added, quietly, only to me: "If that front comes down the pass before we do, Captain, none of the rest of it matters."

I looked at the bruised sky. I looked at the iron box, wrapped now in oilskin, riding against Wren's chest even in her sleep.

Two days. Maybe less.

"Wake-up call at first light," I told the dark, and the dark, for once, didn't argue.
