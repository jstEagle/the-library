## 32. Descent Ahead of the Snows

### Elodie

The mountain told us to leave the way a landlord tells you he's raising the rent: not with words, but with a change in the air that made clear staying was no longer an option.

It started as a smell—that clean, mineral cold that comes down off the icefields ahead of a storm, the smell of weather being manufactured somewhere above you. I'd grown up on rivers where storms announced themselves with a green light and a wall of cloud you could watch coming for an hour. Mountains were less courteous. Up here the weather simply arrived, like a debt collector who doesn't knock.

"Snow by dark," Ferro said.

He said it from the ground, because that's where most of his sentences happened now. He was propped against a boulder with his coat buttoned to the chin and his color somewhere between candle wax and old paper, and he was still the one reading the sky, because being right was the last professional habit he had left and he clung to it like a man clinging to a rail in floodwater.

"You can't walk that fast," Wren said. It wasn't cruelty. It was arithmetic. She'd been doing the same sum all morning: distance below, hours of light, Ferro's legs, and the answer kept coming out wrong.

"I don't have to walk," Ferro said. "I just have to fall downhill efficiently."

I crouched beside him and did something I hate doing, which is let my face show what I'm thinking. "Can you ride?"

"I can be tied to a mule, if that's what you're asking."

"It is."

"Then tie me." He managed something like his old crooked smile. "Captain Voss, I've spent thirty years being carried by other people's opinions of me. Being carried by a mule will be a step up in honesty."

We broke camp in twenty minutes, which would have taken forty if we'd had the luxury of arguing about it. The mules knew what was coming before we did—animals always do—and they came down off the moraine at a pace that suggested they'd read the same sky Ferro had. Behind us the glacier sat in its valley like something sleeping that was not going to stay asleep much longer, and above it the clouds had organized themselves into the flat, bruised ceiling that means business.

Three weeks, the calving season had promised us. We'd used eleven getting up, crossing, and nearly dying, and now the window was closing over our heads like water over a dropped coin.

"We make the treeline tonight," I said, "or we winter on this mountain, and I don't mean comfortably."

Wren looked back once at the glacier—the blue cathedral we'd laughed inside of two days ago, already dimming behind us—and then she didn't look back again. That's a Reyes trait, I was learning. Tomas had it too, according to everyone in Coldharbor who'd cursed him for it. When a Reyes decides which way is forward, the past stops existing.

I envied them that. I'd spent ten years making a profession out of the past—out of running from it, mostly, and occasionally selling tickets to other people so they could run from theirs.

The snow caught us an hour after dark, exactly as promised, fine and dry and hissing against the rocks like sand poured from a great height. By midnight it was ankle-deep and the trail had become a rumor. I walked point with the compass in my hand and realized, somewhere around the second hour, that I wasn't checking it. My feet knew this slope. Not this exact slope—but every slope like it, every grade and grain of stone, the way a riverwoman knows current without looking at the water. The mountain and the river turned out to speak the same language after all. Both of them only ever say one thing: *down*.

Ferro lasted until the third hour before the mule-riding stopped working. He didn't complain. He just got quieter, which from Ferro was the loudest alarm there was, and when I put my hand on his knee to check the tie-downs his leg was shaking like a struck cable.

"How bad?" I asked.

"I've been better," he said. "In fairness, I've also been considerably worse. There was an incident in Ossining involving a survey chain and a bear."

"Save it for the doctor."

"There may not be a doctor," he said quietly, "on the far side of whatever's coming."

There wasn't an argument to make against that, so I didn't make one. I just tightened the girth, checked the wound dressing under his coat—the bullet had gone through the meat of his shoulder two days ago and the fever had moved in like a squatter—and kept walking.

Somewhere past midnight the trail crossed a scree chute, forty yards of loose stone angled like a ship's deck in a blow, and the mule carrying Ferro balked. Animals are honest. The mule could feel the mountain shifting under its hooves the way I could feel it, that deep subsonic grumble of a slope deciding whether to hold or let go.

"Ferro." I got my shoulder under his good arm. "Off. We walk this part."

He came off the mule and immediately became ninety pounds heavier than any man has a right to be, but he walked. I'll give him that—he walked, one foot dragging, my shoulder taking half his weight, Wren ahead with the lead line calling out the safe stones in her father's prospecting cadence, *left-foot-high, weight on the heel*, tricks a girl learns at seven without knowing she's learning a survival manual.

Halfway across, the mountain made its decision. Not a slide—a slough, a few tons of stone letting go above us with a sound like the river clearing its throat. Wren shouted and we moved, all three of us and three mules, in the ugly scrambling way that isn't running and isn't falling, and the stones came down behind us and missed us by the width of a decision.

Afterward, in the quiet, Ferro started laughing. A thin, wheezing, feverish laugh, but laughing.

"What," I said.

"I spent six years," he said, "mapping these ranges for men who paid me to lie about them. Six years of careful instruments and false numbers. And the whole time the mountain was telling the truth to anyone who'd stand still and listen." He shook his head. "I should have been a riverboat captain. All that lying, and at least you get to move around."

### Wren

Here is what nobody tells you about carrying a grown man downhill through a snowstorm: it is the least romantic thing in the world, and it will teach you more about a person than a year of conversation.

Papa used to say that about prospecting. *You learn a man's worth by how he acts when the claim plays out,* he'd tell me, sitting on the porch steps in Coldharbor with his hands around a tin cup, back when I thought those hands had done nothing but fail. *Any fool can be decent when the silver's showing.*

Well. The claim had played out, in a manner of speaking. The map was burned—or rather, Elodie had burned it on the moraine two days ago, fed it into a little fire while Dain's blood was still fresh on the stones, and I had watched her do it and understood, finally, what Papa had traded his life to teach me: that some things are worth more unread. The documents were sewn into Elodie's coat lining now, iron-box contents wrapped in oilcloth against her ribs, and the silver could go hang itself. What mattered was getting Ferro off this mountain alive, and getting the truth to Vellport before the Harrows buried it under six feet of lawyers.

So we carried him. In shifts, because that's the only way anything impossible gets done—one of us under each arm when the trail allowed it, or one hauling him upright from behind while the other kicked steps into the snow, trading places every hundred yards without discussing it, because discussing it would have meant admitting how bad it was.

Elodie carried him like she does everything—efficiently, silently, with a face like a locked drawer. But I noticed things. I noticed that she gave him the lee side of her body, away from the wind, every single time they swapped positions, without ever appearing to choose it. I noticed she talked to him—not conversation, exactly, but a low running commentary, *root there, step wide, almost down*—the way you talk to keep a person's mind above water. And I noticed that when Ferro stumbled badly around four in the morning and went down hard enough to take her with him, she got up first, every time, so that he'd never have to see her struggle.

That's love, I decided. Or whatever it is that people like us do instead, because we never learned the regular kind. Endurance as devotion. Showing up, again and again, when showing up costs something.

My turn came at dawn, gray and windless and colder for it, the kind of stillness that means the storm is reloading. Ferro was half-delirious by then, mumbling survey coordinates, and I got my shoulder under him and took his weight and he said, clear as a bell, "You have your father's balance."

I nearly dropped him.

"He taught you without teaching you," Ferro went on, eyes half-closed, feet finding the trail on their own. "The weight-on-the-heel trick. The reading of stone. I watched him do it on the original survey—watched him walk a talus slope like it was a parlor floor. I signed the report that ruined him and the whole time I was thinking, *that man knows this mountain better than my instruments do.*" His breath fogged and tore away. "Knowledge doesn't die with the man, Wren. It just waits for someone to inherit it."

"Papa didn't know I was paying attention," I said. "He thought I was just tagging along."

"They always think that," Ferro said. "About daughters. About apprentices. About anyone small walking behind them." He smiled at the snow. "They're always wrong."

I carried him another quarter mile on that sentence. It was better ballast than his actual weight.

### Ferro

I want it recorded—I am recording it, here, in the ledger of my own skull, which is the only court that will ever hear full testimony—that I did not ask them to slow down. Whatever else Augustin Ferro has been in his life, fraud, coward, instrument of other men's greed, he was not, on this mountain, a burden by choice.

Though God knows I was a burden by physics.

The fever had a geography of its own. There were stretches of trail I remember with terrible clarity—the exact blue of the shadow in the couloir, the sound of Elodie's boots packing snow—and other stretches that simply do not exist, hours subtracted from the record, during which I was evidently walking, because they tell me I was, supported between the two of them like a drunk between bailiffs. In one of those missing hours I apparently gave a speech. Wren mentioned it later, delicately, the way you mention that someone sang at a wedding.

"You apologized," she said. "To both of us. To Captain Voss for the false charts, and to me for—" she paused, choosing, "—for everything the signature cost our family. Then you recited the elevation of Coldharbor to within fifty feet."

"Was I close?"

"To the foot," she said. "Papa would have appreciated that. He hated sloppy numbers worse than he hated liars." A beat. "Which, given the company he kept, must have been exhausting."

She was seventeen years old and she was carrying half my weight down a mountainside and she was making jokes. Reyes steel. I'd seen it in Tomas on the original survey, that quality that looks like stubbornness from the outside and is actually load-bearing from within. I had helped break that man with a pen, and his daughter was keeping me alive with her shoulder, and if there is a punishment adequate to that arithmetic, the mountain was welcome to invent it.

Near midday—we'd stopped counting hours and started counting landmarks, which is what exhausted people do instead of praying—we came down out of the rock and into the first stunted pines, and the change was so sudden it felt like passing through a door. Below the treeline the snow lay softer, the wind lost its teeth, and the air smelled of resin and living wood instead of stone and nothing.

Elodie stood under the first real tree and looked back up the way we'd come. The peaks behind us had vanished entirely into cloud; the storm had closed the pass behind us like a book.

"That's it, then," she said. "Nobody follows. Nobody leaves, either, till spring." She said it flatly, but I heard underneath it the thing she'd never say aloud: that the mountain had just made her choice for her, sealed her in with us, cut the escape route she'd been keeping open in her head for ten years. Elodie Voss, marooned among people who knew her name.

"Captain," I said, because my mouth was operating independently of my judgment, as it had all my life, "when we get to the boat, I intend to resign."

"Don't be dramatic."

"I intend to resign from lying," I clarified. "The position has worn me out. I'd like to apply for honest work. I understand the pay is terrible."

Wren snorted. Elodie looked at me for a long moment with that expression she has, the one that's either contempt or its opposite, and said, "The Kestrel needs a surveyor anyway. River charts. Nobody's ever properly mapped the middle Vell shoals."

It took me a moment to understand she was offering me a job. It took me longer to understand that offering me a job was her way of saying what she couldn't say, which was roughly: *you're coming with us, you're ours now, don't make it strange.*

"I accept," I said, "pending medical attention."

"Pending medical attention," she agreed, and shouldered her pack, and led us down toward the river.

* * *

### Elodie

We hit the valley floor at dusk with the snow coming down thick and straight, no wind, the kind of fall that buries a trail in an hour and a landmark in three. The Kestrel sat where we'd left her, moored in the willows above the shingle, wearing four inches of white like a woman pretending not to have been waiting up.

Getting aboard was its own small epic—Ferro lowered over the rail on a cargo sling, protesting weakly that this was undignified, Wren and I hauling the mules' loads down and turning the animals loose for whoever found them, because there was no grazing them home and no strength left to lead them. Then the stove lit, the kettle on, and the three of us standing in the Kestrel's cabin steaming like laundry, and nobody saying anything, because there's a kind of tired that makes talk redundant.

Ferro went down onto the bunk like a felled tree. I pulled his boot off and felt the heat radiating off him from a foot away. The wound in his shoulder had gone angry-red at the edges, and when I peeled the dressing the smell was wrong—not rot yet, but rehearsing for it.

"He needs a doctor," Wren said. She didn't say *soon*. She didn't have to. "Where's the nearest one?"

I looked at the chart pinned above the desk. Downriver. Of course downriver. Everything was downriver; that was the deal with rivers.

"Vellport's got the best surgeon on the Vell," I said. "Old Marta Okonkwo. Took a bullet out of me once and lectured me the entire time."

"And Vellport is..."

"Harrow waters. Deep ones." I traced the channel with one finger. "Two days' run, and we'd pass the toll station at Gray's Bend, the picket boats below the lock, and whatever Dain's surviving friends have floating by now. They know we're coming down eventually. It's the only way down."

"So we sail into the wolf's mouth," Wren said, "with the evidence sewn into your coat, and a wanted surveyor, and me."

"When you say it like that it sounds reckless."

"How would you say it?"

I looked at Ferro, breathing shallow and fast on the bunk. At Wren, standing at the chart table with her father's balance in her stance and her jaw set exactly the way Tomas Reyes's jaw must have set when they told him his claim belonged to someone else. At the stove ticking, the kettle beginning its low note, the small warm machinery of a home I hadn't known I was building.

I'd spent ten years learning every trick the river had. Time to find out if the river had one left.

"We wait for full dark," I said, "and then we run it black—no lights, no smoke, engine at half so she's quiet. There's a side channel below Gray's Bend that isn't on the Syndicate charts because the men who draw their charts have never been drunk enough to try it." I tapped the blank stretch of river below the bend. "But I have."

Wren grinned, and it was Tomas's grin, and it was aimed at me like a lamp.

"Captain Voss," she said, "you're going to get us killed."

"No," I said, unpinning the chart and spreading it flat between us. "I'm going to get us *home*. Killing us is the Harrows' department. Try to keep the departments separate—it matters to them."

Outside, the snow kept falling, patient and total, erasing the mountain behind us and the river ahead and every trace of the road between. Somewhere up in that white silence was a glacier closing its corridor, a dead man's vindication riding against my ribs, and a warrant with my name on it gathering speed down the telegraph lines.

Full dark was two hours off. I put the kettle on again, and we planned the crime.
