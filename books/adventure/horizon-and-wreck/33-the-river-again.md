## 33. The River Again

The Kestrel was exactly where we'd left her, which surprised me more than it should have. I'd half expected to find her burned, or stripped to the ribs, or flying a Harrow pennant and a note pinned to the pilothouse door that read *nice boat*. Instead she sat at her mooring below Coldharbor like a good dog waiting for a master who didn't deserve her, snow gathering on her awning in a thin white shroud, and I felt something crack open in my chest that I decided not to examine too closely, because examining things is how you end up crying on a riverbank with two mules and a dying surveyor.

"Is that—" Wren started.

"That's her."

"She's smaller than I imagined."

"Everything's smaller than you imagine," Ferro said, from where he hung off his saddle like laundry left out past its season. "The mountains were bigger. The glacier was bigger. Boats are always smaller." He'd been making jokes for three days, which worried me more than moaning would have. Men who joke through fever are either tougher than iron or sicker than they're letting on, and I'd watched Augustin Ferro's color go from weathered brown to the gray of old dishwater somewhere around the second moraine.

We got him down the last stretch of trail mostly by letting him lean on us and lie about how well he was doing. Wren took one arm. I took the other. The mules followed out of what I can only assume was spite, because they had been spiteful the entire journey and I saw no reason for them to change character now that it was over.

The first snow came down while we were still fifty yards from the water. Not the fat lazy flakes of early autumn but the small dry kind, the kind that means business, the kind that says *I'm just the advance scout; wait until you meet my brothers.* It hissed against the river, which was still running black and fast, and it settled on Ferro's shoulders and did not melt.

That's when I knew we weren't going to have time to be careful.

* * *

Getting him aboard was ugly. The Kestrel's gangplank was built for passengers with luggage and opinions about their cabins, not for a half-conscious man and the two women dragging him. We got him up it anyway, by increments, Wren pulling and me pushing and Ferro contributing a running commentary of apologies that would have been charming if he hadn't kept apologizing to the plank itself.

Below decks, I put him in my own bunk, because it was the warmest berth on the boat and because I have never once in my life slept when there was work to do anyway. His skin was hot as a stove plate. The wound in his side—the bullet had gone through, thank whatever gods bother listening to river people, clean through—but clean through doesn't mean clean. Three days of mountain cold and bad bandaging had done their work, and the flesh around the entry was angry red streaking up toward his ribs like something crawling.

Wren stood over him with her sleeves rolled up, and I watched this girl who four months ago couldn't tie off a cleat hitch take stock of an infected wound with the flat practical eyes of a woman twice her age.

"We need carbolic," she said. "And fresh linen. And someone who knows more than I do, which is nearly anyone."

"I know a doctor," I said.

Her head came up. "In Coldharbor?"

"Coldharbor's got a bonesetter and a drunk with a medical degree from somewhere he won't name. Neither one's touching this." I looked at Ferro, whose breathing had gone shallow and quick, the way breathing goes when the body starts quietly panicking about infection before the mind admits anything's wrong. "There's a real surgeon in Vellport. Dr. Anselm. Trained in the capital. He pulled a bullet out of my shoulder six years ago and only lectured me twice."

"Vellport." Wren said the word the way you'd say *the bottom of the lake.*

I said nothing, because there was nothing to say that wasn't obvious. Vellport was Harrow country. Had been for years—since before my debt, since before any of it. The Syndicate owned the docks, the warehouses, the assayer's office clerks, half the aldermen, and at least one judge whose name appeared in enough ledgers to wallpaper a cabin. Sailing the Kestrel into Vellport harbor was not sailing into a friendly port where a warrant might be waiting. It was sailing into the wolf's mouth and asking the wolf to please hold still while we borrowed its teeth.

"You could run him upriver instead," Wren said. "To the territorial capital. There are doctors—"

"Four days' steam, if the ice lets us, and it won't. First snow's here. Give it another day and the Vell starts thinking about freezing, and then nobody's going anywhere except by sled, and sleds don't do surgery." I crouched by the bunk. Ferro's eyes opened partway, found me, and produced the ghost of his usual insufferable smile.

"Captain," he murmured. "Are we discussing me?"

"Lie still."

"I'm excellent at lying. It's one of my qualifications." His eyes drifted shut again. "You should know... whatever you decide... I've made my peace with being a burden. I've had practice. Years of it."

"Shut up, Augustin."

"Yes, Captain."

Wren and I went up to the pilothouse, because some conversations you can't have over a sick man without him hearing every word, and Ferro had ears like a river rat even at death's door. Especially at death's door. Dying men listen hardest; it's the living who don't pay attention.

* * *

The pilothouse smelled like cold iron and old woodsmoke, and through the windows the snow was coming down harder, erasing the far bank, erasing the sky, erasing everything except the river right in front of us, which is the way I like the world best: one problem at a time, visible, navigable.

"He'll die if we don't get him to Anselm," Wren said. Not a question. She'd done the arithmetic already. She was good at arithmetic—it was why I'd handed her the books back in chapter whatever-this-was of our shared disaster.

"He'll die if we do, possibly. Depends on whether the Harrows ask questions first and shoot later, or shoot first and skip the questions."

"What's the likelihood they know we're coming?"

I leaned on the wheel, which is where I do my best thinking, or at least my most comfortable thinking, which amounts to the same thing on a long winter night. "Dain survived the mountain. You saw him walk onto that ice. A man like that doesn't crawl home to die—he crawls home to report. And what does he report? That the Reyes documents exist, that they're proof of everything the Syndicate buried twenty years ago, and that the woman carrying them owes them money and has a boat." I tapped the wheel. "He doesn't need to catch us on the river. He just needs to be standing on the dock in Vellport when we arrive, with friends."

"So we don't go to Vellport."

"We go to Vellport."

She stared at me. Snow ticked against the glass. Down below, faintly, Ferro coughed—a wet sound I didn't like at all.

"Explain that to me," Wren said, "because from where I'm standing, sailing into Harrow waters with stolen evidence and a wanted woman looks less like courage and more like suicide with extra steps."

"It's not stolen evidence. It's evidence that was stolen *from* your father, and we're returning it to the law. There's a difference, and the difference is everything." I turned to face her fully. "And I'll tell you the other difference. Every trick I know—and I know all of them—I learned running cargo for the Harrows or hiding from the Harrows. Blockade-running, false manifests, dead drops, running dark at night with no lights, knowing which channel wardens are bought and which ones are merely lazy. Six years I've been invisible on this river. Invisible was the whole point. Invisible was the *strategy*." I heard my own voice do something strange. "But invisible doesn't save Augustin Ferro. Invisible gets us to some quiet cove where he dies politely, out of everyone's way, and the documents rot in an oilskin packet, and Tomas Reyes stays a thief forever, and you spend your life knowing the truth and never able to say it out loud."

"That's not—"

"It is exactly what happens. I've watched it happen. I *was* it happening, for six years." I looked out at the snow. "Courage isn't what I did on the glacier, Wren. That was just stubbornness with scenery. Courage is pointing this boat at the place I've spent six years avoiding, because the man bleeding in my bunk deserves a surgeon and your father deserves his name back, and neither of those things is going to happen anywhere safe."

Wren was quiet a long moment. Then she said, in a voice much steadier than mine felt, "Then we'd better plan it properly."

And that—that right there—is why I keep her.

* * *

We planned it properly.

The Kestrel, for those who haven't had the pleasure, is a hundred and ten feet of shallow-draft sternwheeler with a hold big enough for forty tons of freight and a hull that draws barely three feet of water when empty. She is not fast. She is not pretty. What she is, is *mine*, top to bottom, and I know every board of her the way you know your own hands—which means I know things about her that no Harrow clerk has ever bothered to learn.

First: papers. A boat arriving in Vellport needs a manifest, and a manifest needs cargo, and cargo needs a story. We had, as it happened, actual legitimate freight—Ferro's survey instruments, the mules' tack, leftover provisions, and a fair quantity of silver ore samples Wren had insisted on bringing down from the cache, wrapped in flour sacks, because she said her father taught her never to leave proof lying on a mountain. Ore samples make a fine manifest entry. *Prospecting outfit, returning from upper Vell, consigned to the territorial assay office.* Boring. Boring is armor.

Second: timing. Vellport's dock wardens changed shift at dusk, and the dusk shift was run by a man named Oskar Pell whom I had known since he was a deckhand stealing tobacco from my freight. Oskar was not corrupt. Oskar was *tired*, which is better, because tired men check seals and count bales and never once look at faces. We'd arrive at dusk.

Third: the documents themselves. Those did not travel in the hold, or in my cabin, or anywhere a searching hand might reasonably look. They traveled on my body, sewn into the lining of my coat by Wren, who turned out to sew like a surgeon and swore like a stevedore while doing it. If they took the boat, they wouldn't find the reason for taking it.

"And if they search *you*?" Wren asked, biting off thread.

"Then I'm a liar with a very good memory, and the truth dies with me, and you take the mule trail north and finish it yourself." I shrugged the coat on. It hung heavy on one side, like a conscience. "But they won't. Nobody searches the captain of a boat they think they already own."

"Do you believe that?"

"No," I said honestly. "But belief is for church. On the river we deal in probabilities, and the probability is that Corvus Dain is currently lying in some Harrow sickroom being stitched back together and hating every minute, and his masters are spending their energy hunting the documents, not the boat. We are, for perhaps two more days, beneath their notice." I rang the engine telegraph to slow ahead. "Let's stay there."

* * *

We cast off at dawn into falling snow, and I will tell you what it is to take a boat you love into water you fear.

The Vell in early winter is a different animal than the summer river. The current runs higher with the freeze-up push, hauling meltwater and broken shelf ice down from the headwaters, and the sandbars I could name in my sleep have all shifted a few feet, the way they do, so that every mark I trusted had to be re-earned. Ice pans came down in flotillas, gray plates the size of wagon wheels, knocking softly against the hull like fingers testing a door. The paddlewheel chewed through the thin stuff fine. The thick stuff we dodged, and I stood at the wheel for the whole first day without sitting, reading the water the way you read a face across a card table.

Wren took the deck watch. She'd learned the lead line on the way upriver and gotten frighteningly good at it, calling depths in a clear flat voice—"*mark twain*, quarter less three"—and I let her, because a crew needs a crew, and because every hour she spent learning my river was an hour she'd survive without me someday, though I didn't let myself finish that thought.

Ferro surfaced once, mid-afternoon, gray and sweating, and demanded to know if we were there yet.

"No."

"Good. I'm not done being embarrassing yet." Then he slept again, and Wren sat with him, and I drove the boat.

At dusk the second day, the lights of Vellport came up out of the snowfall, and I want you to understand what that looks like when you're sailing toward the thing you've feared for six years. The town sits in a bend of the river where the valley widens, and its lamps strung along the waterfront look almost gentle from the water—gold points trembling on the black current, smoke rising straight from a hundred chimneys, the assayer's office clock tower lit against the clouds. From out here it looks like safety. That's the trick of it. Every trap on this river looks like shelter until you're inside it.

"Dock warden's office, starboard side past the grain elevator," I said. "Oskar Pell. He'll wave us in. Whatever happens after that, nobody speaks to anyone official but me."

"And if Dain's there?"

"Then we improvise." I checked the coat lining with two fingers, feeling the stiff rectangle of paper against my ribs. "Improvising is also one of my qualifications."

The Kestrel slid in past the elevator, past the moored barges riding low with coal, past the Harrow warehouse with its painted sign and its lamps burning late, and I brought her alongside the public dock with a touch so soft the fenders barely sighed, because if I was walking into the wolf's mouth, I was going to do it with a clean landing.

Oskar Pell stood under the dock lamp with his ledger book and his lantern, older and tireder than I remembered, and he raised the light to read our name off the bow.

"Kestrel," he said. "Out of—"

"Upper Vell. Prospecting outfit, ore samples consigned to the assay office. Manifest's ready." I handed it down along with the fee, cash, counted flat. Our eyes met for half a second. His flicked—just barely—to the Harrow warehouse, where a lamp burned behind the office window, and then to me, and then he became extremely interested in his ledger.

"Manifest looks in order," he said loudly, for the benefit of anyone listening. "Tie off at berth four. Wardens come round at eight."

"Thank you kindly," I said, equally loudly, and tied off at berth four, directly across the waterfront from the Harrow offices, in full view of their lamplit window, because sometimes the boldest move is the one nobody believes you'd make.

Wren came down the gangplank beside me, coat buttoned to her chin, Ferro's arm over my shoulders as we half-carried him onto solid ground. The snow had thinned to a few drifting flakes. The assayer's clock began striking eight.

And from the shadow of the grain elevator, unhurried, a figure detached itself and fell into step behind us.

I didn't turn. I didn't speed up. I walked toward the assayer's office with a dying man on my shoulder and a syndicate's sins sewn into my coat, and I listened to the footsteps follow, and I counted them—one set, only one—and I thought: *all right, wolf. Let's see your teeth.*
