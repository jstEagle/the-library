## 34. Into Harrow Waters

The footsteps behind us belonged to a small man in a good coat, and good coats in Coldharbor meant one of two things: the assayer's office or the Syndicate, and lately those were the same address.

He caught up to us at the bottom of the assayer's steps, where Ferro's weight had become less a burden I was carrying and more a decision we were both making together, moment by moment. Wren shifted to take more of him without being asked. She'd gotten good at that on the mountain—reading where the load wanted to go before it went there.

"Captain Voss." The small man smiled like a door opening onto an empty room. "Mr. Pell, deputy assayer. We received word you'd be coming down off the ice."

*We received word.* From whom, I didn't ask. There was exactly one wire office in Coldharbor and exactly one family that treated it like a parlor.

"Then you know what I'm carrying," I said.

"I know what you *claim* to be carrying." He gestured upward, gracious as a hotel doorman. "If you'll come inside, we can begin the intake process. There are forms. There's always forms."

Ferro lifted his head off my shoulder. Fever had burned him down to wire and stubbornness, but his eyes still worked, and they were working now, moving from Pell's face to the office windows behind him—to the lamp burning late inside, and the second silhouette standing at the glass, and the way that silhouette turned away when it saw us looking.

"Ferro," I said quietly.

"I see them." His voice was gravel and ash. "Elodie. That's not a federal office anymore. Look at the plate on the door."

I looked. The brass plate read FEDERAL ASSAY OFFICE — COLDHARBOR DISTRICT, and beneath it, newer, brighter, screwed in over older screw holes: **UNDER SUPERVISION OF THE HOLLOW BROOK CONSOLIDATED**, which was the Harrow Syndicate's respectable name, the one they put on church donation plaques and headstones.

"They reorganized the districts last spring," Pell said pleasantly. "Efficiency measures. Now—if the packet?"

Wren's hand found my sleeve. Not pulling. Just anchoring.

Here is a thing the river taught me before anything else taught me anything: when a door closes politely, don't knock. Walk around the back. Every building has a back, and every back has a weakness, and if it doesn't, the building isn't worth entering.

"No packet tonight," I said. "My surveyor needs a doctor before he needs a bureaucrat. Where's the nearest one who doesn't fly your colors?"

Pell's smile thinned by a degree. "The doctor's office keeps syndicate hours too, I'm afraid."

"Then he'll keep bleeding on syndicate time." I turned us all around, slow, unhurried, a woman with nowhere particular to be, and walked back toward the docks with my dying friend and my dead man's truth sewn into my coat lining, and I felt Pell's eyes on my back the whole way like a hand between my shoulder blades.

Behind us, the assayer's lamp went out.

* * *

The Kestrel sat low and tired at her mooring, snow gathering in the seams of her deck like the river itself trying to bury her early. I got Ferro into the cabin and into his bunk, and Wren built up the stove while I stood in the cold and did arithmetic.

Vellport was four days downriver. Three, if the ice held off and I pushed her the way I'd promised myself I never would again. The federal assayer for the whole territory sat in Vellport—a real one, appointed before the Harrows finished eating the map, an old man named Quill whom I'd once carried a crate of contraband oysters past and who had looked at me with the weary omniscience of a man who sees every boat and believes none of them.

And between here and there lay ninety miles of Harrow water.

They'd have word already. Pell would wire Vellport tonight, and Corvus Dain—who was alive, because men like Dain don't die on mountains, they just get recalled and refitted—would have every toll boat and hired gun from here to the delta watching the channel. They wouldn't be looking for a fight. They'd be looking for a *packet*, and they knew it was on my boat, because Pell knew, because the wire knew, because the whole rotten territory was one long nerve ending and I'd stepped on it.

"You're going to run it," Wren said from the cabin doorway. Not a question. She'd stopped asking questions somewhere on the glacier and started reading me instead, which was either love or a warning sign.

"I'm going to run it."

"With him burning up down there and no doctor until Vellport."

"With him burning up down there and no *honest* doctor anywhere closer." I came inside and shut the door against the snow. "There's a woman in Millbrook—Sera Oduya. Trained in the capital, came out here for the mining camps. She patched Ferro once before. If we stop, we lose the night, and if we lose the night, we lose the river."

"The river?"

"Flood tide comes up from the delta after midnight. Four hours of water running our way, deep enough to float the Kestrel over bars she couldn't cross against it. After that, we're crawling." I pulled the chart drawer open and spread the river charts across the table, weighting the corners with the salt cellar and a tin cup and Ferro's boot. "Dain will have boats at the narrows and the toll station at Gray Rock, probably a picket at the Splits. He'll expect us to hug the channel, because that's where the water is."

"And you won't."

"Wren." I looked up at her—seventeen years old, frost still in the seams of her braid, her father's patience sitting on her face like it had always lived there—and something in my chest did a thing I didn't have a name for. "Your father taught you to read rock. Who do you think taught me to read water?"

She sat down across from me. "Show me."

So I showed her. Ninety miles of river rendered in ink and memory: the Widow's Teeth, where the channel split three ways and only one split stayed honest after dark. Gray Rock and its toll lanterns, visible two miles upstream on a clear night, which meant they'd see us coming unless the night wasn't clear. The Splits, where the Vell braided out into six threads through drowned timber, any one of which could open a hull like a letter. And threading through all of it, marked in my own hand over the surveyor's faded lines, the things maps don't usually carry: *soft bar, shifts east in spring*. *Deadhead, half-submerged, moves after floods*. *Old ferry cable, rotted, still there*. *Coal seam seep—water smells of sulfur, trust your nose over your eyes*.

"This line here." Wren tapped a stretch below Gray Rock where I'd drawn nothing at all. "Why's it blank?"

"Because I've never run it."

"Why not?"

"Because the guidebook says it can't be run." I tapped the blank space. "But the guidebook was written by a man who drew the Vell from a desk in Vellport, and I've stood on the bank there in low water, and what he calls a wall of rocks is a wall with a door in it. Six feet wide, maybe seven. It only shows at flood tide, and it only stays honest for about twenty minutes, and if you're a foot wrong you become a story people tell in taverns."

"What's the story called?"

"*The Fool and the Door*," I said. "Or possibly just *the fool*."

Wren studied the chart the way she'd studied the glacier—with that flat, total attention that made me think Tomas Reyes had spent more evenings teaching his daughter than he'd ever known he was doing. Then she said, "Twenty minutes," and underlined it with one finger, and that was that. My crew had signed on.

Ferro woke once while we worked, enough to hear the plan, and laughed—a dry, cracked sound, half cough. "The Door," he said. "I surveyed that bank in '71. Told the company it was unrunnable."

"It is unrunnable."

"Mm." He closed his eyes. "Told them a lot of things. Get it right this time, Captain. For both of us."

* * *

We cast off at eleven, blacked down—lamp screens rigged, brass wrapped in sacking, even the bell muffled with a wool sock, so the Kestrel went downriver looking like what the night already contained: nothing. The snow had quit. The sky had cracked open in the west, and the cold that came down through the crack was the kind that made wood sing and iron bite, and I loved it, because clear and bitter meant the flood tide would run strong and the Harrow watchmen would be huddled over their stoves wishing they were anywhere else.

Wren took the wheel first. I'd taught her the basics above the treeline, jokingly, the way you teach a child card tricks, and she'd absorbed it the way she absorbed everything—silently, completely, and then handing it back to you improved. She stood the helm with her weight forward, reading the water ahead by the way it wrinkled, calling the current lines in a low voice like she was praying to them.

"Dark water left of the point."

"Steer for it. Dark means deep, white means lying."

"I know, Elodie."

Right. Of course she knew. Her father had been a prospector; prospectors read water too, or they died thirsty.

We ran the first twenty miles clean. The flood picked us up below the Splits and carried us like a rumor, engine at half-throttle, hull whispering, the drowned timber sliding past close enough to touch. I stood at the rail and let the river do the work and felt—for one dangerous hour—like a woman being forgiven.

Then Gray Rock.

I smelled the toll station before I saw it: coal smoke on the wind, and under it lamp oil, and under that the particular wet-wool smell of men who've been waiting in the cold too long and have started to hate the wait. Two lanterns hung from the station gallery, and out in the channel a chain-lighter sat athwart the narrows with a swivel gun bolted to her foredeck, because the Harrows had never met a problem that more iron couldn't improve.

"They'll have a signal wire to the picket below," I murmured. "One flare and we're lit up from here to the delta."

"So we don't give them a flare."

"We don't give them a *reason*." I checked the flood tide against my pocket watch, then the moon, then the smoke bending off the station chimney—wind out of the northwest, steady, and thickening. Downriver of the station, where the valley pinched, fog was already pooling off the warm water like breath on glass. It would build. In an hour it would swallow the banks. In two it would swallow the lanterns.

An hour was a long time to sit motionless within sight of a swivel gun.

Unless.

"Wren," I said. "How do you feel about theater?"

* * *

The Kestrel's dinghy had been hanging on her davits for six years, mostly as a storage shelf for rope. We lowered it now with the muffled blocks complaining, and into it went a lantern, a slow-burning fuse made of powder fuse and patience, and every scrap of driftwood and old canvas we could spare. I lashed the tiller amidships, set the little craft on the flood tide's shoulder aimed straight down the middle of the narrows, lit the lantern, and let her go.

From two miles off, in rising fog, a small boat carrying a light looks exactly like a steamboat's gig. And a gig means a landing party, and a landing party means the boarding crew's attention goes exactly where you want it: forward, and down.

We gave them five minutes. Then, right on schedule, the powder fuse spat, and the driftwood pile flared up green and ugly—copper salts, salvaged from Ferro's old kit, the trick every smuggler learns and no honest person needs—and the dinghy became a burning ship in the fog, and every man on that chain-lighter saw his career flash before his eyes.

Shouting. A bell. The swivel gun's crew scrambling. Someone firing a flare anyway, high and red, that bloomed over the fog and lit nothing but cloud.

And the Kestrel slid past their starboard side at fifty yards, blacked down, engine at a whisper, so close I could hear a man on the lighter yell "*It's drifting, it's just burning driftwood, hold your fire*" while his officer screamed the opposite, and I stood at the rail in the dark and tipped my hat to neither of them.

Wren was shaking when we cleared the narrows—not fear. I recognized it. It was the laugh she was refusing to have yet, banked like a stove fire for later.

"That," she managed, "was *awful*."

"It was *effective*."

"It was awful and effective and I want to learn how to do it forever."

God help me, so had I, at her age. I put a hand on her shoulder and steered us on into the fog.

* * *

The fog held for thirty miles, and then the river decided we'd been comfortable long enough.

Below the old ferry crossing, the wind swung south, warm and wet, and ate the fog in twenty minutes, and there was the rest of the night laid out bare under a hard-rising moon: the channel glittering, the banks sharp as cut paper, and every boat on ninety miles of water suddenly able to see us.

Including, forty minutes later, the one behind us.

I heard it before I saw it—a different engine note than ours, higher, hungrier, the sound of a Harrow patrol launch running flat out. When I looked back there were two lights chasing our wake, closing, and a voice carrying flat across the water through a speaking trumpet:

"*Steamboat Kestrel! Heave to! This is a syndicate toll inspection!*"

Syndicate toll inspection. The honesty was almost refreshing.

"Ferro's below?" I asked.

"Asleep, or performing sleep," Wren said. "Elodie, they're faster than us."

"On open water, yes." I took the wheel from her. "But the river isn't open. It never was. Hold on to something and don't tell me afterward how scared you were. I don't want to know. Ignorance is how captains sleep."

The Widow's Teeth opened ahead—the channel splitting three ways around a spine of rock, the honest passage marked by the Harrows' own beacon, because they needed the traffic to tax it. The other two splits were graveyard water, shoals and sweepers, unmarked, unmapped, unmourned.

I took the middle split.

Not the graveyard—there's brave and there's stupid, and I'd spent my whole life confusing them, but not tonight, not with Ferro below and this girl beside me. The middle split was legal-ish, difficult, and mine: a quarter mile of water I'd run maybe forty times, at night, in weather, and once upside down emotionally, which is its own kind of hazard. It had a rhythm. Sandbar, swing, rock, thread-the-gap, and every beat of it I knew the way you know a song you learned by breaking something.

The launches followed. Of course they did. They had the chart, the beacon, and contempt, and contempt is the most expensive thing you can carry on a river.

The first launch hit the bar at the second bend doing better than twelve knots. I heard it from a half mile off—that hollow, grinding *shudder* through the water, the engine note climbing into panic, and then the sound every riverman knows and dreads: props chewing mud. By the time we rounded the third bend he was still there, aground, lit up now with his own distress lanterns, and his partner had slowed to fish him off, and the gap between us and them went from four minutes to twenty in the space of one beautiful, terrible crunch.

"Did you—" Wren started.

"I didn't do anything. He did it himself. I just knew where the river would let him make his mistake." I handed her back the wheel. "That's the whole secret, if you ever write it down. You don't beat the river. You just arrive earlier than the other fellow's arrogance."

* * *

We came down on Vellport in the gray beginning of the fourth morning, the city smelling of coal smoke and coffee and money changing hands, the harbor crusted with shipping the way a dock pilings are crusted with barnacles. Ferro was worse—I'd lie to you if it would help either of us, but he was worse, flushed and muttering, and Dr. Oduya's name was written on a card in my pocket along with directions, because Wren had gone ashore at a fuel stop against my orders and gotten them from a woman who owed me, and I hadn't even been angry, because that's what a crew is: people who disobey you correctly.

The federal assay office stood on Customs Row, granite-faced, flying the territorial flag, and I brought the Kestrel in against the commercial dock below it with the documents strapped against my ribs where they'd ridden since the glacier, warm from my body, damp with it.

Wren stood beside me at the rail, watching the office windows.

"Once we hand it over," she said, "it stops being ours. His. Mine."

"It stops being hidden. That's not the same thing." I looked at her. "Nothing about what happens next belongs to us, Wren. That's rather the point. Your father spent his whole life with the truth locked in a box. We're delivering the key."

She nodded slowly. Then: "Whatever happens—we come back to the boat. All of us. That's the deal."

"That's the deal."

We went up Customs Row together, the three of us, Ferro walking between us on pure spite, and the assayer's clerk took the oilskin packet with the reverence of a man handling something radioactive, and old Assayer Quill himself came out of the back room in shirtsleeves to look at the claim documents, and I watched his face do something complicated and quiet as he read the forged signatures, the dates, the transfer papers that proved the Reyes claim had been stolen twice—once with ink, once with a rifle.

Quill looked up at me over his spectacles. "Where did you get these?"

"A dying man's coat," I said. "And a glacier's keeping."

He nodded once, slowly, and reached for the telegraph key on his desk—and that was when Wren, at the window, went very still, and said, in a voice I'd only heard from her once before, on the moraine, with Dain's men coming:

"Elodie. The notice board."

I crossed to the glass. Pinned there, fresh, the ink barely dry, was a circular from the territorial marshal's office, wired upriver sometime in the night:

**WANTED FOR QUESTIONING — CLAIM-JUMPING, THEFT OF SYNDICATE PROPERTY: E. VOSS, MASTER OF STEAMER KESTREL. LAST SEEN COLDHARBOR. APPROACH WITH CAUTION. ARMED.**

Armed. Well. They'd gotten one thing right.

Quill's hand hovered over the telegraph key. Behind me, Ferro leaned against the doorframe, gray as ash, and started, very softly, to laugh.

"Well, Captain," he said. "You got the truth to Vellport. Question is whether it gets out before you do."
