## 35. The Assayer's Office

The assayer's office in Vellport smelled of ink, lamp oil, and the particular despair of men who had spent thirty years stamping other people's dreams into ledgers.

I noticed all this from the doorway, because Elodie had stopped so suddenly that Ferro's mule—yes, we still had one mule, and yes, she was named after a river hazard, because Elodie Voss did not believe in good luck, only in bad luck that hadn't happened yet—walked into her back and complained about it at length.

"Problem?" I asked.

"No," said Elodie, who was lying, which was how I knew there was a problem. "It's just been a while."

"A while since what?"

"Since I've been inside a building where they write down your name."

I looked at her. She looked at the street behind us with the particular attention of a woman counting exits. Behind her, Ferro sat propped against the hitching rail looking like something the mountain had chewed on and returned with apologies—a gray-faced, fever-bright version of the man who'd once surveyed half these ranges, held upright mostly by stubbornness and my shoulder.

"He needs a doctor," I said. "And you need to hand over those documents before you talk yourself out of it. Both of those things are on the same side of that door."

"That's what worries me," Elodie said, but she went in.

* * *

The assayer was a small man named Prewitt with spectacles thick enough to start fires and hands that trembled until they touched paper, at which point they became the steadiest things in the territory. He read the claim documents—the iron box's contents spread across his desk like the autopsy of a crime, which is more or less what they were—and his face went through a sequence of expressions I'll be describing to my grandchildren someday, if I ever have any and if they ever ask, which they won't, because nobody asks prospectors' daughters anything interesting.

First: professional boredom. Then: confusion. Then: the slow, dawning horror of a man realizing the paperwork in front of him could end careers, including possibly his own if he handled it wrong.

"These are Harrow Syndicate filing seals," he said finally. "Originals. With the Reyes name struck through and re-entered under Harrow holdings." His finger hovered over the date. "This predates their charter amendment by four years. They couldn't have legally held this claim when they filed it."

"They knew," Elodie said. "They took it off my—" She stopped. Off my father's body, she'd been about to say, or maybe off my father's life, and either way the sentence wasn't going to survive the trip out of her mouth. "They took it from Tomas Reyes. Killed him for it, near as we can tell. The prospector's affidavit in there is signed by three witnesses upriver who saw Harrow men asking after him two days before he turned up shot."

Prewitt took off his spectacles, polished them, put them back on, and looked at the documents again as though hoping they'd become less true. They didn't. Paper has a way of doing that—staying exactly as true as it was when you wrote it, no matter how inconvenient the intervening years have made it.

"This will go to the marshals," he said. "Territorial office. I'll wire tonight." Then, with the caution of a man who had survived thirty years in Vellport by knowing which questions not to ask: "You found these where?"

"On a glacier," I said, "at the top of a silver vein my father hid from the people who stole it from him."

Prewitt looked at me over his spectacles. "You're the daughter, then."

"I'm the daughter."

He nodded slowly, and then he did something I didn't expect: he stood up, came around the desk, and shook my hand with both of his, like I was somebody. Like I was the daughter of a man whose name had been dragged through every mudflat between here and Coldharbor for twelve years, and who had turned out to be right the whole time.

"Your father filed an original claim in '61," he said quietly. "I remember the paperwork. It was beautiful work. Clean metes and bounds. Then it just... vanished into the Harrow files, and when I asked, I was told I'd misremembered." He let go of my hand. "I didn't misremember."

* * *

They wired the marshals from the telegraph office next door, because Prewitt said the Syndicate had eyes on the assayer's line and he trusted the telegraph girl at the central office the way you trust a person who once told you, unprompted, that your hat made you look like a bankrupt undertaker. Honest, in other words, and immune to flattery.

Elodie sent the message herself. I watched her write it out in block letters, careful as a schoolgirl: *REYES CLAIM DOCUMENTS AUTHENTICATED STOP FORGERY EVIDENCE ENCLOSED STOP REQUEST MARSHAL PRESENCE VELLPORT STOP.*

She hesitated before signing it. I saw the hesitation—I'd gotten good at seeing them, the little flinches where Elodie Voss decided whether to be visible or not. For eleven years she'd been *the Kestrel's captain*, *ma'am*, *that riverwoman*, everything except a full name spoken aloud in a room with a window. Now she wrote **ELODIE VOSS** at the bottom of a telegram that would be read by federal officers, and her pen pressed hard enough to nearly tear the form.

"There," she said, handing it over. "That's the first time I've signed my whole name to anything official since—" She didn't finish. She never finished that sentence; I'd learned not to wait for it.

"You could've used a false name," I pointed out. "You bought our entire outfit under three different ones. One of them was 'Mrs. Cornelius Blatch,' which I want noted was deeply unconvincing."

"Mrs. Blatch was perfectly convincing."

"The clerk asked if Mr. Blatch would be joining us and you said he was dead, ma'am, and then you looked so pleased about it that the clerk wrote down *suspicious* in his little book. I saw him do it."

Elodie almost smiled. It was the first almost-smile since the moraine, since Ferro had taken the bullet meant for her, since four days in a cave listening to him breathe wrong. "Well," she said. "Maybe I'm getting out of practice."

* * *

We got Ferro to Dr. Halloran's surgery by bribing the doctor with the promise of a story he wouldn't believe, delivered by a woman he'd recognize. Halloran dug the bullet out—it had gone through the meat of Ferro's shoulder and lodged against bone, which the doctor called lucky and Ferro, half-conscious on the table, called *typical*. The fever was the danger now. Halloran said words like *sepsis* and *if it reaches the blood* and *keep him warm and keep him drinking*, and then he charged us an amount of money that suggested he thought we'd robbed the mint instead of a glacier.

Ferro drifted in and out. When he surfaced, he talked. Mostly he apologized—to me, to Elodie, once, memorably, to the mule—and once, in a voice stripped down to the boards, he said: "I told them the claim was worthless. I signed it. Twelve years, Wren. Your father spent twelve years being called a thief because I wanted to keep my license and my reputation, and I lost both anyway, and he lost everything." His eyes found mine, fever-bright and terrible. "The map was real. I knew it was real. That's why they paid me to bury it."

"I know," I said.

"You should hate me."

"I'm saving it," I told him. "For when you're well enough to properly appreciate it."

He laughed, which turned into coughing, which turned into Halloran shooing me out. But as I left, he caught my sleeve. "Your father taught you the scree tricks," he said. "The way you read rock. Nobody learns that from books."

"No," I said. "Nobody does."

He nodded, satisfied, and went back under.

* * *

Here is what I knew about my father, as of that evening, sitting in the surgery's kitchen drinking coffee that could have stripped paint:

He was a careful man. He filed beautiful claims. He taught his daughter to read talus slopes and meltwater and the color of stone that meant silver underneath, and he did it casually, as games, so that I'd never know I was being taught—so that no one watching would know either. He left Coldharbor in disgrace rather than fight the Harrows with the only weapon he had, which was a seven-year-old girl. And then he spent twelve years alone in the high country keeping his claim alive the only way left to him: by making sure nobody could find it, and making sure that someday, somehow, someone would.

He'd drawn that map for a stranger. Not even a stranger—for a riverboat captain he'd known for an hour, dying in the reeds, because she was the best navigator on the Vell and because she was going north and because sometimes a dying man doesn't get to choose his messenger.

He'd tried to come home first. That was the part that kept catching in my chest like a fishhook. In Coldharbor, old Mrs. Tannet had told us he'd stood outside our house one night, years back—she'd seen him from her window, just standing there in the dark looking at the lit windows, and then gone again. He'd been twenty miles away the whole time. He'd watched the light in the kitchen where I was doing lessons, and he hadn't knocked, because knocking would have brought the Harrows down on us both.

I set the coffee down because my hands were doing something embarrassing.

"Wren?" Elodie was in the doorway. She'd developed a habit, since the cave, of checking on me every hour or so—not saying much, just appearing, the way she used to appear at the rail when I was learning the wheel. Present without pressing. It was, I suspected, the only kind of family she knew how to be, and she was doing it deliberately, which made it worth more than the accidental kind.

"I'm fine," I said.

"You're crying into coffee that deserves better."

"It's terrible coffee. It should be cried into."

She came in and sat across from me, and for a while neither of us said anything, which was its own kind of conversation, the kind we'd gotten fluent in somewhere between the Widow's Teeth and the blue cathedral. Finally she said, "Tomorrow the marshals come. There'll be statements. Questions. You ready?"

"I've been rehearsing my testimony since Coldharbor."

"I know. You've been rehearsing it *out loud*. On the trail. At the mule."

"The mule is a captive audience."

"The mule is a hostile witness. She bit me twice."

"She bites everyone she distrusts. Take it up with her."

Elodie huffed something that was almost a laugh, and then went quiet in a way that made me look up. She was staring at her own hands—those rope-scarred, river-cured hands—and there was an expression on her face I hadn't seen before. Not fear, exactly. Something adjacent to hope, which for Elodie was worse.

"What?" I said.

"Nothing. It's just—if this works, if the marshals break the Syndicate open—" She stopped. Started again. "My debt dies with them. All of it. Eleven years of running ends tomorrow morning, and I don't—" Her jaw worked. "I don't entirely know who I am without the running."

"Yes, you do," I said. "You're the woman who ran the Kestrel through the Teeth in the dark with three strangers aboard because one of them asked her to. You're the woman who carried a dead man's promise up a glacier. You're the captain of the Reyes-Voss line, pending paperwork." I leaned forward. "You're the woman who's about to sign her name to a federal statement. Third time this week. At this rate you'll be introducing yourself to strangers by spring."

"That's a horrifying thought."

"I know. Endure it."

* * *

The marshals arrived at nine the next morning. There were four of them, led by a Marshal Okonkwo, a tall, unhurried woman with a voice like a courthouse door and eyes that catalogued everything in a room including, I suspected, the room's opinion of itself. She read the documents for an hour without speaking. Then she wired the territorial capital. Then she thanked us, took our statements—mine first, and I gave it clean and complete, every word of it rehearsed on a mule—and said, "The warrants will go out today. Dain and six associates. The Syndicate's charters will be suspended pending investigation."

Victory. Actual, documented, stamped-in-triplicate victory. My father's name cleared. Our names cleared. The silver ours by law, the Harrows finished, the whole rotten edifice coming down because a dying man had trusted the right riverboat captain.

I should have known better than to enjoy it.

It was Marshal Okonkwo's junior deputy who brought the telegram in, face carefully blank in the way of young lawmen delivering bad news to superiors. Okonkwo read it. Her expression didn't change, but something behind it did—the way water changes behind ice.

"Well," she said. "It appears Mr. Corvus Dain is alive."

"We assumed," Elodie said. "He escaped the moraine with a mule and most of our food."

"He escaped considerably further than that." Okonkwo set the telegram down on Prewitt's desk and turned it so we could read it. "This came through the Harrow relay at Graywater Junction an hour ago. Dain reached their line station last night. He's wired the territorial marshal's office directly."

The telegram was short. Dain had always been short; it was part of what made him frightening, that economy, that refusal to waste a word on you. It read:

*ELODIE VOSS AND ACCOMPLICES SEIZED REYES PACKET BY FORCE STOP CLAIM-JUMPING THEFT ASSAULT UPON SYNDICATE AGENTS STOP FERRO AUGUSTIN CO-CONSPIRATOR KNOWN FORGER OF SURVEY RECORDS STOP REQUEST WARRANTS IMMEDIATE STOP CORVUS DAIN SPECIAL INVESTIGATOR HARROW SYNDICATE.*

There was a silence in that office you could have moored a boat in.

"He's naming *us*," I said. My voice came out strange. "He killed my father, he hunted us across a glacier, he shot Ferro on the moraine—and he's wiring in that *we're* the criminals?"

"He's wiring it to an office that has received, as of this morning, evidence that his employer forged federal filings and committed murder to conceal it," Okonkwo said. Her voice stayed level, but I watched her read the telegram a second time, and I understood what she was actually reading: not the accusations, but the timing. "Which means one of two things. Either Mr. Dain hasn't heard the news—which is unlikely, given that the Harrow relay carries commercial traffic—or he heard it and sent this anyway."

"Why would he send it anyway?" Elodie asked, though I could see her working it out even as she asked, the same way I was, the same cold arithmetic climbing up both our spines.

"Because a warrant," Okonkwo said, "is a faster instrument than an investigation. If a warrant for your arrest reaches a local constable before my report reaches the capital, you can be held indefinitely while the question of who's telling the truth gets sorted out. And the Harrows have a great many friends among local constables." She looked at Elodie, and there was something almost apologetic in it. "Ms. Voss. Is there anywhere in this territory where a Harrow-friendly constable might already know your face?"

Elodie was quiet for a long moment. When she answered, her voice was flat and river-calm, which I had learned was the sound of her being most afraid.

"Everywhere," she said. "Eleven years of paying cash and keeping my head down, and everywhere south of the divide knows the shape of me. Dain doesn't need them to believe him. He just needs one of them to hold me long enough."

Okonkwo nodded slowly. "Then here's what happens now. My report goes to the capital tonight, priority seal. The warrants Dain requested are technically valid until countermanded—that's the ugly machinery of the law, and I won't pretend otherwise. Which means that as of roughly"—she checked her watch—"two hours ago, there is a legal document in this territory describing you as a thief, and it will take days to unwind, and in those days every constable between here and the capital is entitled to detain you on sight."

"And Ferro can't travel," I said. "Not for weeks. Maybe longer."

"No," Okonkwo agreed. "He can't."

Another silence. This one had teeth in it.

"So we run," Elodie said. It wasn't a question, and it wasn't despair, either. It was the oldest sentence she knew, worn smooth as river stone from use. "We run, we hide, we wait for the countermand—"

"No," I said.

Everyone looked at me. Okonkwo raised an eyebrow. Elodie's face did something complicated.

"Wren—"

"No. Listen to me." I stood up, and I discovered I was angrier than I'd realized, anger arriving late the way it always did with me, after the fear had already done its work. "Eleven years of running is how we got here. Running is why my father never came home. Running is why you owe a debt to people who murder prospectors, why Ferro signed a lie, why Dain has been three steps behind us since the treeline. Every single disaster in this story started with somebody deciding that hiding was safer than standing still." I pointed at the telegram. "He thinks we'll run. He's *counting* on it. A moving target with a warrant out is guilty until proven nothing, and he knows the country better than we do now, because he's got the Harrow relay network feeding him our position every time we show our faces at a dock."

"And the alternative?" Elodie asked quietly.

"The alternative is we stay exactly where we are, in a federal assayer's office, under a federal marshal's eye, with the documents that hang him sitting in a locked cabinet ten feet away. Let him come to us. Let him explain to Marshal Okonkwo, in person, why a special investigator for a syndicate under federal suspension is chasing a dead man's daughter across a glacier."

Okonkwo was smiling. It was a small smile, and slightly terrifying. "The girl has a point," she said. "Though I'd note that I cannot legally guarantee your safety if Dain arrives with a valid warrant and friends."

"You can't," I agreed. "But he doesn't know how many friends *we* have."

"Neither do I," said Elodie.

"River people," I said. "Deckhands, lockkeepers, the widow who runs the fuel dock at Millbrook, the telegraph girl who hates hats. Everybody you ever paid in cash and never lied to, Captain. Turns out that's a longer list than the Harrows' list. Turns out honesty compounds."

Elodie looked at me for a long moment. Outside, Vellport went about its morning—gulls screaming over the fish market, a steam whistle, the ordinary music of a town that didn't yet know its oldest rot had been exposed. Somewhere in that noise, a telegram was traveling south with her name on it, calling her a thief.

"Third time this week," she said at last, and there was something in her voice I'd never heard there before—not defeat, not flight, but the thing on the far side of both. "Signing my name to trouble."

"Fourth," I said. "You signed the provisioning ledger too."

"Mrs. Blatch signed that."

"Mrs. Blatch is done, Captain. Retire her." I held out my hand, palm up, the way you offer a rail to someone crossing a gangplank. "Stay. Stand. Let them come."

Elodie Voss looked at my hand. She looked at the door, the way she always did, counting exits out of habit. And then—for the first time since I'd known her—she stopped counting, took my hand, and turned her back to the door entirely.

"All right," she said. "Let him come."

Somewhere south of the divide, Corvus Dain was reading the same river we were. And for the first time, I understood, we weren't fleeing down it.

We were waiting at the bend.
