## 36. Trial by Testimony

I have testified before. Twice, in fact—once as an expert witness in a boundary dispute where I was paid handsomely to be vague, and once at my own licensing hearing, where I was not paid at all and was anything but vague. Both times I lied by degrees, the way a surveyor does: never a false line, only a slightly generous tolerance.

This time there would be no tolerances. This time I intended to stand up in front of a territorial marshal, a federal assayer, and whatever remained of Vellport's respectable citizenry and tell the truth so plainly it would end me.

I woke before dawn on the day of the hearing because Ferro's body, even ruined Ferro's body, still kept surveyor's hours. The bullet had gone through my shoulder in July; it was nearly October now, and the doctor in Harrow territory—a nervous man who took our coin without meeting anyone's eyes—had dug the slug out and pronounced me "lucky." He had said it the way you'd say *drowned* or *bankrupt*. Lucky meant alive. It did not mean whole.

The Kestrel lay moored at the Vellport public dock, which felt like sleeping in a church vestibule. Elodie had wanted a quieter berth, somewhere downriver among the coal barges, but Wren had argued for the public dock with the particular stubbornness she inherited from a father she'd spent most of her life resenting. "We're not hiding anymore," she'd said. "That's the whole point."

Elodie had looked at her for a long moment—the look of a woman watching her own philosophy come back at her from a younger mouth—and then thrown the bowline to the dockhand without a word.

So we slept in the light. And in the gray morning, I sat up in my bunk below decks, tested the shoulder against its sutures, and listened to the river slap the hull while I rehearsed what I was about to do.

*My name is Augustin Ferro. In the spring of the year before last, I surveyed the Reyes claim in the Coldharbor range, and I filed a report that I knew to be false.*

There. That was the shape of it. Everything else was just weather around that stone.

* * *

The territorial courthouse in Vellport had been a granary in some earlier incarnation of the town, and it still smelled faintly of wheat and mouse. The hearing room held perhaps eighty souls on benches built for men half again their size, and every bench was full. Word travels fast when a syndicate falls. Faster than snowmelt.

Elodie wore her good coat—the one with the collar that almost hid the scar tissue on her neck—and sat with her hands folded like a woman attending someone else's funeral. Wren sat between us in a dress that had clearly been bought two days ago off a rack, tags removed hastily, one pin still holding the hem. She kept touching her pocket, where she'd stowed the iron box's contents in oilskin copies. The originals were already in the assayer's vault. She carried the copies the way other girls carried letters from sweethearts.

Dain sat across the aisle in irons, flanked by two marshals, and he watched us the way a hawk watches a snake—not hungry, exactly. Calculating. His face had been rearranged somewhat since the glacier; the mountain had taken its fee. One eye didn't open all the way anymore. I found I could not summon much pity about it.

The presiding officer was Marshal Corbett, a broad, gray man with the patient voice of someone who has spent thirty years listening to liars and has developed, accordingly, a magnificent capacity for silence. He opened the proceedings by reading the charges into the record, and when he reached *claim-jumping, theft, obstruction of commerce,* and heard his own clerk say *the accused, Elodie Voss,* I saw Elodie's jaw tighten. Her name spoken aloud in a room full of strangers. For years she'd have paid money to avoid that. Now it was read out like scripture and she couldn't do a thing but sit there.

Then they called witnesses.

* * *

Wren went second, after the assayer's clerk established provenance of the documents. I will tell you honestly: I expected her to shake. Seventeen years old, father dead two months, standing in front of a room that included the men who'd arranged his death. I expected tears, or fury, or both—the raw materials of a bad testimony.

What I got instead was a surveyor's dream.

She spoke slowly. She gave dates. When Corbett asked how she knew the documents were genuine, she didn't say *because they're my father's*—she said, "Because the hand matches letters he wrote me that I can produce, and because the claim numbers cross-reference to filings in the Coldharbor recorder's office, which your clerk has already pulled." She had spent three evenings in the assayer's back room doing exactly that cross-referencing, and it showed.

Then Corbett asked her about her father, and this is where she could have broken, and instead she did something better. She told the truth about him—all of it.

"Tomas Reyes left Coldharbor when I was eleven," she said. "Everyone in town told me he abandoned us. My mother believed it until she died. I believed it until two months ago." Her voice caught on *two months ago*, one small hitch, and then steadied. "He left because staying would have killed him. The Harrow Syndicate had taken his claim with forged papers, and when he protested, they arranged for a surveyor to file a report saying the vein was worthless. He couldn't prove otherwise, and he'd learned what happens to men who try. So he went into the high country and stayed there nine years, working the vein alone, keeping the proof alive, waiting for a way to bring it down without getting himself killed in the attempt." She paused. "It nearly worked."

Corbett let the silence sit. Then: "Nearly?"

"He was shot by Syndicate agents on the Vell River in August. He died aboard the steamboat Kestrel two days later, after giving his map and his evidence to its captain for delivery to me." She turned her head—not toward Dain, though she must have wanted to, but toward the marshal, toward the record itself. "I'm telling you this so the court understands those papers aren't a thief's trophies. They're a dead man's testimony. He just couldn't deliver it himself."

I have heard better oratory. I have never heard better truth-telling. Half the room had stopped breathing.

Dain's lawyer—an expensive, soft-handed man named Pell who smelled of bay rum—cross-examined for twenty minutes and got nowhere. He tried to suggest the girl had been coached. She said, "By whom? The captain who owes these people money? Ask her yourself." Which was either very brave or very reckless, and knowing Wren, both.

* * *

They called Elodie third, and here is what I learned about her that day, sitting six feet away and watching her lie for the first time in my presence by telling nothing but the truth.

Pell tried to hang the claim-jumping charge on her: *You possessed the map. You traveled to the claim. You extracted silver.* All true, all damning, if you squinted.

"Yes," Elodie said. "I possessed the map. A dying man traded it to me for a promise, and I kept the promise. I traveled to the claim because the map's landmarks described more than silver—they described documents hidden in the same cache, and I understood, once I'd seen them, that those documents weren't mine to keep or sell. They belonged to a dead man's daughter and to whatever court would finally listen." She unfolded her hands for the first time. "As for the silver—I never touched it. Miss Reyes holds title to that claim as of last week's filing, and every ounce of ore we brought down is catalogued and stored under her name. You're welcome to count it."

"You owed the Harrow Syndicate a substantial debt," Pell said. "Isn't it true you stood to gain its forgiveness by delivering the map to Mr. Dain?"

"It's true I was offered that," Elodie said. "Twice. On the second occasion, Mr. Dain informed me—in error, I believe—that the Syndicate had murdered Tomas Reyes. After which the offer lost its charm."

A ripple went through the benches. Corbett's gavel came down once, softly, the way you'd tap a keg to hear how full it was.

"And why should this court believe you refused?" Pell pressed. "A woman in your position—indebted, hunted, holding the single most valuable piece of paper in the territory—"

"Because," Elodie said, "if I'd sold it, I wouldn't be sitting here. I'd be on the Kestrel, downriver, under a different name, and none of you would ever have found me. Ask anyone on this river whether that's beyond my abilities." She looked at Corbett, not Pell. "I spent nine years being nobody, Marshal. It's easy work. What's hard is being somebody in front of a room like this one. I'm doing the hard thing. Draw your conclusions."

Corbett drew them later. But first—

* * *

"—the court will hear from Augustin Ferro, regarding the Reyes survey of the spring prior."

I stood. My shoulder announced itself. Eighty faces turned, and among them I found Elodie's eyes, and I want to record, for my own private honesty, that what passed between us was not encouragement. It was permission. *Say it*, her look said. *Or don't. But whichever you choose, choose it whole.*

"Marshal Corbett," I said, "before counsel questions me, I wish to make a statement. Voluntarily. Under oath."

Corbett raised an eyebrow but gestured me forward.

"I am a licensed surveyor—or I was, until the Territorial Board revoked my commission eighteen months ago for irregularities in my field reports. What the Board did not know, because I did not tell them, is the nature of the largest of those irregularities." I made myself look at the room, not the floor. Looking at floors is how cowards testify. "In the spring of the year before last, I was retained by agents of the Harrow Syndicate to survey the Reyes claim in the Coldharbor range. I surveyed it properly. I found the vein. It was rich—richer than the filings suggested. And then I met with Mr. Dain's people, and I accepted payment, and I filed a report stating the vein was exhausted, the assays marginal, the claim not worth contesting."

The word *worthless* hung in the wheat-and-mouse air.

"I knew it was false when I signed it. I knew what the report would be used for—to strip Tomas Reyes of his property through the courts rather than the gun, which the Syndicate considered tidier. I told myself it was one report, one old prospector, one transaction in a career of transactions. I told myself that for two years." I turned, finally, and looked at Dain. His one good eye regarded me with something almost like respect, which is the most obscene thing I witnessed that day, and I witnessed several candidates. "It was not one report. It was a man's life. It was a girl's childhood. It was nine years of exile in the ice, and a bullet on the Vell River, and every one of those things passes through my signature on its way to happening. I am not here as a witness for the defense. I am here as a witness for the prosecution, and if this court finds room in its judgment for a man who sold his pen, I ask only that it find less room than it finds for the men who bought it."

Silence. The kind you could survey.

Pell rose, bay rum and all, and did his best. "Mr. Ferro, you admit to perjury before this territory's institutions. Why should the court credit your testimony now? Perhaps you were paid by the defendants. Perhaps this confession is simply another transaction."

"It might be," I agreed pleasantly. "Check my accounts. I've been living on a riverboat eating salt pork for two months, and before that I was broke, which is why the Harrows' money tempted me in the first place. If someone paid me for this testimony, they paid me in beans." A ripple of something that wasn't quite laughter moved through the benches. "No, counselor. There's no version of this that profits me. I lose my reputation—which is already lost. I expose myself to criminal charges—which I deserve. The only thing this confession buys is the truth entering the record, and the truth entering the record is the only currency I've got left. I'm spending all of it."

Corbett asked me two questions—one about the date of the false filing, one about whether Dain had been present at the meeting where the report's purpose was discussed. I answered both precisely, with dates and figures, because precision is the only honesty I've ever been any good at, and I watched the marshal write *premeditation* in the margin of his notes without trying to hide it.

When I stepped down, Wren's hand found my sleeve—just briefly, just two fingers, there and gone. It was worth more than the Harrows ever paid me.

* * *

The verdicts came the following afternoon, and I'll render them quickly because verdicts are the least interesting part of any story.

The charges against Elodie Voss were dismissed in full. Corbett, delivering the finding, said something I intend to have engraved on my headstone whenever the mountain finishes collecting its fees from me: *"The court observes that the accused's liberty today rests entirely on the testimony of witnesses who had nothing material to gain and considerable to lose by speaking. Character, in this court's experience, is what people say about you when lying would be cheaper."*

Dain was remanded to face charges of murder, conspiracy, fraud, and a dozen counts I've forgotten. As the marshals walked him out, he stopped beside our bench. The guards allowed it—three seconds of it, no more.

"The river keeps everyone eventually," he said to Elodie. It wasn't a threat, quite. It was almost theological.

"So does the mountain," Elodie said. "But the river gives you a ride first. That's the difference between them, and it's the difference between you and me."

They took him away. I confess I watched until the door closed, looking for grief in myself and finding only relief, which is its own kind of indictment, and I've decided to live with it.

* * *

That evening we sat on the Kestrel's roof deck with a bottle of passable whiskey and the first hard frost forming on the railings. Downriver, the Syndicate's toll boat sat dark at its mooring—no crew, no lantern, its authority evaporating the way fog does when the sun finally commits. The collapse hadn't taken long, once the documents went to the assayer and the assayer wired the marshals. Creditors, rivals, and the territorial government descended on the Harrow ledgers like gulls on a spilled hold. Debts were voided wholesale. Including, as of yesterday's notice nailed to the harbor office door, mine.

Nine years. Nine years of cash payments, false names, unlisted harbors, of flinching at men in gray coats. Gone with a notice and a nail.

"You don't look happy," Wren observed. She was wrapped in a blanket shaped like a cocoon, only her face showing, and her face was doing arithmetic.

"I'm adjusting," Elodie said. "It turns out I built my entire personality around being hunted. I don't know who I am when nobody's chasing me."

"Surveyor," I offered. "Cook. Occasional philosopher. Man who takes up too much bunk."

"That's your list. I asked about mine."

We let the river answer. It was running low and clear, autumn-shallow, talking over its stones the way it always did, indifferent to warrants and verdicts and the entire architecture of human debt. Somewhere north, the passes were closing. Somewhere south, spring was a rumor. Here, frost on the rail and whiskey in the cup and two people—or three, counting me, and lately I've begun counting myself, which is progress of a sort—who had gone into the ice as strangers and come out as something the paperwork had no column for.

"There's a hearing in Coldharbor next month," Wren said, to the river as much as to us. "Formal restoration of the claim. The recorder wants the heir present." She paused. "He also mentioned the claim includes water rights. River rights. Frontage on the Vell, actually. Enough frontage to berth a boat and register a line."

Elodie went very still, the way she does when a sandbar shifts under the hull.

"A line," she repeated carefully.

"Reyes-Voss," Wren said. "Ferro can do the surveys. I can do the books. And the captain—" she unwound one arm from the blanket and pointed it, with terrible accuracy, at the woman who had spent forty chapters pretending she wanted nothing but the horizon— "the captain needs to decide whether she's running from something or toward something. Because the river goes both ways, and so does she."

Elodie looked down into her cup for a long time. Long enough that I started composing her refusal in my head—*I don't anchor, girl, I never anchor*—and long enough that I finished composing it and realized, with a surveyor's certainty, that she wasn't going to say it.

She reached into her coat instead and brought out the compass. Her father's, the one she'd followed away from every harbor she'd ever loved. She set it on the railing between the three of us, brass catching the last light, needle swinging, settling, pointing north—toward Coldharbor, toward the mountains, toward everything she'd sworn she'd never go back to.

"Huh," said Elodie Voss, to the compass, to the river, to whatever dead man had built the thing. "It's still pointing at home."

And she left it sitting there on the rail, needle steady, while the frost came down and the Kestrel rocked gently at the dock, going nowhere, belonging exactly where it was.
