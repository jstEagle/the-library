## 37. Settlements

The marshals' office in Vellport smelled of ink, wet wool, and the particular despair of men who filed paperwork for a living. I sat on a bench outside the assayer's chambers for four hours while clerks carried documents in and out like pallbearers, and I thought about all the ways a woman can be free.

I had been free for eleven years. Free as a name nobody spoke. Free as a debt paid in cash and a berth paid under aliases. Free as a riverboat with no home port, which is to say: free as a thing that belongs nowhere, drifting between banks that were never hers.

The bench was hard. Freedom usually is, once you sit still long enough to notice.

* * *

They read the verdict aloud at noon, or whatever passes for a verdict when the Syndicate it concerns has collapsed like a bad card table. The Reyes claim—Survey 114-C, Silver Bend District—restored to its lawful heir, Wren Tomasina Reyes, daughter of Tomas Reyes, deceased, whose claim had been "fraudulently transferred" in the year of our Lord whatever-it-was by instruments forged under the seal of the Harrow Consolidated. The word *forged* did a lot of work in that document. So did the word *deceased*. Neither of them could bring a man back, but together they made his daughter the richest woman in Coldharbor, which I suspect would have made him laugh until he cried.

Wren stood when they finished reading. She didn't cheer. She didn't cry, either, though her jaw did that thing it does when she's holding something behind her teeth. She just nodded at the clerk like he'd told her the price of flour, and then she looked at me across the room, and what was in her face wasn't triumph.

It was an invitation. Which frightened me considerably more than Dain ever had.

* * *

Ferro took the news of his own ruin the way other men take good brandy.

He'd confessed to the false survey in open court—stood up in front of marshals, lawyers, and half of Vellport's curious, and said, in a voice that only shook twice, "I signed a report I knew to be false because the Harrow Syndicate paid me forty dollars and told me no one would be hurt." Then he'd described, in the precise language of his old profession, exactly how he'd falsified the assays, moved the boundary markers, and buried the true figures where no honest man would find them. He'd done it without notes. Twenty years of guilt makes for excellent recall.

His license was struck before sundown. His name went into the territorial register beside a black line, and any man hiring Augustin Ferro to survey so much as a chicken coop would be hiring a felon.

"You understand," he said to me that evening, on the Kestrel's aft deck, "that I've never been more employable in my life."

I laughed. It surprised us both.

"Nobody trusts a clean surveyor," he went on, turning his glass so the lamplight swam in it. "A clean surveyor tells you what the land says. A ruined one tells you what the land *is*." He drank. "Wren's already asked me to walk the claim boundaries come thaw. Says she wants every marker set by someone who knows how they lie."

"She's smarter than both of us."

"She's smarter than the whole territory. That's not saying much, but still."

We sat with that a while. The Vell moved past us in the dark, doing what rivers do, which is everything and nothing, endlessly.

* * *

The offer came three days later, and it came the way Wren did everything: directly, without preamble, at a moment calculated to make refusal awkward.

She'd taken over the Kestrel's galley—not permanently, she said, but someone had to cook, and Ferro burned water, and I had a standing policy against cooking anything I also had to feel. She was frying trout when I came below, and she said, without turning around:

"I want you as a partner."

The pan spat. I leaned in the doorway and gave her the look I give sandbars: assessing, noncommittal, prepared for the worst.

"The Reyes-Voss Line," she said. "That's what Ferro's calling it. He's already drawn up letterhead, which I told him was premature, but you know how he gets with a ruler." She turned, spatula in hand, seventeen years old and utterly unafraid of me, which remained the most unnerving thing about her. "Two boats by summer. The Kestrel and something bigger for freight. The claim will pay for it. I need someone who knows the river, and there isn't another soul on this water who knows it like you."

"There are pilots in Vellport who'd work cheaper."

"They don't know where the river keeps its secrets."

"That's not a skill, girl. That's a vice."

"It's a *profession*," she said, "and you're the only one licensed in it." She flipped the trout. "Think about it. That's all I'm asking. Think about it."

But I had already thought about it, in the way a man thinks about a hole in the ice he's about to be pushed toward. And what I'd thought was: *no.* No, because partnerships meant names on paper, and names on paper meant a fixed address, and a fixed address meant that every debt collector, every marshal, every ghost from the eleven years of careful anonymity would know exactly where to find Elodie Voss, captain, partner, *person*.

I had spent a decade being no one. It turns out being no one is a full-time occupation, and I was very good at it, and I did not know how to do anything else.

* * *

So I did what I always do when a thing frightens me.

I ran.

Not dramatically. I'm too old for dramatic exits and too practical for them anyway. I simply waited until Wren had gone ashore to see the assayer's clerk about filing schedules, waited until Ferro was asleep in his bunk with a book sliding off his chest, and then I cast off the Kestrel's lines myself, quiet as a thief, and took her downriver into the dark.

I told myself it was a supply run. There was truth in that—we needed paint, rope, and a new stove grate—but the truth was mostly ballast around a heavier cargo, which was this: I needed to be alone on the water one more time before I let anyone tie me to a dock.

The night run was beautiful. I'll say that for cowardice; it has excellent taste in scenery. The moon came up fat over the eastern bluffs and laid a silver road down the middle of the channel, and I ran it alone, hand on the wheel, no one shouting soundings at me, no one asking questions, no one needing anything. Just me and the current and the old familiar arithmetic of the Vell—the sandbar off Miller's Point grown two feet since spring, the deadfall at Crooked Narrows finally gone, the eddy below Harker's Landing still spinning its slow clockwise lie.

This was mine. Eleven years of it. Every shoal memorized, every trick learned, every mile paid for in solitude.

And somewhere around the second bend past Harker's, alone in the dark with the wheel warm under my hands, I finally admitted what I'd been refusing to admit since a dying man pressed an oilskin packet into my arms:

It wasn't enough anymore.

It hadn't been enough for a long time. Maybe since the day I hauled Tomas Reyes out of the reeds against every instinct I owned. Maybe since the night Wren saved Ferro from going over the Widow's Teeth and looked at me afterward with river-water streaming off her and said, "You could have warned me," and I said, "Where's the fun in that," and she *laughed*, and something in my chest that had been rusted shut for eleven years creaked open one inch.

I brought the Kestrel about at the mouth of Sable Creek and anchored her in the lee, and I sat up most of the night drinking coffee and arguing with myself, and losing.

* * *

Here is the argument I lost, laid out plain, because I promised myself honesty if nothing else:

A compass needle doesn't point north because north is comfortable. It points north because that's what it's *for*. My father's compass—I carry it still, brass case worn smooth, the needle drunk since the year it got dropped in the shallows off Teller's Bend—has pointed every direction except true for as long as I've owned it, and I followed that drunken needle away from every harbor, every offer, every hand extended, telling myself that wandering was freedom.

It isn't. Wandering is just fear with better scenery.

I knew that at dawn, sitting in the anchor watch with frost on the rails, watching the sun come up over water I loved and had used as a hiding place. The river had never been my home. It had been my excuse.

* * *

I was back at the Vellport dock by mid-morning, and Wren was waiting on the pilings, arms crossed, wearing the expression of a person who has been awake since five and is furious about it in a controlled, bookkeeping sort of way.

"You stole your own boat," she said.

"Borrowed. Indefinitely."

"You snuck off in the *night*."

"I'm told that's when boats are best."

"Don't." Her voice cracked, just once, and she hated it, and I watched her hate it. "Don't do the thing where you make it a joke so nobody can get close enough to matter. Ferro does it. I grew up with a man who did it. It's how people drown, Elodie. They joke right up until the water's over their heads."

I tied off the line. My hands were steady, which surprised me.

"Tomas offered me a map," I said. "For a promise. And I said no, because promises are anchors, and I told myself I was a boat, and boats don't anchor." I coiled the line slow, giving my mouth time to catch up with the rest of me. "Then he died on my deck, and I kept the map anyway, and I told myself that was pragmatism. Then I met you, and I told myself you were cargo. Then Ferro, and he was expertise. Then the glacier, and the cave, and the ice, and every single time, I found a word for what was actually happening, which is that I stopped wanting to be invisible and didn't have the nerve to say so out loud."

Wren had gone very still. Trout-frying stillness. Waiting-for-the-strike stillness.

"So here's me saying it out loud," I said. "I'm a coward, and I ran last night, and I'm back, and if the partnership's still on offer, I want in. Not because the silver's good—though I hear the silver's very good—and not because I owe you, though I probably do. Because I'm tired." My voice did something embarrassing near the end and I let it. "I'm tired of being free."

She crossed the gangplank and hugged me, hard, quick, the way you hug someone you're afraid will bolt—which was accurate—and then stepped back and became brisk immediately, because we're related in spirit if not blood, and neither of us can stand a scene.

"Partnership's fifty-fifty," she said. "Ferro gets a salary and first pick of the bunks. You cook."

"I absolutely do not cook."

"Fifty-five, forty-five, and you cook."

"You drive a harder bargain than the Harrows ever did."

"The Harrows never had to eat your cooking." She stuck out her hand, and I shook it, and that was that. No papers yet, no seals, just spit-and-scratch terms on a dock in the cold, which is how the best contracts are made and the worst ones too, and we'd find out which this was.

* * *

Ferro appeared at noon with a rolled sheet of drafting paper and the sheepish look of a man caught doing something kind. He'd drawn up the letterhead after all. *Reyes–Voss River Line,* it said, in his beautiful surveyor's hand, with a little kestrel stamped above the words, wings spread, talons out.

"I did the bird from memory," he admitted. "It's not quite right. The real ones are meaner."

"The real ones steal fish from herons," Wren said. "Draw it meaner."

"I'll draw it meaner."

We hung the paper inside the pilot house, crooked, because none of us could hang anything straight, and stood looking at it like three people who couldn't quite believe the room belonged to them.

Below us, the Vell ran high and fast with snowmelt, carrying ice out toward the sea, and upstream the mountains stood white and enormous over the pass we'd come through—the pass that had nearly killed us, that had given us back to each other changed. On the horizon, small as a thumbnail, the shoulder of the glacier country caught the light.

"Launch season's eight weeks out," Wren said. "We'll need a second hull, crew, contracts—"

"And a stove grate," I said. "Which is why I went downriver last night, before I went downriver for worse reasons. There's a foundry at Harker's Landing sells them cheap."

"Then we'd better sail tonight," she said, and grinned at me, and it was her father's grin, and I let it land.

I went forward to check the lines. My father's compass sat in its bracket by the wheel, needle swinging lazy and drunk as always, pointing nowhere in particular, nowhere useful, nowhere I'd ever follow again.

Behind us lay the wreck. Ahead, the horizon. For the first time in eleven years, I wanted both.

We cast off at dusk, the three of us, into the running dark of the Vell—and if the river noticed that the Kestrel sailed lighter than she had in years, carrying less fear and more future, the river kept it to herself, the way she keeps everything.
