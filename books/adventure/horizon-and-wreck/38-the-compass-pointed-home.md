## 38. The Compass Pointed Home

The compass sat in my coat pocket for thirty-one years before I finally looked at it properly.

That's not quite true. I looked at it every day. I checked it the way other people check their pulse—out of habit, out of fear that it might stop, out of some superstitious belief that as long as the needle swung true, so did I. But looking at a thing and seeing it are different trades, and I had spent three decades plying one while pretending to practice the other.

It was my father's. Brass case gone soft at the edges from handling, the glass scratched in an arc where he'd worn it against his watch chain for years before he wore it against mine. He gave it to me on the dock at Vellport the day I took my first command—a leaky forty-foot scow with more ambition than freeboard—and he said, "This will never lie to you, Ellie. People will. Rivers will, when they're feeling clever. But this little thing has no imagination whatsoever."

I thought he meant north.

I sailed away that afternoon and didn't come back for eleven years, and by then there was nothing to come back to but a headstone and a harbor master who'd sold his moorings to the Harrow Syndicate. So I kept sailing. That's the shape my whole life took, if you want the honest geometry of it: a woman running from a fixed point and calling the running freedom.

* * *

The morning after Wren made her offer—the partnership, the papers drawn up in her careful schoolroom hand, *Voss & Reyes, River Transport, est. spring*—I did what I always do when a thing frightens me. I went down to the Kestrel alone and started working on her hull like the boat was the problem.

She wasn't the problem. She's never been the problem. The Kestrel is the most honest relationship of my life, which tells you something sad about the rest of them.

I scraped barnacles off the stern wheel in weather cold enough to make the river steam, and I let myself think about the offer the way I let myself think about most painful things: sideways, in fragments, while my hands were busy so my mind could pretend it wasn't doing anything at all.

A partnership. A name on a shingle. A share of a business tied to a town, to a dock, to a daughter who expected me to be there in the fall and the winter and the spring after that. Everything I had spent my whole adult life arranging myself to avoid, offered up on one sheet of paper by a seventeen-year-old with her father's stubborn jaw and none of his talent for leaving.

"You could just say yes," Ferro said from the dock above me. I hadn't heard him come down. The man moves like a cat that's been declawed and knows it—careful, apologetic, but still somehow everywhere.

"I'm busy."

"Scraping barnacles in November. Riveting stuff." He lowered himself onto the edge of the dock with the caution of a man whose body had recently been shot and hadn't entirely forgiven him for it. His leg stuck out stiff. He'd need the doctor in Harrow territory soon, and we both knew it, and neither of us said it, because that's the kind of crew we were now—we carried each other's dread without comment, like ballast. "Wren's been up since five redoing the figures. She's got projections. Charts. The girl made a chart of our future, Elodie. There are columns."

"God help us all."

"She wants you to say yes," he said. "But she'll survive if you don't. That's the difference between her and the rest of us sentimental fools. She's already survived worse than your answer."

I flicked barnacle scrapings off my knife. "And what about you? You planning to sign on with Voss & Reyes?"

"I'm planning to get my leg seen to before it turns colors I don't have names for. After that—" He shrugged, winced, regretted shrugging. "After that I suppose I'll find out whether a man can be a surveyor again after spending ten years being a liar instead. Wren seems to think so." He looked at me then, direct, the way he almost never did. "You know what I keep thinking about? The cache. Tomas Reyes sat on that mountain for twenty years with silver under his boots and documents in an iron box, and he never came down. Never sent word. Never cleared his own name. Do you know why?"

"Because the Harrows would've killed him."

"No. Well—yes, probably, eventually. But that's not why. He stayed because coming down meant facing his daughter with the truth, and he was more afraid of her face than of their guns." Ferro tapped the dock boards with one knuckle. "I recognize that arithmetic. I ran the same numbers for ten years. Fear of one person's opinion is worth more than any fortune or any bullet. It's the only debt that compounds forever."

He levered himself up and left me alone with the barnacles, which was his way of being kind—he always knew exactly how long a man could stand company before company became a mirror.

* * *

That night I couldn't sleep, so I did the thing I do instead of sleeping, which is walk the deck and talk to a boat that doesn't listen. Somewhere past midnight I took the compass out of my coat and set it on the pilothouse chart table under the lamp, and for the first time in thirty-one years I actually looked at it.

Here's what I found, and I'm almost embarrassed to write it down because it was there the whole time, graven into the brass, and I had read past it ten thousand times the way you read past your own name in a document until someone says it aloud:

Around the needle, where the cardinal points should have been plain, my father had engraved words instead. Tiny ones. You needed the lamp low and the light raking to catch them.

Where NORTH should be: *what you owe.*

EAST: *who you left.*

SOUTH: *the easy water.*

WEST: *her.*

I sat down hard on the pilot's bench. My hands weren't steady, and I'm not going to pretend they were.

My father was a riverman of the old school, which is to say a man who communicated exclusively through objects and expected you to decode them over decades. He'd given me a compass that didn't point north. It pointed at everything I was running from, arranged around a needle that swung faithfully toward whichever one I was currently refusing to face.

All those years—all those harbors I put behind me, all those towns where I paid cash and gave no name, all those sandbars I read better than any pilot alive because reading water was safer than reading people—I thought I was following the compass. I wasn't. I was following it backwards. Every time the needle settled, I turned the Kestrel the other way. The compass pointed home, and I steered for the horizon, and I called the wake behind me a life.

The worst part—the part that made me laugh, actually, alone in the pilothouse at two in the morning like a madwoman—was WEST: *her.* My mother died when I was six. I barely remember her voice. But my father remembered it fine, and he'd engraved it into the west point of a boy's compass, and I had carried his grief around in my pocket for thirty-one years without once asking him what it meant, because by the time I was old enough to ask, asking had become impossible, and after he died it became permanent.

We are all of us carrying messages we never opened. Some of us are lucky enough to have them delivered posthumously by lamplight.

* * *

I went to see Wren the next morning. She was in the forward cabin with the ledger open and Ferro's charts spread across the bunk, and she had that look she gets—chin up, eyes level, braced for whatever the world was going to hand her, because the world had handed her a dead father and a stolen claim and a winter on a glacier before her eighteenth birthday, and she'd decided somewhere in the dark of all that that she would simply take what came and make use of it. Her father's daughter down to the marrow, though she was still learning to believe it.

"I read the papers," I said. "The partnership ones."

"And?"

"And I need to tell you something first, because if I sign my name next to yours I'd rather you knew what you're signing it to." I sat down across from her, and I told her about the compass. All of it. The engraving, the years of steering away, the arithmetic of a life spent calculating distances from everything that mattered. "Your father ran from one thing for twenty years," I finished. "I ran from everything for thirty-one. Between the two of us we could teach a masterclass in cowardice, and I'd hate for you to think you're going into business with a brave woman."

Wren was quiet for a long moment. Then she said, "You went back up the mountain for me. Twice. Once through the Widow's Teeth in the dark, once onto the ice with Dain behind us."

"That was different."

"How?"

"It just was."

"It wasn't, though." She closed the ledger—not in frustration, the way I close things, but gently, the way she does everything, like even her gestures had decided to be the opposite of mine. "Elodie. You keep telling yourself the story where you run. But I was there. I watched you turn *toward* every bad thing on that mountain. Maybe the running was the old story and you just haven't written the new one yet." She slid the papers across the table. "So write it."

There it was. Seventeen years old and she hands out wisdom like a ferryman hands out tickets—routinely, reasonably priced, no fuss. I signed. My hand shook on the last letter of *Voss*, and I let it shake, because hiding it felt like one more thing I was done doing.

"There," Wren said, and blew on the ink like it was 1840. "Partners. Now the hard part."

"Which is?"

"Telling Ferro he's hired. He'll pretend to be modest for a full hour."

* * *

We told Ferro that afternoon. He pretended to be modest for fifty minutes, which was nearly a record, and then asked about wages with the delicacy of a man negotiating his own ransom, and Wren quoted him a figure so fair he visibly lost his argument, and that was that. Three liars and a legacy, incorporated.

But a signature on paper isn't the same as staying. Staying is a thing you do daily, in small motions, and I learned that over the following weeks the way you learn any new water: badly at first, then by feel.

I stayed through the refit, which meant staying in one port long enough to argue with shipwrights, and arguing with shipwrights requires a reputation, and a reputation requires a name spoken aloud. *Voss,* I said, at the yard office, in daylight, to a man who wrote it down. It didn't kill me. I checked.

I stayed through the first frost, when the Vell went thin and gray and every instinct I owned screamed at me to run south ahead of the ice. Instead I helped Wren inventory the hold, and taught her the sandbars below Millbrook, and discovered that teaching is its own strange anchor—you can't leave a place where someone is counting on you to show them where the channel bends.

I stayed through the night the doctor in Harrow territory finally dug the bullet out of Ferro's leg, sitting in the waiting room of a Syndicate town with the documents' aftermath still settling around us, and found that I wasn't afraid anymore—or rather, that I was afraid of the ordinary things, a fever, a bad outcome, and not of the Syndicate, whose power over me had died on a moraine along with everything else Dain thought he owned. Fear needs secrecy to live in. I'd evicted it.

And I stayed, one gray evening in late winter, through the hardest part of all, which was walking out to the Vellport cemetery and standing in front of my father's stone with the compass in my hand and saying, out loud, to a dead man, "I figured it out. Took me long enough. You could've just *said* something."

The wind moved through the bare trees, which is not an answer, but from a riverman of the old school, it was practically a conversation.

I set the compass on the stone for a minute—letting the old man see it, letting the needle swing—and then I took it back, because it was mine now, and I had a new use for it. Not for finding my way away. For checking, now and then, that I was still pointed at the things I used to refuse.

North: what I owed. Paid, mostly, though debts to the living renew themselves daily, and I'd begun to suspect that was the point of them.

East: who I'd left. Nothing left to leave. That point was empty for the first time in my life, and empty in the good way, like a cleared channel.

South: the easy water. Still there. Still tempting. Always will be. But easy water doesn't carry freight, and it doesn't carry people, and I was done traveling light.

West: *her.*

My mother. Whose face I didn't remember, and whose name I suddenly wanted to know the way Wren had wanted to know her father's—not for silver, not for vindication, but to learn who she really was. There were records in Vellport. There was an old parish register. There might be cousins, a history, a grave with flowers on it somewhere east of here.

I stood there in the cold and realized I was already planning the trip. Not fleeing toward it. Going.

There's a difference. It took me thirty-one years and a mountain to learn it, but it turns out the difference is everything: the same distance traveled, the same miles logged, and one direction means running while the other means arriving.

* * *

The Kestrel comes out of the yard in six weeks. Wren has the books balanced to the penny and a route mapped that runs from Coldharbor down to Vellport and back, regular as a heartbeat, and Ferro—leg healing, humility wearing off right on schedule—has already surveyed two new landings and pronounced both "adequate," which from Ferro is a parade with brass bands.

Launch season's coming. First passengers booked: a widow going home to Coldharbor, two federal clerks riding circuit, and a survey team wanting passage up to the pass to mark the glacier corridor safe for summer trade. Our waters now. Marked, charted, named.

Last night I walked the deck with the compass in my palm and the needle steady, and I noticed something I'd never once seen in thirty-one years of owning the thing.

The needle wasn't pointing anywhere I wanted to refuse.

It was pointing straight downriver—toward the Kestrel's mooring, toward the lit windows of the harbormaster's office where Wren was working late, toward the whole small, anchored, terrifying life I'd signed my name to. And for the first time since my father pressed it into my hand on a dock I was too young to appreciate, the compass and I agreed on the heading.

Spring's coming early this year. The ice on the Vell is talking already—that long groaning language rivers speak when they're getting ready to move. Wren says we'll launch on the first clear day. Ferro says the channel below the yard has shifted and we'll want to sound it twice. And I say—

Well. What I say is: ready the boat. The horizon can wait. It's very good at waiting.

Home, it turns out, was the one bearing I never needed a compass to find.
