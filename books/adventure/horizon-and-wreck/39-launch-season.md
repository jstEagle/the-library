## 39. Launch Season

There is a particular smell to a riverboat in spring, and I have decided it is my favorite smell in the world: tar warming in the sun, fresh-cut pine from the winter's repairs, river mud thawing into something almost sweet, and underneath it all the faint iron tang of a hull that has been through too much and come out the other side stubborn. The Kestrel smelled like all of it that first morning of launch season, and I stood on her new-painted deck with a ledger under my arm and paint on my knuckles and thought: *this is what home smells like. It smells like work.*

I did not used to think that way. I used to think home was a word other people got to use.

* * *

The winter had been long and strange and, against every expectation I'd carried down off the mountain, good. We had overwintered at Millbrook, in a rented boathouse with a stove that Elodie swore at daily and Ferro had coaxed back to life twice before Christmas. Ferro's shoulder—the one the bullet went through on the moraine—had healed crooked, the doctor said, but functional, which was more than any of us had bet on in that cave during the Long Dark. He'd lost weight he couldn't afford and gained back most of it on Elodie's cooking, which was terrible in a way that grew on you, like moss.

And somewhere in those months, without anyone announcing it, we had become an outfit.

It happened the way ice goes out on the Vell—not all at once but in groans and cracks and one morning you look out and the channel is open and nobody can quite say when it changed. For me it changed the day Elodie slid a ledger across the boathouse table and said, "You're better with numbers than either of us, and I'm not proud enough to pretend otherwise. Books are yours."

The Reyes-Voss line. That was the name painted on the Kestrel's new stern board, gold leaf on green, done by a sign painter in Millbrook who charged us double because Ferro kept correcting his lettering. Reyes first. Elodie had insisted on the order, and when I argued—because I argue, it's one of my qualities—she said, "Your father bought this boat with his blood whether he meant to or not. His name goes first," and then she'd looked away out the window at the frozen river, because Elodie could say enormous things in small sentences only as long as she didn't have to watch you hear them.

* * *

Launch day itself was chaos, and I mean that as a compliment.

Ferro was up the mast at dawn re-rigging the halyards one-handed, calling down measurements in surveyor's shorthand that no one else understood, and Elodie was below checking the new boiler seams for the fourth time even though the boilermaker had certified them twice, and I was on the dock arguing with a freight agent about tonnage rates while simultaneously trying to keep three mules from eating the mooring lines.

"Miss Reyes." The freight agent, a damp little man named Pullet, consulted his papers again. "Your rate schedule says grain runs at four cents the hundredweight to Vellport."

"It does."

"The Harrow line charges three."

"The Harrow line," I said pleasantly, "is currently under federal receivership pending trial, so their rates are somewhat theoretical. Also, Captain Voss doesn't run grain through shoal water at night to save a penny, which is why your grain arrives instead of decorating a sandbar."

He blinked at me. I smiled the smile I'd learned from watching Elodie lie to Syndicate collectors—a smile that says *I am entirely reasonable and also you will lose this conversation.* It works better than you'd think, coming from a seventeen-year-old girl with ink on her fingers. People underestimate bookkeepers. That is the entire secret of bookkeeping.

Pullet signed. Two hundred sacks of barley, first cargo of the Reyes-Voss line. I wrote it in the ledger in my best hand, and my hand shook slightly, and I told myself it was the cold.

It wasn't the cold. My father's name was on a boat, carrying someone's harvest to market, honest as sunrise. Tomas Reyes, who died in the reeds clutching a map, whose own town believed him a thief, whose daughter spent years being *that man's girl*—his name was going upriver on the water, doing honest work, and I had to stand very still for a moment among the mules until my eyes agreed to behave.

"You're leaking," Elodie said, appearing beside me with a coil of rope over her shoulder. She didn't look at me. She never looked at me when she said things like that; she looked at the river, giving me privacy the way she gave everyone privacy, which was by pretending not to notice anything while noticing everything.

"Paint fumes," I said.

"Mm." She dropped the rope. "Boarding's at eight. Try not to bankrupt us before nine."

That was Elodie's whole heart, right there, folded into a joke so you could carry it without it weighing anything.

* * *

The first run upriver was everything chapter one of my life had never been.

I'll explain that badly, probably, but here it is: I grew up in Coldharbor, which is a town the river forgot on purpose. Everything there was waiting—for the mines to reopen, for my father to come back, for something to happen to somebody else. And then I ran away downriver as a stowaway on a boat captained by a woman who owed money to murderers, and everything that happened after happened fast: the map, the mountain, the glacier, the gunshots, the truth. My life had been either waiting or falling. There had been very little in between.

But a working run up the Vell is all in-between. It's loading at dawn and coffee on the foredeck and Ferro standing at the rail with his notebook, taking soundings out of pure habit, muttering depths to himself like prayers. It's Elodie at the wheel reading the water the way other people read letters from home—every riffle a sentence, every dark patch a warning—and occasionally saying "hold on" in a tone that means *hold on*, and the whole boat leaning, and then straightening, and then the ordinary chug of the engine resuming as if nothing had happened, except your heart takes another minute to get the memo.

On the third day, coming up through the Narrows where the banks pinch close and the current runs contrary, a passenger—an elderly woman with a hatbox full of chickens—asked me if the captain had been running this river long.

"All her life," I said.

"She handles her like she was born on her."

"She nearly was." And then, because the words were already out and might as well finish the job: "This is her boat. I keep the books. The Reyes-Voss line."

"Reyes," the woman said, tasting it. "Any relation to the prospectors up Coldharbor way? There was a Reyes had a claim—"

"My father," I said.

And here is the thing I will remember until I die: she didn't flinch. She didn't do the Coldharbor thing, the little pause, the glance away, the *oh*. She just nodded and said, "Good stock, prospectors. Stubborn as granite, the honest ones," and went back to her chickens.

Word travels strange on a river. Faster than telegraph sometimes, and kinder than towns. Somewhere between Millbrook and the Narrows, apparently, the story had gotten around: the Reyes claim restored, the Harrow documents, the trial, the marshals, the girl who testified. By the time we tied up at Vellport people were waving from the dock. Actually waving. At us.

Elodie stood at the wheelhouse window watching them wave and wore an expression I can only describe as a woman being deeply suspicious of a trap.

"They're waving," I told her.

"I see that."

"At *you*."

"I see that too." A pause. "Wren, if this is some elaborate Syndicate scheme, it's the nicest one they've ever run."

"The Syndicate's in receivership, Elodie."

"So was Rome, once, probably." But she lifted her hand off the wheel—one finger, a small grudging wave—and half the dock cheered, and she turned back to the window so fast I pretended not to see her ears go red.

* * *

Ferro changed that spring too, though he'd deny it with footnotes.

The disgraced surveyor who'd come aboard the Kestrel a year ago was a man made entirely of deflection—jokes at wrong angles, expertise offered like contraband, guilt worn under the coat where it chafed. The man who came down the gangplank at Vellport that April walked differently. Straighter, despite the shoulder. He'd taken to teaching, of all things. There were boys at the Millbrook landing—dock kids, mostly, the ones nobody was schooling—who'd figured out that the tall gray man on the green boat would show you how a transit worked if you asked, and would check your arithmetic without making you feel stupid, which was a service available nowhere else in the territory.

"Surveying is just lying carefully with numbers," he told a gap-toothed boy named Oskar on our second run, drawing lines in the dock dust. "So learn to lie carefully, and then spend your whole life earning back the privilege."

"That's bleak, Mr. Ferro," the boy said.

"That's a career, boy." But he was smiling, and his hand on the boy's shoulder didn't shake anymore, and I wrote in the margin of the ledger that evening, where no entry belonged: *A. Ferro, recovering.*

Elodie found the note later and read it and said nothing, but the next week she ordered a proper secondhand transit out of the Vellport outfitting catalogue and left it on Ferro's bunk without a card, and Ferro held it for a long time like a man holding something breakable, and then disappeared up the bank to shoot angles at the far shore until dark, and came back down whistling, which none of us had ever heard him do.

We didn't discuss it. That was the family style. Enormous things, delivered sideways, received without looking directly at them.

* * *

I should say something about the books, because the books became my territory and I defended it like one.

Elodie's method of accounting, I discovered that first winter, was a cigar box of receipts and a memory she claimed was perfect and a system best described as *vibes*. I found a fare recorded as "fat man, Vellport, paid in chickens (2)." I found fuel costs written on the back of a playing card. I found, wedged in the box behind a receipt for tar, a scrap of paper in Elodie's hand that just said *owed: 1 promise, delivered* — and I knew exactly what that was, and I put it back exactly where I found it, and I never mentioned it, and I caught myself touching that corner of the box gently ever after, the way you'd touch a grave marker.

I rebuilt the whole thing in double columns. Debits, credits, dates, names. I made rate cards. I made Ferro sign for his transit supplies like a grown man. I made Elodie—

Well. Making Elodie do anything was a negotiation conducted mostly through silence and eyebrow warfare, but by May she was bringing me her receipts unprompted, dropping them on my desk with the air of a cat presenting a dead bird, and saying "your kingdom awaits" in a tone of profound irony that fooled neither of us. She liked knowing where the money was. She'd spent years not knowing anything except how to survive, and the ledger was a kind of shore for her, fixed and legible. She'd never have admitted it. I've gotten good at reading her the way she reads water.

Some nights, in port, she'd sit across from me while I posted entries, drinking coffee gone bitter, and watch the lamplight on the pages, and once she said, quietly, not really to me: "My father kept books. Steamboat man, on the Assiniboine. I used to think it was the dullest thing a person could do." A sip. "Turns out it's just counting what matters."

Then she ruffled my hair, which I tolerated because complaining would have revealed that I liked it, and went up to check the boiler seams a fifth time.

* * *

Small joys. Let me inventory them, because the mountain taught me that inventories matter—you count what you have, or you forget you have it.

The mornings Ferro cooked, because his camp bread was genuinely excellent and he made it in a little dutch oven with the concentration of a man defusing something. The way Elodie hummed at the wheel when she thought no one could hear over the engine—old river songs, half-remembered, always trailing off at the same verses. The chickens lady from the Narrows came back every run with eggs for the crew, which felt like the river paying interest. The day Oskar and two other dock boys stowed aboard to Vellport and Elodie, instead of putting them off, put them to work chipping rust and paid them in meals, and by summer they were the best deck crew on the Vell and fiercely loyal to a boat they'd technically boarded illegally.

The evening in late May when we tied up at a nameless bend above Millbrook because the moon was doing something ridiculous on the water—laying a road of silver clear across the current, straight to us—and none of us suggested moving on, and we ate Ferro's bread and hard cheese on the roof of the cabin and nobody talked much, and it wasn't silence like the cave's silence, hungry and afraid. It was the other kind. The kind with room in it.

I caught Elodie looking at the two of us—Ferro pointing out stars with his bad arm propped on his knee, me sketching the bend in the ledger's margin because I'd started drawing the river everywhere, in everything—and she had an expression I'd never seen on her. Not suspicion. Not the careful blankness. Something open and slightly bewildered, like a woman who'd spent her whole life steering by escape routes and had just realized she couldn't find one.

"What?" I said.

"Nothing." She looked back at the moon-road. "Just checking my soundings."

"On what?"

She took her time answering. On the water, a fish rose and rang the surface like a small bell.

"How deep I'm in," she said finally.

"And?"

"And it turns out I don't mind." She stood, briskly, before the moment could be examined further. "Early start tomorrow. Somebody has to sleep."

"Somebody has to *not* check the boiler a sixth time," Ferro said.

"Mind your angles, surveyor."

* * *

June came in hot and high-watered, snowmelt roaring down from the range, and the Vell ran brown and muscular and full of debris—whole trees sailing past like drowned giants. We ran cargo twice a week now. The name on the stern was starting to mean something at the landings; shippers asked for the Reyes-Voss by name, and Pullet the freight agent had stopped arguing rates and started bringing me peppermints, which I chose to interpret as professional respect.

It was on the June run, heavy-laden with mill machinery for the upper landings, that I noticed the letter.

It came aboard at Millbrook, ordinary envelope, ordinary postmark, addressed to *Captain E. Voss, the Kestrel.* Nothing odd in that—we got mail now, actual mail, forwarded through the Millbrook office like a real concern. But the handwriting on this one was cramped and hurried, and the return corner said *Coldharbor*, and when I carried it up to the wheelhouse Elodie read the front of it and went still in a way I'd learned to recognize from the bad old days.

"Wren," she said slowly. "You know anyone in Coldharbor who writes like they're being charged by the word?"

"No." I took the envelope. The paper inside was thin, single sheet, and the handwriting slanted downhill like a scree slope. I read it standing in the wheelhouse doorway with the engine talking underneath us and the brown river shouldering past the windows, and I read it twice, and then I handed it to Elodie without a word because my voice wasn't going to cooperate.

*Miss Reyes* — it began, and stumbled — *or Captain, I don't know what to call you now. This is Marta Ellery, you won't recall me, I kept the dry goods store. I'll be short. Men have come to Coldharbor asking after the Reyes claim. Not buyers. They wear no colors but they ask questions like the Harrow men used to, before the trouble, and they've been to the county records office twice, and yesterday one of them asked me, plain as church, whether the Reyes girl still had family up the valley who might contest title. I don't like the smell of it. Whatever your outfit is worth now, I'd come see to it. The passes are open. Come soon. — M.E.*

Elodie read it. Read it again. Then she looked up at me, and whatever she saw in my face made her set the letter down on the chart table very carefully, the way you set down something that might go off.

"Ferro," she called down the deck, without raising her voice much, because her voice carried the way river voices do. "Get your transit. Bring the Reyes claim plats."

Above us the mountains stood white and patient, the same ones we'd crossed, the ones where a promise had been kept and a wreck left behind. The river ran strong beneath the hull, going where rivers go.

Elodie put her hand on the wheel and brought the Kestrel's head around, upstream, toward Coldharbor.

"Wren," she said. "How fast can you close the books?"
