## 40. Horizon and Wreck

The Vell River in spring is a liar with a beautiful face. It runs high and green-gold with snowmelt, all confidence on the surface, and underneath it's still moving the same old sandbars around like a card sharp who never learned to quit. I've spent eleven years learning that river's tells. It took me one winter to learn the harder lesson—that a woman can know every lie the water tells and still be lying worst of all to herself.

But I'm getting ahead of myself, which is unlike me. A riverwoman doesn't get ahead of her current.

I came off the dock at Vellport just past dawn, the way I always do, except that this time there were people on the dock to see me go, and this time I let them see me.

"Captain Voss!" called Marta Ondine from the chandlery steps, waving like we were neighbors, because we were now. "You'll want to mind the Grange sandbar—moved again after the thaw!"

"I heard it grew a new finger," I called back. "I'll shake its hand anyway."

She laughed. Eleven years of running that river anonymous—*the Kestrel's captain*, no name offered, no name asked, debts paid in cash and eyes kept down—and it turns out all it took to become a person was nearly dying on a glacier with two people who refused to let me stay a ghost.

The Kestrel sat light in the water and rode easy on the current, and she had passengers. Actual paying passengers, tickets written out in ink, three of them bound for Millbrook: a seed merchant with too many crates and too few opinions, an elderly widow going to visit her sister, and a young surveyor's assistant who spent the whole first hour pretending not to stare at me before finally working up the nerve to ask if I was *the* Elodie Voss, the one from the trial.

"That depends," I said, "on what they said at the trial."

"They said you ran the Harrow blockade with federal marshals shooting at your wake."

"They said that, did they." I checked a cleat that didn't need checking. "Marshals shoot high when they're excited. And the Harrows weren't doing much shooting by then. Mostly drowning."

He grinned like I'd confirmed something holy. I let him have it. There was a time when being known would have felt like a noose. Now it felt more like a mooring line—something that holds you in place whether or not you asked for it, and some mornings you're grateful for the holding.

Wren came up from below deck around mid-morning, hair tied back with a strip of red cloth, carrying the ledger under her arm like a weapon she'd learned to use gently. Seventeen going on forty, that girl, with her father's stubborn jaw and none of his talent for disappearing.

"We're ahead of schedule," she announced. "If we hold this speed we'll make Millbrook by four."

"You've been reading my river log again."

"Somebody has to. You write it in code half the time."

"It's not code. It's shorthand."

"It's code. Ferro taught me the symbols. He says you invented half of them yourself so nobody else could read your notes."

"Ferro," I said, "talks too much for a man who used to be paid to keep quiet."

"He also says—" and here she paused, and I knew whatever came next had been rehearsed, because Wren Reyes only pauses when she's loaded something carefully, "—that you used to run this stretch alone. Before us. That you'd go weeks without speaking to anyone but lock-keepers."

"The lock-keepers and I had rich conversations."

"Elodie."

"Wren."

"Why didn't you ever get off?"

I looked at the water instead of her. The current off the starboard bow was doing that thing where it folds over itself just before the channel bends, and I watched it fold, and I gave the wheel a small correction I didn't need, because some questions are easier to answer with your hands than your mouth.

"Because getting off means arriving somewhere," I said finally. "And a boat that arrives can be found. A woman who belongs nowhere can't be owed anything, can't be blamed for anything, can't be left by anything. I thought that was freedom. Turns out it's just a slower way of drowning."

Wren was quiet a moment. Then she wrote something in the ledger—I'm sure of it, she writes everything down, her father's habit passed down like eye color—and said, "I'm putting that in the company history."

"There is no company history."

"There will be. Ferro's already started it. He has you running the Widow's Teeth in chapter three."

"In chapter three? The Widow's Teeth should be chapter nine at least. Build suspense."

She laughed, and went forward to argue with the seed merchant about the proper stowage of his crates, and I stood at the wheel with the spring sun on my neck and thought about how strange it is that the moments you brace against are never the ones that change you. Not the calving ice, not Dain's gun, not the warrant with my name on it. It was a girl with a ledger asking why I never got off the boat.

* * *

We made Millbrook by four, as promised. Ferro was waiting on the dock, which he does now even when there's no reason, leaning on his cane—he walks without it most days, but he says the cane is useful for pointing at things, and Ferro does love to point at things—with a stack of survey documents under one arm and an expression of profound grievance aimed at the weather.

"You're late," he said as I threw him the mooring line.

"We're twenty minutes early."

"Then I'm early. You're late. Hand that line properly, Captain, you throw like a woman who learned on her own."

"I did learn on my own."

"And it shows." He caught the line anyway, wrapped it twice around the bollard, and then stood there a moment with his hand on it, looking up at me, and his voice dropped into the register he saves for things that matter. "The assayer's office wired this morning. Final disposition on the Reyes claim. It's done, Elodie. Wren holds title. All of it. The vein, the land, the water rights. Signed by the territorial judge himself."

I had known it was coming. I had helped carry the documents that made it come. But hearing it said out loud, on a dock in the ordinary daylight, with gulls arguing over fish guts two slips down—it landed different than knowing. Somewhere behind those mountains was a hole in the ground full of silver that belonged, fair and square and legally, to a girl who'd grown up poor and fatherless believing her daddy had been a thief.

"How's she taking it?" I asked.

"She hasn't stopped smiling since Tuesday. It's unsettling. She smiled at the bank clerk and he approved her loan application out of pure terror." Ferro climbed aboard, moving stiffly on his bad leg—the leg the bullet found, the one that aches now when storms come, though he pretends otherwise. "There's a ceremony, you know. In Coldharbor. Next month. They want to dedicate the new claim office. Put up a plaque."

"A plaque."

"With names on it."

"Whose names?"

"Yours. Mine. Hers." He said it lightly, watching me the way he watches terrain—reading the slope of me, judging where I might slide. "Tomas's name too. First on the list."

I turned back to the wheel and began untying the lines we'd need for departure, which was absurd, since we'd just arrived, and Ferro let me do it for a full minute before he said, gently, "You're going to bolt. Right now. I can see it in your shoulders."

"I am standing still."

"You are standing still the way a river stands still right before it goes over a falls." He lowered himself onto the cargo hatch with a wince. "Eleven years, Elodie. Eleven years invisible. And now there's going to be a plaque with your name cast in bronze where every soul in Coldharbor can read it, and part of you wants to be sick, and part of you wants to sail upriver tonight and never stop."

"Which part do you think wins?"

"Neither. That's the thing about you I understood somewhere around day three in that cave, feverish, watching you and that girl take turns keeping me alive—you don't actually want to run anymore. You just don't know any other way to stand still. Nobody ever taught you. So you stand still the way you do everything else—alone, at night, at speed."

I hated him a little, the way you hate a man who reads your chart better than you do. "You should have stuck to surveying."

"I did stick to surveying. I'm surveying you. It's the most important mapping I've ever done, and I intend to publish nothing." He tapped the hatch beside him. "Sit down, Captain. The passengers aren't boarding till tomorrow morning. Wren's gone to the assayer's sub-office to file the season schedules. We have an hour, and a bottle in my bag, and I think it's time somebody told you what Tomas told me."

That stopped me cold. "Tomas never met you."

"No," Ferro said. "But I surveyed his claim. I signed the report that ruined him. And before you throw me off this boat—yes, I've confessed all of it, publicly, at cost of my license and reputation, and yes, I'd do it again. But there's a piece I never told either of you. The last time I saw Tomas Reyes alive—before, years before, when we were both young and honest—he said something to me that I've been turning over all winter. And I think it was meant for you, though neither of us knew you existed yet."

The river slapped against the hull. Somewhere upstream a steam whistle blew, long and low, and the sound of it went through me like the sound of home goes through a man who thought he didn't have one.

"What did he say?" I asked.

Ferro uncorked the bottle. "He said: *A map's no good to a man who won't admit where he already is.*"

* * *

I didn't sleep much that night, which was fine, because I hadn't slept much most nights for eleven years and I'd gotten good work done on bad sleep. But this wasn't work. This was me sitting in the pilothouse of my own boat, in my own harbor, with my own name painted fresh on the stern—*Kestrel, Vellport*—and beneath it, smaller, newer, in Wren's careful lettering: *Reyes–Voss Line.*

I'd fought the addition for a week. She'd won in three days, the way she wins most things now, by simply refusing to acknowledge defeat as a concept.

My father's compass sat on the binnacle where it always sits. Brass case worn smooth, needle steady. He carried it away from every harbor we ever had, and when he died I carried it the same way, and for eleven years I told myself it pointed north. It doesn't. I finally worked that out on the glacier, of all places, in the blue cathedral light with Wren laughing beside me. It points wherever I refuse to go. It always has. Every harbor I skipped, every dock I ghosted past at dawn, every name I never gave—the needle swung toward all of them, patient as water, waiting for me to quit lying.

Tonight the needle pointed straight at the dock. At the lamplit windows of the chandlery where Marta was closing up. At the mooring lines, doubled now, the way Ferro insists. At the town I'd spent a decade treating as a place to refuel and forget.

I picked up the compass and held it level, and I said, out loud, alone in the pilothouse like a madwoman, "All right. I'm here. Are you satisfied?"

The needle trembled once and settled, which was probably just the engine vibration, and which I chose to believe was an answer.

Below and behind me lay the wreck of it—the debt, the running, the man I loved who let me hang for his crimes, the long cold years of competence as a substitute for living. I've stopped trying to pretend that wreck isn't mine. It is mine, every splinter. A riverwoman knows you don't clear a wreck by sailing past it. You mark it on the chart. You warn others off it. And sometimes, on a quiet night, you look down through the clear water at what it cost you, and you let it teach you what it knows.

Ahead of me, through the pilothouse glass, the mountains stood up black against the stars—the pass, the glacier, the whole high white country where a dying man handed a stranger a map and a promise, and the stranger, against every instinct and every year of hard-learned self-preservation, said yes.

Somewhere in that country, silver waits in the dark for a girl named after a bird. It can wait. It's waited this long. Wren says she wants to work the claim slow and right, hire local men, pay honest wages, build something her father would have built if the world hadn't lied to him. Ferro has already drawn up the survey plans; he showed me the maps last week, and I noticed he'd labeled the main adit *Tomas's Cut* in letters big enough to read from a distance, and I noticed Wren notice, and I noticed neither of them say anything about it, because that's how love works in this outfit—quietly, in margins, in labels on maps.

And me? I'm the river leg of it. The Kestrel hauls ore down from the Coldharbor landing when the passes open, carries mail and passengers and the occasional nervous investor up. The Reyes–Voss Line. Two names on the stern, and I answer to both of them now, out loud, in daylight, to anyone who asks.

Dawn came up gray-gold over the water. I heard footsteps on the deck below—two sets, one light and quick, one uneven and deliberate—and the smell of coffee climbing the ladder, and Wren's voice saying, "She's up there. She's always up there. You could set the river's clock by her."

"Rivers don't have clocks," Ferro's voice answered. "They have moods. Fortunately, so does she, and they match."

I didn't turn around when they came into the pilothouse. I just moved over, making room at the glass, the way you make room at a window for people whose view matters as much as yours.

"Millbrook by eight?" Wren asked, pouring coffee into three tin cups because there were three of us now and there always would be.

"Millbrook by eight," I said. "Mind the Grange bar. It moved."

"You always say that."

"It always moves."

Ferro raised his cup toward the mountains, faintly visible now in the lifting dawn, white-crowned and far off and no longer anything to run from. "To the horizon," he said.

"To the wreck," I said, and Wren, catching on slow and then all at once, grinned and finished it:

"To home."

The Kestrel took the current, and the current took us down the bright morning river, and the compass on the binnacle pointed—not north, not away, not anywhere I refused to go—but straight ahead, steady as a promise kept, at the wide open water where the horizon waits, and the wreck lies marked behind us, and home lies in both directions, and I steered toward all of it at once.

The river rose to meet us. For once, so did I.
