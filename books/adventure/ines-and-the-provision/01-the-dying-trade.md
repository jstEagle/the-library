## 1. The Dying Trade

The rain had been falling for three days, and Ada Zhou had stopped counting the hours since she'd last spoken aloud. That was the way she liked it. The closed line between Kessel Junction and the drowned trestle at Mile 41 had been hers alone for eleven weeks—her survey, her instruments, her silence. The railway had written this stretch off in a ledger nobody read anymore, and that made it the safest country in the world for a woman who needed to be nowhere.

She worked the chain line through the cutting with her hood up and her collar sealed, shooting elevations through the drizzle because the drizzle didn't care and neither did the schedule she kept in her own head. The track here was a green vein through the monsoon country: rails furred with rust, sleepers soft as bread, ferns shouldering up through the ballast like they'd been paid to do it. Somewhere north, thunder rolled along the ridgeline and didn't bother coming down.

She was forty-one chains from the old culvert when she saw the pack first.

It lay against the rail like something thrown from a passing train—a prospector's frame pack, canvas gone black with wet, straps trailing in the weeds. Ada stopped. Her hand went to the hatchet on her belt, an old habit from the first year, when she'd learned that the empty country was not the same as the safe one.

Then she saw the boot. Then the leg attached to it, and the rest of Ines.

The woman was folded against the up-side rail with her back to a signal post that hadn't carried a signal in a decade. Sixties, maybe seventies—hard to tell with prospectors, whose faces weathered into something older than their years. Her oilskin had split along one shoulder and the rain had gotten into her, and she was shivering in a way that Ada recognized from bridge crews and flood camps: the deep, mechanical shiver of a body spending its last fuel on warmth and losing.

Ada should have walked on. Every instinct she'd built like masonry since the trial said walk on. Strangers were exposure. Strangers were questions, and questions were the tribunal, and the tribunal was the end of everything she'd built out of silence and survey chain.

But the woman's eyes opened, and they found her, and they were clear in a way that dying eyes sometimes are—terribly, briefly clear.

"You're a rail hand," the woman said. Her voice was gravel dragged through water. "Chain and level. I watched you work from the cutting for an hour."

Ada said nothing. She crouched, out of arm's reach, hatchet still loose in its loop.

"Name's Ines." A cough took her, long and wet, and when it released her she smiled with the effort of a woman who had made peace with worse things than coughing. "You're not going to say yours. That's fine. I know what you are. I've seen the notices."

The word *notices* went through Ada like a spike through a tie. Her hand stopped moving on the hatchet.

"I don't want the reward," Ines said. "I couldn't carry it anywhere, and I'll be past spending it by dark. I want a trade."

"You don't have anything I want," Ada said. It came out flat and cold and she was glad of it. Cold was armor. Cold had kept her alive.

"Everybody wants something." Ines's hand moved, slow as a crane arm, to the flap of her pack. "Help me sit up proper and I'll show you."

Ada told herself she wouldn't. She told herself she'd stand, and walk north to the culvert, and finish her chain, and by morning the rain would have taken care of what the river started. She told herself all of it while her boots carried her forward and her hands took the woman under the arms—light, impossibly light, a body mostly dried out already, like driftwood in a coat—and settled her against the post where the water ran off the cap and not down her collar.

"Thank you," Ines said. "That's the first decent thing anyone's done for me in a month, and I include myself in that."

She worked the pack flap open with fingers that had gone the gray-white of drowned things, and drew out an oilcloth roll tied with copper wire. She unwound the wire with the care of a surgeon and unrolled the cloth on her knee.

A map. Hand-drawn, on drafting linen, in ink that had faded to the brown of old blood. Ada's eyes went to it before she could stop them—eleven years an engineer, and a drawing on linen would always pull her gaze like a hook. Contour lines, fine and confident. A river system she knew: the Merrow watershed, upper reaches, the country past the flood line where the old provision network had died when the line closed. And marked in red, precise as a survey monument, a square with a notation in a cramped, certain hand.

*Provision Depot 7 — Kessel Branch. Sealed '09. Contents intact. Key under—*

The rest was smudged by water.

"You know what a provision depot is," Ines said. Not a question. "Railway kept them up the closed branches. Food, fuel, parts, records. Enough to run a line for a year if you ever needed to. Most were stripped when the branch died. This one wasn't. Flood cut the road in '09 and the company just—sealed it and forgot it. I found it three years ago." Her mouth bent into something that was almost a grin. "I've been living fat on company beans ever since. But that's not why you want it."

"It's not," Ada agreed.

"No. It's the records." Ines's eyes held hers, and there was no mercy in them, only knowledge. "Depots kept manifests. Everything that moved on the branch, everything that was inspected, everything that was signed off. Paper doesn't rot if it's sealed right. I've seen it. Boxes of it, dry as the day it was filed."

Ada's heart was doing something she didn't permit it to do. She made it stop.

"I'm not interested in railway paper," she said.

"Then you're the only engineer in the north who isn't." Ines closed her hand over the map, and Ada saw how the effort cost her, how the shivering had gone shallow and fast, the bad kind. "Here's the trade. The map for a promise. Not for me. For my daughter."

The rain filled the silence between them.

"Mara," Ines said. "Port Merrow. She runs boats—ferries, repairs, whatever floats. She'll be at the yard on the east quay, or someone there will know where she is." The name came out of her the way a splinter comes out, slow and tearing. "I left when she was fourteen. Don't ask me why. The answer takes longer than I've got, and you wouldn't believe it anyway. I've written her eleven letters and sent none of them. This—" she lifted the map a finger's width off her knee, then let it fall back— "this is the twelfth."

"I'm not a courier," Ada said. "I'm not anything to you."

"No," Ines said. "You're a stranger I found in the rain, and I'm betting my daughter's inheritance on the kind of stranger who stops for a dying woman when it costs her something to stop. That's the whole of my logic, and I know it's thin. Thin lines hold, though. I've crossed enough of them."

Ada looked at the map. She looked at the red square, and the notation, and the contour lines that climbed past the flood line into country she'd been circling for weeks without a reason to enter. She thought about manifests. She thought about inspection reports, and signatures, and the report that had gone into the tribunal with her name on the bottom of it and Voss's fingerprints all over everything above it. She thought about how she'd run instead of fighting, because fighting meant standing in a room full of men who had already decided, and she had told herself that living invisible was victory enough.

She had never once believed it. She had only survived it.

"I can't promise you she'll want it," Ada said. "I can't promise she'll even take it from a stranger."

"I'm not asking for her forgiveness. I'm asking for delivery." Ines's breath rattled. "What she does with it is hers. That's the only fair trade left to me."

The shivering had nearly stopped. Ada had seen that before too, in a flood camp outside Harrow's Bend, in a ganger named Oduya who'd talked about his garden until the talking stopped. The body gives up on warmth and starts on something else. There wasn't an hour left in this woman. There might not be two.

Ada could walk away. The map would die here with her, and the promise would die with it, and Ada Zhou could go on being no one, safely, forever.

"Give me your hand," Ada said.

Ines looked at her. Something moved behind the clear, dying eyes—not surprise, exactly. More like a bet paying out.

Ada took the oilcloth roll from her knee, wrapped it, and held out the copper wire. "Show me how you tie this. I'm not losing it to the first branch."

Ines showed her. Three turns, a hitch, the tail tucked under so it couldn't work loose. Her fingers failed on the second demonstration and Ada finished it herself.

"There's a tarp in my pack," Ines said. "Green one. And a bottle in the left pocket that I was saving for a worse day than this one, and I don't think there's a worse one coming. Take both. A trade should cost both sides."

Ada found the tarp and rigged it over the signal post with her ridge line, a lean-to against the driving rain. She found the bottle—some country spirit, sealed with wax—and put it in her own pack without a word, because arguing with a dying woman about gifts was a cruelty she wasn't willing to add to the day.

Then she did the thing she had not done for anyone in eleven years. She sat down in the mud beside a stranger and stayed.

Ines talked. Not much, and less as it went—a prospector's talk, in pieces, the way men and women of the empty country always talked at the end. A claim up the Serrat that never paid. A dog, long dead, named after a railway station. A girl who could strip a marine engine at twelve and who looked at her mother, in the last year they had, like she was already deciding to stop. The rain came harder and the thunder walked down off the ridge at last, and Ines's voice went with it, word by word, like a tide going out.

Near the end, her hand found Ada's sleeve and gripped it with what strength remained, which was not much, and which she spent anyway.

"You'll want to know if it's worth it," she said. "The depot. The risk. A woman like you doesn't move without knowing the return." Her eyes were going dim, but the clarity came back one last time, like a lamp turned up before it fails. "It's worth it. I don't know what you did or what they say you did. But I know paper, and I know what's in that depot, and I'd stake a dying woman's judgment on it: whatever they buried you with, the proof it wasn't yours is sitting on a shelf past the flood line, dry and waiting. I found it. I just didn't know what it was for. Now I do."

Ada's throat closed. She said nothing, because there was nothing to say that wouldn't be a confession, and confessions were the one cargo she'd sworn never to carry.

Ines smiled—the whole face this time, the grin of a woman who had gotten, at the very end, exactly the trade she wanted.

"Port Merrow," she said. "East quay. Tell her the twelfth letter came."

The talking stopped an hour before dawn. Ada sat with her until the rain eased, because you did that, because some debts were incurred the moment you sat down and couldn't be discharged by standing up. Then she did what the country required: she dug, she marked, she raised a cairn of ballast stone beside the track where the woman had chosen to stop, and she wired the map into the inner pocket of her coat, against her chest, where she would feel it with every step like a second heartbeat.

At first light she stood at the culvert with her level on its tripod and tried to shoot her elevations, because routine was the wall she'd lived behind, and walls don't fall in a night.

She got three readings. On the fourth, her eye left the scope and went north, up the dead branch, past the flood line, to a red square on a dead woman's linen and a shelf of dry paper waiting in the dark with her name in it, maybe, or something close enough to it.

She struck the tripod. She broke down the chain. She stood a long moment in the gray light with the rain starting again, hating the woman on the linen and the dying woman under the stones and, most of all, the part of herself that had already decided.

Then Ada Zhou turned north, up the closed line, toward the water.
