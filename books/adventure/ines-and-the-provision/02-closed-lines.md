## 2. Closed Lines

The map was drawn on the back of a provision chit, which told Ada something about Ines before she'd studied a single line. Nobody who expected to live long used government paper for anything but what it was issued for. Ines had known she was spending her last days, and she'd spent them in ink.

Ada spread it across her camp table by lamplight, weighting the corners with four brass survey weights she'd carried through three seasons and two identities. The paper was soft as cloth at the folds. Someone had opened and closed this map a hundred times, tracing routes with a thumb gone to leather at the tip.

It was good work. That was the first thing Ada noticed, and the thing that unsettled her most. She had spent eleven years making maps—first for the Northern Extension office, with its stamped linen and its official cartouche, and then alone, in pencil, on whatever paper she could keep dry—and she knew the difference between a map made to sell and a map made to be used. This was the second kind. Elevations noted in a cramped hand. Water depths marked in fathoms along the old canal cut. A warning in the margin: *weir washed out 3 yrs back, portage left bank.*

Ines had walked this country the way Ada did. Alone, probably. Watching the water.

The depot itself sat at the junction of two dead spurs, forty miles past the last signal box on the Merrow branch, in a basin the map marked with a single word in blue ink: *SEASONAL.* Below it, smaller: *dry Jan–Apr. Under 9 ft May–Oct.*

Ada sat back and let that arithmetic settle.

Nine feet of water over a rail depot. Which meant the depot lay below the flood line, down in one of those drowned valleys where the monsoon rivers backed up when the hills let go of their rain. You could walk to it in the dry months, maybe—but the dry months were gone. The air already tasted like wet iron. Every morning the mist came off the river later and hung heavier, and the frogs had started their deafening congregation in the culverts, and the barometer on her wall—a gift from a life she no longer owned—had been falling for six days without a pause.

If she went now, she went by river.

She looked out the open door of her shelter at the boat.

* * *

She'd traded a season's salvage for it from a fisherman up at Kettle Bend who hadn't asked her name, only whether she wanted the tarp thrown in. It was a flat-bottomed work skiff, twenty feet, built for shallow water and overloaded days, and it leaked. Not fatally—not yet—but steadily, through a seam near the stern post that wept even when the boat sat on the trailer. She'd been meaning to deal with it for a month. She'd been meaning to deal with it the way she meant to deal with most things: indefinitely, because dealing with things meant being somewhere long enough to finish them.

Now she had a promise to keep, and promises didn't care about indefinite.

She worked through the next two days with the methodical fury she brought to everything she couldn't afford to feel about. She rolled the skiff on its side against two felled poles and dug the seam out with a gouge, packed it with oakum and pitch until her fingers were black to the wrist, and flooded the hull to test it. It held. Mostly. A slow seep remained, one drop every few minutes, the kind of leak you could bail forever if you bailed forever.

She took inventory the way she'd been taught to take inventory, kneeling on the ground with everything laid out in rows, because order was the one prayer she still believed in.

Rice, nine pounds. Salt pork, four. Dried fish, two bundles. Lamp oil, a gallon and a half. Pitch, half a tin. Rope, sixty feet. Tools: gouge, hammer, pry bar, the brass sextant her father had left her, wrapped in oilcloth like a relic. Her survey instruments. Her pistol, with eleven rounds, which was ten more than she'd ever wanted a reason to use. And the map, which she finally wrapped in its own oilcloth sleeve and kept inside her coat, against her ribs, where she could feel it when she breathed.

She divided the food into thirds. One third for the run downriver. One third cached here, sealed in a tarred box under the floorboards, in case she came back this way needing to vanish again. The last third—

The last third she weighed in her hand for a long time before she understood what she was doing. She was rationing for a return trip. She was planning, already, to come back. To hand the map over in Port Merrow, collect nothing, owe nothing, and slide back into the brush like water into sand.

*You'll take it to my daughter,* Ines had said, gripping Ada's wrist with a strength that had surprised them both, as if dying were a job and she intended to see it through. *Mara. She works the ferries. Tell her I didn't forget the birthday. She'll know what that means.*

*I'm not a courier,* Ada had said, because she'd needed to say something, because kindness terrified her more than Voss did, though she hadn't known the name Voss yet, wouldn't learn it until later, and would spend whole nights wishing she never had.

*No,* Ines had said, and smiled with cracked lips. *You're worse. You're honest.*

Then she'd died with her hand still on the map, and Ada had pried the fingers loose one by one, gently, like opening a lock she hadn't asked for the key to, and buried her above the flood line where the water couldn't follow.

* * *

That night the rain came early, a warm preliminary drumming on the tarpaper roof, and Ada lay awake listening to it and remembering a different rain.

Four years ago. The Kestrel Viaduct, span three, a night of rain exactly like this one. She had signed off on those piers herself—she had *insisted* on reaming out the caissons when the contractor's concrete came back with honeycombing in the cores, had written the memo, had refused to initial the inspection sheet until the remediation was done. And the remediation had been done, on paper. She'd seen the paperwork afterward, at the tribunal, documents bearing her own initials certifying pours she had never witnessed, tests she had never run, dated to days she could prove—had tried to prove—she'd spent eighty miles away running a level line on the upper branch.

*Engineer Zhou,* the tribunal chairman had said, not unkindly, which had been the worst part, *the record shows your signature.*

The record showed her signature. Eleven times. On sheets she had never touched. Someone had lifted her initials from an earlier filing and stamped them forward like currency, and the someone was a man named Voss, chief of railway security, whose signature appeared nowhere on anything, whose name she didn't even learn until eight months after the bridge came down and took four men with it, because a man like Voss builds his alibis into the architecture of other people's careers.

She had run. That was the sentence she returned to in the dark, turning it over like a stone with something underneath. Not fled—run. There was a difference and the difference shamed her. Fleeing was what you did when you were beaten. Running was what you did instead of standing in front of twelve tired men and shouting the truth until they threw you in irons, which is what it would have taken, which is what she should have done, and every year the brush got thicker around that failure and harder to cut back through.

Because now, if she surfaced, she wasn't just a fugitive engineer claiming innocence. She was a fugitive with four years of forged papers and false names behind her, and Voss had had four years to burn whatever records might have cleared her. The truth had a shelf life. Hers had expired while she was busy staying invisible.

And yet.

Ada turned the lamp low and unrolled the map again, because looking at it was easier than looking at the ceiling. Past the depot junction, in Ines's cramped hand, ran a note she'd read a dozen times and only now truly heard:

*Depot keeps the divisional shipping files. Everything. They never threw anything out. Too much trouble to haul it out before the floods took the line.*

Shipping files. Manifests. Inspection records. For a division that had included the Kestrel crossing.

Her heart did something complicated and painful, part hope and part horror, because if there was paper in that drowned warehouse with her real initials beside dates she could account for—if there was a single sheet showing the true state of those caissons—then the map in her coat wasn't a dead woman's errand anymore. It was a door. And doors, once seen, could not be unseen.

Voss would know that too, eventually. Men like Voss always knew where the bodies and the paperwork were buried, because they'd buried both.

* * *

She launched at gray dawn, before the rain thickened, loading the skiff to its marks and no further. The river had come up overnight—she could tell by the mud collar on the pilings, a full hand higher than yesterday—and it moved with the brown, muscular confidence of water that has somewhere to be.

Ada shipped the oars, checked the bail bucket twice, and pushed off from the bank.

The current took her the way it takes everything in monsoon country: patiently, and all at once. She ran the first reach easy, reading the water ahead the way she read track grades—the smooth green tongues that meant channel, the ugly boiling V's that meant snag or weir—and by midmorning she had made six miles and bailed four times, which was within tolerance, barely.

At noon she ate a handful of rice and a strip of salt pork and let the boat drift while she studied the map's lower reaches. The route bent south past the derelict lock at Halfway Cut—marked on Ines's map with a small, ominous cross and the words *old lock, passable, watch for salvage men*—and then into the drowned valley proper, where the dead rails ran out into open water and the tops of signal masts stood up like drowned men's fingers.

Salvage men. Ada frowned at the notation. Ines hadn't wasted ink on warnings that didn't matter.

Around her the rain settled in for the duration, fine and ceaseless, silvering the river, erasing the far bank. Somewhere upstream behind her was a grave with a prospector in it, and somewhere downstream ahead was a daughter who didn't want to be found, and between them lay forty miles of rising water, a leaking boat, and a warehouse full of paper that could either hang her or free her.

Ada set the oars and pulled.

By late afternoon the banks began to close in, the jungle leaning over the channel, and through the gray curtain of rain she heard it before she saw it: the clang of metal on metal, rhythmic, deliberate, coming from the direction of Halfway Cut.

Someone was working the old lock.
