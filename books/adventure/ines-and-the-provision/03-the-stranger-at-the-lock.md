## 3. The Stranger at the Lock

The lock appeared out of the rain like a drowned cathedral.

Ada had been poling the boat through the flooded cut for six hours, her shoulders burning, the rain coming down in gray sheets that turned the whole world into water. The old navigation lock rose ahead of her—two stone towers flanking a gate that hadn't swung since before she was born, its ironwork furred with moss, its machinery seized into sculpture. Somewhere behind that gate was the lower reach of the river, and beyond it, if Ines's map could be believed, the channel that would carry her toward the depot.

She brought the boat alongside the lock keeper's walk and tied off to a rusted bollard, grateful for any excuse to stop moving. Her hands had gone past pain into a kind of numbness that worried her more than pain would have. She flexed them, watching the fingers respond sluggishly, and told herself it was the cold.

The lock keeper's cottage crouched on the embankment above, roof half-collapsed, windows blind with grime. Smoke was rising from it.

Ada went still. Smoke meant fire. Fire meant people, and people meant questions, and questions were the one cargo she couldn't afford to carry. She thought about untying the boat, about finding some other way around—there was always another way around, that was the whole discipline of her life now—but the map said otherwise. The map said the cut below the lock was the only navigable channel this far north, everything else silted or collapsed, and the monsoon was already two days early. She didn't have time to be careful.

She climbed the embankment anyway, keeping low, one hand on the wet stone.

The cottage door stood open. Inside, a lantern burned on a table scarred by decades of boots and mugs, and beside it sat a woman Ada's age or near it, short-haired, broad-shouldered, drying something that looked like a diving mask with a rag of yellow cloth. She looked up without surprise.

"You're making enough noise on that bank to wake the dead," the woman said. "And the dead around here are mostly railway men. They're spiteful sleepers."

"I'm just passing through."

"Nobody passes through." The woman set down the mask. "That's the point of a place like this. You want tea? It's not good tea, but it's hot, and you look like a woman who's been poling against the current since dawn, which is stupid, because the current's going your way and you've been fighting it out of habit."

Ada stood in the doorway, rain running off her oilskin, and weighed the doorframe between herself and the river. "I don't take tea from strangers."

"No," the woman agreed. "You don't take anything from strangers. That's why you're soaked to the bone and eating whatever's in those tins I can see through your pack strap. Sit down. I'm Ruth."

"I didn't tell you my name."

"You didn't have to." Ruth poured a second cup without asking and pushed it across the table. "Ada Zhou. Railroad engineer. Wanted in connection with the Kestrel Bridge collapse, eleven dead, reward posted in every station house from here to the coast. There's a notice with your face on it nailed up in Harrow Junction, except the face they drew makes you look like a criminal. Whoever did the sketch flattered you, honestly."

The rain filled the silence. Ada's hand had found the pry bar under her coat without her deciding anything; she made herself let go of it.

"You're going to turn me in," she said. It wasn't a question. There was no point pretending anymore—the pretense had died the moment Ruth said *Kestrel Bridge*.

Ruth laughed, a short, hard sound with no pleasure in it. "Turn you in. To who? Voss?" She said the name the way other people spat. "His patrol boats run the main channel, not this ditch. And even if he came up here himself, what's he paying these days? Fifty? A hundred? I make more than that in an afternoon pulling copper off a drowned barge, and I don't have to stand in a courtroom while they do it." She leaned back, chair creaking. "Sit down, engineer. If I wanted the money, you'd already be tied to your own mast."

Ada didn't sit. But she didn't leave either, and after a moment she crossed the room and took the cup, because her body overruled her judgment, the way bodies do when they've been worked too long. The tea was terrible—bark and something metallic—and it was the best thing she'd tasted in days.

"How do you know Voss pays fifty?"

"Because I used to work for him." Ruth said it flatly, watching Ada over the rim of her own cup. "Salvage division. When the railway wants something raised off the bottom of a river—a locomotive, a bridge span, a body—they send for me. Three years. Then I stopped."

"People who stop working for Voss usually stop breathing shortly after."

"Yes," Ruth said. "Usually."

* * *

They talked, or rather Ruth talked and Ada rationed out answers like the last of her food, and somewhere in the next hour the shape of the woman across the table resolved itself. Ruth was a salvage diver who knew every closed line and drowned structure in the northern country better than the survey maps did, because she'd been under all of them. She kept her gear in the lock keeper's cottage during monsoon season and worked the flood channels when the water was high enough to float her gear raft. And she owed Ines.

"Ines," Ada repeated. "You know her."

"Knew." Ruth corrected herself with a small wince. "Knows. Present tense until someone tells me different. Why?"

Ada looked into her cup. She had rehearsed nothing, and the words came out clumsy. "I found her on the old Merrow spur, four days ago. She'd fallen. Broken hip, maybe worse. She was dying when I got there."

Ruth set her cup down very carefully, as though the table might break. "Go on."

"She had a map. A provision depot, flooded, past the flood line. She traded it to me—for a promise. That I'd deliver it to her daughter in Port Merrow." Ada heard her own voice go dry. "She died about an hour after I reached her. I buried her by the track and marked the stones."

For a long moment Ruth didn't move. The lantern hissed. Outside, the rain intensified, drumming on the ruined roof like applause from a hostile crowd.

"The daughter's name is Mara," Ruth said finally. "Mara Voss."

The name landed like ice water. "Voss."

"Ferry mechanic in Port Merrow. Good one. And yes—that Voss. Ines never told the girl who her father was, and when she found out, that's why she left. Or part of why." Ruth rubbed her face with both hands. "Ines pulled me out of the Wren Channel seven years ago. My air line fouled, my tender panicked and ran, and Ines—prospectors aren't supposed to dive, she had no business being down there—came in after me on a breath held out of pure stubbornness. Carried me up. Never let me pay her back, never let me forget it either." She looked up, eyes red-rimmed and furious with grief. "And you're telling me she's dead on a spur line and some stranger has her map."

"Not a stranger." Ada heard how thin the protest sounded. "She chose me. I didn't ask for any of it."

"No. Ines never asked for anything. That was her whole disease." Ruth stood abruptly and began moving around the cottage, pulling gear from corners with sudden violence—a canvas bag, coiled rope, a waterproof case she checked and rechecked. "Where's your boat?"

"At the bollard. Why?"

"Because you're going to need it, and you're going to need me in it. That channel below the lock floods fast when the monsoon comes up the valley, and it's choked with debris from the old timber boom—you'll hole your hull on the first submerged snag and lose everything, including the map, including yourself. I know where the snags are. I've mapped them from underneath." She slung the bag over her shoulder. "Also, you pole like a farmer. No offense."

"Considerable offense."

"Good. Means you're listening." Ruth stopped at the door and turned, and for the first time her voice lost its edge. "I'm not doing this for you, engineer. I'm doing it for Ines, and for Mara, who deserves to hear how her mother died from someone who was actually there. You happen to be the boat it rides in. That's all."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I follow you downriver anyway, and we both waste energy pretending we're not traveling together." Ruth smiled without warmth. "Your call."

Ada looked at her—at this sharp-tongued woman who knew her face from a wanted notice and her enemy's name from the inside, who owed a debt to a dead prospector and intended to collect it from Ada whether Ada consented or not. Every instinct she'd honed in three years of hiding said: *alone is safe, alone is survivable, alone means no one can sell you.* 

But alone also meant poling a leaking boat through a debris-choked flood channel in the teeth of an early monsoon, and the map was Ines's dying wish sitting in her breast pocket, and Mara Voss was waiting in Port Merrow for news that would land on her like a collapsing bridge.

"One condition," Ada said. "You don't ask me about Kestrel Bridge. Not yet."

Ruth studied her for a long moment. "Fine. And you don't ask why I really left Voss's salvage division. Not yet." She stuck out a hand, calloused and scarred at the knuckles. "Two secrets, circling each other like dogs. We'll see which one bites first."

Ada shook it. Her grip was weaker than she wanted it to be, and she suspected Ruth noticed and was polite enough—or cruel enough—not to say so.

They loaded the boat in the last gray light, Ruth stowing her gear with the economy of someone who had packed for drowning many times. As Ada cast off the line, Ruth paused, one foot on the gunwale, and nodded upstream toward the blackening sky.

"For what it's worth," she said, "that cloud bank over the ridge isn't ordinary rain. I've seen that color twice before. Both times, the river came up faster than anyone believed possible."

Ada followed her gaze. The clouds over the western ridge had gone a deep bruised green-black, piled high and lit from within by silent heat lightning, and the wind had begun to carry a smell she recognized from her childhood in the delta—the smell of a storm still hours away but already filling its lungs.

"How fast are we talking?" she asked.

Ruth stepped down into the boat and took the second pole. "Faster than us," she said. "Row."
