## 4. Monsoon Rising

The storm came early. Ruth felt it in her teeth before she saw it—a pressure behind her jaw like a fist closing, the kind of ache that had followed her through six monsoon seasons on the salvage boats, and she'd learned to trust that ache over any forecast.

"Sky's wrong," she said, and stopped poling.

Ada looked up from the chart spread across her knees. She'd been studying it for an hour without speaking, which Ruth was learning was simply how Ada did things—silently, thoroughly, as if the world were a set of drawings she intended to correct. "It's noon," Ada said. "Storms don't come at noon."

"This one does." Ruth pointed with the pole. The southern horizon had gone the color of wet slate, and above it the clouds were stacking themselves into something with architecture, something built, tier upon tier rising like a city no one had ever lived in. The wind had died completely. That was the worst part—the dead air, the river lying flat as poured glass, everything holding its breath. "That's not weather. That's a wall."

Ada folded the chart with quick, precise movements and stowed it in the oilskin bag between them. "How far to shelter?"

Ruth scanned the bank. They were passing through country where the railway had once run along the river's edge before the floods took it—here and there a rotting piling broke the water's surface, and once the rusted hulk of a flatcar sat half-sunk in the shallows like the ribcage of something enormous. And there, ahead and to the left, where the bank rose slightly: a signal box on stilts, its windows dark, its ladder still standing.

"There." She swung the pole. "Signal box. Maybe two hundred meters if we cut across the current."

"The boat leaks."

"The boat leaks slower than you drown." Ruth was already moving, driving the pole down into the muck, feeling for the old ballast bed of the drowned line beneath the silt. It was there—she could feel it, the firm gravel spine of the embankment running parallel to shore, and she used it, walking the pole along it like a road. "Get the gear bagged. Everything you can't afford to lose goes in oilskin, and everything you can't afford to lose twice goes inside your shirt."

Ada didn't argue. That was another thing Ruth was learning about her: she argued with words, endlessly, but when it mattered she moved like water finding the low ground. In under a minute the chart was wrapped, the food was wrapped, the little brass instruments Ada kept wrapped in cloth even from Ruth's eyes were wrapped, and Ada had stripped off her boots and tied them around her neck by the laces.

The first drop hit the water between them like a thrown stone.

Then the sky opened.

* * *

They climbed the ladder with the rain trying to peel their hands off the rungs. Ruth went last, hauling the gear bag up by its strap while the river below them turned white and chaotic, rain so dense it became a kind of falling architecture of its own. Inside the signal box, they collapsed against the wall and lay gasping on the floorboards while the storm hammered the tin roof like a mob demanding entry.

The box was small—one room, maybe four meters square, with the levers still mounted along one wall, forty of them in a row, painted black, their handles worn bright by hands fifty years dead. A bench. A stove with a rusted pipe going through the roof. Someone had left a coil of rope and a clay jug, both furred with mold. Through the leeward window, the world was a gray blur.

Ruth got the little spirit stove lit in the lee of the bench, wedged a tin cup of river water over it, and watched the flame fight the damp. Ada sat with her back against the lever frame, arms around her knees, watching the door.

"You count exits," Ruth said.

"What?"

"You've been counting exits since I met you. Every room, every stretch of river. You do it without thinking about it." Ruth nodded at the door, the window, the trapdoor in the floor that led down to the stilts. "Door, window, hatch. You've already ranked them."

Ada said nothing. Rain drummed. Somewhere out in the flood, something metal groaned against something else metal, slow and mournful.

"I used to do that too," Ruth said. "Still do. It's what working for Voss teaches you."

She hadn't planned to say it. She'd told herself she wouldn't—not yet, maybe not ever—and then she'd said it anyway, the way you step off a bank into deep water, all at once because otherwise you never would.

Ada's head came around slowly. "Voss."

"Chief of railway security. You know the name." Ruth fed a twist of dry bark to the stove. Her hands were steady; she made sure of that. "I can see you know the name."

"How."

"Six years ago I was a diver on the security salvage crews. Wreck recovery, evidence retrieval, whatever Voss wanted pulled out of the water and whatever he wanted left there." The water in the cup was shivering now, close to boiling. "Good pay. Better than the civilian boats. You didn't ask questions about why a bridge inspection report needed recovering from a sunken barge, or why certain manifests went into the incinerator barge instead of the archive. You didn't ask, and you got paid, and after a while not asking starts to feel like a skill. Like a trade."

"And then?"

"And then I found his ledgers." Ruth looked at her. "Not the official ones. The real ones. I was sent down to recover a strongbox from a scuttled patrol launch—official story was it went down in a squall with records aboard. It hadn't gone down in any squall. It had been holed below the waterline, deliberate, neat, three holes the size of my fist. And inside the strongbox, sealed in wax, were ledgers. Payments. Dates. Names of inspectors who signed reports they never wrote, and amounts, and initials at the bottom of every page. *V.*"

The storm hit a new register, a long rolling boom of thunder that Ruth felt in the floorboards, in her sternum.

"I copied what I could. Dived back twice more before anyone noticed the strongbox had been opened. Then I ran." She laughed, short and without humor. "Six years running. Salvage work on the closed lines, where nobody checks papers and nobody asks why a woman who reads current like a priest reads scripture is hiding out in the drowned country. Same reason you're here, I'd guess."

Ada was very still. Rain sheeted past the window. When she finally spoke, her voice was flat and careful, each word placed like a stone in a wall. "You knew. When you saw me at the lock. You knew who I was."

"I recognized you from the wanted notices. Your face was on every depot wall from here to the capital for a year. 'Engineer Ada Zhou, wanted in connection with the Kestrel Bridge collapse, seventeen dead.'" Ruth held her gaze. "Yes. I knew."

"And you helped me anyway."

"I owed Ines." Ruth turned back to the stove, though the water was boiling now and needed nothing. "She pulled me out of a flooded shaft three winters ago when my air line fouled. Carried me half a kilometer on her back with a broken wrist. So when she asked me to watch the river for a woman traveling alone toward Port Merrow, I watched." She paused. "But that's not the whole truth, and you're too smart to accept it as the whole truth, so here's the rest. I helped you because I've spent six years sitting on copies of those ledgers with no way to use them. A runaway diver with stolen documents is just a thief. But the engineer who was framed? The engineer whose name is on the falsified inspection report for Kestrel Bridge?" She looked at Ada again. "You're not a fugitive, Ada. You're a witness. You're the thing those ledgers have been waiting for."

The silence stretched. Outside, the storm raged on with the patience of something that intended to be here for days. Water was coming through the roof seam now, dripping onto the floorboards in a slow irregular rhythm, like a clock counting down something.

"They'll kill you for this," Ada said at last. "If Voss finds out what you have. What you know."

"He's been trying to find me for six years." Ruth shrugged. "I'm good at not being found. Question is whether you are—or whether you're done pretending that's a life." She leaned forward. "I've read your file. All of it, before I ran. Do you know what's in it? Not evidence. There was never any evidence against you. There's a timeline, and a signature, and a decision made before the bridge even fell. Voss needed someone to blame, and you were the engineer of record, and you were young, and you had no family to make noise. That's the whole case. That's what you've been running from for five years. A filing decision."

Ada's face did something complicated—grief and fury fighting for the same ground. "I ran because nobody would have listened."

"Somebody would have listened eventually. But that's not even the point." Ruth stood, crossed to the window, watched the gray chaos. "The point is you're not running anymore. You're carrying a dead woman's map to her daughter, down a river, straight through the middle of the country where Voss hunts. That's not hiding, Ada. That's a declaration. You just haven't admitted it yet."

* * *

The storm held them prisoner for two days.

Two days in a four-meter room, taking turns sleeping on the bench, boiling river water, splitting a dwindling store of dried fish and hardtack. Two days of the rain's roar and the drip through the roof seam and the levers standing in their row like a jury. They talked more than either of them had talked in years—about Ines, about the drowned lines, about the geometry of bridges and the physics of currents—and carefully, carefully, not about the future.

On the second night, with the storm finally losing its argument and the wind dropping to a long exhausted sigh, Ruth woke to find Ada awake, sitting by the window with the chart open on her knees, memorizing it by the light of the spirit stove.

"We lost time," Ada said quietly, without turning. "Two days. The river will be high and fast, and the depot sits past the flood line—we'll be running rapids that were canals last month."

"I know these waters. High water is my trade."

"And Voss will know the storm flushed every hiding place on the river. His patrols will be out sweeping the banks within a day of it clearing." Ada rolled the chart. "We should split up. You take your ledgers and go north. I take the map and go on alone. One of us might make it."

Ruth was quiet for a moment. Then she said, "No."

"It's the sensible—"

"No." She said it gently, but she said it. "Ines didn't trade that map to a stranger so the stranger could die alone in the flood country. And I didn't spend six years keeping those ledgers dry to hand them to a woman who's going to get herself shot on some nameless bend of river with no one to carry the story out." She stood, joints stiff from the bench, and joined Ada at the window. Below them the river had begun to drop, brown and muscular, dragging whole trees along in its grip. "Besides. If Voss is sweeping the banks, you'll need a diver. There are things underwater between here and that depot. Things I put there myself, six years ago, when I was still signing his payroll."

Ada turned to look at her. In the stove light her eyes were shadowed and fierce and, for the first time since the lock, not entirely alone.

"What things?" she said.

Ruth smiled, and outside, far downstream, a searchlight swept the flooded tree line—slow, methodical, patient as the storm had been.

"That," she said, "is the next problem."
