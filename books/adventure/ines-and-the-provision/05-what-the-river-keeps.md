## 5. What the River Keeps

The river changed its mind about them an hour past the drowned junction.

Ada felt it before she saw it—a new note in the water's voice against the hull, a hollowness where there had been only the steady conversation of current and wood. She'd spent eleven years listening to boats talk. Her father had built ferries on the coast before the railways ate his trade, and he'd taught her that every hull was honest: it told you what it carried, what it struck, and when it was dying.

This one was dying.

"Ruth." She cut the small outboard to half-throttle and put her palm flat on the deck planking. "Bail."

Ruth looked up from the map spread across her knees, rain sheeting off her oilskin hood. "We're not taking water."

"We are." Ada pointed at the seam along the starboard chine, just aft of where her boot rested. A dark thread of river was welling up through the caulking, thin as wire, patient as anything in this country. "It started after the snag field. I heard it change."

Ruth swore—something short and professional—and reached for the bail bucket without another word. That was the thing about Ruth that Ada kept forgetting to distrust: she didn't argue with bad news. She just picked it up and started carrying it.

They ran for the next high bank with the bucket going double-time between them, the outboard coughing on fuel that had sat too long in its lines, the monsoon pressing down like a hand on the back of the neck. The leak didn't stay thin. By the time they grounded the boat against a shelf of black mud beneath a leaning stand of bamboo, the bilge sloshed above their ankles, and the thread had become a seam had become a wound.

* * *

They beached her, rolled her, and found it: a split plank six feet long, sprung open along a rotted scarf joint that some previous owner had papered over with tar and prayer. The repair was possible. Ada could see the whole shape of it in her head—drain the joint, pack it with the spare oakum, clamp it with the screw jack from her survey kit, let the tar set overnight.

Possible, if they stayed here a full day.

Impossible, if they wanted to reach the depot before the flood peak turned the last navigable channel into whitewater.

She crouched over the wound in the hull with the rain running off her chin and did the arithmetic she didn't want to do. Weight. They were carrying too much of it. Two people's gear, two people's food, Ruth's salvage tools, Ada's instruments, and the crate—the long, sealed crate Ruth had lashed under the tarp at the lock and never once explained, which Ada had never once asked about, because asking questions was how you got answers you couldn't unhear.

"The crate goes," Ada said.

Ruth went very still. Rain ticked off her hood. "No."

"It's forty pounds of dead weight and we're sitting on a mud shelf in rising water with a hole in our floor. It goes, or we both go slower, and slower means the channel closes ahead of us."

"It doesn't go." Ruth's voice didn't rise. That was somehow worse. She stepped between Ada and the tarp like a woman standing in front of a door. "That crate is the reason I'm on this river, Ada. You get your depot. I get my crate. We don't audit each other's reasons—that was the deal."

"There was no deal. There was a stranger at a lock who knew my face and offered help anyway, and I was stupid enough to need it."

"And I was stupid enough to give it." Something moved behind Ruth's eyes—not anger, older than that. Fear, maybe, wearing anger's coat. "You want to throw weight overboard? Throw your survey kit. Throw the tent. Don't touch the crate."

Ada opened her mouth to say something she would regret, and the river made the argument for her.

It came up fast—the way monsoon water always came up fast, all at once, as if somewhere upstream a door had been opened. One moment the mud shelf held them; the next, brown water slid over their boots, then their shins, and the boat lifted on her keel and began to swing sideways into the current with the split plank still gaping.

They worked like animals for the next hour, and Ada would remember it afterward in fragments: Ruth hauling the bow line around a bamboo root thick as a man's leg; Ada bailing with the bucket while the jack clamps bit into the swollen plank; the crate sliding on the flooded deck, and Ruth throwing herself across it bodily, arms locked, screaming at Ada to hold the line, *hold the line*, and Ada holding it, because there was nothing else left to hold.

But the provisions locker had been stowed forward, and forward was where the water went first.

When they finally grounded her again—higher up the bank, wedged between two deadfall trunks, the plank patched well enough to hold if the river allowed them the courtesy of a night—the locker stood open and half-empty. Rice gone. Half the fuel cans gone. The dried fish, the condensed milk, the medical kit's lower tray, all of it silted and scattered downstream or sunk into the mud of the bank like offerings.

And one corner of the crate, where the floodwater had found a seam of its own, was soft. Warped. Weeping something dark and pulpy that might have been paper.

Ruth knelt over it and didn't speak for a long time.

* * *

They camped under the deadfalls because there was nowhere else. Ada got a fire going with damp kindling and stubbornness, and they ate a measured handful of rice each from the tin that had survived, and watched the river run past their refuge like a country that had decided against them.

It was Ruth who finally opened the damaged corner of the crate, working with a knife by firelight, peeling back warped canvas and swollen board.

Inside were ledgers. Or they had been ledgers. Three of the five volumes were fused into a single brick of pulp and mold, ink bloomed into gray ghosts, pages welded shut. Ruth lifted the brick out with both hands, the way you'd lift a dead bird, and held it in the firelight, and Ada watched her face do something it hadn't done at the lock, or in the signal box, or anywhere on three days of river.

It broke. Quietly, the way things break underwater.

"That's four years," Ruth said. "Four years of Voss's requisition ledgers. Payments. Names. Dates. I copied the critical pages twice—I hid the duplicates separately—but these are the originals, and originals are what make tribunals sit up." She set the brick down in the mud with a gentleness that hurt to watch. "Half of it's still readable. Maybe. If it dries right. Which it won't, out here."

Ada looked at the ruined books, and then at the half-empty locker, and then at the boat with its patched plank and its bilge that still whispered, and something gave way inside her that had been holding since the lock.

"This is my fault."

"Don't."

"I stowed the locker forward. I said she rode fine. I've run boats since I was nine years old, Ruth, and I loaded us like a Sunday excursionist because—" She stopped. The words underneath were rising now, and they weren't about the locker at all. "Because I wanted to be moving. Because stopping means thinking, and thinking means—" She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "I ran."

"You ran from a frame-up. That's called surviving."

"I ran from everything." The confession came out of her like water through the split plank, finding the gap, widening it. "The bridge came down at Kettering Cut and eleven men went into the water, and I knew—I *knew*—the inspection reports were falsified, because I wrote the counter-report, the real one, and Voss buried it and used my signature page to build his version. And instead of standing up in front of the tribunal and saying *look at my report, look at the dates, look at the steel certs*, I got on a freight car north with a bag and a false name and I let them call me a murderer for eleven years." She laughed, and it wasn't a laugh. "Ines is dying on a track bed and hands a stranger her whole life in a folded map, and I can't even hand a tribunal mine."

The fire popped. The river ran. Ruth said nothing for a while, and Ada was grateful, because pity would have finished her.

Then Ruth said, "You know what I did when I found those ledgers?"

Ada shook her head.

"I ran too. Straight out the side door of the security office with copies sewn into my coat, and I didn't stop running for two years. Diving work, salvage work, any job that kept me wet and anonymous." She nudged the ruined brick with her boot. "So don't sell me the sermon about fighting. Neither of us fought. We hid. And hiding has a cost, and today the river collected some of it." She looked up, firelight catching the hard planes of her face. "The difference is what you do when hiding stops working."

"And if it never stops working? If we crawl to this depot and it's flooded to the rafters and empty?"

"Then we'll have crawled for nothing, same as everyone does eventually." Ruth shrugged, and it was such a salvage-diver's shrug—practical, unsentimental, the philosophy of a woman who made her living pulling value out of wrecks. "But Ines didn't strike me as a woman who traded in nothing. And neither, for all your practice at it, are you."

Ada pulled her knees up and watched the water. Somewhere below the surface of her fear, an old instrument was taking a reading she didn't want: the bearing back south, toward the closed lines, toward the life where nobody knew her name and no tribunal waited. It would be so easy. Beach the boat at the next rail crossing, walk inland, vanish again into the abandoned geometry of the map. Eleven years of practice. She was good at disappearing. It was the only thing she'd ever been praised for.

But Ines's map was folded against her ribs, dry in its oilskin sleeve, and it weighed more than the crate ever had.

"We lose a day drying what we can save," Ada said at last, her voice flat with decision and exhaustion in equal measure. "We ration to half. And tomorrow we run the channel at first light, before the peak."

Ruth nodded once. Then she lay back against the deadfall, pulled her hood down over her eyes, and said, to the darkness or to Ada, "For what it's worth—I'm glad it was your fault. Yours I can work with."

Ada didn't answer. But she sat up most of the night with the surviving ledger pages pegged above the fire, turning them like a woman learning a language, and by the time the sky went gray she could read enough of the ghost-ink to understand why Ruth had crossed a country to keep them dry.

One entry, dated nine days before the Kettering bridge failed, bore Voss's own initials beside a payment code Ada recognized from her old life: *structural certification, expedited.*

He hadn't just buried her report. He'd sold the inspection.

Ada folded the page into the oilskin sleeve beside Ines's map, and looked downriver, where the channel narrowed between drowned trees and the water ran high and brown and fast toward whatever the depot kept.

"First light," she said, and woke Ruth.
