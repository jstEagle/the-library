## 6. The Depot

The river changed its mind about them twice before they found the depot.

First it narrowed, funneling them between drowned pilings where the current ran like a millrace and Ruth had to pole while Ada bailed—bailing had become a religion now, a rhythm of scoop and fling that Ada performed without thinking, the way other people breathed. Then it widened again into a brown lake dotted with the crowns of trees, dead oaks standing to their shoulders in water, their branches bare as lightning rods. Somewhere under all of it was a rail line. Somewhere under all of it were towns that had surrendered to the monsoon decades ago and never been given back.

"Map," Ruth said.

Ada held the oilskin packet between her teeth while she scooped. The map inside had gone soft at the folds from handling, Ines's pencil lines blurring where Ada's thumb had worried them for three days straight. The depot sat on a rise—or had once been a rise—marked with a symbol Ada hadn't understood until this morning: a small square with a diagonal slash, drawn in a hand shakier than the rest.

"That's a flood gauge," Ruth said when Ada showed her. "Slash means high-water mark. She's telling you how deep it gets."

"How deep?"

Ruth squinted at the pencil line against the little square's edge. "Two-thirds up the wall."

"And we're arriving when the river's already—" Ada looked out at the drowned oaks, at the water lapping the tops of fence posts that marked nothing anymore. "We're arriving at two-thirds."

"We're arriving at two-thirds," Ruth agreed cheerfully, and put her pole down hard.

* * *

They found it in the last hour of light, which felt less like luck than like mercy, and Ada did not believe in mercy.

It rose out of the floodwater the way a whale rises: first a dark suggestion beneath the surface, then a long gray back breaking through—the roof of the provision depot, corrugated iron furred with moss, sagging in the middle but holding. A loading dock faced the old rail spur, its timbers black with sixty years of wet. The water stood chest-high against the dock's edge, and when Ada leaned over the gunwale to look, she saw the doors below the waterline, painted numbers still legible through the murk: *BAY 3. BAY 4.*

Ines's mark pointed not to the dock but around the far side, where the building's stone foundation climbed out of the flood—a gable end, a ventilation grate, and above the grate, scratched into the mortar with something sharp, a single letter: *I.*

"She knew you'd come," Ruth said quietly.

"She hoped." Ada shipped the pole. "There's a difference."

"There is." Ruth looked at her sideways. "But I've known a lot of dying people, and dying people don't hope small. If she carved her initial, she meant for you to get in."

Getting in meant getting out of the boat, and getting out of the boat meant swimming, and swimming meant the manifests—if there were manifests—would be soaked before they ever saw light. Ada circled the building twice, reading it the way she read track: structure, load, failure points. The gable end was stone, older than the rest, some pre-railway farm building swallowed by the depot's construction. The ventilation grate was rusted through at one corner. Above it, half-hidden by ivy gone feral, a hatch cover.

"Chimney shaft," Ruth said, following her gaze. "Old drying kiln, maybe. If the shaft runs vertical, the top floor might be dry."

"Might."

"You have a better word?"

"No," Ada admitted. "Might is the best word I've got left."

* * *

Ruth went up first because she was lighter and because she argued faster, and Ada let her win the argument because the truth was Ada's arms were shaking and had been since morning. She watched Ruth's hands find holds in the ivy and the mortared joints, watched her haul herself onto the hatch lip and lift it, and then Ruth's face appeared over the edge, backlit by gray sky, grinning like a skull.

"Get up here, engineer. You're going to want to see this."

The shaft was narrow and smelled of bird droppings and cold stone, and the top floor of the depot was dry.

Not just dry—intact. Ada came up through the hatch into a long room running the length of the building, floored in hardwood gone silver with age, lit by clerestory windows crusted with salt-film but unbroken. Crates lined the walls in ranks, stenciled with railway codes and dates going back forty years. Dust lay thick as snowfall. And in the center of the room, arranged with a deliberateness that stopped Ada's breath, someone had built a camp: a bedroll, a spirit stove, a stack of tinned food, a lantern. Everything squared away. Everything waiting.

Beside the bedroll, weighted down with a flat iron, lay a ledger.

Ada crossed the room without deciding to. Her knees cracked when she knelt. The ledger was bound in oilcloth, double-wrapped, tied with waxed cord—and beneath the wrapping, when she peeled it back, the cover read:

*NORTHERN PROVISION DEPOT — RECEIVING MANIFESTS — YEARS 11–14.*

Year twelve. The year of the bridge.

Her hands stopped working. That was the thing she noticed first—not fear, not triumph, just a mechanical failure, her fingers refusing the cord as if the body understood before the mind did what was sitting under the flat iron. Because she had spent six years telling herself that the documents proving her innocence were gone, destroyed, or locked in Voss's safe, and here was a receiving manifest from the provision depot, and provision depots supplied construction crews, and construction crews kept records of every load of steel and every bag of cement that crossed their docks—

"Ada." Ruth's voice, careful. "Open it."

"I know what it is."

"Then open it anyway."

She opened it.

The pages were ruled columns: date, consignment, quantity, condition, inspector's sign-off. Her eyes ran down the entries for March of year twelve, the month the Kestrel Viaduct came down with a work train on it, the month eleven men died and one engineer—herself—took the blame for steel she'd flagged as substandard in writing, three times, in reports that vanished from the file. And there, in the column marked CONDITION, in a clerk's neat hand:

*Bridge steel, lot 44-K. Delivered cracked. Rejected per inspection. Returned to sender — V., personal authorization.*

Returned to sender. Except it hadn't been returned. Ada had measured lot 44-K herself, had run her calipers along the fracture lines and written the report that ended her career. The steel had stayed on site because Voss signed the delivery docket himself and burned the rejection. But he couldn't burn what had already been logged at the depot it came *from*. He'd never even known this place existed. It was beyond the flood line. It was off every working route. It was the kind of hole a man like Voss believed the river kept shut forever.

"He falsified the inspection reports," she heard herself say. Her voice sounded far away. "I always thought—I thought he forged my signature on acceptance papers. That was bad enough. But this—" She turned the page. Another entry. Another rejection, another *V.* "He rejected the steel in writing. Here. Six years ago. He *knew*, and he put it on the bridge anyway because the delay would cost him his contract bonus, and then when it fell he needed an engineer to blame, and I was the one who'd filed the complaint, so I was—" 

She stopped. The room tilted gently, the way rooms do when you've eaten too little for too many days.

Ruth crouched beside her. For once she wasn't smiling.

"The map was never just a map," Ada said.

"No."

"Ines prospected these lines for thirty years. She must have sheltered here during the floods. She must have *read* these." Ada looked down at the ledger, at the clerk's dead meticulous hand. "She knew what she was giving me. She was dying and she gave me the one thing that could—" Her throat closed. "Why? She didn't know me. I was a stranger on a closed line."

"Maybe she didn't give it to you," Ruth said. "Maybe she gave it to whoever came. She just made sure somebody came." She touched the ledger's edge, lightly, almost reverently. "Dying people don't hope small. Told you."

* * *

They worked until the light failed, and then by lantern light, because neither of them could stop.

The stores were everything Ines had promised and more: rice and flour sealed in tin-lined crates, salt pork in crocks, lamp oil, rope, a carpenter's chest with tools still bright in their grease. Whoever had stocked this depot had done it expecting a war or a famine, and gotten instead sixty years of silence. Ada catalogued it all in her head automatically—engineer's habit, inventory as prayer—and for the first time since the boat began leaking, the arithmetic of survival tipped the right way.

But it was the paper that mattered. They found three more ledgers in the clerk's desk, and a bundle of delivery dockets rubber-banded together, and a correspondence file with Voss's name on four letters—all of them innocuous alone, all of them damning in company. Ada laid them out on the dry floorboards in chronological order and read the whole record of the crime in one evening: the cracked lots, the rejections, the personal authorizations, and finally, in April of year twelve, a memo on railway letterhead instructing the depot clerk to *discontinue written condition reports for bridge consignments, per Chief Inspector Voss.* Two weeks later, the viaduct fell. Three weeks after that, Ada Zhou was arrested.

"They'll call it forgery," she said. Ruth was watching her across the spread of paper. "Voss will say I fabricated these. Planted them."

"Will he?" Ruth picked up the memo, turned it toward the lantern. "Look at the watermark. Look at the clerk's initials—there's a verification stamp, bottom corner, depot registry number. This isn't a copy, Ada. This is the book. Books don't lie about being books."

"Books can be stolen."

"Not from here." Ruth gestured at the flooded world outside the windows, at the drowned spur and the dead oaks and the two-thirds-up-the-wall waterline. "Nobody steals anything from a place the river eats. He'd have to explain how he even knew it existed." She set the memo down. "This is why he'll come. Not because he suspects. Because Ines traded this map before she died, and somewhere, somehow, word travels. It always does."

Ada looked at the ledgers, at the stores, at the dry floor under her knees, and felt something she hadn't felt in six years and barely recognized: not safety, exactly. Momentum. The sense of standing on solid ground with a clear line ahead.

"We take everything that matters," she said. "Ledgers, dockets, the memo. We wrap them in oilcloth, we split them between us, and if the boat goes down again, one set survives." She was already planning routes in her head—upriver to the tribunal circuit at Corven Ford, or east to Port Merrow and Mara, and the word *testify* arrived in her mind without permission and sat there, enormous, refusing to leave. "And then I stop hiding."

Ruth raised an eyebrow. "That's a big sentence for a woman who's spent six years being nobody."

"I was nobody because being somebody got eleven men killed." Ada wrapped the memo carefully, her hands steady now. "Except it didn't. *He* killed them. And I have spent six years letting him spend me as the receipt." She tied the cord. "No more."

Outside, the light died over the floodwater, and the river murmured against the stones of the old foundation, patient as a creditor.

And far downstream—though neither of them could know it yet—an engine bell rang twice in the dark, and a patrol boat turned upriver toward the depot, running without lights.
