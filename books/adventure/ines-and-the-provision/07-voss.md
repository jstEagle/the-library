## 7. Voss

Ruth heard the engine before she saw it—a low, diesel-throated growl rolling up the flooded channel, wrong in every way a sound could be wrong. Nothing that belonged out here ran an engine like that. Not the fisherfolk with their patched outboards, not the timber rafts drifting down from the cuttings. That was a patrol boat, and it was coming fast.

She was on the depot's upper catwalk, prying at a rusted vent cover Ada had sworn led to the maintenance tunnel, when the sound reached her. Below, through the shattered skylight, she could see Ada in the flooded ground floor, waist-deep in brown water, hauling the last of Ines's crates onto a raft of salvaged planks. The manifests were wrapped in oilcloth against her chest, strapped there like a second heartbeat.

"Ada." Ruth's voice came out flat and low, the way it did when she was scared and refused to admit it. "Company."

Ada's head came up. For one second—just one—Ruth saw the whole history of the woman in her face: the bridge, the trial, the years of running compressed into a single flinch. Then it was gone, banked like a fire under wet ash.

"How long?"

"Minutes. Maybe less." Ruth swung down the ladder, boots ringing on iron. "There's a side channel behind the reed beds. If they come up the main line of the flood, we might have—"

The engine note changed. Slowed. Then died altogether.

Silence on the water is its own kind of scream. Ruth stood at the broken window, watching the mist off the monsoon swell part around a grey hull sliding into view. Two figures at the rail. One of them raised binoculars.

"That's him," she said. Her mouth had gone dry. "Voss."

"You're sure?"

"I'd know his boat before I'd know my own mother's face." She almost laughed at that, and it came out ugly. "I serviced that engine for two years. I know the sound it makes when he's feeling patient, and I know the sound it makes when he isn't." She listened again. "He isn't."

* * *

They had perhaps four minutes, and they spent them well, because Ada Zhou had never in her life spent a crisis badly.

"The tunnel," Ada said, unrolling the map across a dry corner of the office mezzanine, her finger stabbing at a mark Ines had inked decades ago. "It runs west under the loading platform, comes up past the old pump house. Flooded at the far end, but passable if you keep to the right wall. You take the manifests. I'll draw them off."

"No." Ruth heard herself say it before she'd decided to. "No, that's backwards and you know it. He wants *you*. You go through the tunnel, quiet as you like—he's never seen your face in person, he only knows the wanted notices, and those are five years stale. But me—" she tapped her own chest, "—he knows me. He'll want to talk to me first. And Voss always talks before he shoots. It's the one vanity he has."

"Ruth—"

"He thinks I stole from him. Let him think it. Every minute he spends gloating over me is a minute you're swimming west with his whole case against you rolled up in oilcloth." She was already moving as she talked, pulling her knife, checking the flare pistol in her belt, doing all the small mechanical things that kept her hands from shaking. "You get to Port Merrow. You find the daughter. You burn him down with paper, not blood. That was always the plan."

"And what happens to you?"

Ruth grinned, and it cost her more than any grin had ever cost. "I'm a salvage diver, love. Drowning is my native language."

Ada caught her arm. Her grip was stronger than it looked, engineer's hands, rope-and-iron hands. For a moment neither of them said anything, and the rain hammered the tin roof like a crowd demanding an answer.

"You owe him nothing," Ada said quietly. "Whatever debt you think you're paying—to Ines, to anyone—you've paid it. Twice over since the lock."

"That's not why I'm staying." Ruth pulled free, gently. "I'm staying because five years ago I read those ledgers and I ran, same as you. Same as everyone he's ever touched. And running felt like living until I met a woman dying beside a rail line who traded her last map for a promise." Her throat tightened. "Somebody has to stop being the one who runs. Might as well be me. I float better than you do."

Outside, gravel crunched under boots. Voices. The slap of a gangplank thrown against the depot's loading apron.

Ada looked at her for one long second—the look of a woman memorizing a face—and then she went over the mezzanine rail into the water without a splash, and was gone beneath the floorboards toward the tunnel mouth.

Ruth turned to face the door.

* * *

Voss entered the way he entered everything: slowly, letting the room announce him. He was smaller than Ruth remembered, or maybe she'd simply grown, but the stillness of him hadn't changed—that coiled, unhurried patience of a man who had never once needed to raise his voice because everyone around him learned to lower theirs.

"Well," he said, surveying the flooded warehouse, the floating crates, the woman standing ankle-deep on the mezzanine stairs with a flare pistol held loosely at her side. "Kestrel. Or should I say Ruth. You've been a difficult file to close."

"You closed plenty of files," Ruth said. "Most of them people."

His smile was thin and correct. "Where is she? The engineer."

"What engineer?" Ruth spread her hands. "I'm salvaging. Ines's stores, if you must know. Dead woman's goods, fair claim."

"Ines." He tasted the name. "Yes. The prospector. She was thorough, that woman. Thorough enough to be dangerous." He stepped closer, boots deliberate on the stair, water dripping from them. "You were thorough too, once. Until you read things you shouldn't have and vanished with a copy of my ledger. I've wondered for three years where you buried it."

"Maybe I didn't bury it," Ruth said. "Maybe I mailed it somewhere. Maybe there's a dead-drop with instructions that activates if I stop reporting in. Men like you hate that arithmetic."

Something flickered behind his eyes—calculation, not fear. He gestured, and one of his men came up the stairs behind him, rifle slung, rain streaming off his hat brim.

"Search the lower floor," Voss said. "Slowly."

And that was the moment Ruth understood the shape of the next ten minutes: every second the man spent wading through Ines's drowned empire was a second bought with her body standing here as collateral. She could live with that arithmetic. She'd told Ada she could.

What she hadn't counted on was how loud her heart would get.

"So talk," she said, louder than necessary, angling herself so Voss faced her and his man faced away from the western wall—away from the hairline seam of the maintenance hatch, which sat just above the waterline and was, at this moment, very slightly ajar. "Tell me about the bridge, Voss. Tell me how you falsified the inspection reports and let eleven people die, and then pinned it on the one engineer who actually flagged the fault. I've had three years to wonder how you sleep."

"I sleep fine," Voss said mildly. "I sleep fine because the railway runs, the north pays its taxes, and no one remembers eleven names. Order is a machine, Kestrel. It requires sacrifices. You of all people understand salvage—something always goes down so something else can rise."

"You're not order. You're rust wearing a uniform."

He sighed, genuinely regretful, and nodded to the man with the rifle. "Tie her to the railing. We'll question her properly when the floor is searched."

Ruth talked then—loud, filthy, magnificent, buying seconds with insults, with a sudden theatrical lunge that made both men reach for weapons, with a story about a second cache of documents hidden upriver that she'd trade for passage. She saw Voss's attention snag on that, greed doing its old work, and she fed it, invented details, named a tributary, watched him lean in—

Which was why she was the only one who heard it: a soft metallic knock from below, near the western wall. The hatch. Ada, going through, catching the frame.

One of the men heard it too. He turned, rifle rising, tracking toward the sound.

Everything after happened in pieces.

Ruth threw herself sideways into the rifleman, and the shot went high and wide and punched a hole in the skylight instead of a hole in the water where a dark head was slipping away. Voss shouted. The flare pistol was in Ruth's hand and then it was firing, white phosphorus light and screaming smoke filling the warehouse like a small furious sun, and in that blinding churn she got one arm around the rifleman's neck and one thought in her head, clear as a bell:

*Swim, Ada. Swim.*

Then something struck the back of her skull, and the white light went black.

* * *

She woke to water slapping her cheek and the taste of blood and gunpowder.

The warehouse was empty. The patrol boat was gone—or rather, its engine was, receding up the main channel, hunting. Voss had left her trussed to the loading crane with wire, half-submerged as the flood rose, which told Ruth two things: he believed her dead-drop story enough to keep her breathing, and he believed Ada had escaped alone, with nothing but her life.

He didn't know about the manifests. He didn't know about Port Merrow. He didn't know that the map Ines had traded with her dying breath had been evidence all along, wrapped now against the chest of a woman swimming west through the dark with a bullet graze on her shoulder and five years of stolen future in her hands.

Ruth worked the wire against the crane's rusted edge, wrists burning, laughing quietly to herself in the rain—because terror and relief feel identical in the throat, and she couldn't tell which one was making the noise.

By the time the wire parted, the eastern sky had gone the color of pewter. Somewhere out on the flood, a small leaky boat was limping toward Port Merrow carrying everything that mattered.

Ruth spat blood into the water, found her knife floating in a crate, and started walking along the drowned rail bed toward the nearest telegraph shed.

Mara Ines's daughter deserved to know her mother's map had arrived. And Ruth Kestrel intended to be standing next to Ada Zhou when Voss learned what a promise was worth.
