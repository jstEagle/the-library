## 8. Port Merrow

The river gave her up at dawn, the way it gives up everything eventually—driftwood, drowned lambs, and one hollow-eyed woman clinging to a boat that had wanted to die for three days.

Ada ran the last mile under oar because the engine had swallowed water above the falls and she no longer trusted anything mechanical but her own arms. Port Merrow announced itself first by smell—diesel and brine and frying fish—then by sound: gulls screaming over the fish market, a winch groaning somewhere behind the fog. She came in low along the breakwater, past moored ferries furred with salt, and let the current carry her hull against the pilings of the public slip with a knock that sounded, to her ears, like a door closing on the whole journey.

She stayed there a while, forehead against the gunwale, listening to her own breath come back to her.

Her left arm was wrapped in Ruth's spare shirt, soaked through twice since the tunnel, and beneath it the graze had gone from angry red to a dull, throbbing heat that climbed toward her shoulder. Fever, then. She'd known it would come; she'd been rationing herself the last of the willow bark like everything else. Her hands shook when she lifted them. Three days of half-rations after losing half their stores to the river, and the last two days alone, eating cold rice out of the bag with fingers that wouldn't stop trembling.

*Ruth.* The name sat in her chest like a stone dropped down a well. She hadn't heard the splash. She hadn't heard anything after the shot except Voss shouting and the tunnel swallowing her whole, and every hour since, on the water, she had made herself not imagine it. Made herself believe that a woman who could hold her breath four minutes in black water could talk her way out of a flooding warehouse too.

Ada stood, swayed, caught the piling. One thing at a time. That was how you kept a boat alive, and it was how you kept yourself alive: one plank, one seam, one stroke. Find the girl. Give her the map. Then decide whether to run or stand.

* * *

The ferry yard sat at the river's mouth where the channel widened into the harbor, a sprawl of sheds and cradled hulls and ramps slick with weed. Ada walked in through the open gate as if she belonged there, which was the oldest trick she knew—invisibility wasn't hiding, it was looking like you had a reason.

A woman was under the nearest ferry, flat on a creeper, working a wrench somewhere deep in the bilge. Boots, canvas trousers, forearms streaked to the elbow with grease. A radio on an oil drum played something tinny and cheerful that nobody was listening to.

"Mara?" Ada said.

The wrench stopped. The creeper rolled out, and Ines's daughter looked up at her—and Ada felt the ground tilt, because the face was Ines's face. The same broad cheekbones, the same stubborn jaw, twenty-five years younger and unsoftened by weather or regret. Only the eyes were different: Ines's eyes had held a kind of amused sorrow, like someone who'd already read the ending. These eyes just waited.

"Who's asking?"

"My name is Ada Zhou." She kept her voice level. "Your mother sent me."

Something moved across Mara's face and was gone—too fast to name, too slow to miss. She got to her feet, unhurried, wiping her hands on a rag with the deliberate calm of someone buying time.

"My mother," Mara said, "doesn't send people. My mother doesn't do anything. That's rather the point of her."

"She's dead."

The rag stopped moving. The radio played on, oblivious, and somewhere across the yard a hammer rang against a hull, three slow strokes, like a bell counting something out.

"When?" Mara asked. Her voice hadn't changed at all. That was the frightening part.

"Eleven days ago. On the old Vellacott line, forty miles north. She collapsed beside the track. I found her." Ada's throat tightened around the rest. "I stayed with her until the end. It wasn't long."

Mara set the rag down on the oil drum, folded it once, precisely, the way you'd fold a flag. When she looked up again her eyes were dry and very bright.

"And what did she want? At the end. Let me guess." She held up grease-blackened fingers, ticking them off. "An apology. A letter. A promise I'd think well of her. Something she could carry out of this world so it wouldn't weigh anything."

"She traded me a map," Ada said quietly. "For a promise that I'd bring it to you."

"A map." Mara laughed—one short, ugly note. "Of course. A map. Not a letter. Not her reasons. A *map*, like I'm a depot she can route a delivery to." She turned away, picked up the wrench, knelt back onto the creeper as if the conversation were cargo she'd finished unloading. "You can leave it on the drum. And then you can go."

Ada didn't move. The fever was climbing now; she could feel it gathering behind her eyes like weather. But she had carried this thing through floodwater and gunfire and three hundred miles of closed country, and she would be damned if she'd leave it on an oil drum like a parcel of bolts.

"She talked about you," Ada said. "At the end. When she still had breath for it."

The wrench paused.

"She said your hands were better than hers ever were. Said you could strip a marine engine at nine years old while she watched and pretended to supervise." Ada's voice roughened. "She said leaving was the one repair she never managed. Those were her words. *The one repair I never managed.*"

"Stop."

"She wasn't sorry in the easy way. There isn't an easy way. She knew that. She said—"

"I said *stop*." Mara came up off the creeper fast, and for a moment Ada thought the woman might strike her. Instead she stood there, chest heaving, wrench hanging from her fist, and her voice cracked clean down the middle. "You don't get to say those things to me. You're a stranger. You're—she sent a *stranger*. Twenty-two years, and when she finally reaches for me, she reaches through some passing surveyor with a package. Do you know what that is? That's not love. That's logistics."

"Yes," Ada said. "It is."

Mara blinked.

"It's exactly that," Ada went on, and the fever made her careless, made the words come up raw from somewhere below caution. "I've spent six years being a logistics problem myself. Moving quietly, routing around attention, treating every person as a risk. So I know what it looks like when someone manages their life instead of living it. Your mother did it for twenty-two years, and it killed her alone on a dead line with nothing but a stranger for company. And you know what she did with her last hour? She didn't manage. She spent it on you."

The yard was silent now. The hammering had stopped. Mara stared at her, and the anger in her face was fighting something else and losing ground inch by inch.

"You don't know anything about her," she said, but it came out thin.

"No. I knew her for forty minutes." Ada reached into her coat—the good coat, the one she'd kept dry by wrapping it around the map tube even when her own arm was bleeding—and drew out the oilskin packet. "But I know she was in pain, and scared, and she thought about you anyway. That's more than most of us manage."

She held out the packet.

Mara didn't take it. For a long moment she just looked at it, the way you look at a live wire. Then she wiped her hand on her thigh, twice, as if preparing for surgery, and took it.

She turned away from Ada to open it—Ada understood; grief needs its back turned—and worked the oilskin ties with those famous quick fingers. Inside: the map itself, dense with Ines's cramped annotations, and beneath it a second sheet Ada had never seen, tucked flat against the backing board. Mara went very still.

"What is this?"

"What is what?"

Mara turned. She was holding a shipping manifest, yellowed, stamped with a railway authority seal. Her voice had changed entirely. "This is a provision ledger. Depot 9. Dated—" she checked, frowning, "—the spring before the Kestrel Bridge collapse. These are inspection countersignatures."

Ada crossed the yard in four steps, fever forgotten. She took the page. Read it. Read it again. Her heart began to slam against her ribs.

Ines's handwriting filled the margins. *Voss signed these himself. He never came within fifty miles of the depot. Kept copies. Insurance, he thinks—but insurance cuts both ways.*

"She knew," Ada breathed. "She always knew. The map was never just a map. She was carrying evidence, and she hid it inside the one thing she could trust a stranger to deliver." She laughed, and it came out half sob. "To you. To the daughter she abandoned. Because who else would she trust?"

Mara was staring at her. "Who's Voss?"

"The man who destroyed my life," Ada said, "and built his career on the bodies they pulled out of the Kestrel River. Including—" her voice caught, steadied, "—including eleven men whose inspection reports he forged. This page proves he never inspected the depot stores he certified. Which means the fraud has roots, and roots can be pulled."

Mara looked down at the manifest in Ada's hands, then at the map, then at Ada—at the bloodstained shirt, the shaking hands, the fever-bright eyes—and something shifted in her face. Not forgiveness. Not yet. Maybe not ever. But recognition, perhaps: one abandoned person reading another.

"You're bleeding," Mara said. "And burning up. Sit down before you fall down."

"I need—"

"I know what you need. You need a doctor who doesn't ask questions, and I know exactly one." Mara was already moving, grabbing a jacket off a hook, pocketing the manifest with a decisiveness that was pure Ines. She stopped at the gate and looked back, jaw tight. "My mother left me. I haven't forgiven her, and I'm not going to perform grief for your benefit. But she chose you to finish this. So we'll finish it. After that—" she pushed the gate open onto the bright morning harbor, "—we'll see."

Beyond the yard, out on the gray water, a patrol boat was coming up the channel, white hull, railway security livery, moving fast against the tide.

Ada's blood went cold.

"That's Voss's mark," she said.

Mara followed her gaze, and her hand closed hard around Ada's wrist.

"Then you'd better tell me everything," she said, "on the way."
