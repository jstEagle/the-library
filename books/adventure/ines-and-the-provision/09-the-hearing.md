## 9. The Hearing

The tribunal hall in Port Merrow had been a customs house once, back when the river still carried cargo worth taxing. Its ceiling was high enough to hold echoes, and its windows were tall enough to let the monsoon light fall in long gray columns across the floor. Ada stood outside it in the rain for eleven minutes, counting.

She'd counted through worse. Through the night Ines died against her shoulder. Through the hours in the flooded depot while Ruth bled on the loading dock below. Through the three days since, moving by night along the old coast spur with the manifests wrapped in oilcloth against her chest like a second heartbeat. Eleven minutes of rain was nothing. But her hands wouldn't stop shaking, and she needed to know if it was fear or something else.

It wasn't fear. She understood that when she finally pushed open the doors. It was the opposite of fear—the strange, weightless vertigo of a woman who has spent four years making herself small walking into the largest room she could find.

The tribunal sat three days out of every month in the north gallery: a raised bench, a clerk's table, a rail of polished wood that separated justice from everyone who needed it. This morning it was hearing routine matters—freight disputes, a boundary quarrel between two section houses, a claim for a cow killed at a crossing. Ada stood at the back, dripping, and waited for the docket to thin.

Mara found her first. The ferry mechanic slipped in through a side door with the unhurried gait of someone who belonged anywhere she decided to stand, and took up position at Ada's shoulder. She smelled of diesel and rain. She didn't look at Ada.

"You came," Mara said quietly.

"You doubted it?"

"I doubted everything about you until two nights ago." Her jaw worked. "My mother died on a closed line holding a map she thought was worth more than her life, because she believed I'd want it. And you carried it through a flood and a bullet to bring it to me. So no. I don't doubt anymore." A pause. "Ruth's ledgers are already where they need to be. Clerk's office. Filed this morning as an anonymous submission—Voss's own duplicate books, the ones he kept off the railway's records. The clerk thinks they're from a whistleblower inside security. Let him think it."

"And your testimony?"

"Sworn and scheduled. They'll call me when they get to the Merrow bridge petition." Mara finally turned her head, and her eyes were Ines's eyes—dark, level, impossible to lie to. "When they do, walk up beside me. Don't hang back. Whatever happens in that room, we do it standing up."

Ada looked at the bench, at the bored magistrate shuffling papers, at the brass plaque bolted to the wall reading *RAILWAY TRIBUNAL — NORTHERN DISTRICT* — the same institution that had tried her in absentia four years ago, in a room like this one, on evidence assembled by a man named Voss. She had spent four years assuming that rooms like this only ever ratified lies.

"Standing up," she said.

* * *

They heard the patrol boat before they saw anything else—a low diesel note under the rain, coming up the harbor channel toward the tribunal wharf. Ada felt it in her sternum before her ears caught it, the way you feel a train through the rails before you hear it. Voss was arriving.

She moved to the window. Down on the gray water, the security launch cut a white seam toward the dock, and standing in its bow, rain beading on his uniform cap, was Chief Inspector Voss himself. He looked exactly as she remembered him from the trial she'd fled: broad, immaculate, patient as a trap. He had come personally. Of course he had. Ruth's disappearance from his service, the depot burned into rumor, a graze on his record—he would want to close all of it himself, today, quietly, before the tribunal ever opened a file.

"He knows," Ada said. "Somehow he knows I'm here."

"Then he's early for the same reason we are," Mara said. "To get his story on the record first."

They went back inside. The docket thinned. The magistrate called the Merrow bridge petition—a routine request to reopen inspection of the collapsed span—and Mara rose, and Ada rose beside her, and together they walked down the center aisle past forty turned heads, and did not stop at the rail.

"Names?" the magistrate asked.

"Mara Inesdottir, mechanic, Port Merrow ferry works. Testifying regarding provision manifests recovered from the northern depot."

"And the second party?"

Ada stepped forward. Rain still dripped from her coat onto the polished floor, each drop loud as a hammer.

"Ada Zhou. Engineer. Fourth section, northern lines." She let the silence hold. "Convicted in absentia, four years ago, of falsifying inspection reports prior to the Merrow bridge collapse."

The room changed temperature. Somewhere behind her a chair scraped; a clerk's pen stopped mid-word. The magistrate leaned forward, and Ada watched him decide—she could see the exact moment caution lost to curiosity, the way she could read a stress fracture in a rail head.

"That is a serious claim to make in an open session, Ms. Zhou."

"It's a serious claim to make anywhere, sir. That's why I'm making it here." She unwrapped the oilcloth and set the manifests on the rail. "These are shipping records from Provision Depot Nine, northern district, sealed in Ines's stores. They show material requisitions signed by Chief Inspector Voss—requisitions for inspection equipment that was never delivered to the field crews. Stamped received. Signed for. Dated." She laid each page down like laying track, one tie at a time. "The bridge failed because the inspection regime on that span was a fiction. The fiction has a signature on it. Four years ago that signature convicted me instead."

"The court has also received," the clerk said suddenly, from his table, his voice odd, "a submission this morning. Duplicate ledgers. Security division. Unofficial accounts."

"Read the dates," Ada said.

And the clerk read them. And the room stopped being a tribunal and became something older—a crowd, a witness, a reckoning.

That was when the doors opened.

* * *

Voss entered the way he entered everything, as though the building had been constructed around his arrival. Two security men flanked him, water streaming off their capes. He got six steps down the aisle before he saw her at the rail, and Ada had the rare satisfaction of watching a patient man become an instant one.

"There she is," Voss said, and his voice filled the hall the way his reputation did. "Ada Zhou. Fugitive. Chief Inspector's office takes custody of this woman now—" He gestured to his men. "She's wanted in connection with theft of security documents, assault on a railway officer, and the original charges stand. This tribunal has no jurisdiction—"

"On the contrary." The magistrate's voice cut clean. "This tribunal has original jurisdiction over the Merrow bridge matter, and Ms. Zhou has just entered evidence bearing directly upon it. Sit down, Chief Inspector, or wait outside. Those are your options."

For a heartbeat Ada thought Voss would refuse—that he would order his men forward and turn the hall into the kind of ending he preferred, quick and unrecorded. His hand actually moved toward his sidearm. And then he made the error she had prayed he would make, the error of a man who had spent four years believing his own paperwork: he smiled, and chose the record.

"Then let's have the record," Voss said, and took a seat in the front row, legs crossed, hands folded. "I welcome scrutiny. I built the northern inspection regime. If there are discrepancies, I'll account for every one."

"Good," said a voice from the doorway.

Ruth stood there, soaked to the skin, one arm bound in a sling of knotted rope, her face still carrying the bruise-map of the depot. She walked down the aisle slowly, deliberately, letting every eye track her, and stopped an arm's length from Voss.

"Hello, Chief Inspector. You'll remember me. Dive contractor, salvage division, eighteen months. You had me recover things from the river bottom. One of them was a strongbox from the bridge site." She turned to face the bench. "He paid me to find it and then ordered me to say I never did. Inside were the original gauge readings from the span's last certified inspection—readings that showed the failure points. Readings that were replaced, in the official file, with clean ones. I kept copies. I hid them separately from everything else, in case the rest went into the river. Which it did." She looked at Ada, briefly, and something passed between them that neither of them would ever need to explain. "The river keeps what it keeps. That's why divers make duplicates."

"Fabrication," Voss said. "A dismissed contractor and a convicted fugitive. Is this what passes for evidence now?"

"The gauge readings will match the metallurgy," Ada said. Her voice was steady now, and she marveled at it. "Order the tests. You built the inspection regime—you know exactly what they'll show. That's the thing about fraud, Chief Inspector. It requires competence. Competence leaves fingerprints."

The magistrate ordered the recess himself. Tests were run by noon—gauge readings against fracture patterns, ledger dates against requisition stamps, manifest signatures against known exemplars of Voss's hand. Ada waited in the corridor with Mara and Ruth, three women on a bench beneath a map of the northern lines, watching officials hurry past with their faces carefully blank.

"I keep waiting to feel afraid," Ada said.

"Don't," Mara said. "Feel it later. Right now just sit here and let it happen. My mother waited her whole life for someone to stand in a room like that for her. You're doing it. Let it happen."

At half past three the tribunal reconvened, and it took the magistrate less than ten minutes. The conviction of Ada Zhou was vacated in full. The evidence was referred to the crown prosecutor with a recommendation of charges against Chief Inspector Voss—fraud, falsification of records, and obstruction inquest into fourteen deaths. Security officers who had escorted Voss into the hall now escorted him out of it, and Ada watched his face over the crowd's heads as they did. He wasn't looking at her. He was looking at the paperwork in the clerk's hands, at his own signatures being read aloud into the record, and Ada understood that for a man like him, this—this, not the cell—was the punishment. Every date, every stamp, every lie, read back to him in a public room, forever part of the permanent file.

Justice seized outside the law, ratified within it. Ines would have laughed at the phrasing. Ines would have said the law never gives you anything you haven't already carried to its door yourself.

Outside, the rain had softened to mist. The harbor lay silver and quiet. Mara stood on the tribunal steps with her mother's map rolled in her fist, and Ruth leaned against the railing with her bad arm cradled, and for a long moment none of them spoke.

"So," Mara said at last, turning the map over in her hands, studying the faded ink of the depot approaches, the little annotations in Ines's cramped hand. "There's a spur marked here. Closed since before I was born. Runs east along the escarpment." She looked up, and her mouth curved—not quite a smile, but the beginning of one. "Somebody told me once there's talk of reopening the northern lines. Survey work. New track over old ground." She held the map out to Ada. "I hear the engineer they'd want for it is very good. And I hear she doesn't work alone anymore."

Ada took the map. The paper was soft with age and river damp, and it weighed almost nothing, and it weighed everything.

"Survey season starts in three weeks," she said. "I'll need a crew."
