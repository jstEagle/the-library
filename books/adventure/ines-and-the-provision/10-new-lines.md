## 10. New Lines

The morning fog had not yet burned off the valley when Ada set the level on its tripod and looked through the eyepiece at a world made of numbers again.

That was the thing she'd missed most, in the years of hiding. Not the work itself—she'd done plenty of work that kept her hands busy and her face forgettable—but the *sight*. The way a line of track resolved through a surveyor's instrument into something clean and inevitable, grade and curve and cant all agreeing with one another, the whole geometry of it humming like a struck bell. Through the lens, the reopened stretch of the Merrow branch ran straight as a ruled line toward the head of the valley, new ballast pale as bone, ties still smelling of creosote.

"Marksman to chain," Mara's voice crackled in her earpiece, easy and dry. "You're staring again, boss."

"I'm working."

"You're admiring." A pause, the sound of a ranging pole being driven into gravel. "Rod's up. Give me a number before I die of old age."

Ada read the staff, called the figure, watched Mara's silhouette down the line raise a thumb. Twenty-two years old and already better with a chain than half the crews Ada had trained in her old life—quicker, less superstitious about the numbers, willing to argue when an elevation didn't look right. She argued the way her mother must have argued, Ada thought. Flat, unimpressed, certain the world owed her nothing.

The thought arrived without pain these days. That was new too.

They worked the section through the morning: pegging the realignment where the flood had scoured the embankment, checking culvert grades against the drawings Ada had redrawn from memory and Ines's map. The map itself lived now in a waterproof sleeve in her breast pocket, soft as cloth from handling. Half its annotations were Ines's—a prospector's shorthand for water sources, safe camps, the drowned culvert where the river bit hardest—and half were Ada's own, added on the journey north. Two hands on one sheet of paper. It seemed right that it should come back out here, marking ground instead of graves.

By midday the fog was gone and the heat stood up off the ballast in shimmering columns. Ada called the lunch halt, and they sat in the shade of the instrument truck with their boots off, passing a tin of tea between them.

"Ruth's boat came through the lower lock at dawn," Mara said. "I saw it when I rode down for the water drums. She had a barge behind her, riding low."

"Salvage contract," Ada said. "Bridge iron out of the old crossing. She'll strip it clean by Friday and sell it back to us at a criminal markup."

"She told you that?"

"She told me she'd charge what the market would bear and that I should be grateful the market bears anything at all." Ada drank the tea. "She wasn't wrong."

Mara laughed—short, reluctant, the laugh she was still learning to spend freely—and lay back against a tie stack with her arm over her eyes. Watching her, Ada felt the old reflex stir, the one that catalogued exits and counted strangers, and found, testing it like a loose tooth, that it had gone quiet. Not gone. She doubted it would ever be entirely gone. But quiet, the way a guard dog sleeps when the yard is truly empty of threats.

Voss had been in custody nine weeks. The tribunal had reconvened in Port Merrow under a chairman brought in from the coast division, and Ada had stood in front of him with the depot manifests in her scarred hands and read Voss's own falsified inspection reports aloud into the record—the dates altered, the load ratings inflated, the bridge certified sound eleven days before it came down with forty-one people on the morning train. Ruth's duplicate ledgers had corroborated everything. Mara's testimony about the security chief's men harassing ferry crews who asked questions had filled in the edges. When the chairman asked Ada why she had waited six years to speak, she had expected the question to gut her.

*Because I was afraid,* she had said. *And then someone dying gave me a job to do, and doing it turned out to be braver than hiding. I'd like to say I learned that sooner. I didn't.*

The conviction had collapsed in an afternoon. The warrant had been withdrawn by the following week. And somewhere in the middle of it all, standing on the tribunal steps blinking in ordinary daylight while reporters shouted questions she no longer had to run from, Ada had understood that invisibility was a country she could emigrate from—that she had been living there so long she'd mistaken it for home.

*

The depot shelf was Ruth's idea.

They'd gone back upriver together in the second week after the hearing—Ada, Ruth, and Mara, who had refused at first and then shown up at the dock anyway with her toolbag, saying only that she wanted to see where her mother died. The provision depot still stood above the flood line, its corrugated skin rust-streaked, its warehouse floor silted ankle-deep. They spent three days hauling out what the water hadn't taken: tinned stores, lamp oil, crates of rail spikes, and the records room where Ada had knelt with shaking hands and pulled the truth out of a mildewed filing cabinet.

On the last day, Ruth pried a shelf off the wall of the records room—solid hardwood, salvager's eye finding value where Ada saw only furniture—and said, "This is coming with us."

"Why?"

"Because things that survive ought to get used." Ruth had hefted it onto her shoulder, already walking. "Ask fewer questions, engineer."

It sat now in the survey office at the Merrow works yard, re-sanded, re-oiled, mounted on the wall where anyone who came in could see it. And carved into its face, in three different hands, were three names.

Ada had cut hers first, the night the shelf went up, alone in the office with a chisel and more feeling than she knew what to do with. *ADA ZHOU*, deep and even, engineer's lettering. Ruth had added hers the next morning without asking permission—*RUTH OKONKWO* in a slashing hand, angled like everything else she did—and stood back with her arms crossed as if daring the shelf to object. It took Mara another week. Ada had seen her standing in front of the empty space one evening, jaw tight, chisel in her fist, and had left her to it. The next morning the name was there: *MARA INESDOTTIR*, smaller than the others, cut shallow, but unmistakably deliberate. She had taken her mother's name as a middle patronymic. She never explained it, and nobody asked.

Three names side by side on wood that had survived a flood, a frame-up, a gunfight, and a monsoon. Some nights Ada caught herself reading them like a manifest, checking that everything accounted for had arrived.

*

Late afternoon, the light going long and gold across the valley, the survey crew reached the boundary stake at the top of the realigned section. Beyond it the old line ran on toward the mountains, closed since the collapse, rails brown with rust, waiting for the crews that would follow in spring. Ada planted the final peg herself and stood looking down the corridor of the future track, and Mara came and stood beside her.

"That's where my mother's trail met the line," Mara said quietly. "Ines's trail. The one she walked for twenty years."

"I know."

"The map shows it. She marked every camp." Mara turned the words over like stones. "I've been reading it at night. There are thirty years of her handwriting on that paper, and I never saw any of it until you put it in my hands." She was silent a moment. "I'm not glad she's dead. But I'm glad I have the map."

"That's not a contradiction," Ada said. "Grief keeps strange books."

Mara huffed—not quite a laugh, but near kin to one—and drove the peg cap flush with two blows of her mallet, precise as everything she did. Down the valley, faint on the wind, came the thud of pile drivers from the bridge site where Ruth's salvaged iron was already going back into new spans. The river ran high and glittering below it, carrying the monsoon's leavings seaward, keeping its secrets, giving up its dead.

"Boss," Mara said, shouldering her rod. "Crew's asking if we push the next section before the rains end or wait for the dry season."

Ada looked at the open line, the graded earth, the stakes marching away into distance that was no longer something to flee along but something to build across. Six years ago she had memorized timetables and slept in culverts and taught herself to be a rumor. Now her name was carved on a shelf, printed on a work order, spoken aloud by a crew that answered to it without knowing or caring what a tribunal had once torn out of the record.

"We push," she said. "We don't stop for weather we haven't met yet."

Mara grinned and turned to pass the word down the chain, her voice ringing off the cut banks, and Ada lifted the level from its tripod and settled the instrument against her shoulder like something that belonged there. Behind her the crew fell into motion; ahead the line ran straight into the gold light, drawn in new ink on old country, and for the first time in longer than she could reckon, Ada Zhou was not looking over her shoulder at all—

—because coming up the access road, waving both arms like a woman flagging a runaway train, was Ruth, standing in a moving barge, shouting something about a discovery in the old mountain tunnel that Ada was going to want to see *immediately*.
