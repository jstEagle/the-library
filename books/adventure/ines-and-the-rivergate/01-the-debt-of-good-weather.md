## 1. The Debt of Good Weather

The supercell came up out of the western flats like something being born rather than arriving — a green-black bruise swelling over the horizon, its anvil spreading so wide it seemed to be pressing down on the whole sky from underneath. Orsen Ishikawa stood on the tailgate of his truck with a camera in one hand and a cup of gas-station coffee going cold in the other, and felt the particular joy that had ruined his life arrive on schedule.

"Come on, sweetheart," he murmured to the storm. "Show me the wall."

He'd been chasing weather for eleven years, and he still hadn't found a way to explain the feeling to anyone who didn't have it. It wasn't adrenaline, exactly. Adrenaline was cheap; he could get that driving too fast on dry roads. This was closer to recognition. A storm like this one was the most honest thing in the world. It told you exactly what it was going to do and then it did it, magnificent and indifferent, asking nothing of you except that you pay attention. Everything else in Orsen's life lied. The storm never did.

He filmed for two hours as the cell organized itself over the empty country north of the Rivergate headwaters: the lowering, the rotation tightening into a caged gray column, the first rope funnel touching down a mile off the county road and dancing there like it was deciding whether to commit. He got all of it. He got the lightning crawling inside the rain shaft like veins under skin. He got the moment the tornado lifted and the whole structure seemed to exhale. Standing in the truck bed with wind trying to peel his jacket off his shoulders, Orsen thought, not for the first time, *this is the only thing I'm good at*, and thought it the way you'd think a prayer.

Then the hail hit.

It always did. You got greedy for the shot and forgot that a supercell's forward flank was a machine for throwing ice at anything foolish enough to park beneath it. The first stones were marble-sized and sang off the roof like rifle fire. By the time he'd scrambled into the cab they were golf balls, then nearly tennis balls, white and hissing through the grass, and the windshield went from clear to cobwebbed in four seconds flat.

Orsen swore, dropped the camera into the passenger seat, and pulled out onto the road blind — which was stupid, and he knew it was stupid even as he did it, because the light was dying and the hail was burying the ditches and he wanted to get ahead of the cell before it reorganized. The wipers thrashed uselessly against the cracked glass. The road curved where he remembered it curving, and where he remembered it curving was apparently wrong, or the hail had washed out the shoulder, or the world had simply decided he'd had enough good luck for one day.

The truck left the pavement at fifty miles an hour, went airborne over the ditch, and came down nose-first in a flooded barrow pit with a sound like the end of an argument.

* * *

He woke to water seeping through the driver's door and the smell of gasoline and cut grass. The windshield was a lace curtain of fractures with a hole in the middle where a stone the size of a fist had come through and landed, absurdly intact, in the cupholder beside the coffee. His forehead was bleeding. The engine ticked as it cooled. Outside, the storm had moved off east, trailing its anvil across the sunset like smoke from a fire nobody would ever find.

Orsen sat there for a long minute, taking inventory. Head: bleeding but attached. Camera: fine, cushioned by the seat, worth more than the truck. Truck: totaled, or close enough that the distinction was academic. Bank account: he didn't check, because checking it was a form of self-harm and he'd promised himself he'd stop.

"Well," he said aloud, to the hailstones melting on the dash. "That's that."

He climbed out through the passenger door, retrieved his gear, and walked the half mile back to the crossroads where there was a fuel station, a diner, and — this being the crucial fact of the geography — a phone line that reached the Vane brothers' office in Corrick Landing.

* * *

The diner smelled of fryer oil and coffee held too long on the burner. Orsen bought a towel for his forehead, a plate of eggs he couldn't taste, and a seat by the window, and dialed the number he knew better than his own mother's.

"Vane Prosperity Partners, good evening!" The voice that answered was warm as fresh bread. Merrick Vane. The younger brother, the front-of-house, the man who could make a loan agreement sound like a favor between friends. "How can we brighten your ledger today?"

"Merrick. It's Orsen Ishikawa."

A pause — half a second, no more, but Orsen had spent enough years reading storms to read silences too. "Orsen! The storm chaser himself. Aldous and I were just talking about you. How's the season treating you? Get anything good out west? We saw the radar — that cell looked like a beauty."

"It was a beauty." Orsen pressed the towel to his hairline. "Merrick, I'm going to be straight with you, because I think you appreciate that."

"I appreciate nothing more."

"My truck's in a ditch. My last payment bounced. And I know what today is."

Today was the fifteenth. The fifteenth of October meant winter collections began on the first of November, and everyone who owed the Vane brothers money understood what winter collections meant without anyone having to say it. The Vanes called themselves an optimistic loan operation, and that wasn't irony — they genuinely believed in people, believed in their futures, believed so hard in what a man might become that they'd lend him money against it at rates that guaranteed he never got there. Their optimism was the most patient cruelty in the valley. It never threatened. It just waited, smiling, for the season to turn.

"Orsen." Merrick's voice softened into something almost tender. "You've been a valued client for three years. Do you know what I admire about you? Most men chase security. You chase the sky. That takes nerve. That takes vision. Aldous says vision is just debt with good lighting, but Aldous is a cynic." A chuckle, shared generously. "Here's my problem, though, friend. My problem is that optimism has to eat too. And right now your account is — well. Let's call it weather-worn."

"How much."

"With penalties? Four thousand, two hundred and sixty dollars. Plus the truck, if the bank calls the lien, which they will, because banks are such gloomy creatures." Papers rustled somewhere in Corrick Landing; Orsen pictured the office, the green-shaded lamps, the framed motto over the desk — *EVERY CLOUD HAS ITS LEDGER* — which Aldous Vane had commissioned from a sign painter in a fit of what passed, in that family, for whimsy. "But Orsen. Orsen, Orsen, Orsen. Don't despair. I wouldn't have called you tonight if I didn't have something. Are you interested in hearing about work?"

Orsen closed his eyes. Rain had started again outside, soft and ordinary, drumming on the diner roof. "I'm listening."

"A salvage job. Upriver. The Rivergate."

"The Rivergate freezes by December. Nobody salvages up there after October."

"Which is why we're doing it now." Merrick's voice dropped into the register he used for opportunities, the one that made each sentence feel like a hand on your shoulder steering you toward a door. "We had a survey team working the upper reaches. Mineral assessment, timber cruise, the usual diligence. Nine souls. They went quiet eleven days ago. Radio silence since the ninth. No word at the relay stations, nothing at Mile Forty, nothing at the mouth. Now, teams go quiet sometimes — equipment fails, men drink, radios drown. But nine people, Orsen. That's families waiting. That's insurance. That's *our* investment sitting up there in the dark, and winter coming down the valley like a landlord."

"You want me to find them."

"We want you to *ascertain*. Find them, or find out what happened to them, and report back. There's a boatman in Harrow Bend who'll take you upriver — name of Quist, tell him the Vanes sent you, he'll grumble but he'll go. Two weeks' provisions, satellite phone, and a fee." A pause, delicately measured. "Three thousand dollars. Enough to settle your account with a little left over for a new windshield. Call it a fresh start, courtesy of our continued faith in you."

Orsen looked down at his eggs, gone gray and cold, and at the blood drying brown on the towel, and at the dark window where his own reflection sat ghosted over the empty road — a lean, weathered man of thirty-four with a stitched-up face and eleven years of spectacular footage earning him almost nothing, because the world paid for storms the way it paid for honesty: in admiration, rarely in cash.

"And if I don't take it?"

The warmth in Merrick's voice didn't vanish — that was the terrible thing about the Vanes, the warmth never vanished — it simply acquired weight, like deep water. "Then on the first of November, our collections associate, Mr. Dree, begins his winter rounds. You remember Dree. Big fellow, very thorough, no imagination whatsoever. He'll want the truck — what's left of it — and whatever else has value. And Orsen, I say this with real affection: I don't want that. Aldous doesn't want that. Nobody wants that. All we've ever wanted is for you to succeed." A breath, and then, brightly, the sun coming back out: "So! Shall I tell Quist to expect you tomorrow?"

Outside, the rain kept falling on a road that led nowhere he wanted to go, and somewhere east of him the wrecked truck sat filling slowly with river water, and somewhere above it all the sky was already clearing toward a hard frost, the first of the season, winter reaching down the valleys ahead of schedule like everything else in his life arriving early to collect.

Orsen thought about the supercell. About how honest it had been, and how beautiful, and how it had put him in a ditch all the same. The sky never lied to you, but it never saved you either. That was the deal. You paid attention, and it showed you wonders, and then it went on doing what it was going to do whether you lived or drowned.

People, though. People smiled at you and called it optimism while they wrote your name in a book.

"Yeah, Merrick," he said. "Tell him to expect me."

"Wonderful! Wonderful. You won't regret this, Orsen. Faith rewarded — that's all we're about, really. Oh — one small thing." The papers again, lighter now, almost casual. "The survey team was carrying documentation. Claims assessments, maps, that sort of thing. Company property. If you happen to come across any paperwork up there, you'll bring it back to us along with your report. Standard practice. Men lose things in the wilderness; we'd hate for the company to lose its property."

Something turned over in Orsen's chest, small and cold, like a fish under ice. Eleven days of silence. Nine people. And the Vanes worried about their *paperwork*.

"Sure," he said. "Standard practice."

"That's the spirit. Safe travels upriver, friend. And Orsen — do dress warm. The mountains don't share our optimism."

The line clicked dead. Orsen sat a while longer in the humming fluorescent light, watching the rain crawl down the glass, and tried to name the feeling in his gut. It wasn't dread, exactly. Dread was simple. This was dread wearing charm's clothing, dread that shook your hand and asked about your family — the same feeling he got when a storm went quiet too fast, when the pressure steadied and the air went sweet and still, because every chaser learned sooner or later that the calmest part of any system was the part built to kill you.

He paid for the eggs, bought a second towel and a paper map of the upper Rivergate, and walked out into the freezing rain to see about borrowing a ride to Harrow Bend.

Forty miles upstream, nine people had stopped existing. In the morning, he'd go find out why.
