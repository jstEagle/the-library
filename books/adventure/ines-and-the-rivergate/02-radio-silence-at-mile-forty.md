## 2. Radio Silence at Mile Forty

The boat was called the *Maggie B*, which Orsen took as a bad omen, because every boat named after a woman he'd ever known had tried to kill him.

Her owner was a squat man with a beard like rusted wire and a name — Dov — that he offered without a handshake. He sat on the dock at Rivergate's lower landing, mending a net he clearly never used, and listened to Orsen's pitch with the flat patience of a man who'd heard a hundred lies and believed maybe four of them.

"Forty miles up the Gate," Orsen said. "Past the Narrows, past Harrow Bend. I need to reach the survey camp at Mile Forty and be back inside six days."

"Six days." Dov sucked his teeth. "In October."

"I'll pay for October."

That got him something like attention. Dov set down the net and looked at Orsen properly — at the bruised cheekbone still yellowing from the rollover, at the truck he no longer owned sitting in an impound lot two counties south, at the coat that had been good five years ago. Men who read people for a living read Orsen like a gauge with a cracked face: the needle said *fine* and the glass said otherwise.

"Who's paying you?" Dov asked.

"A salvage concern."

"Which one?"

"The kind that doesn't like its name in the mouths of boatmen."

Dov grunted, which might have been respect. "Three hundred up front. Three hundred when you step back onto this dock alive. Fuel's yours to buy. If you're not back by the sixth day, I report the boat stolen to whoever's still answering phones at the sheriff's office, and I keep your deposit."

"Fair."

"It's not fair. It's what it costs." He spat over the rail into the brown water. "You know what's up there, son?"

"River," Orsen said. "Timber. A missing survey team."

"That's what's written down." Dov began coiling rope with quick, economical hands. "What's up there is forty miles of water nobody's run since the equinox, because the Gate came up three feet overnight and nobody can say why. No rain worth mentioning. No dam break anybody admits to. The river just decided to grow." He tied off the coil and looked at Orsen with eyes the color of old ice. "I don't like rivers that make decisions."

Orsen thought about the Vane brothers' letter, the one that had come with the salvage contract, printed on paper heavy enough to be money itself. *Recovery of personnel and equipment. Standard hazard rates apply.* Aldous Vane's signature at the bottom, looping and cheerful, like a man signing a birthday card.

"Then we understand each other," Orsen said, and counted out the deposit from the last of the cash the Vanes had advanced him, and tried not to think of it as thirty pieces of anything.

* * *

He left at first light with fog standing on the water in gray columns, and the Rivergate took him the way a throat takes a pill — reluctantly, with the whole length of it working against him.

The lower river was wide and slow and deceptively kind. Log rafts drifted down from the timber camps, and once he passed a fish weir where two men waved without waving, just lifting their chins, watching him go by with the expression of people who had seen boats head upstream before and knew the arithmetic of how many came back. The *Maggie B* was a shallow-draft workboat with a cranky diesel and a wheel that pulled left, and Orsen ran her alone, hopping from throttle to tiller to chart, drinking bad coffee from a thermos and telling himself the story he'd been telling himself since the hospital: this is a job, this is just a job, find the team, find the equipment, get paid, get square, get out.

The lie had a rhythm to it, like the engine. He let it carry him.

By Mile Twelve the valley narrowed and the current woke up. The Gate here ran between shoulders of black rock, and the water went from brown to a strange, muscular green, folding over submerged ledges in long glassy veins. Orsen had chased storms through eleven states; he knew the feeling of a place holding its breath. This was that. The air had a pressure to it, not meteorological but animal, the silence of a woods that has stopped making noise because something is walking through it.

At Mile Twenty he passed the first sign of the expedition, and it was wrong in a way he couldn't immediately name.

A supply cache — standard issue, orange barrels, stenciled with the survey company's mark — stood on a gravel bar ten feet above the current line. One barrel had been opened. Not raided: opened, carefully, lid set back on loose, as if someone had checked the contents and closed it again out of politeness. Inside, when Orsen beached and looked, everything was there. Rope, rations, medical kit, flare gun. Untouched. Unneeded.

Nobody leaves a flare gun behind, he thought, unless they've already stopped believing anyone will come looking.

He stood on the gravel bar a while, listening to the river work the rocks, and then he loaded the barrel into the boat anyway, because taking things aboard was easier than thinking.

* * *

Mile Thirty was where the drowned town started.

Harrowgate, the charts called it — HARROWGATE (ABANDONED), in letters so faded they were more memory than label. The river had taken the lower streets decades ago, and now rooftops broke the surface like the backs of sleeping animals, shingles furred green, chimneys leaning at angles that suggested slow surrender rather than collapse. A church steeple stood on the high bank, whole and dark, watching the water with the patience of something that intended to outlast it. Orsen cut the throttle and drifted, and in the quiet he heard the town. Water moving through doorways. Wind in a window frame somewhere, playing the empty rooms like bottle necks. A sound that might have been a bell cable tapping its own post.

He did not know yet that the town mattered. He would learn. For now it was just another mile marker on the way to a paycheck, and he pushed past it with the fog burning off and the sun coming up thin and white as skimmed milk.

Mile Forty announced itself with a smell: woodsmoke, days dead, and under it something sweeter and worse that he chose not to identify until he had to.

The camp sat on a bench of flat ground above a side channel, exactly where the contract's coordinates said it should be. Three wall tents. A cook shelter. A gear line. A flagpole with no flag. From the water it looked almost peaceful, almost ordinary — a camp paused mid-morning, waiting for its people to come back from wherever people go.

It was not ordinary. It took Orsen ten minutes of standing on the bank to articulate why, and when he did, the words arrived in his head in the calm, precise voice he used for reading radar, and that voice frightened him more than any shouting could have.

Everything was tidy. That was the wrongness. Abandoned camps are never tidy. People leave in a hurry or they leave in stages, and either way they leave debris — dropped gloves, scattered tools, the archaeology of departure. This camp had been *closed*. Tent flaps lashed down. Cook shelter swept — actually swept, the dirt showing broom strokes. Gear hung on the line in neat rows, drying, and had hung there long enough for the rain to soak it and the sun to dry it and the rain to soak it again. Someone had folded this camp like a shirt.

Except for two things.

The first was the radio. It sat on the camp table under the cook shelter, and someone had destroyed it with a rock or a hatchet — not smashed in rage, which scatters, but crushed in method, which concentrates. Every strike had gone to the same place: the power section, the antenna feed, the battery housing. The casing was split open like a rib cage. Orsen turned the wreckage over twice. He wasn't a radio man, but he'd spent enough nights in storm country listening to static to know that this radio hadn't failed. It had been silenced. And whoever had done it had known exactly where to hit.

The second thing was the coffee.

There was a mug on the table beside the radio. Enamel, white, dented. Half full. Orsen touched it and the dregs inside were dry — not dried recently, but dried long ago, shrunk to a dark ring and a puck of sediment that crumbled when he prodded it. Weeks, at least. Maybe more. Somebody had been sitting at this table, drinking coffee, doing whatever you do at a radio — logging readings, calling in, complaining about the food — and had stopped mid-cup. Mid-sentence, maybe. And nobody had ever come back to wash the mug.

You don't leave a half cup of coffee, Orsen thought. You leave it when you mean to come back in five minutes. You don't leave it for six weeks.

Unless you didn't leave. Unless leaving was done for you.

He made himself walk the perimeter, because the alternative was standing still while his skin crawled, and the ground told him the rest of it in the flat grammar of tracks and disturbed earth. There were boot prints, many sets, most weathered past reading. Near the treeline he found where the story stopped pretending: a churned patch of ground the size of a small room, leaf litter gouged down to mud, and running out of it toward the timber, a pair of parallel furrows — drag marks, deep and continuous, the kind left by heels, by bodies being moved without ceremony. Two furrows. Then, twenty feet on, the marks of a third, joining them, as if someone had woken up partway and objected.

The drag marks went into the trees and the trees closed over them like water.

Orsen stood at the edge of the timber for a long time. The sun climbed. Somewhere upslope a woodpecker worked a dead snag with idiot persistence. His hands, he noticed distantly, were cold, though the morning was mild.

Here was the arithmetic, and he made himself do it the way he made himself do fuel calculations in rising wind: Six people at this camp, per the manifest. The cache at Mile Twenty untouched — they hadn't relocated voluntarily, or they'd have taken the flares. The radio killed by someone who understood radios. The camp closed by someone with time and confidence — you don't sweep a floor if you expect investigators. The drag marks going *up*, away from the river, deeper into country that got wilder every mile. And the Vanes' contract, signed in a cheerful loop, specifying recovery of *personnel and equipment*, sent upriver six weeks after the last scheduled check-in, to a man with debts, alone, in a boat, in October.

Alone. They'd sent him alone. Either they thought one man was enough, or they thought one man was expendable, and both possibilities tasted like copper.

The river talked to itself below the bank. The dead camp kept its swept, patient silence. Orsen became aware, gradually and then all at once, that he was being watched — not by anything he could see, but by the whole arrangement of the place, by the folded tents and the dry coffee and the furrows in the leaves, all of it oriented toward him like instruments on a panel, all of it reading him back.

"This isn't a salvage job," he said aloud, just to hear a human voice in it. It came out hoarse. "This is a cleanup."

And if it was a cleanup, then the question stopped being *where is the survey team* and became *what am I supposed to find here — and what am I supposed to not find?* Because the Vanes hadn't asked him to investigate. They'd asked him to recover. Recover the equipment, inventory it, log it. And the personnel — the contract had a clause for that too, buried in the schedule of fees, a line item he'd skimmed past at the lawyer's office with his ears ringing from the word *collections*: *in the event of confirmed fatalities, a finder's fee applies per individual, payable upon verification.*

Per individual. Payable upon verification.

They wanted him to count bodies. They wanted a number, certified, so the paperwork of six disappeared lives could be filed and forgotten. And the tidy camp, the killed radio, the drag marks into the timber — none of it was chaos. It was a ledger entry, already balanced, waiting only for his signature.

Orsen walked back to the boat because his legs wanted something to do, and stood in the shallows with the water pulling at his boots, and looked upriver. Past Mile Forty the Gate bent north into country the charts gave up on — hatched blank, annotated only with a warning about seasonal flooding and a single penciled note in some earlier hand: *HARROWGATE DIST. — DO NOT TRUST THE CHANNELS.*

The channels. As if the river had opinions. As if the water itself might take sides.

His radio — his own, bolted to the dash of the *Maggie B* — crackled once, a breath of static, and went quiet. He stared at it. It did it again, longer, a soft rush of white noise like the river learning to speak, and then died to nothing. He checked the connections with hands that weren't quite steady. Antenna fine. Power fine. Nothing wrong with it at all.

High on the ridge above the camp, at the exact moment he looked up, a bird flushed from the timber — a big gray shape lifting out of the firs and banking away north. Except it flew wrong for a bird, low and deliberate, following the contour of the slope the way a man walks a ridgeline he doesn't want to silhouette against the sky.

Orsen stood in the cold water and did the last of the arithmetic, the kind that doesn't fit on a form.

Whatever had happened at Mile Forty hadn't ended. The sweepers had closed the camp, but they hadn't found everyone — otherwise why send him? Why pay a finder's fee per individual, *upon verification*? The Vanes weren't asking him to confirm deaths. They were asking him to finish them. To be the last clause in a contract the river hadn't quite honored.

And somewhere ahead — up those blank miles, in a drowned town whose steeple he'd drifted past that morning — whatever remained of the survey team was hiding from the men who paid his debts.

Orsen hauled the anchor line in, hand over hand, and told himself the old story one more time — *this is a job, this is just a job* — and heard, finally, how thin it had worn. The engine caught on the second try. The *Maggie B* swung her bow north, into the current, toward the town that flooded on purpose, and behind him the dead camp kept its swept and silent vigil, and the mug with its six-week-old coffee sat on the table like a question nobody had stayed to answer.

Halfway around the bend, he saw the smoke.

One thread of it, thin and gray, rising off the far bank near the drowned town — too small for a forest fire, too steady for an accident. A cook fire. Tended. Fed. Hidden by someone who knew exactly how much smoke a single person could send up before the wrong eyes caught it.

Someone was alive up there. And they were watching the river for boats.

Orsen cut the throttle and let the *Maggie B* drift, silent, into the shadow of the bend, and reached for the binoculars with a heart that had begun, quietly, to hammer.
