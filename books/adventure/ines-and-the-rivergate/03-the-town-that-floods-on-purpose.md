## 3. The Town That Floods on Purpose

The river changed its mind three times in the last mile, and Orsen Ishikawa learned to read the water the way he'd learned to read storm cells — by the way it lied.

Pell's boat was a flat-bottomed skiff with an outboard that coughed like a two-pack-a-day man, and Pell himself had refused to go past the old ferry landing at Mile Forty-One. "Nothing up there but Harrowgate," he'd said, untying the line and not looking at Orsen while he did it. "And nothing in Harrowgate but what the river left."

"What did the river leave?"

"Ask it." And then he'd pushed off and let the current take him back downstream toward the world of people who paid their debts.

So Orsen walked the last stretch along the north bank, pack on his shoulders, camera bag slung crosswise, the salvage job folded up tight in his chest where the dread lived. The Rivergate narrowed here between granite shoulders, running fast and green-black over drowned foundations. He could see them through the surface in places — corners of walls, a chimney standing alone out of the current like a man waist-deep refusing to admit it. The town of Harrowgate hadn't drowned all at once. It had drowned on purpose, or so the stories went: the mining company that built it had cut a deal with the river, letting the water in seasonally to work their sluices, flooding the lower streets every spring and draining them again by August. A town that held its breath for sixty years.

Then the company failed, the gates rotted open, and the river moved in full-time.

Orsen came around a bend where the bank pinched against a slide of talus and stopped, because there it was.

Harrowgate sat in a bowl of hills with its feet in the water. Half the town stood above the floodline still — a row of brick facades along what had been Main Street, their upper windows dark and whole, their doorways underwater. A church steeple rose from the flooded district at the town's center, tilted maybe ten degrees, its bell visible through the clear shallows where it had come to rest against a rooftop. Behind everything, the mountain went up dark with timber, and above the timber the ridgeline carried the first white suggestion of the season, snow dusting the granite like flour sifted over a knuckle.

It should have been beautiful. It was beautiful. But Orsen had spent eleven years filming weather, and he knew the difference between a landscape and a mood, and this was a mood. The silence had weight. No birdsong he could name. No smoke from any chimney. Just the river talking to itself among the drowned streets, patient as a landlord.

He checked the sun. Two hours of light, maybe less in the canyon shadow. He wanted to search the upper town before dark — the survey team's last radio fix had been somewhere in these hills, and if they'd holed up anywhere, it would be high and dry and defensible.

He made it forty yards up the ruined main street before the first stone hit him.

* * *

It caught him across the knuckles of his right hand — the hand holding the camera bag strap — and the crack of it echoed off the brick like a rifle shot. Orsen dropped, rolled behind a fallen lintel, and came up with nothing useful in his hands because he owned nothing useful for this. Eleven years of chasing supercells and he'd never once been shot at, stabbed at, or slung at.

"Who sent you?" The voice came from above — a girl's voice, young but stripped of anything young in it. Flat. Certain. "Say the name and I'll know if you're lying."

"Nobody sent me," Orsen called back, keeping his hands wide and empty. "I'm looking for a survey team. Four people. They went quiet three weeks ago."

Another stone hit the lintel a foot from his ear. She wasn't aiming at him. She was aiming *near* him, which was worse, because it meant she could put one through his eye whenever she felt like it and was choosing, moment to moment, not to.

"The survey team is dead," the voice said.

"They're not. I found their camp at Mile Forty. Coffee cups. Bedrolls gone. Drag marks into the timber — somebody walked out of there alive."

Silence. Long enough that Orsen counted his own heartbeats. Then, from a different position entirely — she'd moved twenty feet without a sound, down through the bones of some collapsed awning — the voice again, closer:

"You counted the drag marks."

"I counted four sets going in. None coming out."

More silence. Then she said, almost to herself, "Four sets. He said three." And something in her voice shifted, a hairline fracture of doubt running through the certainty, and Orsen understood suddenly that this girl had been alone up here for a long time guarding something, and that being wrong about a number frightened her more than a stranger with a camera ever could.

"I'm coming out," he said. "Slow. Don't shoot my face off, I need it for work."

He stood with his hands up. She was perched on the second-story ledge of the old assay office, twenty feet up, barefoot on the brick despite the cold, a slingshot drawn and level with his sternum. She was maybe fourteen, small and wiry, with river-gray eyes and hair the color of wet slate pulled back with a strip of inner tube. Her clothes were layers of mismatched wool and oilskin, patched with a competence that looked older than she was. She didn't lower the slingshot.

"Camera," she said.

"It's a camera. Weather photography. Storms, mostly." He turned slowly so she could see the gear. "I chase them. Film them. Sell the footage."

"To who?"

"Meteorological services. News. Whoever pays." He smiled — the smile that had gotten him out of a hundred bar arguments and one arrest, the charm that was supposed to mask the dread. "You want to see? I've got footage of a twister eating a grain silo outside Cardell. Best thing I ever shot."

"I don't care about your twister." But her eyes flicked to the camera bag, and he saw the hunger there — not for the device itself but for what it meant, proof of a world beyond the waterline, evidence that the rest of the planet was still turning. Then the eyes came back to him, hard again. "You counted four sets of drag marks."

"Yeah."

"Show me your boots."

He lifted one foot, then the other. She studied the tread like a banker studying collateral.

"Your prints won't match the ones at the camp," she said. "I already walked it. Two days ago. There were five men who came out of the timber after the survey team left. Armed. They burned what was left and went downstream." The slingshot dipped an inch. "You're not one of them. They don't ask questions. They just do what they're told."

"That's a very specific thing to know about them."

"I watch the river road." She finally lowered the sling, though her thumb stayed on the leather pouch. "Everyone comes up it eventually. You're the first one in a month who came alone and asked questions instead of shooting."

"Shooting? Somebody shot at you?"

"Somebody shot at us." She said *us* the way a person drops a coin into a well and listens for how deep it goes. Then her jaw set, and Orsen watched her decide something — watched the calculation move behind those gray eyes faster than any computer he'd ever seen, weighing him, the light remaining, the distance to cover, the risk. "You want your survey team. They want to stay lost. Those are different wants, and only one of them can win."

"And which one are you?"

She didn't answer that. She stepped off the ledge instead — no hesitation, no test of footing, just stepped off into empty air — and landed on a rusted fire escape bolted to the building's flank that Orsen hadn't even seen, so buried under ivy and silt that it was part of the wall now. She came down it rung by rung, sideways, fast, and dropped to the street in front of him close enough that he could see the chapped skin around her mouth and the old scar splitting one eyebrow.

"Ines," she said. Not a handshake. An announcement.

"Orsen. Orsen Ishikawa."

"Ishikawa." She tried the name once, filed it away. "You came up the river road past the ferry landing. Did you cross the culvert at the old millrace?"

"There wasn't a culvert. There was a gap where a culvert used to be. I jumped it."

"You jumped it." Something that was almost approval crossed her face. "Most people walk upstream looking for a ford and lose an hour. The gap looks wider than it is. It's four feet, six inches. I measured it last spring when the water dropped."

"Why would you measure that?"

"Because someday someone might need to jump it." She said it like it was obvious, and maybe to her it was. She was already walking, moving up the dead main street with a gait that avoided obstacles Orsen couldn't identify until he nearly tripped over them — a submerged cellar door hidden under leaf litter, a length of rebar waiting ankle-high in the weeds. She never looked down. She never missed one. "Stay where I walk. The town lies about its ground. The river took the streets and gave them back wrong."

"How do you know where everything is?"

Ines stopped. For the first time since the stones started flying, she looked at him directly, and Orsen felt the full strange weight of her attention — the sense of being mapped, measured, entered into some ledger she kept behind her eyes.

"I remember it," she said simply. "All of it. What the water covers and what it used to be. My father drew the town before the company broke the gates. I have his maps." She tapped her temple once. "Up here. Better than paper. Paper burns."

* * *

She led him up through the terraces of the dead town as the light went copper and long. She talked the way a pilot talks over unfamiliar terrain — sparse, precise, all business — but the business itself was astonishing. Every few hundred yards she'd point with her chin: *"That was the hotel. Third floor's dry if you go through the window on the east side; the stairs went in the flood of '09. There's a cistern behind it, still sweet, but you boil it anyway because a mule died in it two winters back."* Or: *"Don't take the low path here after rain. Looks fine. It isn't. The hillside lets go about six inches below the surface and takes you into the flooded quarter before you know you're falling. Took a dog that way once. Didn't get the dog back."*

"You've lived here long?" Orsen asked, and immediately regretted the shape of the question — too gentle, too much like pity.

"Long enough." She scrambled up a collapsed retaining wall without using her hands. "My father worked for the survey companies. Before the Vanes bought them all."

The name landed in Orsen's stomach like a swallowed stone. "The Vanes."

"Aldous and Merrick Vane." She pronounced the names the way other people pronounced diseases. "They own the loans, the mills, the barge lines. They own the paper on half the valley. When the survey crews find something they don't like, the crews stop sending radio calls." She glanced back at him, and her eyes were old, old, old. "You came upriver asking about a silent crew. Who paid you to ask?"

Orsen opened his mouth to lie — the reflex was right there, smooth and practiced, *I'm independent, freelance, nobody's man* — and what came out instead, God knew why, was the truth.

"I owe them money. A lot of it. They called the loan and offered me a salvage contract. Find the survey team, bring back word of what happened to them, and winter collections get postponed." He laughed, short and ugly. "Postponed. Not forgiven. Postponed. That's the kind of generosity the Vanes deal in."

Ines had stopped walking. She stood on the terrace with the drowned church below them and the darkening ridge above, and she studied him with an expression he couldn't read — not trust, not yet, but maybe the end of the arithmetic.

"So you're their dog," she said.

"Pretty much."

"But you told me anyway. About the debt. About the contract." Her head tilted. "A smart dog wouldn't tell me that."

"Maybe I'm a stupid dog."

"Maybe." She turned and kept climbing. "Or maybe you're a dog deciding whose hand feeds it. We'll find out which."

* * *

The survivors' hideout, when Ines finally brought him to it, was a collapsed stamp mill halfway up the mountainside, its timber skeleton draped in fir boughs, invisible from thirty feet in any direction. She stopped him at the treeline with a raised hand and whistled — three notes, low, rising — and waited. From inside the ruin, a whistle answered: two notes, falling.

"Countersign changes every third day," she said. "You'll learn it if you earn it."

Inside, in the amber light of a shielded lantern, four people looked up at him with the particular wariness of animals that had been hunted long enough to stop hoping. A heavyset woman with a splinted forearm — Mara, Ines said, the expedition's geologist. A lean young man with a bandaged ankle — Teo. An older man named Hollis with a prospector's squint and hands that wouldn't stop moving. And a fourth, a quiet woman named Ade, who said nothing at all and watched Orsen the way the river watched its banks.

They were the survey team. All four of them. Alive.

Mara spoke first, her voice rough from disuse. "Ines says you counted the drag marks at our camp."

"Four sets going into the timber. Five armed men came later and burned it. That's what I'm told."

"That's right." Mara looked at her companions, and something passed between them — a decision being weighed, a door about to open that couldn't be closed again. "Then you should sit down, Mr. Ishikawa. Because we know why those men came. We know who sent them. And what we found up in those hills is worth more than your debt and theirs put together."

Outside, the light was failing over Harrowgate, and the river kept talking to itself in the drowned streets below, patient as a creditor, remembering everything.
