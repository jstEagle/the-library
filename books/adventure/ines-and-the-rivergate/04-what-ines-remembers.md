## 4. What Ines Remembers

The church sat where the river couldn't reach it — that was the whole reason it still stood.

Ines led Orsen up the goat path behind the assay office, through a stand of birches gone yellow and half-bare, and stopped at the treeline to look him over one more time. He was soaked to the knee from the crossing at the old millrace, mud to his hips, and he kept glancing back the way they'd come like the water itself might be following them. Most grown men who came upriver looked like that by now. The ones who didn't were worse.

"Rules," she said.

Orsen blinked down at her. Up close, in the flat gray light, he looked younger than his voice had sounded on the bank — a lean man with storm-chaser's sunburn and a week of bad sleep carved under his eyes. "Rules?"

"You don't talk first. You don't touch anything. You don't ask their names until they give them." She counted on her fingers, then folded the hand away. "And if anyone runs, you let them run. You don't chase."

"That's a lot of rules for a rescue."

"I'm not rescuing you," Ines said. "I'm deciding if you're worth the trouble."

She watched that land on him. He had a face that tried to smile its way out of things — she'd seen it already twice, once when she'd put a stone an inch from his ear and once when she'd asked what he was really doing on the river. But underneath the smile there was something else, something tired and honest that kept surfacing no matter how fast he pushed it down. She hadn't decided yet which one was the real Orsen Ishikawa. She was good at waiting.

"This way," she said.

* * *

The church of Harrowgate had been built by men who believed the town would last forever, so they'd set it on the highest bench of rock above the river bend, above even the flood line of '09. Everything below it had drowned on schedule every spring since. The steeple leaned a little, the way old men lean, but it held. Inside, the pews had been dragged into a rough wall across the sanctuary door, and behind that wall, four people were living out of survey crates.

Ines knocked the way she always did — three quick, two slow — and heard the scrape of someone moving inside before the door cracked open and Mara Voss's face appeared in the gap: sharp-cheeked, gray-lipped, eyes going straight past Ines to the stranger.

"It's the one from the river," Ines said. "He came alone. He says he was sent to find you."

Mara's gaze came back to her, and Ines met it steadily. Mara was the one who decided things; Ines had understood that within a day of finding them huddled in the vestry, half-starved, listening to the water. Mara decided, Teo argued, Priya patched everyone together with fishing line and willow bark, and Halden — Halden mostly stared at the ceiling beams and counted things under his breath. Four survivors out of nine. Ines knew exactly where the other five were. She could have walked to each of them blindfolded, in the dark, in flood season.

That wasn't a thing she said out loud. People didn't like hearing it.

Mara opened the door.

Inside smelled of candle wax, wet wool, and the particular sourness of people who have been afraid too long. Teo sat propped against a pew with his boot off and his ankle wrapped in strips of shirt, trying to stand and failing with dignity. Priya looked up from a pot of nettle soup over a spirit stove. Halden didn't look up at all; he was scratching marks into the seat of a pew with a nail, little tally strokes, row after row.

"This is Orsen," Ines said. "He films storms."

"A photographer," Priya said, and there was something almost like relief in it. "Not a gunman."

"Do I look like a gunman?" Orsen said.

"You look like a man who hasn't eaten," Mara said. "Sit down. Slowly."

Ines took her place on the windowsill — her place, the one with the sightline to both doors and the gap in the trees where the path came up from the water — and watched them take his measure while he took theirs. He was good at this part, she'd give him that. He didn't push. He told them about the smashed radio at the camp at Mile Forty, the cold coffee still in the pot, the drag marks going into the timber, and he told it plain, without dressing it up, and she saw the survivors trade looks over the details because those were *their* details, the coffee and the radio, and nobody who hadn't stood in that camp could have known them.

"We heard the boat go past two days ago," Teo said. "We thought it was them coming back to finish it."

"Who?" Orsen asked. "Them who?"

Nobody answered. The spirit stove hissed. Halden scratched another tally mark.

"They won't say it yet," Ines said, from the window. "They've been saying it to themselves, though. I hear them at night."

"Ines." Mara's voice had an edge.

"He crossed the range to get here," Ines said. "He waded the millrace in October. If he were theirs, they'd have just shot us from the boat." She shrugged. "I checked. There's no one else on the water. There's no one else in the town. It's him or nothing."

Orsen looked at her then, really looked, and Ines got the feeling she was being measured right back. Fine. Let him measure. She'd been weighed against worse than a debt-ridden storm chaser.

"The job I was given," Orsen said slowly, "was salvage. Find the expedition, recover equipment, confirm casualties." He paused. "I don't think my employers expected survivors."

"No," Mara said. "I don't imagine they did."

* * *

After the soup — thin, green, but hot, and Orsen ate it like it was a feast, which told Ines more about his finances than his charm ever would — Mara asked her to tell it.

She asked the way she always asked, formal, like a request being entered into a record: "Ines. The night of the twenty-third. Would you say it again?"

Ines slid down off the windowsill. This was the part where strangers usually started looking at her sideways, the way you look at a dog that can count. She'd stopped minding years ago. The river had taken everything else from Harrowgate; it had left her this instead, and she figured that was a fair trade, most days.

"The twenty-third of September," she said. "Rain started at the headwaters around noon. By four it was coming down the Narrows at maybe eleven feet over normal — I could tell you the exact gauge reading off the old intake tower, but the tower's been underwater since spring, so eleven feet is close enough." She moved to the middle of the sanctuary floor as she talked, because the floor helped. She crouched and began dragging her finger through the dust between the pews, drawing the valley the way you'd draw it on a map, except hers wasn't a map. It was the thing itself, remembered.

"The river came down the main channel here—" the finger swept left, "—but the rain had loosened the slide slope above the old Kestrel adit, so at roughly six in the evening a mudslide went off the north bank and dammed the channel here, at the bend below the smelter ruins. The river backed up. For about forty minutes it pooled, and then it found the flood channel — the one the miners cut in 1911 and everybody forgot, except it doesn't forget, it's still there under the silt, six feet wide, running southeast toward the flats." Her finger cut a hard diagonal through the dust. "That's the route it took. Through the flood channel, across the flats, over the low ground where your camp was."

She glanced up. Orsen had gone very still.

"Your camp was on the low ground," she said. "Nine tents, on the gravel bar by the slough, because the gravel bar drains fast and it seemed safe. And it would have been safe, in any ordinary September rain. But the river didn't take its ordinary route that night. It took the 1911 channel, and the 1911 channel comes out exactly there." She tapped the dust. "Your camp was sitting on top of the one path the water wanted."

Priya had stopped stirring. Teo had closed his eyes.

"The water hit the camp around seven-forty," Ines went on, quieter now, because this was the part that mattered. "It took two tents immediately. Three of yours were on the high side and got to the tree line. Two didn't make it — I know where. The rest of you scattered. You went north along the bank in the dark, and the current had turned the slough backwards, so the crossing you'd used all summer was suddenly moving the wrong way, and that's where—" she hesitated, "—that's where the boat was."

The word dropped into the room like a stone into a well.

"What boat?" Orsen said.

"There shouldn't have been a boat," Mara said. Her voice was flat and careful, the voice of someone walking on ice they already know is thin. "There was no traffic on that stretch. No one runs the upper Rivergate after the season closes. But there was a boat, with a spotlight, working the bank in the flood. And it wasn't looking for survivors." She looked at Orsen. "It was looking for us specifically. It had our camp layout. It knew which tents to sweep first."

"How do you know it wasn't rescue?"

"Because rescue doesn't fire on people in the water," Teo said from the floor. His voice cracked on *water*. "Because rescue doesn't take Halden's sample cases off the high ground and burn them in the rain. We watched it. Five of us, in the timber, watching our own expedition burn."

Halden looked up from his tallies for the first time all evening. "Seventeen cases," he said. "They burned seventeen. They missed the two I buried."

"Halden," Priya murmured, and he went back to his nail.

Orsen sat down slowly on a pew, like his legs had made the decision without consulting him. Ines stayed where she was, kneeling in the dust map, watching him work through it. She was getting good at reading him, in the space of a single day. Right now his face was doing arithmetic he didn't like the answer to.

"The Vanes sent me up here," he said finally.

"We know whose money paid for that boat," Mara said. "Teo saw the marking on the hull before the light swung off it. A blue heron, wings up. That's the Vane Consolidated barge mark. It's on every crate of freight that ever came up this river."

"And you're telling me this," Orsen said, "the day we met."

"I'm telling you because Ines vouched for you," Mara said, "and because in five days the pass snows shut and we either trust somebody with a boat or we die in this church waiting for the next one to come burn us out." She spread her hands. "You want to know why there are four of us instead of nine? Ask your employers. You want to know what we found up here that was worth nine lives? That's mine to give, and I'll give it when I know which way you fall. Not before."

Silence. The stove hissed. Somewhere up in the steeple, wind worried at loose boards, a sound like slow knocking.

Ines rose from the dust map and brushed off her knees. She looked at Orsen Ishikawa, at the storm-chaser with the charming face and the tired eyes, and she did the thing she was best in the world at: she remembered. Every word he'd said since the bank. The way he'd said *salvage* like it tasted wrong in his mouth. The way he'd flinched — small, but she caught it, she caught everything — when Mara said *your employers*.

"You already knew some of this," she said.

Everyone turned to her.

"Not the boat," she said. "Not the burning. But you knew the silence wasn't weather. At Mile Forty, when you saw the radio, you weren't surprised. You were confirming something you'd already suspected." She tilted her head. "Why did you really come upriver, Orsen?"

He held her gaze for a long moment. Outside, the light was failing, and the river spoke to itself in the dark below the church, patient, keeping its accounts.

"Because they called my loan," he said at last. "Because they offered me a job nobody sane would take, and I needed the money so badly I didn't ask why the job existed." He laughed, short and humorless. "Because I think I've been rowing their boat for two years without knowing it. And because when I saw that smashed radio, I thought — maybe this is what I've been part of."

Ines studied him. Then she nodded once, and looked at Mara, and said the only thing that mattered:

"He stays the night. I'll watch him."

* * *

Later, when the others slept and Orsen lay on a pew staring at the rafters, Ines sat in the doorway with her slingshot across her knees and listened to the river come up in the dark.

It was talking tonight. Rain somewhere in the high country, a long way off, but the water heard it first and passed the news down, branch to branch, the way it always did. Ines could read it the way other people read print. *Rise of a foot by morning,* it said. *Nothing angry. Nothing yet.*

She thought about the five graves she kept in her head, up on the bench above the slideslope, marked with river stones because there was nothing else to mark them with. She thought about her father's brass transit, wrapped in oilcloth under the floor of the smelter ruin, and the route she'd have to show these people when the time came, and whether any of them would live long enough to use it.

Then she looked back at the stranger sleeping in their church, and she added him to the ledger too — tentatively, in pencil, the way you write down a forecast.

The river never forgot anything. Neither did she.

The question, the one she turned over as the dark deepened and the water rose, was whether Orsen Ishikawa was going to remember whose boat he'd been rowing — or whether, when the Vanes' next offer came upriver, he'd climb aboard.
