## 5. Ledger of a Drowned Claim

Mara Voss had counted the days by the drip of water through the church roof. Forty-one drips to the hour, more when the wind pushed rain through the broken slats on the north side, fewer in the cold snaps when the leak froze into a slender finger of ice that Ines would break off each morning and hand to Teo for his fevered forehead. Forty-one days since the radio went dead. Forty-one days since the world decided they were dead too.

She sat in the vestry with the claim documents spread across her knees, wrapped in oilcloth that smelled of mildew and kerosene, and listened to the stranger's boots on the flagstones above.

Orsen Ishikawa. She'd watched him from the choir loft window as he came up the drowned main street, poling a borrowed skiff through water the color of old tea, and she'd thought: *he has the look of a man being paid.* It was in his shoulders, the way they carried tension like cargo rather than fear. Men who were afraid moved differently. Men who were bought moved like that — efficient, purposeful, already calculating what a thing was worth before they'd finished looking at it.

Ines had put an arrow within a foot of his head, and he hadn't run. That was either courage or stupidity, and Mara had learned over forty-one days that the two looked identical until the moment of testing.

She heard Ines's voice now, low and quick, giving him the layout of the church the way other girls gave directions to a market. *The floor gives way on the east side, past the third pew. The bell rope is rotten but the beam it hangs from is sound. If anyone comes up the river road you'll hear them at the bend, because the water there is shallow and their boots will splash.* The girl recited it all without hesitation, without a single correction or backtrack, and Mara felt the familiar ache in her chest that Ines's mind always produced — wonder braided tight with grief, because a gift like that had no business living in a child raised by floodwater.

The stranger descended the vestry stairs behind Ines, ducking under the lintel. Up close he was worse than she'd feared: handsome in a worn way, with storm-chaser's squint lines around his eyes and hands that couldn't stay still. He looked at the survivors huddled along the vestry wall — Teo gray-faced with infection, Priya holding a hatchet across her knees like a rosary, young Callum who hadn't spoken above a whisper since the night of the ambush — and Mara watched him do the arithmetic. Four lives. What were four lives worth against whatever the Vanes were paying?

"I'm not going to waste your time," Orsen said. "I was hired to find your expedition. I found you. Now somebody needs to tell me why a survey crew went silent for six weeks, and why the first thing I found at your camp was a radio smashed with a rock instead of burned."

"Because burning leaves residue," Mara said. "And whoever smashed it wanted it to look like panic. Like animals got in, or the river did." She stood, tucking the oilcloth bundle under her arm. "You're Ishikawa. The storm chaser. Your name was on the permit filings we pulled in Rivergate before we came upriver — every salvage operator licensed on this stretch of the Gate. We read everything. It's what surveyors do."

Something flickered across his face — surprise, then a grudging respect. "You knew about me?"

"We knew about everyone with a boat and a debt." She let that land. His jaw tightened, just slightly, and she knew she'd guessed right. "Sit down, Mr. Ishikawa. You've come a long way to hear a story, and it's a long one."

He didn't sit. She hadn't expected him to. Men like him didn't sit until they'd decided whether the room was safe, and she respected that even as it irritated her.

"My name is Mara Voss. Senior geologist, Corvane Survey Expedition, commissioned out of Rivergate by the Provincial Land Office." She said it formally, the way she'd said it a hundred times in her head during the dark hours, because saying it formally kept it true. "Six weeks ago my team located a deposit in the north bank shale — copper, silver, and something rarer underneath. Enough ore body to support forty years of extraction. Enough to employ half the valley."

"And?" Orsen said.

"And the mineral rights underneath it are held as collateral." She met his eyes. "By the Vane brothers' lending house. Every parcel. Aldous and Merrick Vane hold paper on eleven thousand acres of the north bank, and they hold it because they wrote loans to homesteaders and small operators that were designed to fail. When the claims defaulted, the land came to them. They've been sitting on it for years, waiting for someone to tell them what was under it. Waiting for someone like me."

The vestry was quiet except for the drip. Forty-one. Forty-two.

"So when we filed our preliminary findings," Mara continued, "when we sent word downriver that the Corvane Survey had identified a commercially viable deposit on Vane-held collateral — we didn't know we were handing them a death sentence. We thought we were doing our job. The Land Office would exercise its option, buy the parcels at assessed value, void the predatory liens, and open the mine properly. The Vanes would be paid off at assessment rates — a fraction of what the ore is worth. It would have bankrupted them, Mr. Ishikawa. Not hurt them. *Bankrupted* them. Everything they own is leveraged against that land staying worthless."

Orsen had gone very still. Stillness, in her experience, was what guilt looked like on a man who was trying to decide something.

"They sent men upriver," she said. "Six of them, maybe eight. They came at night, three weeks after our preliminary report went downriver. They didn't rob us. They didn't take our supplies or our boats. They took our *samples* and our field copies and they tried to take us." Her voice stayed level; she'd practiced that too. "Teo took a bullet through the shoulder. Priya carried him two miles through the timber while I ran interference with a flare gun. Callum—" she paused, and the pause cost her something every time— "Callum saw the rest of the crew die. Seven people. Good people. Dead because they wrote down numbers that were accurate."

"That's not possible," Orsen said, but his voice had no conviction in it. "The Vanes are lenders. Optimists, they call themselves. They don't—"

"They don't get their hands dirty themselves," Mara said. "No. They hire men who do. And those men carry Vane money and Vane instructions, and if you ever traced it back, it would be one contractor's word against a pair of smiling brothers who donate to the church restoration fund in Rivergate." She stepped closer. "You want to know how I know it was them? Because after the ambush, when we were hiding in the timber watching our camp burn, one of the men used a signal whistle. Three notes, rising. I grew up in a mining family, Mr. Ishikawa. That's the Vane company whistle. It hasn't changed since the old man Vane ran the smelter at Harrowgate. These hills remember it. So do I."

She watched him absorb it. Watched the storm chaser's restless hands go quiet against his thighs.

"You said field copies," he said finally. "Implying there are others."

"There are originals," Mara said. "Assay results, chain-of-custody records, the lien documents showing exactly which parcels the Vanes hold and exactly how the defaults were engineered — forged appraisals, inflated interest schedules, default triggers written into contracts that the borrowers never could have met. My father taught geology, but my mother was a court clerk. I know what evidence looks like, Mr. Ishikawa. And I know that what we're carrying in this church is enough to put the Vane brothers in front of a magistrate and take every asset they own away from them." She patted the oilcloth bundle against her hip. "This is only the duplicate set. The originals went into the ground somewhere safe, and I'm one of two people alive who knows where."

"The other person?"

"Is fourteen years old," Mara said, "and remembers everything."

* * *

They moved to the nave, where the light came down through the broken roof in dusty columns and the river showed itself through the doorway at the end — flat and patient, holding the drowned town in its throat. Ines perched on the communion rail with her sling coiled in her lap like a sleeping snake, watching Orsen the way she watched weather.

Mara laid it out for him plainly, because she wanted to see what he did with plainness.

"Here's where we stand. The Vanes believe the expedition is dead. When they learn otherwise — and they will, because you came upriver asking questions and questions travel — they'll send more men. Better men. Winter is coming, and winter is their ally: the pass closes, the river freezes, and nobody can testify from inside a snowbank." She ticked the points off on her fingers. "We need to cross the range to the magistrate at Fort Meridian before the snows. We need the original documents. And we need to survive long enough to do both."

"You're assuming I help you," Orsen said.

"I'm assuming nothing. I'm telling you what's true and letting you choose." She studied him. "But I'll tell you what I see, storm chaser. I see a man whose truck is financed, whose gear is financed, whose life is probably mortgaged to the same smiling brothers who tried to murder my team. I see a man they sent upriver because they think a debtor is a disposable man. And I see a man who hasn't yet asked me what his fee is for getting us out — which means either you haven't been offered one, or you have, and you're ashamed of it."

The color left his face so completely that Ines tilted her head, reading him like a change in current.

For a moment Mara thought he'd walk out — down the vestry stairs, into his skiff, back downriver to whatever clean lie the Vanes had bought him with. She braced for it. Forty-one days had taught her exactly how much hope cost, and she kept the ledger carefully.

Then Orsen Ishikawa sat down on a fallen roof beam, put his elbows on his knees, and looked at the river through the doorway for a long time.

"When I was nineteen," he said, "a flash flood took out a bridge crossing outside of Cedar Hollow. My father was on it. They never found the truck." His voice was flat, recitation-flat, the voice of a man who'd told himself this story so many times it had worn smooth. "I chase storms because the water owes me something. That's the stupid truth of it. And now I'm working for men who use water and fire and distance to make people disappear, and I didn't ask a single question until I saw drag marks going into the timber at your camp." He turned and looked at her directly. "I haven't been offered anything. Yet. But the moment they figure out I found you, they'll offer me everything. My debt. A clean slate. All I'd have to do is bring them you, and the girl, and burn whatever papers you're carrying."

"Will you?"

"I don't know," he said, and Mara respected that too — the terrible honesty of it. "Forty-one days ago I'd have said no without thinking. But I've spent six years learning exactly how much a clean slate costs, and I've learned that men like the Vanes always leave the price written in small print." He exhaled. "So here's what I can give you today. I'll get you across the range. All of you. And when the Vanes make their offer — and they will — I'll tell you about it. Both times. Before I answer, and after."

Mara searched his face for the flaw, the tell, the small print of her own. She found only exhaustion and something that might, given time and hard country, become resolve.

"That's more than most men offer," she said. "It's less than I need. But it's a start."

From the communion rail, Ines spoke for the first time, her voice quiet and certain as water finding its level.

"He'll keep the second promise," she said. "Not sure about the first."

Orsen stared at her. "How could you possibly—"

"The river doesn't explain itself," Ines said, sliding off the rail. "Neither do I." She paused at the doorway, silhouetted against the flat gray water, and added, almost as an afterthought: "Smoke on the north bank tonight. Two fires, close together. Men cook in pairs when they're hunting something."

Mara felt the cold move through her that had nothing to do with the season. She looked at Orsen, and Orsen looked at the north bank, where the timber stood dark against the dying light.

"How long do we have?" he asked.

Ines considered the river, the smoke, the memory of every flood this valley had ever swallowed.

"Less than the night," she said.
