## 6. Smoke on the North Bank

The decoy camp went up like a lie told well.

Orsen watched it from the ridge above Harrowgate, flat on his belly in dead bracken, and thought: *that's what I would have believed, once.* The tents stood in the old logging clearing on the north bank, pitched that morning from the expedition's own spares — Mara's idea, Teo's rigging, Ines's siting. A fire burned in the fire pit. A bedroll lay rumpled in each tent. From the river, at dusk, it looked exactly like five people who had made camp carelessly and gone to sleep.

Then the first boat came around the bend with its running lights killed, and the men in it did not look like rescuers.

There were six of them in the first boat, four in the second. They came ashore without shouting, which was the worst part. Orsen had filmed enough disasters to know how people moved when they were afraid or urgent — fast, loud, arms windmilling. These men came up the bank at a walk, spread out in a line like harvesters taking a field, and one of them crouched at the fire pit and held his palm over the coals as if checking whether the stew was warm.

"They're going to burn it," Ines said beside him.

She'd gotten there without a sound. Orsen still hadn't learned how she did that — a girl of maybe fourteen moving through brush like water through a culvert — and he'd stopped being embarrassed about flinching when she appeared.

"How do you know?"

"Because that's what I'd do." Her eyes never left the clearing. "You don't want to search a camp at night. You want them to run. Running people make noise, leave tracks, break cover. Burning is cheaper than hunting."

The man by the fire stood. He said something Orsen couldn't hear, and something small and bright left his hand and arced into the nearest tent.

The nylon caught with a sound like a gasp. Fire ran up the fabric faster than Orsen would have credited, orange light slamming out across the clearing, throwing the enforcers' shadows huge against the treeline. Within a minute all three tents were burning, and the men stood in a loose semicircle watching, hands in pockets, patient as men watching a kettle.

One of them kicked the rumpled bedroll into the flames. Another laughed at something.

"They think we're in it," Orsen said quietly.

"They hope we're in it." Ines's jaw was set. "Hoping's not knowing. They'll sweep the south bank tomorrow anyway."

Below, the fire collapsed inward with a soft roar, and sparks went up into the dark like the camp's whole inventory of stars leaving at once. Orsen found his hands were shaking, and made fists of them, and hated that too.

*This is a salvage job,* he told himself, the way he'd been telling himself since Mile Forty. *Find the team, secure the assets, get paid.* That was the language of it. Salvage. Assets. A fee that would buy back his truck, his camera rig, his name off the Vanes' ledger. Clean words for dirty work, and he'd been reaching for them like a man patting his pockets for a cigarette he'd already quit.

But you didn't burn a salvage target. You didn't send twelve men upriver in two boats at night to recover a survey crew that some paperwork said was missing. You sent twelve men because the paperwork was the thing that needed to stay missing, and everyone on this ridge knew it now — Mara with her mineral assays, Teo with his half-burned field book, the other two survivors whose names Orsen was only just learning. Witnesses. That was the word underneath all the clean ones.

The last tent fell. The enforcers doused their boots in the shallows, waded back to the boats, and pushed off downstream without lights, unhurried, certain.

Ines stood up out of the bracken. "Come on," she said. "We have until dawn, and less than that if the moon comes out."

* * *

The church was chaos by lamplight, but it was the organized kind — the kind Orsen recognized from storm crews breaking camp ahead of a cell that meant business. Mara had the claim documents sewn into the lining of her coat and was now redistributing food into everyone's packs with the brisk tyranny of a woman who had fed a field crew for six years. Teo sat with his foot propped on a pew, the bandage around it gray at the edges, arguing that he could walk. Renn and Sefa, the two quiet ones, were breaking down the radio they'd repaired far enough to listen but not transmit, wrapping components in oilcloth.

Ines stood at the head of the aisle, in front of the altar, waiting.

"We're not leaving Harrowgate," she said, when the noise died down. "We're going under it."

Nobody spoke. Orsen looked at her, then at the floorboards, then understood.

"The mine," he said.

"The mine." She nodded once. "Harrowgate was built on the Kestrel Lode. Three levels, main adit behind the stamp mill, air shafts every two hundred yards up the hillside. My father mapped them before the flood. I have them here." She touched her temple. "All of them."

"Mines collapse," Teo said.

"This one hasn't in sixty years."

"Mines flood."

"Not these. The Kestrel sits above the waterline — that's why they dug it where they did. The river took the town, not the rock under it." She looked around at each of them. "It's cold, it's dark, and it smells like rust. But no one finds us in it who doesn't already know the way, and the only map lives in my head."

Mara finished cinching a pack strap. "How long?"

"Two days. Maybe three. Long enough for them to decide we drowned or ran, and stop sweeping." Ines turned to Orsen, and there was something in her look that wasn't quite a question and wasn't quite an order. "You've been underground before?"

"Once. A cave in the Wichita Mountains, filming bats." He paused. "I didn't love it."

"Good," she said. "If you loved it, you'd be dangerous. People who love caves take risks in them."

* * *

The adit mouth hid behind the collapsed stamp mill, under slabs of corrugated roofing that had been sliding downhill toward the river for half a century. Ines moved the boards aside like she was lifting the lid off something sleeping, and the dark inside breathed out at them — cold, mineral, older than any of their troubles.

They went in single file, Ines first with a lantern, Orsen second, then the survivors, then Renn carrying the rear lantern. The tunnel was tall enough to walk upright for the first hundred yards, timbered with beams gone silver-black with age, and then the roof came down to meet them and they were bent double, and then the walls came in too, and Orsen discovered what fear felt like when it had a ceiling.

It wasn't the dark. He'd chased storms into nights black enough to swallow the truck's headlights; darkness he could handle, because darkness was just weather indoors. It was the weight. Somewhere above his spine — thirty feet? sixty? — there was a mountain doing nothing at all, forever, and the only thing between his skull and all that indifferent tonnage was timber cut by men dead before the flood. Every few minutes the rock ticked. Not loudly. Just a small, dry sound, like knuckles cracking in another room, and every time it did, Orsen's breath went short and he had to count himself back down from it.

*Four in, four out. Four in, four out.*

"You're breathing wrong," Ines said, without turning around. Her voice came back off the stone flattened and strange, stripped of everything that made a voice hers. "Short on top, long on bottom. Breathe like the tunnel's long. Slow in, slower out."

"I'm fine."

"You're breathing like a man being buried alive slowly. Everyone does, the first hour. Fix it before you need the air."

He fixed it. It helped more than he wanted to admit.

Behind him, Teo was having a harder time than anyone would say out loud. His foot had gone past pain into a hot bright territory he described, through his teeth, as *manageable*, and the tunnel's low stretches forced him onto hands and knees, which he bore with curses so inventive that Sefa, ahead of him, actually laughed once — a short, startled bark that echoed down the shaft and made everyone freeze until the silence reassured them.

They stopped at the first junction, where the tunnel forked around a pillar of raw ore-veined rock, and Ines put her lantern down and pressed her palm flat against the wall.

"Level one runs east-west along the lode," she said. "We sleep in the drift at the second air shaft — there's a vertical draft there, keeps the air sweet. Tomorrow we go deeper while they search the surface." She glanced back at Orsen, and in the lantern light her face was all shadows and certainty. "The trick isn't hiding. Anyone can hide. The trick is leaving no story. No tracks in, no fires, no trash, no noise. We're not here. The mountain agrees to say nothing, and we agree to be nothing."

"And when the three days are up?" Mara asked.

"Then we move north through the drainage adit and come out above the falls, and after that—" Ines stopped. Orsen watched her choose, visibly, how much to promise. "After that we talk about the pass. Winter's coming either way. We can't stay in this country and neither can they, and snow doesn't take sides."

They walked on. The tunnel narrowed, widened, narrowed again. Once they crossed a stretch where the old miners had hit water and driven the drift around it, and the sound of a hidden pool dripping somewhere in the dark was so precisely like a clock that Orsen had to stop himself counting the drips, because counting led to measuring, and measuring led to thinking about how much air five people and two lanterns actually consumed in a sealed throat of stone.

At the second air shaft they made camp: blankets on planking, lanterns hooded to coins of light, watches agreed upon. Teo's foot got rebandaged by lamplight, and Mara fed everyone cold beans and hardtack with the stern generosity of a woman who considered appetite a moral obligation. Then the hoods came down and the dark took the rest, and Orsen lay on his back on sixty years of dust and listened to the mountain tick.

He couldn't sleep. Sleep required believing tomorrow existed on its own terms, and his tomorrows had all been mortgaged.

*Salvage job.* He turned the phrase over again, examining it like a coin he suspected was shaved. Find the team. Secure the assets. And then what? Hand four witnesses and a land claim to the men who'd sent twelve arsonists upriver in the dark? Take the fee, drive south, watch the Vane brothers' names shine on new letterhead while Harrowgate stayed drowned and the people who'd survived the drowning got filed under *resolved*?

He thought about the man by the fire pit, holding his palm over the coals. Checking the warmth. That gesture would not leave him. There had been such patience in it, such housekeeping. These weren't angry men. They were tidy ones.

Somewhere in the black, Ines whispered, "You're awake."

"How can you tell?"

"You breathe different when you're lying to yourself. Shorter." A pause. The drip-clock counted three seconds away. "My father used to do contract surveys for the Vanes' father. Before. He used to say the family never lied to you — they just handed you words that were true in a way that wasn't. 'Salvage.' 'Forgiveness.' 'Good weather.'" Her voice was very quiet. "Which word did they hand you?"

Orsen stared up into a darkness so total it had texture. "Does it matter?"

"It matters to you. That's why you're awake."

He didn't answer. After a while her breathing evened out, and the drip kept its time, and Orsen lay there understanding, with the simple terrible clarity of a man at altitude, that he had stopped pretending somewhere between the burning tents and the ticking rock — that whatever he told the Vanes, whatever he told himself, there was no version of the road south where he drove it alone.

The lantern hood glowed faintly, a coin of amber in all that black. He watched it until his eyes closed.

* * *

He woke to Ines's hand on his shoulder and her face close, lit from below by a shielded match, and her expression was the one he imagined she'd worn the night the river came for the town.

"Smoke," she said. "On the north bank. Right above the second air shaft."

Above them, muffled by sixty feet of stone, something crackled — faint, rhythmic, patient. And threaded through it, thin as wire, came a sound that froze the blood in Orsen's ears:

Voices. Men talking. Directly overhead.
