## 7. Terms of Forgiveness

The radio crackled to life at dusk, when the light in the church had gone the color of old pewter and Ines was counting heads by memory, her lips moving silently as she touched each survivor's shoulder in turn. Orsen was on the porch of the ruined rectory, ostensibly keeping watch, actually trying to remember what it felt like not to owe anyone anything.

"Orsen." The voice came through the static like a hand through water. Merrick Vane. The younger brother, the smoother one, the one who'd shaken his hand at the loan signing with a grip like a promise. "You there, friend?"

Orsen's thumb found the transmit key and rested there. Behind him, through the broken window, he could hear Mara's low voice inside, walking Teo through breathing exercises for the pain in his ankle. He could hear the river, three hundred yards down and falling, patient as a creditor.

"I'm here," he said.

"There's my guy." Warmth flooded the frequency, that particular Vane warmth, sunshine sold by the yard. "We've been worried sick. Radio silence for six days — you know how Aldous gets. He pictures things. Terrible things. Drownings mostly. He's got an imagination like a drowned cat."

"What do you want, Merrick."

A pause. Just long enough. Then the laugh, soft and easy, the laugh of a man who has never once been surprised by bad news because he arranges all of it himself.

"Straight to business. I respect that. It's why we picked you, you know. Not the storm footage — though the supercell reel was beautiful, Orsen, genuinely, Aldous watched it four times — but the directness. A man who says what he wants is a man you can do business with."

Orsen looked out at the water. Harrowgate's drowned main street lay beneath it, rooftops furred with weed, the church steeple leaning like a drunk against the darkening sky. Somewhere under all that water was a town that had learned to flood on purpose, and somewhere above it was a girl who remembered every street the river had eaten, and somewhere behind him were four people whose survival was currently his responsibility, and none of it — not one square inch of any of it — was his business. His business was a debt. Forty-one thousand dollars, compounding weekly, held by men who smiled while they counted.

"So here's the weather report," Merrick said. "It's clearing up, Orsen. For you specifically. We're prepared to call the loan. Paid in full. Zero balance. We'll burn the paper ourselves, you can watch us do it. And there's a bonus in it — enough to get you a new truck, new rig, maybe chase next season without looking over your shoulder at collections. You could be free, friend. Clean slate. First day of the rest of your life, all that."

The words landed in Orsen's chest like coins dropped down a well. He heard them fall and fall and never hit bottom.

"And in exchange," Merrick went on, gentle now, reasonable, a man explaining simple arithmetic to a slow student, "you bring us the surveyor. Mara Vasquez. And whatever documents she's carrying. Alone. Tonight if you can manage it, tomorrow at the latest — the snow's coming early this year, and we'd hate for anyone to be caught up in it. There's a landing at Mile Thirty-One. You know it. Flat water, good sightlines. You bring her down, hand her over, walk away whole. Nobody else needs to know anything. Nobody else needs to be involved at all."

*Alone.* The word sat in Orsen's mouth like a stone.

"Merrick," he said carefully, "what happens to her."

Another pause. Shorter this time. When Merrick answered, the warmth was still there but something moved underneath it, the way a big fish moves under calm water.

"Nothing happens to her. She gets a generous severance and a ride home. Orsen. *Friend.* What kind of operation do you think we run?"

Orsen thought about the smashed radio at the abandoned camp forty miles upriver. About the drag marks in the soft ground leading into the timber, spaced too evenly to be stumbling, too deliberate to be anything but escort. About six days of silence from a team that had checked in twice daily for two years.

"The kind that loses survey teams," he said.

The static hissed. Somewhere far off in the church, Teo groaned, and Mara murmured something soothing, and the ordinary sounds of people taking care of each other filled the dark like a language Orsen didn't deserve to speak.

"We lost them to the river," Merrick said. "Rivers are dangerous. That's why we're offering so much. Risk compensation. You understand risk better than anybody — you drive *toward* the worst weather in the hemisphere for pocket change. We're just offering you fair market rate for once in your life. Think of it that way. Fair market rate for a man who's never once been paid what he's worth."

That was the knife of it. That was the part that slid in clean between the ribs, because it was true. Orsen Ishikawa, thirty-four years old, celebrated in exactly three storm-chasing forums and one regional news segment, broke in every sense the word had, sleeping in a crashed truck and eating canned peaches bought with borrowed money — he had never once been paid what he was worth. And here was a man on a radio offering him, finally, the number.

"How long do I have to decide?" Orsen asked.

"You've decided already," Merrick said pleasantly. "You just haven't told yourself yet. That's fine. That's human. Take until tomorrow night. Landing at Mile Thirty-One. Bring the woman and the papers, come alone, and drive home a free man. If she's not there — well." The warmth thinned to almost nothing. "Then we come up there ourselves, and I'm afraid the weather turns ugly for everybody. Even you, friend. *Especially* you. You know where the loan stands."

The radio clicked off. Orsen stood on the rectory porch with the handset dead in his hand and the first stars coming out over the ridge, and he did the thing he was best at in the world besides driving into storms: he did nothing. He let the silence pile up around him like snow.

Forty-one thousand dollars. Gone like smoke. A new truck. A season of chasing with nothing chasing him back. All he had to do was hand one tired woman to men who had already made six other people disappear, and lie about it, and live.

He could see it so clearly. That was the terrible part. He could see the depot at the Rivergate, the brothers burning his loan paper in a tin barrel, Merrick clapping him on the shoulder, Aldous counting out the bonus with those long pale fingers. He could see himself driving south with the windows down, debtless, the sky going green ahead of him over the flats, the supercell building like a cathedral. Freedom. He could taste it, metallic as the air before hail.

And underneath the vision, faint but getting louder, Mara's voice through the broken window: *again, Teo. Again. In through the nose.*

*

He went inside. That was the mistake, or the salvation; he wouldn't know which for a long time. He climbed through the rectory window and crossed the nave where the survivors had made their camp among the fallen pews, and the smell of the place hit him — woodsmoke and wet stone and someone's attempt at stew, wild onions and river fish — and Ines looked up at him from beside the fire with those eyes that missed nothing, that had never once missed anything, and said:

"You were talking to somebody."

"Just checking the weather," Orsen said.

Ines looked at him for three full seconds. He felt each one like a coin dropping into that same bottomless well. Then she nodded slowly and went back to braiding a fishing line out of salvaged twine, and Orsen understood that he had just lied to the only person in the world who could hear lies the way other people heard thunder.

Mara was sitting against a pillar with the claim documents wrapped in oilcloth in her lap, as she always kept them, asleep or awake, eating or praying. She looked up when he approached, and her face did what it always did: nothing. She was a woman who had spent six days being hunted and had decided her face was no longer available for public use.

"Anything?" she asked.

"Static," Orsen said. "Storms building over the divide. We should think about the pass soon — five days, maybe less, before it closes."

Teo, propped against a pew with his swollen ankle elevated on a hymnal, laughed weakly. "Five days. Wonderful. Nothing like a deadline to improve morale."

"It improves more than morale," Mara said quietly. "Deadlines make decisions for you." She looked at Orsen then, a long look, level and unreadable. "Did you hear anything else? On the radio? Anyone calling for us?"

The question hung there. Orsen had a moment — one clean, bright, terrible moment — in which telling the truth was still possible. He could say: *they offered me your life for my debt. They offered to erase everything I owe if I hand you over at Mile Thirty-One. I haven't said yes. But I haven't said no either, and I need you to know that, because you deserve to know exactly what kind of man is standing between you and the people hunting you.*

He could say that. The words were right there, fully formed, waiting.

"Nothing," Orsen said. "Nobody knows where we are. We're ghosts."

Mara nodded and closed her eyes, and some of the tension went out of her shoulders — trust, plain and unguarded, given to him like a gift he was already stealing from. Beside the fire, Ines pulled a knot tight in her fishing line and did not look up, and did not look up, and did not look up.

*

He couldn't sleep. He took the middle watch instead of his share of it, told the others he wasn't tired, and sat in the bell tower with the radio off and the cold coming down off the ridge like a slow tide. Below him the town breathed — the survivors' small fires, the river's long exhale, the creak of the steeple leaning its weight against the night.

He tried to build the case for it, honestly, the way he'd once built storm models. *They're going to catch her anyway,* he told himself. *Six armed men against five half-starved civilians and a girl with a slingshot. If I don't take the deal, they die and I die with them, and the documents die, and the Vanes win regardless. At least this way I live, and maybe I can find another way to fight them later, when I'm free, when I have resources—*

The argument collapsed under its own weight, the way arguments always do when they require you to pretend you're a future version of yourself who doesn't exist yet. There was no later Orsen with resources. There was only this one, in the bell tower, holding a dead radio, having already lied twice tonight to people who trusted him, and feeling the lie settle into him like silt into a riverbed — layer on layer, quiet, permanent, changing the course of everything that flowed over it.

Below, a door scraped. Ines came out of the church and crossed the flooded street on stones only she could see, moving through the dark with the certainty of water finding its level. She stopped at the base of the tower and stood there, not calling up, not leaving. Just standing. A small figure in a man's coat, looking north toward the pass where the snow was already falling somewhere above the tree line.

Orsen didn't go down. He couldn't have said what he was afraid of — her questions, or his answers. He sat in the dark with the temptation still warm in his chest, forty-one thousand dollars of it, freedom-shaped, and he told himself he hadn't decided.

But he knew the number of the landing. Mile Thirty-One. Flat water, good sightlines. He knew it the way he knew the pressure drop before hail — completely, and against his will.

Tomorrow night, Merrick had said.

Above the ridge, the first clouds of the front rolled over the stars, and somewhere in the white dark of the high country, the snow began.
