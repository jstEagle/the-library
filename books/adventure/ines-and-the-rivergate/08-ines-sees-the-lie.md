## 8. Ines Sees the Lie

Ines counted things the way other people breathed. It wasn't a choice. Her father had taught her the river's arithmetic before he'd taught her to read — *count the channels, count the days, count what the water takes and what it gives back* — and somewhere along the way the counting had spread into everything, into footsteps and heartbeats and the words people said when they thought no one was keeping score.

She was keeping score.

The radio exchange had happened at dusk, two nights after the smoke on the north bank, while the survivors huddled in the church's flooded crypt and Orsen climbed to the bell tower with the handset cupped to his ear. Ines had been in the tower too, three steps below him in the dark, mending a net with numb fingers. She hadn't meant to listen. But listening was what she did, the way the river listened to rain — without effort, without mercy.

*Terms of forgiveness,* the voice on the radio had said, smooth as silt. Merrick Vane's voice; she knew it from the year the Vanes' survey crews came through Harrowgate, from the year her father stopped sleeping. *Deliver the woman Mara and whatever papers she's carrying. Alone. Do that, Galloway, and every cent you owe us drowns. Clean ledger. Fresh start.*

And Orsen — Orsen Ishikawa, who had carried Teo's pack for two days without being asked, who had laughed at Mara's terrible jokes over the cook fire like a man who'd forgotten he was pretending — Orsen had been quiet for eleven seconds. She counted them. Eleven seconds is a long time for a man who talks the way water runs, always filling silence before it can open under him.

Then he'd said: *No offer? Nothing like that came up.*

A lie. A small one, the size of a coin, but coins sink men.

He'd come down from the tower whistling, and he'd lied again at supper — *quiet night, nobody on the radio* — and he'd smiled his charm-smile, the one that sat on his face like ice on still water, pretty and thin and hiding depth. And Ines had said nothing, because she was still deciding what the number meant.

Now it was morning, and the fog off the flooded streets was thick as wool, and Orsen was alone at the edge of town checking the snare lines he'd set among the drowned fences, and Ines followed him out across the boardwalk that only she could find — the submerged planks remembered by her feet, not her eyes — and stood behind him until he turned around.

He startled. Everyone startled when she did that. The river had taught her to arrive without sound.

"Morning," he said, easy, warm, automatic. "Snare's got a rabbit. We'll eat good tonight."

"You told them nothing came up on the radio."

The warmth didn't fall off his face so much as drain out of it, slow, like water finding a crack. He looked past her at the fog, at the drowned rooftops poking up black down Main Street, anywhere but at her.

"Ines—"

"Merrick Vane." She said the name flat, the way you name a dead thing. "He offered to wipe your debt. For Mara alone. You said yes with your silence for eleven seconds, and then you said *no offer*, and then you ate stew with us and asked Teo if his fingers were warming up." She tilted her head. "His left ring finger isn't. It's going black at the tip. Did you notice that, or were you thinking about your clean ledger?"

Orsen sat down slowly on the fence rail, which groaned under him even though it hadn't held a fence in thirty years. He rubbed his face with both hands, and when he took them away he looked older, and tired, and — this was the part Ines found herself cataloguing, filing away like a new channel in a flood — honest. Caught fish don't look honest. Drowned men don't either. But he did.

"How much did you hear?"

"All of it. I hear all of it. That's not a trick I turn on and off." She crouched on her heels in front of him, forearms on knees, the way her father used to crouch at the waterline. "I'm not telling the others."

That surprised him. She watched the surprise move through him like a current under ice.

"Why not?"

"Because Mara would make you leave today, and we need your hands, and Teo needs someone big to lean on crossing the scree, and—" She stopped herself. Recounted. Started over, because the first answer was about need and the true one was underneath it. "Because I want to know something first."

"Ask me anything." He said it fast, reflexively, the way he said everything, and then winced, because anything was exactly the wrong thing to promise her.

Ines looked at him for a long moment. Fog beaded in his hair. Behind him the town breathed its slow drowned breath, water moving in rooms where families had eaten supper, and she thought about how everything in Harrowgate looked fine from above — rooftops, steeples, chimney pots — and was hollow and cold and full of dark water underneath. People were like that too. She'd learned that the hard way, at twelve years old, from men with Vane money in their pockets.

"When the river lets you go," she said, "what do you want to be?"

Orsen blinked. "That's — what kind of question is that?"

"The only kind I ask." She kept her voice level. "You owe the Vanes money. Fine. Debts end. They drown them or you pay them or the magistrate burns the paper — debts end. So there's an after. A day when the river lets you go." She pointed downstream, toward the Rivergate, invisible beyond the hills but present in her mind the way every mile of water was present. "On that day, you walk out of their depot with a clean ledger. What's standing there? Who's standing there? Because right now I don't think you know. I think you've been running so long from what you owe that you never decided what you'd *be* once you didn't owe it."

The fog moved between them. Somewhere out in the flooded streets, a loon called, that wild laughing cry, and neither of them spoke until it finished.

"I'd be free," Orsen said finally, and it came out smaller than he probably wanted it to.

"Free of what?"

"Of them. Of the debt. Of—" He gestured at himself, at all of it, the crashed truck and the chased storms and the years of charming lenders out of money he'd spent on fuel and lenses and the next storm over the horizon. "Of being the guy who's always one bad week from drowning. You wouldn't understand. You live in a town that owns itself. Nobody holds paper on you."

"My father held paper on himself," Ines said quietly. "The Vanes bought it anyway. Ruined his surveys, called his loans, took his name off maps he drew. He walked into the river in spring when I was nine." She let that sit. She didn't dress grief up; the river didn't. "So I understand drowning better than you do. And here's what I know about it: the water doesn't care what you were running from. It just takes what's on top. If what's on top of you is a man who sells a woman who saved his life — that's what the river gets. That's what *we* get."

"I wasn't going to sell anyone." His voice cracked on it, defensive, and then he heard himself and shut his eyes. "God. Listen to me. I was going to — I told myself I'd find a way to give them Mara's location without — I told myself the papers stay with her, I'd just hand over — " He opened his eyes. "There's no version where I'm not handing them her. That's the arithmetic, isn't it? You'd have done the sum already."

"Eleven seconds," Ines said. "Then a lie. Then stew."

He laughed, and it was a bad sound, a sound with a stone in it. He put his head back and looked up at the white sky and said, mostly to the sky, "I'm good at charming people, Ines. It's the only real skill I've got. Charm a lender, charm a viewer, charm a storm — get close enough to take the picture and get out before it turns on you. That's my whole life. Get close, take what you need, get out." He looked at her. "And now I'm upriver with five people who trusted me, and I keep catching myself doing the math on the exit."

"Do you want to stop?"

"What?"

"Doing the math. On the exit." She rose out of her crouch, unhurried. "It's a habit, not a nature. My father said the river carves channels, but channels aren't the river. Water can go anywhere. It just forgets." She held out her hand, palm up, an old gesture from her father, meaning: *show me what you're carrying.* "You don't have to tell the others yet. But tell me the truth once, out loud, with nobody holding paper on you. Say what you almost did. Say it plain."

Orsen looked at her open hand for a long time. Then he took it — his grip cold, calloused, shaking slightly — and pulled himself upright off the rail, and stood in front of a girl half his age in a drowned town at the top of the world, and said:

"They offered me a clean ledger for Mara, and I said yes with my silence, and then I lied to your faces, and I've been lying since, and I don't know how to stop being that man because I've been him so long I don't know what's underneath."

The words came out ragged, and after them came silence, and in the silence Ines closed her fingers around his hand once and let go.

"Now you know what's underneath," she said. "It's a man who feels sick saying it. That's the start of a channel."

"And the others? When you—"

"I won't carry it for you. That's yours to say, and you'll say it soon, before the pass, because lies get heavy at altitude." She turned toward the boardwalk, toward the church, toward the smell of woodsmoke and the others waking. "But I'll stand next to you when you do. That's not forgiveness. Forgiveness comes later, if it comes. This is just — me knowing where the deep water is, so I don't step in it twice."

She took three steps along the sunken planks, sure-footed on nothing, and then stopped without turning around.

"Orsen. When the river lets you go — I think what's standing there is somebody worth the crossing. That's why I'm not telling them. Don't make me wrong."

Behind her she heard him exhale, long and unsteady, a man setting down weight he'd forgotten he was carrying.

* * *

They came down off the ridge above Harrowgate at midday, and the world had changed while Ines had been looking inward.

Snow. Not falling — waiting. It stood on the far peaks in a white line like a held breath, and the wind coming off the range had teeth in it now, and Mara stood in the church doorway squinting north with the expression of a woman doing arithmetic of her own.

"Five days," she said. "Maybe four. After that the pass closes till spring, and spring means the Vanes' crews come back upriver with the thaw." She looked at Orsen. "We move tomorrow. First light."

Orsen nodded. Then, before the others drifted off to packing, before the courage could cool, he set down the pack he was shouldering and said, loud enough for the whole nave:

"Before we go — there's something I have to tell you. About the radio. About what the Vanes offered me."

Ines, folding nets by the door, kept her eyes on her hands.

But she was counting his heartbeats. She couldn't help it. And she noticed they'd gone steady — steadier than they'd been any night he'd lied by the fire — and she thought: *there. There's the channel cutting. There's the water going somewhere new.*

Outside, far off to the north, the first flakes came down through the pines like the mountain clearing its throat.
