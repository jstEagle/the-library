## 9. The Range Ahead of the Snows

The frost came in the night like a debt collector, and by dawn it had taken everything green.

Orsen stood at the mouth of the mine shaft with his breath hanging in front of him, watching the light come up over Harrowgate's drowned rooftops. The water below the town had gone still and gray, holding the sky the way a ledger holds numbers — without opinion, without mercy. Somewhere under that surface lay streets, doorsteps, a schoolhouse bell. The river kept them all, and gave nothing back.

Behind him, the survivors were packing.

They'd been at it since first light, working by feel more than sight, because Mara insisted on no fires now and no lanterns near the shaft entrance. Everything they owned fit into six packs and two oilskin bundles: the claim documents sewn into Teo's coat lining, dried fish Ines had cured herself, rope, a hatchet, a tin of morphine tablets that Mara counted twice and then counted again. Five days of food if they ate like monks. Four days, if anyone got hurt.

And above them, five days before the snow closed the pass.

Orsen had done the arithmetic a dozen times since the radio went quiet last night, and every time it came out the same: the math was against them, and the only thing they had more of than trouble was distance.

"You're staring at the water again," Ines said.

She'd come up beside him without a sound, which was her way. She carried her slingshot slung across her back like other girls wore ribbons, and she was looking not at the river but at the ridge line north of town, where the timber climbed into scree and the scree climbed into the white shoulder of the pass.

"I'm looking at what we have to cross," Orsen said.

"No." She shook her head once, small and certain. "You're looking at the water. You always do when you're counting what you owe."

He almost laughed. It would have come out wrong, so he didn't. "You want to tell me what I owe?"

"The mountain doesn't care about your debts." She said it plainly, the way she said everything, as though reciting from a book no one else could read. "Neither does the snow. That's the good news."

* * *

They left Harrowgate an hour after sunrise, single file along a path that did not exist.

That was the only way Orsen could describe it. Ines walked point, and where she walked, there was footing — a shelf of stone under the flood-silt, a root system that had held a bank together for sixty years, a gravel bar the river had forgotten to move. Where Orsen walked, following in her exact steps, there was also footing. He tested this theory three times by drifting half a pace off her line, and three times the ground lied to him: soft shale, a sink of mud that took his boot to the ankle, once a hollow place over old mine workings that rang faintly, like a drumhead, under his weight.

"Stay in my prints," Ines said, without turning around. "The ground remembers where it's been stepped on. Mostly."

Mara came behind her, then the others — Hollis the geologist, quiet and gray-faced; Petter and Sanna, the young surveyors who had stopped speaking much since the ambush; and Teo at the rear, because Teo refused to be anywhere but the rear, his rifle slung and his bad hand wrapped in wool. They moved the way people move who have already been hunted once and intend never to be caught standing still: fast, low, silent, eyes always moving.

By midmorning they were above the flood line, and the country changed. The drowned town fell away behind them, and the Rivergate narrowed below into its gorge, a seam of white water stitching north. The timber thickened, then thinned, then gave way to the first scree fields — slopes of broken stone that shifted and sighed under their boots like something breathing in its sleep.

It was on the first scree field that Orsen understood what kind of journey this was going to be.

Not an adventure. He'd written enough adventures, back when he wrote anything at all, and he knew the shape of one: obstacles that meant something, weather that arrived on schedule, danger that made a person better by the end of it. This wasn't that. This was just country — enormous, indifferent, and already halfway into winter. The mountain did not know they were carrying proof that could ruin the Vane brothers. The mountain did not care about the ambush at the camp, or the men with torches, or the girl who remembered everything. If they all died on this slope, the snow would cover them by Thursday and the pass would close on schedule, and the world would proceed exactly as designed.

There was a strange comfort in that, he decided. Justice was theirs to carry. The wilderness owed nobody anything, which meant it couldn't be bought by the Vanes either.

"Ines." Mara's voice, low, from the middle of the line. "How far to the saddle?"

Ines stopped. She didn't consult a map — none of them had one worth trusting; the expedition's maps had burned with the camp — and she didn't guess. She stood with her head slightly tilted, listening to something, and Orsen had learned by now not to interrupt whatever it was.

"Two ridges," she said finally. "Then the long moraine. We sleep at the tree line tonight, at the bend where the creek goes under the stones. Tomorrow we climb. Day after, the saddle." A pause. "If the wind stays down."

"And if it doesn't?" Petter asked.

Ines looked at him. She was maybe sixteen, small in her father's oversized canvas coat, and she had the kind of face that made adults stop asking questions they didn't want answered.

"Then we learn what the saddle thinks of us," she said, and walked on.

* * *

They made the tree line by late afternoon, exactly where she'd said, at the bend where the creek went under the stones. Orsen got the tent up while Hollis coaxed a smokeless fire from dead spruce in a hollow the wind couldn't reach — a fire you could stand next to and not see from thirty feet, which was the only kind of fire they allowed themselves anymore.

Teo sat apart, working his frostbitten hand inside the wool wrap, flexing fingers that didn't want to flex. Orsen crouched beside him and held out the tin cup.

"How is it?"

"It's a hand," Teo said. "Ask me tomorrow."

Orsen drank his tea and watched the light die on the upper ridges. The peaks to the north had gone the color of iron, then the color of ash, and the wind had begun to make its evening argument with the summit rocks — a low, continuous grinding sound, like millstones, like the mountain chewing on itself.

"Mara says you were a storm chaser," Teo said suddenly.

"Was. Am. Depends on whether the truck ever gets out of impound."

"What's it like? Chasing something that big?"

Orsen considered the question honestly, which was more than he usually managed. "It's like standing in front of something that has completely made up its mind," he said. "About everything. And knowing you can't argue with it — you can only watch, and take notes, and try not to be where it's decided to go."

Teo nodded slowly. "And you miss it."

"I miss being the one who chose to be there."

That sat between them for a while. Downriver, somewhere behind the darkening hills, the Vanes' enforcers were moving too — Orsen was sure of it. Smoke on the north bank two days ago hadn't been an accident, and neither had the cut fence at the lower ford. Aldous and Merrick Vane did not spend money; they invested it. And Orsen knew, with the flat certainty of a man reading his own contract, that somewhere in their optimistic ledgers there was a new entry being drafted: *one salvage operator, overdue, value declining.*

"We'll make the saddle tomorrow night," he said, standing. "Day after we're over. Then it's downhill all the way to the magistrate at the Rivergate mouth."

"You believe that?" Teo asked. "The downhill part?"

Orsen looked north, at the pass — a pale notch between black shoulders, already wearing its winter collar of cloud.

"No," he said. "But I believe in the five days."

* * *

The second day the wind stayed down, and they climbed.

It was the hardest walking Orsen had ever done, and he'd once spent nine hours wading through flooded bottomland filming a waterspout. The moraine Ines had promised was a mile of loose stone stacked by some ancient glacier and left where it fell, and it moved underfoot with every step, giving back nothing. Hollis fell twice and got up both times without complaint, which frightened Orsen more than shouting would have. Sanna twisted an ankle at noon and bound it herself and kept walking. Nobody suggested stopping, because everyone had done the same arithmetic: five days minus two was three, and the pass closed when it closed, with or without them.

Ines never faltered. She moved up the moraine like water finding its level, testing each stone with a tap of her boot before committing her weight, and twice she changed the route without explanation — cutting east around a slope that looked fine to everyone else, and ten minutes later they heard the west side let go, a whole sheet of scree sliding away in a long rumbling exhale that went on and on until the valley swallowed it.

After the second time, Orsen caught up to her at a rest stop and sat down beside her on a boulder the size of a wagon.

"How do you do that?" he asked. "Know which slope is going to fail?"

She was eating dried fish in small, economical bites, watching the ridge ahead. "My father taught me the river," she said. "The river teaches you everything else. Water picks the weak places. Stone does too. You just have to look at a slope the way water would look at it." She glanced at him. "You look at mountains the way a man looks at a bill he can't pay."

"That obvious?"

"To me."

He huffed something that was almost a laugh. "Fair. I've been insolvent so long I've started thinking in installments. Every day up here is a payment toward something. I keep waiting for the part where I own something instead of owing it."

Ines chewed, swallowed, considered him with those level dark eyes. "What would you own? If it worked."

Orsen opened his mouth to say *my truck*, which was the true answer and also the small one, and found he couldn't say it. Up here, with the pass shouldering into the clouds ahead of them and four people sleeping badly behind him because of a lie he was still carrying — the Vanes' offer, the one he'd told them never happened — the small answers felt like what they were.

"I don't know yet," he said.

Ines nodded, as if that were the correct answer, and stood, and pointed north. "Saddle by dark," she said. "Walk faster."

* * *

They reached the saddle as the sun went down behind them, and the pass showed them its teeth.

The notch between the peaks was filled with cloud — not drifting over it, but sitting in it, packed like wool into a box, glowing faintly red with the last light. The temperature had fallen twenty degrees in the last hour. Their breath came out and turned to rime on their collars. And from the notch, the wind came down at them in long, steady pulses, each one colder than the last, each one carrying a smell Orsen knew from a hundred storm fronts: the smell of air that has been wrung dry and is coming back for moisture.

Snow-smell. Winter, loading its weapon.

"We camp here," Mara said. "Below the notch, in the lee. We cross at first light."

"If it's clear," Hollis said.

"If it's clear," Mara agreed, and no one said the other thing, which was: *and if it isn't.*

They made camp in a hollow of stone below the eastern wall, backs to the wind, packs stacked as a windbreak. Orsen took first watch, because he always took first watch — it was easier to stay awake guarding other people's sleep than to lie in the dark doing sums about lies and debts and the distance to the Rivergate mouth.

The stars came out hard and close. The cold deepened by the hour. And sometime past midnight, Orsen saw it: a light, far below and behind them, down in the valley they'd crossed yesterday. Not a star. Not a reflection. A small orange point that moved, stopped, moved again — patient, methodical, sweeping the slopes of the lower moraine the way a lamp sweeps a room.

A searchlight. Or a lantern on a pole. Either way, someone was climbing.

He watched it for a long time, doing the arithmetic one more time. A day behind them, maybe less. Moving at night, which meant they weren't worried about the terrain, which meant they knew it, or didn't care, or had lights enough to buy their way through the dark.

Orsen woke Ines without touching her — just said her name once, low, the way you'd wake someone you trusted with a knife.

She was instantly awake. He pointed. She looked, and her face in the starlight didn't show fear. It showed recognition, the way you'd recognize handwriting.

"They're counting our camps," she said quietly. "Reading where we slept. Like tracks in a ledger." She turned to him. "How many days of food?"

"Three. Maybe four."

"And how many days are we from the river?"

"Two. Three, if the weather turns."

Ines looked north, at the pass, where the cloud in the notch had begun, very slowly, to glow white instead of red — the first moonlight on new snow falling somewhere above them, sifting down into the dark.

"Then we walk tonight," she said. "While they're still reading yesterday."

Orsen looked at the sleeping shapes of the others — Mara with the documents sewn against her ribs, Teo with his ruined hand, the children of a drowned town who had already survived one massacre and were not going to survive another by resting. Then he looked at the notch, where the snow was falling, thin and silent, onto the only road out of these mountains.

Five days, he'd promised them. The mountain had just taken two back.

"All right," he said, and reached for his boots. "Wake them gently. From here on, we don't stop."
