## 10. Cold Camp at the Saddle

Teo had counted the days wrong. He knew it the way you know a lie you told yourself — not all at once, but in the slow accumulation of small betrayals: the light going gray an hour too early, the wind swinging north, the smell of snow arriving before the snow itself. Five days to cross the pass, Orsen had said. They were on day three and the saddle was still a rumor above them, a pale notch in the black teeth of the range.

"Two more hours," Orsen said, for the third time.

"You said that two hours ago," Teo said. His voice came out smaller than he intended. The cold had gotten into his hands overnight — he'd slept with them tucked into his armpits like everyone else, but his sleeping bag was the cheap one, the one he'd bought because the expedition's budget had been cut by men who owned banks instead of boots — and sometime before dawn his left hand had stopped hurting.

That was the bad part. Pain meant the blood was arguing. No pain meant the blood had given up.

Orsen looked back at him, and Teo watched the man do the arithmetic on his face: the hand, the sky, the distance. Orsen Ishikawa had a face built for charm, quick and easy, but up here at eleven thousand feet with the weather closing like a fist, the charm had burned off him and what was left was something steadier. A man who'd been lying to himself about why he'd come, and had recently stopped.

"We camp," Orsen said.

"Ines?" Mara called ahead.

The girl had been walking point all morning without a map, without a compass, without anything but her own head, and she turned now with the wind pulling her hair sideways across her face. She looked at the sky the way other people read documents — carefully, completely, once.

"The saddle's behind that ridge," she said. "There's a hollow under the east wall. Stone on three sides. My father sheltered there in a storm like this one." She paused, and her eyes went somewhere else for a moment, somewhere years away. "He wrote about it. I remember where he wrote it."

Nobody asked how a girl could remember a sentence her father had written in a journal she might have seen once, or heard described, or dreamed. By now they'd all stopped asking. Ines remembered things the river remembered, and the river did not forget, and neither did she.

They cut east along the ridge's shoulder, and within twenty minutes the world began to end.

It didn't come down as snow at first. It came down as white — a horizontal whiteness, a whiteness with intent, moving so fast and so thick that Teo lost sight of Mara four feet ahead of him and had to follow the sound of her breathing. The temperature dropped through the floor of the world. His wet cuffs went stiff. Somewhere behind him, Dr. Anselm — the oldest of them, the geologist who hadn't spoken more than ten words since the ambush — made a sound that might have been a prayer or just his teeth chattering in a new rhythm.

Then Ines's voice came out of the white, calm as still water.

"Ten steps down. There's a drop-off on your left. Hold my rope."

* * *

The hollow was exactly where she said it would be, which was to say it shouldn't have been there at all. A scoop of stone beneath an overhang, floored with old pine needles blown in from a stand of trees that had no business growing this high, walled from the wind on three sides. Ines walked them into it one at a time, counting them in, and when Teo's turn came she took his wrist — the right one, the good hand — and guided him the last few steps like he was a child being led through a dark house.

Inside, out of the screaming, the silence rang.

They got a fire going — Orsen's stove, hoarded fuel, a flame the size of a cupped palm — and huddled around it while the storm hammered the overhang above them and spindrift sifted down like the mountain shedding its skin. Teo sat with his left hand held toward the heat and felt nothing, and understood, with a clarity that frightened him more than the enforcers ever had, that he was running out of hand.

Mara saw it first. She always saw things first; it was what had kept four of them alive when nine of their expedition died. She took his fingers gently, turned them, pressed a thumbnail into the flesh of his palm.

"Teo."

"I know."

"It's gone gray."

"I *know*, Mara."

She didn't flinch. She'd survived an ambush that killed five colleagues; a man shouting at her wasn't going to move her. "We rewarm it. Slowly. Skin to skin, not direct heat — you'll burn it and never feel it happen." She was already pulling off her own outer glove, already working his frozen fingers against the warmth of her own palm, matter-of-fact, clinical, kind. "And we keep it warm from now until we're off this mountain. You sleep with it against someone's body every night. That's not a request."

"That's—" Teo laughed, and it came out cracked. "That's humiliating."

"Yes," Mara agreed. "So is losing the hand. Choose your humiliation."

Dr. Anselm snorted from the shadows, and even Orsen smiled, and the little fire popped, and for one moment — one strange, suspended moment — they were not five fugitives in a stone hole above the tree line. They were just people taking care of each other.

Ines sat apart, as she usually did, knees drawn up, watching the white wall of the storm with an expression Teo couldn't read. Not fear. Something more like attention. As if the storm were speaking and she was listening.

* * *

Night came early and stayed long. The storm didn't break; if anything it deepened, the wind finding new harmonies in the rocks above them, and twice during the dark hours the whole overhang shuddered under gusts that made Orsen sit bolt upright with his hand on his knife.

Teo couldn't sleep. The pain had come back to his hand around midnight — Mara had been right, the rewarming worked, and the blood returning felt like being stung by a thousand bees — and pain plus altitude plus terror made a poor lullaby. So he lay there between Mara and Anselm, his ruined hand tucked against Mara's ribs as ordered, and listened to the mountain try to kill them, and thought about the survey team they used to be. Nine people and a radio and a grant and a future. Now: four survivors, a debt-ridden stranger with a good arm, and a girl who navigated whiteouts by memory.

Around what he guessed was three in the morning, he gave up and crawled to the mouth of the hollow, where Ines sat wrapped in a tarp, awake.

"You should sleep," he said.

"So should you."

"Fair." He lowered himself beside her, careful of his hand, and looked out into the white. It wasn't darkness out there — that was the eerie thing. The storm-light made a kind of luminous gray blindness, snow moving past the hollow's mouth in rivers, and beyond six feet nothing existed. "How do you do it? Out there. You walked us two miles through that today and never hesitated. Never once checked yourself."

Ines was quiet for a while. When she answered, her voice was low, almost private.

"When I was small, my father walked me everywhere and made me close my eyes. Count the steps. Feel the slope change under my feet. Smell the water coming." She pulled the tarp tighter. "After the flood took Harrowgate, I kept doing it. Because if I stopped, I'd forget him. And then one day I realized I knew the country better with my eyes shut than anyone knew it with maps." A pause. "The storm doesn't hide the land from me. The land is in here." She touched her temple. "The storm is just weather."

Teo stared at her. He was a surveyor's assistant; he'd spent three years learning instruments, triangulation, contour reading, and he could not have found that hollow tonight with satellite imagery and a prayer. This girl carried the entire range in her head, every fold of it, the way other people carried a song.

"You're the most valuable thing on this mountain," he said. "You know that?"

Something flickered across her face — discomfort, maybe, or the ghost of grief. "I'm just what's left," she said. "But I'm enough."

* * *

Morning brought no improvement. If anything the storm had organized itself, tightened from chaos into a sustained, methodical violence. Orsen stood at the hollow's mouth for a long time, watching the white, and when he turned around his face had gone grim in a way Teo was learning to dread.

"We can't move in this. We'd walk off a cliff inside an hour."

"Then we wait," Mara said.

"Waiting costs us. The pass closes for the season soon — really closes. And those men don't stop for weather." He rubbed his jaw. "But dead is dead, whether it's frostbite or a bullet. We wait for a window."

The window, such as it was, came at midday — a thinning of the storm, not a break, the white easing from absolute to merely terrible. Ines stood, tilted her head as though listening to something far away, and said:

"Now. While it's like this. Two hours to the saddle, and after the saddle the wind drops. After that it gets worse again, and worse than this."

"How can you possibly know—"

"Because I've been listening to it all night." She was already tying her rope. "It repeats. Storms repeat. This one has a rhythm and the rhythm has a gap. We go in the gap."

Orsen looked at her for a long moment, and then at Mara, and Mara shrugged: *she hasn't been wrong yet.* So they broke camp in eight minutes flat, and stepped out into the roaring gray, and Ines led.

What followed was the strangest two hours of Teo's life. Blind, essentially — visibility of thirty feet, then twenty, then ten — following a sixteen-year-old girl who moved through the whiteout like it was a familiar hallway. She never slowed. She called changes before they happened: *"Rocks under the snow here, high steps, count them"*; *"the slope tips left after the next rise, lean into it"*; *"wind funnel coming, heads down, twelve seconds."* Twelve seconds later the wind hit like a train and passed, exactly as promised. Teo, stumbling in her wake with his bandaged hand cradled against his chest, felt his skepticism die and something else take root in its place — awe, plain and undiluted, the awe you feel watching a person do the impossible casually, without showing off, because to her it isn't impossible at all.

Once, only once, she stopped. Stood motionless in the maelstrom, head cocked, for a full minute, snow building on her shoulders.

"What is it?" Orsen shouted.

"This ridge ends." She frowned. "My father's notes say it ends in scree. But the sound is wrong. It ends in *air*." She moved left, forty feet, picked a new line. "The flood changed it. The river took part of this ridge last spring. Nobody's map knows that." She glanced back at them, and for the first time Teo saw her afraid — not of the storm, but of the gap between what she remembered and what the world had become. "Except mine. I have to update myself as we go. Stay exactly behind me."

They crossed the missing ridge on a shelf she found by feel, single file, the void yawning somewhere to their right, invisible and enormous. Teo didn't look. He fixed his eyes on the back of Ines's hood and put one foot in front of the other and trusted her with everything he had left, which, he was beginning to understand, was exactly what all of them were doing.

* * *

They reached the saddle at dusk, half-frozen, half-dead, wholly alive.

It was a shallow notch between two black towers of rock, drifted deep with powder, and true to Ines's word the wind dropped the moment they dropped below its lip — as if they'd stepped over a threshold into another, gentler country. They dug in against the lee wall, got the stove going, and ate cold rations warmed against their bodies, and nobody spoke much because speech cost energy they were saving.

Teo sat with his hand buried in his coat against his stomach, per doctor-Mara's orders, and watched the others through the blue twilight. Anselm had thawed enough to mutter geology at the rock walls, identifying strata like old friends' names. Mara was rewrapping Teo's spare bandages with the efficiency of someone who'd decided he was going to live and considered the matter settled. Orsen was checking the rope, coiling it, checking it again — a man whose hands needed work the way other men's needed drink.

And Ines stood at the edge of the saddle, looking back the way they'd come, into the storm they'd crossed.

Teo got up and joined her. Below them, the pass they'd climbed was already erased — no tracks, no trail, no evidence that five human beings had ever come that way. The mountain had wiped them clean. Any pursuit would start from nothing.

"They can't follow us through that," he said. "Not tonight. Maybe not at all."

"They can follow," Ines said quietly. "Men like that always find a way. But they'll be slow, and lost, and angry." She turned to him, and in the last gray light her eyes were steady. "Slow and lost and angry makes mistakes. We won't."

"No?"

"No. Because when we get lost, I find us. That's the difference." She said it without pride, the way you'd state the weather. Then, softer: "Your hand. How is it?"

Teo flexed his fingers inside his coat. They answered — stiff, stupid, painful, but they answered. "Still mine," he said.

"Good." She looked back down the pass, and her voice dropped, almost to herself. "Hold onto what's yours, Teo. Up here, the mountain takes whatever you don't hold."

He was about to answer when Orsen's shout cracked across the saddle.

"Light! Down the south slope — look!"

They turned. Far below, threading the dark foothills like a lit fuse, a chain of lights was climbing. Headlamps. Six of them, maybe seven, moving with the unhurried confidence of men who knew where they were going — moving, Teo realized with a cold that had nothing to do with the altitude, along the exact route Ines had chosen for them.

Not following their trail. Following their *plan*.

Beside him, Ines went very still, and when she spoke, her voice was barely a breath.

"They know the gap in the storm too," she said. "Someone down there remembers this country."
