## 11. The Bridge the River Ate

The river announced the bridge before we saw it — a change in its voice, from the low conversational mutter it had kept since the saddle to something harder, angrier, the sound of water being forced through a throat too narrow for it.

Ines stopped at the head of the trail and held up one hand, and the whole column stopped with her. We had learned that much in four days. Mara shifted Teo's arm higher across her shoulders; the boy walked on his own feet but only barely, the frostbite in his left hand having crept up past the wrist into something gray and silent. He hadn't complained once. That frightened me more than the hand did.

"There," Ines said.

The trestle came out of the mist the way bones come out of a melting snowbank — all at once, and worse than you'd hoped. It had been a rail bridge once, back when Harrowgate shipped ore instead of ghosts: timber bents rising out of the white water like the legs of drowned giants, stringers running bank to bank maybe ninety yards through the spray. Half the deck planks were gone. Of the ones that remained, I'd have trusted perhaps a third, and I was a man who had trusted a tornado's path to a hunch more than once.

"That's the only crossing?" I asked, though I already knew. Ines didn't answer questions she considered answered. She just looked at me with those flat river-gray eyes until I felt foolish for asking.

"Below the gorge it's forty-foot water," she said instead. "Above, the canyon walls come together. There's a place my father knew, but it's two days north and Teo doesn't have two days." She said it without drama, the way she said everything, a fact set down on a ledger. "It's the bridge."

* * *

We camped above the crossing while I went down to look at it properly, because someone had to, and because everyone else had quietly agreed not to look at me when the decision got made. That was the thing about this group I still hadn't gotten used to: they'd made me the leader somewhere around mile sixty of the pass without ever saying so, and they let me carry it like a pack I couldn't put down.

The trestle was worse up close. The timber had gone soft as bread left in milk. I could push my thumb into a stringer and leave a print. Where the bents met the water, the current had carved the pilings thin as fence posts, and the whole structure shivered — actually shivered — under the vibration of the river running through it. Downstream, where the gorge narrowed, the water stood up in standing waves taller than a man and threw spray thirty feet in the air. The mist hung over everything like a curtain of cold glass beads.

I rigged the line anyway. It's what I knew how to do — storm work is mostly rigging, mostly tying yourself to something solid before the world comes apart. I found sound wood at the near anchorage, an old iron ring bolted into the abutment by some dead railroad man with more faith than sense, and ran my climbing rope through it, then a second line as a handrail, tensioned off to a bent that looked less rotten than its neighbors. I tested each plank ahead of me by kicking it, crossed alone first, came back. It took two hours. My hands were shaking by the end, and not entirely from the cold.

"It'll hold," I told them. "One at a time. Light steps, middle of the span where the stringers are doubled. Don't look down."

Mara went first, then the others — Halloway the geologist, who crossed like a man walking to his own funeral and was sick when he reached the far side; Petra, quick and light as a bird; the cook, whose name I still got wrong. Then Mara brought Teo across, slow, his boots dragging, and I watched every plank under them and hated every second of it.

That left me, the rope, and the problem I'd been carrying since the saddle.

The salvage fee. Half of it, anyway — the Vane brothers' advance, paid in cash because men like the Vanes paid in cash so there'd be nothing to trace and nothing to argue about. Fourteen hundred dollars in a waterproof satchel I'd worn against my chest since Harrowgate like a second heart. It was the number that had gotten me up the Rivergate. It was the number that would keep the collections men off my door through winter, that would buy back my truck from impound, that would let me stand in front of Aldous Vane and say *we're square* and mean it. Every step of this crossing, I'd been thinking about that satchel the way a drowning man thinks about air.

I clipped it to the handrail line on its own carabiner, so it would ride the rope across independent of me. Clean. Sensible. I was halfway across the span when I heard Teo shout.

He'd gone back for the rope coil. We'd left it coiled at the near anchorage — my mistake, my oversight, mine — and the boy, feverish, half-frozen, trying to be useful, had shouldered it and started out onto the deck before anyone could stop him. I turned and saw him four planks in, swaying, and saw the plank under his left foot give way with a crack like a rifle shot.

He dropped through to his waist. His good hand caught a crossbeam and held. The river took his legs and pulled, and the whole broken section of deck around him groaned and began, slowly, to fold inward like a closing hand.

* * *

I don't remember deciding. I remember the rope burning through my gloved hands and the spray hitting me like thrown gravel and the world narrowing down to the boy's face, white as the foam below him. The handrail line sang under my weight. A plank kicked loose behind me and spun away into the gorge and was gone before the sound of it caught up.

"Teo! Look at me. Look at my voice." He was looking at the water. Everyone looks at the water. "Up here, kid. Grab my wrist."

His good hand found my forearm and gripped with a strength that surprised me, fever-strength, dying-animal strength. I got my other arm around his chest and braced against the crossbeam he'd been hanging from, and that was when the beam let go.

Not broke — *let go*, the whole rotted section sagging off its stringers like wet cardboard, and we went with it. The river hit me the way a supercell's downdraft hits you on a ridge: total, instant, indifferent. Cold went through me like a blade finding the space between ribs. The current had us both and it wasn't curious about us, it wasn't cruel, it simply moved, the way weather moves, and we were things in its path.

The satchel saved my life before it killed anything else. Its tether — the carabiner on the handrail — snapped taut as I went under, jerking me sideways hard enough that I saw stars, and that sideways jerk carried me and Teo out of the main chute and into the eddy behind the third bent. I got an arm around a piling. The piling was slimy and thinner than it looked and flexed under my grip like something alive, but it held, and I got Teo's collar in my fist and pinned him against the timber, and for a long moment the two of us just hung there in the freezing spray with the gorge roaring past close enough to swallow.

My chest was empty where the satchel had been. I felt it happen almost gently — the strap sliding off my shoulder as the current worked at me, the carabiner holding, the rope paying out, and then the buckle giving, the way cheap buckles give, and fourteen hundred dollars going down the throat of the Rivergate like a coin into a well. I watched the strap whip away into the white. I didn't even shout. There was nothing to shout at. You can't bargain with moving water; any chaser learns that young.

"Ines!" Mara's voice, torn apart by the gorge. Then Ines was on the deck above us — I saw her boots between the missing planks, small and certain — and she had the rope's free end in her hands, and she was paying it out and calling angles to me in that flat clear voice that never rose, telling me where the current would ease, where the dead water pooled behind the bent, counting me down the river like she was reading it off a page written inside her skull.

"On three, let the piling go and kick left. The water will do the rest. One. Two."

On three I let go.

The river did the rest. It swept us left, exactly where she'd said, into slack water under the far abutment, and hands were on us — Halloway, Petra, Mara hauling her brother's dead weight over the stones — and then I was on my back on the gravel with the sky spinning overhead and my lungs full of fire, alive, which felt at that moment less like a victory than a technicality.

Teo coughed, rolled, vomited river water, and started to cry — the first sound I'd heard him make in two days. Mara folded around him and didn't say anything at all, which was the right thing to say.

I lay there and did the arithmetic, because I'm a man who does arithmetic at the worst possible moments. The satchel was gone. The advance was gone. Which meant that when I walked into the Vanes' depot at the Rivergate mouth, I wouldn't be a contractor delivering; I'd be a debtor returning with nothing, owing more than ever, with no proof I'd ever been owed anything at all. Aldous Vane's smile. Merrick's little laugh, the one like a hinge. *Fourteen hundred, Ishikawa. Where's our fourteen hundred?*

I'd lost the money that was supposed to save me, and what I'd bought with it was a boy's life, and the terrible thing — the thing I sat with while the fire was built and my clothes steamed and nobody asked me anything — was how light I felt. How the loss sat in me like a stone finally set down.

* * *

They dried me and fed me and pretended not to notice that I kept touching the empty place on my chest where the satchel rode. Ines sat across the fire sharpening the knife she used for everything, and after a while she spoke without looking up.

"You went back onto the deck for him before anyone called you."

"He shouldn't have been out there. The rope was my oversight."

"The rope was an accident. Going after him was a choice." She tested the edge against her thumbnail. "The river takes things, Orsen. It took the town, it took my father's roads, it took your bag. It doesn't give things back. People are the only ones who do that." She looked up then, and the firelight moved in her eyes. "You gave something back today."

"I gave away my fee."

"Yes," she said. "Both true."

Mara came and sat beside me, wrapping Teo in every dry thing we owned. For a long time she watched the fire. When she finally spoke, her voice was low enough that only I could hear it.

"The documents we're carrying are worth more than your fee. Worth more than everything the Vanes own, if we get them to the magistrate. Remember that, when you're counting what the river cost you." She glanced at me sidelong. "And remember that I saw what you traded for my brother. Debts run both directions, Mr. Ishikawa. Even ones nobody signed."

I wanted to tell her then about the radio. About the offer. About the lie sitting in me like swallowed glass, getting sharper every day. The words were right there, warming in my throat.

That was when Petra came down from the rocks above camp, fast, no words, just pointing north across the gorge.

Smoke. Thin and deliberate, three fires in a line along the ridgeline on the far bank — spaced like men spacing themselves on a search grid. Not a hunter's camp. A net, being drawn closed.

Ines stood, knife still in her hand, and read the smoke the way she read water.

"They know the bridge is the crossing," she said. "They're waiting for us on the other side."
