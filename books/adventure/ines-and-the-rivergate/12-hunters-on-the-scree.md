## 12. Hunters on the Scree

The scree field announced them before we saw them.

It was a small sound at first — a tick of stone against stone, somewhere below and to the east, where the slope fell away in a long gray tongue of broken rock toward the tree line. I'd been walking point for an hour with my collar up against the wind, counting switchbacks out of habit, when the sound came again. Not a goat. Goats move in bursts, careless. This was deliberate. Weight placed, then weight placed again.

I put my fist up and the column stopped behind me. Five days out from Harrowgate, two days past the saddle, and we had become a machine of small signals: Mara's hand on Teo's shoulder to steady him, Ines reading the wind by wetting a finger, the survivors moving when I moved because they'd learned that I didn't raise my voice for nothing.

Ines came up beside me without a word. She crouched, laid her palm flat on a boulder the size of a bread loaf, and held it there like she was taking its pulse.

"Three of them," she said. "Maybe four. They're not trying to be quiet anymore."

She was right. That was the part that turned my stomach cold. For five days we'd been ghosts — doubling back, sleeping cold, burning no fires after dark — and it had worked. But now the sounds below us were unhurried, almost casual. Men who knew they didn't need to hurry.

"They found our trail," Mara said, coming up on my left. Her face was wind-scoured and set. "Where?"

"The descent off the saddle." I hated saying it. The storm had cost us. We'd sheltered two days in a rock overhang while Teo's frostbite went from bad to worse, and when we'd finally moved, our tracks through the meltwater slush had been printed into the mountainside like a signature. "They've been behind us since yesterday. I thought we'd bought more distance."

"You hoped," Ines said. Not cruel. Just precise, the way she always was about terrain and truth alike.

"I hoped," I admitted.

Below us, maybe six hundred yards down the scree, something glinted. Then again, further along. Sunlight off glass or off a scope — I couldn't tell which, and I wasn't eager to learn.

* * *

We pulled back off the skyline into a fold of rock and held a council that lasted ninety seconds, because ninety seconds was what we had.

Mara spread the map she'd drawn from memory — the survey map, the one that had started all of this, the one that showed mineral rights under half the Rivergate headwaters worth more than the Vane brothers' entire optimistic empire. It was wrapped in oilcloth inside her coat, against her skin, and she touched it now like a woman touching a wound.

"There's only one way down from here that isn't the scree," she said. "The canyon. Ines?"

Ines was already looking north, toward a seam in the mountainside I would have sworn was solid rock until you got within fifty feet of it and saw the shadow of a slot canyon cutting back into the range. She'd been staring at it since dawn. I understood now that she hadn't been admiring the view. She'd been memorizing it.

"The canyon works," she said. "For us. Not for them."

"How do you figure?" Teo asked. He was sitting propped against a boulder, his bandaged foot stretched out, his color bad. He'd stopped complaining on day three, which worried me more than complaining ever had.

"Because the canyon floods," Ines said. "There's snowmelt coming down from the high cirque. If rain hits the peaks tonight — and it will, look at the clouds — the water comes through that slot like a freight train. Anyone in it when that happens is gone." She looked at me. "Anyone who doesn't know exactly where the high shelves are is gone too."

"And you know where the shelves are."

"I know where every shelf is." She said it without pride, the way you'd say *the sun rises*. "My father mapped this whole face before the Vanes took everything from him. He walked me through it when I was eight. Every ledge, every choke point, every place the water goes when the water decides to go somewhere." A pause. "He never came back down from the high route. But he told me about it enough times that I've walked it in my head a thousand times."

Nobody said anything for a moment. There was nothing to say. The river had raised this girl on stories of a dead man's maps, and now those maps were the only thing standing between us and men with rifles.

"So we take the canyon," I said. "And they take the scree, and we meet on the other side of the mountain."

"No," Mara said quietly. "They won't take the scree. They'll split. Some will follow the canyon rim and try to shoot down into the slot. That's what I'd do."

She was right, and we all knew it. I looked at the terrain the way I used to look at a supercell — reading structure, reading rotation, trying to see what the sky intended before it did it. The canyon mouth was visible from the lower scree. Any group entering it would be fish in a slot. Unless —

Unless the fish were bait.

"We split," I said, and heard my own voice go flat and calm, the voice I used when the hail was coming down like God throwing gravel. "Two groups. One goes loud and visible across the open slope to the west — makes noise, shows themselves, gives them something to chase. The other takes the canyon quiet."

"That's suicide for whoever's loud," Teo said.

"It's suicide if they get caught. They won't get caught. There's a bench of timber halfway down the west slope — I saw it this morning. Old burn, thick regrowth. You can lose anyone in there, and it funnels back to the canyon's lower outlet." I traced it on Mara's map with a finger. "Whoever draws the decoy runs for the timber, sits tight till dark, then comes down to the outlet. We rendezvous there."

"And who draws the decoy?" Mara asked.

Everyone looked at everyone else, which is how these things always go, and then everyone looked at me, because I was the biggest, and because I was the one being paid — or who had been paid, once, before I threw the fee into a river saving Teo from a rotted trestle. I was the one whose debt had started this. Somewhere in a ledger in a warm office in Rivergate City, my name still sat in a column, waiting for the Vanes to collect it in whatever currency they preferred.

"I'll take it," I said.

"No," Ines said.

It wasn't a plea. It was a flat, geological fact of a word, and it stopped the conversation like a boulder stops a creek.

"You don't know the west slope," she said. "You'll miss the timber line in the smoke haze and end up on the open rock. And you're the only one besides me who can handle the rope work at the canyon's second choke. If you're not there, the others can't get down." She met my eyes. Hers were gray as the scree and just as unmovable. "You go in the canyon. I take the decoy."

"Absolutely not—"

"I'm the fastest climber here. I'm the smallest target. I've been hiding from grown men with guns since I was nine years old, Orsen. This is the one job in this entire disaster I'm actually qualified for." Something flickered under the flatness then — not fear, but its cousin. "And nobody shoots at me. Nobody. Because if they do, I'll lead them into the burn timber and let the mountain have them, and I won't feel bad about it, and neither should you."

Teo made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled by cold. Mara closed her eyes briefly, the way a person does when a child says something true and terrible.

"She's right," Mara said. "God help us, she's right."

* * *

We split at the top of the slope with the light going long and coppery, the way it does before weather.

The decoy party was Ines and Teo — Teo because he couldn't manage the canyon's rope pitches on his ruined foot, and because Ines swore she could carry him if it came to it, and because the sight of a limping man on open ground made the decoy more believable, which none of us said aloud. Mara, the Hollis brothers, and I would take the slot. The claim documents stayed with Mara, pressed against her ribs.

Before we parted, Ines stood in front of me and recited the rendezvous: the lower outlet, a fan of pale gravel where the canyon spat out into the valley, moonrise plus two hours. She recited it twice, once forward and once backward, and made me repeat it back, and corrected my pronunciation of a landmark name — *the Split Anvil, not the Split Valley, there's no valley there, there's an anvil-shaped rock split by lightning* — until I had it perfect.

"Ines." I caught her shoulder as she turned away. "If it goes wrong—"

"It won't go wrong." She looked up at me, and for a second the iron in her face cracked and I saw the kid underneath, the one the river had raised alone. "But if it does — don't come looking. Come through the canyon. Get them to the magistrate. Promise me."

"I promise."

"Liar's promise or real one?"

"Real one."

She nodded once, satisfied, and then she and Teo were gone, two small figures angling out across the open slope, deliberately slow, deliberately visible, Teo's limp exaggerated into a stagger. Within four minutes a rifle crack rolled up the valley, and then another, and the two of them broke into a run for the burn timber, and my heart went with them.

* * *

The canyon swallowed us whole.

That's the only way I can describe it. One moment we were on the mountainside with the sky enormous above us; the next we were in a slot barely wider than my shoulders, walls rising sixty, eighty feet on either side, polished stone streaked with the high-water marks of a hundred springs. The light came down in threads. Our breath echoed. Mara moved ahead of me with the documents against her ribs, and behind her the Hollis brothers — silent, competent men who had signed onto a survey expedition and gotten a war — passed packs hand over hand up the first pitch.

Ines had described every obstacle to me before we parted, in order, with distances. First pitch: slab climb, twelve feet, good holds on the left. Second choke: downclimb into a pothole, rope anchored on a flake she promised would be there, and it was, worn smooth by decades of spring floods, exactly where a dead surveyor's memory said it would be. I kept waiting for the map in my head to fail, and it never did. Her father had walked this canyon through her so thoroughly that she'd handed me his eyes.

At the second choke, hanging on the rope above a churning pothole of snowmelt, I heard it.

Voices. Above us. On the rim.

I froze, one boot braced on wet stone, and put my hand up. Below me, Mara went statue-still. The voices were indistinct — wind-shredded, bouncing off the walls — but there were at least two of them, moving along the canyon's edge, and then a third called out from further east, and the whole rim came alive with the particular rhythm of men fanning out on a search pattern.

They'd split, like Mara said. And they'd found the canyon anyway.

A stone clattered down the wall thirty feet ahead of us and dropped into the pothole with a gulp. Someone on the rim had kicked it loose. A voice shouted something down into the dark — I couldn't make out the words, and I thanked God for the echo, which scrambled everything — and then a flashlight beam swung across the slot below us, sweeping the walls, catching spray, sliding past our position by ten feet of darkness.

We hung there. Four people plastered to a wet rock face in the dark, breathing through our teeth. The beam swept back. Closer.

And that's when the mountain spoke.

It started as a sound I knew from a lifetime of chasing storms — the deep, arterial rush of a lot of water moving through a small space. The snowmelt from the high cirque, backed up all day behind some natural dam of ice and debris, letting go all at once. Ines had warned me: *the water comes through that slot like a freight train.* She'd also told me, in her flat inventory way, about the high shelves — three of them, at measured intervals, cut into the walls by some ancient flood, big enough for a man to stand on.

"Shelves!" I hissed down. "Fifteen feet up, left side! Move!"

We climbed like the drowned clawing at a hull. The Hollis brothers hauled Mara up; I shoved them ahead of me and came last, dragging the rope, and we flattened ourselves onto ledges barely wide enough for kneeling as the flood arrived.

It didn't arrive like water. It arrived like a living thing — brown and white and thunderous, filling the slot wall to wall, tearing the rope anchor off the flake and swallowing it, ripping a boulder the size of a truck from the channel floor and rolling it downstream with a grinding roar that I felt in my sternum. Spray hit us like sleet. The flashlight on the rim died — knocked from someone's hand, or dropped in panic — and above us the voices turned from hunters' cadence to something rawer. Shouting. Running. One scream, short, cut off by the water's noise, and I couldn't tell you whether the man it belonged to died or merely believed he was dying, and I found I didn't much care.

Then, as fast as it had come, the flood began to drop, the cirque's sudden gift spending itself down-canyon toward the valley, toward the outlet, toward the rendezvous.

We clung to our shelves in the dark and listened to the mountain finish its sentence.

* * *

An hour later, moving carefully down a canyon floor scoured clean of everything loose, we reached the lower outlet as the moon rose.

The fan of pale gravel was exactly where Ines said it would be. So was the Split Anvil — a lightning-cracked monolith rearing out of the gravel like a broken tooth. We came out of the slot stiff and soaked and shaking, and I counted heads twice, three times, unable to believe we were all still alive.

Then I looked west, across the valley, to the burn timber on the lower slope.

Firelight. A single point of it, tucked deep in the regrowth, blinking — once, twice, three times, pause — in a rhythm I recognized because Ines had drilled it into all of us at the saddle camp. *Three short means safe. Three long means run.*

Three short. Pause. Three short.

"She made it," Mara breathed beside me. "Both of them."

I stood in the gravel with the flood-water draining out of my boots and let myself feel it for exactly three seconds — relief like a drug, hot and dizzying. Then I made myself stop, because the firelight answering from the timber meant Ines and Teo were alive, and it also meant they were lighting a signal in country where men with rifles had just lost their quarry into a flood, and angry men with rifles follow lights.

We crossed the valley at a hard walk, and when we reached the edge of the burn timber, Ines stepped out of the dark to meet us, Teo leaning on her shoulder, both of them gray-faced and exhausted and grinning like lunatics.

"Four of them followed us," she said. "We lost two in the timber. The other two went back toward the rim when the canyon flooded." Her grin faded. She looked past me, up the valley, toward the pass we still had to cross — two more days of it, higher and colder, with the first real snow already smoking off the summits in the moonlight. Then she looked at the far side of the valley, where a thread of woodsmoke was rising from the tree line near the river road. Fresh smoke. A camp that hadn't been there yesterday.

More of them. Ahead of us now, not behind.

"How many?" I asked.

Ines studied the smoke the way she studied water, reading what it remembered.

"Enough," she said.
