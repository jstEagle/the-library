## 13. Ines's Country

The canyon began where the map ended.

Mara had spread the survey sheets on a flat stone that morning, weighting the corners with pebbles, and Orsen had traced their route with a finger and stopped at a place where the contour lines simply gave up — a blank wedge of paper marked only with the surveyor's oldest confession: *terrain unsurveyable.*

"That's three days of nothing," he said. "We can't cross three days of nothing."

"We can," Ines said.

Everyone looked at her. Teo, propped against his pack with his bandaged hand cradled in his lap like something borrowed. Mara with her ink-stained fingers pressed flat against the blank space as if she could feel through it. The two younger survivors — Pell and Anselm, who had stopped asking questions somewhere around the second day of the pass and now simply watched. And Orsen, who watched her differently than the others did, the way a man watches weather.

"The river made this canyon," Ines said. "Before it was a canyon it was a river, and before that it was a river going the other way. Water doesn't forget its own roads. It just moves furniture."

"That's not an answer," Orsen said.

"It's the only kind there is." She folded the corner of the map down over the blank space. "Your paper says nothing because paper only remembers what men measured. I remember what the water measured."

* * *

She took them in at noon, through a mouth in the rock so narrow they went single file with their packs turned sideways, and within an hour the world above them was a blue thread and the world around them was stone the color of old rust and dried blood. The light came down in shafts. Their footsteps echoed wrong — doubled, delayed, as if someone else were walking behind them at a polite distance.

Ines walked first, and she did not walk like someone choosing a path. She walked like someone following one.

There were marks, if you knew to look. A streak of pale mineral on the north wall at shoulder height, laid down by some flood years ago, running straight as a carpenter's line for forty yards before it bent. A shelf of gravel fanned out from a side slot like spilled grain, telling her which way the water had come out of it and how fast. A tumble of boulders sorted by size — big ones dropped first when the flood tired, small ones carried farther — and she read the sorting the way Mara read ledgers.

"Left here," she said, though there was no left; there was a wall and a shadow that might have been a wall.

"There's no left," Pell said behind her.

"There is. It's just full of rock right now." She squeezed through the gap between two slabs leaning together like conspirators, and on the other side the canyon opened into a chamber tall enough to hold the church at Harrowgate twice over, and the left was there, waiting, exactly where she'd said.

Behind her she heard Orsen exhale through his teeth. She didn't turn around. But she heard it, and she kept it, the way she kept everything.

They camped that night in the chamber, under a skylight of broken rock where the stars came down in a column. Teo's hand was worse. Ines could smell it — a sweetish wrongness under the woodsmoke — and she saw that Mara could too, and that neither of them said anything yet, because there was nothing to say until they were out of the canyon and Teo's hand could be dealt with by someone who wasn't a girl with a memory.

Orsen sat beside her at the fire's edge. He did this more often now. He never asked for anything when he did it. He just sat, the way you sit near a stove.

"How far tomorrow?" he asked.

"Far. Then not far at all."

"That's not an answer either."

"It's the truest one I have." She poked the coals. A knot popped and threw sparks up toward the column of stars. "Tomorrow the canyon forks three ways and two of them are lies. They look open. They go down into slots that close over your head. My father called them the river's apologies. It's sorry it trapped you, but it traps you anyway."

"Your father," Orsen said carefully, "was a surveyor."

It wasn't quite a question. She let it hang anyway, because she wanted to see if he'd push, and he didn't, and that told her something too. Finally she said, "He surveyed everything the Vanes' paper says nobody ever surveyed. That blank spot on your map? He filled it. Twice. Once for the mining company that hired him, and once for himself, because he didn't trust anyone else's copy to survive."

"And the company's copy?"

"Burned. Conveniently. Along with the office it lived in, the winter Aldous Vane bought the company's debts." She said it flatly, without heat, because heat was for things that still surprised you, and this hadn't surprised her since she was nine years old. "My father understood what had happened before anyone told him. You don't burn a survey office unless the survey found something. So he hid his own copy, and he taught me the country instead of teaching me letters, because he said paper burns and country doesn't."

Orsen was quiet a long time. The fire worked at its knots.

"He sounds like he planned for the worst," Orsen said finally.

"He planned for the Vanes. Same thing." She looked at him then, straight on, the way she looked at water before crossing it. "You're going to ask me if I hate them. Everyone wants to know that. Like hating them is the interesting part."

"Aren't you?"

"They're weather," Ines said. "I don't hate weather. I read it, and I get everyone I love out from under it." She fed a stick to the fire. "Hate is just memory pointed the wrong direction. I keep mine pointed forward."

* * *

The third day was the fork, and the fork was where the canyon tested them.

She smelled the rain before anyone else did — not rain falling on them, but rain falling somewhere above and behind, miles off, coming down on the high plateau the way autumn rain does, patient and total. The others felt nothing. The air in the canyon was dry and warm and smelled of dust and Teo's bandage. But Ines stood in the middle of the channel with her face tipped back and her eyes closed, listening to a storm she couldn't see, doing arithmetic with it.

Distance: half a morning's flow. Volume: heavy, the kind that comes all at once off bare rock. Drainage: everything above them funneled into this canyon, and the canyon narrowed ahead at a place her father had named the Throat, because he named things plainly and expected the names to be obeyed.

"We move now," she said. "Fast. We make the high shelf past the Throat before the water makes the low road."

"What water?" Anselm asked, squinting at the blue thread of sky. "It's clear as glass."

"It's clear here. Up there it isn't." She was already walking. "Argue while we march."

They marched. She set a pace that was cruel and did not apologize for it, and behind her she heard the group become something different than it had been — not five frightened people following a girl, but a body moving to one pulse. Teo gritted through it, gray-faced, his bad hand strapped tight against his chest. Mara stayed at his elbow without being asked. Orsen drifted to the rear and became the thing he apparently had been before debts made him soft: a man who could keep people moving.

At the fork she didn't hesitate. Left was the lie that looked open. Right was the lie that looked impossible — a wall of tumbled slabs with no visible passage at all. She went right, and put her hand flat on the lowest slab, and felt it: cold, wet-slicked already, hours before any rain could reach them. Seepage. The mountain's early warning.

"In here," she said, and threaded them up through a crack no wider than a doorway, and out onto a shelf thirty feet above the channel floor, wide enough for all of them, walled behind by stone.

An hour later the river arrived.

It didn't arrive like water. It arrived like a train — a brown roaring wall filling the canyon below them bank to bank, carrying whole trees standing upright, boulders grinding along the bottom with a sound like enormous teeth. The chamber where they'd slept the night before vanished. The fork vanished. Everything she had walked them through, every place they had stood and rested and argued, was gone under ten feet of moving mountain.

Pell made a sound that wasn't a word. Anselm gripped the rock with both hands as if the shelf itself might wash away. Even Orsen had gone very still, watching the flood with the expression of a man revising every estimate he'd ever made.

And Ines stood at the edge of the shelf with the spray hitting her face, and she felt it — the grief, the old grief, rising in her like the water rose in the channel.

Because her father had stood on shelves like this one. He had read floods like this one. And then he had gone into Harrowgate one October to argue about a boundary line with men whose smiles were wider than their honesty, and the river had risen that night higher than it had any right to rise, and the town had drowned, and he had drowned with it, and afterward the Vanes' engineers had produced documents showing the floodgates upstream had been opened for "maintenance." Maintenance, in the wettest week of the decade. The magistrate had shrugged. Magistrates shrug at weather.

But Ines had been in the hills that night. Eleven years old, sent up to check a gauge, stubborn about doing it right. She had come down at dawn to a town with its roofs underwater and her father's survey stakes floating past her like candles at a funeral, and she had stood on the ridge and memorized it. All of it. Every street the water took, every house it spared, every minute of its rising — because if she held it perfectly enough, maybe the river would have to give him back.

The river had not given him back. But it had given her this: a mind that held terrain the way other minds held faces. And the river had raised her after that, in the practical sense — she had eaten what the floods delivered, slept in the houses the water abandoned, learned the drowned streets of Harrowgate as streets first and ruins second. The water had taken her father and appointed itself her guardian, and she had never decided whether those were the same act.

"Ines." Orsen's voice, close. Careful. "You okay?"

She realized she was crying, which annoyed her, because crying was memory pointed the wrong direction. She wiped her face with her sleeve and pointed at the flood.

"My father opened that gauge house every spring for nineteen years," she said. "He measured this canyon so many times he could draw it blind. When the Vanes burned his work, they thought they'd erased it." She tapped her own temple, hard, once. "They erased a copy. I'm the original."

Orsen looked at her for a long moment. Whatever he saw made him nod slowly, the way men nod at rivers — acknowledging something older and larger than themselves.

"Mara said the claim your father cached," he said. "The original documents. They're down here somewhere, aren't they. In this canyon."

"Past the Throat," Ines said. "Where the water keeps its promises." She turned back to the flood, roaring and brown and honest, and felt her grief settle into its proper shape — not a weight but a compass needle, trembling, pointing forward. "We wait for it to drop. Tomorrow we walk the channel it just showed us. Floods are rude, but they're thorough. Anything my father hid well enough to survive thirty years will still be there. The water just cleaned the approach for us."

* * *

They waited out the flood on the shelf, and the waiting became its own strange country.

Mara rewrapped Teo's hand with the last of the clean cloth, and her face while she did it was a ledger Ines chose not to read. Pell slept. Anselm told a long pointless story about a mule he'd once owned, and the story was pointless on purpose, the way stories are when people need the sound of a voice that isn't counting dangers. Orsen sat beside Ines again, and this time he spoke first.

"I lied to you," he said. "Back at the church. About the radio."

She had known for eleven days. She had known the way she knew rain coming — not from evidence but from pressure. She said nothing, and let the silence do the asking.

"The Vanes offered to erase my debt if I brought them Mara and the papers. Alone." His voice was low, pitched under the flood's roar, meant for her only. "I told the others there was no offer. I've been walking with that in my pack ever since, next to the food."

Ines watched the water go by. Trees rode it standing up, like witnesses being carried somewhere against their will.

"Why tell me now?" she asked.

"Because you read this canyon like scripture, and you got us onto this shelf an hour before a wall of water came through it, and I figured if anyone would know what happens to a man who keeps lying in country like yours—" he gestured at the flood, at the grinding boulders, at the whole indifferent engine of it— "it'd be you."

"The water takes them," Ines said. "Not fast. Not dramatically. It just stops carrying them. One day the group crosses a river and nobody offers the liar a hand." She turned and looked at him, and let him see that she was not angry, which was worse than anger. "I'm not offering you forgiveness, Orsen. Forgiveness is Mara's to give, and Teo's, and theirs. What I'm telling you is the terrain. There's a path out of what you did. It goes through the truth, and it's narrow, and you'll have to turn your pack sideways to fit through it. But it's there. I checked."

Something moved through his face — relief and shame braided together, the exact expression of a man who has been handed a rope over deep water.

"Tomorrow," he said. "After the cache. I'll tell them all of it, after we have the papers in our hands. I want to come to them holding something besides my apology."

"Then hold it tight," Ines said. "And Orsen — when you tell them, tell them standing up. Liars sit. It matters more than you'd think."

Below them the flood was already beginning to fall, the brown dropping inch by inch off the canyon walls, uncovering the channel floor stone by stone, the way the river had uncovered her whole life: taking everything, then showing her exactly where everything had been.

By dark the roar had softened to a mutter. By midnight the stars were out again in their broken column, and Ines lay with her back against warm rock and rehearsed tomorrow — the Throat, the channel, the side slot behind the fallen slab where her father had pressed a chisel-mark into stone when she was seven years old and told her: *when you're grown, if the world goes wrong, the river knows where I kept the truth.*

The world had gone wrong. The river knew.

And at first light, when they dropped down into the washed-clean channel and rounded the last bend of the Throat, Ines stopped so suddenly that Orsen walked into her back — because there on the canyon wall, fresh-scoured by the flood, gleaming wet in the dawn, was the chisel-mark.

And beneath it, wedged in the crack where no flood could reach, a rusted tin box, waiting, with her father's initials cut into the lid.

Someone else had found the mark too. Boot prints in the fresh mud — two sets, days old, heading deeper into the canyon.
