## 15. The Vane Brothers

The Rivergate Room at the top of the Vane Brothers' depot had been built to make visitors feel small. Aldous Vane had designed it himself: floor-to-ceiling windows facing the water, a long table of black walnut polished to a mirror shine, and a single chair on the far side for whoever had come to ask for money. There was no second chair. Asking was done standing.

Tonight the room held no visitors at all. Aldous sat at the head of the table with a glass of plum brandy he wasn't drinking, and his brother stood at the window with his back turned, watching the river run brown and fast under the last of the autumn light.

"Say it again," Aldous said. "The part about the girl."

Merrick didn't turn. "There's a girl with them. Teenager, maybe younger. The scout we pulled off the mountain — Kessler — says she reads the country like a ledger. Knew the scree would slide before it slid. Took his party through a canyon that isn't on any map and came out the other side. He's still shaking, Aldous. Kessler. The man gutted a bear with a skinning knife two winters back because it was faster than reaching for his rifle."

"And the surveyor's daughter."

"That's the theory. Galloway's father worked the Harrowgate claims before the buyouts. If the girl is his, then she was raised in that drowned town, and she's been living in it ever since. Which means she knows every shaft, every adit, every collapsed tunnel between here and the saddle." Merrick finally turned. He had their mother's face — soft jaw, patient eyes — and it had always unsettled Aldous, the way a beautiful room unsettles you when you find the stain under the rug. "She's not a complication. She's the whole problem. Everything else out there we can buy, outrun, or bury. Her we can't."

Aldous turned his glass a quarter turn on the table. The brandy caught the window light and threw a small trembling coin of it onto the ceiling. He watched the coin instead of his brother.

"Then we stop sending men to do what men are bad at," he said.

* * *

Their father had built the loan business on a single principle, and Aldous had carved it into the lintel of the first office he ever opened: *Everyone is optimistic about something.* The old man had meant it as a kindness, almost. A farmer optimistic about rain. A miner optimistic about the vein. A young man optimistic about his own luck. You didn't prey on the desperate — the desperate had nothing left to lever. You preyed on the hopeful, because hope was collateral that never ran dry, and when the loan came due the hopeful always believed, right up to the last hour, that something would save them.

Aldous had refined the principle. Merrick had perfected it. Between them they held paper on half the Rivergate valley — sawmills, ferry rights, freight contracts, a hotel, two churches' worth of mortgages — and not one signature had been coerced. Every one of their borrowers had come to them smiling. That was the art of it. That was the *optimism*. You never took anything a man didn't hand you, grinning, with both hands.

Orsen Ishikawa had signed four times. Aldous remembered each signature because he remembered everything — it was his gift, the mirror-mind, the same one Merrick had for faces and debts. Storm chaser, twenty-six years old, truck worth a third of what he'd financed against it, eyes like a man watching weather come in from a long way off. He'd talked about supercells the way other men talked about women. He'd signed because he believed the footage would sell, the next season would be bigger, the next storm would make him. Aldous had liked him instantly. That was the trouble with the work. You had to like them. Liking them was how you got the signature.

"Call it," Aldous said now. "The Ishikawa paper. What's it worth?"

Merrick came to the table and sat across from him, which he only did when the conversation had stopped being brotherly and started being business. "Principal, interest, the second note, the collection fees we've stacked on since spring — call it eleven thousand. Against a man with a wrecked truck and no income." He laced his fingers. "Worth nothing. Worth less than nothing. It's worth whatever we decide it's worth, which is the whole point of paper."

"Eleven thousand." Aldous let the number sit in the room. "And the claim documents, if they're real, if they surface, if a magistrate reads them — what's that worth?"

Merrick didn't answer, which was an answer. The Harrowgate mineral claim was the one hole in thirty years of careful work. Their father had bought the town's debts, not its ground, and the ground under Harrowgate — the old deep vein, the one the surveyors had finally gotten around to confirming this summer — was rich enough that whoever held the claim could buy the Vane brothers' entire portfolio and light it on fire for warmth. The expedition had been supposed to lose its paperwork. A river accident. Tragic. The valley was full of them.

Instead four of them were alive, and a storm chaser with eleven thousand dollars of optimism against his name was walking them toward the magistrate's dock at the Rivergate mouth.

"Five days," Merrick said. "Maybe six, if the snows hold. The pass closes and they're stuck on the far side till spring, and by spring we own every judge in the territory. But if they make the mouth—" He stopped. He was good at stopping. It made the unfinished sentence do the work of a threat.

Aldous stood and went to the window. Below, the depot yard was lamplit and busy — crews loading the winter barges, the crane swinging crates of bonded whisky and dry goods upriver to the settlements they owned. Everything in the yard was theirs. Everything on the river, from the mouth to the falls, was theirs in one instrument or another. He had spent his whole life building a machine where nothing moved without his permission, and now a nineteen-year-old girl with a slingshot and a dead father's memory was moving through it like water through a cracked hull.

"Buy him," Aldous said.

Merrick's eyebrows rose a fraction. "The chaser."

"The chaser. Eleven thousand against a claim worth a hundred times that. It's not even arithmetic, Merrick, it's manners." Aldous turned back to the table. "He's not one of them. Look at the pattern. A salvage contract — he took *our* contract to find them. He's a hired man, not a believer. Believers die on mountains. Hired men make deals. We offer to wipe the paper, add a bonus, put him on a barge south before the snow flies. He takes it, delivers the surveyor woman and the documents, and the girl—" He paused. "The girl is mountain-born. Mountain girls don't come down for magistrates."

"And if he doesn't take it?"

"Then we know what he is, and we stop wasting scouts on him."

Merrick was quiet a moment. When he spoke again his voice had gone soft, which in Merrick was the sound of a blade being drawn rather than sheathed. "You want to make him the offer yourself."

"I want him to hear it from the man whose name is on his paper. There's a difference between a message and a voice. A message can be doubted. A voice—" Aldous smiled, and it was the smile from the loan office, the one that had closed four hundred deals. "A voice gets remembered."

* * *

They made the call at nine, from the depot's radio room, on the expedition band they'd been monitoring since the first smoke went up on the north bank. The operator — a narrow, obedient man named Pell — had the frequency keyed and the recorder turning before Aldous finished shrugging off his coat.

"Orsen Ishikawa," Aldous said into the microphone, and heard his own voice come back off the tin walls, warm and unhurried, the voice of a man with all the time in the world. "This is Aldous Vane. I'll keep this short, because I know your battery is worth more than your truck."

Static. Then, faint, wind-raked: "I'm listening."

"Eleven thousand, four hundred and six dollars. That's your paper, Orsen. Principal, interest, and every fee my brother's clerks have been stacking on you since the crash — and I'll be honest, some of those fees were creative. I'm prepared to burn it. All of it. Tonight. You walk into the Rivergate office a free man, and I'll add two thousand on top, cash, and a berth on the southbound barge, because I hear the winters up there are hard on a man with no truck." He let the silence stretch just long enough to be insulting. "All I need is Mara Voss and the claim documents. Just her. Just the paper. The rest of your party walks free — my word on it, and my word, as you know, is the only thing in this valley worth more than my signature."

The static breathed. Somewhere in it, Aldous could hear the wind off the pass, and under the wind, faintly, a second voice — low, quick, a girl's voice, though he couldn't make out the words.

"You're recording this," Ishikawa said at last.

"I am. For my files."

"Then record this too. The answer's no."

Aldous closed his eyes briefly, and it was almost pleasure. "No *today*," he said gently. "Orsen. I've been in the optimism business thirty years. Nobody says no today. They say no today and yes on the mountain when it's cold and the food's gone and the girl with the slingshot can't find them a way down. I'll leave this frequency open. When you're ready — and you will be ready — you key the mic twice and say my name." He paused. "One more thing, and I say this with real affection. The man who taught me this business used to say everyone is optimistic about something. You're optimistic about storms. About being the man who runs at the weather everyone else runs from. It's a beautiful thing to be. It's also, and I mean no cruelty by this, the exact temperament that signs my paper. Think about which one you'd rather be when the snow comes."

He keyed off. Pell stopped the recorder and looked up with the careful blankness of a man who had learned never to have a face in this room.

Merrick was leaning in the doorway. He'd heard all of it. "He said no."

"He said no today."

"And the girl's voice on the channel. You heard it."

"I heard it." Aldous poured two fingers of brandy, finally, and drank. It tasted like the inside of a barrel — like wood and dark and time. "She was telling him something. Coaching him, maybe. Or memorizing."

"Memorizing what?"

"Me." Aldous set the glass down. "My voice, my numbers, my phrasing. Merrick, that girl has been memorizing the river her whole life. Tonight she started memorizing us." He said it lightly, and meant it lightly, and it was only later — much later, on a night of floodwater and lamplight at the riverfront depot — that he would understand he had been describing his own undoing and had heard it only as weather coming in from a long way off.

Merrick pushed off the doorframe. "Then we stop being patient. I'll put every man we have on the pass. Kessler, the Brandt cousins, the two we pulled off the ferry line. If the chaser won't sell them, we take them, and we do it above the snowline where the river can't testify."

"No." Aldous was at the window again. The last light was off the water now, and the river had gone the color of the walnut table, black and polished and moving without pause toward the sea. "We don't take them. Taking makes martyrs, and martyrs make magistrates curious. We *intercept*. There's a difference, brother, and the difference is the whole business." He turned, and for a moment the two of them looked like what they were — not monsters, not in their own accounting, only two men keeping a machine running, the way their father had, the way the river kept running, without malice and without mercy and without ever once stopping to ask what it drowned.

"Send them up," Aldous said. "And Merrick — if it comes to it, if the girl forces it — make sure whatever happens above the snowline looks like weather."

Merrick smiled their mother's soft smile. "Everything does," he said, "if you file the report first."

* * *

In the radio room, Pell logged the transmission, locked the recorder in the cabinet, and doused the lamps. The depot settled into its night sounds — crane cables ticking in the wind, the river working at its pilings, the great patient machine of the Vanes' valley breathing in its sleep.

On the shelf above the recorder cabinet, a coil of spare aerial wire hung where it had hung for six years, and against the stone wall behind it, in the cold and the dark, a hairline crack in the foundation — old, ignored, mapped by no one — ran down through the mortar toward the river, widening a fraction of a millimeter each spring when the ice went out.

The river, which forgets nothing, was already taking notes.
