## 16. Ambush at the Narrows

The canyon narrowed the way a fist closes.

Orsen felt it before he saw it — the walls leaning in, the river compressing to a single green voice, the sky reduced to a seam of gray overhead. Ines had stopped walking twenty minutes ago and hadn't said why, but she'd gone quiet in the particular way she did when the water was telling her something, head tilted, listening to country no one else could hear.

"Talk to me," he murmured, coming up beside her.

"The Narrows." She didn't look at him. Her eyes tracked the cliff walls, left bank, right bank, reading them like pages. "My father's maps call it that. Half a mile where the canyon pinches down and there's nowhere to go but through. The river runs fast and deep on the east side. On the west—" her finger traced an invisible line along the base of the cliffs, "—there's an old channel. Dry for thirty years. It used to carry spring melt when the snowpack was heavy."

"Used to."

"There's a gate upstream." She said it flatly, the way she said everything about the drowned places, as if reciting facts carved into stone. "My father showed me once. A sluice gate, hand-cranked, from the mining days. They used it to flush tailings down the channel. It still turns. I've turned it."

Mara, limping slightly on the far side of Teo, caught the word *gate* and stopped. "You're saying you can flood this place."

"I'm saying I can flood half of it." Ines finally looked at Orsen, and there was something in her face he'd learned to dread — not fear, but arithmetic. She was counting lives against terrain. "The channel would fill first. Anyone standing in it gets swept toward the main current. Anyone on the high ground stays dry. If we're on the high ground and they're not—"

"They won't be standing in a dry channel," Orsen said. "They'll be behind us or ahead of us, and they'll have rifles."

"Then we make sure they're standing in it when it matters."

Teo laughed — a wet, cracked sound, because his frostbitten fingers couldn't curl around his canteen anymore and Mara had been feeding him water like a child. "The girl wants to drown the Vane brothers' army with a crank handle. I've died in worse ways than believing her."

"You haven't died at all yet," Mara said sharply, "and you won't today."

But Orsen had seen the smoke on the scree two nights ago. He'd counted fires. Nine, maybe ten men, moving in a crescent, herding them the way wolves herd elk toward a cutbank. The Vanes weren't chasing anymore. They were funneling. And every path Ines chose, however brilliant, led them deeper into country shaped like a trap.

He said none of this. He'd already lied to these people once — the radio offer, the debt forgiveness, *deliver Mara alone and walk away clean* — and the lie sat in his chest like swallowed ballast. He wasn't going to add doubt on top of it. Not today.

"We move through at first light," he said instead. "Ines takes the high route with the gate. Rest of us stay low, single file, fast. Nobody stops in the channel. Nobody stops anywhere."

Ines nodded once, then looked at him a moment longer than the plan required — that unnerving way she had of seeing straight through skin into intention — and Orsen had to turn away and check the rifle he barely knew how to use.

*When the river lets you go,* she'd asked him, *what do you want to be?*

He still didn't have an answer. But he was running out of river.

* * *

They entered the Narrows at dawn under a sky the color of dirty wool.

The walls rose sheer on both sides, granite streaked with quartz veins that caught the gray light like old scars. The trail ran along the west bank, sometimes wide enough for four abreast, sometimes narrowing to a ledge barely wider than a coffin. Below it, the dry channel waited — a gravel scar thirty feet across, choked with bleached driftwood and the skeletons of long-dead willows. Above, the high ground climbed in broken terraces of talus.

Orsen went first. Behind him came Mara with the documents wrapped in oilcloth against her chest, then Teo, then the other three survivors — Aldana and the brothers Pell, exhausted and silent, moving on stubbornness alone. Ines had peeled off before the entrance, climbing the goat track on the north wall with the ease of someone ascending stairs she'd memorized in the dark. She carried nothing but the slingshot and a length of rope.

For forty minutes, the only sounds were boots on gravel and the river's compressed roar.

Then a rock clattered somewhere above the south rim, and Orsen's blood went cold.

It might have been a bighorn. It might have been gravity. But he'd spent ten years reading weather, and weather had taught him the difference between a landscape breathing and a landscape holding its breath. This was holding its breath.

"Move," he breathed. "Don't run. Move."

They moved. The trail bent, dipped through a stand of dead aspens white as bone, and bent again — and there, filling the gap ahead like a wall built by patient hands, stood six men.

Rifles shouldered. Wax jackets dark with rain. The one in front was broad and gray-bearded, with a face like a shovel, and Orsen knew him by reputation if not name: Hollis, the Vanes' senior enforcer, the man who'd burned the decoy camp, the man whose job description was problems that needed to stop existing.

"Afternoon," Hollis said pleasantly, though it was morning. "Mr. Ishikawa. The Vanes send their regards. They also send an offer, but I suspect you've already heard it."

Behind him — Orsen turned, slowly — four more men dropped onto the trail from the talus they'd been holding since before dawn. Rifles leveled. The channel below, the cliffs above, the men ahead and behind. A fist, fully closed.

Mara's hand went into her jacket. Hollis shook his head, almost gently. "I wouldn't, ma'am. We only need you alive. The rest is negotiable."

"That's the thing about negotiable," Mara said, her voice steady as bedrock, "is it usually isn't."

"Hollis." One of the younger enforcers shifted, nervous, eyes flicking up to the rim. "We're exposed here. Let's just take them and—"

And that was when the world ended.

Not all at once. First there was a sound from high on the north wall — a groan of iron, deep and ancient, like a ship waking. Then a second groan. Then a crash of released water that Orsen felt in his teeth.

The dry channel filled in seconds.

It didn't fill politely. Thirty years of silt went up in a brown wall eight feet high, carrying driftwood and dead willows and half a hillside, slamming down the channel like a freight train derailing sideways. The lower enforcers — the four who'd dropped from the talus, who'd made the tactical error of standing in a dead river because dead rivers stay dead — vanished into it. Orsen heard screaming, saw a rifle spin away on the flood's surface like a toy, saw one man claw onto a driftwood snag and hold on while the water tried to peel him off.

"GO!" Orsen roared, and drove forward at Hollis's group, because chaos was the only weapon they had and hesitation was death. Mara shoved Teo toward the talus slope; the Pell brothers scrambled up the broken ground like goats. Orsen himself closed the distance to Hollis in six strides and hit him — a tackle born of pure desperation, no technique, all weight — and the two of them went down together on the wet granite as the flood thundered past close enough to throw spray across Orsen's back.

Hollis was strong the way old timber is strong. He got a forearm across Orsen's throat and started to squeeze, methodical, unhurried, a man doing a chore. Black spots bloomed at the edges of Orsen's vision. Through them he saw the sky, the gray seam of it, and thought absurdly: *this is what the river sees.*

A stone the size of a plum struck Hollis above the ear with a crack like a rifle shot.

He reeled. Orsen bucked him off, rolled, gasped, and looked up to see Ines on the terrace above, already loading another stone, her face expressionless, her arm drawing back with the calm of a clock mechanism. The second stone took Hollis in the knee. He went down howling.

"Up!" Ines screamed, and her voice was the loudest thing Orsen had ever heard her say. "Everyone up! The channel feeds the low trail — the whole west side goes under in four minutes! MOVE!"

They moved. All of them, up the talus, over the terraces, climbing while the flood spread and swallowed the trail they'd been standing on, the aspens, the coffined ledge — everything below the high line disappeared under churning brown water. Two of Hollis's remaining men made the mistake of retreating downstream along the low route and were simply erased from the day. The third threw down his rifle and climbed, hands raised, and Mara, arriving breathless at the top, looked at him and said, "Sit down and don't be stupid," and he sat.

It had worked. God help him, it had worked. The ambush was scattered, Hollis was down and bleeding from the ear, the survivors were alive and above the waterline, and Ines stood on the rocks with the flood roaring past below her like she'd simply asked the river for a favor and the river had remembered an old debt.

Orsen laughed — a ragged, disbelieving sound — and turned to count heads.

Mara. Aldana. Pell, Pell. The surrendered enforcer. Himself. Ines—

His stomach dropped through the mountain.

"Where's Teo?"

* * *

They found the answer in the worst possible way: by following the drag marks.

Teo had been on the talus with the others. Orsen had seen him climb. But in the chaos — in the flood and the screaming and the stones — two of the enforcers who'd been positioned downstream, below the gate, outside the flood's reach, had come up the south side. Quiet men. Professional men. They'd grabbed the slowest member of the group in the confusion and gone, and the roaring water had eaten their tracks within minutes.

No. Not all their tracks. Ines found what the river missed: a boot print in clay on the south rim, a torn scrap of waxed canvas on a branch, a smear of blood — Teo's, from his bandaged hands — on a granite shelf. The trail ran downstream along the south rim, toward the mouth of the Narrows, where the canyon opened out and the enforcers' boats waited on the wider river.

"He's bait now," Mara said. Her voice was flat, but Orsen could see the fault lines running through it, the way ice cracks before it lets go. "They can't get the documents without me. So they'll trade him. Or they'll use him to set the next trap, and we'll walk into it, because what else can we do? He's one of ours."

The words landed on Orsen like stones.

*One of ours.* Five days ago these people had been cargo — salvage, a fee, a number on a ledger the Vanes held against him. Now they were *ours*, and one of theirs was being carried away downriver, and Orsen stood on the high terrace with the flood still churning below and understood with total clarity that the price of the victory was sitting in his own chest, and always had been.

Because he'd known. Not about the ambush specifically — but he'd known the Vanes played for keeps. He'd known what their optimism curdled into when accounts came due. And when the radio had crackled with Merrick Vane's silken offer — *the girl's documents, delivered alone, and your ledger burns, Orsen, every page* — he had said yes in the silence of his own cowardice and told the survivors nothing. He'd told himself he'd never act on it. He'd told himself the lie was harmless, a door kept open in case the house burned down.

But lies are doors, and doors swing both ways. Every step of this route, every camp, every crossing — he'd chosen them while carrying a secret that, if the wrong moment came, could sell them all. And maybe the enforcers had read the route anyway, maybe this was pure fieldcraft — but Orsen would never know, would he? That was the arithmetic of betrayal. Even unused, it contaminated everything it touched. He would spend the rest of his life wondering which decision had leaked.

Ines appeared beside him at the rim. She didn't look at him. She watched the brown water, and her voice, when it came, was quiet enough that only he could hear it.

"You knew they were capable of this."

"Yes."

"And you still didn't tell us about the offer."

It wasn't a question. It was a verdict. Orsen's throat worked. "Ines—"

"Not now." She finally turned, and her eyes were the color of the flooded channel — deep, carrying things, moving whether you wanted them to or not. "Teo doesn't have time for your confession. Neither do we. But hear this, Orsen Ishikawa: the river keeps everything. Every lie is sediment. It settles, and it waits, and one day the water rises and shows exactly what's on the bottom." She shouldered her slingshot. "Yours is showing."

She walked away toward Mara and the others, already talking — low, fast, reading the south rim, calculating distances and currents, planning the pursuit that had to happen before nightfall put Teo beyond reach forever.

Orsen stood alone on the terrace a moment longer, the flood roaring past below, the guilt roaring louder inside.

Then he checked the rifle, tightened his pack straps, and went after her — because whatever he wanted to be when the river let him go, he knew one thing now with certainty:

It wasn't this. Not yet. But the river wasn't done with him, and neither was the night coming, and somewhere downstream a frostbitten man who fed him bad jokes and worse coffee was waiting to see which kind of man arrived.
