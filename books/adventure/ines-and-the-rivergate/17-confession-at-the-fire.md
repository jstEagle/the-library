## 17. Confession at the Fire

The fire was small on purpose.

Orsen had built it that way — a fist of flame cupped in the hollow beneath the overhang where the canyon bent north, just enough heat to thaw their hands and not enough light to paint them against the rock for anyone watching from the ridgeline. He crouched at its edge with his palms out and watched the others arrange themselves around it the way people do when they've been running too long to sit properly: Teo with his bandaged foot propped on a stone, Mara folding and refolding the oilcloth packet in her lap as if she could press the documents flatter than they already were, the two younger surveyors — Pell and Anselm — shoulder to shoulder like bookends nobody had asked for. Ines sat apart, cross-legged on a shelf of stone above the fire, her slingshot across her knees, her face turned toward the dark mouth of the canyon as if she were listening to water move somewhere under the earth.

She probably was. Orsen had stopped doubting it days ago.

He had been rehearsing this since the saddle. Since the whiteout, since the trestle, since he'd hauled Teo out of the river with his lungs full of it and felt something in his chest crack loose that hadn't cracked when the truck rolled, or when the enforcers' bullets chewed the scree around him. He'd rehearsed it in fragments, walking, one sentence at a time, and every version ended differently and none of them ended well.

There was no version where it ended well. That was the whole point of telling them.

"The radio," he said.

His voice came out wrong — too loud in the small space, too flat. He tried again.

"Back at Harrowgate, before we left. The night the Vanes raised me on the longwave." He kept his eyes on the fire because looking up was impossible yet. "They made me an offer."

Pell stopped chewing. Anselm's head came up slowly, the way a deer's does. Mara's hands went still on the oilcloth.

Ines didn't move at all. But Orsen felt her attention swing onto him like a weight settling, and he understood that she had known — had known since the night it happened, since she'd stood in the church doorway with the receiver's voice still hanging in the cold air — and she had waited this long for him to say it himself. That was worse. That was so much worse than being caught.

"They offered to wipe my debt," he said. "All of it. Every cent I owe them. In exchange for you, Mara. Just you, and the claim papers. Delivered alone. Everyone else they'd let walk."

The fire popped. Somewhere above them, wind moved through the canyon's throat with a sound like a long exhale.

"How long have you known?" Mara asked. Her voice was level. That was the frightening thing about Mara — she never wasted anger on volume. She spent it like a surgeon spends a blade.

"Since that night."

"Eleven days." She said it without checking anything, because Mara counted things the way other people breathed. "You've walked beside us for eleven days knowing they wanted to buy you with my life."

"Yes."

"And you didn't tell us."

"No."

Teo shifted, and his boot scraped stone, and even that small sound made everyone flinch. "Orsen," he said carefully, "the night at the bridge. You went into the water after me. You lost your salvage fee doing it — the money that was supposed to—" He stopped. Started again. "Why would you do that if you were planning to sell us?"

"I don't know," Orsen said, and it was the truest thing he'd said in eleven days. "I've been asking myself that since the water. I don't have an answer that holds up. I can tell you what I told myself — that I was going to refuse them, that I just needed time, that saying no out loud would get us all killed faster. And some of that was even true. But underneath it there was a part of me that kept the offer in my pocket like a door I might need later. A way out. I'm a man who's always kept a door open, and keeping doors open is how you end up betraying everybody at once instead of one at a time."

Mara unfolded the oilcloth. The claim documents caught the firelight — old paper, Ines's father's careful surveyor's hand, the mineral rights that would bankrupt the Vane brothers twice over. She looked at them for a long moment.

"Do you know what they did to us?" she said quietly. "Not the ambush. Not the shooting. Before that. They hired us. Aldous Vane shook my hand at the depot and told me the Rivergate country was empty and the work would be easy money. He smiled while he said it. They sent six of us upriver knowing what we'd find, and when we found it, they sent men with rifles. Joss 's dead. Rennick's dead. We buried them in the flooded town because the ground was the only place the river couldn't follow." Her eyes came up. "And you knew these men owned you. You knew what they were. You took their job anyway."

"I owed them everything," Orsen said. "My truck. My gear. Two years of chasing weather on credit because optimism is the only thing the Vanes lend cheaper than money. When they called the loan, I thought finding your expedition was the only rope left. I didn't know what I'd find. I swear that. But I'd be lying if I said I'd have turned the boat around if I had."

"At least you're honest about the lying," Pell muttered.

"Pell." Anselm put a hand on his arm.

"No, let him talk," Pell said. "Let him tell us how sorry he is while we're still eating his rations."

"I am sorry," Orsen said. "But sorry isn't worth much from a man who hid an offer for eleven days, so I won't ask you to weigh it. I'll tell you what I'm going to do instead, and you can decide whether to believe me before I finish talking or after."

He finally looked up. Mara's face was stone. Teo's was tired in a way that had nothing to do with the march. Pell's jaw was set. Anselm watched him with the flat patience of a man who'd learned to read instruments and people the same way — by waiting for the needle to settle.

"Ines," Orsen said. "You memorized the exchange. All of it?"

She looked down at him from her stone shelf. In the firelight her eyes were the color of the river in November — brown gone gray, patient, carrying everything.

"Word for word," she said.

"Then say it back. All of it. So they hear exactly what was offered and exactly what I answered — and what I didn't answer."

Ines held his gaze for a moment longer. Then she recited.

Her voice was quiet and absolutely precise, the same voice she used to navigate whiteouts and drowned canyons, and hearing his own conversation come back to him in it was like reading his own obituary written by someone who'd taken shorthand at the scene. *Ishikawa. We understand you've found our missing surveyors. Merrick and I are prepared to be generous.* — *Generous how?* — *Your debt. Every figure on our ledger, struck through. One signature. You bring us the woman Mara Voss and the claim documents she's carrying. The rest of them can go wherever the river takes them. We're not monsters, Orsen. We're businessmen.* — And then the pause. The long static-filled pause that Orsen had filled, eleven days ago, with nothing. With silence. With a door held open.

*Think about it,* Aldous Vane had said. *Optimism, Ishikawa. It's what we sell.*

And Orsen had said: *I'll think about it.*

"You said you'd think about it," Ines finished. "That was the last thing either of you said. Then the channel closed." She folded her hands in her lap. "That's all of it."

"That's all of it," Orsen agreed. His throat was raw. "I never said yes. I also never said no. And a man who doesn't say no has already said yes, he just hasn't picked the day. I know that now. I should have known it then."

Silence. The fire burned down a little, and nobody fed it.

It was Teo who spoke first, which surprised Orsen; he'd expected Mara, or the long cold nothing of Pell's contempt. Teo leaned forward, wincing as his bandaged foot shifted, and studied Orsen across the flames with the expression of a man examining a knot to see whether it would hold weight.

"When they took me at the narrows," Teo said, "before Ines flooded the channel — one of the enforcers leaned down and told me the deal was still good. Told me, *your guide knows the terms.* I thought he meant Ines." He paused. "He meant you. Didn't he."

"He meant me."

"So they're still expecting delivery."

"They're expecting *something*," Orsen said. "Which gives us one card we wouldn't otherwise have. Which brings me to what I'm going to do."

He laid it out plainly, because plainness was all he had left: the depot at the Rivergate's mouth, the magistrate's launch that Mara had arranged by signal mirror three days ago, the window between the Vanes learning their cargo had arrived and the law arriving behind it. He would walk in alone. He would carry the documents — copies, Mara's hand copying Ines's father's originals through two cold evenings at the saddle camp, though he didn't say that part yet, not in front of anyone who might repeat it within earshot of the dark. He would offer himself and the claim in trade, draw Aldous and Merrick out from behind their guards, hold their eyes and their attention for the minutes it took.

"It's a con," he said. "It's the only kind of thing I'm any good at anymore — standing in front of dangerous men and making them believe a story. And I want to be clear about why I'm doing it, because I know how it sounds. It sounds like a man buying back his debt with your evidence. Maybe part of it is that. I've spent eleven days learning that the parts of myself I liked best were the parts I lied about. But here's the truth I'm choosing, tonight, out loud, where the river can hear it: I'm done keeping doors open. Either I walk out of that depot having finished this, or I don't walk out. There's no version where I hand Mara over. There's no signature. If you never trust me again, fine — trust the plan, not the man. The plan works without trusting me at all."

Nobody spoke for a long time.

Then Mara began, very deliberately, to fold the oilcloth closed again.

"Mara," Orsen started.

"Don't." She tied the cloth with short, hard motions. "Don't use my name like we're friends deciding something together. Here is where I stand, Ishikawa, and I want you to hear every word. I forgive you for nothing. Not yet. Maybe not ever — that's mine to decide, and grief doesn't keep a schedule. But I will work with you, because you're useful and because the plan is sound and because Joss and Rennick deserve better than my pride getting us killed in a canyon." She stood, stiff with cold and fury and something underneath both that might, in another season, in another life, have been the beginning of respect. "Conditional. That's what you get from me. Conditional forgiveness, reviewed daily, revocable without notice. Prove me wrong and maybe the condition lifts. Give me one reason and it closes forever."

"I can live with conditional," Orsen said.

"You'll have to." She moved away toward the far wall of the overhang, wrapping herself in her bedroll with her back to the fire, and the conversation, as far as Mara Voss was concerned, was over.

Pell spat into the flames. Anselm simply nodded once, the nod of a man filing information, and followed Mara. Teo lingered, met Orsen's eyes, and said nothing at all — but he squeezed Orsen's shoulder as he passed, briefly, heavily, the way you steady something you're not sure will stay upright.

And then it was just the fire, and the canyon wind, and Ines.

She slid down from her stone shelf and sat beside him, close enough that their shoulders almost touched, and for a while neither of them said anything. Below them, far down the canyon, the river ran black and constant, carrying its cold toward the lowlands, carrying everything it had ever swallowed somewhere no one could follow.

"You knew," Orsen said finally. "That night at the church. You knew what they'd offered, and you said nothing for eleven days."

"I wanted to see what you'd do with it."

"That's a hell of a thing to do to a man."

"My father used to say the river does the same thing." She poked the fire with a stick, sending up a scatter of sparks. "It gives you a current and watches. Most people fight it wrong and drown. Some people fight it right and drown anyway. And once in a very long while, somebody stops fighting and starts reading the water, and then they live." She glanced at him. "I wanted to know which one you were. I wasn't sure until the bridge."

"The bridge nearly killed me."

"Yes," Ines said. "That's usually how it goes."

Orsen laughed — a short, broken sound, surprised out of him — and then pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes, because the shame was still there, sitting in his chest like swallowed stone, and the laughter hadn't dislodged it, and he didn't suppose anything would for a long time. "Mara forgives nothing. Teo squeezes shoulders. Pell wants me dead. And you—"

"I forgave you the night you lied," Ines said simply.

He lowered his hands and stared at her.

"Not because the lie was all right. It wasn't. Lies are how the Vanes got their country, and lies are how my father lost his, and I hate them more than I hate almost anything." She fed the stick to the flames. "But I watched you carry that lie up a mountain in a snowstorm, and I watched it get heavier every mile, and I watched you set it down tonight in front of the people it could hurt most, when you could have carried it two more days and traded it at the depot and been free. Free, Orsen. Debtless. A new truck. Warm rooms in the city." She shrugged, small and matter-of-fact, a girl who had grown up measuring the world in flood lines and high-water marks. "People who choose the heavy thing at the last possible minute are still choosing it. The river doesn't care when you turn for shore. Only that you turn."

"That's generous."

"That's hydrology." Almost a smile. Then it faded, and she looked at him with an intensity that made the fire feel cold. "Tomorrow you walk into that depot alone. So listen to me, because this is the part I memorized the river for. The Vanes' depot sits on the old ferry landing, and the old ferry landing sits on ground my father surveyed before they ruined him. There's a sluice gate under the east foundation — 1908 construction, iron, seized with rust but not rusted through. I've been listening to the water under it since we came down off the pass. The channel behind it is full. If I open that gate, the depot's back lot floods to the knee in four minutes and to the waist in nine. Nobody runs fast in waist-deep water. Nobody drives out of a flooded yard."

Orsen stared at her. "You've had this planned since the saddle."

"I've had this planned since I was seven years old," Ines said. "You're just the first person with a reason to use it." She stood, brushing ash from her knees, and looked down at him with the river's own patience. "Sleep, Orsen. Tomorrow you sell us to the men who bought you, and tomorrow evening the water settles the account."

Above the canyon rim, past the black teeth of the ridge, the sky had begun to bruise with the season's first real snowlight — and far downstream, at the mouth of the Rivergate, the lamps of the Vane brothers' depot were burning late, as if the men inside them already knew their cargo was coming home.
