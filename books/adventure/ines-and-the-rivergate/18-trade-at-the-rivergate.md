## 18. Trade at the Rivergate

The Rivergate smelled of diesel and cold water, and Orsen stood on the boardwalk across from the Vane brothers' depot for a long time before he let himself move.

He had dressed for it. That was the joke of it — he'd spent twenty minutes in the ruined hotel above the landing, buttoning a shirt that didn't fit him anymore because he'd lost weight on the pass, tucking it into trousers stiff with river mud at the hems. A man walking into a negotiation should look like he had something to lose. He'd learned that much from the Vanes themselves: Aldous with his pressed collars even in the backcountry, Merrick with his smile like a handshake you couldn't get out of.

The documents were against his chest, wrapped in oilcloth inside his coat. Copies — Mara's copies, made by lantern light in the church while Ines watched the door. The originals were already gone, moving downriver in the hands of a fisherman who owed Teo a favor and asked no questions, bound for the magistrate's station at Graywater. If everything went right, the launch would arrive tomorrow morning.

If everything went right.

Orsen crossed the street.

* * *

The depot had been a cannery once, back when Harrowgate was a town instead of a memory. The Vanes had bought it cheap after the flood and turned it into something between an office and a fortress: new timber over old bones, barred windows, a loading dock where two flatboats sat low in the water with their engines covered. Enforcers on the dock. Two more by the doors. One on the roof with a rifle slung casual as a fishing rod.

They knew he was coming. He'd signaled from the bend three miles up, one flash of mirror, the agreed signal, and now they watched him walk down the boardwalk with their hands loose near their belts and their faces arranged into nothing at all.

"Mr. Ishikawa." The door guard was a big man named Corliss whom Orsen remembered from collections — the polite one, the one who'd sat in Orsen's kitchen drinking coffee while explaining what happened to men who missed winter payments. "They're expecting you."

"I'd be worried if they weren't."

Corliss almost smiled. He patted Orsen down with brisk professional hands, found the oilcloth packet, weighed it, and handed it back. "They said to leave that with you. They want to see it opened."

"Of course they do."

Inside, the depot's main floor had been cleared to bare boards except for a long table at the far end, lit by a hanging lamp. Maps on the wall behind it — the whole upper Rivergate, inked with survey lines and, here and there, small red X marks that Orsen tried not to look at too carefully. He knew what some of those X marks meant now. He'd seen what was under them.

Aldous Vane sat behind the table. Merrick stood at the window with his hands clasped behind his back, watching the river come down gray and swollen toward the sea.

"Orsen." Aldous spread his arms like a man greeting family at a wedding. "You look terrible. Sit down. There's coffee — real coffee, not that pine-bark tea you've been living on up there, I'd wager."

"I'll stand."

"Suit yourself." Aldous poured anyway, two cups, steam rising. "Merrick, look at him. Five weeks ago this man was begging us for another month. Now look. Shoulders back. Eyes clear. You can always tell when a man's found religion."

"You can always tell," Merrick agreed without turning around, "when a man thinks he's found leverage."

Orsen kept his breathing slow. This was the part he'd rehearsed on the trail, lying awake while the others slept, running the conversation like a film he could pause and correct. Don't lead with the documents. Let them ask. Let them think it's their idea.

"I brought what you asked for," he said. "Mara Voss. The claim papers. Both of them are within my reach — I'm the only one they trust enough to hand them over."

"Mm." Aldous sipped his coffee. "And the girl?"

"The girl isn't part of the trade."

"The girl," Merrick said mildly, still facing the window, "is the entire problem, Orsen. Papers burn. People forget. But that child remembers things she has no business remembering. Do you know what she recited to our people through a mine shaft wall? Elevations. Dates. The name of the man who signed off on the Harrowgate levee assessment in '09 — which was our father, God rest him, may he rot. She's not a witness, she's an archive. Archives don't get to walk away."

"She's fourteen."

"She's evidence." Merrick turned then, and his smile was exactly as Orsen remembered it, warm and bottomless and wrong. "But we're getting ahead of ourselves. Show me the copies."

Orsen took out the oilcloth packet and set it on the table without opening it. His heart was going hard but his hands were steady, and he was grateful for that, grateful in a distant way, the way a man is grateful for anything at all when he's standing on ice and listening to it talk.

"Copies," he said. "Made from the originals. You'll want your man to verify them — the seal impressions match, the signatures match, and page nine has the assay figures your own surveyor filed before your people burned his camp. Page nine is the one that hangs you, Aldous. I'd read it twice."

Aldous didn't reach for the packet. That was the first thing that went wrong. He just looked at it, then at Orsen, with an expression of genuine, almost paternal disappointment.

"Orsen. Sit down."

"I said I'll—"

"Sit down." No warmth in it now. The lamp hissed. On the roof overhead, boots shifted across planking. Orsen sat.

Aldous leaned forward, laced his fingers, and studied him the way he'd once studied loan applications — looking for the flaw, the soft place, the thing to press.

"Here's my difficulty," Aldous said. "You came down off that mountain five days ago. Our riders picked up your trail at the scree — you split your group, clever boy, but you left sign like a wounded elk. We know where the survivors are holed up. We know about the fisherman who went downriver Tuesday with a dry sack he wouldn't show the toll agent at Bend Ferry. And we know—" he tapped the table once, gently, "—that the magistrate's launch at Graywater doesn't sail until tomorrow morning, weather permitting, and the weather is turning."

The room got very quiet. Somewhere out on the water, a loon called, long and descending, like something being lowered into the dark.

"So you understand my difficulty," Aldous continued softly. "Either you're betraying them, in which case these copies are worthless theater and you've wasted both our evenings. Or you're betraying us — you walked in here wearing a dead man's shirt to buy time while your little courier floats toward Graywater. And if it's the second one, Orsen, then I have to ask myself what a man like that is actually doing in my depot."

* * *

Later — much later, in the years afterward, when the story got told and retold around Harrowgate stoves until it wore smooth as river stone — Orsen would say the lie came to him the way the storm had once come over the ridge at Cattleman's Fork: all at once, and with a sound like the sky opening.

He laughed.

It wasn't performed. That was the terrible gift of it — the fear underneath was so total, so honest, that what came out of him sounded exactly like a man who had nothing left to hide. He laughed until Aldous's eyes narrowed, until Merrick pushed off from the window, and then he wiped his eyes and shook his head.

"You want to know what I'm doing here? Fine. I'll tell you what I'm doing here." He leaned forward, elbows on his knees, and dropped his voice like a conspirator. "I'm doing what you taught me. I'm cutting my losses."

Merrick drifted closer. "Go on."

"The originals aren't with the fisherman. The fisherman's carrying paper I wrote myself — assay numbers off a whiskey label, signatures I practiced for two days. It buys the magistrate a week of confusion, maybe two, and by then the snow's closed the pass and nobody can prove anything anyway." He shrugged. "The real claim file is in a cache up the canyon, and the only person alive who knows where is the girl. Which means the only way any of this ever gets out of these mountains is through her. Through me. So yes — I'm here to trade. But I'm not stupid enough to trade for debt forgiveness alone. Not anymore. I want terms."

Aldous sat back slowly. Beside him, Merrick had gone very still, the stillness of a man recalculating, and Orsen watched the brothers exchange a glance that lasted half a second and contained an entire argument.

"Terms," Aldous repeated.

"Fifty percent of whatever the claim's worth, once you've got it quieted. Registered interest, on paper, in my name. And the girl delivered to me — alive, unmarked — because she's my insurance. She's the only one who can find that cache again if either of you decides I'm worth less than the paperwork says." He let that sit. "You wanted to know if I was betraying them or betraying you. The answer's yes. To both. That's the only kind of deal that was ever going to hold, and you know it, because it's the only kind of deal you two have ever honored yourselves."

Silence. The lamp flame bent in a draft from under the door. Out on the loading dock, one of the enforcers coughed.

Then Merrick started to laugh too — low, delighted, shaking his head — and Orsen felt the floor of the world tilt back toward level.

"Oh, Aldous. Oh, you beautiful, ruined son of a gun." Merrick pulled out a chair and sat, close, knee nearly touching Orsen's. "Do you see it? Do you see what five weeks in the woods does? He comes down here smelling like woodsmoke and lies, and he's finally speaking our language." He clapped Orsen on the shoulder, and his hand stayed there, heavy. "Fifty percent is insane. Ten, plus the girl, plus your debt burns tonight."

"Twenty-five, and the debt burns when the cache is in your hands, not before."

"Fifteen, debt burns tonight, and you ride with us to the canyon tomorrow."

"Twenty," Orsen said. "And I ride with you. Because if your boys go up that canyon without me, the girl will put every one of them in the river, and you know it."

Another silence, shorter this time. Aldous reached across the table, turned the oilcloth packet over once with one finger, and slid it back toward Orsen.

"Keep your copies," he said. "We'll compare them against the real thing when we have it. Twenty percent. Debt closed on delivery." He extended his hand. "Welcome back to civilization, Orsen."

Orsen took it. The grip was dry and strong and warm, and holding it felt like holding a snake just behind the head — safe only for exactly as long as you never relaxed.

* * *

They gave him a room in the loft above the office, with a barred window and a plate of food he ate mechanically, tasting nothing. A guard outside the door. A guard on the stairs. Through the floorboards he could hear the brothers below, voices rising and falling, and once the sharp crack of Aldous's palm on the tabletop.

He lay on the bunk in the dark and did the arithmetic of his own treachery, because that was the price of the con — you had to carry it honestly or it showed. Tomorrow morning he would lead the Vanes' boats upriver toward the canyon. Ines would be waiting, because Ines was always waiting, because Ines had memorized the plan in the firelight two nights ago and repeated it back to him word for word, including the parts he hadn't said aloud, including — he suspected — the parts he'd hidden from himself.

The magistrate's launch would reach the mouth of the Rivergate by midmorning. Everything depended on the timing. Everything depended on the brothers leaving their guards at the depot and coming up the canyon themselves, greedy, certain, following the man they thought they'd bought.

And everything — the last piece, the piece he hadn't told even Mara — depended on the old floodgates above the Narrows, rusted shut for thirty years, that a girl who remembered everything the river had ever done had promised him she could open.

He heard the office door open below. Footsteps on the stairs — two sets, unhurried. A key turned in his lock, and lamplight spilled across the floor, and Aldous Vane stood in the doorway with his coat already on and his hat in his hand, smiling like a man arriving early to his own wedding.

"Change of plan, partner," Aldous said pleasantly. "Weather's turning faster than we thought. We're going up the canyon tonight — all of us, right now, while the river's high and your friends are sleeping." Behind him, Merrick checked the cylinder of a revolver with small, contented clicks. "I do hope the girl's expecting us."

Downriver, far off in the dark, thunder rolled — though the sky above the Rivergate was perfectly, terribly clear.

Orsen sat up slowly, his blood gone cold and bright, and understood that the con had just become a race.
