## 19. The Water Rises

The river had taught Ines its first lesson before she could walk: water is patient, and patience is a weapon.

She crouched now in the dark above the Vane brothers' riverfront depot, her palms flat against the cold iron of the old floodgate wheel, and thought about how long the river had been waiting for someone to remember what it knew.

Harrowgate had flooded on purpose once a year, in her father's time. The miners built the gates to drown their own streets on schedule — spring melt, controlled release, the town bending to the water like a reed instead of breaking like a post. Her father had walked her through the gatehouse when she was six, his big hand over hers on the wheel, explaining the counterweights, the sluice channels, the way the whole valley below the dam could be turned into a shallow sea if a person understood which levers to pull and in what order.

*You won't need this, little fish,* he'd said. *But you'll remember it.*

He'd been right about the second part. He'd been wrong about the first.

Below her, the depot blazed with lamplight. The Vanes' men moved along the dock like ants that had found something sweet and couldn't agree on how to carry it. Orsen was down there — she could pick out his silhouette even at this distance, even through the rain, because she had spent weeks learning the shape of him, the way he carried his weight on his heels like a man always ready to run, the way he stood too straight when he was afraid. He'd walked in alone, just as he'd promised, carrying the satchel with the claim documents, offering himself and the papers to Aldous and Merrick Vane as if they were merchants and he had come to strike an honest bargain.

It was a lie, and it was a good one, and Ines had helped him build it.

Mara was upriver with the real documents — or copies so exact that Ines herself, who remembered every line of them, could not have said which set was true. Mara and the magistrate's launch. The launch was coming down from Fort Callum, summoned by a letter Mara had written in a hand steady enough that no one would guess it had been written by firelight in a half-drowned church by a woman who hadn't slept properly in two months. The letter named names. The letter cited dates. The letter included the survey numbers of a mineral claim that would have made the Vane brothers rich beyond drowning and had instead gotten four people hunted through the high country like animals.

The law was slow. The river was not.

Ines pressed her cheek against the wheel and listened. Under the rain, under the groan of the depot's timbers, she could hear it — the deep, patient pressure of the reservoir behind the old dam, backed up all season behind a gate everyone in the valley had forgotten because everyone who remembered it was dead or scattered or a girl with a slingshot living in a drowned town.

*Everyone who remembered it,* she corrected herself. Almost everyone. There was one other.

Teo.

They were holding him in the depot. She'd seen him at the window an hour ago, when she'd come down through the willows to scout — his face pale in the lamplight, one hand bandaged where the frostbite had taken the tips of two fingers, guarded by a man with a rifle who looked bored. Teo had seen her too. He hadn't shouted. He'd looked at her for three seconds — she counted them, because counting was what she did instead of panicking — and then he'd turned away, deliberately, and given the guard nothing.

Three seconds was enough. It meant: *I know you're here. I know there's a plan. Do it.*

It also meant: *whatever you do, don't do it for me.*

Ines closed her eyes and let the map unroll behind them, the way it always did — the valley rendered in water, every channel and culvert and forgotten ditch laid out like veins under skin. The old flood system didn't feed into the depot directly. That would have been simple, and rivers were never simple. But the gatehouse controlled the millrace, and the millrace ran beneath the eastern quarter of the depot foundations — the builders a hundred years ago had anchored their warehouse to bedrock, but bedrock sat on gravel, and gravel sat on the old race channel, and the race channel, if the gates opened full, would become a river again for the first time in forty years.

Open the gates halfway, and the millrace would fill and undermine the dock pilings. Slow. Hours. Too slow.

Open them all the way, and the water would come down the race like a freight train, burst through the old culvert under the east wall, and turn the depot's lower floor into a pond in less than a minute. Every man inside would be scrambling for high ground. The boats at the dock would be torn loose or swamped. The escape routes — the road south along the levee, the stair down to the lower landing, the back door through the coal shed — all of them sat below the flood line. All of them would drown.

So would Teo, if no one got to him.

*Don't do it for me.*

She heard Orsen's voice in her memory, from the night at the fire after his confession, when Mara's forgiveness had come with conditions attached and Ines's had not. *Why not?* he'd asked her. *I lied to you. I lied to all of you.*

*Because lying is just forgetting on purpose,* she'd told him. *And you stopped forgetting. That's the only thing anyone can actually do.*

Now she put both hands on the wheel and did the thing her father had taught her to do without ever meaning to.

* * *

The wheel fought her.

Forty years of rust and silt had welded the mechanism nearly solid, and the first quarter-turn took everything she had — shoulders, back, the whole architecture of her small body thrown against the iron until her vision sparked. Rain ran down her face and dripped off her chin onto the wheel housing. Somewhere below, a voice called out on the dock, and another answered, and neither of them was looking up at the gatehouse on the bluff because no one alive believed the gatehouse mattered anymore.

That was the thing about memory, Ines thought, wrenching the wheel through the second quarter-turn. People forgot places the moment the water took them. They wrote the drowned things off as gone, erased, finished. But the river never finished anything. It just kept everything, held it all in suspension, waiting for the current to change.

The third quarter-turn broke something inside the mechanism with a crack like a rifle shot, and then the wheel spun free, and Ines threw her whole weight backward to keep it from running away with her.

The gates opened.

She felt it before she heard it — a shudder through the bedrock, through the soles of her boots, through the bones of her hands still locked on the wheel. Then the sound arrived: a low, rising roar from the throat of the mountain, the reservoir finding its old throat after four decades of silence. Water slammed into the millrace below the gatehouse and went down the mountain toward the depot faster than any horse, faster than any man, carrying forty years of accumulated patience in a single green-black fist.

Ines let go of the wheel and ran.

She knew the path down the bluff in the dark the way other people knew their own names — left at the split cedar, down the scree chute, across the shelf of rock above the willows, drop to the game trail, follow the smell of the water. She ran it in the rain with the roar growing behind her and below her, filling the whole valley, and she thought, with a clarity that felt almost like joy: *this is what I am for.* Not the slingshot. Not the hiding. This — the knowing, the remembering, the valley's own mind walking around in a girl's body, finally pointed at something that deserved it.

She came out of the willows at the north end of the dock, soaked to the skin, and the world was ending exactly on schedule.

The millrace water hit the old culvert under the depot's east wall and blew it apart. The wall didn't collapse so much as exhale — timber and stone shoved outward, and then the water was through, waist-deep in seconds, rolling barrels and crates and one entire coal shed door across the depot yard like toys. Lamplight caught the flood and turned it gold and terrible. Men were screaming. A rifle went off twice, wildly, at nothing. The dock pilings groaned as the surge hit them broadside, and the whole structure leaned, and the boat tied at the lower landing tore loose and spun away downstream with no one aboard.

Through it all, Ines watched the doors.

The front doors of the depot burst open and men came out of them like the building itself was vomiting — six, seven, eight of them, stumbling into water that was already past their knees, hauling each other toward the levee road, which was, as she had known it would be, turning into a canal. The Vane brothers' careful, optimistic empire, built on paper and other people's hope, dissolved in ninety seconds of honest water.

But no Teo.

She counted the men coming out again. Seven. The guard from the window was among them, rifle abandoned, clawing his way up the levee. No second figure. No lurching, frostbitten gait.

Her stomach dropped through the flood and kept going.

*The upper floor.* Of course. The guard wouldn't have kept him below, not with the documents and the brothers all in the same building — they'd have stowed him upstairs, in the office, out of sight. And the stairs to the upper floor were on the east side.

The east side was where the river had come in.

Ines went into the water.

It grabbed her immediately — cold enough to stop breath, strong enough to make a lie of every plan she'd ever made. She'd known the flood line, had calculated it to the inch from memory, but knowing a flood and standing in one were different countries. The current took her feet out from under her in three steps. She went down, struck something — a crate, a door, a man's dropped coat — and came up with her lungs burning, and swam, because the river had raised her and swimming was the first language she'd ever spoken.

The east wall was a black gap vomiting white water. She angled for it anyway, caught the frame of the broken culvert, and hauled herself hand over hand into the depot's roaring interior, where the water was chest-high and full of debris and the ceiling beams overhead were dripping like a cave.

"TEO!" Her voice vanished into the roar. She screamed it again, louder, and this time — faintly, from above and to her left — an answer. A thump. A boot heel against floorboards.

The staircase was ahead, half-submerged, the newel post sticking up out of the flood like a drowning man's arm. She climbed it on hands and knees as the water chased her up the steps, and at the top she found him: Teo, wrists bound to a radiator pipe with wire, sitting in an inch of spreading water, grinning at her with bloodless lips.

"Took you long enough," he said. His teeth were chattering so hard the words barely survived. "I was starting to think you'd forgotten the building."

"I forget nothing," Ines said, and pulled the slingshot from inside her coat, and used the steel bearing to hammer the wire apart link by link while the depot groaned around them like a ship going down.

* * *

They came out through the roof hatch, because the stairs were gone and the windows were underwater, and crossed the depot's flat roof through the rain to the ladder on the north side, above the willows, above the flood line. Ines went first and Teo followed, moving on her exact words — *left foot, the rung with the white paint, don't look down, the water will catch anything that falls wrong but it won't catch it gently* — and when they reached the ground she allowed herself, for exactly one breath, to lean against the willow trunk and shake.

Then she heard the launch.

It came around the bend of the Rivergate with its lamp burning and its magistrate's pennant streaming wet in the rain, and it came steadily, unhurried, into a scene the flood had prepared for it. The depot was a lit ruin, its lower story a brown lake, its guards scattered and sodden on the levee with their rifles rusting in the water. And tied at the main dock, untouched by the flood — because Ines had calculated that too, had chosen the gate timing so the surge would spend itself on the east wall before reaching the main pier — rode the Vane brothers' private steamboat, its brass fittings gleaming, its cabin windows glowing.

Aldous and Merrick Vane stood on its deck.

She saw them clearly as the launch swept closer — two tall figures in good coats, motionless amid the ruin of everything they'd built, watching the magistrate's launch come on with the expressions of men watching weather they had convinced themselves would never arrive. Merrick's hand was on the rail. Aldous's hand was on his brother's shoulder, and even at this distance Ines could read the gesture: not comfort. Restraint.

On the launch's foredeck, wrapped in an oilskin, stood Mara.

Ines's heart did something complicated. Mara had the documents — the true ones, the ones her father had cached in the canyon and Ines had led them to through country no one else could read. Mara had the survey numbers, the dates, the letters between the Vanes and the men they'd hired to make a survey expedition disappear. Everything the law was too slow to find on its own was crossing the water right now, in a canvas satchel, toward a magistrate whose name Mara had memorized from a two-year-old newspaper because Mara forgot nothing either — she just kept it differently, in ink instead of water.

The launches closed the distance. Ropes went across. Voices carried over the rain — the magistrate's, hard and official; Mara's, level and relentless; and then, cutting through both, Aldous Vane's voice, still smooth, still optimistic, still trying to sell the flood itself as an act of God and bad luck.

Ines stood in the willows with Teo's arm over her shoulder and listened to optimism die.

It didn't die quickly. That was the frightening thing. Even cornered, even drowning in the evidence of what they'd done, the brothers talked — offers, alternatives, a settlement, a partnership, the word *misunderstanding* deployed like a life raft. The magistrate's answers got shorter. Somewhere in the middle of it, a whistle blew, and men from the launch came up the dock, and the Vane brothers' steamboat was claimed the way a wolf claims a kill.

And through all of it, Ines kept looking for one silhouette.

She found him at last on the dock's far end, apart from everyone, drenched, one hand pressed flat against a piling as if checking whether the wood still remembered how to stand. Orsen. He wasn't watching the arrest. He was watching the water — the flood he'd known she would bring, the river he'd spent his whole life chasing in its wilder moods, now standing calm and satisfied over the ruins of the men who'd owned him.

He turned, as if her attention had weight, and looked straight at the willows. Straight at her.

Even across the dark water, even through the rain, she saw him mouth it. Two words.

*Thank you.*

Ines put her hand on Teo's shoulder and turned them both toward the high ground, toward the pass, toward Harrowgate and the church with its broken roof and the household waiting there — and behind her, on the dock, she heard the magistrate say the words she had waited her whole life to hear someone say about the Vanes:

*"Take them up."*

The river rose a final inch against the pilings, quiet now, its work done, keeping everything.
