## 20. Ledgers Closed

The water came for the Vane brothers' empire the way it came for everything else in the Rivergate country — without malice, without hurry, and without any intention of stopping.

Orsen stood on the depot's loading dock with riverwater swirling around his boots, watching Ines's floodgates do their work. The old sluice channels that Harrowgate's miners had cut a century ago were running full again, pouring through the culverts beneath the rail spur and turning the Vane brothers' warehouse district into a shallow brown sea. Crates of survey equipment bobbed against the dock pilings. A ledger — an actual paper ledger, fat as a Bible — floated past him open to a page of columns, ink blooming into gray clouds as the water took it.

He thought about reaching for it. He didn't.

Behind him, inside the depot, Merrick Vane was shouting.

* * *

It had gone almost exactly the way Orsen had planned it, which frightened him more than if it hadn't. He'd walked in alone through the front doors at midmorning, soaked to the waist from the crossing, carrying the oilskin packet of claim documents against his chest like a man carrying a bomb. Aldous Vane had been waiting behind his desk with the patience of a man who had never once doubted he'd win, and Merrick had stood by the window with a shotgun broken open over his arm, casual as a farmer leaning on a fence.

"You actually came," Aldous had said. "I told you, Merrick. I told you the storm chaser was a reasonable man."

"I'm here to trade," Orsen had said. "The documents for the debt. All of it. Every cent, every signature, burned."

"And the girl?"

"The girl's not part of this."

Aldous had smiled then, and it was the smile Orsen would remember afterward — not cruel, exactly. Worse than cruel. *Amused.* "Everything is part of this, Mr. Ishikawa. That's what a ledger is. It's the honest recognition that everything connects to everything else." He'd held out one manicured hand. "The documents."

And Orsen had put them in his hand — because that was the con within the con, because the moment Aldous Vane touched those papers with his own fingers, he'd committed the crime of receiving stolen evidence in front of the two witnesses hiding in the loft above the loading bay, and because Mara's sworn statement was already in the magistrate's hands, launched downriver from the Narrows two nights before on a raft Teo's brother had built from mine timbers.

What Orsen hadn't planned — what nobody could have planned except a girl who remembered where every gate handle was buried under forty years of silt — was the water.

It came through the floor drains first, gurgling up like the building itself clearing its throat. Then through the loading doors, a brown tongue licking across the boards. By the time Merrick said, "Aldous, what in God's name—" the depot was ankle-deep and rising, and the magistrate's launch was already rounding the bend below the rail bridge, its whistle cutting the morning like a knife.

* * *

Now Orsen watched from the dock as the launch nosed through the flooded streets, and four constables in oilskins waded up the depot steps. He heard the words read out — *fraud, conspiracy, assault, the murder of Josiah Grieve* — and heard Aldous Vane stop shouting.

That was the strange thing. Not the arrest. The silence.

Aldous went quiet the way a man goes quiet when he finally reads the last page of his own account and finds the balance isn't what he thought. He looked at the water rising around his desk — around the filing cabinets, the deed boxes, the neat stacks of loan papers that had bought half the Rivergate valley with other people's optimism — and Orsen saw him understand, all at once, that none of it was paper anymore. Paper floats. Paper can be dried. But the ledgers in those cabinets were dissolving even as the constables cuffed him, ink lifting off the pages in gray ghosts, and there was something in Aldous's face that wasn't fear of prison. It was the look of a man watching arithmetic itself drown.

Merrick fought. Of course Merrick fought — he swung the broken shotgun like a club and caught a constable across the shoulder before they took him down into the floodwater, and for one ugly moment Orsen thought they might lose him to the current, cuffs and all. They didn't. They hauled him up spitting, and as they dragged him past the dock he found Orsen's eyes and held them.

"You had it forgiven," Merrick said. Water ran off his chin. His voice wasn't angry. It was bewildered, which was somehow worse. "You had it *in your hand*. I watched him write the release. Why?"

Orsen didn't answer. There wasn't an answer Merrick Vane could have understood, and Orsen was tired of translating himself into a language made entirely of numbers.

They took the brothers down to the launch. The crowd that had gathered along the rail spur — dockhands, a fisherman or two, faces Orsen half-knew from the river towns — didn't cheer. They just watched, the way people watch a flood recede: not glad so much as unclenching.

* * *

The magistrate's name was Corven, and she was nothing like Orsen had imagined. He'd pictured some city appointee, soft-handed and wary of the whole affair. Instead she was sixty-odd and weathered as driftwood, with a voice that carried across water without effort, and when she came up the dock steps she looked at Orsen the way a surveyor looks at a suspect bridge.

"You're the one who sent the statement downriver."

"Mara Voss sent it. I carried it as far as the Narrows."

"Teo Reyes built the raft." She ticked them off like entries. "Ines — Ines what? No surname given."

"She doesn't use one. Her father was Elias Marchetti, a surveyor. The Vanes ruined him." Orsen heard his own voice go flat and careful. "She was nine."

Corven studied him. Behind her, constables were hauling sodden file boxes up onto the dock, stacking them like sandbags. "You're the debtor. Orsen Ishikawa. The Vanes held paper on you — a truck loan, and more besides, from the look of their books. Half the valley's in those books." She nodded at the floating ledger still turning slowly in the current off the pilings. "You know what happens to debt records when the creditor's arrested for fraud? The fraudulent instruments get voided. The rest go to probate, and probate takes years, and meanwhile collectors circle like gulls." She paused. "There'll be a hearing. You'll be called. And there's the matter of a reward — the expedition's backers posted one, and the Vanes' seized assets will cover damages. You'd be entitled to a share. More than a share, given what you did."

"I don't want it."

Corven raised an eyebrow. It was a well-practiced eyebrow; it had probably ended arguments before they started.

"I want the claim honored," Orsen said. "The mineral rights Mara's expedition surveyed. The ones the Vanes buried the expedition to bury. The documents are real — you've got them now, the originals, from the cache in the canyon. Those rights belong to the people who found them. Mara Voss, Teo Reyes, Priya Sundaram, Daniel Okafor. And—" he stopped, and started again, "—and to the family of Elias Marchetti, whose survey they were finishing. That's what I want. Not money. Put it in whatever record you keep. The claim gets honored, and the land goes to them."

"That's worth a great deal more than your reward, Mr. Ishikawa. Do you understand that? Those veins run under half the north bank."

"I know what they're worth. That's why I'm asking."

Corven looked at him for a long moment. Then she did something Orsen hadn't expected: she took off her hat, tucked it under her arm, and looked out at the drowned warehouse district, at the water sliding brown and patient between the buildings, already beginning its slow withdrawal now that the gates had done their work.

"My grandfather mined at Harrowgate," she said. "Before the last flood. Before the company bought the town's debts and drowned it on purpose to clear the title." She put the hat back on. "I've been waiting twenty years for someone to ask me for the right thing instead of the profitable one. Consider it entered into the record."

* * *

They let him take a boat back upriver that afternoon — a constable's skiff, borrowed with a wave of Corven's authority — because there was no road yet and no reason to wait for one. He rowed against the current with the sun low and the first hard edge of cold coming off the water, and he passed the spot where his truck lay at the bottom of the washout, and he didn't feel anything about it at all, which felt like the truest measure of how far he'd come.

Harrowgate appeared around the last bend the way it always did: chimneys first, then rooflines, then the church steeple standing above the floodline like a hand raised in oath. Smoke rose from three chimneys. Someone had strung a line between the church and the old assay office, and laundry hung on it — actual laundry, shirts and sheets, the most defiantly ordinary sight the Rivergate country had shown him since he'd turned upriver.

They were on the church steps when he beached the skiff. All of them. Mara sat wrapped in a blanket with the swollen knuckles of her writing hand resting on her knee; Priya was mending a net; Daniel was splitting kindling with the steady rhythm of a man who had decided the world was worth preparing firewood for. Teo sat propped against the doorpost, his bandaged foot elevated on a crate, arguing cheerfully with all of them at once.

And Ines stood at the top of the steps, slingshot hanging loose in one hand, watching the boat come in.

Orsen climbed out and stood in the shallows, and for a moment nobody spoke. He'd rehearsed something on the row up — a speech about the trial, about testifying, about the long winter ahead. What came out instead was:

"They're arrested. Both of them. Fraud, conspiracy, Grieve's murder. Corven's good — she knew Harrowgate. Knew what the company did to it."

Mara closed her eyes. When she opened them they were wet, but her voice was level. "And the claim?"

"Honored. Entered into the record this morning. The land's yours — yours and Marchetti's. All of it, north bank to the ridge." He hesitated. "I asked for that instead of the reward."

Teo laughed — a short, disbelieving bark. "Instead of the— Orsen, that reward would have cleared you twice over. You could have bought a new truck, a new rig, chased storms clean down to the Gulf and back."

"I know what it was worth."

"Do you?" Teo's voice wasn't mocking. It was genuinely curious, the way only a man who'd lost a foot to frostbite could be curious about another man's losses. "Because I've been thinking about what things are worth. Lying in the mine shafts, listening to the water. And I think maybe you didn't give anything up at all. Maybe you finally spent your money on the right thing."

Daniel set down the axe. "Come inside. There's stew."

It was such a small sentence. *There's stew.* Two days ago these people had been cargo he was paid to salvage, then witnesses he'd lied to, then comrades forged in whiteouts and gunfire. Now they were telling him there was stew, and the word landed in his chest with the weight of a deed signed and sealed.

He followed them up the steps. At the top, Ines fell into step beside him, matching his stride without looking at him, the way she matched everything — terrain, weather, the tempo of other people's hearts.

"You watched them take the ledgers out," she said. It wasn't a question. She'd have known; she always knew, reading the water's memory backward from the wake of the magistrate's launch.

"Some of them. Most are drowning."

"Good." Then, after ten paces of silence: "Your debt. Is it gone too?"

"Gone with the rest. Voided instruments." He shrugged, and the shrug felt enormous, like setting down something he'd carried so long he'd forgotten the shape of his own shoulders without it. "I'm nobody's ledger entry anymore."

Ines considered this with the gravity she gave everything. "Then what are you?"

Ahead of them, the church door stood open, lamplight spilling gold across the threshold, and the smell of stew and woodsmoke rolled out to meet them, and somewhere inside Teo was already loudly reorganizing the seating arrangements. Orsen realized he didn't have an answer — not the old one, anyway. Storm chaser. Debtor. Salvage man. All those entries closed, the ink dissolved, the pages pulped back into the current that remembered everything and forgave nothing.

"I'm working on it," he said.

Ines nodded, satisfied, as though that were the only honest answer anyone had ever given her. "Good," she said again. "Because the roof leaks over the east pew, and I've decided that's your job now."

Above the pass, unseen but already on its way, the first snow of the season was gathering over the range — and for the first time in longer than he could remember, Orsen Ishikawa was not afraid of what winter would collect from him.
