## 21. First Snow

The snow came the way Ines had promised it would — not all at once, but in a slow gray hush that began at the pass and worked its way down the valley like a hand smoothing a bedsheet. She stood on the church steps and watched it come, counting the ridgelines as they whitened one by one: Saddleback first, then the long spine of the Narrows, then the black teeth of the scree where Teo had been taken and where, three weeks ago now, they had taken him back.

Behind her, inside the church, Mara was arguing with Teo about firewood.

"You are not carrying a full cord down from the cut," Mara said, in the voice she used for cross-examinations. "Your fingers are still purple."

"My fingers are *fine*."

"Show me your fingers."

"I'm not showing you my fingers."

Ines smiled and let the door swing shut behind her. She had learned, over these weeks of rebuilding, that an argument about firewood was not really an argument about firewood. It was how this household said *I am glad you are alive*. Teo's frostbite had cost him two fingertips on his left hand, and he wore their absence like a badge he hadn't asked for, and Mara hovered over him with a ferocity that neither of them would ever name aloud. The river didn't do things by accident, Ines thought. It took what it took, and what it left behind it left tangled together.

She pulled her coat tighter and went out into the first flakes.

Harrowgate in winter was a town learning to be a town again. That was the strange thing about it — stranger than the floodgates, stranger than the depot going under, stranger even than the magistrate's launch coming upriver with its lanterns lit and its writs signed. A drowned place could be drained. But a *forgotten* place had to be remembered back into existence, board by board, window by window, and that work fell to people.

There were nine of them now. Nine, plus the river.

Mara and Teo in the church, holding court over the stove. Priya and Okafor — the last two survivors, the quiet ones who had kept the expedition's instruments dry through everything — installed in the old assay office with its iron door, cataloguing every document her father's cache had given them. The Hollis brothers, who had run the ferry before the Vanes bought the river, back at their moorings since the arrest, hauling timber up from the flats because Orsen had paid them in storm footage, of all things, and they'd found it hilarious. And old Wren, who had lived alone in the upper town for eleven years and pretended not to be pleased about neighbors, but who had baked forty rolls the day the magistrate's launch arrived and had baked forty more every Sunday since.

And Orsen.

Ines found him where she always found him, halfway up the ladder against the church's south face, a hammer in his hand and shingles stacked beside him on the scaffold planks, working in weather no sensible roofer would work in because the roof had to be done before the snow got serious and Orsen Ishikawa had never once been mistaken for a sensible man.

"You're supposed to be resting," she called up.

"I'm resting *right now*." He looked down at her, and there was a smear of tar across his cheekbone like war paint. "See? Completely still."

"The ladder is moving."

"The ladder is *breathing*." He came down anyway, unhurried, and stood beside her in the falling white. For a while neither of them said anything. They watched the flakes come down over the rooftops of the lower town, over the water standing dark and patient in the streets that hadn't been pumped yet, over the mine heads and the trestle stumps and the long gray ribbon of the Rivergate itself, running away south toward the world.

"It's good snow," Orsen said finally. "Dry. Light. Won't load the new sections."

"You memorized my roof pitch."

"I memorized your whole town, Ines. You made me walk it four times." He said it lightly, but she heard what was underneath it, the way she heard everything underneath everything: *you made me learn your country, and I stayed.* "Besides, if I'm going to keep a roof on over your head, I ought to know its angles."

She looked at him sideways. "You could have gone south with the magistrate's launch. He offered you passage. He offered you money."

"He did."

"You refused both."

"I did." Orsen turned a shingle over in his hands, studying its grain as though it were very interesting. "Turns out I already spent my fee. On a bridge, and a rope, and a fool's errand across a mountain range." He glanced at her, and the charm in his face was real now, which was the difference between the man she'd ambushed with a slingshot in September and the man standing here in November. Back then the charm had been a wall. Now it was just a face. "Anyway. Somebody has to fix this roof. You'd do it yourself and fall through the ceiling onto Mara during supper, and then we'd all starve."

"That happened once."

"It happened *once*, and I've never let any of you forget it."

* * *

They worked together through the morning, Ines handing shingles while Orsen nailed, and the snow thickening around them until the world went soft and muffled and the only sounds were the tap of the hammer and the creak of the scaffold and, far off, the river talking to itself under its first skin of ice.

Ines liked working with Orsen because he didn't fill silence. Most people feared her silences — she knew they did; she'd watched them shift their weight and glance at each other when she went quiet, as though stillness in her were a warning light. But Orsen treated silence like terrain. Something you moved through carefully, without complaint, trusting it to open again.

Halfway through the morning he said, without looking up from his nailing: "I dreamed about the saddle last night."

Ines handed him the next shingle. "The whiteout?"

"Mm. Except in the dream I couldn't find the line. The cairns were gone. Everything was gone, just white, and I was walking in circles calling your name." He drove three nails, precise and even. "Then I woke up and you were asleep on the church floor with the claim documents under your head like a pillow, and I thought — right. That's why I'm here. Not the debt. Not the forgiveness. That."

"You could have told us," Ines said quietly. "On the radio night. About the offer. We would have understood."

Orsen stopped hammering.

For a long moment the only sound was the snow.

"I know," he said. "That's the worst part. You'd have understood, and you'd have been right to, and I'd have deserved whatever you decided about me after. I lied because lying was easier, and easier is the only thing I've ever chosen until this year." He looked at her then, straight on, snow gathering in his hair. "Your father built floodgates so the valley could choose when the water came. I spent thirty years letting other people choose for me. Vane, debt, weather, luck. I'm done being flooded on someone else's schedule."

Ines considered this the way she considered everything — fully, and without hurry.

"The river doesn't forgive," she said. "It only remembers. People are different. People get to start ledgers."

Orsen laughed — a short, surprised sound, breath smoking in the cold. "Did you just quote me back at myself?"

"You said it at the fire. Confession night. Mara wasn't listening, she was asleep sitting up. But I remember everything anyone says near water." She shrugged. "We were near water."

He shook his head, grinning, and went back to the shingles, and Ines went back to handing them up, and the snow came down on Harrowgate like a blessing nobody had prayed for.

* * *

At noon the household converged on the church for Wren's stew, and Ines sat on the floor beneath the new east window — glass still missing, boarded with planks Orsen had planed smooth — and listened to the sound of them.

This was the thing she could never have explained to anyone before this autumn, because she had never had it: a room full of voices that were glad she was there. All her life her memory had made her a curiosity or a tool or a ghost. The surveyors who'd passed through when she was small had wanted her to recite distances. Her father, before the Vanes ruined him, had wanted her to recite *him* — his routes, his claims, his grievances. And the river itself, the great remembering thing, had raised her the way water raises anything: without affection, without malice, simply carrying her along.

But this room argued about firewood and second helpings and whether Teo's frostbitten hand could hold a hammer (it could, mostly) and whether Priya's instrument cases should be stored high or low (high; everyone agreed; the vote was unanimous and immediate) and none of it required Ines to be anything except present.

"So," Mara said, ladling stew with the authority of a woman who had once deposed a mining company, "the magistrate's letter came up with the supply barge yesterday."

The table went quiet.

"He confirms the hearing. Spring thaw, at the Rivergate courthouse. Our claim will be entered formally. The Vanes' assets are frozen pending prosecution — Aldous is cooperating, which tells you everything about Aldous, and Merrick is not, which tells you everything about Merrick." She set down the ladle. "And he asks, formally, whether the claimants wish to name an agent of record to represent the Harrowgate interest."

Silence. Then, all at once, everyone started talking at once — Teo saying *Mara, obviously,* and Mara saying *absolutely not, I'm a witness, conflict of interest,* and Priya saying something careful and legalistic, and Okafor saying nothing at all but looking, Ines noticed, directly at her.

Okafor always looked at her when decisions were being made. He had figured out early what the others had taken longer to understand: that Ines's memory was not a party trick. It was the town's deed, its map, its history, its witness stand. Every boundary her father had surveyed, every stone he'd marked, every line the river had redrawn — she held it. If Harrowgate belonged to anyone, it belonged to whoever could say where *here* ended, and she was the only one who could.

"Ines," Okafor said, over the noise. Just her name. But the table quieted, because Okafor didn't spend words.

Everyone looked at her.

Ines set down her bowl. Her heart was doing something strange and slow, like the river before a flood — a deep gathering pressure, not frightening, just enormous.

"I can't sign," she said. "I'm not — there's no record of me. No birth paper, no census line. The Vanes saw to that when they ruined my father. On paper I don't exist."

"On paper," Mara said, "neither does half of what happened here. The magistrate knows that. It's why he's holding the hearing *here* and not at the Rivergate." She leaned forward. "But an agent of record needs to stand up in that courtroom and speak for this land. Someone who knows it. Someone the river—" she caught herself, smiled slightly, "—someone who remembers."

"I'll do it," Teo said. "I owe this place a hand."

"You owe this place a *rest*," said Mara.

"I'll do it," said Priya, and then, honestly: "I'll be terrible at it. I'll cite regulations until they fall asleep."

And then, from the doorway, wet with snow and grinning, hammer still in his hand:

"I nominate Ines."

Orsen had come in without any of them hearing — the snow ate footsteps now, the way it ate everything, gently and completely. He leaned against the doorframe, dripping, entirely too pleased with himself.

"She walked us across a mountain range in a whiteout from memory. She flooded the Narrows on command. She knows every legal boundary of this valley better than the county surveyor, because her father *was* the county surveyor, and she carries his maps in her head where no one could burn them." He looked at Ines, and his voice lost its performance, went quiet and true. "Say the word and I'll draft the paperwork tonight. I've read enough loan contracts to know how documents work. Mostly how to survive them."

Ines looked around the table. Mara, sharp-eyed and waiting. Teo, cradling his shortened hand. Priya and Okafor, steady as instruments. Wren in the corner pretending not to listen, flour on her sleeves.

A family, chosen and kept. And now they were asking her to speak for the ground under all of them.

"The river keeps everything," Ines said slowly. "But it doesn't testify. People have to do that part." She looked at Orsen. "Write the papers."

* * *

Evening came early, the way it did now, blue dusk settling into the valley while the snow kept falling, fat and unhurried. After supper Ines climbed the ladder to the church roof, because she wanted to see it — the first true snowfall on the rebuilt section, Orsen's shingles catching white along their edges row by row, the whole south slope turning silver under her feet.

He came up a few minutes later, having known exactly where she'd be, and sat down beside her on the ridge with a groan that was half theater and half honest.

"We should put a railing here," he said.

"We should put a railing everywhere. This town is trying to kill us."

"Speak for yourself. This town loves me." He nodded out at the valley. Below, lamplight was blooming in the windows of the lower houses — Wren's, the Hollises', the assay office where Priya worked late among the rescued papers. The river ran black between the banks, ice forming at its edges in delicate lacework, still flowing, still going south, still remembering.

"Do you ever miss it?" Ines asked. "The chasing. The storms."

Orsen was quiet for a while. Snow accumulated on his shoulders and he didn't brush it off.

"I used to think I chased storms because I loved them," he said. "Took me thirty years to figure out I was chasing them because it was easier than staying anywhere. A supercell never asks you to fix its roof. Never asks you to sit at its table. Never asks you to be its agent of record in front of a magistrate." He smiled at the darkening sky. "Now I chase them a little, in the season, and then I come home upriver. Best of both. I get the sky *and* the table."

"*Home*," Ines repeated.

He went slightly pink, which the cold did not fully explain. "Well. The rent's reasonable."

Ines laughed — a real laugh, rare enough that Orsen turned to look at her like he'd seen something valuable wash up on a bank.

Below them, the church door banged open and Teo's voice carried up through the snow: "Are you two going to sit on the roof all night? There's cocoa. Wren made cocoa. There are *rules* about the cocoa, Mara's rationing it, you need to get down here before it becomes an incident."

"We're coming!" Orsen bellowed back, and made no move whatsoever to come.

Ines hugged her knees and watched the snow come down on the pass, on the Narrows, on the scree and the saddle and the drowned streets and the living ones, on everything the river had taken and everything it had given back. Somewhere south, in a frozen courthouse, papers waited with her name on them — a name that would exist now, officially, in ink, because people had chosen to write it there.

"Orsen," she said.

"Mm?"

"When the thaw comes. When the hearing's done. When the town's really ours." She watched a flake land on her sleeve and hold its shape, perfect and six-pointed, before the warmth of her skin undid it. "Will you teach me to read the storms? The way I taught you the water?"

Orsen looked at her for a long moment. Then he smiled — the real smile, the one with no wall behind it — and pulled off his glove and held out his hand, palm up, to catch the snow.

"Deal," he said. "First lesson starts when the ice breaks."

Far up the valley, past the pass, past the range, the storm systems of the southern plains were already stirring in their winter sleep — and Ines, who remembered everything, found she was looking forward to forgetting nothing that came next.
