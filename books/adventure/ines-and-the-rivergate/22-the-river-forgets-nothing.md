## 22. The River Forgets Nothing

**Orsen**

The supercell came down out of the western flats at four in the afternoon, green-black and rotating, dragging a skirt of dust behind it like something being born. Orsen Ishikawa stood on the shoulder of the county road with his camera on the tripod and his heart doing what it had always done in the presence of weather that could kill him — beating faster, not from fear but from recognition.

Some men went to church. Orsen drove toward the sky when it turned ugly.

He'd been chasing again for eight months now. Not for the Vanes — never for anyone like the Vanes again — but for a documentary outfit out of the capital that paid half of what he was worth and asked no questions about why a storm chaser with his reputation had spent a year off the map. The footage was good. His eye hadn't dulled. If anything, the year upriver had taught him patience, and patience was the one thing most chasers never learned. They wanted the tornado the way gamblers wanted the jackpot, all appetite and no attention.

Orsen waited. He watched the wall cloud organize itself. He let the storm tell him what it was going to do before he committed to a road.

And when it was over — when the funnel roped out over empty pasture without touching down, when the light went gold and ordinary again — he did what he always did. He pointed the truck north, toward the Rivergate.

It was two hundred miles of bad road to the mouth of the river, then a day's run by boat to Harrowgate, and he made the trip every time he finished a chase, no matter how far the storm had dragged him. The crew teased him about it. *Got a woman up there, Ishikawa?* And he'd say yes, which was true, though not the way they meant. He had a whole town. He had a household. He had a girl who remembered everything the water had ever taken, and a woman named Mara who was learning to be a magistrate's witness instead of a fugitive, and a man named Teo who still couldn't feel three fingers on his left hand but had learned to laugh anyway, and a roof over the old church that Orsen had repaired so many times he knew every board by its grain.

The debt was gone. It had burned with the Vanes' records, along with the brothers themselves, in a manner of speaking — Aldous and Merrick were in the territorial prison at Coldbrook, their optimistic loan operation dissolved by court order, their ledgers read aloud into the public record by a magistrate who had arrived too late to stop the flood but just in time to drown them in paper. Orsen owed nothing to anyone now except the people he loved, and that was a debt he paid gladly, in trips upriver, in shingles and nails, in showing Teo how to rig a winch line, in sitting quiet while Mara rehearsed her testimony for the land commission even a year after she no longer needed to give it.

The claim had been honored. That was the thing he asked for instead of the reward, and the thing he was proudest of: Harrowgate and the mineral rights beneath it belonged to the survivors, to Ines, to the memory of her father. The river country had owners again, and they were the kind who listened to it.

But tonight, driving north through the washed-clean evening, Orsen wasn't thinking about any of that. He was thinking about the letter in his jacket pocket.

It had come to the documentary outfit's office, forwarded twice, addressed in handwriting he didn't recognize. *Mr. Ishikawa,* it began. *You don't know me. I worked for the Vane brothers' operation as a clerk, before. There are others like me — people who signed things we didn't understand, who were made promises the same way you were. Some of us have been talking. We want to know if you'd speak at the hearings this winter. We want to know if you'd help us find each other.*

At the bottom, a list of names. Eleven names. Every one of them, he suspected, someone the Vanes had owned the way they'd tried to own him.

He'd been carrying the letter for six days, turning it over on long drives, and he knew — the way he knew a storm was going to turn before it turned — that he would say yes. But first there was someone he needed to ask. Someone whose opinion had become, without his ever deciding it, the compass he steered by.

The Rivergate opened ahead of him in the last light, wide and brown and patient, carrying the mountains down to the sea.

**Ines**

Ines met the boat at the old landing below Harrowgate, because she always did, because the river told her when it was coming.

She felt it the way other people heard their names called — a change in the current against the pilings, a particular muddiness in the shallows where the channel shifted after rain upstream. A boat pushing hard against the current made the water fold differently than driftwood or fish or the slow tumble of the season. She'd known Orsen was coming since midmorning, and she'd had the kettle on since noon, and she pretended both facts were unremarkable.

He climbed the landing ladder with the easy tiredness of a man who'd been driving for two days, and she looked at him the way she always looked at him now — checking, cataloguing, reading him the way she read the water. Thinner than winter. Sunburned along the jaw. Something folded in his jacket pocket that he kept touching with his elbow, which meant paper, which meant news, which meant he'd wait until after supper to show her because he was polite in ways that still surprised her.

"You missed the spring flood," she said, by way of hello.

"I heard about it. Teo wrote me." He fell into step beside her on the path above the waterline, the path that existed only because she had walked it into being, memorizing where the ground rose and fell beneath the brush. "Said the river came within four feet of the church."

"Four feet and seven inches," Ines said. "I measured."

"Of course you did."

"The river remembers, so I remember. Somebody has to keep the records." She said it lightly, but it was the truest sentence she owned. Her father had taught her surveying the way other fathers taught prayers — as a conversation with the land, a promise to pay attention. Then the Vanes had ruined him, and the river had raised her, and the remembering had become hers alone. Until it hadn't. Until there were people to remember *for*, which was heavier and better.

Harrowgate rose out of the evening around the bend — half-drowned still, the lower streets given back to the water forever, the upper town lit with the small stubborn lamps of people who had chosen to stay. The church stood at the top with its new roof, Orsen's roof, silvered now with weather so it looked like it had grown there. Smoke from the cookhouse. Teo's dog barking the announcement of Orsen's return before Teo himself appeared in the doorway, shouting something joyful and profane.

Ines hung back a moment and watched Orsen walk into it — the noise, the arms, the belonging — and thought about the man who had come up the river a year ago with a salvage contract and a lie in his mouth. She had put a stone from her slingshot into the water an inch from his ear that day, and she had known within ten minutes that he was dangerous, and within an hour that he was frightened, and within a day that he was good underneath it all, the way ore is good underneath the rock.

She had also known, that first night, that he lied to them about the radio. She had memorized the exchange word for word — the Vanes' offer, his voice going smooth and careful, his *no offer was made* delivered in the exact tone her father used to use when the survey numbers were bad and he didn't want to frighten the crew. She had carried that knowledge for weeks like a stone in her boot. And in the end she hadn't used it as a weapon. She'd asked him instead what he wanted to be when the river let him go, and he had broken open like ice at thaw, and confessed at the fire, and chosen.

People could do that. The river never did. That was the whole difference, and she'd built her life on knowing it.

Supper was loud. Mara argued with Teo about the placement of a new fence; the two youngest survivors, Pell and Ansel, reenacted the spring flood with such exaggeration that even Mara laughed. Orsen ate three helpings and told storm stories with his hands, and under the table his elbow kept touching that folded paper, and Ines kept not saying anything, because some things you let a man arrive at himself.

Afterward, he found her on the church steps, where she went every clear night to read the stars against her father's charts, and sat down beside her without asking, which was his way.

"You got a letter," she said.

He huffed a laugh. "Of course you know that."

"I know everything that happens in this town. You're the least sneaky person I've ever met." She held out her hand. He gave her the letter, and she read it twice by starlight, fast, the way she read everything — total recall, no rereading required, but she liked the feeling of the words twice anyway.

"Hearings in the capital," she said. "Eleven families the Vanes bled. They want you to stand up and say what happened."

"They want me to help them find each other. The standing up part is—" He rubbed his jaw. "I spent a year being ashamed to say the Vanes' name out loud. Feels like the least I can do."

Ines folded the letter along its original creases and handed it back. "You'll go."

"Yeah."

"And you'll come back."

"That's the plan."

"That's not what I asked." She turned and looked at him straight on, the way she did when she wanted the truth more than she wanted comfort. "The river doesn't forgive, Orsen. It forgets nothing and forgives nothing — that's what my father said, and he was right. Every flood is exact. Every loss stays lost. The water doesn't care if you're sorry." She tapped the step between them. "People are the only thing in this country that can forgive. So I'm asking. Are you coming back?"

Orsen was quiet for a long moment. Down in the drowned streets, the river moved in the dark, making its small exact sounds against the foundations of houses nobody would ever live in again.

"Ines," he said, "a year ago I'd have traded every one of you for a clean ledger. You knew it. You caught me in it. And you gave me a way out anyway — you asked me who I wanted to be instead of telling everyone what I'd done." He shook his head slowly. "There isn't a storm anywhere that scares me as much as the idea of becoming that man again. Yeah. I'm coming back. This is the only place I've ever been that I'd call mine."

Ines nodded once, satisfied, and looked back at the stars, and felt the thing in her chest that she had stopped pretending was small.

"Good," she said. "Because the north bank trail washed out in the spring, and I need someone strong and stupid to help me reroute it. And because—" she hesitated, which she almost never did, "—because I've been mapping something. Down past the Narrows. My father's old notes mention a spring-fed channel the surveys never recorded. If it's real, it changes where the safe ground is for the whole valley. I want to go look. I want you to come."

"A survey," Orsen said. "You're inviting me on a survey."

"My father would have liked you," Ines said. "He liked people who paid attention."

Above the drowned town the stars wheeled, indifferent and exact, keeping their own perfect records. Below, the river ran on toward the sea, forgetting nothing, forgiving nothing — and between the two, in the lamplit rooms of Harrowgate, the people went on doing the one thing the wilderness never could: letting each other begin again.

Ines marked one star's position on her chart, closed the book, and started planning the route south.
