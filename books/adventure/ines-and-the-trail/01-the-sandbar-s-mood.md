## 1. The Sandbar's Mood

The river told Mirabel Montrose its mood before the sun did.

He stood on the foredeck of the *Kestrel* with his boots bare and his coffee going cold in one hand, watching the water come down out of the dark the way a man reads a letter from someone he loves and does not entirely trust. The Serein at dawn was never the same river twice. This morning it ran low and sullen, brown-green where it should have been silver, and along the eastern bank the reeds leaned all one direction like a congregation listening to bad news. Upstream weather, then. Rain in the high country that hadn't reached them yet but would, in its own time, the way everything upstream eventually arrived downstream.

There was a new sandbar below the ferry landing—a long yellow tongue of silt that hadn't been there Tuesday. The river had built it in the night, patient as a mason, and by August it would be an island with birds nesting on it, and by October it would be gone as if it had never existed. That was the thing about sandbars. They were the river's opinions, and the river changed its mind constantly.

Mirabel had learned to read those opinions the hard way, which was the only way the Serein taught anything. Twelve years on this water. He knew what a green tongue of foam meant two hundred yards above a rock shelf, knew the smell the gorge gave off when the snowmelt came down heavy, knew the particular hush that fell over the current just before it decided to kill someone. He could stand here for ten minutes and know whether the day would be easy or whether the river intended to test him, and this morning—this morning the mood was watchful. Not angry. Not generous. Watchful, the way a dog watches a stranger come up the road.

He drank the cold coffee anyway. Waste was a sin out here, though not the only kind he'd committed.

Behind him the *Kestrel* slept at her moorings, a hundred and ten feet of patched steel and stubbornness, her paddle wheel dripping in the slow current like something breathing in its sleep. She'd been a cargo boat once, hauling ore concentrate down from the middle camps before the mines played out and the rail line took the freight. Now she hauled whatever wouldn't spoil: lumber, salt, drums of lamp oil, the occasional coffin going upriver to a family that wanted their dead buried where they'd been born. Honest work. Quiet work. Work that asked no questions about the man who did it.

That last part mattered more than the other two put together.

* * *

Port Verany woke the way port towns always wake—badly, and smelling of fish.

By seven the market stalls were rattling open along the quay and the first barges were jostling for space at the fuel dock, and Mirabel went ashore with a canvas list in his pocket: diesel filters, a coil of three-quarter-inch line, lamp wicks, and if Bram's luck held, actual coffee instead of the roasted chicory sludge the crew had been pretending to enjoy since spring. He wore his work coat with the collar up, not because the morning was cold—it wasn't—but because Port Verany was a town of four thousand people and every one of them seemed to want to talk to him.

"Captain! Captain Montrose!" A boy of maybe eleven came pelting down the quay, all elbows and bare feet, waving a folded paper. "Telegram for the *Kestrel*, sir!"

"No telegrams." Mirabel didn't break stride. "No mail either. We're not a post office."

"But it says *urgent*—"

"Everything says urgent." He flipped the boy a coin anyway, because refusing a child's errand money felt like a different order of sin than refusing the errand, and the boy caught it out of the air with the reflexes of someone who'd grow up to be either a banker or a pickpocket. "Buy yourself breakfast."

The boy looked at the coin, looked at the telegram, looked at Mirabel's retreating back, and made the sensible choice.

The chandler's on Rope Street still had filters. The rope walk had the line. It was at the grocer's, weighing out wicks while the proprietor complained about the price of tallow, that Mirabel saw the man in the gray suit standing across the street under the awning of the assay office, doing a very poor job of not looking at him.

Gray suits didn't belong in Port Verany. Gray suits belonged in the capital, in courtrooms and counting houses, in the kinds of rooms where men decided things about other men without ever getting their shoes wet. This one was sweating through the shoulders already and kept touching his hat brim, and when Mirabel turned back to the counter he saw the reflection of the man crossing the street in the grocer's window glass.

"Mr. Aldous," Mirabel said to the proprietor, without turning around. "How much do I owe you?"

"One pound six, and you're robbing me."

"I'll rob you properly next month." He set coins on the counter, collected his parcel, and walked out the front door at exactly the pace of a man with nowhere in particular to be.

"Captain Montrose?" The gray suit materialized at his elbow with the breathless cheer of a man who had rehearsed this opening on the coach ride in. "Mirabel Montrose? Of the *Kestrel*?"

"There's no Montrose aboard the *Kestrel*." Mirabel kept walking. "You've got the wrong boat."

"I—beg your pardon? But I was told—" The man scurried to keep up, briefcase swinging. "I have it on good authority that a Captain Montrose operates out of this landing. I represent the firm of Calloway and Prue, factors, of Bellhaven, and I'm authorized to discuss a matter of outstanding debt—"

"You're authorized to discuss it with somebody who cares." The quay opened ahead of them, wide and bright and crowded with morning traffic: stevedores, fishwives, a gang of dock kids fighting over a crate lid. Mirabel angled toward the thickest part of it. "Which isn't me."

"Sir, if you'd simply—there are records. Signatures. A vessel registered under the name *Fair Wind*, out of Bellhaven, twelve years ago, and the surety against that registration was signed by a Mirabel Montrose, and the debt has been carried forward with interest, and my firm has been very patient—"

Twelve years ago. The words landed somewhere under Mirabel's ribs like a fishhook finding old scar tissue. He didn't change pace. He'd had practice at this—not this exact conversation, but this exact feeling, the sensation of the past reaching up through the floorboards of the present with its cold fingers. You didn't run. Running was how they knew they'd found you. You walked, and you bored them, because nothing killed a bill collector faster than a man who genuinely did not care.

Except that he did care, which was the problem, and caring showed in the pulse point at the throat, and this collector looked like the sort who'd been trained to look there.

"*Fair Wind*," Mirabel said thoughtfully. "Sank, didn't she? Off the Bellhaven bar? Whole crew drowned?"

"That is—the vessel was lost, yes, but the debt—"

"Then take it up with the dead." He stepped sideways between two drays loaded with salt cod, and the crowd closed behind him the way water closes over a dropped stone. He heard the gray suit call out twice more, growing fainter, growing confused, and then the market noise swallowed it whole.

He didn't go straight back to the boat. That was rule number one of being followed, and he'd learned it from a man who'd known a great deal about being followed: never return by the door you left from. So he cut through the fish market, bought Bram his coffee beans at an outrageous price out of pure spite toward the morning, doubled back along the seawall, and came down to the *Kestrel*'s mooring from the mudflat side, stepping across the old pilings like a man who'd done it a thousand times, because he had.

Odile was in the engine room, naturally. Odile was always in the engine room; he suspected she slept there some nights, curled up in the warm space beside the boiler like a cat that had decided the machinery was her family. Her head appeared through the deck hatch as his boots hit the planking, a smudge of grease across one cheekbone and her gray hair tied back with what appeared to be a piece of gasket cord.

"You're walking fast," she said. "You only walk fast when something's wrong."

"Nothing's wrong."

"You're carrying the coffee like it owes you money."

"Bram will drink it either way." He handed the parcel down through the hatch and watched it disappear into the dark, into the smell of hot metal and diesel that was, as much as anything, the smell of home. "We need filters too. I got the last ones."

"We always need filters." Odile studied him a moment longer, and he gave her the face he'd perfected over twelve years—the mild, unremarkable face of a man thinking about nothing—and after a while she shrugged and sank back into her engine room, because Odile respected privacy the way she respected torque specifications: precisely, and only up to the point where failure became dangerous.

Pao was up in the rigging coiling line, twenty feet up the mast with his bare feet hooked in the ratlines, singing something tuneless and cheerful in a language Mirabel had never bothered to identify. Pao sang when he was happy, sang when he was nervous, sang when he was hungry. The song changed; the singing didn't. From the galley came the percussion of Bram's knives against the cutting board, keeping time with Pao like the world's strangest rhythm section.

His crew. His strays. Every one of them had washed up on the *Kestrel* the way driftwood washes against a piling—no questions asked, no stories required. Odile had appeared one winter with a toolbox and a genius for engines and a past she guarded like a strongbox. Pao had stowed away at fourteen and never left, and nobody had ever asked why a boy would rather coil line on a working boat than go wherever it was boys were supposed to go. Bram had cooked for river crews up and down the whole watershed and talked about everyone he'd ever met except himself.

And Mirabel had made them a promise, the same promise he made himself every morning when he read the river's mood: this boat stays clean. No names that aren't the ones we choose. No pasts dragged aboard like bilgewater. And no passengers—

Well. Passengers were the complicated one.

It wasn't that he hated people. People were fine, in small doses, at arm's length, paid in advance. But passengers brought luggage, and luggage brought questions, and questions brought attention, and attention was the one cargo the *Kestrel* could not afford to carry. A boat full of strangers was a floating witness stand. He'd seen what happened when the wrong eyes found the right boat. He'd spent twelve years making sure it would never happen to this one.

So: no passengers. Cargo only. It was painted on the sign at the landing, more or less. It was the first thing he told anyone who asked, before they even finished asking.

He was standing at the rail, watching the new sandbar slowly assemble itself in the shallows, when Pao called down from the mast.

"Cap! Boat coming up the channel. Little one. Heading for our landing."

"Let them land. There's room enough."

"It's just one fella." Pao shaded his eyes. "Funny-looking. Old coat, no hat. Walking like his legs hurt." A pause, and then, with the casual cruelty of youth: "He's coughing something awful, Cap. Sounds like he's trying to turn himself inside out."

Mirabel watched the little skiff come on. It moved badly, wallowing, the man at the oars pulling uneven strokes like each one cost him something he couldn't spare. The morning light lay flat on the water, and the river's mood hadn't changed—still that watchful hush, still the reeds leaning east—and Mirabel found himself thinking, absurdly, that the river had known about this skiff before he had. That the sandbar's mood this morning had been the mood of a door about to open that he very much wanted to keep shut.

The skiff bumped against the landing pilings. The man in it sat for a long moment with his head bowed, gathering himself, and then began the slow, terrible business of climbing out.

Mirabel stayed at the rail. He did not go down to help. Helping was how conversations started.

But he watched, and he waited, and the comfortable isolation he had built plank by plank over twelve years stood quietly around him like a wall—and somewhere beneath it, faint as water seeping through a hull, he felt the first crack begin.
