## 2. A Man Made of Dust

The cough reached Mirabel before the man did.

He heard it across the whole width of the dockyard, threading through the morning sounds the way a hairline crack threads through porcelain—thin, wrong, insistent. He was kneeling on the deck of the *Vela*, scraping barnacles off the hull seam with a paint knife gone dull from three seasons of the same work, and he knew the sound the way a riverman knows the sound of water changing under a boat. Something was coming. Something was already coming apart.

He kept scraping. The barnacles came away in pale crescents, and the river breathed beneath him, and the cough came closer.

Port Meridian at dawn was a town of half-voices. Fishermen mending nets with their mouths full of curses. Stevedores arguing freight in the low, careful tones of men who had already lost money that day. Gulls screaming their one opinion over and over. Mirabel had learned, in twelve years of docking here, to move through it like smoke through a reed bed—present, unremarked, gone by the time anyone thought to look. The *Vela* sat at the far end of the long pier, stern to the wall, where the shadows pooled deepest and the lamplight didn't reach. He chose his moorings the way he chose everything: for concealment, never for convenience.

The cough stopped at the head of his gangplank.

Mirabel didn't look up right away. That was a discipline, too. Curiosity was a debt, and he paid no debts. He let the stranger stand there in the salt wind, let the silence do its work, and only when the man shifted his weight and the plank groaned under him did Mirabel straighten, set the paint knife on a folded rag, and turn.

He had seen a thousand men come down that pier looking for passage, and they sorted themselves quickly, the way silt sorts itself in still water. The desperate ones came fast, eyes down, already rehearsing their plea. The rich ones came slow, eyes everywhere, offended by the rust on his rails. The dangerous ones came quiet, hands loose, taking the measure of the boat and the man on it in the same glance.

This one was none of those, or all of them at once.

He was tall the way a fence post is tall—vertical, but weathered, leaning a degree off true. His coat had once been good; you could tell by the buttons, real brass, though two were missing and a third had been replaced with twine. Dust had gotten into everything, into the seams of his sleeves, into the gray of his stubble, into the deep creases around eyes that were still, against all odds, a fierce and unapologetic blue. He carried a canvas satchel over one shoulder, and he carried it the way a man carries a child: close, protective, unwilling to set it down even to steady himself on the rail.

"Captain," the man said. His voice was gravel rolled in honey. "They said down the quay there's a boat that goes where other boats won't."

"They said wrong."

"They said it like it was a warning." The stranger smiled, and the smile cracked the dust on his face into little riverbeds. "I don't scare easy."

"You should. It keeps you alive." Mirabel picked up the paint knife again, not because there was barnacle left but because a man with a tool in his hand was a man with a reason not to talk. "I run cargo. Timber, salt, machine parts. I don't run people."

"I'm not people." The stranger coughed then, a deep, wet, structural sound, and Mirabel's hands went still on the knife before he could order them not to. "I'm freight. One hundred and forty pounds of it, more or less, and getting less by the week."

"That's not funny."

"No," the stranger agreed, wiping his mouth with the back of his hand, and Mirabel saw the fleck of red on his knuckles and looked away from it, hard, the way you look away from a hole in a hull. "It's not. But it's true, and true things are the only currency I've got left. So let's trade in it. My name's Ines. Elias Ines."

"No names." The rule came out of Mirabel flat and automatic, worn smooth as a mooring cleat from use. "That's the first rule aboard this boat. No names, no pasts, no passengers. You want the second and third rule, they're the same as the first."

"Then what do I call you?"

"Captain."

"Captain of what? A boat with no name on her stern?"

"The *Vela*," Mirabel said, and regretted it immediately, because the stranger's blue eyes lit with the particular hunger of a man who has gotten a first answer and intends to collect a hundred more.

"*Vela*," Ines repeated, tasting it. "Sail. Somebody named her hopeful."

"Named her before I had her."

"Everything's named before we have it. That's the trouble with names." Ines eased himself down onto a cargo crate with the careful, economical movements of a man who had learned to spend his strength like a miser spends coin. Up close, the dust on him resolved into something worse than dust—alkali, Mirabel thought, the white mineral crust of the high desert country, a hundred miles and a lifetime away from this green river. The man had come down out of the dry places. People who came down out of the dry places were usually chasing one of two things, and neither of them was healthy.

"I'll say this once," Mirabel told him, "and then I'm going back to work. I don't take passengers. I especially don't take sick ones. A man dies aboard my boat, I lose the season, maybe the boat, maybe more. You want passage, there's a packet steamer at the government dock. She runs the lower gorge twice a week."

"The packet steamer stops at Corben's Landing and turns around." Ines said it without heat, without argument, the way you state the position of a rock in a channel you intend to run anyway. "I need to go past Corben's Landing. I need to go all the way up the Serein. To the head of the gorge."

Mirabel let the silence stretch. Below the hull, the river made its endless low conversation against the planks, and somewhere down the quay a winch began to turn, and the gulls kept screaming their one opinion. He had run the Serein Gorge twice in twelve years, both times alone, both times with his heart in his throat and his hands remembering things his mind had ordered them to forget. Forty miles of water that fell eleven hundred feet. The Widow's Teeth. The Devil's Throat. Channels that the maps marked with a single word—*unsurveyed*—in the particular apologetic italics of cartographers who had never been brave or foolish enough to go and look.

Nobody ran the upper Serein in monsoon season. Nobody ran it in the weeks *before* monsoon season, either, when the headwaters were already drinking the first storms and the gauge at Corben's Landing climbed a hand's width every day. The gorge in that window was a throat slowly closing, and every year it swallowed somebody's boat, somebody's son, somebody's whole foolish plan, and the river kept the change.

"You're asking for a run that kills crews," Mirabel said. "In season. With a passenger who can't haul a line."

"I'm asking for a run that most crews won't attempt." Ines's voice had changed. The honey was still in it, but the gravel had hardened into something like bedrock. "There's a difference, and I think you know the difference, because I watched you scrape that hull for ten minutes before you'd look at me, and a man who works that careful on the part of the boat nobody sees is a man who knows exactly what his boat can do."

Mirabel looked at him then, fully, and hated himself for the thing that happened in his chest—the small, traitorous, waking thing, the curiosity he had spent twelve years drowning and which, like everything on this river, kept floating back up.

*No pasts.* He had built the rule for good reason. He had built it the way you build a levee, after the flood. Every soul he'd taken aboard who'd wanted to talk had wanted, eventually, to be asked, and every question was a door, and every door opened both ways. He asked nothing of anyone. In exchange, no one asked anything of him. Odile kept her engine room and her silences. Pao kept his laughter and whatever was underneath it. Bram kept his recipes and his reasons. It was a fair economy. It was the only economy that had never ruined him.

And here was this man made of dust, sitting on a crate, already spending names like they cost nothing, looking at Mirabel with those blue furnace eyes as though he could see straight through the paint and the rust and the twelve careful years to whatever was buried at the bottom of the hull.

"Where's your money?" Mirabel asked, because it was the question that ended conversations, and he wanted this one ended.

Ines reached into the satchel—slowly, showing his hand the whole way, a courtesy Mirabel noted and distrusted—and brought out a leather wallet gone soft as cloth. From it he drew a fold of banknotes, and he peeled them off one at a time and laid them on the crate between them, and he kept peeling, and kept laying, until there was more money on that salt-stained crate than the *Vela* earned in a good season.

"That's half," Ines said. "Half now, half at the head of the gorge. And before you ask—yes. It's clean. It's slow money, river money, the kind that takes a man thirty years to save when he's spending most of it on mules and dynamite and the other things a claim eats. It's mine. I earned it in the dirt."

"A claim." Mirabel looked at the money and did not touch it. Money on a crate was bait. Money in your hand was a contract. He knew the difference, and he intended to keep knowing it. "You're a prospector."

"I was. I found what I was looking for." The cough took him again, longer this time, folding him forward over the satchel until Mirabel half-rose without meaning to, and then the spasm passed and Ines waved him back down with two fingers, an odd, imperious, gentle gesture, the gesture of a man who had once been used to being obeyed. "Found it a long time ago. That's not why I'm here."

"Then why are you here?"

"Because of what I found *with* it."

The wind shifted, coming up the gorge from the north, and it carried the smell that had been sitting on the horizon all morning—the green, metallic, waiting smell of rain that hadn't started yet. Mirabel felt it on the back of his neck like a hand. The season was turning. He had maybe three weeks of safe water, and he had timber contracts waiting, and he had a rule, and the rule was the only thing standing between the *Vela* and all the drowned and desperate history of the upper river.

"No," he said.

Ines looked at him. "You haven't heard the—"

"No. I don't need to hear the rest. The rest is the part that gets people killed. There's always a rest. There's always a reason, and a story, and a thing you found with the gold, and I have heard every version of it, and the ending is always the same: my boat on the rocks, my crew in the water, and some version of you explaining to me, very reasonably, why none of it was your fault." Mirabel swept the banknotes off the crate and held them out. "Take your money, Captain Ines. Take it to the packet steamer. Take it to Corben's Landing. There are pilots up there who'll run the gorge for that kind of money. They're younger than me and stupider than me, and you'll find one inside of a day."

Ines did not take the money. He sat with his hands folded on the satchel and looked out at the river, and for a long moment the dust and the gray stubble and the ruined coat fell away from him, or Mirabel saw through them, and what was underneath was not a dying prospector at all. It was something harder. A man standing in a doorway of his own past, deciding whether to walk through it one more time.

"You're right about all of it," Ines said quietly. "Every word. I have heard the ending too. I've had twelve years to think about endings." He looked back, and his eyes were wet, whether from the cough or the wind or something older, Mirabel could not tell and refused to try. "Twelve years. Isn't that something. You count in them too. I can see it. You've got a man's face that's counting something."

"I don't count anything. I scrape barnacles."

"You count miles." Ines said it simply, without accusation, the way you'd say *the river runs north*. "I watched your hands while I talked. They were moving like they were steering. Men who've made peace with their lives don't steer when they're standing still." He rose, slowly, gathering himself joint by joint, and shouldered the satchel, and left the money where it lay. "Leave it there. Call it a mooring fee. You can throw it in the river if it offends you, though I'd rather you bought your crew a decent dinner."

"I don't have a crew."

"Of course you don't." Ines smiled again, that cracked-riverbed smile, and began to walk back down the plank, and every step of it sounded like the cough sounded—thin, wrong, insistent. He stopped at the bottom, one hand on the rail, and did not turn around. "I'll be at the Salt Lantern inn. I'll be there a while. I'm not going anywhere fast." A dry sound that might have been a laugh. "First time in my life that's been a comfort."

He walked away up the pier, into the noise of the fish market, and the dust of him blended into the dust of the town, and Mirabel stood on his own deck with a fistful of another man's money and the taste of the word *twelve* sitting on his tongue like river silt.

Twelve years. The number had a weight. He set it down carefully, the way you set down something that might still be live, and went back to the barnacles.

He scraped for an hour. The knife moved. The river talked. The rain-smell thickened in the north until the far hills went soft and blue and indistinct, and the gulls came down off the roofs and stood hunched along the pilings like a jury waiting to be sworn.

At some point he realized he had stopped steering.

His hands lay still on the deck, the knife forgotten, and he was looking upriver—up the long green reach, past the last moorings, past Corben's Landing, to where the Serein Gorge opened its dark mouth in the hills and the water came down out of it cold and fast and full of everything it had taken.

He did not want to know what Elias Ines had found with the gold. He told himself that, plainly, in the plain voice he used for the truth: he did not want to know, and he was not going to ask, and the man would be gone in a week or dead in a month, and the *Vela* would still be here, and the rule would still hold, and the past would stay where he had sunk it, in the deepest channel he knew, weighted and dark and never, never rising.

The cough did not come back. But all afternoon, whenever the wind swung around from the north, Mirabel thought he heard it—threading through the gulls and the winches and the river's low talk, thin and wrong and insistent, like a hairline crack through porcelain, like the first sound a hull makes before it starts to take on water.

* * *

That night he went to the Salt Lantern.

He told himself it was to return the money. He told himself that on the walk over, through the lamplit lanes where the fish smell gave way to woodsmoke and frying onions, past the harbor office with its rain-streaked notice board where the season's first storm advisory had been pinned up crooked, and he told it to himself again on the inn's narrow stairs, and he was still telling it when the landlady pointed him down the hall to the last room, and it was only when he raised his hand to knock that he understood the money was in his pocket because he had not been able to make himself leave it on the boat, and a man who carries a thing that far is not returning it.

The door opened before he knocked.

Ines stood in the lamplight with his coat off and his sleeves rolled, and the room behind him was a disaster of paper—charts, letters, a journal with a broken spine, all of it weighted down with whatever objects came to hand, a pipe, a tin cup, a river stone. And spread across the center of the bed, held flat at the four corners by the tin cup and the pipe and two river stones, was a length of oiled silk, and on the silk, drawn in ink gone brown with age, was a line of the Serein Gorge that Mirabel had never seen on any chart, because no chart had ever carried it.

Ines looked at him for a long moment. The blue eyes were calm. The dust was still on him, but under the lamplight it looked less like dust and more like ash, like a man who had already been burned down to what he was made of.

"I was hoping you'd come," Elias Ines said, and stepped back from the door. "I need to show you something. And then I need you to promise me two things. The first is that you'll get me up that river."

"And the second?"

The dying man's gaze went past him, down the hall, down the stairs, out toward the dark water where the *Vela* lay at her mooring, and when he spoke his voice was low and even and absolutely certain.

"Silence."
