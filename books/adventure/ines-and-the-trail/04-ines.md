## 4. Ines

The photograph had been in my pocket for eleven days, and by the time the ferry from Corvane grounded itself on the mudflats below Port Serein, I had looked at it so many times that my father's face had begun to feel like something I was inventing.

That was the trick of a photograph, I'd learned. You could wear a face smooth with looking. Elias Ines, thirty-nine years old in the picture, standing on the deck of some excursion steamer with his hand shading his eyes and his mouth caught half-open, mid-laugh, as if whoever held the camera had just said something foolish and beloved. My mother had taken it. She'd been dead six years now, and still the photograph smelled faintly of her—camphor and lavender, the drawer she'd kept it in, the house that wasn't mine anymore. Or maybe that smell was gone too, worn away by my thumb, and what I remembered was only memory of a memory, the way a river remembers a flood in the shape of its banks long after the water has left.

I stepped down onto the mudflats with one bag and no umbrella, because the sky over Port Serein was the color of old pewter and rain seemed less like weather than like a mood the town had agreed upon. The mud took my boots to the ankle. Somewhere behind me a gull screamed its one opinion about everything.

*Find him,* my mother had said, or hadn't said—I couldn't anymore separate the instruction from my own invention of it. *Or don't.* That was more honest. She had never asked me to find him. She had only died, slowly and then suddenly, leaving me a house full of his absence: his tools I didn't know how to use, his books about rock strata, the photograph, and a letter he'd written her the year he left, which I had read perhaps four hundred times and which contained, in all those readings, exactly one piece of useful information—the name of a port at the mouth of the Serein River, and the words *there's a boat, there's always a boat.*

There was always a boat. Port Serein proved it within an hour. There were ferries and barges, dredgers and derelicts, a paddle-steamer being cannibalized for brass by boys who whistled while they worked. And there was one boat, moored alone at the far end of the long stone jetty, that did not look like any of them.

She was a riverboat of the older kind, black-hulled, low in the water, with a shallow draft and a wheelhouse set forward like a man leaning into wind. Her paint had been renewed often enough to be respectable but not enough to be proud. No name on the bow. No flag. No manifest posted, no agent's shingle, no clerk dozing over a ledger of fares. Just a gangplank down to the stones, and a smell of engine oil and coffee drifting from somewhere below, and a woman in canvas trousers working at the rail with a wrench, swearing at a fitting in a voice that suggested the fitting had insulted her family.

I stood at the foot of the gangplank for a long moment, doing what I had done outside every door for twelve years: rehearsing.

*I'm looking for a man named Elias Ines.* No. Too naked. *I'm told there's work upriver.* Better. *I have money and I need passage into the gorge before the rains.* Truest, and truth is the best disguise, because nobody checks it.

The woman with the wrench glanced down at me, decided I was furniture, and went back to swearing.

"I'm looking for the captain," I said.

"He's not buying anything."

"I'm selling nothing."

"Everyone's selling something." But she came to the rail anyway, wiping her hands on a rag that had given up its original color years ago. Up close she had iron-gray hair cut short and forearms like dock rope, and eyes that did arithmetic on you without appearing to move. "You want Mirabel, you'll find him in the town before noon, drinking coffee he complains about and reading the shipping notices like they're scripture. What's your cargo?"

"Myself."

"How much do you weigh?"

"That's hardly—"

"It's entirely relevant. Shallow draft, this boat. Every pound matters." She looked past me at the pewter sky, and something crossed her face—not fear, exactly, but the particular attention of a person who has learned to count days. "Monsoon's coming early this year, if you believe the barometers, and I believe the barometers. If you want to go up the Serein Gorge, you want to go soon, and you want to go with someone who knows the water." She jerked her chin at the wheelhouse. "He knows the water."

"And you are?"

"Odile. Engineer." She said it the way other people say *surgeon*. "Go find him in town. And if he says no—and he will say no—don't take it personal. He says no to everyone. It's practically his signature."

* * *

I found him at a café with a tin roof, three streets back from the waterfront, where the coffee was bad and the shipping notices were pinned to a corkboard by the door like a wall of small confessions. He sat alone at a corner table with his back to the wall—that detail registered in me with a recognition I didn't examine—and he was doing exactly what Odile had promised: reading the notices, complaining into his cup, wearing a canvas coat that had been rained on many times and dried badly.

He was younger than I expected. Not young—young the way driftwood is younger than the tree it used to be. Hard-used, weathered gray at the temples, with hands wrapped around the cup like it might make a run for it. When I stopped at his table he didn't look up right away. He finished the paragraph first. Then he looked up, and I watched him do to me precisely what I had spent the walk over learning not to do to anyone: measure, dismiss, forget.

"Captain Mirabel?" I said.

"No passengers," he said, and returned to the notices.

"I haven't asked yet."

"You were going to. Everyone who says *Captain Mirabel?* in that voice is going to ask." He turned a page. "No passengers. No cargo contracts under two hundred weight. No charters into the gorge after the first of the month. Those are the answers. Save us both the conversation."

"What if I'm none of those things?"

"Then you're lost, and the ferry office is two streets east."

I sat down across from him without being invited. His eyes came up again, and this time they stayed, and I understood that I had just done something interesting, though I couldn't have said what. Later I would learn that the interesting thing was simple: I sat like a person with nowhere else to be. On that river, in that port, in that whole season of my life, sitting like that was rarer than money.

"I need passage up the Serein," I said. "Into the gorge. Before the rains close it."

"Then you're the first thing after all. No."

"You haven't heard—"

"There's nothing to hear. The gorge floods. Boats die in it every year, usually with their captains' names in the paper and their debts unpaid. I'm not dying in the gorge for anyone's fare." He lifted the cup, found it empty, and grimaced at it as if it had betrayed him personally.

"I can pay well."

"Everyone can pay well. That's how people talk themselves into drowning."

"I'm not looking for gold, if that's what you think."

Something moved behind his eyes at the word *gold*—a flinch so fast I almost missed it, a door closing on a lit room. "I don't think anything," he said. "I think no."

We sat in silence for a moment. Rain began on the tin roof, soft at first, then committed, a sound like applause from a very large and indifferent audience. A boy came through the door, shook water off his cap, changed the notices on the corkboard, and went out again. Mirabel watched him go with an attention that had nothing to do with shipping.

"Who told you about my boat?" he asked, not looking at me.

"A woman at the jetty. Odile."

"Odile talks too much." But he said it fondly, the way you say a thing you've given up trying to fix. He turned another page of the notices he clearly wasn't reading. "Where in the gorge?"

"Why?"

"Because if you say *the middle reaches*, I'll tell you the middle reaches are where boats go to become kindling. If you say *the headwaters*, I'll know you're lying, because nobody goes to the headwaters except fools and prospectors, and prospectors stopped coming two seasons ago when the Corvus Line bought up every claim office between here and Vane's Landing." He finally set the notices down. "So. Where."

"The upper gorge," I said. "Past Vane's Landing."

His face did something complicated and closed. "And what's up there?"

"A mining claim."

"Whose?"

"Mine," I said, and heard how the lie sounded even as I said it—too quick, too flat, a coin dropped on a counter instead of pressed into a palm. "It's mine. By inheritance. I've never seen it."

Mirabel studied me for a long moment. Outside, the rain thickened; inside, the lamp over our table buzzed and swung slightly, and the shadows of the two of us leaned together on the wall and apart again.

"You're lying," he said, without heat, the way a man reports the weather.

"I'm not."

"You're lying about something. Maybe not the claim. Maybe the claim's real. But you're lying about something, and I've spent twelve years getting very good at hearing it." He folded the notices once, twice, precise as a surgeon. "Here's my rule, since Odile apparently didn't give you the full catechism. No names. No pasts. No passengers. The last one's the important one. Passengers bring questions, and questions bring—" He stopped himself, jaw tightening around whatever word had been coming. "Questions," he finished, lamely, and I understood that whatever he'd swallowed had teeth.

"Then we're agreed," I said. "No names."

His eyebrows rose a fraction. "That's not what I—"

"I don't want to exchange them. Yours or mine. I'm not curious about you, Captain. I'm not curious about anyone. I want a boat, a quiet crossing, and silence. You're offering silence as a service. I'm offering to buy it. I can't imagine a transaction better suited to both parties."

For the first time, something that was almost a smile touched the corner of his mouth and thought better of it. "You've rehearsed that."

"On the ferry. Eleven days."

"Eleven days." He looked out at the rain, and I watched him weigh me against something I couldn't see—a ledger of his own, some private accounting of risks run and prices paid. "What's the name you're booking under, if we're not exchanging names?"

"Mara Voss," I said.

It was the first false name I'd ever chosen, and I chose it badly, out of the air, out of panic, out of the photograph in my pocket pressing against my ribs like a second heartbeat. I would regret it within the hour. I would regret it for reasons I couldn't yet imagine.

But Mirabel only nodded slowly, and something passed over his face—something I mistook at the time for boredom, and would understand much later, much too late, as recognition. Not of the name. Of the lying. Two liars across a table, each hearing in the other's voice the same practiced flatness, the same too-smooth surface over moving water.

"Mara Voss," he repeated, tasting it. "Fine. Mara Voss buys silence. Mara Voss gets silence." He stood, dropping coins beside his cup. "Be at the boat at first light. Bring your own food for the first three days. And Mara—" he paused at the door, rain silvering the air behind him, "—whatever you're really carrying up that river, carry it quietly. My crew doesn't ask questions either. We're all very good at it. It's the one thing we're excellent at together."

He went out into the rain. I sat a long while with the cooling air where he'd been, my hand flat over my pocket, over the photograph, over my father's laughing face.

*There's a boat,* the letter had said. *There's always a boat.*

I had found the boat. Now I only had to survive being aboard it—lying to a captain who could hear lies the way sailors hear weather, carrying a name I'd stolen from thin air, toward a father who had left me twelve years ago and didn't know I was coming.

Outside, the rain kept applauding. Somewhere upriver, in the high gray gorge, the monsoon was gathering like a debt come due.

And I would learn, later—much later, when it nearly killed us all—that the name I'd chosen out of panic and thin air, *Voss*, belonged already to a man on this river. A man with a boat of his own, and a habit of asking questions.

But that night, I knew none of it. That night I only walked back through the drowned streets of Port Serein to a room above a chandlery, laid the photograph face-down on the table so I could sleep, and lay awake listening to the river, which sounded, all night long, like someone practicing my father's laugh.
