## 5. Crew of Strays

The *Sable Wren* smelled of engine oil and burnt coffee and the particular salt-sweet rot of a boat that had lived too long on a river that forgave nothing. Mirabel stood in her galley doorway at dusk with Elias Ines's refusal still sitting in his chest like a swallowed stone—*no names, no pasts, no passengers*—and watched his crew eat dinner without him.

They always saved him a plate. That was the trouble with them.

Bram had outdone himself tonight, which meant he'd burned something and then drowned it in gravy until it surrendered. The cook was built like a rain barrel, bald as an egg, with forearms that could wring a chicken's neck at forty paces—a skill Mirabel suspected he'd once practiced professionally, though Bram had never said so and Mirabel had never asked. Rule one of the *Wren*: no pasts. Bram cooked like a man making amends to someone invisible, plating every dish with a care that bordered on penance, and if his hands shook sometimes when he thought no one was looking—if he flinched at the crack of a mooring line snapping taut—well. The river was full of men who flinched.

Odile sat at the head of the table she'd claimed the day she arrived, four years back, with a toolbox chained to her wrist and a story about a fire that explained neither the burns nor the way she checked every lock twice. She was small and precise, gray-streaked hair cut short as a hedge, and she ate the way she did everything: efficiently, without pleasure, as though eating were a maintenance task the body required and she resented the interruption. Her hands were never clean. There was always grease in the creases of her knuckles, always a smudge on her jaw where she pushed her hair back, and Mirabel had long ago stopped noticing because Odile without grease would have been like the river without silt—wrong at some fundamental level.

And Pao. Pao was seventeen going on ancient, all elbows and appetite, sprawled across the bench with his boots up until Odile knocked them down without looking. He'd shown up two seasons ago stowed away in the coal bunker, half-starved, lying about his age with the transparent fluency of a boy who'd been lying since before he could talk. Mirabel had put him ashore at three different ports. Pao had swum back twice and hidden in the woodpile the third time, and by then Bram was feeding him and Odile had taught him to read a pressure gauge, and the vote to keep him had been two to one before Mirabel even called it.

That was the other trouble with them. They voted.

"—and I'm telling you," Pao was saying, mouth full, "the old man's got money. Real money. You should've seen the notes he peeled off. Crisp as lettuce."

"Crisp as what?" said Bram.

"Lettuce. Money. It's a saying."

"It is not a saying."

"It is where I'm from."

"Where you're from is a coal bunker," Odile said, "and you didn't wash for a week."

Mirabel cleared his throat from the doorway, and the table went quiet in the particular way that told him they'd been talking about exactly what he'd walked in on. He crossed to his chair, sat, and looked at the plate Bram slid toward him. Gravy. So much gravy.

"Elias Ines," Mirabel said. "The coughing man. He wants passage up the Serein Gorge. To a mining claim above the Narrows."

Pao whistled low. Even Odile's fork paused.

"Before monsoon," Mirabel went on. "Which is—" He counted days the way he counted everything, in miles and margins. "Nineteen days out, maybe less. The gorge runs high water by then. Nobody goes up the Serein in high water. Nobody goes up the Serein at all, mostly, because there's nothing up there worth the Widow's Teeth."

"There's gold," Pao said.

"There's a claim," Mirabel corrected. "Maybe. And a dying man who wants me to ferry him into country that eats boats." He took a bite of whatever Bram had buried under the gravy. It was, against all evidence, good. "I told him no."

Silence. The kind of silence that had weight and texture and, Mirabel was beginning to understand with a sinking feeling, an agenda.

"You told him no," Odile repeated.

"I did."

"And then you came home," she said, "and stood in that doorway for a full minute listening to us talk about him."

"I came home to eat."

"You came home," Odile said, setting down her fork with a click that in Odile-language constituted shouting, "because part of you already knows we're going to say yes, and you wanted to be here to lose the vote properly."

"I'm the captain."

"You're the captain," she agreed. "Of a boat we keep afloat. Of a route we run. Of debts we pay." She leaned forward, elbows on the table, grease-smudged chin on laced fingers. "You want to invoke captain's privilege, Mirabel? Fine. Then let me tell you what captain's privilege costs this month. The port fees went up again. The starboard boiler tube is paper-thin—I patched it twice and it'll take a third patch and then it'll take a replacement, and a replacement costs money we don't have. We are eleven days of fuel from broke, and the freight contracts downstream dried up when the rail line opened. You know what's left? Passengers."

"No passengers." His own rule, spoken back to him. "No names, no pasts, no passengers. That's how nobody on this boat ends up dead or in irons."

"Nobody on this boat ended up dead or in irons anyway," Bram said quietly, "which is a miracle given the cargo we're carrying."

Every head turned to him. Bram almost never spoke first, and never about that.

The cook shrugged his great shoulders, uncomfortable under the attention. "Look at us," he said. "Look around this table. Captain won't give a last name. Engineer sleeps with a crowbar under her bunk and checks the hull seams after every nightmare. Boy—" he nodded at Pao, who had the grace to look briefly sheepish, "—boy's got scars on his wrists that don't come from rigging work, and I've seen him hide his food like the famine's coming back tomorrow. And me." He spread his hands, palms up, calloused and steady now, steady as anything. "You all know what I am. Or you know enough. A man doesn't get hands like mine doing pastry."

"Bram makes excellent pastry," Pao offered.

"Thank you, Pao."

"The point," Bram said, "is that this boat isn't a business. It's a lifeboat. Every soul aboard climbed in from drowning, and the only rule that's ever mattered—the only one—is that we don't ask each other what we were swimming from." He looked at Mirabel, and his eyes were kind and terrible. "So I'll ask you this instead, Captain. When a drowning man offers us money to carry him somewhere, what exactly are we supposed to say?"

Mirabel set down his fork. Outside, the river muttered against the hull, patient as ever, keeping its own counsel. Through the galley's salt-fogged window he could see the dock lamps coming on along the waterfront, and beyond them the dark shoulder of the hills where the Serein Gorge began its climb into country he'd sworn, twelve years ago, never to see again.

Because that was the thing he hadn't told them. Couldn't. The gorge wasn't just dangerous water. The gorge was *that direction*—upriver, inland, toward the valleys and the courts and the whole geography of a life he'd sawed loose and let sink. Taking this fare wasn't a job. It was a return ticket.

"He's dying," Mirabel said. "Did I mention that? Coughs blood. Maybe weeks left. And he wants to go find a claim he hasn't seen in years, with a map on oiled silk and a look in his eye like a man walking back into a burning house for a photograph." He shook his head. "Dying men make bad passengers. They make demands. They make promises. And when they die—and he will die, probably on my deck—they leave things behind. Questions. Papers. People come asking."

"People are already coming asking," Odile said.

He looked at her sharply. "What do you mean?"

"A man came round the engine shop this afternoon while I was haggling for boiler tube. Well-dressed. River boots too new to be honest. Asked had I heard of any boat taking on a long charter, upriver, unusual cargo." She held his gaze. "I said the *Wren* runs freight and fools, nothing else. But he wrote something down in a little book, Mirabel. And he knew the boat. Knew your name—or the name you wear."

The galley felt suddenly smaller. Mirabel became aware of his own heartbeat, slow and deliberate, the way it went when he was afraid and refused to admit it. Someone had talked. The harbormaster, maybe, or the clerk at the chandler's, or the boy who swept the dock—somebody had seen a coughing stranger with deep pockets asking after the *Sable Wren*, and word traveled on the waterfront faster than water ran downhill.

"That settles it," Mirabel said. "We stay put. We take freight. We keep our heads below the gunwale until this blows over."

"No," said Pao.

Everyone looked at him. Seventeen years old, gravy on his chin, wrists scarred under his sleeves, and he said it again, louder, the way boys do when they've finally found something worth being loud about.

"No. I don't accept that." He stood up, which made him taller than anyone expected him to be. "Captain, I love this boat. I love—okay, I tolerate this boat, Odile, stop looking at me—but I didn't swim out to a coal bunker and nearly drown twice to spend my life hiding under a dock waiting for the world to forget me. Because it won't. Forget us, I mean. Whatever we did, whoever we were—it doesn't blow over. I know that better than anybody." His voice cracked and he hated it and pushed through anyway. "Hiding isn't living. Hiding is just dying slower, with better scenery."

"Pao," Mirabel started.

"You said no names, no pasts. Fine. But the old man—he's got no name either, has he? No past either? He's just like us. Drowning. And he's offering to *pay* us to help him, which is more than anybody offered any of us." Pao's fists were on the table now. "Vote. I move we take the charter. Up the gorge, before monsoon, all the way."

"Seconded," said Bram immediately.

Mirabel turned to Odile, his last hope for sanity, and found her watching him with those flat gray eyes that missed nothing and forgave less.

"Odile," he said. "Tell them."

"I will," she said. "Here's what I'll tell them. That boiler tube needs replacing whether we sit in port or run the river. Sitting here, we go broke in six weeks and the bank takes the boat, and I've been on a boat the bank took." Something flickered behind her eyes, quick as a fish turning. "It doesn't end well for the boat. Running the charter, we get paid enough to refit proper—new tube, new seals, maybe new paint if Bram insists. And we get paid in advance, because a dying man can't chase us if we quit halfway." She straightened. "Also, someone's sniffing around asking questions, and I'd rather answer those questions from moving water than from a tied-up dock. A moving target's harder to read. Ask any engineer."

"That's not—you're describing tactics, not—"

"I'm describing why yes is smarter than no," Odile said. "The heart reasons follow. Vote's called. All in favor."

Three hands went up before Mirabel finished drawing breath to object.

"All opposed."

His own hand stayed down. Not because he'd conceded—because raising it would have meant admitting the vote mattered, and it didn't, it couldn't, because he was the captain and—

But he wasn't, not really, and he knew it, and they knew it, and the beautiful rotten truth of the *Sable Wren* was that she belonged to no one and everyone, a democracy of the shipwrecked, and Mirabel Montrose—who was not named Mirabel Montrose—had built it that way on purpose, because the last time a man had absolute authority over people who trusted him, eleven families had learned what that authority cost.

"Carried," Odile said. "Two to one, with the captain abstaining, which we'll note in the log as *captain sulking*."

"I don't sulk."

"You absolutely sulk," said Pao. "You've got a face for it. Like a wet cat."

Bram laughed, a big rolling sound that filled the galley and pushed the shadows back, and even Odile's mouth twitched at one corner, which from Odile was a standing ovation. Mirabel looked around the table—at the cook who fed ghosts, the engineer who slept armed, the boy who'd chosen them over dry land—and felt the familiar ache underneath the irritation, the one he never named: that these people had voted themselves into his life the way water votes itself into a hull, patiently, everywhere at once, and that he would sink before he threw any of them overboard.

"Terms," he said at last, and the table quieted, because they knew that tone. "We take the charter. One charter. The old man, his crates, his map, his business—all of it stays sealed and none of it becomes ours. We go up, we put him where he wants to go, we come down. If he dies aboard, we bury him at the next port and we ask nothing, learn nothing, keep nothing. And if anyone comes asking about us—" he met each of their eyes in turn, "—we know nothing about each other either. Agreed?"

"Agreed," said Bram.

"Agreed," said Odile.

"Agreed," said Pao, and then, unable to help himself: "But Captain—when do we cast off?"

Mirabel looked out the fogged window at the darkening river, at the far black line of the hills, at the whole drowned geography of his past lying upriver like a reef beneath calm water. Somewhere out there a well-dressed man was writing in a little book. Somewhere up the gorge a dying man was counting his remaining breaths against the monsoon calendar. And here, in a galley that smelled of gravy and engine oil, his family had just voted to sail straight through everything he'd spent twelve years rowing away from.

"First light," he said. "Day after tomorrow. Tell the old man."

Pao whooped and was out of his chair before the words finished, thundering up the companionway to carry the news, and Bram began clearing plates with the contentment of a man whose cooking had finally accomplished something, and Odile lingered, gathering her tools, and paused beside Mirabel's chair on her way out.

"For what it's worth," she said quietly, "whatever's up that gorge—you don't have to tell us. But you might have to tell yourself."

Then she was gone, boots ringing on the iron stairs, and Mirabel sat alone with the lamp and the river's mutter and the strange lightness in his chest that felt dangerously close to hope.

Above him, through the deck planks, he heard Pao's voice carrying across the water to the dockside hotel, bright and carrying: *"Hey! Old man! Old man Ines! You're on!"*

And from the shore, faint but distinct, came the sound of coughing—and then laughter, ragged and grateful, like a man who had just been told he was allowed to die somewhere that mattered.

* * *

Mirabel climbed to the wheelhouse last, as was his habit, to check the log and the barometer and the small brass compass that had never once pointed anywhere he wanted to go.

The barometer was falling. Early, for the season. He tapped the glass and frowned at it, and it fell a fraction more, indifferent to his opinion, and outside the window the first stars came out over the gorge like sparks off a grindstone.

Nineteen days, he thought. Give or take.

He wrote the charter into the log in his careful hand—*E. INES, ONE PASSENGER, UPRIVER CHARTER, TERMS PRIVATE*—and beneath it, after a moment, the heading he used for every voyage the *Wren* had ever made:

*Departure: first light.*

He did not write a destination. Some habits, he told himself, were load-bearing.

Below and behind him, the boat settled into the rhythms of a family going to sleep—Bram banking the stove, Odile's lock-checking rounds, Pao's hammock creaking like rigging in a wind only he could feel. Mirabel blew out the lamp and stood a while in the dark, reading the river by ear: the slap of small water, the groan of the dock fenders, and far upstream, so faint it might have been imagination, a sound like weather gathering behind the mountains, patient as a debt collector, keeping its own count of the days.

First light, then. First light, and no more hiding.

The river, which had heard that promise before, said nothing at all.
