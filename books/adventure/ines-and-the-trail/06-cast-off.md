## 6. Cast Off

Pao woke before the gulls did.

That was the trick of it—beat the birds to the water and you beat the day to your bones. He'd learned it young, on a different river in a different country, from an uncle whose name he no longer said aloud. Some habits survived translation. Rising in the blue-black hour was one of them. Trusting no one who slept past it was another.

He dressed in the dark of the fo'c'sle, careful not to wake Bram, who snored like a landslide deciding whether to happen, and slipped out onto the deck of the *Merry Adeline* with his boots unlaced. The harbor lay flat as poured tin. Mist stood on the water in columns, slow-moving, patient, like things waiting to be introduced. Downriver the channel buoys ticked against their moorings, a small iron heartbeat. Pao put his hands on the rail and breathed it all in: tar, bilge, cold mud, the green rot-smell of the reed beds upstream. The smell of leaving. There was no better smell in the world.

Three days ago this had been a boat going nowhere in particular, which was Pao's favorite kind of boat. Now crates were stacked on her deck like a secret learning to stand up straight, and a dying man slept in the aft cabin, and a woman with a false name had paid cash for a berth she didn't need, and Captain Mirabel—who never took passengers, who had a rule about it painted into his frown if not onto the wheelhouse glass—had let them aboard anyway.

Pao didn't trust any of it. But he trusted the crew, and the crew had voted, and that was how the *Adeline* made her decisions: not by one will but by five, the way a raft makes its way down a rapid—not steering so much as agreeing, over and over, with the water.

He went forward to check the lashings.

* * *

The crates had come aboard at midnight, which was the first strange thing. Nobody loads honest cargo at midnight unless they're ashamed of daylight. Pao had been on the dock with the hand truck when Elias Ines's hired dray rumbled up out of the fog, and he'd watched the old man count each crate off the wagon himself, touching every one, murmuring numbers under his breath like a prayer he was embarrassed by. Six crates. All of them heavy in the wrong way—heavy low, heavy even, no shifting weight inside, no clink, no slosh. When Pao had tilted one to test it, something inside had slid with a dry whisper, paper on paper, or maybe metal on cloth, and Elias had put a hand on the crate's edge—not grabbing, just resting, the way you'd rest a hand on a dog's shoulder—and said, "Gently, son. She's old."

Not *it*. *She.*

Pao had looked up then and caught the old man watching him with eyes gone yellow at the rims, bright as lamp oil, and there had been a moment—Pao would think about it for days afterward—when the two of them understood each other perfectly across the gap of forty years and a hundred lies: *I know you looked. You know I know. Let's both be polite about it.*

"Captain says they stay sealed," Pao had said.

"Then they'll stay sealed," Elias had agreed, and coughed into his sleeve for so long that Pao had wanted to hold him upright, the way you'd steady a stack of plates in an earthquake. "But sealed isn't hidden, boy. Sealed is just a promise I'm asking you to keep."

Pao hadn't liked that. Promises were cargo too, and heavier than most, and he'd spent his whole life keeping his holds empty of them.

Now, in the gray before dawn, he walked the rows of them. They were lashed amidships under the cargo awning, squared and roped and tarped, six dark shapes with stenciled markings mostly scraped away—one crate still showed a fragment: **SEREIN SUR** and then nothing, the rest planed off or painted over. Survey company stencil. Pao ran his palm along the nearest lid. Cold. Dry. No smell of ore, no mineral tang, none of the sour dust that came off rock crushed fine. Whatever these held had lived indoors a long time, wrapped and kept, tended like relics.

He heard the hatch behind him and turned, hand drifting to the coiled line at his hip out of pure habit.

It was the daughter.

She came up the ladder quietly for a landsman, moving like someone who'd learned to be quiet in a house where noise cost money, and she stopped at the top and looked around the deck the way a cat looks around a new room: corners first, exits second, everything else third. She wore the same gray coat from the day she'd booked passage, and her hair was tied back with what looked like a strip cut from a flour sack, and she had the photograph again—the little one, cracked across one corner—that she carried everywhere like a ticket she was afraid wouldn't be honored.

"You're up early," she said.

"You're up earlier."

"I didn't sleep." She said it without self-pity, the way you'd report the weather. Her eyes went past him to the crates. Stayed there. "Those came aboard at night."

"Midnight," Pao agreed.

"That seems late for loading."

"It seems late for anything honest."

Something flickered between them—not friendship, nothing so warm, but the quick recognition of two people running the same arithmetic. Pao had watched her since the day she'd stepped aboard with her false name and her cash fare and her eyes that never quite finished looking at anything. He knew liars. He was one. And the thing about two liars on a small boat was that neither could relax, because each one kept catching the other doing the work—the counting, the checking, the small constant labor of maintaining a story—and every time their eyes met, some silent ledger updated itself.

Her name wasn't Ines. Pao was nearly sure of it. She'd signed the manifest *Ines Marlow*, but she'd hesitated half a breath before the surname, the way a man steps over a gap in a bridge he doesn't fully trust, and when Bram had called her *missus Marlow* at dinner she'd taken a full second too long to answer. Also—and this was the thing that had settled it for Pao—she watched Elias the way you watch a fire you're not sure is contained. Not with love. Not only with love. With accounting.

"He's worse," she said now, nodding toward the aft cabin. "This morning. I heard him getting up to the basin three times."

"The captain knows?"

"I haven't told him." She said it quickly, and then, hearing how it sounded, added, "I don't want the trip stopped. He needs—" She stopped herself. Started over. "I've waited twelve years to be in the same country as my father. I'm not letting a bad night turn us around."

Twelve years. She'd given Pao that number unprompted, which meant it was load-bearing; it held up something in her story she couldn't yet show him. Pao filed it away with the other facts he was collecting the way a magpie collects: the false name, the cash, the hesitation, the photograph, the number twelve.

"They stay sealed," Pao said, nodding at the crates. "Captain's rule."

"I don't care about the crates." But her eyes cared. Her eyes stayed on them a long moment, reading the tarps like a page, before she dragged them away and out toward the mouth of the harbor, where the sky was beginning to do its work—a thinning along the eastern hills, the color of a bruise healing, gold coming up underneath like blood through a bandage.

"Is it always like this?" she asked. Quieter now.

"What?"

"This. Leaving." She hugged her elbows inside the gray coat. "The water standing so still, like it's holding its breath. Like the whole world is one held breath before—"

"Before the wind finds out we're moving," Pao finished. He smiled despite himself. "Yeah. It's always like this. It's the best part. Everything still possible. Nothing broken yet."

She looked at him sideways. "You talk like someone who's left a lot of places."

"You sign like someone who's invented a lot of names."

For a heartbeat he thought she'd deny it. He watched the denial assemble itself behind her eyes—watched her weigh him, weigh the crew, weigh the narrow hull of the *Adeline* and the forty miles of gorge ahead—and then, instead of lying, she simply said nothing, which from a liar is the closest thing to a confession there is.

"Pao," he offered, tapping his own chest. "Just Pao. One name. Cheapest thing I own."

"Ines," she said after a moment. Tapping hers. And then, almost smiling, almost: "Cheapest thing I own too."

Above the eastern hills the sun broke its rim, and the mist caught fire all at once, every column of it burning white-gold, and the harbor went from tin to hammered copper, and somewhere back in town a church bell that nobody attended began ringing the hour anyway, because bells don't care whether anyone listens.

* * *

By full light the whole crew was up and the *Adeline* was waking in her usual order: Odile first below, thumping the engine's flanks and talking to it in a low fluent mutter that Pao suspected was actual conversation; Bram in the galley banging pans and singing something obscene in two languages; Mirabel last, appearing at the wheelhouse door already dressed, already watching, as though he'd been awake for hours somewhere no one could see.

Pao cast off the stern line while Odile took the bow, and the ropes came aboard wet and heavy, and the current took the hull the way a hand takes a shoulder—firm, familiar, slightly proprietary. The engine caught on the second try, that old diesel stutter-then-roar that Pao had heard a thousand times and would hear in his dreams until he died, and the *Merry Adeline* leaned her nose upstream and began, gently, to argue with the river.

Elias Ines came out on deck for the departure. He had to lean on the doorframe to do it, and then on the rail, and Pao saw the daughter see it—saw her take half a step toward him and stop herself, hands curling closed at her sides, because whatever history stood between those two was wider than a deck and deeper than the channel, and twelve years of silence doesn't cross itself just because there's finally a floor beneath it.

"Good morning," Elias said to the deck at large, to the crew, to the river, to the morning itself, as though greeting all of them personally. His voice was gravel rolled in honey. "Fine vessel. Fine crew. Fine day for it."

"Sit down before you fall down," Mirabel said from the wheelhouse, not unkindly, not kindly either.

"There's time enough for falling later." But he sat, lowering himself onto the bench by the rail with the care of a man handling his own last cargo, and pulled a blanket over his knees, and fixed his burning eyes on the upstream haze where the hills began to close together and the river narrowed toward the Serein Gorge. Toward whatever was in the crates. Toward whatever he'd come back from the dead to fetch.

Ines sat beside him. Not close. A careful arm's length of not-close, precise as surveying. And Pao, coiling line at the bow, watched the two of them sit there in the strengthening sun—father and daughter, or whatever they truly were to each other—and saw her glance sideways at the old man once, twice, a third time, each glance a little longer, a little hungrier, cataloguing him: the hollowed cheeks, the tremor in the hands, the way he favored his right side when he breathed. She was memorizing him. That was the word for it. She was memorizing him the way you memorize a house you expect to lose.

And Elias, for his part, never once looked at her. He looked at the river, at the hills, at the crates. Anywhere else. And Pao thought: *there's the real cargo.* Not the six sealed boxes under the tarp. These two people, sitting arm's length apart, carrying freight they couldn't lash down and couldn't unload, and forty miles of gorge ahead in which to try.

The town fell astern. The warehouses shrank, the church bell faded, the debt collector's office window glinted once and was gone—Pao had seen Mirabel scan that window twice before casting off, quick as a man checking a mirror—and then the banks went green and wild and the reeds closed in and the *Adeline* was alone on the water with her secrets.

Odile came up from the engine room wiping her hands, stood beside Pao at the bow, and squinted upstream.

"Widow's Teeth by midafternoon," she said. "Water's high for the season."

"High good or high bad?"

"High fast." She spat over the side, ritual, punctuation. "Fast means we find out what this boat and this crew are made of sooner than I'd like."

Behind them, from the aft deck, came the sound of Elias Ines coughing—long, wet, tearing, the sound of a man being slowly unmade—and then the sound of his daughter's voice, low and urgent, saying words Pao couldn't catch, and then silence, a silence with weight to it, a silence like the one before wind finds out you're moving.

Pao looked at the tarped crates. Six shapes, dark and patient, riding amidships where the hull was strongest.

Whatever the old man had sealed up, whatever promise he'd asked a stranger's crew to keep, it was coming up the gorge with them. And the river, high and fast and rising, was already asking its first questions.
