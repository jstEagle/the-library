## 7. Twelve Years of Miles

The river woke before he did. It always did.

Mirabel stood at the wheel in the gray hour, when the Serein ran the color of pewter and the mist came off the water in slow ribbons that unraveled against the banks. He had not slept well—sleep was a country he visited less and less these days—and so he had been up since four, checking the bilge, oiling the wheel bearing, doing all the small liturgies of a man who trusted work more than memory.

Behind him, the *Kestrel* slept on. Odile's engine room ticked and settled as the iron cooled. Pao was a shapeless bundle in the bow hammock, one arm dangling, dreaming whatever deckhands dreamed. Bram's galley window glowed amber; the cook rose even earlier than Mirabel did, though what he did down there before dawn—besides burn the first pot of coffee and sing to himself in a language no one aboard spoke—remained a mystery Mirabel had never felt entitled to solve.

And aft, in the cabin they'd rigged for passengers, Elias Ines coughed in his sleep. A wet, tearing sound, like a rope paying out too fast. Then silence. Then again.

Mirabel turned his face back to the river.

He had a rule about passengers. Had had it for twelve years, and the rule had held through drought years and flood years, through offers of triple fare and once, memorably, a magistrate's bribe. No names, no pasts, no passengers. The *Kestrel* hauled freight: salt pork, lamp oil, tin sheeting, the dull honest cargo of people who wanted things moved and nothing else known about them. Freight didn't ask questions. Freight didn't cough all night in a voice that reminded him—

But there it was. That was the trouble with this job, with Elias Ines folded into his stern cabin like a letter he hadn't agreed to carry. The old man's cough sounded like another cough, twelve years gone, from a sickroom in a house that no longer existed except in the place Mirabel went when he let his mind wander, which was why he never let it wander.

He measured his life in miles instead. It was a clean arithmetic. The Serein from the delta town of Vairmont to the head of navigation above Gorse Landing: two hundred eleven miles, give or take what the spring floods stole or gave. A round trip, loaded light, six days. Twelve days if the Widow's Teeth were running mean. In twelve years he had worn a groove in that distance the way water wears a groove in limestone—not by force but by repetition, by showing up every morning and putting the wheel over and letting the miles accumulate behind him like ballast.

Miles asked nothing of him. Years asked everything.

* * *

The trial had lasted nine days. He knew that the way he knew the number of stairs to Odile's engine room—by having climbed them until climbing was no longer a decision.

Nine days in a courtroom that smelled of beeswax and wet wool, in a river town where everyone knew everyone's business except the one piece of business that mattered. He had been nineteen. He had sat in the gallery in a borrowed coat, his hands folded like a good boy's, and watched men in dark suits take turns saying the name *Aurel Vane* as though it were something you scraped off your boot.

Aurel Vane. The man who raised him. Who had found a half-drowned orphan of eight in the reeds below the smelter town and carried him home across four miles of marsh because there was no road and no cart and the child was too fevered to walk. Who had fed him, taught him letters off shipping manifests, taught him knots, taught him the difference between pride and stubbornness—which was, Aurel said, that pride knows when to quit. Who had never once, in eleven years, asked the boy where he came from, because Aurel Vane understood better than any man alive that a person is not their past; a person is what they do with their hands today.

And now the suits were saying he had falsified the inspection reports. That he had signed off on timbering he knew was rotten. That eleven miners dead in the Number Three collapse of the Corvus Deep mine were eleven miners *he* had killed—with a pen, with a signature, with the small daily corruptions of a greedy man.

Mirabel had sat in that gallery and listened, and the terrible thing—the thing he still could not say aloud, could barely say to himself in the privacy of the pilothouse at dawn—was that for the first three days, some poisoned part of him had wondered if it might be true. Not because he believed it. Because the men saying it wore good coats and used words like *evidence*, and he was nobody, a river rat Aurel had fished out of the reeds, and who was he to know anything?

By day five he knew. By day five he had found the paper.

That was the part he never got to. Every time the memory reached for that door, it slammed. Not because he'd forgotten—he had the paper's contents burned into him like a brand—but because remembering the finding of it meant remembering everything after, and everything after was the reason he stood alone at a wheel at dawn measuring his life in miles.

The paper had proven Aurel innocent. And it had vanished before he could put it in front of anyone who mattered. And Aurel Vane had gone to prison for eleven years—one year for each miner—and Mirabel had run.

Not immediately. That was the shame of it, the sediment at the bottom of everything. He had stayed through the verdict. He had stood in the courthouse square while Aurel was taken away in a wagon, and the old man had looked for him in the crowd—Mirabel had seen him looking, that long gray face turning, turning—and Mirabel had stepped back, into the shadow of a feed store, and let the wagon pass without him.

Because the men who took the paper had seen his face. Because staying meant becoming the next name in a courtroom. Because he was nineteen and afraid, and fear had made his choice for him before courage finished lacing its boots.

He had told himself he would come back. That he'd find the proof again, that he'd build a case, that he'd return in a year with lawyers and money and the truth laid out like a chart. He had told himself that story for maybe the first hundred miles. After that, the miles themselves became the story. One more trip. One more season. The river was always about to give him time, and time was always about to give him courage, and neither ever paid up.

Twelve years. Aurel would be out now, if he lived. Or he would be dead in a prison yard, and Mirabel wouldn't even know which, because knowing would require asking, and asking required a name, and he had buried his name the way you sink a body—with weight enough that it stays down.

"Captain."

Bram's voice, from the galley hatch. Mirabel blinked and the pewter river was gold-edged, the mist burning off, the world returned.

"Coffee," Bram said. "And you've been standing there talking to yourself with your face."

"I don't talk to myself."

"You do. Quiet, though. Like a man arguing with somebody who ain't there." Bram set the mug on the ledge beside the wheel—a chipped enamel thing, black and bitter as tar—and looked at Mirabel a moment longer than coffee required. Bram had a face like a root vegetable and eyes that missed nothing, which was an unfortunate combination in a cook. "Passenger kept me up," he offered. "Coughing. I made him willow bark tea. He drank it and thanked me and called me *sir*, which nobody's done since my mother died."

"He's polite."

"He's dying." Bram said it flatly, without cruelty, the way he'd report a fouled propeller. "I've cooked for dying men before. On the coast, before. They get polite near the end. All their meanness burns off first." He shrugged his enormous shrug. "Anyway. Coffee."

He went back below, singing something mournful in his foreign tongue, and Mirabel drank the coffee and did not argue with anybody who wasn't there.

* * *

Midmorning, he went aft to check on the passenger, because that was the captain's job, and not for any other reason.

Elias Ines lay propped on pillows that Pao had stolen—borrowed—from somewhere, a thin man made mostly of angles and dust-colored skin, his hands resting on the blanket like two dried fish. His eyes were open. They were clear eyes, which surprised Mirabel; dying men usually had fogged ones. These were patient eyes. Eyes that had already read to the last page and were just waiting politely for the rest of the world to catch up.

"Captain Montrose." The old man smiled. "Or do you prefer just Captain? I notice nobody aboard uses your first name."

"It's on the registry. That's all it's for."

"A man who keeps his name locked in a drawer." Ines coughed, recovered, waved off the concern Mirabel hadn't shown. "My daughter does the same thing. Did. When she was small she had a box under her floorboard and she'd sit on it when company came. Never told her what was in it. I never asked." He looked at Mirabel with those clear eyes. "You sit on your box very well, Captain. But you should know that sitting on a box makes a shape. Anyone watching can see where it is."

"I'll keep that in mind."

"The gorge," Ines said. "How many miles?"

"From here to the claim's nearest landing? Hundred and forty, if the map's honest. Forty of it gorge."

"And the monsoon?"

"Ten days out, maybe. Less if the mountains catch it early. When it comes, the gorge fills. River goes from rapids to a millrace. Nothing runs it. Nothing floats out. You're sealed in until it drops, weeks later, and by then—" He stopped. There was no need to finish it. A dying man sealed in a flooded gorge needed no arithmetic.

Ines nodded slowly, as if Mirabel had confirmed something he'd already written down. "Then we're on time," he said, "if we leave tomorrow."

"*We.*"

"I've hired a boat, Captain. You voted." The ghost of a smile. "Your crew. Interesting custom, your crew voting. Most boats are little tyrannies. Yours is a republic of strays."

"They're not strays."

"No?" Ines closed his eyes, settling deeper into the pillows. "An engineer who flinches at the word *foreman*. A deckhand who signs his pay over to a family he won't visit. A cook who came upriver one day with a knife scar and no explanation and has been feeding everyone ever since, as if feeding were a penance." He opened one eye. "I'm a debt collector of sorts myself, Captain. I collect debts owed to the dead. I know a stray when I see one. I raised one."

Something cold walked down Mirabel's spine.

"You said you were a prospector."

"I said a lot of things. Prospector's true enough. I dug holes for forty years." He turned his face toward the small porthole, toward the green sliding light of the riverbank. "But before that, I kept records. Mines keep records, Captain. Everything that happens underground gets written down by someone. The question is only whether the writing survives the men who'd rather it didn't."

Mirabel stood very still. The wheel ticked behind him in the current's grip. Somewhere forward, Pao laughed at something Odile said, bright and carrying over the water.

"What kind of records?" he heard himself ask. His voice came out level. Twelve years of practice, buying him that.

Ines didn't answer right away. His breathing had gone thin again, and for a moment Mirabel thought he'd fallen asleep mid-sentence, the way dying men do, dropping off between one word and the next like stepping off a dock. Then:

"Ask me again when we're past the Teeth," the old man murmured. "Some things shouldn't be said in shallow water."

Mirabel went forward. He took the wheel. He steered.

* * *

That evening he allowed himself the ritual. He told no one it was a ritual. As far as the crew knew, the captain simply liked to stand at the rail after supper and look at the water, and captains were entitled to their habits.

But it was a ritual, and rituals are just grief wearing work clothes.

He counted. That was the heart of it. He stood at the rail and he counted the miles behind them the way other men count beads. Two hundred eleven to Vairmont. Sixty-one since Gorse Landing. Nine hundred and change since the season began. And beneath the season, stacked like strata: the years. Twelve of them. He had once tried to compute the total—all the miles, all the seasons, every load of freight and every turn of the wheel—and he had gotten a number so large it meant nothing, and that was the point. That was the mercy of it. Miles added up to nothing. They were the only currency he knew of that could be spent endlessly without ever buying anything, without ever coming due.

Years came due. Years had interest. Twelve years of interest on one step backward in a courthouse square, compounding quietly, waiting.

*Aurel,* he thought, letting the name surface just this once, at night, on the water, where thoughts could dissolve before they drowned anyone. *Old man. Are you alive? Do they treat you—*

No. He shut the ledger on that page. Because the next question—the question under the question—was the one he could not afford: *If you're alive, what do I say to you? Sorry I hid? Sorry I let them take you while I learned to read sandbars?*

Sorry wasn't a cargo. It couldn't be hauled. It just sat in the hold getting heavier.

The stars were out hard and close over the eastern ridge. Ahead, faint in the last light, the walls of the Serein Gorge showed as a black seam in the hills—the river narrowing, gathering itself, preparing to become something faster and less forgiving. Tomorrow they'd reach the mouth of it. The day after, the Widow's Teeth.

"Captain." Pao, at his elbow, silent as always in his rope-soled feet. The boy nodded downstream. "Light. On the water. Been there ten minutes. Thought you'd want to see."

Mirabel followed his pointing hand.

Far back, at the edge of the dark, a lantern burned low on the river. Not a fishing light—fishing lights bobbed and wandered. This one held steady, holding their wake, keeping pace, patient as a debt.

It was too far to make out the hull. Too far to see the flag.

But Mirabel had spent twelve years learning to read water, and water had taught him this much: nothing follows a boat upriver at night unless it wants something.

He watched the lantern until his eyes ached, and beside him Pao watched too, and neither of them said the word *trouble*, because naming it would make it cargo, and cargo had to be carried the whole way.

Behind them, muffled through the bulkhead, Elias Ines began to cough again.
