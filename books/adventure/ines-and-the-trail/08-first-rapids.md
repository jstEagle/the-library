## 8. First Rapids

Odile Vann had a theory about rivers that she never shared with anyone, because sharing it would have required conversation, and conversation was not her preferred medium. The theory was this: a river was just an engine with no parts you could tighten. Water moved the way steam moved, the way pistons moved—pressure seeking release, energy looking for the path of least resistance and taking it whether you liked it or not. Every rapid was a mechanical failure waiting to be read. Every eddy was a bearing running dry. If you understood machines, you understood water, and if you understood water, you understood why people drowned: they kept trying to argue with the physics.

She stood in the engine room of the *Magpie's Rest* at first light, palm flat against the boiler casing, feeling the heartbeat of the boat come up through the steel. Sixty-two pounds of pressure. Steady. She'd coaxed the fire through the night herself rather than trust Bram to bank it properly—the man cooked like an angel and stoked like a drunkard throwing coal at a wall—and now the whole boat hummed beneath her feet with the particular note she'd spent three years tuning it to.

Above her, through the deck planking, came the sounds of morning: Pao's bare feet slapping the boards as he ran the rail lines, Bram swearing cheerfully at something in the galley, the creak of cargo being shifted. And under all of it, growing louder by the minute, the river.

The Serein had changed its voice overnight.

Odile had heard it from her bunk before dawn—a low, continuous rumble, like a wagon train crossing distant gravel—and she'd lain there cataloguing it the way other people counted sheep. *Widow's Teeth,* the charts said, six miles upstream where the gorge pinched to half its width and the river dropped eleven feet over a quarter mile of granite teeth. Eleven feet didn't sound like much. Eleven feet over a quarter mile meant the water arrived at the bottom going fast enough to fold a boat like tin.

She climbed the ladder into grey light and found Mirabel already at the wheel, studying the water the way he always did, which was to say the way a man studies a letter he's been dreading. His hands rested on the spokes without gripping them. That was the thing about Mirabel Montrose that Odile had learned early and never mentioned: he touched the wheel like he was listening to it, not steering it.

"Pressure's good," she said. "Sixty-two."

He nodded without turning. "You hear it?"

"Heard it in my sleep." She came up beside him and looked downstream, though the Teeth were still hidden behind the bend. "Eleven-foot drop?"

"Twelve, this time of year. The gauge at Port Lachlan said the spring melt's still coming down off the high country." He finally glanced at her, and there was something in his face she rarely saw—something almost like apology. "I'd have gone around if there was a around."

"There isn't."

"There isn't," he agreed.

From the stern came voices. Elias Ines sat wrapped in two blankets despite the warming air, propped against the cargo crates he refused to let anyone open, his daughter beside him. Ines—that wasn't her real name, Odile was nearly certain; nobody who booked passage under a false name picked one so close to their father's, which meant either she was careless or she wanted them to know, and Odile hadn't decided which worried her more—Ines was watching the water too. But differently. Not like Mirabel listened to it. Like she was memorizing it.

* * *

Mirabel called the crew together at the bend above the rapids, and Odile stood with her arms crossed while he laid it out in his flat, unhurried voice, pointing at features in the churning water below as if introducing guests at a party.

"The main channel runs left of center until the second tooth, then it kicks right hard. There's a hole behind the third tooth big enough to swallow us stern-first if we're slow on the correction. After that it's read-and-react for a quarter mile." He looked at each of them in turn. "Pao, you're on the bow line. If I point right, throw right. If I point left, don't ask questions. Bram, you're ballast—center of the boat, low, hands on whatever won't move. Odile—"

"I know my job."

"Tell me anyway."

She sighed. "Throttle answers to your hand through mine. You call for power, I give it. You call for none, I cut it clean. No heroics from the engine room."

"No heroics from anywhere," Mirabel said. "That's the whole religion of running whitewater. You don't fight the river. You negotiate."

It was then that Ines spoke up from where she'd been standing slightly apart, arms folded, that watchful look on her face. "And what do we do?" She tipped her head toward her father, gray-faced against the crates. "He can't hold a line. I'm not leaving him loose on the deck."

"You'll be at the stern rail," Mirabel said. "Both hands. When I tell you to move, you move. Otherwise you're dead weight, and dead weight stays put."

Something flickered across her face—not quite anger, not quite acceptance. "And him?"

"Elias rides amidships, strapped to the mast partner. He'll hate it. He'll live."

They rigged it while the rumble grew from a rumor into a fact. Odile went below and stood at her throttle, one hand on the brass lever, one on the pressure gauge, and listened to the boat change character around her. The hull ticked as the water temperature dropped. The current began to tug at them even in the slack water above the drop, gentle as a pickpocket testing a pocket. Through the engine room's open hatch she could see a slice of sky and the tops of the granite fins rising ahead, wet and black and fanged, and between them the river stood up in ridges and folded over itself with a sound like sustained applause from ten thousand hands.

"Odile." Mirabel's voice down the speaking tube, calm as a man ordering coffee. "Half ahead."

She gave him half. The *Magpie's Rest* leaned into the current, and the negotiation began.

* * *

The first tooth took them by surprise only in the sense that reading about a punch is different from receiving it.

The channel did exactly what Mirabel had promised—it ran left, then the water hit the submerged fin and kicked right with a violence that laid the boat over fifteen degrees and sent everything not lashed down sliding. Odile rode the throttle through it, knees bent, weight low, giving him power when he called it and cutting it when he didn't, and through the deck above her she could feel the whole choreography: Pao's boots hammering to the bow, the line snapping taut, Bram's cheerful voice gone silent and serious, the groan of timber flexing the way good timber flexes when it's built by someone who hated shortcuts.

"Right line! Hard right!" Mirabel's voice, still level, but faster now.

She gave him full ahead. The engine answered with a surge she felt in her back teeth, and the boat swung its nose toward the gap between the second and third teeth—a gap that looked, from her angle, approximately the width of a coffin standing on end.

They went through it sideways-ish, which was apparently correct, because Mirabel didn't scream, and then the hole behind the third tooth reached up for them.

Odile saw it through the hatch: a rotating pit of green water, smooth as oil in the center, and she understood in one cold flash of engineering clarity that they were going to miss clearing it by exactly enough to matter. The stern dropped. The propeller bit air instead of water and screamed a note no propeller should scream. The whole boat slewed, and for one long second they were a machine with no purchase on anything, falling sideways toward the whirlpool—

"POWER!"

She gave it everything. All sixty-two pounds, every ounce of steam the night's fire had banked, the governor crying and her hand holding the lever past its detent because physics did not care about detents today. The propeller found water again, grabbed, and hauled. The stern swung. The hole sucked at them, missed by a yard, maybe less, and the *Magpie's Rest* shot out past the last of the teeth into the long read-and-react water beyond, wallowing, shipping green spray over the rails, alive.

Above her, whooping. Pao, hanging off the bow line, laughing like a boy at a fair. Even Bram made a sound Odile had never heard from him—something between a prayer and a cheer.

And Mirabel's voice down the tube, quiet again already: "Good work. Watch your pressures. We're not done."

They weren't done. The next quarter mile was a series of arguments, each one smaller than the last but each one demanding attention—the boat threading between rocks that appeared out of the foam with no warning, Mirabel calling lines in that same level voice, Odile answering with the throttle, the two of them conducting a conversation in power and absence-of-power that needed no words. By the time the gorge opened and the water lay down again, her shirt was soaked through with sweat and spray both, and her hands ached from gripping the lever, and she felt wonderful.

* * *

It was afterward, in the flat water below the Teeth, that Ines earned whatever it was she earned.

Odile was on deck checking the paddlebox bearings when she heard the splash—not alarming, just a bucket going in—and turned to see Ines at the stern rail hauling up riverwater to douse her father's forehead. Elias had gone gray during the run, properly gray, the color of a man whose body was filing complaints his pride wouldn't let him voice, and he lay now against the crates with his eyes closed while his daughter cooled him with the efficiency of someone who had done nursing before, or farming, or some other work where you learned that care was mostly logistics.

But it was the next thing that made Odile stop with her grease rag in her hand.

"You corrected late on the second tooth," Ines said. She wasn't looking at Mirabel, who stood at the wheel twenty feet away. She was looking at the water. "You knew the kick was coming—I could see you set up for it—but you held the left line longer than seemed safe. Why?"

Odile braced for Mirabel to shut it down. He had a talent for shutting things down; it might have been his defining feature. Instead he was quiet a moment, and then he said, "What did the water look like where I held?"

Ines frowned at the river. "Faster. Smoother on top. There were—lines in it. Dark streaks running straight."

"Tongues." Mirabel nodded slowly. "Deep water runs smooth and dark. Shallow water shows you the rocks underneath—it goes green, or it breaks white. I held the left line because the dark tongue was the deep channel, and the deep channel was the only place wide enough for our draft." He turned and looked at her fully for the first time since she'd come aboard. "Most passengers see white water and panic at the white. The white is just water telling you where it's shallow. The dangerous part is the part that looks easy."

Ines absorbed this the way Odile imagined she absorbed everything—silently, completely, with some internal ledger being updated. "So the smooth water is the lie."

"The smooth water is the truth wearing a boring face." Something moved at the corner of Mirabel's mouth. It might, under laboratory conditions, have qualified as the beginning of a smile. "Where did you learn to look at water?"

"Flood year, when I was twelve. Our town lost the bridge. I watched the river take it apart for three days." She wrung out the bucket cloth without looking at it. "I've hated it ever since. But I've watched it."

"Hating it and watching it," Mirabel said. "That's most of the education."

He went back to his wheel. Ines went back to her father. And Odile stood there with her rag, thinking that in three years aboard the *Magpie's Rest* she had seen Mirabel Montrose give perhaps four sentences of instruction to any living soul, and he'd just handed this lying woman with the false name a free lesson in the only subject he actually cared about.

Interesting, she thought, in the way she thought about anomalous gauge readings. Interesting, and worth monitoring.

* * *

The afternoon settled into the long steady labor of making miles, and Odile went below to do the post-rapids ritual: inspection, tightening, gratitude. The *Magpie's Rest* had taken the Teeth well, better than she had any right to expect from a hull her age, but whitewater always collected its audit afterward. She worked her way forward checking seams, and it was in the forward hold, near the crates, that she paused.

Bram was there. Crouched by the largest crate, ostensibly re-securing a lashing that didn't need re-securing, his head tilted at an angle Odile recognized from a hundred galley conversations—the angle of a man listening to something interesting.

"That crate's been talking to you all day," she said.

Bram started, then grinned up at her, unrepentant as sunrise. "It shifted in the rapids. I'm just making sure it's snug." He patted the lid. "Snug as a coffin."

"It's survey equipment or I'm a nun," Odile said. "Elias told Mirabel as much."

"Mm." Bram's grin stayed but his eyes didn't. "Survey equipment. For a gold claim." He tapped the crate once, softly, like knocking on a door he hadn't been invited to knock on. "Funny kind of survey gear, though. Sealed, chained, and heavy as sin at the bottom and light as lies at the top. Whatever's in there, it's packed by somebody who cared more about the bottom layer than the top."

Odile looked at the crate. She was an engineer; she understood loading, mass distribution, the grammar of how things were packed. Bram was right. The bottom-heavy configuration meant dense material below—metal, probably, or paper in quantity, protected by lighter padding above. Nobody packed ore samples that carefully. Ore samples were rocks. Rocks didn't need armor.

"Not our business," she said.

"Never said it was." Bram stood, brushed off his knees, and smiled his kitchen smile, warm and utterly unreadable. "Just saying the boat talks to me. Crates talk too, if you listen."

He wandered aft toward the galley, humming. Odile lingered a moment longer, one hand resting on the crate's lid, feeling through the wood the faint vibration of the engine and the fainter vibration of the river beneath everything, and told herself—as she had told herself about engines her whole life—that a machine would tell you the truth if you knew how to listen, and that whatever was packed in the bottom of this crate was heavier than gold.

Gold, after all, was just metal. Metal didn't need to be hidden from the crew of a boat that had voted to carry it.

She went back to her engine room, fired the boiler up to cruising pressure, and tried very hard not to listen.

Through the hull, all the same, the river went on talking—steady, patient, carrying them deeper into the gorge with every mile, toward whatever waited at the end of a dying man's map. And somewhere behind them, far back down the water they'd already run, Odile would remember later, she had glimpsed a smudge on the southern horizon that she'd taken for smoke from a trapper's fire.

It was moving. Smoke from a trapper's fire didn't keep pace with a boat.

But that realization was still a day away, sleeping in the back of her mind like pressure building in a cold boiler, and the evening came on golden and calm, and Ines stood at the stern rail learning the water, and the *Magpie's Rest* ran on into the narrowing gorge with its secrets lashed tight to its deck.
