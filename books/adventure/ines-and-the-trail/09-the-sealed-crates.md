## 9. The Sealed Crates

Bram had a rule about other people's food and another rule about other people's secrets, and the second one was simpler: leave them in the pot they came in. Stir them and you'd only taste what you weren't meant to.

He'd held to that rule for eleven years aboard the *Kestrel's Wake*, through three captains before Mirabel and two floods and one season when the boat ran gunpowder upriver for men who never looked him in the eye. He knew what sealed cargo meant. Sealed cargo meant the owner didn't trust you, and the correct response was not to earn that trust but to stop caring whether you had it. Curiosity was a hunger, and hunger was something Bram understood better than most men, having spent his boyhood acquainted with it, and he also knew that the hungriest thing in any kitchen was the cook who tasted everything.

So it was a failure of discipline, plain and simple, when he opened the first crate on the third night out of port.

He would tell himself afterward that it started as housekeeping. That was even true, in the way most lies are true. The crates sat amidships under the cargo tarp where they'd been lashed since Cast Off, four of them, squat and pine-slat and stenciled with no name at all—which was itself a kind of name, because everything else on the *Wake* wore its history like paint. The crates said nothing. They just sat there being heavy, and heavy things on a shallow-draft riverboat were the captain's problem, which made them Bram's problem too, since Bram had appointed himself the keeper of everything Mirabel forgot, which was nearly everything except the river.

The tarp had worked loose at its aft corner where Pao had tied it off in a hurry, and the evening rain—it wasn't monsoon yet, but the air had begun practicing for it, short warm showers that came down like applause and stopped just as suddenly—had found the gap and soaked into the top slats of the uppermost crate. Water swelling pine is a slow violence. By the time Bram noticed it, coming off the galley with a pot of night tea for Odile in the engine room, the corner of the crate had gone soft as bread.

He set the tea down on an ammo can. He told himself he was checking for rot.

The lid gave along the swollen seam with a sound like a knuckle cracking, and the smell that came out of it stopped his hand mid-lift.

Not gold. He'd smelled gold once, or rather he'd smelled the mercury and sweat and despair around it, back in the years he didn't talk about, and this wasn't that. This was paper and machine oil and something mineral and cold, like the air off a cave mouth. Like stone that hadn't seen sun in a long time.

Bram stood there in the rain-soft dark with the lid raised six inches and his own reflection faintly visible in the wet varnish of the deckhouse glass behind it, and he heard Mirabel's voice from the day they cast off, flat as a sounding line: *We don't open what we don't ask about.*

But Mirabel hadn't asked about these crates either. That was the thing Bram kept turning over like a stone in his pocket. The old man had paid triple fare and asked for silence, and Mirabel—who read every manifest twice, who weighed cargo personally with a hand scale like a suspicious mother weighing flour—had let those crates come aboard without so much as lifting a corner of the tarp. Either the captain had gone soft, or the captain already knew, and both possibilities were worse than curiosity.

Bram lifted the lid the rest of the way.

* * *

Inside, packed in wood shavings gone gray with damp, lay instruments. Not mining tools—that much he knew even with his limited education in such matters. No drills, no blasting caps, no pans or sluice boxes. These were delicate things, wrapped in oilcloth like surgery: a transit level in a fitted leather case, its brass tube greened at the joints with age; a box of glass vials nested in straw, each stoppered with wax, each holding a thumb of grayish dust labeled in a small precise hand—*Adit face, 40 ft; Adit face, 90 ft; Lower gallery, timber line.* A folding rule of ivory and brass. A camera, bellows-collapsed, heavier than it looked, with a leather strap worn shiny by someone's neck. And beneath all of it, wrapped separately, a ledger bound in oilskin, tied with cord, and Bram did not touch the ledger. Even with the lid open and the rain starting again and his heart going like a drum in a parade, he did not touch the ledger. Some lines you don't cross until you've decided which side of them you live on.

He crouched there a long time, longer than was safe, listening to the river slap the hull and Odile's engine turning over steady below like a big animal digesting. Survey instruments. Sample vials. A camera. Men who went looking for gold carried pans and greed. Men who carried transits and labeled dust were men who intended to prove something—to measure it, fix it in place, make it undeniable. You surveyed a claim to establish ownership. But you also surveyed a disaster site.

The thought arrived whole, the way the worst thoughts do, without knocking.

He closed the lid gently, pressed the swollen seam back together as best he could, retied the tarp with a proper bowline instead of Pao's hurried granny knot, and picked up Odile's tea, which had gone cold in the ammo can. He carried it below anyway.

"Tea's cold," Odile said, taking it without looking up from her pressure gauges. She was a small woman built entirely of forearms and opinions, and she could hear a bearing going bad from forty feet the way other people heard their names called. She took a sip of the cold tea, grimaced on principle, drank more of it. "You look like a man who's seen a ghost."

"Just rain," Bram said.

"Mm." Odile turned a valve a quarter-turn. "Rain doesn't put that look on a man. I've seen rain."

Bram wiped down the galley that night long after the crew had turned in, wiping surfaces that were already clean, and thought about the crates, and thought about the old man coughing in the passenger berth, the wet rattle in him like gravel shifting in a pan. Elias Ines. Dying man. Triple fare. Silence requested and given. And now instruments for proving things, packed by careful hands, labeled in a careful script, riding up a gorge that would seal itself behind them in a matter of weeks.

Bram had run from something once. He recognized the freight.

* * *

The trouble with suspicion, he reflected over the next two days, was that it seasoned everything. He couldn't fry breakfast without wondering if the old man's appetite was failing because of the sickness or because of fear. He couldn't hand Ines her coffee—black, no sugar, she took it like a woman punishing herself for something—without studying her face for whatever she knew and wasn't saying. And she wasn't saying plenty; Bram had cooked for enough families to know the weather between a father and daughter, and theirs was squall country. She watched Elias the way you watch a pot you're afraid will boil over. He watched her back with something worse than guilt. Recognition, maybe. Or apology rehearsed so many times it had worn smooth and could no longer be spoken aloud.

On the fifth morning Bram caught himself counting the crates again. Four. He was sure it had been four. He counted them twice more while pretending to check the lashings, and got four both times, and hated himself for the relief he felt, and then hated himself more for feeling relieved, as if the number mattered, as if anything about this arrangement could be made right by arithmetic.

It was Pao who loosened him up, in the way Pao loosened everyone, without meaning to and without effort. The boy came aft that afternoon where Bram was salting a brace of river fish on the stern deck, and sat cross-legged on the hatch cover, and watched the gorge walls slide past with his chin on his fists.

"Bram," Pao said. "What's in the crates?"

"Ask your captain."

"I did. He said ask Mr. Ines. Mr. Ines said ask my daughter. Miss Ines said—" Pao screwed up his face, doing the impression, "—'none of your business, and none of mine either, apparently.' So now everybody's asked everybody and nobody's answered anybody, which means either it's nothing or it's everything." He plucked a strip of salt fish off the board and chewed it. "Which is it?"

"You're fourteen," Bram said. "Aren't you supposed to be afraid of things?"

"I'm fifteen. And I'm afraid of lots of things. Snakes. The Widow's Teeth. Odile when the pressure gauge does the thing." He held out his hand for more fish, and Bram gave it to him, because refusing Pao food was against some law older than any of them. "I'm mostly afraid we're carrying something that's going to get us killed, and everybody knows what it is except me."

Bram looked at the boy—at the sharp wrists, the secondhand shirt, the way he flinched, still, after five months aboard, whenever a door slammed—and felt the old familiar ache of the found family doing what it did, which was make you responsible for people you'd have died for anyway. Pao had come aboard with nothing but a stolen knife and a story about an uncle in the delta who was mostly invented. Nobody aboard asked him to improve the story. That was the deal. That was always the deal.

"There's instruments," Bram said. "Surveying gear. Sample vials. A camera."

Pao considered this. "That's not treasure stuff."

"No."

"That's—" The boy frowned, working it. "That's court stuff."

Bram looked at him. Fifteen years old, delta-raised, half-educated, and he'd gotten there in four seconds flat, faster than Bram had, though Bram suspected Bram had gotten there too and simply refused to say the word even inside his own skull. Court stuff. Evidence. A dying man hauling proof up a river toward a place the river itself wanted to bury.

"Don't say that word loud," Bram said.

"To who? Everybody's already thinking it." Pao licked salt off his thumb. "Even the captain. Especially the captain. Have you seen how he looks at those crates? Like they owe him money."

* * *

That night the rain came back and stayed, a fine persistent drizzle rather than a shower, the kind that got into everything and made the whole boat whisper. Bram couldn't sleep. He told himself it was the weather, then told himself it was the beans, then gave up lying to himself, which was the one luxury he allowed.

He went forward past the galley, past the crew bunks where Odile snored like a piston and Pao slept curled tight as a fiddlehead, and stood in the drizzle looking at the tarp. It was tied properly now, with his own bowline. Nothing had gotten in. Nothing needed checking.

He checked anyway.

And it was while he stood there, hands on the wet rope, that he heard voices from the passenger berths—not words, just the low music of them, Elias's rasp and Ines's lower register, rising and falling, rising and falling, and once, briefly, Ines's voice climbing to something hard and quick before it cut off, and the silence after it was worse than the argument. Bram stood in the rain and listened to a family fail to tell each other the truth, and thought: *that's what's in the crates.* Not instruments. Not samples. The instruments were just the shape the truth took when you tried to carry it. Twelve years of it, packed in shavings, labeled in a careful hand, riding upriver in the belly of a boat full of liars.

Because that was the other thing he'd figured out, standing over those vials with their little labels. *Adit face, 40 feet.* Somebody had written those labels standing in a mine. Somebody had scraped that dust from stone by lamplight, stoppered it, waxed it, carried it out. And then somebody had buried it or hidden it or run from it for long enough that the man who carried it now was coughing his lungs out on a stranger's boat, spending triple fare and a dead man's urgency to get it back before the water closed over everything.

Bram untied nothing. He opened nothing further. He stood in the drizzle until his shoulders were soaked and his teeth ached, and then he went back to his bunk and lay staring at the overhead boards and did the arithmetic he'd been avoiding.

Four crates. One opened. Three unopened, and one of them—he'd felt the weight of it when he'd shifted the tarp, he was sure of it now, sure the way you're sure of a wrong note in a song—one of them was heavier than survey gear had any business being. Metal, a lot of it, packed dense. He'd assumed strongbox. Now he wondered.

And underneath all of it, the question he'd been circling for five days finally landed and stayed:

Why did Mirabel let them aboard?

Bram had served under captains who'd throw a man overboard for an unlabeled barrel of spirits. Mirabel Montrose ran his boat like a monk runs a chapel—everything named, everything known, everything in its place, and nothing, *nothing*, came aboard that he hadn't turned over twice. Except these crates. Except the old man's silence, purchased without haggling. Except the way Mirabel had looked at Elias Ines on the dock at Cast Off—not like a fare, not like a burden. Like a letter he'd been dreading.

Bram fell asleep somewhere in that thought and dreamed of kitchens, which was where he dreamed of the past, and woke before dawn with the taste of smoke in his mouth that wasn't from any stove.

He was up early, as always, getting the fire laid, and he was grinding coffee when Ines came out onto the deck in the gray light, alone, moving quiet as a burglar in her own family, and stood over the sealed crates with her arms crossed, looking at them the way Bram had looked at them.

Their eyes met across the deck. Two liars, one secret apiece, and neither of them willing to spend it.

"Coffee?" Bram asked.

"Yes," said Ines. "Please."

He poured two cups, and neither of them said a word about the crates, and the river carried them on toward whatever the old man had spent his last miles digging up—and Bram, stirring the pot, understood with cook's certainty that the lid was off now whether anyone admitted it or not. Things simmered until they boiled. It was the oldest law he knew.

By noon, he figured, somebody aboard was going to start asking questions out loud.
