## 10. What Papa Buried

The river had a sound at night that the day never admitted to. By daylight it was all business—slap and churn, the engine's low complaint, Pao's bare feet slapping the deck—but after dark it became something older, a long slow breathing under everything, as if the Serein were asleep with its eyes open. I lay in my narrow berth beneath the wheelhouse and listened to it breathe, and I did not sleep.

Three days upriver now. Three days of watching my father from behind the mask of a false name, and he had not once looked at me the way a man looks at a stranger. That was the cruelest part. He looked *through* me, past me, with those gray eyes gone watery and distant, and some animal part of me kept whispering: *he knows, he has always known, he simply will not say it.* Twelve years of silence between us, and now we shared a boat the length of a church aisle, and neither of us would break it.

I gave up on sleep somewhere past midnight and climbed out into the wet dark. The *Vesper's* deck was slick with river mist, the lanterns hooded to amber coins. Bram had left coffee on the stove—Bram left everything everywhere, a cook who moved through the galley like weather—and I poured a cup I didn't want just to have something warm to hold. The mountains stood along both banks like closed doors. Somewhere above them, the claim was waiting. Somewhere below decks, in the sealed crates he would not let anyone touch, whatever he'd actually found was waiting too.

I found him aft, wrapped in a blanket against the rail, coughing into his fist with that deep wet rattle that had been getting worse since Cast Off. He didn't startle when I sat beside him. Maybe he'd heard me coming. Maybe he'd been listening for footsteps for twelve years.

"Couldn't sleep either," he said. His voice came out sanded down, half its old weight. "The river talks too much."

"You used to say that about Mother."

A pause. The lantern light caught the edge of his jaw, the stubble gone more white than gray. "Did I?"

"Don't." I set the cup down hard enough to slosh. "Don't do that. Don't pretend you don't remember her, or the house, or the winter you left. You're dying, Papa. Not senile. Dying. There's a difference, and I've had three days on this boat to learn it."

He turned his head and looked at me then—really looked, for the first time since I'd boarded under a name that wasn't mine—and what crossed his face wasn't surprise. It was relief, terrible and naked, like a man taking off a pack he'd carried so long his shoulders had grown around it.

"Mira," he said. My real name. It sounded strange in his mouth, rusted at the hinges. "You booked passage under 'Ruth Calloway.' Terrible alias. Ruth Calloway is a widow in Fairhaven who sells hats."

"I know. I bought the hat shop's letterhead to forge the reference." I hadn't meant to confess that; it came out anyway, and something almost like laughter moved through his chest before it collapsed into coughing. I waited, hating how practiced I'd already become at waiting through the fits, counting the seconds until he could breathe again.

"How long have you known?" I asked when the fit passed.

"Since the gangplank." He wiped his mouth with the back of his hand. "You walk like your mother. She never once entered a room quietly in her life. And you hold a cup the same way—like it owes you money."

I laughed despite myself, and the laugh cracked something, and then I was crying, furious about crying, staring out at the black water because I could not look at him while I did it. The river breathed on beneath us, indifferent.

"Twelve years," I said. "Twelve years, and you send nothing. No letters. No word through anyone. Mother died not knowing if you were alive or dead or in prison, and she went to her grave rehearsing the speech she'd give you if you ever walked back through the door. I know because I heard her practicing it. At night. Through the wall." My voice steadied as it hardened, which was always the way with me. "And now you show up coughing your lungs into a handkerchief, with a map and a fortune you can't spend, and you hire a stranger's boat because you can't ask your own daughter for a ride. So tell me why. Tell me what's in those crates. Tell me what you found up there that was worth more than coming home."

He didn't answer right away. He pulled the blanket tighter, and for a moment he seemed to shrink inside it, the big frame of him—the man who'd once carried me four miles on his shoulders when I sprained my ankle at the fair—reduced to bones and canvas and breath.

"The claim is real," he said finally. "That much I'll say. There's gold up there. Enough of it that men would kill for the paper that says it's mine."

"But that's not why we're going."

"No."

"Papa—"

"Elias." He said it gently but firmly, correcting me the way he used to correct my grip on a fishing rod. "Out here I'm Elias. A passenger. If the crew learns who you are to me, they learn things I'm not ready for them to know, and neither are you. Please. Indulge a dying man one last piece of stubbornness."

"That's all you've ever given me. Pieces."

The words landed. I saw them land, saw them go in under his ribs where no blanket could reach, and I hated myself a little and said them anyway, because I had carried them twelve years and they had gotten heavy.

He looked down at his hands. They were ruined hands, miner's hands, knuckles swollen like walnut burls, and I remembered them smaller and stronger, turning a whetstone, teaching me to sharpen a knife when I was nine because he said every person should know how to make a dull thing useful again.

"When I left," he said slowly, "I told your mother I'd found something worth more than the mine. That was true. But it wasn't gold, Mira. And it wasn't a thing I could bring home. It was a thing I had to *bury*, and burying it took me away from everything I—" His voice snagged. He started again. "Some discoveries aren't treasure. Some discoveries are wounds. You find them anyway, because the ground opens, because you're the one holding the shovel, and then you spend the rest of your life deciding what to do with what came out of the hole."

"What came out of the hole?"

He was quiet so long I thought he wouldn't answer. Mist beaded on the rail. Forward, near the bow, I heard Odile's soft murmur through the engine-room hatch—she slept down there sometimes, among her machines, I'd learned—and the creak of the wheelhouse where Mirabel Montrose stood his endless watch, a man made entirely of locked rooms.

"Eleven men," Elias said at last, so quietly I had to lean close to hear him over the water. "Eleven men died up there, twenty years ago. The company called it an accident. A seam of gas, a spark, bad luck. Everyone believed it because everyone wanted to believe it, because the alternative was believing that men we knew—men who sat in our kitchens—had let it happen." His hands worked against each other in his lap. "I was young then. I dug for that company. And twelve years ago I went back, up past the flooded adit, following a hunch and a map I'd copied off a dead man, and I found proof. Records. Ledgers. Photographs. Things with names on them."

My heart was going hard. "Names of whom?"

"Men who knew the shaft was dangerous. Men who were warned—warned in writing, weeks before—and sent those eleven down anyway because sealing the level cost less than shoring it." His breath caught, and this time it wasn't the sickness. "They covered it up. They burned what they could, bought what they couldn't, buried the rest under rock and paperwork. And I found what they missed."

The river breathed. The mountains kept their doors shut.

"So we're not going for gold," I said. "We're going for evidence."

"We're going for the truth, and the gold is just the lock on the door. Nobody guards a grave. Everybody guards a treasure." He smiled without any joy in it. "That's the whole trick, Mira. That's why the crates stay sealed. That's why I hired a captain with no questions and paid in advance. Because if the wrong people learn what's in those boxes before it reaches daylight, we all end up at the bottom of this river, and the eleven stay dead for nothing."

"And the names?" I asked. "Whose names are on the records?"

His face did something I had never seen it do. It closed. All the way closed, like a shop pulling its shutters at dusk, and behind the shutters there was nothing I could read at all.

"Men who are still powerful," he said. "Men whose reach is longer than you'd believe. That's all you need to know tonight."

"It's not all I need to know. It's not even close." I stood, because sitting beside him felt suddenly like complicity, and I didn't want to be comfortable with this. "You want me to help carry your boxes to daylight? Fine. You want me to sit at your deathbed and hear your last words? Fine. But don't hand me half a truth and call it trust. Half a truth is what you gave Mother when you left. Look how well that served us."

"Mira—"

"*Elias,*" I corrected, bitter as alum. "Right. Out here you're a passenger. Well, so am I. And passengers keep their secrets to themselves."

I walked forward along the deck, fast, my boots loud on the planks, angrier at the sting in my eyes than at him. Behind me he didn't call out. He never had. That was the whole history of us in a single fact: he let me walk away, every time, because he believed the leaving protected me, and he had never once asked whether the protection was worse than the wound.

At the bow I stopped and gripped the rail and breathed river air until my chest unclenched.

Eleven men. Ledgers. Names. I turned it over in my mind like ore in a pan, looking for what glittered underneath. He'd told me the shape of it but withheld the heart of it, and the withholding itself told me something—he was protecting someone. Or something. A name he couldn't say even now, even dying, even to me. And a cold little voice in me, a voice I didn't like, wondered whether that name might belong to someone aboard this boat. Whether the reason he'd chosen this particular captain, this particular crew of strays, was written somewhere in those sealed crates.

I looked back aft. He was still at the rail, a humped shadow under his blanket, small against the enormous dark. Watching the way we'd come. Watching, I realized with a chill that had nothing to do with the mist, the way a man watches when he believes something is following him.

I followed his gaze downstream, into the black miles behind us, and saw nothing. Of course I saw nothing. Just water, and mist, and the dark.

But the feeling stayed with me all the way to dawn—that we were being measured by eyes we couldn't see, and that whatever my father had buried, it had not stayed buried. Something had dug it up. Something was coming upriver behind us, patient as the flood season, and my father knew it, and he still wouldn't say the name.

* * *

Morning came gray and windless. I took coffee to the wheelhouse, mostly to escape my own head, and found Mirabel Montrose standing at the wheel with his binoculars raised—not ahead, where a careful captain looks, but astern, fixed on the empty water we'd already crossed.

"Something wrong?" I asked.

He lowered the glasses slowly. Whatever he'd seen—or hadn't—went behind his face and locked there with everything else.

"Smoke," he said. "Far back. Could be a campfire on the bank."

"And could it not be?"

He didn't answer. He turned the wheel a few degrees, unhurried, and the *Vesper* leaned into the current like a horse changing leads, and I understood that the question had frightened him more than any answer would have.

Behind us, faint as a held breath, the smoke kept its distance—and kept coming.
