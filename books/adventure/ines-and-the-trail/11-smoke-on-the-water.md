## 11. Smoke on the Water

Mirabel saw the smoke before he saw the boat.

It came up over the eastern bend of the Serein just after midday, a gray smudge against the pale green wall of the gorge, thin as a pencil line drawn on wet paper. Most people would have taken it for a brushfire, some farmer burning off a field above the waterline. But Mirabel had spent twelve years learning to read the river the way other men read faces, and the river had taught him that nothing up here was ever what it pretended to be.

Brushfire smoke rose straight and then scattered with the wind. This smoke moved *with* them. It kept their pace. It hung at the same distance behind them for two hours, patient as a debt.

He said nothing about it through the noon meal. Bram laid out rice and salted fish on the deck plates, and Pao ate cross-legged with his back against the boiler housing, soaking up warmth like a cat, and Odile came up from the engine room with grease to her elbows and complained about a bearing she didn't trust, and Elias Ines sat wrapped in two blankets in the lee of the pilothouse, coughing into a handkerchief he folded away too quickly each time, as if Mirabel might not notice the color of what it held. And Ines—the daughter, who still hadn't given them a name they believed—sat beside her father and fed him small pieces of fish when his hands shook too badly to manage the fork himself.

A family portrait, Mirabel thought. Almost convincing.

He watched the smoke over the rim of his bowl.

"You're not eating," Ines said. She had a way of watching him sideways, he'd noticed, without turning her head—a habit of surveillance she probably thought was subtle. He recognized it because it was his own.

"I'm reading," he said.

"The river doesn't have words."

"No. But it has grammar." He nodded upstream, casual, a man pointing out scenery. "That bend ahead—the current piles up on the outside of it where the cliff drops shadow. We'll want to hug the left bank coming around or we'll get shoved into the seam. And behind us—" he lifted his chin a fraction, "—somebody's cooking lunch on a stove that burns hotter than mine."

Pao stopped chewing. Odile went very still, which for Odile meant her whole body locked except her eyes, which flicked astern with the speed of a wading bird.

"Smoke?" Bram said. "Where?"

"Two hours back now. Same distance. Same speed." Mirabel set down his bowl. "We're making six knots against a four-knot current. Whatever's back there is making ten."

"Could be anyone," Odile said. "Freighter out of Calloway. Mail boat."

"Mail boats don't run the Serein this time of year. The schedule ends next week." He kept his voice level, unhurried, the voice he used for reporting weather. "And freighters burn coal. That smoke's too dark and too steady. Oil burner. A fast one."

The silence that followed had weight to it. Pao set down his bowl. Bram wiped his hands slowly on his apron, buying time the way cooks do, with motion.

"So we're being followed," Ines said. Not a question. Her father's hand found her wrist and gripped it, and she covered his knuckles with her palm without looking away from Mirabel. "By whom?"

"That," Mirabel said, "is an excellent question I've been asking myself since the bend below the talus slide." He stood, brushing rice from his trousers. "Odile, I want everything she's got. If we need to run, I don't want to be coaxing a cold boiler. Pao, topside, eyes on our friend. Don't stare—just count funnel bands and hull paint and anything else a man could use to identify a boat later. Bram—" he hesitated, then decided honesty cost less than comfort, "—batten the galley. Anything loose goes in a locker. If we take rough water today, I want it taken clean."

"And us?" Ines asked.

"You stay with your father. Keep him low in the lee if there's trouble." He met her eyes, and something passed between them that neither of them named—the understanding of two people who had both spent years scanning horizons for things they hoped never to see. "You've been followed before. I can tell."

Her chin came up a half-inch. "Have you?"

"Once or twice." He turned for the pilothouse. "It doesn't get easier. You just get faster at pretending it hasn't started yet."

* * *

The pilothouse smelled of varnish and old sun, of the brass wheel worn smooth by hands going back forty years, of the chart table where the Serein Gorge unrolled in faded ink. Mirabel spread the chart and studied the next twelve miles with the particular attention he gave to problems he couldn't solve directly.

The river here ran wide and shallow between gravel bars, braiding and re-braiding itself like a rope unraveling. Good water for making time. Bad water for hiding. There was no side channel deep enough for the *Kestrel's* draft until the Narrows, eight miles up, where the gorge pinched to a quarter-mile and the current doubled and the cliffs closed overhead so tight that a man could spit from one wall to the other. If someone wanted to come alongside and board them, the Narrows was where they'd try it—or nowhere, because above the Narrows the water turned mean enough that only a fool or a local would push a boat into it.

He knew the water. Whoever was behind him might know it better.

Through the forward glass he watched Pao climb the cargo netting to the top of the deckhouse and settle there, small and still against the sky, a boy-shaped piece of rigging. Pao was seventeen and had been with Mirabel three years, washed up half-drowned at a fuel dock two hundred miles downstream with no name he'd give and no story he'd tell, and Mirabel had hired him on the spot because he understood exactly what that kind of silence was worth and exactly what it cost. The boy had eyes like a hawk and the patience of driftwood. If anyone could watch without being seen to watch, it was Pao.

Below, Odile had the engine room hatch open and was talking to her machinery in the low, profane, affectionate murmur she used when she was worried. Odile had built that engine twice from salvage and once from memory, and she trusted it more than she trusted any living person, Mirabel included, and he had never once held it against her.

His crew. Three strays and a captain, none of them asked to explain themselves, all of them bound by the oldest contract on the river: you work, you eat, your past stays your own.

And now, riding in the middle of his careful anonymity, a dying prospector with a map on oiled silk and a daughter traveling under a name that wasn't hers, and somewhere behind them a fast boat with an oil burner closing at a knot an hour.

He should have refused the fare. He'd told himself that every morning since Cast Off, standing at this same wheel: *you should have refused.* But the crew had voted, and the vote had gone against him three to one, and there were limits to how far a captain could override the people who had made his exile livable. Besides—and this was the part he admitted only in the dark hours, only to the river—there had been something in Elias Ines's face when he'd offered that impossible fare. Something Mirabel recognized. The look of a man carrying a weight he could no longer put down alone.

The past always found a way to buy passage.

* * *

It was near dusk when Pao slid down the netting and dropped onto the deck, breathless but controlled, the way he did everything.

"Stern paddle-wheeler," he reported, crouched low beside the pilothouse door. "Black hull, white superstructure, red funnel band. Two funnels. Deck guns—not big ones, swivel mounts, but they're mounted. Maybe thirty souls aboard, hard to count. They're running dark now, no lights, but I caught the name painted on the bow when they crossed a sandbar reflection."

Mirabel felt the day tilt, very slightly, the way the deck tilted when the current shifted under the hull. "And?"

"*Corvus.*" Pao pronounced it carefully, having sounded it out. "*Corvus Line.* Painted fresh. And Captain—" the boy hesitated, choosing, "—he came out on the wing once, when we were in the long straight below the Narrows approach. Binoculars. He looked at us a long time."

"Did he see you looking back?"

"He was looking at the wheelhouse. At you, I think. Or trying to." Pao's voice dropped. "Captain—he raised his hat. Like a greeting. Like he knew you."

The word landed in Mirabel's chest and sat there, cold as ballast.

*Like he knew you.*

There were perhaps five men alive who might raise a hat to the man Mirabel Montrose had been before he was Mirabel Montrose, and every one of them was a reason he lived two thousand miles from where he'd been born, running a shallow-draft packet boat under a name he'd taken from a gravestone in a churchyard he'd passed through once, hungry and nineteen, and never forgotten because the dead man had needed a name more than he did.

"Describe him," Mirabel said. His voice came out flat, which took effort.

"Tall. Gray at the temples, black otherwise. Wore it long, tied back. Long coat even in the heat. Stood like—" Pao frowned, searching. "Like a man who's used to rooms going quiet when he walks in."

Something behind Mirabel's ribs turned over. Not recognition—not yet—but its shadow, the shape of a memory pressing against a door he'd nailed shut years ago. Tall. Dark, graying. A man who commanded silence. He had known men like that. He had been raised, in whatever broken way he'd been raised, in the orbit of one.

"Get below," he said. "Tell Odile I want steam up full by midnight. Tell Bram to break out the lamp oil and fill every lantern we own. And tell nobody else what you saw—not the old man, not the girl."

"Why not them?"

Because Elias Ines would understand immediately what a claim-jumping outfit meant, and dying men panic, and panic burns fuel a body doesn't have. Because the daughter would ask questions Mirabel wasn't ready to answer, questions with names in them. Because—

"Because right now the only thing we know for certain," Mirabel said, "is that they haven't shown us what they want. Until they do, everyone aboard gets to sleep tonight believing this is still an adventure. Let them have it. It may be the last night the lie holds."

Pao looked at him for a long moment with those hawk's eyes, and Mirabel saw the boy deciding whether to press, and saw him decide not to—the same silent contract, renewed. Then he slipped below, and the deck was Mirabel's alone.

* * *

Night came down into the gorge like ink poured into a glass. The cliffs went black on either side; the sky narrowed to a river of stars overhead, brilliant and indifferent. Mirabel ran without lights, steering by the sound of the water against the banks and the pale suggestion of the current seams, the way his guardian had taught him—*the river tells you where it is, boy, if you'll shut up long enough to listen*—and the thought of his guardian brought the tall gray-templed figure astern closer, somehow, though the Corvus must have fallen back miles by now.

He heard footsteps on the deck boards and knew them before he turned. Not Pao's quickness, not Odile's deliberate tread. The lighter step of someone who had learned to move quietly in houses where quiet mattered.

Ines came into the pilothouse without invitation and stood at the edge of the lamplight, arms crossed, watching him steer.

"You know who they are," she said.

"I know what they are. A claim-jumping outfit called the Corvus Line. Whether they're after us specifically or just working the river, I can't say yet."

"My father flinched when you said the name. Just now, through the bulkhead—I heard it." Her voice was steady, but he could hear the effort holding it there. "So he knows them. Which means they're not random. Which means whoever's captaining that boat knows what he's carrying." She stepped closer, into the light, and her face was sharp and tired and frightened in the way of people who have decided fear is a luxury. "Captain. Who is he? The man who raised his hat?"

The wheel hummed under Mirabel's palms. Ahead, the Narrows waited, a black slot between blacker walls, the current singing faintly off the rocks like wire under tension.

"I don't know," he said, and it was mostly true, and the part that wasn't true was the part that mattered most. "But I think he knows me. And that, Miss Whoever-You-Are, is a problem I've been running from for twelve years—one that appears to have finally bought a faster boat."

Behind them, far down the black throat of the gorge, a searchlight blinked on—once, twice, three times. Deliberate. Patient. A signal, or a summons.

Mirabel took the wheel and steered for the Narrows, and did not look back.
