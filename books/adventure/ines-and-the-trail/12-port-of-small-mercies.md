## 12. Port of Small Mercies

The town announced itself by smell before it ever showed itself at all: woodsmoke and tar and the sweet rot of fruit left too long in the sun, all of it braided together on a wind that came downriver ahead of us like a scout sent to say we were expected. Pao stood at the bow rail with his elbows on the wet wood and watched Port of Small Mercies assemble itself out of the morning haze—first the church spire leaning like a man listening hard, then the tin roofs catching light in patches, then the long crooked pier with its mooring posts wrapped in old tires, black and bloated as drowned things.

He had been to this port four times in three years. It never changed, which was why he liked it. Towns that changed asked questions about who you used to be.

"Mind the fenders," Odile called from the engine hatch, her voice half-swallowed by machinery. "This pier's held together by prayer and termites."

Pao threw the lines without answering. He had learned early aboard the *Vesper's Grace*—no, that wasn't it, the boat had a plainer name, *Marrow*, named for nothing anyone would explain—that silence was a currency here. Mirabel paid his crew in it. You did your work, you kept your mouth shut about your yesterdays, and nobody looked too hard at your tomorrows. For a boy who had crossed a border in the bottom of a fish truck at fourteen, pressed flat against ice that burned his skin through his shirt, this arrangement was not merely comfortable. It was sacred.

He made fast amidships and looked up to find Ines already on the pier, standing too straight, watching the town the way she watched everything now—with her jaw set like she was chewing on a question she hadn't yet decided to swallow or spit out. Twelve days out of port and she had stopped flinching at the rapids, started sleeping on deck, learned where the coffee pot lived. But she still watched her father's cabin door the way sailors watch weather.

"Pao." Bram came up from the galley wiping his hands on an apron gone gray with use. "List says flour, salt pork, lamp oil, and whatever citrus hasn't gone soft. And quinine if they've got it. Captain didn't say so, but I've been hearing him cough."

"He doesn't cough."

"Bram coughs," Bram said, tapping his own chest. "I know a cough when I hear one hiding."

* * *

Port of Small Mercies earned its name honestly, Pao supposed. It was a town of maybe two hundred people strung along a shelf of mud between the river and a wall of green so dense it looked solid, a place where nothing was asked of you except coin and courtesy. The main street was one street. There was a chandlery, a dry goods store that sold everything else, a cantina with a roof of corrugated tin that turned rain into applause, and a church whose congregation, according to Bram, consisted entirely of river pilots and their regrets.

Mirabel gave Pao the list and the money pouch and told him to take his time. That alone should have warned him. Mirabel never let anyone take their time; time was the one cargo he counted more carefully than fuel.

"Take Pao," Mirabel said. "And—" a small hesitation, barely a hitch in the current of his voice, "—Ines can come along. She should learn what a resupply looks like. We won't see another port before the gorge."

So the three of them went up the pier together into the heat, and Pao carried the list folded in his breast pocket like a letter he was afraid to read aloud.

The chandlery smelled of hemp and kerosene. Its owner was a woman named Sefa with forearms like dock rope and a way of looking at customers that made them confess to purchases they hadn't intended. She filled their order without ceremony—flour, salt pork, oil, a crate of oranges soft at one end—and while she wrote the tally she talked, because Sefa always talked, and Pao had always found her talk pleasant the way rain on a roof is pleasant: ambient, requiring nothing of him.

"You're running up-gorge, then," she said. "Late in the season for it."

"We're late in every season," Pao said. "It's how we keep ahead of our reputations."

Sefa laughed her gravel laugh and then didn't laugh anymore. Her pencil stopped moving. She glanced past Pao's shoulder toward the doorway, where Ines was examining a rack of oilskins, and lowered her voice in a way that made the hair on Pao's arms stand up under his sleeves.

"There were men here," she said. "Two nights back. Off a boat painted black as a beetle, flying no colors I knew. They bought fuel and rope and they asked questions."

"What kind of questions?"

"The kind you don't answer unless you want trouble." Sefa set the pencil down flat, deliberately, the way a dealer lays down a card. "They were asking after a boat going up the Serein. Asking its name, its captain, its crew. And asking—" she paused, and her eyes did something complicated, "—asking whether anyone had come through carrying a map. Not asking for a map, mind. Describing one. Oiled silk, they said. Folded eight ways. A red thread stitched along one edge where the fold wore through."

Pao heard his own heartbeat. Once. Twice. The chandlery seemed to tilt very slightly, the way a boat tilts when someone steps aboard without warning.

Oiled silk. Folded eight ways. A red thread.

He had seen that map exactly once, the night Elias Ines first came aboard, when the old man unrolled it across the chart table with hands that shook like leaves deciding whether to fall. Red thread along the worn fold. Pao had noticed it because his mother had once sewn with thread like that, before the war took the shop and the shop took her, and he had thought: *that is a map somebody loved.*

Nobody outside the crew should have known that thread existed.

"Sefa," he said carefully, "who were these men?"

"Corvus Line," she said. "That's what the boat said on her stern, though the paint was fresh over something older. Their man did the talking. Big fellow, gray at the temples, gloves indoors like he was ashamed of his hands. Polite. That's the worst kind." She resumed writing, faster now, as if she could outrun the conversation. "There was another one with him. Younger. Kept looking at the water like he expected it to do something."

"And what did you tell them?"

"I told them I sell rope and kerosene and I don't sell gossip." Sefa looked up, and for the first time since Pao had known her, she looked old. "But Pao. They weren't only asking me. They'd been to the cantina. To the pilot house. To the church, God help us, asking Father Anselm if any soul had come through headed for the old Ines claim. Somebody in your outfit has been talking. Or somebody who knows your outfit has."

* * *

He walked out of the chandlery with the crate of oranges in his arms and the whole world rearranged around him, familiar streets suddenly full of corners where a man might be standing. The heat pressed down. Somewhere a dog was barking at nothing, or at something, and Pao couldn't decide which was worse.

Ines fell into step beside him. She had bought an oilskin and a paper of needles and she carried both like weapons she hadn't chosen yet.

"You've gone white," she said. "For you that's practically transparent."

"Your father's map," Pao said. He kept walking. He kept his voice level, pitched under the noise of the market stalls, under the gulls fighting over fish guts on the pier. "It's oiled silk. It has a red thread along one fold."

"Yes."

"People in this town are describing it. Two nights ago. Men off a black boat called the Corvus Line."

Ines stopped walking. Pao walked three more steps before he realized and turned back. She stood in the middle of the street with the crowd parting around her the way water parts around a piling, and her face had done something he had never seen it do—not fear exactly, but the thing underneath fear, the load-bearing wall of it.

"They described it," she said. "By the thread."

"By the thread."

"Then whoever told them has *seen* it." Her voice dropped to almost nothing. "Pao, six people have seen that map. My father. Me. Your captain. Your engineer. Your cook. You." She counted them off on her fingers and then closed the hand into a fist, as if she could hold the number still. "And we're twelve days from the last port where my father showed it to anyone."

Pao set the orange crate down on a barrel. His arms felt wrong, too light, as if the information had weight and his body had lost ballast somewhere between the chandlery door and here.

He thought about Bram. Good-hearted Bram, who peeked into sealed crates because curiosity was in his blood like salt, who would sell nothing and betray no one but who talked in his sleep, who told stories in cantinas, who had once—in Pao's presence, in a port much like this one—described a stranger's belongings to a barmaid just to watch her laugh. Bram wouldn't sell them out. Bram would simply *talk*, the way rivers run downhill, without malice and without stopping.

He thought about Odile, who kept her own counsel so completely that after three years none of them knew so much as her mother's name. Odile was a vault. Vaults didn't leak.

He thought about himself, and the thought arrived cold and unwelcome: *every man aboard is innocent until you imagine otherwise, and then everyone is guilty, and that is how paranoia works, it doesn't need evidence, it needs only company.*

"We tell the captain," Ines said. It wasn't a question.

"We tell the captain," Pao agreed. "Carefully. On the boat. Not in the street." He looked up and down the thoroughfare—the fruit seller fanning her bananas, two boys rolling a hoop, an old man asleep under a hat that had given up its shape years ago. Ordinary life, all of it, and none of it trustworthy anymore. "And Ines—we buy what we came for, and we don't linger, and we watch the water."

"Why the water?"

"Because Sefa said the younger one kept looking at it." Pao picked up the crate again. "Like he expected it to do something. Men who expect the river to do something are men waiting for a boat."

* * *

They bought the quinine at the dry goods store, where the proprietor—a narrow man named Tull with spectacles thick as portholes—wrapped the bottle in newspaper and asked no questions, which Pao noted with gratitude and then immediately distrusted, because a man who asks no questions either has none or has already had them answered.

At the cantina, buying bread and hard cheese for the road, Pao made himself small in a corner and listened. This was his oldest skill, older than deckhand work, older than the fish truck: the skill of being furniture. He sat with his back to the wall and his face aimed at his cup and he let the room talk around him.

Mostly it talked about the river rising early in the headwaters, about a bridge washed out two valleys east, about a wedding and a feud and the price of rubber. Ordinary talk. But beneath it, the way a snag lies beneath brown water, there was other talk, quieter, passed between a table of deckhands wearing boots too good for their wages.

"—black hull, I'm telling you, fresh paint over the old name, you could still read it if the sun hit right—"

"—said they'd pay fifty for word of it, fifty, just for *word*, not even the thing itself—"

"—map on silk, some rich man's treasure, and the old fool carrying it's half dead already—"

Pao finished his coffee. He paid. He left without hurrying, because hurrying was a confession, and he walked back down to the pier with the bread under his arm and his mind running the river ahead of him, reading snags that weren't there yet.

Fifty for word. Which meant the Corvus Line didn't know where they were. Which meant the talkers had talked in generalities—*a boat, an old man, a map on silk*—without names or headings. Which meant the leak was shallow, not deep. Someone had spoken to strangers in a port behind them, weeks ago perhaps, before the shape of the journey was fixed. Someone who hadn't understood what they were describing.

Or someone who had understood perfectly and wanted the Corvus to believe they didn't.

Pao stood at the head of the pier and looked at the *Marrow* riding at her lines, and for one long moment he saw her as a stranger might: a working boat, patched and honest, carrying five people and a secret that was already bleeding into the world.

* * *

Mirabel listened without moving. That was the terrible thing about him—Pao had learned it on the first voyage, watching him take news of a broken rudder pin—he listened the way stone listens to rain, absorbing everything, giving back nothing until he chose to.

They gathered in the cabin, all five of them, because Ines insisted and Mirabel, after a pause Pao couldn't parse, allowed it. Elias lay in his bunk below, feverish and sleeping, his breath whistling through him like wind through a cracked window. Through the bulkhead they could hear it, that thin torn sound, keeping its own time.

Pao told it all: Sefa and the description of the map, the thread, the black boat, the gloves, the fifty for word, the deckhands in the cantina. He told it plainly because plainness was all he had, and when he finished, the cabin was so quiet he could hear the pier posts creaking against their tires.

"Someone talked," Odile said at last. She sat with her arms crossed, one boot resting on a coil of line, and her voice had no blame in it, which somehow made it worse. "That's the arithmetic. Six of us knew the map existed. Now the Corvus Line knows its color, its material, its stitching."

"Not necessarily one of us." Bram's voice came out rough. He was standing by the stove he wasn't using, and his big hands hung at his sides like tools put down. "Elias came from somewhere. He booked passage from somewhere. He may have shown that map to a dozen people before he ever saw our rail. Doctors. Landladies. Whoever he trusted enough to help a dying man plan a trip."

"Did he?" Mirabel asked quietly, and it took Pao a moment to realize the question wasn't rhetorical—it was aimed, gently, at Ines.

Ines stood near the chart table with her arms wrapped around herself. "I don't know," she said. "I hadn't spoken to my father in twelve years until I found him in that hotel. I don't know who he told. I don't know who he *is*, really. I know what he was to me, and that man left." Her voice caught and steadied itself, the way a boat catches a wave. "But I know this: those men described the thread. The red thread. That's not a detail a landlady remembers. That's a detail for someone who studied the map close and long."

"Or stole a look at it," Odile said.

"Or was *shown* it," Pao said, "by someone who wanted them to know exactly what to look for."

Bram turned on him. "Say what you mean, Pao."

"I'm saying I don't know. That's what frightens me. Three years on this boat and I don't know any of you well enough to clear you, and none of you know me well enough to clear me, and that's how the captain likes it—" Pao stopped, hearing himself, hearing the accusation arrive in the room like a third passenger. He looked at Mirabel. "No. That's not fair. I'm tired and I'm spooked."

"No," Mirabel said. "It's fair. It's the truest thing said in this cabin today." He rose from his chair, unhurried, and moved to the chart table, and his finger came down on the river above them, on the gorge, on the long blue wound of it drawn in faded ink. "Here's what I know. The Corvus Line is behind us, and rivers only run one way, and they'll gain on us because they're lighter and newer and they don't care what they scrape their hull on. Here's what else I know. Somebody talked, and it doesn't matter who, because I'm not going to spend the rest of this run turning my crew into suspects. Suspicion is a hole in the hull. You don't argue with it about whose fault it is. You patch it or you drown."

He looked around the cabin, at each of them in turn, and Pao felt the look pass over him like a searchlight and find, for once, nothing to report.

"So here's the new rule, just for this run. Nobody goes ashore alone. Nobody talks to strangers about cargo, course, or passengers. We cast off within the hour, we run through the night watches, and we make the gorge before anything black-hulled finds us on open water." His hand flattened on the chart. "And nobody wakes Mr. Ines to tell him. He needs sleep more than he needs fear. Fear is expensive and he's running low on everything else."

"Aye," Odile said.

"Aye," said Bram, and then, softer, to Pao: "I'd never sell you out. Any of you. I'd die stupid before I did that."

"I know," Pao said. And he did know, and knowing it didn't help, and that was the whole rotten lesson of the day.

* * *

They cast off as the light went long and copper, the town sliding astern in pieces—spire, roofs, pier, the tire-wrapped posts letting go of their lines one by one. Pao took the bow watch because he asked for it, wanting the wind in his face and nothing behind him but water already traveled.

It was Ines who spotted it. She had taken to standing the evening watch beside him without being asked, learning the bow, learning the lines, and she touched his arm and pointed upstream, back the way they'd come, toward the smudge of the port.

A boat was coming out of the haze there. Black hull. No colors. Too far to read her wake properly, but she was moving with the unhurried confidence of a vessel that knew where she was going and believed the river would wait for her.

"She cast off right behind us," Ines said. Her voice was steady. Her knuckles, on the rail, were not.

Pao watched the dark shape for a long moment, measuring it against the sky, against his memory of every chase he had ever seen or fled. Then he turned and cupped his hands and called aft, loud enough to reach the wheelhouse, loud enough to reach the man who never spoke of his own past:

"Captain! We've got company—and I don't think they're sightseeing!"

Behind them, the black boat turned its bow upriver, into their wake, and began to follow.
