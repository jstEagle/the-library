## 13. The Captain Who Looked Back

Mirabel had learned long ago that a river tells you everything twice—once in whispers and once in shouts—and that the wise man listens to the whisper. The whisper came at dusk on the fourth day above Port of Small Mercies, when the light went the color of old brass and the Serein ran slow and glassy between walls of red shale that held the day's heat like a held breath.

The whisper was smoke.

Not their own. The *Vesper's* stack put out a thin gray thread that Odile kept tuned to a needle's width, a thread so familiar to Mirabel that he no longer saw it, the way a man stops seeing his own hands. This smoke was different. It hung low over the water two bends downstream, broad and brownish, coal-fed rather than wood-fed, and it moved against the current because whatever burned it was moving too.

He stood at the wheel with one hand resting on the brass rim and did the arithmetic he had been avoiding since Pao came back from the resupply dock with his collar up and his eyes down. A boat behind them. A boat asking questions about maps by description. A boat whose captain, according to the fuel clerk who'd talked too freely for the price of a beer, wore a gray coat and never smiled.

Coal-fired boats on the Serein were rare. The gorge didn't forgive deep draft, and coal cost more than most outfits would spend to chase a rumor. Whoever burned it believed absolutely in what they were chasing.

"Captain." Odile's voice from the engine hatch, muffled, then her head emerging through it, grease to her elbows, dark hair bound back with what looked suspiciously like one of Bram's kitchen twines. "You see it?"

"I see it."

"Corvus Line pennant. Pao spotted it with the glass before the light went. Red bird on black."

"I know the pennant."

She wiped her hands on a rag that was dirtier than her arms and climbed the rest of the way onto the deck, studying him with the particular frankness she permitted herself because she'd been with him six years and had earned it twice over. "You know more than the pennant."

It wasn't a question. Odile didn't ask questions she wasn't prepared to answer herself; it was one of the things he loved about her and one of the things that made lying to her a waste of good craft.

"There's a man," Mirabel said, and stopped, because the sentence had a cliff at the end of it and he could feel the drop.

The river slid past. Somewhere aft, Bram was singing to himself over the supper pots, something tuneless and cheerful about a woman in Tulane, and the sound of it drifted forward across the water like proof that the world they'd built aboard this hull still held.

"Renn Voss," Mirabel said.

Odile went very still. Not dramatically—she wasn't a dramatic woman—but the rag in her hands stopped moving, and that was as loud as a shout from anyone else.

"The investigator," she said.

"You know the name."

"Everyone from the delta knows the name. Eleven miners in the Kestrel Hollow collapse and the whole country wanted someone to blame, and Voss was the man who wrote the report everyone hated because it blamed nobody powerful enough to matter." She frowned. "That was twelve years ago. What does a man like that want with Elias Ines's map?"

"That," Mirabel said, "is the question I've been not asking myself for four days."

* * *

He took the middle watch alone, as he always did, standing at the wheel while the crew slept and the river ran silver under a half-risen moon. It was his favorite hour and had been for twelve years, the hour when the boat belonged only to him and the water asked nothing of him except attention. Attention was easy. Attention was the one form of honesty he'd never had to abandon.

But tonight the water wouldn't hold his eyes. They kept going aft, to the stern rail where Ines sat wrapped in a blanket, keeping her father company through the worst of his coughing. Elias hadn't come out of his cabin since noon. Through the bulkhead Mirabel could hear the wet rattle of his breathing, and beside it his daughter's voice, low and steady, reading to him from something—he couldn't make out what, only the rhythm of it, patient as rain.

Twelve years ago, Renn Voss had stood in a courtroom in the delta city with a folder under his arm and a face like a closed door, and he had said words that ended a life and began another one. Mirabel had been nineteen years old and sitting in the third row, close enough to see the dust motes turning in the light around Voss's shoulders, close enough to memorize the exact angle of his jaw and the flat, unhurried way he delivered each fact as though facts were stones he was laying one atop another to build a wall.

The wall had gone up around a man named Aldous Renwick—the man who had taken Mirabel in off the streets at fourteen, fed him, taught him engines and rivers and the difference between pride and dignity, the man he had called uncle until the word *uncle* became evidence.

Voss hadn't built the wall alone. But he had laid the capstone. He had stood before the bench and summarized eleven deaths into a narrative neat enough to swallow, and the narrative had swallowed Aldous Renwick whole, and the appeals had gone nowhere, and Aldous had died in Corben Federal two years into a twenty-year sentence with Mirabel's last letter unopened on his bunk—or so the chaplain wrote—and Mirabel had changed his name the following spring and pointed a borrowed boat upstream and never once, in twelve years, looked back at the delta.

Until now. Until a dying prospector with an oiled-silk map walked up his gangplank and offered him a fare he should have refused three times harder than he refused it.

And now Renn Voss was coming up the river behind them, coal smoke and gray coat and all, and every mile of water between here and the claim was a mile Mirabel had chosen, deliberately, knowingly, to sail toward the one man alive who could look at his face and say his old name aloud.

* * *

He nearly turned the boat.

It happened somewhere in the small hours, in the narrows below the Split Rock, where the channel pinched between two shelves of stone and the current leaned hard against the eastern bank. There was a side channel there—shallow, braided, barely passable even in high water—that bent away southwest toward the lowland route, the long way around, the coward's way. He knew it because he'd mapped every escape route on this river the way other men memorized psalms. Twelve years of practice. You could take a boy off the delta but you couldn't take the running out of him.

His hand was actually on the telegraph. He could feel the brass lever cold under his palm, could feel the engine answer before he'd even rung it—a half-telegraph astern, swing her nose to the side channel, and by dawn the Corvus boat would be a story Pao told over cards and nothing more. Turn back. Give Elias his fare money and his map and his daughter and his dying wish, and let them find some other fool to carry it. Let the truth stay buried where he'd buried his own name, where it had kept him safe for twelve years, where safety had cost exactly nothing except everything he pretended not to remember.

The stern lantern swayed. Through the cabin window he could see Ines asleep in the chair beside her father's bunk, her head tipped back, her mouth slightly open, one hand still resting on the blanket over his feet—as if even in sleep she was holding on. As if letting go was a skill she'd lost along with her childhood, somewhere in the twelve years of silence between her and this man, and was relearning now at the worst possible tuition.

And forward, in the fo'c'sle, Pao snored in three-part harmony with the bilge pump. And amidships, Odile slept sitting up the way she always did, claiming beds were for people without responsibilities. And in the galley Bram had left a pot of beans warming on the banked stove because he'd noticed, weeks ago, that the captain ate at strange hours and never said why, and had simply started leaving food where Mirabel would find it, and neither of them had ever spoken of it, and that silence was the truest conversation Mirabel had ever had with another human being.

They'd voted. That was the thing. He had stood on his own deck and told them the job was poison, and they had voted to take it anyway—Odile with her arms crossed, Pao with his hand halfway up before the question finished, Bram with a ladle raised like a scepter. Their votes. Their risk. Their river now, as much as his.

If he turned back, he wouldn't be saving them. He'd be deciding for them. And he had spent twelve years learning the difference between hiding and peace, mostly by practicing the first and failing at the second, and Ines had looked at him across a chart table not ten days ago and said, in that flat way she had of dropping truths like stones down wells, *you keep running the boat like you're being chased, Captain. Who's left?*

Nobody's left, he'd told her.

Then who are you still running from?

His hand came off the telegraph.

* * *

Dawn found him still at the wheel, having surrendered the middle watch to no one, watching the bend ahead where the river swung north around a bluff of black slate. The Corvus boat would round that same bend within the hour if it held its pace. Two boats on one river, converging, the way tributaries converge—inevitable as arithmetic.

He thought about waking Odile. He thought about calling a crew meeting, laying it all out—Renwick, the trial, the name he'd shed like a skin—and letting them decide whether their captain was worth the risk of sailing with. That was the honest thing. That was probably the smart thing.

Instead he rang for half ahead and steered for the bend, because there was a kind of knowledge a man could only get by looking directly at the thing he feared, and Mirabel Montrose had spent twelve years looking at everything else.

The sun cleared the shale bluffs and set the water burning. The *Vesper* came around the bend at a stately five knots, her hull groaning the complaint she always made in cold morning water, and there—two hundred yards downstream, moored against the near bank with her stack idling a lazy brown rope into the windless air—rode the Corvus Line boat.

She was bigger than the *Vesper*. Steel-hulled, twin-screw, a working gunboat dressed in a survey company's paint, with a pennant at her masthead snapping red-on-black and men on her deck who stood the way men stand when they're paid to be ready. Her name was lettered on the bow in white: *Kestrel*.

Of course it was. Someone had a sense of humor, or a memory, or both.

And on the *Kestrel's* afterdeck, alone, hands clasped behind his back, stood a man in a gray coat, watching the *Vesper* come down on him with the patience of a man who has already read the last page.

Twelve years had done to Renn Voss what twelve years do to everyone—it had silvered him at the temples and thickened him through the shoulders—but it had not touched the thing Mirabel had memorized from the third row of that courtroom: the absolute stillness of him. Other men fidgeted. Voss observed. He stood on that deck the way he'd stood at the witness stand, a closed door with the whole world knocking, and even at two hundred yards, even across moving water and morning glare, Mirabel felt those eyes find the *Vesper's* wheelhouse and settle on it with the weight of a hand on his shoulder.

Voss raised one hand. Not a wave. An acknowledgment. *I see you*, it said. *I have seen you for some time.*

Mirabel's mouth had gone dry as August. His hands were steady on the wheel—they were always steady, that was the one inheritance from his old life he'd never managed to lose—and he brought the *Vesper* down the channel at an even five knots, passing the *Kestrel* at a distance of eighty yards, close enough to count the rivets, far enough to pretend courtesy.

The crews watched each other across the water. One of the Corvus men lifted a camera—not casually, not for scenery—and took a photograph of the *Vesper* as she passed. Mirabel marked his face. Young, freckled, bored. The kind of face that developed film in a darkroom and handed prints to a man in a gray coat.

And Voss—Voss did not reach for a camera. Voss simply turned as the boats passed, rotating slowly at the waist like a compass needle finding north, and his eyes stayed on the wheelhouse window, on the figure at the wheel, and just before the distance and the glare swallowed the detail of him, his head tilted. A small motion. Almost gentle.

The tilt of a man hearing an echo. The tilt of a man thinking, *I know that silhouette. I've prosecuted that silhouette.*

Mirabel held the course. Held the speed. Held his face into the pleasant, vacant mask he'd worn through a thousand port inspections, and did not give the man across the water so much as a glance, because a glance was a confession and he had spent twelve years becoming innocent.

The *Kestrel* fell astern. Her smoke rope lengthened, thinned, dissolved. For a full minute the only sounds were the river and the engine and Mirabel's own blood, loud as surf in his ears.

Then, from the engine hatch, Odile's voice, very quiet:

"He looked back."

"Yes."

"Captain." She climbed out and stood beside him, wiping her hands, not looking at him, giving him the mercy of profile. "That man put your uncle in Corben Federal."

"He did."

"And we're sailing deeper into the gorge with him behind us, carrying the one piece of paper in the world he wants."

"It appears so."

Odile was silent for a moment. Below them, through the hull, he could feel the river changing—the faint new tremor in the deck plates, the water's pitch rising half a tone, the first murmurs of the Widow's Teeth still miles off but already announcing themselves, the way storms announce themselves, the way pasts announce themselves.

"Well," she said at last, and spat over the rail with great precision. "Then we'd best get there first."

Mirabel almost laughed. It came out as a breath, which was as much as he had. He reached forward and rang the telegraph to full ahead, and the *Vesper* gathered herself and leaned into the current, running toward the rapids and the claim and the truth, with the past smoking patiently at her heels.

Behind them, faint over the water, came the sound of the *Kestrel's* whistle—one short blast, courteous, almost friendly.

Ahead, around the next bend, the Widow's Teeth were waiting.
