## 14. Monsoon Warnings

Odile Vasseur had a superstition about radios, which was that they only ever told you the truth when you didn't want to hear it. She'd kept that rule through nine years of engine rooms and river stations, and the morning of the eleventh day out from port, hunched over the crackling set in the *Serein Queen*'s wheelhouse while rain needled the canvas awning, the radio held true to form.

"—monsoon system forming over the high plateau, tracking southeast. Barometric readings at Kessel Station down eleven millibars since midnight and falling. Estimated landfall at the headwaters in ten days, possibly fewer. All vessels on the upper Serein are advised to clear the gorge before—" Static swallowed the rest, then the announcer's voice swam back up like a drowned man. "—repeat, ten days. The gorge will not be passable once the flood pulse arrives. There will be no rescue. There is never any rescue."

Odile turned the volume down with two fingers, the way she'd turn down a hymn.

Behind her, Mirabel stood at the wheel with his hands loose on the spokes, watching the water the way he always did—like a man reading a letter from someone who might still betray him. He hadn't said anything yet. He'd heard it; she knew by the small change in his shoulders, the way a man's body goes quiet before his mouth does.

"Well," she said.

"Well," he agreed.

The rain thickened against the awning, drumming now instead of tapping. Below them the river ran brown-green and muscular, carrying whole trees on its back, and somewhere ahead—two days' run, maybe three if the current kept rising—the Serein Gorge opened its throat and waited for them the way it waited for everything: patiently, and without mercy.

* * *

She went below to think in numbers, because numbers were where Odile lived. Numbers had never lied to her the way people did, the way her own name had lied to her for six years before she'd burned her papers in a barrel behind a machine shop in Corvane and become simply Odile, engineer, no surname, no history, no debts worth collecting.

She spread the chart on the workbench in the engine room, weighted the corners with wrenches, and did the arithmetic with a grease pencil because pencils were for things you might have to take back.

Distance to the gorge mouth: forty miles of open river, running fast now, which helped. Distance through the gorge itself: sixty miles of canyon, most of it runnable, some of it not, depending on what the water decided to do between here and there. The Widow's Teeth were behind them—a memory she still felt in her wrists—but the Devil's Throat lay ahead, and past that the Narrows, where the walls came so close together that in flood season the river rose thirty feet in an hour and turned the whole passage into a single drowning thought.

Ten days. At their best speed, loaded as they were with Elias Ines's sealed crates and his coughing, fading body, they could make the far end in five. Six, if the fuel line trouble cost them again. Seven if anything else broke, and something always broke; Odile had built her whole philosophy around that certainty.

Five days of margin, then. Five days of grace.

Except the radio had said *possibly fewer*, and Odile had learned to read weathermen the way Mirabel read sandbars. The good ones hedged. The bad ones were certain. This one had hedged twice, which meant the storm was either worse than he knew or exactly as bad as he feared, and either way the number ten was a courtesy, not a promise.

She marked the chart in grease pencil: a line at the gorge mouth, a line at the Devil's Throat, a line at the Narrows. Then she wrote the dates beside them, working backward from the storm, forward from today, until the whole route was stitched with deadlines like a dress being let out one seam too many.

Bram found her there. He filled the doorway the way he always did, flour on his forearms, smelling of yeast and woodsmoke, and looked at the chart for a long moment without asking. That was Bram's gift—he never asked. He just stood there until you told him, because silence with Bram was company, not pressure.

"Ten days," she said finally.

"I heard." He wiped his hands on his apron, leaving pale streaks. "The girl heard too. She's up top asking Mirabel questions faster than he can dodge them."

"She's good at that."

"She's good at a lot of things." Bram leaned against the bulkhead, arms crossed over the expanse of him. "You want me to say it, or are we going to stand here pretending the math is friendly?"

"The math isn't friendly."

"No," he agreed. "It isn't."

Odile looked at the chart again. At the little grease-pencil dates marching up the gorge like soldiers going into a country they'd been warned about. Five days of margin, minus breakdowns, minus the Corvus Line's boat shadowing them somewhere back there in the rain, minus whatever Elias Ines's lungs were doing to him hour by hour—she'd heard the coughing through the deck planking last night, wet and deep, the sound of a man drowning slowly on dry air.

Minus the fact that turning back wasn't really turning back, because the Corvus Line sat between them and home like a hand across a door.

"Tell the others," she said. "All of them. No softening it. If we're going to run this river against a clock, everybody aboard should know what the clock says."

* * *

They gathered in the mess that evening, all six of them crowded around the table that Pao had cleared of plates and Bram had covered anyway with bread and stew, because Bram's answer to every crisis was to feed people first and consult them second. Rain hammered the deck overhead. The lamp swung gently with the boat's motion, throwing shadows that climbed the walls and slid back down.

Mirabel laid it out plainly, which Odile respected. He drew the route in spilled salt on the tabletop, marked the lines, spoke the dates aloud. Ten days, possibly fewer. Five of margin. Turn back and face the Corvus Line in open water, or go on and race the sky.

Nobody spoke for a moment. Outside, thunder rolled down the valley like furniture being dragged across heaven's floor.

Ines broke the silence. Elias's daughter—still traveling under her false name, though by now the falseness of it had worn thin as old paint, everyone knowing and nobody saying—sat with her elbows on the table and her jaw set in a way Odile recognized, having worn the same expression herself in other years, other lives.

"Then we don't stop," she said. "We run nights."

"Nights are how boats die in this river," Mirabel said. "You can't read the water by lantern light. You can't see the strainers."

"You can't see the storm coming either, but it's coming anyway." She tapped the salt map, right on the Narrows. "My father has maybe ten days in him. Maybe eight. I'm not arriving at that claim to bury him on the way."

"It's not just your father's life against the schedule," Mirabel said, and his voice stayed level but something moved underneath it, the way a log moves under calm water. "It's everyone's. Pao. Bram. Odile. Me. If the river takes us in the gorge, it takes all of us, and it won't apologize, and it won't distinguish between the guilty and the innocent."

"Then we'd better not be slow," Ines said.

Pao laughed—one short bark—and then covered his mouth, embarrassed, but the laugh had already done its work. Even Mirabel's mouth twitched at its corner.

"She's got you there, Captain," Odile said.

Mirabel looked around the table. Odile watched him do it—the slow survey, the weighing, the man counting his crew the way she counted rivets, checking each one for stress fractures. His gaze rested on her last, and she gave him a small nod. *I'll keep the engine alive. That part's mine. The rest is yours.*

"We make up time where we can," he said at last. "Longer runs each day. We skip the resupply at Halfway Bend—we've got enough. We run the Devil's Throat at first light, when the light's behind us and the standing waves haven't built yet. And Odile—" he turned to her fully, "—I want the engine tuned tonight. Everything tightened, everything spare on deck where we can reach it. If we break down inside the gorge with that water rising, we don't get a second chance at fixing it."

"You'll have an engine that sings," she said. "Whether it sings soprano or funeral dirge depends on the river."

"That's more than I ask of anyone," Mirabel said, and there it was again, that dry ghost of humor he used like a lantern—just enough light to keep walking by.

Bram pushed the bread basket toward the middle of the table. "Then eat," he said. "Fighting a monsoon on an empty stomach is just dying with extra steps."

* * *

Odile worked through the night, and she was glad of it. Work was the one prayer she still believed in.

The engine room at midnight was her cathedral: the smell of hot metal and diesel, the tick of cooling pipes, the single droplight swinging its yellow circle over the great black flank of the engine. She pulled the fuel line and held it up to the light, turning it slowly, looking for the sabotage Bram had found and patched days ago—checking the splice, checking everywhere else besides, because trust frayed fast on a river and she trusted her own eyes more than anyone's word. The splice held. The rest of the line was clean. Whatever enemy had cut it once hadn't come back—or hadn't come back *yet*, and she filed that *yet* away in the drawer where she kept all the things she couldn't fix with a wrench.

She retorqued the head bolts. She packed the spare impeller, the spare gaskets, a coil of patching wire, three lengths of hose, and a full tool roll into a canvas bag and stowed it in the lazarette nearest the engine, where hands could find it blind, in the dark, in spray, in panic. She greased everything that wanted grease and argued with a stubborn valve until it admitted defeat. Somewhere past two in the morning she caught herself talking to the engine—low, half-audible, the way she always did, calling it *girl* and *old terror* and *you beautiful bastard*—and she didn't stop, because the engine was the only conversation partner she'd ever had who never pretended to be anything other than what it was.

Around three, footsteps on the ladder. Ines came down into the yellow light, hair tied back, sleeves rolled, holding two cups of coffee that smelled strong enough to strip paint.

"You're up," Odile said.

"So is the river." Ines handed her a cup and leaned against the workbench, looking at the engine with the frank curiosity of someone who genuinely wanted to understand it. "Can I ask what you're doing?"

"Making sure this machine can outrun a storm." Odile wiped her hands. "Why are you awake? You should be sleeping. You've been steering practice all day—you'll be sore tomorrow."

"I can't sleep when he's coughing." Ines said it simply, without drama, which made it worse. "Every cough comes up through the deck like it's coming through my own chest. So I walk. And I ended up here."

Odile studied her over the rim of the cup. The girl had her father's stubbornness and none of his dust; she had hands that had gone from soft to capable in eleven days flat, rope burns healing on her palms, a new steadiness in her stance that Odile privately called *riverborn*. Twelve years of resentment against a dying man, and she walked the decks at night listening to him breathe.

"Fear keeps odd hours," Odile said. "Might as well put it to work. Hand me that feeler gauge."

Ines did, and then stayed, and Odile showed her—because teaching was also a kind of prayer—how to check valve clearance, how to listen to an engine the way you listened to a person, hearing the lie inside the honest sound. Ines learned fast. Faster than Pao had, though she'd never tell him that.

"What happens," Ines asked quietly, somewhere in the fourth hour, "if we don't beat it? The storm."

Odile set down the wrench. Considered lying, decided the girl had earned better.

"If the flood pulse catches us in the Narrows, the walls funnel the water. Thirty feet of rise in an hour, they say. The river fills the canyon wall to wall, white from edge to edge, and nothing floats in it—not us, not the Corvus Line, not God himself in a steam launch. The gorge becomes a gun barrel and we're the bullet fired the wrong direction." She met the girl's eyes. "So we won't be there when it arrives. That's not hope talking. That's scheduling."

"Scheduling," Ines repeated, and almost smiled. "Is that what you call faith?"

"On this boat, yes. It's the only kind that holds weight."

* * *

Dawn came gray and hard, the rain slackening to a fine cold mist that made the whole world look unfinished. Odile climbed topside with her coffee and found Mirabel already at the wheel, and beside him, standing at the rail, Elias Ines wrapped in two blankets, upright by sheer contract of will, looking upriver.

He was thinner than when they'd started. Everyone saw it; no one said it. His skin had gone the color of the fog, and his breath came in shallow sips, but his eyes—his eyes were fixed ahead with such ferocity of purpose that Odile had to look away, the way you look away from a candle burning down to its final inch.

"Engine report, Miss Vasseur?" he asked, without turning. His voice was gravel washed downstream.

"She'll hold," Odile said. "Better than she's ever held."

"Good." A pause. The mist beaded on his blankets. "Then we're racing the sky, and I mean to win."

Behind him, at the rail, his daughter stood with her hand hovering near his shoulder—not quite touching, twelve years of distance measured in that half-inch of air—and Odile saw the girl look from her father's face to the gray wall of the gorge waiting ahead, and then to Mirabel at the wheel, and Odile understood that the countdown had already begun for all of them, in different currencies: the storm counting in days, Elias counting in breaths, and the river counting in miles that grew shorter and meaner with every one they gained.

Upstream, faint through the mist, the gorge mouth opened like a held breath.

And far behind them—so faint Odile might have imagined it, but knew she hadn't—came the low, patient throb of another engine, keeping their pace, keeping their shadow, closing by inches the way night closes on a lamp.

Mirabel heard it too. She saw it in the sudden stillness of his hands on the spokes.

"Odile," he said quietly, without turning from the mist. "How long since our shadow last sounded like that?"

She listened. Counted beats. Felt the bottom drop out of her stomach.

"Captain," she said, "that's not the Corvus Line's boat. That's bigger. And it's gaining."
