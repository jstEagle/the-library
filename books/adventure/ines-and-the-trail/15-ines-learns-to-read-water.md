## 15. Ines Learns to Read Water

My father taught me to read when I was four years old, with a primer and a stub of pencil, tracing letters in the dust of a porch in a town I can no longer find on any map. He was patient then. He was present. Whatever else the years had done to him—whatever distance and silence and whatever-it-was that made him leave—he gave me words before he took himself away, and I have never decided which debt is larger.

Mirabel Montrose taught me to read water on the ninth morning out from port, and it was nothing like learning letters.

We stood at the bow while the crew slept off Bram's card game, the boat tied to a willow snag in a slow green eddy above the second set of riffles. The river at dawn was the color of pewter and old smoke, breathing mist the way my father breathed when he slept now—that wet, rattling patience. Mirabel handed me a mug of coffee he'd made himself, which surprised me more than anything he'd said all week, because I had privately filed him under *men who do not serve other people*, and the file needed revising.

"Look at the water," he said. "Not at the banks. The banks lie."

"The banks lie," I repeated.

"Everything solid lies. Rock, wood, a man's face. The water's the only thing out here telling you the truth, and it tells you sideways." He pointed with his chin, not his hand—I'd noticed he did that, kept his hands for the wheel or the lines or his own pockets, as if gestures were a currency he refused to spend. "See where the current goes smooth? Glassy, like oil?"

I saw it. A long slick tongue of river running between two stretches of chop.

"A beginner thinks smooth means safe. Smooth means deep, and deep means fast underneath, and fast underneath means it'll take your hull and slam it into whatever's downstream before you've finished saying *oh*." His voice was low and even, a man reading scripture he didn't especially believe in but knew by heart. "The choppy water—the broken stuff—that's shallow. That's the river showing you its bones. You want your boat where you can see the bottom."

"So safety looks dangerous."

"Safety always does." Something moved behind his eyes when he said it, some door opening a crack onto a lit room and shutting again before I could see what was inside. "Again. What do you see?"

I looked. Really looked, maybe for the first time since we'd cast off, because until that morning the river had been scenery—a beautiful, indifferent corridor we were being pulled through—and Mirabel was making it a text. There were V's on the surface, thin chevrons pointing downstream, and darker seams where currents braided together, and one place off our starboard bow where the water seemed to be boiling gently over nothing at all.

"There," I said, pointing at the boil. "That's wrong."

"That's a rock with an attitude. Water piles up over it and rolls back on itself. We call it a hole. Small ones are rude. Big ones are widows." He glanced at me sidelong. "You've got the eye. Most people look at a river their whole lives and see nothing but weather."

"I've been looking at things my whole life," I said, "and seeing nothing but weather."

He didn't ask what I meant. That was the thing about Mirabel Montrose—everyone on this boat was running from something, that much I'd worked out by the third day, and the unspoken treaty among us was that nobody asked anybody anything. Odile's engine room was her confessional and she kept the door shut. Pao laughed too easily and slept too lightly. Bram fed us like guilt was an ingredient. And the captain stood at his wheel twelve hours a day measuring the world in miles, as if distance were a virtue instead of just another way of not arriving.

But I had come aboard under a false name, so I was in no position to throw stones at anyone's windows.

* * *

My father coughed behind us and we both turned. He'd taken to sleeping on deck when the nights were warm enough, wrapped in blankets near the boiler vent, saying the cabin air sat heavy on his chest—which was true, but not the whole truth. The whole truth was that Elias Ines had begun rationing his views of the world the way a dying man rations everything: sunrises, riverbanks, his daughter's face. He spent them carefully, like coins he couldn't earn more of.

"Morning, Papa," I said.

"Ines." He smiled at me using the name I'd stolen for myself, and there was a joke buried in it somewhere, that he of all people should be the one to hand me my alias back like a gift. "You're up early."

"She's learning the river," Mirabel said.

"Is she." My father lowered himself onto the cargo hatch with the deliberateness of a man negotiating with his own body. "Her grandfather ran barges on the Aldis, you know. Before the war. She comes by it honest, even if she doesn't come by it at all these days."

"Papa."

"What? It's a compliment." He waved a hand, dismissive, and then the cough took him, doubled him, wrung him out like laundry. When it passed he sat there gray and grinning, and I hated how practiced his grin had become, how it fit over the pain like a lid fits a pot.

Mirabel looked away. I watched him do it—not rudely, but with the specific discomfort of a man who has seen dying before and knows there's no polite place to put his eyes. Then he said, quietly, "Your grandfather. Was he any good?"

"At boats?"

"At reading water."

"He drowned," my father said simply. "Spring flood, year I was nine. So I suppose he read it wrong once." He said it without bitterness, the way you'd report a fact about weather. "I've thought about that my whole life. How a man can know a thing completely and still have it kill him. I built a career on knowing things completely." Another smile, thinner. "Look how that's gone."

Mirabel said nothing. But I caught the look that passed between them—two men acknowledging something across a gap neither would name—and I understood that my father had said something true on purpose, had offered it like bait, and that Mirabel had declined to bite. These two were having an entire conversation in ellipses, and I was tired of being the ellipsis.

* * *

Midmorning we cast off and ran the riffles, and Mirabel let me stand beside him at the wheel.

Not touch the wheel. Not yet. But he narrated, and his narration was unlike anything he'd given me before—unguarded, almost unconscious, the way a man talks when he's doing the thing he loves best and forgets to be suspicious. This, I began to understand, was the real Mirabel. Not the closed captain, not the debtor-dodging drifter from the sandbar town. The man with his hands on the spokes and the river talking to him through forty feet of pine keel.

"Seam on the left. Take the seam. No—*read* it first, tell me why."

"Because the left channel's broken water, shallow, and the right side has that glassy tongue that's actually—" I stopped. "Wait. You said glassy was bad."

"Glassy over *deep* is bad. Look again. Is it smooth all the way down, or does it wrinkle at the edges?"

I squinted. The tongue had fine wrinkles along its margins, little dimples like gooseflesh. "It wrinkles."

"That's current feeding in from the sides. It's moving. Moving water over depth is a road." He nodded once, and coming from him it felt like a medal pinned to my chest. "Rivers are like people, Ines. The still ones aren't necessarily at peace. Sometimes they're just deep, and hiding what's moving underneath."

"You'd know," I said, before I could stop myself.

His hands didn't flinch on the wheel. But the temperature around us dropped ten degrees, the way it does when you say a true thing to someone who has organized their entire life around not hearing it.

"Keep your eyes on the seam," he said.

"I'm not trying to pick at you, Captain."

"No," he agreed. "You're just doing it anyway."

And because I am apparently my father's daughter, incapable of leaving a locked box locked, I said: "You measure everything in miles. Odile told me. Twelve hours at the wheel, and when Pao asks how far to the gorge you answer in miles, and when Bram asks how long you've had the boat you answer in miles. Nobody measures a life in miles unless they're trying not to measure it in years."

The riffle chattered under the hull. Somewhere aft, Pao was singing something tuneless and cheerful about a girl and a mule. The world went on being ordinary, and Mirabel Montrose steered through it with his jaw set like a man carrying a load up a hill.

"My father used to say that," he said finally. "Miles are honest. A mile is a mile no matter who you were when you started it."

"*Was?*"

The word landed between us like a dropped glass. He went very still, and I watched him decide—watched the calculation move through his face, the same calculation I'd seen him make at every port, every stranger's question, every moment the past knocked. *Tell or don't tell.* I knew that arithmetic intimately. I'd done it myself in a shipping office three weeks ago, giving the clerk a name that wasn't mine, feeling the small clean relief of it and the small dirty shame.

"Don't answer," I said, and meant it. "I know what it costs. I've been paying it since I came up your gangplank with a name I borrowed off a headstone." His eyebrows rose a fraction; he hadn't known that detail, and I watched him file it away. "I'm not asking you to open anything, Captain. I'm just telling you what I see from where I'm standing. Still water isn't peace. I hid from my father for twelve years and called it peace, and it wasn't. It was just still. And still water over depth runs fast underneath, and it'll take your hull and slam it into whatever's downstream before you've finished saying *oh*."

For a long moment he said nothing. The boat ran the seam, true and steady, because his hands knew the work even when the rest of him was elsewhere.

Then, quietly, without looking at me: "Who taught you to talk like that?"

"A man who left," I said. "Turns out he taught me everything except how to stop loving him. Maybe that lesson's in the ledgers he won't show me."

Something flickered across his face at the word *ledgers*—quick, quickly buried, but I was learning to read Mirabel the way he was teaching me to read water, and I caught it. A dimple in the smooth surface. A rock with an attitude, down where nobody was supposed to look.

Interesting, I thought. Very interesting.

* * *

We moored that evening in a cutbank willow grove, and after supper I found him checking the lines alone, lantern light turning his shadow long and crooked against the trunks.

"Show me the holes tomorrow," I said. "The big ones. The widows."

"Why?" He straightened, and the lantern threw his face half into bronze, half into dark. "So you can take the wheel someday? This run ends at the claim, Miss Ines. One way. There's no someday on this river for you."

"Maybe not. But my father's dying, Captain, and he's got secrets sewn into the lining of this trip, and sooner or later somebody's going to have to keep this boat alive while everybody else is busy with the truth." I held his gaze. "I'd rather learn from you than from the river's sense of humor."

He studied me for a long time. Behind us the current worried the willow roots, patient, conversational, saying the same true thing over and over in its sideways language.

"All right," he said at last. "First light. Wear boots you don't mind ruining."

He turned to go, then stopped, his back to me, and I heard him say it so softly I might have imagined it:

"For what it's worth—still water. I know exactly what's moving under mine. That's the problem."

He walked aft toward the engine room, where Odile's lamplight burned late as always, and I stood among the willows listening to the river lie smoothly over its deep places, and thought about my father's sealed crates, and the word *ledgers*, and the dimple in the captain's calm.

Two men on this boat were hiding the same secret from each other. I could feel it the way you feel a hole in the river before you ever see the boil—some deep obstruction, bending the current of everything around it.

Tomorrow I'd learn to read the widows.

Tonight, I was starting to read the men.
