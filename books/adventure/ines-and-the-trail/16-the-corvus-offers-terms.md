## 16. The Corvus Offers Terms

The river had been talking to me all morning, and what it said was: *hurry*.

I stood at the wheel with the brass warm under my palms and watched the Serein run high and brown and impatient, carrying whole trees past us like matchsticks, like it was showing off what it could do when it decided to be angry. The monsoon was still a rumor up in the headwaters, but rumors travel downstream faster than boats. Every eddy had an edge to it. Every sandbar sat lower than it had two days ago.

Behind me, Elias Ines lay on the bench under the awning with his hat over his face, breathing in that wet-paper way of his. His daughter sat beside him with a book she wasn't reading. She'd turned the same page four times. I knew because I'd been counting, the way I count everything—miles, knots, the small dishonesties of people who are trying very hard not to think about something.

"Water's rising," I told her, because it was true and because saying it out loud made it feel manageable.

"I know." She didn't look up. "Papa won't say so, but he's scared of the gorge more than he's scared of dying."

"That's sensible of him."

That earned me half a smile before she went back to her unread page.

Odile had the engine tuned to a purr that would've been beautiful if I hadn't known what it was costing us in fuel. We were burning daylight and diesel both, trying to outrun a storm and a boat we couldn't quite see anymore but hadn't lost either. The Corvus Line's steamer had fallen back around noon yesterday—too deep in the draft for the shallows above the Split Rock channel—but falling back isn't the same as going away. A hunter who can see your dust doesn't need to keep pace with you. He only needs to know where you're going.

I'd been telling myself all morning that the river was the only thing I needed to read.

Then Pao came up from the bow fast, one hand on the rail, and said, "Captain. Smoke."

* * *

It came around the long bend below the Palisades, where the cliffs lean in close enough that their shadows cool the water by a degree you can feel through your boots. A launch—not the big Corvus steamer but a shallow-draft skiff with an outboard that growled like something fed on meat—and it came straight at us across the current, cutting the angle, arrogant about it, the way a boat moves when its captain doesn't care whether you yield or not.

Bram appeared on deck with a cleaver in his hand and no memory of having picked it up, I'd have bet money. Odile killed the engine to half and came out wiping her hands on a rag that had been black since before I met her. Ines stood up without being asked to, which surprised me, and put herself between her father and the rail.

"Easy," I said, to all of them, and maybe to myself.

The skiff swung alongside, close enough that I could see the grain of its planking, see the man at its tiller, and my stomach did something slow and cold, like water finding a crack in a hull.

Renn Voss.

I'd have known him anywhere, and I had known him anywhere—I'd seen his face in newspapers twelve years ago, younger then, harder now, a scar through one eyebrow that he hadn't earned back when the world still called me by my other name. He wore a Corvus Line jacket with the collar open and a pistol on his hip worn the way some men wear a watch: as a fact about time running out.

He raised one hand, palm out. Not a threat. An invitation.

"Captain," he called across the gap. "A word, if the river allows it."

"The river doesn't allow anything," I called back. "It just takes."

He smiled at that, and the smile was worse than the gun. "Then let's say I'm asking politely while the asking's cheap."

I looked at Odile. She looked at me. Behind her, Bram shifted his grip on the cleaver, and Pao had drifted, casual as weather, to stand near the fuel line—the same fuel line somebody had cut two nights ago, the repair holding so far, held together with a clamp and prayer and Odile's swearing. We were all thinking the same arithmetic: they had one man visible and probably six not; we had five, one dying, and a boat with a fresh seam patch.

"Keep her steady," I told Odile. "If I raise both hands, get us out of here like the devil's rowing behind us."

"He usually is," Odile said, and throttled down to a crawl.

* * *

Voss brought his skiff alongside with a boatman's touch I hated him for. He stepped aboard without asking, which is a thing you can do when you're carrying a gun and the other man's pretending not to carry a temper. Up close he smelled of engine oil and tobacco and something underneath both, something like old paper—like files, like records, like a case that had never been closed.

"Mirabel Montrose," he said, tasting the name.

"That's me today."

"And yesterday. And the day before." He took off his hat, turned it in his hands. He had gray coming in at the temples now, and the scar had gone white. "You know, I've read every word ever written about you. There weren't many words. That was rather the point of you, wasn't it? A man built out of silence."

"You've got the wrong man for a conversation about silence. You're the one who talked for a living."

"Investigator," he agreed. "Retired. Mostly." He glanced past me at the awning, at the shape of Elias Ines under his hat, at the daughter standing guard over him with her jaw set like her father's had once been set in a photograph I'd never seen but could imagine. "How is the old man?"

"Dying. Slowly enough to be inconvenient to everyone."

"Yes, they generally do it that way." Voss settled against the rail, unhurried, a man who had decided the river was on his schedule. "Here's the shape of things, Captain, because I respect you enough not to waste your fuel. The Corvus Line wants the map. They want the claim. And they want Elias Ines—or rather, they want what Elias Ines knows, delivered somewhere quiet where it can be forgotten properly. You can give them all three, and walk away with enough money to buy a boat twice this size and a crew that doesn't ask questions. Or you can keep going, and I'll take all three anyway, and you'll walk away with nothing but the bill for the repairs."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I'll be very disappointed," he said mildly, "and you'll find out what disappointment costs."

I looked at him. I let myself look at him the way I look at water before a rapid—reading the surface for what's moving underneath. And underneath, where most men keep their greed, Renn Voss kept something else. Something older and more tired. It bothered me, that tiredness. Greed I could fight. Tiredness might mean doubt, and doubt makes men unpredictable.

"There's a problem with your offer," I said.

"Only one? I'll count that a victory."

"The map isn't mine to sell. Neither is the old man. I'm a ferry, not a fence."

"A ferry." He nodded slowly, as though the word pleased him. "Yes. That's the word I'd have used too. Twelve years ago, there was a boy who ferried things. Messages, mostly. Small parcels. Nothing he understood, I don't think—he was cleverer than the men using him, but not yet clever enough to know it. He ran errands for a man named—" and he stopped, deliberately, letting the name hang unspoken in the air between us like smoke.

My hands were very still on the wheel. Stillness is a skill. I learned it young.

"You're mistaking me for someone else," I said.

"Am I?" Voss put his hat back on, and there it was again, that tiredness, surfacing for just a moment before he smoothed it away. "Hartwell. The name was Hartwell."

The river kept moving. That's the mercy of rivers; they don't pause for the moments that break you. Somewhere behind me I heard Bram shift his weight, heard Ines's breath catch—she'd heard it, of course she'd heard it, Hartwell, a name that meant nothing to her and everything to me—and I kept my eyes on Voss and my voice level and my heart doing its work quietly, behind a closed door, where hearts belong.

"That name died," I said. "Twelve years ago. Along with everyone who cared about it."

"Not everyone." Voss studied me with those investigator's eyes, the ones that had once taken apart a man I loved the way Odile takes apart an engine—piece by piece, laying each part out on clean cloth, deciding which ones to keep. "I cared about it. Not kindly, perhaps. But I cared. I spent three years of my life on that case, Captain. Three years chasing a fire, eleven dead miners, and a trail of paper that led everywhere and nowhere. And at the end of it, the only man who went to prison was the one man whose guilt I could never prove to my own satisfaction. Do you understand what that does to a man who believes in ledgers?"

"It teaches him to stop believing in them."

"No. It teaches him to keep reading them." He pushed off the rail and stood straight, and for a moment the salesman fell away and I saw the man underneath, the one who had haunted my nights for twelve years wearing a dozen different faces, none of them quite his own. "I'm not with the Corvus Line, Captain. Not in my heart. I'm wearing their jacket because it gets me close. What I want is what's in those crates of yours. The truth about Kestrel Hollow. All of it. Even the parts that will hurt."

"Kestrel Hollow," I repeated, and the word tasted like smoke, like a night twelve years gone, like a door I had nailed shut from the inside.

"You remember it."

"I remember nothing. That's my profession."

Voss smiled again, and this time the smile reached his eyes, and it was almost kind, which frightened me more than the gun had. "Then you're a better liar than the men I used to hunt. Here's my last offer, and it's different from the first one, so listen carefully. Give me the map—not the Corvus Line, *me*. Let me come with you to the claim. When we find what's there, I'll write it down and sign it and swear to it, and I'll spend whatever's left of my reputation making sure the world reads it. Eleven men died in that mine, Captain. Somebody buried them twice—once in rock, once in paperwork. I've wanted to dig them up for twelve years." He paused. "And I think you have too. Whoever you call yourself now."

* * *

The silence after he said it lasted four seconds. I counted. Counting is how I pray.

Ines moved first. She came forward along the deck with her chin up and her father's stubbornness written all over her, and she planted herself between me and Voss like a fender between hulls. "He's lying," she said—to me, not to Voss. "Whatever he's offering, the Corvus Line is paying his wages, and the Corvus Line burned Papa's letters and followed us from port and cut our fuel line in the night. You don't make deals with the wolf because he says he's tired of eating sheep."

"A reasonable position," Voss said, "from someone whose father has been lying to her since before she could walk."

She went white. I saw her hands close into fists, saw Elias stir under his hat, and I stepped in before the moment could turn into blood, because that's the job—stepping in.

"Enough," I said. "Nobody's making deals. Not today, not on my deck. You want passage, Mr. Voss, you swim. You want the map, you'll have to take this whole boat apart plank by plank, and I promise you the river will charge you for the privilege."

Voss looked at me for a long moment. Then he looked at Ines, and something in his face shifted—recognition, maybe, of the kind that has nothing to do with names. Two liars, recognizing each other. I'd seen it once before, in a mirror.

"Montrose," he said, and the name came off his tongue careful, deliberate, a man setting down a card he intends to play later. "Or Hartwell. Whichever you're being today. Think about what I said. Not the offer—the question. Because the offer expires when we reach the claim, but the question follows you the rest of your life: *when they finally dig up the truth, whose side of the shovel were you standing on?*"

He stepped back onto his skiff with that same infuriating grace, took up the tiller, and paused.

"One more thing," he said. "Tell the old man that Renn Voss remembers Kestrel Hollow too. Tell him I never believed the verdict. Tell him—" and here he hesitated, the first hesitation I'd seen in him, a hairline crack in all that polished certainty, "—tell him the file was never closed. Not by me."

The skiff swung away, grew small, became smoke, became nothing. The river closed over the place where it had been, indifferent as always.

* * *

For a while nobody spoke. The engine idled. A kingfisher went past low and blue, going about its business, which suddenly seemed like the sanest occupation in the world.

"He knew your name," Ines said at last. Not accusing. Just placing the fact on the deck between us, like a coin.

"He knew a name."

"Was it yours?"

I looked at her. Twelve years of practice went into what I said next, and twelve years of practice failed me completely, because what came out was: "Ask your father. He's had longer to decide how much truth he can afford."

Her eyes flashed—anger, hurt, recognition, all of it at once, because she knew exactly what I was doing. Throwing her own locked doors back at her. We stood there a moment, two people built from the same timber, and then Elias coughed, deep and wet, and pulled the hat from his face, and his eyes found mine with a clarity that frightens me even now to remember.

"You knew Voss," he said. "Before. Before me, before the boat, before any of it."

"Everyone knew Voss. He was famous."

"Don't." Elias's voice cracked like dry wood. "Don't do that. Don't give me the ferryman. I've watched you for two weeks, Mirabel Montrose. I've watched you read water like scripture and lie like a hymn. Whatever name you buried, Voss just dug it up on my deck, and I need to know—" another cough bent him double, and Ines caught his arm, and when he straightened his face was gray and fierce, "—I need to know if the man I'm trusting my daughter's life to is running *toward* my claim or *away* from something in it."

The river ran. The cliffs threw their shadows. Odile stood at the engine with her rag in her hands, watching me, waiting, the way she'd waited through every storm I'd ever steered us into. Pao leaned on the rail. Bram had finally put down the cleaver.

And I opened my mouth to give them the comfortable answer, the ferryman's answer, the one I'd given a hundred times to a hundred strangers—

—and what came out instead was: "Kestrel Hollow. Say it again. Slowly. And tell me why a dying man carries a map to the place that ruined my life."

Elias stared at me. Then, slowly, terribly, he began to smile—not a happy smile, but the smile of a man who has been waiting twelve years for a lock to open and has just heard the tumblers fall.

"So you *do* remember," he whispered.

Behind us, far downstream, thin and patient, the Corvus Line's smoke rose again above the bend.
