## 17. Sabotage

Bram woke to the smell of diesel in his dreams before he smelled it with his nose.

That was the cook's curse, or the cook's gift—depending on which of his ex-wives you asked. Twenty years of feeding crews on moving water had trained him to know a boat by its smells the way other men knew them by their lines and seams. The *Kestrel's Lark* smelled, in her sleep, of woodsmoke banked low in his galley stove, of river mud drying on the deck planks, of Odile's machine oil and Pao's cheap tobacco and the faint medicinal sweetness of Elias Ines's medicine, that bitter green reek that had begun to seep through the cabin walls like something alive. Diesel belonged to all of it, always—a ghost note under everything, the boat's own blood.

But this was different. This was diesel where diesel should not be.

Bram lay in his bunk behind the galley and listened to the night. Rain on the canopy, soft as fingers drumming. The river talking to itself below the hull, that endless gravel-voiced mutter. Somewhere forward, Pao turning over in his hammock with a creak of rope. And beneath it all, a slow, patient dripping—not water.

He got up without lighting a lamp. A man who'd spent nine years cooking for people who didn't ask questions learned to move quiet, learned to count exits, learned that the hour before dawn was when trouble liked to make its rounds. He pulled on his boots, took the lantern but didn't light it, and eased open the galley door onto a deck washed silver-grey with pre-dawn light.

The smell led him aft along the port rail, past the coiled lines stiff with dew, past the crates stacked under canvas—*those* crates, sealed and lashed, that Mirabel had ordered touched by no one—and down the three steps into the engine well, where Odile ruled her kingdom of brass and shadow.

She was already there. Of course she was already there. Odile slept like a cat with one ear open, and the smell would have woken her same as him. She stood at the engine with a wrench hanging loose from her fist, not working it, just holding it, staring at the fuel line the way a woman stares at a wound on her own child.

"Bram," she said, without turning. Her voice was flat as slate. "Come look at this."

He came and looked.

The fuel line ran from the tank along the engine block in a neat curve, and someone had taken a knife to it—one clean stroke, deep enough to open the rubber but not sever it entirely, so that instead of dying all at once the engine would have bled out slowly, a spoonful at a time, until somewhere in the middle of tomorrow's run it coughed, stuttered, and quit. Not in the rapids. Not where they'd hear it and fight it. Here, in the dark, in the quiet, so they'd drift helpless into whatever current chose to take them.

"Clean cut," Odile said. "Not a snag, not a chafe. Knife."

"I can see that."

"One cut. Somebody who knew what they were doing. Somebody who knew if they cut it all the way through we'd smell the flood and find it in an hour." She finally turned to look at him, and in the grey light her face was older than her years, which was saying something, because Odile was sixty if she was a day and had never once admitted it. "Somebody who wanted us dead slow, not dead."

Bram looked at the line a long moment. Then he looked up, at the deck above, at the five sleeping shapes he could map with his eyes closed: Pao in his hammock, young and loyal and too honest for his own good. Ines in the small berth off the wheelhouse, who lied about her name the way other women lied about their age—easily, and only half convincingly. Elias, coughing himself hollow behind a closed door. And Mirabel, who slept—or pretended to sleep—in the wheelhouse chair more often than in his bunk, because captains who were running from something slept light.

And Bram himself. Let's not forget Bram. He was the last one awake, standing over the evidence, holding a lantern he hadn't lit.

"You thinking what I'm thinking?" Odile said quietly.

"That I don't like what we're both thinking."

* * *

By full light the whole crew stood around the engine well like mourners at an open grave, and the question sat among them, taking up more room than any of them would admit: *who?*

Mirabel crouched at the cut line, turning it with two fingers, his face doing what Mirabel's face did—which was nothing at all. That was the thing about the captain that Bram had catalogued years ago and never mentioned: the man could stand in front of bad news and wear it like a coat that fit. No flinch, no bluster. Just stillness, and behind the stillness a kind of arithmetic you could almost hear ticking.

"Not weathering," Mirabel said. "Not vibration. This was done deliberate, sometime between midnight and four."

"Well, obviously," Odile snapped. She was angrier than the situation strictly called for, and Bram understood why. An engineer takes a cut fuel line personally. It wasn't just sabotage; it was a stranger's hand inside her bodywork, touching her machinery, deciding how her engine would die. "Question isn't how. Question is who was awake, who had reason, and who's lying right now while we all stand here being polite about it."

Pao shifted his weight. He was twenty-three and built like a whipstock, and he had the particular misery of a young man watching adults discover that the world contains knives. "It wasn't me," he said, and then winced, because saying it made it worse. "I mean—I know everybody says that. But I was on watch till two, and I'd have seen—"

"You'd have seen somebody come aft, maybe," Odile said. "You were watching the river ahead, boy, not our own deck. That's what watch means on this boat. That's my fault as much as anyone's."

"It wasn't me either," Bram said, and heard how it sounded, and hated it.

Silence. The river went on talking below them, indifferent.

Ines stood apart, arms crossed, her eyes going around the circle with an expression Bram couldn't read—something sharper than fear, something almost like recognition. She'd been aboard six days now, traveling under a name that wasn't hers, and Bram had watched her the way he watched everyone, because watching was free and trust cost money. She said, "What about the Corvus Line?"

"They're a day back," Mirabel said. "We've held our lead since the narrows."

"A day back is a day back. They've got a bigger boat, more hands. Somebody could have come ahead in a skiff in the dark. Or—" she paused, and Bram saw her decide to say it, saw the words cost her something, "—or they paid somebody already aboard."

Nobody moved. The rain had stopped and the air hung thick, and somewhere upstream a bird screamed once and quit.

Mirabel straightened up. He looked at each of them in turn—Pao, Odile, Bram, Ines—and Bram watched him do it and understood that the captain was performing the same inventory they all were, and that none of them, not one, could give the others what they needed, which was proof.

"No one's accused," Mirabel said at last. His voice was even, almost gentle, which somehow made it worse. "But here's what happens now. Watches double. Nobody moves aft alone after dark. The tools lock in the galley, Bram, and you keep the key. And the Corvus Line—" he paused, and something flickered across his face, gone too fast to name, "—the Corvus Line gains a day, because this repair costs us a day, and we all know it."

"We could run the line patched," Odile said. "Wrap it, epoxy it, limp along. Get to the claim tonight instead of tomorrow night."

"And if it lets go mid-rapid?"

"Then we swim."

Mirabel looked at her. For a moment Bram thought he'd say yes—the captain had a hunger in him lately, a clock ticking behind his eyes ever since the storm reports started coming downriver—and then he shook his head once. "No. We fix it proper. One day. I won't run the Devil's stretch of this gorge on a line held together with hope."

He walked away up the steps, and the crew stood in the engine well and did not look at each other, which was its own kind of conversation.

* * *

The day that followed was the longest day Bram could remember aboard the *Lark*, and he'd once spent four days becalmed on the lower Serein eating salt pork and regret.

Odile worked. That was the mercy of Odile—that grief and fury went straight into her hands and came out as competence. She stripped the ruined section of line, spliced in new stock she'd been hoarding against exactly this kind of morning, though she'd always imagined the enemy would be rocks and not neighbors. Bram cooked, because cooking was what he did when the world tilted: he made rice with the good dried chilies, the ones he rationed like a miser, and fried plantains sweet enough to make Pao forget to sulk for ten minutes at a time. Food wouldn't fix a fuel line. But food kept a crew from becoming a jury, and Bram had learned that lesson on boats where the jury had convened and the verdicts had been ugly.

Except today the food didn't work the way it was supposed to.

He served lunch on deck because the galley was too hot, and he watched them eat, and he watched the watching. Pao sat close to Ines and talked too much, the way he did when he was nervous, telling her about the fish he'd caught as a boy off the pilings at home, and Ines smiled at him and answered in the right places, and Bram saw that her eyes kept sliding aft, toward the crates, toward the engine, toward everything. Odile ate alone at the rail, facing downstream, toward the bend where the Corvus Line would eventually appear if it appeared. And Mirabel stood at the wheelhouse window with a plate he hadn't touched, looking at the water, looking at nothing, wearing that face like a locked door.

Elias didn't come out. His cough had gone deeper this week, into the chest, into the nights; Bram carried a tray to his cabin door and heard the old man breathing behind it, wet and labored, like water finding its way through gravel. When Elias opened the door he looked smaller than he had at the start, the way dying men shrink toward their outlines. He took the tray and said thank you, and then, as Bram turned away, said, "Cook."

"Sir."

"Whoever did it." The old man's voice was a thread. "It wasn't anyone on this boat."

Bram stopped. "You know that how?"

Elias's eyes were fever-bright and terribly calm. "Because I know what men look like when they're bought, and nobody on this boat has been bought. They're all running from something, every one of you, but running-from isn't selling-to. Believe me. I've done both." He closed the door gently, and Bram stood in the corridor a moment with the smell of sickness and diesel and river, and found that he believed the old man completely, and that believing him changed nothing, because belief wasn't proof, and proof was the only currency that mattered now.

* * *

Evening came down purple and heavy, and the river turned to hammered pewter, and they tied up early in a narrow bend with high clay banks on both sides—Mirabel's choice, defensible ground, one approach visible. Supper was quiet in the way of a family after a funeral: everyone polite, everyone careful, everyone grieving something they couldn't name yet.

It was Pao who broke first. He was washing dishes—his turn, and he hadn't shirked it, which Bram noted and appreciated—and suddenly the clatter of plates stopped and the boy's voice came out cracked and too loud.

"I want it said plain. All of it. Because the not-saying is worse than anything anybody could actually say." He stood there with a plate in his hand and dishwater dripping off his elbows, and his jaw was set the way it got set when he was scared and refusing to show it. "I've been on this boat three years. Three years, and I never asked any of you one question about where you came from, because the rule is no names, no pasts, and I liked the rule. I *liked* not knowing. It meant nobody could use it against us." He swallowed. "But somebody cut that line, and now the not-knowing has teeth. So. Plain talk. Who are we? What are we carrying? Why does a dying man pay gold to go up a river nobody runs before monsoon? And why does the captain look like he's seen a ghost every time the Corvus Line gets mentioned?"

The silence afterward had weight, like a stone laid on a table.

Bram looked at Mirabel. Mirabel looked at the river. And Bram thought, with a strange clarity, that the boy was right, and that the rule had been a beautiful thing and a doomed thing both, because you could carry secrets safely only as long as nobody came hunting them—and somebody had come hunting, with a knife, in the night.

"The line's fixed," Odile said quietly, from her corner. "Engine's sound. Tomorrow we make up time. Whatever else is true, that much is true, and it's mine, and I'll stand on it."

"That's not enough anymore," Pao said. "With respect. It's not enough."

Ines rose from her seat at the rail. She crossed the deck and stood in the lamplight where they could all see her, and Bram watched her take a breath like a woman stepping off a ledge.

"My name isn't Ines Vale," she said. "It's Ines Ines. Elias Ines is my father, and I haven't spoken to him in twelve years, and I booked passage under a lie because I didn't know if he'd want me aboard, and I didn't know if I'd want to be." Her voice didn't shake, or rather it shook and she made it stop. "There. Plain talk. Someone start."

Bram felt something loosen in his chest—not relief exactly, but the feeling of a knot slipping one degree. He glanced at Mirabel, and caught the captain looking at Ines with an expression Bram had never seen on him before: something raw and almost envious, as if she'd done a thing he'd been unable to do for twelve years.

Before anyone could answer her—before Mirabel could open whatever door he was visibly wrestling with—the sound came down the bend.

An engine. Deep-throated, diesel-heavy, wrong for these waters at dusk, growing louder.

They all turned. Around the clay point, riding the last of the light, came a black-hulled boat with a crow painted on her wheelhouse, running lights burning bright and unafraid, and standing at her rail a tall figure raised a lantern twice—signal, greeting, or challenge, impossible to say—and held it there.

The Corvus Line. A full day early.

Behind Bram, very softly, Odile said what they were all thinking: "Then whoever cut that line didn't need to slow us down at all. They just needed us to stop and fix it—right here, right now, where they knew we'd be."

The lantern at the Corvus's rail swung once more, patient as a pendulum, and began to come closer.
