## 19. Night Portage

The channel died at dusk.

Pao heard it before he saw it—a wrongness in the water's voice, the river's long sentence breaking off mid-word. He was on the bow with the sounding pole, calling depths to Odile in the engine room, when the current beneath them simply quit. The *Kestrel* slowed as if wading into honey, and Pao looked up to find the gorge walls leaning close, and the channel ahead choked with a hillside that had come down sometime since the last flood season. Rock and red clay and whole trees stood on their heads, roots clawing at the sky like drowned men reaching for a shore they'd never touch.

"Hard astern!" Mirabel's voice came down from the wheelhouse, flat and fast, the voice he used when the world narrowed to inches. "Astern, Odile, all you've got!"

The engine coughed, caught, roared. The boat shuddered backward off the mud shelf it had been kissing, and Pao rode the buck of the deck with his knees soft and his pole braced, heart going like a drum in a parade.

They backed out into the wider water below the collapse and let the current hold them while Mirabel brought her alongside the near bank, where an old landing—rotted pilings, a shelf of gravel—still remembered being useful. The anchor went down with a splash that sounded enormous in the gathering dark.

Mirabel came out onto the deck and stood looking upriver for a long time. Pao had learned to read the captain's silences the way he'd learned to read the sandbar's mood: there were silences that meant *thinking*, and silences that meant *measuring*, and one particular silence—the longest kind—that meant the captain had already done the arithmetic and didn't like the answer.

This was the third kind.

"Portage," Mirabel said finally. "We go around."

Pao laughed before he could stop himself. It came out high and disbelieving. "Around? Captain, she draws four feet. She weighs—" He did the math and hated it. "She weighs everything we own."

"She does." Mirabel turned, and in the last grey light his face was all angles and shadow. "There's an old haul road on the far side of the landing. Loggers used it before the slide took the channel. It runs maybe three hundred yards along the bank and drops back in above the blockage. We winch her over on rollers. Bram's skids, Odile's blocks, every rope aboard."

"And if we don't?" Ines asked. She'd come up quietly, arms crossed against the evening chill, her father's blanket around her shoulders. Elias hadn't left his bunk in two days except to cough.

"If we don't," Mirabel said, "we wait for the Corvus Line to catch us sitting still, or we wait for the monsoon to arrive early, which the radio says it may. Pick your poison."

Nobody picked anything. Nobody said anything at all, until Bram came up from the galley wiping his hands and said what everyone was thinking.

"It can't be done, Captain. Not by five people. Not in the dark. You're asking us to move a house across a mountain because the river took a nap."

"I'm asking you to try," Mirabel said. "Because the alternative is standing still, and I've watched men stand still their whole lives waiting for the world to get easier. It doesn't."

Something in the way he said it—not angry, not pleading, just tired and true—landed differently than it would have a week ago. A week ago Pao might have argued. But he'd seen the Widow's Teeth, and he'd seen Ines pull a line hand-over-hand beside him without complaint, and he'd seen Elias Ines dying by inches in the cabin and never once ask anyone to slow down.

Odile emerged from the engine hatch, grease to her elbows, hair tied back with what looked like a strip of flour sack. She looked at the slide, then at the sky, then at Mirabel.

"Tide of light," she said. "We've got maybe ten hours of dark before the heat comes back and makes the work stupid. If we're moving by midnight, we might clear the top by dawn."

"You're agreeing?" Bram said.

"I'm saying if we're doing this fool thing, we do it right." Odile spat over the rail, a habit Pao had never managed to find disgusting, only comforting. "Get me the chain falls. Get me every timber aboard. And somebody feed these people, because nobody moves a boat on an empty stomach."

Bram grumbled—but he went below, and the smell of rice and salt fish began to climb out of the galley vents, and that, more than any speech, decided the matter. Hunger is honest. So is food offered freely.

* * *

They worked by lantern light, and the lantern light made the night into something older than night—something biblical, Pao thought, hauling on a rope in the company of shadows. Six lanterns hung from poles driven into the gravel bar, and their flames bent always toward the gorge, as if even fire wanted to see what had happened upstream.

The plan was Mirabel's and it was simple, which meant it was brutal. They would beach the *Kestrel* on the gravel shelf at high water mark. Then they would dig, lever, and roll her up onto log rollers laid crosswise on the old haul road, and drag her three hundred yards uphill-and-down through the dark, using the capstan, the winch, and every back aboard. At the top, they'd relaunch her into the surviving channel above the slide.

Simple. Brutal. Possible, maybe, if the night stayed dry and the crew stayed whole.

The first hour nearly broke them.

Getting the boat onto the ways was a fight between the river and everything they loved. The current wanted the *Kestrel* broadside; Mirabel wanted her straight; Odile screamed numbers from the stern that Pao couldn't hear over the water and understood anyway, because after a month aboard you learned to hear with your eyes. They grounded her with a grinding crunch that Pao felt in his teeth, and then she settled into the gravel with a groan like an animal giving up, and for one terrible moment nobody moved, everybody listening to the hull, waiting for the sound of a seam opening.

It didn't come. Odile went over the side into thigh-deep water with a lantern held high and ran her hands along the planking, foot by foot, and came up saying, "Sound. Sound all round. Move, move, we're burning dark."

Then the digging started.

Pao had dug ditches before. He'd dug graves, once, in another life he kept folded up small in the back of his skull. But he had never dug like this—four people in a trench under the keel, passing baskets of gravel hand to hand while the fifth held the light, the gravel coming out wet and heavy and endless, the boat above them seeming less like a vessel than a sky about to fall. His shoulders burned. His palms split early and he wrapped them with strips of shirt and kept digging, because Ines was digging beside him, twelve years of city softness burned off her in a single week on the river, and she wasn't complaining, so neither would he.

It was Bram who broke first—and it wasn't anger, exactly, though it wore anger's clothes.

"This is madness," he said, straightening up out of the trench, lantern light jumping on his sweat-slick face. "Madness for a map. Madness for a dying man's errand. I signed on to cook, Captain. I signed on to run cargo and keep my head down and my name my own. Nobody told me I'd be burying myself under a boat in the dark for a stranger's secrets."

"Nobody told you," Mirabel agreed, from the other end of the trench. He hadn't stopped working. His shovel rose and fell, rose and fell, patient as breathing. "What did they tell you, Bram?"

And that question—quiet, genuinely curious, thrown out like a line into dark water—did something strange to the night. Bram opened his mouth. Closed it. Pao saw him look at Odile, and Odile look away, and Ines go very still with her basket half-raised, and Pao understood suddenly that they were all standing in the same trench whether they'd admitted it or not: a cook running from a kitchen where someone had died, an engineer running from a marriage, a girl running from a father, a captain running from a name, and Pao himself, running from nothing he could say aloud even now, even here.

"That's not fair," Bram said hoarsely.

"No," Mirabel said. "None of it is. Keep digging."

For a moment Pao thought it would curdle—that the resentment would set like concrete and the night would end with the *Kestrel* beached forever and the crew scattered into the dark, each one walking a different direction home. He felt it balanced on a knife edge, the whole fragile found-family of them, and he hated Mirabel a little for gambling with it, and loved him for trusting it to hold.

It was Ines who tipped it.

She climbed out of the trench, walked to the galley, and came back with the coffee pot and six tin cups. She poured without asking, handed them out one by one—Bram first, then Odile, then Pao, then Mirabel last, though her father got his carried down to the cabin—and said, "My father spent twelve years proving he didn't need anybody. Look how that ended. Drink your coffee. Then help me move this boat, because I am not letting the Corvus Line take it from us while we argue."

Silence. Steam rising. Somewhere upriver, the blocked channel muttered to itself, patient as a debt collector.

Bram drank his coffee. Then he set down the cup, rolled up his sleeves, and said, "Well. Somebody has to supervise," and climbed back into the trench, and the night went on.

* * *

By midnight the keel was bare, and the rollers went under her one by one—peeled logs, greased with tallow from Bram's stores, each one placed with a care Pao usually saw given only to explosives or infants. The capstan stood on the haul road above, anchored to a stump older than the logging days, and Odile rigged the falls with a neatness that was almost tender, checking each hitch twice, cursing softly in two languages.

"Heave," Mirabel said, and they heaved.

The *Kestrel* moved.

Not much. An inch, maybe two, with a sound like a whale clearing its throat, and then the rollers took her weight and she crept up off the gravel and onto the land, and Pao—who had lived his whole life believing boats belonged to water the way birds belong to air—felt something crack open in his chest. She was flying. Slowly, terribly, by muscle and rope and stubbornness, but flying.

They moved her in surges. Heave, rest, re-block, re-roll, heave again. The haul road climbed through the dark, and the lanterns had to leapfrog forward, and between surges the silence of the gorge pressed in so completely that Pao could hear his own blood. Once, far off, something screamed—a bird, a cat, the wind through a hollow tree—and nobody spoke of it, and everybody listened harder for a while.

It was during the fourth surge that the rope parted.

Not the main fall—one of the guy lines, an old piece that should have been retired years ago. It snapped with a report like a rifle and the load shifted, and the *Kestrel* leaned, and Pao watched the whole world tilt as three tons of boat began to roll sideways off the rollers toward the trench where Odile was kneeling.

He didn't think. Later he would remember not thinking—remember only the lantern light, the shadow of the hull sweeping over him, and his own body hitting Odile at the waist and driving them both down the slope in a tangle of limbs and gravel, rolling, rolling, until they slammed into a root mass and stopped, breathless, alive, the boat settling above them with a groan and holding.

For a moment they lay there in the dirt, cheek to cheek, gasping.

"You," Odile said finally, "are heavier than you look."

"You're welcome," Pao wheezed.

Above them, boots came sliding and scrambling. Hands found them—Bram's grip on Pao's collar, Ines hauling Odile's arm, Mirabel's voice saying, "Talk to me, talk to me," in a tone Pao had never heard from him, raw and unguarded, the voice of a man who had already buried too many people and refused to practice for one more.

"We're whole," Odile said. "We're whole. Re-rig your damn rope."

And that was the moment, Pao thought later—lying in the gravel with the boat looming over him and four people's hands on him, dirty and bleeding and laughing in the ugly way people laugh when terror drains out through the soles of the feet—that was the moment the crew stopped being a crew and became the other thing. The thing none of them had words for. The thing you couldn't sign on for and couldn't quit. He looked up at their faces ringed against the lantern glow, strangers a month ago, liars all of them, and thought: *these are mine now. Whatever else is true, whatever names we're hiding, these are mine.*

They re-rigged. They jacked the boat back level with screw jacks and prayers. They rolled her the last hundred yards in a silence that wasn't empty anymore but full—full of shared blisters and shared coffee and the memory of hands catching hands in the dark.

Dawn found them at the top of the haul road, lowering the *Kestrel* down the last gentle slope into the living channel above the slide. The water received her like a mother taking back a runaway child, and she floated, and Odile went over the rail one more time with her lantern and her hands, and came up saying, "Sound. Sound all round," and this time nobody moved right away. This time they just stood on the deck together, filthy and staggering, watching the sun come up the gorge wall in bands of copper and rose.

Elias Ines had been carried up from the cabin in a chair at some point in the small hours—he'd insisted, waving off his daughter, wanting to see it—and now he sat propped against the wheelhouse with a blanket around his legs, watching the new channel open ahead of them. His face was grey as ash, but his eyes were bright.

"Well," he said, when Mirabel rang for slow ahead. "I have been hauled over a mountain by the finest crew on this river, and I intend to tell the story badly for the rest of my life, however long that is."

"However long that is," Ines said quietly, and took his hand.

Pao went to the bow and took up his sounding pole, because the channel ahead was unknown water and someone had to read it. But as the *Kestrel* gathered way and slid upriver into the morning, he glanced back once—at the portage road, at the scarred gravel, at the place where a rope had snapped and a family had caught itself—and made himself a promise he didn't examine too closely.

Then he looked forward again, and his stomach dropped.

Smoke. Thin and black and deliberate, rising from a bend of the river perhaps two miles upstream. A signal fire, tended. Someone marking their position—or announcing it—to whoever was watching from the water behind.
