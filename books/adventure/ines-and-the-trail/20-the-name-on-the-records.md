## 20. The Name on the Records

The fever came for Elias Ines on the third night past the Narrows, and it came the way the river comes for a moored boat—not all at once, but patiently, working at the knots.

Mirabel heard it first through the bulkhead of the aft cabin: a low, wet rattle of breath that had no business coming from a living man. Then Bram's boots on the deck above, crossing to the galley and back twice, which meant he was making willow bark tea and hating every minute of it, because Bram hated being needed more than he hated anything else in this world. Then Odile's voice, quiet, asking Pao how much longer to the deep pool below Kestrel Bend where they could anchor through the night without dragging anchor.

Mirabel stood at the wheel with his hands on the spokes and did not go below. That was the rule he'd built his life on, one plank at a time: captain doesn't hover, captain doesn't pry, captain keeps his eyes on the water and his heart in a locked box under the floorboards. He had ferried dying men before. He knew what the sound of it did to a boat—to the whole crew, the way grief moves through a hull like water through a seam, finding every gap you didn't know was there.

But at midnight, when Ines's voice rose—thin, cracked, calling a name that wasn't her own—Mirabel found himself going down the companionway anyway, telling himself it was about the manifest.

That was the lie he would carry afterward. It was always about the manifest.

* * *

The aft cabin smelled of camphor and river damp and the sweetish rot-smell that Mirabel had learned, twelve years ago, in another sickroom, in another life. A single lantern swung on its gimbal, throwing Elias's shadow up the wall in long, shuddering strokes, so that the old man seemed to be made of moving parts, none of them attached to each other.

Ines sat beside the bunk with her father's hand in both of hers. She had been crying, but she had stopped by the time Mirabel ducked through the doorway; she had that kind of discipline, the kind that costs something. Her eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely steady.

"He's been talking," she said. "For an hour. Names I don't know. Numbers."

"Delirium does that." Mirabel kept his voice level. "Fever burns the mind like a grass fire. It runs over everything and leaves nothing but ash and shapes."

"It's not shapes." She turned the lantern wick down a fraction, and the shadows leaned in closer. "He said *the ledgers are in the strongbox, tell them the ledgers are in the strongbox*. He said it four times. Papa doesn't repeat himself when he's well. Why would he start now?"

Because dying men are honest, Mirabel thought. Because the fever strips a man down to whatever he couldn't afford to say while he was upright. He looked at Elias—the dust-colored man from the sandbar, eleven days ago and a lifetime ago—and felt again that old unwanted pull, curiosity wearing charity's coat.

"I need to check him," Mirabel said. "Roll him a little. Fever like this, a man can drown in his own—" He didn't finish. Some things you don't say in front of a daughter.

She helped him turn Elias onto his side, and the old man weighed almost nothing, a bundle of sticks wrapped in skin, and when they settled him back his jacket—Bram had put the jacket over him against the night chill even though the cabin was warm as a kiln—slipped off the bunk's edge, and the inner pocket gaped, and Mirabel saw the corner of oiled silk.

The map. Folded small, the way Elias had carried it since the sandbar, since before the sandbar, maybe since the mine. Mirabel should have looked away. He told himself later that he meant to look away.

What happened instead was that Elias opened his eyes.

They were clear for a moment—fever-clear, that terrible lucidity the dying get, when the fog parts and they see straight down into themselves. His hand came up and closed around Mirabel's wrist with a strength that had no business existing, and he said, distinctly:

"You'll want to read them. Everyone wants to read them. Go on, then. Read."

And then his eyes rolled back and the fog took him again, and his grip went slack, and Mirabel was left kneeling there with the jacket in his hands and the pocket open and Ines watching him across the bunk with an expression he couldn't read and didn't want to.

"Captain," she said carefully. "You don't have to."

"No," Mirabel agreed. "I don't."

He reached into the pocket anyway.

* * *

It wasn't the map. The map was folded separately, deeper in, and he set it aside untouched—that mattered to him, that mattered enormously, though he could not have said why. What came out instead was a leather folio, cracked and swollen with damp, tied with a length of tarred twine. Inside: papers. A dozen pages, maybe fifteen, copied in a clerk's careful hand, and beneath them, wrapped in waxed cloth, a photograph gone soft at the corners.

The top page was headed with a name Mirabel didn't know—*Serein Consolidated Mining Company, Underground Operations Ledger, Excerpt*—and dated twelve years and two months ago.

Twelve years and two months.

He knew the date the way you know your own heartbeat. He had measured every mile since then against it.

"Captain." Ines's voice again, sharper now. "Those are my father's. Whatever you think you're doing—"

"I'm not doing anything." He heard how it sounded. He set the folio on his knee and did not close it. "Your father told me to read."

"My father told a wall to read. He doesn't know what he's saying."

"Then I'll stop." And he would have—he swears it still, in the honest hours, that he would have closed the folio and handed it back and gone up to the wheel and steered until dawn and never asked—but Elias chose that moment to speak again from the bottom of his fever, and what he said was:

"Eleven names. Eleven men. Count them, boy. Count them and tell me if I'm mad."

Ines went very still. "Boy," she repeated softly. "He called you *boy*. Papa hasn't called anyone that since—" She stopped. Recalculated. Mirabel watched her do it, watched the arithmetic move behind her eyes, and knew she'd arrived somewhere he didn't want her.

The lantern swung. The shadow of Elias's hand crawled up the wall, opening and closing.

Mirabel untied the twine.

* * *

The ledger pages were copies—good ones, the ink sharp, the columns ruled true. Ore tonnage. Shift rosters. Timber requisitions for shoring the adits. And then, beginning three pages in, a second hand, cramped and angry, annotating the margins: *shoring withdrawn from No. 3 drift, cost saving per directive*. *Water reported rising in lower workings, complaint filed, complaint withdrawn*. *Men ordered below despite standing water, per directive of—*

Per directive of.

The name was there. The name was there in the clerk's neat hand, in the signature block, in the column of men who had signed off on sending eleven miners down into a flooded drift rather than lose a season's contract, and Mirabel read it once, and then he read it again, because the first time his eyes must have lied to him, they must have, because the world did not work that way, rivers did not run backward, and dead men did not sign their names to murder.

**Aurel Hartwell.**

His father's name. Not his father by blood—his father by rescue, by choice, by twelve years of Sundays and shop lessons and the smell of pipe smoke and the way the old man used to say *measure twice, cut once, and measure your life the same way, lad.* Aurel Hartwell, who had taken in a half-drowned orphan of a scandal and taught him engines and honesty and how to be a man who paid his debts. Aurel Hartwell, who had stood trial for it—stood trial for negligence, for falsified reports, for the eleven names—and gone to prison with his spine straight and his mouth shut, refusing to the last to name the men above him, the directors, the money, the machine.

*I took the fall,* everyone had assumed. *A good man scapegoated.* Mirabel had built his whole exile on that assumption. He had fled the trial, fled the town, fled his own name—Hartwell, God, *Hartwell*, he hadn't spoken it aloud in twelve years—because staying meant watching them bury the only father he'd ever had under the mud of a courtroom, and he could not bear it, so he had become Mirabel Montrose, a man with no past, no passengers, no names, measuring his life out in safe miles down safe water.

And here, in a clerk's copy, in a dead prospector's folio, on a riverboat he captained under a false name: Aurel Hartwell's signature. Not coerced. Not forged—the hand was right, Mirabel knew that hand better than his own, the little hitch in the *r*, the heavy downstroke of the final *l*. Aurel had signed the directives. Aurel had ordered the shoring pulled. Aurel had signed off on the shift roster that sent eleven men into the water.

The boat rocked. Or Mirabel rocked. There was no difference anymore.

"Captain." Ines was on her feet. "You've gone white as bone. What is it? What name—"

"Nothing." His voice came out of someone else's mouth. "It's nothing. Old business."

"That is not *nothing*—" She reached for the folio and he moved it back, an inch, instinctive, and the motion between them was suddenly the whole world: her hand out, the papers against his chest, the lantern swinging, Elias breathing his wet broken breath below them both.

"Give it to me," she said quietly. "Or tell me why you won't."

* * *

Later—he would never remember getting there—Mirabel was on the foredeck with the wheel in his hands and the river sliding black and silver under the moon, and the folio was below, returned to the jacket, returned to the bunk, and Ines had not followed him, which was worse than if she had.

Pao found him there near dawn. The boy climbed the steps to the pilothouse the way he did everything, sideways, watchful, and stood at the rail a few feet off and said nothing for a long time, which was Pao's particular gift.

"Odile says we make Kestrel Bend pool by midmorning," he offered finally. "Anchor through the heat. Then—" He glanced at Mirabel. "Then the channel forks. South fork's the long way around. Three extra days. North fork goes through the Devil's Throat."

"I know what the north fork goes through."

"Yeah." Pao picked at a fleck of rust on the rail. "Only. With the boss-man sick, and the Corvus boat behind us gaining every day, and the monsoon clock ticking—three days might be a luxury we don't got. Odile thinks we should run the Throat. Bram thinks we should run the Throat. Ines—" a pause, delicate, "—Ines says it's the captain's call, but she said it looking at you like she wanted to say something else entirely."

Mirabel said nothing. The river said nothing. Somewhere downstream, dark water waited in a slot of rock that had eaten better boats than his.

"Cap." Pao turned to face him fully. "You look like a man who read something he shouldn't have."

"There's nothing aboard this boat I shouldn't read. I'm the captain."

"Sure." Pao nodded slowly, unconvinced, kind about it. "Only, whoever you were before you were Captain Montrose—I hope he wasn't as alone as you look right now."

He went below. Mirabel stayed at the wheel and watched the sun come up the color of brass, and thought about a workshop that no longer existed, and a voice that had said *measure twice*, and eleven men underground in the dark with the water rising, and whether a man who loved you could still be the man who killed them, and which of those facts a son was allowed to keep.

Behind them, far down the river, a smudge of smoke stood up against the morning sky. The Corvus Line, closing the distance, patient as fever.

Ahead, the channel forked, and the north fork sang its low ugly song through the rocks.

Three days, or the Throat. Mercy, or speed. The past he'd buried, or the truth he'd spent twelve years drowning.

Elias coughed below decks, once, long, and did not stop quickly.

Mirabel rang the engine bell twice—all hands—and turned the wheel toward the north fork.
