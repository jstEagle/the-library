## 21. Run the Devil's Throat

I have rebuilt engines in the dark with my hands bleeding. I have held a boiler steady through a storm that peeled the roof off a boathouse like the lid off a tin of sardines. I have been afraid before—properly afraid, the kind that sits in your spine and hums.

But I had never stood at the head of the Devil's Throat and looked at what we were about to do and thought, calmly, *this is how we die.*

The Throat is not on any chart worth trusting. The old pilots called it unrunnable the way they called certain mountains unclimbable—not as a challenge but as an epitaph. It was a half-mile of channel where the Serein dropped forty feet through a gap barely wider than the boat, the water compressed until it stopped behaving like water at all and became something with intentions. From the bluff above, you could see the whole anatomy of it: the green tongue of smooth water feeding in, the false calm, and then the drop, where the river folded over itself in three successive holes, each one big enough to swallow a skiff whole and keep it. Between the holes, the current ran so fast and so shallow over the rock shelf that it sheared white without even forming waves—a sheet of violence stretched flat as glass.

And below it, the outflow: a boil of confused water a quarter mile long, whirlpools spinning in it like slow black eyes, and then, mercifully, the river gathering itself back up and remembering how to be a river again.

We stood on the bluff because Mirabel wanted us to see it first. That was his way—he never lied to you about a rapid, never softened it. He'd stand there with his hands in his pockets and let the thing speak for itself.

"That's the shortcut," he said. "Two days around the long bend, or four minutes through that."

Nobody laughed. Even Pao, who laughed at funerals, kept his mouth shut.

* * *

We had lost the day to the fuel line, and the river had spent that day growing. You could hear it now if you listened—the Serein had developed a voice overnight, a low continuous roar from upstream where the monsoon was already chewing through the headwaters two days ahead of forecast. The gauge rocks we'd marked coming up were drowned. The eddy lines had gone ragged. Every hour, the river gained a hand's width, and every hour it gained, the long bend around Mount Ceres got slower and more dangerous in its own right—flooded timber, strainers, channels that would be closed by morning.

So the arithmetic was simple and terrible. Around was slow and might kill us anyway. Through was fast and would probably kill us quickly.

Mirabel laid it out on the deck that evening with the chart weighted down by four coffee mugs, and he did something I had never seen him do in six years aboard the *Vesper Bell*: he asked.

"I won't order it," he said. "This isn't a captain's decision. This one belongs to everybody." He looked at each of us in turn. "If anyone says no, we go around. I'll run her alone through the Throat before I'll take a crew through against their will."

Bram said nothing for a long time. He was turning his mug in his hands, that big careful way of his, like everything he touched might break or might be the last of its kind. "My grandmother used to say," he finally offered, "the river takes what you clutch and gives back what you open your hands to." He shrugged. "I don't know if that helps."

"It doesn't," Pao said.

"It's true, though," Bram said.

Pao looked at the chart, then at me. "Odile. You're the one who has to keep her together if she comes apart. What do you say?"

That was the question, wasn't it. Everyone else's stake ended when the boat cleared the boil. Mine began there—if we cracked a seam in the Throat, I was the one who'd be stuffing mattresses into the hole while the river drank us. I thought about saying no. I thought about the long bend, the flooded willows standing in eight feet of new water with their branches just under the surface, invisible, patient, the perfect strainer waiting to wrap itself around our hull and hold us under while the river piled up behind us. I had seen what a strainer does. Everyone who works water has seen it, once, and never forgets.

"I say," I heard myself answer, "that if we go around, we die slowly, and if we go through, we die fast, and I've always preferred my bad news quick." I tapped the chart at the throat of the gorge. "But we do it my way. Engine off at the top. Oars only through the drop. And everyone in life vests, lashed gear, hatches dogged—and nobody, *nobody*, touches the throttle until I say the word."

Mirabel nodded slowly. "Your way."

Ines hadn't spoken. She stood at the rail with her arms crossed, watching the dark line of the gorge downstream, and when we all turned to her she said, "My father can't survive two more days of the long bend. He's coughing blood now. He hides it, but I hear him." Her voice didn't waver. "Through."

Elias himself, propped in the deck chair wrapped in two blankets, managed a dry rasp that might have been agreement or might have been a laugh. "I've come four thousand miles to see this claim," he wheezed. "What's another half mile."

* * *

Dawn came up gray and bruised, the light thick as tallow, and we cast off from the gravel bar with the river already pushing at us like a crowd shoving toward a door. I spent the approach hour in the engine room with my ear pressed to the hull, listening to the boat talk to me. Every vessel has a voice. The *Bell* was sixty years old and she spoke in creaks and groans and small percussive ticks, and I knew her vocabulary the way I knew my own heartbeat. That morning she sounded nervous. Or maybe that was me.

I checked everything twice, then a third time because the third time is superstition and superstition is free. Sea cocks. Bilge pumps—I rigged the spare to run off the battery bank directly, bypassing the switchboard, so that even if the panel drowned the pumps would keep turning. Spare planking cut and stacked by the engine room hatch, wedges and mallets and a bucket of oakum within arm's reach. If we took water, I would have maybe ninety seconds between hearing it and needing to be on it.

Up top, I could hear Mirabel walking Ines through the plan one last time. She'd be on the steering oar backup, amidships. Pao on the bow oars. Bram aft with the sweep. Elias lashed into the pilot house bench, which he protested and then didn't.

"Remember," Mirabel said, and his voice carried that particular flat calm he got in hard water, the calm that wasn't calm at all but fear folded very small, "the river runs the boat. We just aim it. Aim, don't fight. If you fight the Throat, the Throat wins."

The green tongue came up to meet us.

From the water, the false calm was worse than from the bluff. It was a hundred yards of glass-smooth current, deep green, almost tender, and the boat picked up speed on it without any engine at all, sliding downhill toward the noise, and the noise grew from a rumble into a roar into a sound like the sky tearing. My hands were steady on the throttle I wasn't allowed to touch. I gripped the brass rail instead and watched the lip of the drop rise in the forward portlight.

"Engine off," Mirabel called.

I killed her. The silence underneath the roar was enormous.

Then the world fell away.

* * *

I have tried since to describe those four minutes and I cannot do it honestly, because memory in terror does not work in sequence—it works in flashes, each one burned separate and complete.

The bow dropping. That first sickening plunge, my stomach arriving late, tools leaping off the bench behind me in a clatter like applause.

White. All of it white, the portlight full of boiling foam, the boat buried to the gunwales in spray, and through the hull a sound I felt in my teeth—the river hitting the rock shelf beneath us, the whole weight of a continent's runoff grinding past six inches of oak.

The first hole taking us. Not gently—we went into it bow-first and the bow went *under*, and I heard the water slap the underside of the foredeck, a boom like a door slamming in a house's bones, and then the *Bell* did the thing old boats do, the thing you pray for: she rose. Slowly, shedding tons of water, groaning like a living creature, and came up streaming.

"AIM!" Mirabel's voice, raw, cutting through everything. "LEFT OAR—HOLD—"

A glimpse of Pao at the bow oars, soaked to the skin, hauling with his whole body, his face set in an expression I'd never seen on him—no grin, no joke, just pure ferocious attention.

The second hole. We hit it quartering, which was wrong, which was sideways enough that the river grabbed the stern and started to swing us, and I felt through my boots the exact moment the hull kissed the rock shelf—a grinding shudder up through the keel, metal-on-stone, a sound no engineer ever wants to feel—and then we were through it, somehow, the keel screaming but holding, and the third hole opened ahead of us like a mouth.

We went into the third hole tail-first.

I remember that with total clarity: the stern dropping away, the bow rearing, the horizon tilting, and the sensation—brief, absolute—that the boat was standing on end, that we were going over backward, that this was it. Water came over the transom in a solid sheet. It hit the deck and exploded outward and found every seam in the boat older than I am, and somewhere below my feet, in the bilge, the *Bell* began to drink.

I was moving before I decided to move. Down the ladder into the engine room, which was already ankle-deep and rising, cold river water swirling around my boots, and I found it by sound before sight—a high thin whistle of spray jetting from the garboard seam just aft of the keelson, a crack maybe two feet long, weeping, then spitting, then pouring as the flex of the hull worked it wider.

"SEAM'S OPEN! AFT OF THE KEELSON!" I screamed up the ladder, and then I stopped talking, because talking wastes breath and breath was for work.

Oakum. Mallet. Wedge. My hands knew the sequence without me. I drove oakum into the seam with the mallet, swinging in the dark, in the wash, the water climbing my shins, my knees, the boat lurching around me like something in its death throes, and the oakum swelled and slowed the flow and the flow found another inch of seam and I drove more. Above me the pumps kicked in—I heard them, blessed mechanical hearts, thumping away—and the bilge level steadied, climbed, steadied again. A war. A slow war measured in inches.

Somewhere in the middle of it, the third hole spat us out.

I know that only because the motion changed—from the violent tumbling chaos of the drop into the long rolling confusion of the boil, the whirlpools tugging at us, the whole boat wallowing heavy with the water in her belly. I could hear Mirabel above, working the rudder lines, calling the whirlpools—"hard left—ease—HARD left"—reading the boils on the surface the way other men read print, steering us through the spinning black eyes of the outflow while I knelt in the dark with a mallet, fighting for every inch of our lives.

The last wedge went home. The flow dropped to a seep. The pumps could handle a seep.

I sat back on my heels in three inches of river water, in the dark, shaking so hard the mallet chattered against the floor plates, and I put both palms flat on the hull and I said, out loud, to the boat, "Not yet. Not yet, old girl. Stay with me."

She creaked. Which from her, I chose to believe, meant yes.

* * *

When I came up the ladder, blinking into the gray light, the boil was behind us and the river had remembered itself—broad, brown, furious still, but a river again, running fast and straight between walls of dripping stone. Rain had begun, fine and needling. Everyone on deck was drenched, lashed cargo awash, the deck littered with debris the drop had shaken loose.

They were laughing.

All of them—Pao flat on his back on the hatch cover, wheezing laughter at the sky; Bram holding the sweep across his shoulders like a yoke, grinning so wide his face looked ready to split; Ines with her hair plastered to her skull, gripping the steering oar she'd never once let go of, laughing and crying at the same time without seeming to know which was which. Even Elias, gray as ash in his blankets, had his eyes closed and his mouth curved, breathing, alive.

Mirabel stood at the wheel, and he wasn't laughing. He looked ten years older than he had at dawn, and lighter too, some way I couldn't name—as though the Throat had taken something out of him along with the paint off the hull. When he saw me he crossed to the ladder and gripped my forearm, hard, and didn't say anything, because there wasn't anything. Engineers know. You don't thank a person for that kind of work; you just hold their arm a second longer than strangers would.

"How bad?" he asked.

"She'll hold. But she needs a proper haul-out—new planking, recaulked, the works—or she'll never survive the flood run downstream." I looked back upstream at the wall of spray where the Throat still roared, having us now in its past tense. "Assuming there is a flood run."

"There'll be a flood run." He said it quietly, looking downstream, and there was something in his face beyond the rapid, beyond the relief—something coiled and private, a man carrying a weight he hadn't been carrying yesterday. He'd been strange since the night with the manifest, stranger since; distant in a way that had nothing to do with the river. I didn't ask. Nobody asks Mirabel anything. That's the deal we all signed.

But later—much later, near dusk, when the rain had settled in earnest and I was below checking the pumps one final time—I came up for air and saw something I wasn't meant to see.

Ines was at the stern rail, and she was watching Mirabel, who stood apart at the bow in the rain with his back to all of us. And in his hands, half-shielded from the wind, was paper. A letter, by its shape, old and soft at the folds. He stood over it a long moment, and then he struck a match, cupped it, and touched the flame to the corner—and even at that distance, even in the rain-haze, I saw his face as the fire took it. It was not the face of a man burning trash.

It was the face of a man burning the last bridge home.

Ines saw it too. I watched her see it—watched her push off the rail and start forward through the rain toward him, her jaw set, her step deliberate, a woman who had decided that whatever the captain of the *Vesper Bell* was hiding, tonight was the night it came out of the dark.
