## 22. What Mirabel Buried

I couldn't sleep.

The boat lay tied up in a narrow eddy below a shelf of black stone, the kind of anchorage Mirabel called a *pocket*—a place the river forgot to bother. We had made good miles that day, enough that even Odile had allowed herself a smile over the engine housing, and Bram had produced a stew with more meat than gruel in it, which was his way of saying he thought we might live. Elias slept in the stern cabin under two blankets, his breathing a thin whistle like wind through a keyhole. Pao had taken first watch and fallen asleep in it, sitting upright against the wheelhouse door, his hat tipped over his eyes like a boy feigning innocence.

And I lay in my bunk listening to the river say the same sentence over and over—*go on, go on, go on*—and found I could not answer it.

So I went up.

The night was the deep blue-black of the gorge at altitude, stars crowded close as moths around a lamp. The air smelled of wet granite and woodsmoke from our own stove, banked hours ago but still breathing through the flue. My boots were quiet on the deck boards; twelve years aboard my father's boats as a girl, then twelve years away from them, and still my feet knew where to step without sound. Some inheritances you can't refuse no matter how far you run.

He was at the stern rail, past the cargo crates, past the coiled line, in the little sheltered notch behind the wheelhouse where the current's voice went soft. He didn't hear me—or pretended not to—and for a moment I simply watched him, because watching him was something I had gotten good at and was not proud of.

Mirabel Montrose stood with his back to me, shoulders squared the way a man stands when he is about to do something he has already decided to be ashamed of. In his hands was a piece of paper. In his other hand, a match.

The flame went up small and yellow in all that dark, and I saw the letter clearly for the half-second before it caught: heavy stock, a city watermark, an official seal pressed in wax the color of old blood. Not a love letter. Not a family letter. A letter with *weight* to it, the kind that arrives in envelopes stamped by institutions.

"Wait," I said.

He turned fast—faster than a man that size should turn—and the match guttered in the moving air between us. For one heartbeat we were both lit from below, two faces floating over the black water, and I saw something in his expression I had never seen there: not anger, not the careful blankness he wore like oilskin. Fear. Plain and human and gone again almost before I could name it.

"You should be asleep," he said.

"So should you."

"It's nothing." He said it the way people say *it's nothing* when it is the only thing. "Old mail. I'm cleaning out a drawer."

"In a riverboat?" I came closer, slowly, the way you approach a wounded animal or a loaded winch. "You don't have drawers. You have a seabag and three shirts and a tin of tea you guard like scripture. What letter takes twelve years to burn?"

The match died. He looked down at it, and then at the paper, and the paper was trembling—not from wind. His hands were steady on a tiller in the Widow's Teeth; I had seen them hold true while the whole world churned green around us. But this piece of paper made them shake.

"You know what's funny," he said, and his voice had gone somewhere flat and far away, a voice reading off a chart. "I've burned this letter four times now. In my head. On four different rivers. Always imagined just letting it go, ash on the water, done. And every time I got as far as the match—" He stopped. Started again. "Paper keeps better than men do. That's the joke. That's the whole joke."

"Mirabel."

"That's not my name."

The river said *go on*. The stars leaned in. Somewhere upstream an owl called once, twice, and gave up.

I had known it, of course. Or known *something*—known that a man does not build himself a name like a wall unless he has something to hide behind it. Two liars recognize each other; I'd felt it the day I booked passage under my mother's maiden name and watched him read the register and say nothing, absolutely nothing, his silence a courtesy and a confession both. But knowing a man is lying about his name and hearing him say so are different animals. One is a suspicion. The other is a door opening onto a dark room, and you cannot un-open it.

"What is your name, then?" I asked. Quietly. Because if I said it loudly it would become a demand, and demands make men bolt, and I could not afford for him to bolt. Not anymore. Not after the Devil's Throat, after the night portage, after he'd taught me to read water with his hand hovering near mine and never touching it. Whatever he was, he was ours now. I needed him to understand that before he spoke, or he never would.

He looked at the letter a long time. Then he did the last thing I expected: he held it out to me.

"Read it," he said. "You've earned worse than this. Read it, and then I'll tell you the rest, because the rest isn't written down anywhere. It only exists in me. And I am so tired"—and here his voice cracked, just once, a hairline fracture across all that competence—"I am so tired of being the only place it lives."

I took the paper. It was heavier than paper should be, or seemed so. The seal was broken, had been broken long ago and re-folded many times; the creases were going soft as cloth. By the light of a second match, which he struck and held without ceremony, I read:

*To the person known as Mirabel Montrose: This office has concluded its review of the matter of the Serein Consolidated Mining disaster, inquest of twelve years prior. New evidence has come to light regarding testimony given at said inquest. Should you possess knowledge of the whereabouts of [the name was water-stained, a ghost of ink] or documentation pertaining thereto, this office requests—*

I stopped reading aloud in my head. Because there, in the middle of the bureaucratic gray language, was a name I knew. Not well. But I knew it. Everyone who had grown up in the mining towns along the Serein headwaters knew it—the name of the man they blamed, the man who signed the order, the man whose trial had been the biggest thing to happen in that country in a generation. The man they said killed eleven miners with a pen.

The man who, if the ledgers in my father's strongbox told any truth at all, had been guilty of exactly what the papers always said.

"Mirabel," I said again, and then, understanding arriving like cold water, "no. You're not—you weren't born to that name. You *took* it. You took the name of the man they convicted."

The match burned down toward his fingers. He didn't seem to notice.

"He raised me," Mirabel said.

* * *

We sat on the hatch cover behind the wheelhouse, shoulder to shoulder, the dead letter folded between us like a third passenger. He talked the way rivers cut: slowly at first, finding his channel, then with a force that seemed to surprise even him.

"I never knew my mother. Or I knew her for nine days, depending on which story you believe. There was a flood the year I was born—the Serein does that, you know, she gives and she takes—and after it there were a lot of children with no names attached. He found me. Or I found him; he used to argue it was me, crawling toward him in the mud like I knew a meal ticket when I saw one." A breath that might have been a laugh. "Aldous Vane. That was him. Company man, company house, company coat. He ran the assay office at Corben's Deep, and he was the loneliest man I ever met until I met myself.

"He taught me everything. Letters, numbers, how to hold a hammer, how to lose at cards gracefully, which is harder. He taught me that a man's word is the only collateral that doesn't rust. And every Sunday he'd take me up on the ridge above the mine and show me the works—all those buildings, the headframe, the smoke—and he'd say, *Someday you'll run something bigger than this, boy, and when you do, remember what the ground costs.* I thought it was a lesson about mining." Mirabel's jaw worked. "It was a confession. I just didn't have the ears for it yet.

"I was seventeen when the eleven died. You know the story—everyone knows the story. Timbering failed on the lower level. Eleven men. And the inquest found the timbering orders had been signed by Aldous Vane, against engineering recommendation, to save cost. His signature. Real signature. Real orders. He never denied it. He stood up in that courtroom with his company coat brushed clean and said, *I signed them. They are my responsibility,* and he would not say another word, not one word, though his lawyers begged him, though I begged him. I sat in that gallery every day for six weeks and he never once looked up at me. I think—I think looking at me would have broken whatever was holding him together, and he needed to be held together, because someone had to hang for it and he had decided it would be him."

"And was it him?" I asked. I had to. The question had been living in my chest since the night I'd seen the manifest through my father's fever, since the name on that page had rearranged the furniture of my whole life. "Was it really?"

"I spent twelve years believing it wasn't." He said it simply, terribly. "That's why I ran. Not from the law—they never wanted me, I was nobody, a ward, a witness to nothing. I ran from the *not knowing*. Because he told me one thing, the night before they took him down to the county jail. He put his hands on my shoulders—he had big hands, miner's hands, even working a desk—and he said, *Boy, whatever they print about me, you go be honest. Honesty's the only inheritance I've got left to give you, and I mean to make it worth having.* And then he smiled at me like he knew something I didn't, and I have spent twelve years trying to decide whether that smile meant *I'm innocent* or *I'm sorry* or *run, boy, run, and don't ever stop running.*

"So I ran. Changed my name to his—God knows why, some twisted loyalty, some punishment I assigned myself, wearing the name of a convicted man like a hair shirt. Took work that kept me moving. Made rules. No names, no pasts, no passengers. Built a boat full of people just like me and called it a crew so I wouldn't have to call it what it was." He finally looked at me, and his eyes in the starlight were wet and furious and free, all at once. "A family of liars, Ines. I collected strays because strays don't ask questions. And then your father comes aboard coughing his lungs out with a map on oiled silk, and I let him, because—because I looked at him and saw a man carrying something too heavy, and I recognized the weight. And now the ledgers say—"

He stopped. His hand went to his pocket, where I knew he'd been keeping the scrap of the manifest he'd torn loose during Elias's delirium, the page with the names, the page he thought none of us had seen him take. I let him keep his secret a moment longer. He'd earned the courtesy; I'd had mine.

"The ledgers say what they say," I finished for him, gently. "And you're afraid they say your father was guilty after all. That he wasn't a martyr. That the smile meant *sorry*."

"Yes." The word came out of him like a tooth coming out of a jaw—relief and agony in the same motion. "Yes. And I don't know which is worse. If he was innocent, then the world murdered an honest man and I owe his ghost a war. If he was guilty, then everything I am is built on a lie, and the boy he raised—the boy who believed in him—is a fool, and the only honest thing I ever inherited was nothing at all."

The river said *go on*. I reached over and took the letter out of the space between us, and before he could stop me, before I could stop myself, I fed it to the lantern flame we'd set on the hatch boards, and held it until the fire walked up the creases and the seal bubbled and the bureaucratic words curled into black lace and were gone. Ash on the water. Done.

"Why did you do that?" His voice was hoarse.

"Because you've burned it four times in your head and it never worked," I said. "Maybe it needs help. Maybe it needs a witness. Everything true needs a witness, Mirabel—that's the whole point of my father's boxes of paper. Things witnessed can't be buried." I looked at him. "But things forgiven need one too. So consider yourself witnessed. Both ways."

He stared at me. And then something happened to his face that I will carry to my grave: it *unclenched*. Twelve years of holding came undone all at once, not in tears—he was not a man built for tears, or thought he wasn't—but in breath. A long, shuddering exhale, like a boiler venting after miles of pressure, and his shoulders came down from around his ears, and he was suddenly just a tired man on a boat at night instead of a fortress.

"Aldous Vane," he said, testing it. Saying it aloud for the first time in twelve years, to another living soul. "My name is Aldous Vane the second. Named for him. He insisted. Said names were anchors." A ragged laugh. "I thought he meant it kindly."

"Anchors aren't kind," I said. "They're necessary. There's a difference."

Down the eddy, the current slid past the hull, patient as ever. Above us Pao snored gently against the wheelhouse door. From the stern cabin, my father's whistle-breath rose and fell, rose and fell, each cycle a little thinner than the last, and we both heard it, and we both pretended we hadn't, because some clocks you don't look at directly.

"There's something else," Mirabel said at last. He pulled the torn scrap of manifest from his pocket and held it, but did not give it to me. His thumb moved over the edge of it, back and forth, back and forth, like a man smoothing a wrinkle that wouldn't smooth. "Your father's ledgers. I've been turning it over all night, every way I can turn it, and I can't make it come out right. If those records are true—if they prove what he says they prove—then they don't just clear the dead. They name the living. Men who are still rich, still powerful, still signing orders. And one of the names on that page—" His thumb stopped moving. "One of the names is the reason I exist, Ines. One way or the other. Guilty or innocent, martyr or murderer, everything I buried is in that strongbox, and tomorrow we're going to open it, and I don't know yet whether I'm digging up a vindication or a grave."

"Then don't decide tonight," I said. "Decide on the water. You taught me that. The river doesn't ask you to know what's around the bend. It just asks you to read what's in front of you and commit."

He looked at me for a long moment, and then he laughed—a real one, short and surprised out of him—and folded the scrap away.

"When did you get wise?" he asked.

"Somewhere between the Widow's Teeth and here," I said. "You were busy. You missed it."

But as I climbed down into the dark of the cabin and lay listening to the river's one sentence, I felt the scrap of paper in his pocket like a splinter in my own skin. Because I had seen the manifest too, that night of his delirium, and there was a name on it I had not yet told Mirabel—a name my father had hidden on the final page, a name that changed everything, that would turn his grief inside out.

Tomorrow, I promised myself. Tomorrow, when the box was open and the truth was loose and there was no burying anything ever again.

Above me, faint through the deck boards, I heard Mirabel climb to the wheel and take the watch he no longer needed to escape into—and the river carried us on through the dark, toward the claim, toward the strongbox, toward the last thing either of us had left to lose.
