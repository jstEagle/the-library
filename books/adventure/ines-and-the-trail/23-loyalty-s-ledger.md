## 23. Loyalty's Ledger

The fire had burned down to a red eye by the time Ines came looking for me.

I heard her before I saw her—the particular care of her footsteps on the shingle, each stone tested before it bore her weight. She had learned that from the river, or from me; I wasn't sure anymore which of us was the teacher and which the student. The Serein ran low and dark behind her, muttering over its gravel bars, and above us the gorge walls stood like the shoulders of something sleeping, waiting for the monsoon to wake it.

She found me at the waterline with the strongbox open on my knees.

* * *

I should explain what a man looks like when he has spent an hour reading his own damnation.

Elias slept in the shelter of the overturned skiff, propped against a coil of rope, his breath coming in the wet, rattling rhythm that had become as familiar to this crew as Odile's engine-song. The fever had him again. His hands moved in his sleep—small motions, turning pages that weren't there, signing things, sealing things—and every few minutes he murmured a name I did not know, or thought I didn't, until one of them landed in my chest like a hook set deep.

But that came later. First there was the ledger itself.

The strongbox had given up its secrets without ceremony: three ledgers bound in oilcloth, a bundle of photographs held together with a rusted paperclip, and a single envelope sealed with wax, addressed in a hand I recognized before I understood why I recognized it. The ledgers smelled of the mine they'd come from—dust and lamp oil and something older, something like fear kept in a closed room for twelve years.

I told myself I was only checking them for water damage. That was even true, at first. A captain checks his cargo. It is not prying; it is prudence.

Then I opened the second ledger to its final season, and the columns stopped being numbers.

Eleven names. Eleven men entered in a neat bookkeeper's hand, each with a date beside it, each date within nine days of the others, each entry followed by the same notation in the margin: *settled.* Not wages settled. Not accounts settled. Settled the way you settle a debt you hope no one will ever audit. Eleven men who had gone into the adit at the Verrone Claim on a morning when the timbering had already groaned for a week, when the water in the lower workings had turned the color of tea, when any man with sense would have walked out—and been paid, quietly, posthumously, to have never raised an alarm.

And beneath the eleventh name, in the same hand but pressed harder, the pen nearly through the page, a twelfth line that was not a name at all. Just a word: *handled.*

I knew that handwriting.

I had watched it write my letters. My lessons. My name—my real name, the one I had buried under twelve years of miles and river silt. Aurel Dain had taught me my letters on the back porch of a house that smelled of pipe smoke and cut pine, guiding my hand with his own, patient as tide. He had fed me when my mother's people wouldn't. He had stood in a courtroom and taken the whole weight of a collapsing world onto his shoulders so that a boy of fourteen could walk away clean, could run, could live.

They said he falsified the safety reports. They said he signed off on the timbering, waved the men down into the dark, and pocketed the difference. He never denied it. Not once, not through the trial, not through the sentencing, not through the years after, when his letters reached me thin and infrequent and always ending the same way: *Don't come back. Don't ask. Live.*

I had built a life on the foundation of his guilt. No names, no pasts, no passengers—that rule wasn't caution. It was penance. I had measured my life in miles because miles don't ask questions, and every mile I put between myself and that courtroom was a mile I owed a man who had ruined himself to buy my escape.

And here, in a dead prospector's strongbox, in a bookkeeper's careful hand, was the arithmetic that unmade everything.

Aurel Dain hadn't falsified the reports. He had written the true ones. He had documented the bad timber, the rising water, the warnings ignored by men whose names filled these margins—men with capital enough to make *handled* mean what it meant. And when the mine came down and eleven families needed someone to blame, those same men had handed the investigator a boy's guardian on a plate, and Aurel Dain had looked at me across that courtroom, seen what testifying would cost me, and swallowed the rope whole.

*Handled.* The word sat in the lamplight like a bullet on a table.

I don't know how long I sat there. Long enough for the fire to die to coals. Long enough for the river to change its voice twice. I remember the weight of the ledger, heavier than any cargo I've ever carried, and I remember thinking—with a clarity that felt almost peaceful—that rivers drown evidence all the time. The Serein takes boats, takes men, takes whole seasons, and gives nothing back. One strongbox, opened, emptied into the current, and the ledgers would be pulp by morning. The photographs would drift apart like leaves. The truth would stay exactly where powerful men had buried it, and Aurel Dain would stay guilty in every record that mattered, and I would go on being no one, which was at least a shape of peace I knew how to wear.

The alternative was unthinkable. If these pages were true, then the man who saved me helped kill eleven miners. That sentence had been sitting in my chest since the manifest, since Elias's delirium, since chapter upon chapter of a dying man's half-confessions—and now the ledger offered me a third thing I hadn't prepared for: if these pages were true, then the man who saved me had died a liar so that liars could live free, and the only proof left on earth was sitting on my knees, and I was the only soul alive who both wanted it destroyed and couldn't bear to touch it.

Both things were true at once. That's the part no one tells you about loyalty. It doesn't break cleanly, like a rope. It tears, like wet canvas, and every fiber screams.

I heard Ines coming and I made my choice—or I thought I had. I closed the ledger. I stood. I carried the strongbox to the waterline, where the current ran fast and black over the gravel, and I held it at my hip like a man holding a door shut against a wind.

That is how she found me.

* * *

"Captain."

She said it carefully. She'd earned the right to the title somewhere back upriver, though she'd never once used it lightly.

"Ines." I didn't turn around. "Your father's fever's worse. Bram's brewing willow bark; make him drink all of it."

"I will." A pause. Stones shifting under her boots as she came closer, close enough that I could see her reflection ghosted in the black water—thin, upright, her father's stubborn jaw. "What are you doing?"

"Checking the bank," I said. "Before we cast off."

"You're standing in it."

"The bank doesn't check itself."

Another pause, longer. She was too smart for this and we both knew it, and the knowing hung between us like the mist coming off the current. Then, quietly, in the voice she used when she'd decided to stop pretending:

"Put it down, Captain."

"It's heavy."

"I know what it weighs." Her voice cracked—not with weakness, with effort, the way ice cracks before it goes. "I've been carrying it since the gorge mouth. Please. Put it down."

I turned. The strongbox hung from my grip, and her eyes went to it, and I watched her understand in real time—watched her face do the terrible arithmetic of a daughter realizing her father's treasure is also her father's confession, and that the man holding it is deciding whether either of them survives the night.

"You read them," she said.

"Some."

"And?" Her chin came up. Whatever she was braced for, she meant to take it standing. "Was Papa lying? Is that it? You read what he really found and it's nothing—it's dust—it's—" Her voice caught on the hope in it, and the hope was worse than anger. Anger I could have borne.

"It's not nothing." The words came out of me like stones off a shelf, one at a time, each one chosen. "It's eleven men. It's a cover-up with signatures. It's the worst thing I've ever read, and I've read court transcripts."

Her breath went out of her. For a moment she just stood in the cold coming off the water, and I saw her hands open and close at her sides, and I saw her look past me—at the skiff where her father coughed in his sleep, at the boat, at the whole fragile congregation of strays we'd become—and I knew she was weighing the same ledger I was, just with different entries. Her father's name was in those margins too. Somewhere in those pages, Elias Ines had signed something, taken something, stayed silent about something. She'd figured that out days ago. She'd chosen truth anyway, back in the lantern light, back when choosing cost less.

"Then give them to me," she said. "We'll take them downriver. There's a journalist in Delta—"

"There's a journalist in Delta, and a Corvus Line boat behind us, and a monsoon ahead of us, and eleven families who got their settlement money and their grief and their silence, all neatly wrapped." I heard my own voice go flat and hard, the voice I used on green deckhands in bad water. "You think a newspaper can reach into the ground and give those men back their names? You think ink undoes *handled?*"

"No." Steady. God, she was steady. "But ink can undo the lie that's still standing. There's a man who took the fall for this, isn't there. Papa said—there's someone who went to prison for it, someone who—"

"Don't." It came out of me faster than I meant it to, sharp as a gaff hook, and I saw her flinch, and I hated myself for it, and I said it again anyway, quieter: "Don't."

Because that was the moment. That was the exact seam in the night where I could have told her everything—could have set the strongbox down in the gravel and said, *the man who took the fall raised me, taught me my letters, gave me his name to run from, and your father's ledgers say he was innocent, and if I let these pages exist then everyone will know he was innocent and it will be twelve years too late and it will also be the only thing that ever mattered.* I could have said it. The words were right there, loaded, waiting.

Instead I lifted the strongbox a little higher, and the current pulled at my shadow, and Ines Montrose—who was not named Montrose, none of us were named what we said we were—looked at the box, and then at my face, and did the bravest thing I have ever witnessed anyone do on this river or any other.

She stepped between me and the water.

Not dramatically. No outflung arms, no speech. She simply moved, unhurried, the way you'd step around a hazard on deck, and stood in the space between the strongbox and the Serein, and folded her hands, and waited.

"Move," I said.

"No."

"Ines."

"You taught me to read water." Her voice was low and even, and beneath it ran something that shook but did not bend. "You told me the river doesn't care what you're running from. You told me it finds every secret eventually—the sandbar shifts, the channel moves, whatever you buried comes up downstream. You said a man who fights the current drowns tired." Her eyes were bright and furious and wet. "So fight me instead. I'm easier to beat. But you're not putting that box in the river while I'm standing here, and I'll stand here until the monsoon comes, and you know I will, because you've watched me stand worse."

"They're just paper," I said, and my voice betrayed me, roughened at the edges. "Paper doesn't raise the dead."

"No." She took one step closer, close enough that I could see the firelight reflected small in her eyes, twin coals. "Paper doesn't raise the dead. But it can stop the living from being killed for it again. Don't you see? Whoever wrote *handled*—those men are still alive, still rich, still powerful, still doing whatever it was they did to eleven miners and one innocent man. Every year those ledgers stay sunk, they win again. Free. Forever." Her voice finally broke, all the way through, and she let it. "My father crawled back to that claim with a hole in his chest to dig these out. He's dying ten feet from us, and his last work on this earth is in your hands, and you want to give it to the river because the truth hurts you. Well, it hurts me too! Do you think I don't know what's in those margins? Do you think I haven't read my father's signature next to their silence?" She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, angry at the tears, furious at them. "I chose the truth over him. Over my own blood. And you—you're going to choose a ghost over eleven graves."

"A ghost," I repeated, and something in my chest tore along a seam I'd been keeping stitched for twelve years.

"Yes. Whoever he is. Whoever you're protecting." She lifted her chin. "You carry your past like ballast, Captain. I've watched you do it since the day I boarded. But ballast keeps a boat upright. What you're doing tonight isn't ballast. It's scuttling."

The river talked behind her. The coals ticked and settled. Somewhere up the gorge, thunder muttered—the first of it, far off, the sky clearing its throat—and neither of us looked up, because we were both watching each other across a gap narrower than the boat's beam and wider than the whole Serein.

And I understood, standing there with my guardian's vindication in my hands and his grave twelve years deep, that she was right, and that being right was the cruelest thing she could have done to me. Because if the ledgers lived, Aurel Dain's name would be cleared in print, in courts, in every record that mattered—and I would have to stand in the light of that clearing and admit I had run from it, hoarded it, nearly drowned it. The truth wouldn't just free the dead. It would expose me. Every mile I'd measured my life in would turn out to have been a circle, drawn tight around my own fear.

Loyalty, I thought. Loyalty's just love that hasn't been audited yet. And the ledger always comes due.

"Step aside," I said one last time, but there was no command left in it, and she heard that, and she didn't move, and I didn't lift the box again.

For a long moment neither of us breathed. Then my arms lowered—all on their own, it seemed, some muscle wiser than its owner—and I set the strongbox down in the gravel between us, gently, the way you lay down a weapon you're not sure you've stopped needing.

Ines let out a breath that seemed to empty her whole body. She crouched, laid both palms flat on the oilcloth, and looked up at me with her father's jaw and her mother's eyes and an expression I couldn't name and have never forgotten.

"Thank you," she whispered.

"Don't thank me." I turned away, toward the fire, toward the skiff, toward the sound of a dying man breathing. "I'm not sure yet which way I'd have gone. Ask me tomorrow."

"Captain." She rose, still holding the box against her chest like a child, or a shield. "Whoever he is—the man you're protecting—I'd bet everything I have left that he'd have done exactly what you just did. Held it over the water. And then set it down." A pause. "People who are guilty don't hesitate like that."

I didn't answer. I couldn't. The thunder spoke again, nearer now, and this time we both looked up, and we both saw it at the same moment: downriver, around the black shoulder of the gorge, a light. Moving. Low on the water. Running without lamps except one—a single white point, the kind of light a boat shows when it wants to be seen by exactly one other boat.

The Corvus Line had found our fire.

Ines clutched the strongbox tighter. Behind us, Elias coughed, and murmured a name, and the river carried it away downstream toward the light.
