## 24. The Corvus Boards

Bram smelled the Corvus before he saw it—coal smoke and river mud and something chemical underneath, like a tannery, like money that had been handled too many times by too many hands. He was in the galley rendering fat for the evening meal when the wind shifted and carried it to him through the open porthole, and his hands went still over the skillet the way they had gone still once in a butcher's shop in Vellamare when two men walked in wearing coats too fine for the neighborhood and asked questions about a woman who had paid him in silver.

He had learned young that trouble announced itself first as a smell.

* * *

The bend at Kettle Reach was the kind of water Mirabel hated—a long right-hand sweep where the current piled against the outside bank and any boat coming down from the north would be hidden until the last hundred yards. Bram stood on the aft deck with a dish towel still slung over his shoulder and watched the far shore, where the willows grew thick as hair, and waited for what he already knew was there.

It came the way a debt comes. Not suddenly. All at once.

The Corvus Line boat slid around the willows with her engine throttled low, running quiet, which no honest boat ever did. She was bigger than the *Marrow*, their own vessel—twin-stacked, iron-bowed, with a wheelhouse glassed in green so you couldn't see who stood behind it. Her name was painted on the bow in letters meant to be read from a distance: *CORVUS QUEEN*. A crow in flight beneath it, wings spread wide enough to shadow half the hull.

Bram had been a cook on eleven boats in nineteen years, and he had seen boardings before. He knew the choreography. The big ship comes alongside slow, matching speed, and men appear along her rail the way ants appear along a dropped crust of bread—not one at a time but all together, as if they'd been waiting for a signal only they could hear.

"Mirabel," he called, and his voice came out steadier than he felt. "Company."

Mirabel was already climbing out of the wheelhouse. He moved the way he always moved, unhurried, a man who had decided long ago that panic was a luxury item and he'd never afford it. But Bram saw his hands check the throttle, saw his eyes go to the fuel gauge, to the depth sounder, to the sky—reading everything at once, the way he read the river, looking for the line of least disaster.

Odile came up from the engine room wiping grease on a rag. Pao appeared from somewhere forward, small and quick as a weasel. And Ines came out of the passenger cabin with her father's coat over her arm, because Elias had been sleeping since noon and she had taken to sitting with him the way you sit with a candle that keeps guttering.

Nobody said anything. There was nothing yet to say. The two boats closed the distance between them at the speed of a funeral procession.

* * *

They heard him before they saw him clearly—the Corvus captain, standing at his rail with a speaking trumpet he didn't need, because the river was quiet and voices carry over water the way grief carries over years.

"Captain Montrose." The name crossed the gap between the boats like a thrown stone.

Bram watched Mirabel take it. Watched the word land on him—*Montrose*, not the name anyone aboard used—and saw something flicker across the captain's face that Bram had never seen there in three years of shared meals and shared watches: not fear exactly. Recognition. The look of a man hearing a song he'd hoped was forgotten.

"That's not my name," Mirabel called back, level.

"It is today." The man lowered the trumpet. He was tall, gray at the temples, dressed better than any riverman Bram had ever seen—a dark coat buttoned to the throat despite the heat, boots that had never known mud. "Renn Voss. I'd like to come aboard. I think you'll want to hear what I have to say."

"I know what you have to say. You've been saying it since Port of Small Mercies. You want the map."

"The map." Voss smiled, and it was a terrible thing to see, because it was patient. "Captain, I've wanted a great many things in my life. Most of them I got. The map is the least of them now."

The boats were fifty yards apart, then forty, then thirty, the Corvus Queen matching their speed with an ease that told Bram everything about her engine and her pilot. Men lined her rail—six of them, seven—big through the shoulders, hands empty, which was somehow worse than if they'd held guns. Guns were a language everyone understood. Empty hands left room for interpretation.

"Keep your distance," Mirabel said.

"Or you'll do what?" Voss spread his arms. "You're carrying a dying man up a gorge that's about to close like a fist. You lost a day to a cut fuel line—I know about that, by the way, and I know it wasn't my doing, which should tell you something about your crew. Your coal's low. Your food—" he paused, and his gaze found Bram across the water, and Bram felt it like a finger pressed to his sternum, "—I'm told is adequate at best. Captain, you have nothing to bargain with except the truth, and the truth is the one thing I'm here to help you carry."

"Liar," Pao said quietly, at Bram's elbow. It wasn't an accusation. It was arithmetic.

* * *

The fenders went out. Bram didn't remember who ordered it—maybe nobody; maybe Odile simply did it, because Odile did things while other men were deciding whether to do things. The hulls kissed, wood groaning against wood, and the Corvus Queen's iron bow scraped their gunwale with a sound like a knife being drawn slowly from a sheath.

Voss came across alone.

That was the detail Bram would turn over later, lying awake in his bunk while the river talked to itself in the dark. Seven men on the rail, and Voss stepped onto the *Marrow*'s deck by himself, hands visible, palms out, the universal grammar of a man who wants you to know he could be searched. He was taller close up than he'd seemed from the water, and older, and tired in a way that sat deep in him, under the good coat, the way rot sits under paint.

"Thank you," he said.

"Don't thank me yet," Mirabel said. "Say what you came to say."

Elias Ines chose that moment to die—or to try to.

It started as a cough from inside the cabin, the wet, tearing cough that had been getting worse by the day, the cough that sounded like a man trying to bring up pieces of himself. Then it stopped. And the silence after it was worse than the coughing, the way a clock stopping is worse than its ticking.

Ines was through the cabin door before anyone else moved. Bram followed, because following was what he did—he'd spent nineteen years learning that the useful man in a crisis is rarely the one with the plan; it's the one with the towel, the water, the steady hands. He found Elias half off the bunk, gray as river clay, his lips going the color of the inside of a shell. Ines had him under the arms, holding him up, and her face was the face of someone watching a house burn from the inside.

"Bram," she said, and didn't finish the sentence, because she didn't have to.

He worked. Water, propped upright, the bitter tonic Odile kept for her own stomach, loosened collar, the window opened to let the river air in. Small mercies, small motions. And through it all he was aware of Voss standing in the cabin doorway—having followed, having invited himself, having done none of the things a claim-jumper does—and aware of the strangeness of it, the wrongness of it, this hunter watching a dying man with an expression Bram could not, for the life of him, name.

It wasn't greed. Bram knew greed; he'd cooked for greedy men for two decades and could smell it through a bulkhead. This was something else. Something that looked, horribly, like grief.

"Elias Ines." Voss said the name softly, testing it.

Elias's eyes opened. They were yellow now, the yellow of old paper, of things left too long in the sun. They found Voss and fixed on him with the last of the man's ferocity.

"You," Elias rasped. "The investigator. Twelve years ago. You sat in the third row."

"I did."

"You wrote it all down. Everything we said. And then you buried it." Each word cost him; Bram could see the price being paid in the twitch of the man's throat. "Eleven men. Eleven. And you buried it."

Something moved through Voss's face then, fast, like a fish under ice. "I didn't bury it," he said. "I was made to stop digging. There's a difference, though I understand why you can't see it from where you lie." He crouched, slowly, bringing himself level with the bunk, and Bram's hand drifted toward the boning knife on the galley shelf behind him without quite touching it. "I've spent twelve years being made to stop digging, Mr. Ines. I'm finished being made to do anything."

"Then why," Elias whispered, "the boats? The questions in port? The men on your rail?"

"Because I needed to find you before someone else did." Voss straightened. "The men who stopped my investigation are still alive, Mr. Ines. Still rich. Still powerful. Two of them sit on the commission that licenses every boat on this river—including yours, Captain, including mine. If they learn what you're carrying, they won't send a boarding party. They'll send fire. I came aboard today because I needed to know how much time you have, and how much proof, and whether either of you can survive what comes after."

"And now?" Ines's voice was raw. She hadn't let go of her father's hand. "Now that you know?"

"Now," Voss said, and looked at each of them in turn—Elias on his bunk, Ines white-knuckled beside him, Bram with his dish towel gone stiff in his fist, and past them, in the doorway, Mirabel with Odile and Pao flanking him like a wall that had grown there, "now I'm going to do the one thing I've never done in my career. I'm going to leave evidence alone."

* * *

He stepped back out onto the deck and they all followed, because it felt dangerous to let him stand anywhere unwatched, even in retreat.

"Here are my terms," Voss said, "and they're simple, which should make you trust them less, so let me add this: I don't expect you to trust them. Trust is for people with time. You don't have time." He gestured upstream, where the sky had begun to stack itself with cloud, high and hard-edged, the color of a bruise coming up. "The monsoon breaks early. My weather man says four days, maybe five. After that, the gorge floods and nothing comes out alive that isn't swimming. So: go. Get to the claim. Get the records. And when you come back down—" he paused, and for just a moment the composure cracked and Bram saw the tired man underneath, the man who had sat in the third row twelve years ago writing everything down, "—when you come back down, I'll be at the delta. With my testimony. Signed. Sworn. Whatever you need. I want it published, Captain. Not buried. That's all I've ever wanted. I just took the long way around to wanting it honestly."

"And if we say no?" Mirabel asked. His voice was flat, unreadable, the voice he used for rapids. "If we throw you off this boat?"

"Then I'll follow you at a respectful distance and hope you change your mind." Voss smiled again, and this time it was almost human. "I'm a patient man, Captain Montrose. Ask anyone whose case I worked."

"My name isn't—"

"It is," Voss said gently. "And when this is over, whoever you decide to be, that'll be your business. But a man who's willing to run the Devil's Throat for a stranger's truth deserves to hear it called by its right name at least once." He turned to go, then stopped, one boot on his own gunwale, and looked back at Elias's open cabin door, at the yellow-eyed man lying within earshot. "Mr. Ines. The third row. I kept the notes. All of them. They're in a safe in Corvus City under a dead man's name. When you're ready, they're yours."

Then he was across the gap and the fenders were in and the Corvus Queen fell away astern, green-glassed and iron-bowed, shrinking into the willow-shadow of the bend until she was only smoke, only a smudge, only a smell.

* * *

Nobody spoke for a long time.

The river ran. The bruised clouds climbed. Somewhere below decks the bilge pump cycled on and off with its small mechanical patience, keeping the boat alive the way it always did, indifferent to what happened above it.

"He's lying," Pao said finally. "Has to be."

"Probably," Odile said.

"Could be," Bram said, and they all looked at him, because he almost never weighed in on anything heavier than salt. "Nineteen years on the water, I've met maybe four men who meant what they said and said what they meant. Three of them drowned. But I've also never met a liar who gave back something he could've kept." He nodded toward the cabin, toward the dying man in it. "He had us dead to rights. Bigger boat, more men, our fuel low. He could've taken everything and told himself a story about it later, same as they all do. Instead he handed us his testimony like a calling card." Bram shrugged, feeling the absurdity of the dish towel still on his shoulder, and took it off, folded it, put it in his pocket. "Liars don't leave collateral. That's the whole art of lying—you keep something back."

Mirabel hadn't spoken. He stood at the rail with his back to them, watching the empty bend where the Corvus had gone, and Bram studied that broad, closed back and thought about names—about the name Voss had used, and the name Mirabel used, and the space between them where a man could live for twelve years if the living was quiet enough.

"Four days," Mirabel said at last, to the water. "Maybe five."

"We'll make the claim in three if the channel holds," Odile said. "Two and a half if we push."

"Then we push."

"And if Voss follows us in?" Pao asked. "If it's a trap? If he waits at the delta and takes it all at the end?"

"Then it's a trap," Mirabel said, turning, and Bram saw that whatever had flickered across his face at the sound of his old name had settled into something harder and cleaner, like ore beaten into a blade. "But we were trapped the day we signed on to this, and we rowed into it anyway. Nothing's changed except now we know the shape of the walls." His eyes went down the line of them—Pao, Odile, Bram, Ines standing in the cabin doorway with her father's hand still warm in hers. "Get the stove lit. We eat, we sleep in shifts, and at first light we make up the day the saboteur stole from us."

He went forward to the wheel. The others scattered to their work, because work was the religion of the *Marrow* and no crisis had ever suspended services.

Bram lingered a moment at the galley door. Through the cabin's open port he could see Ines bent over her father, and Elias's lips moving—too faint now for words, just movement, like a man rehearsing something he meant to say before the end and was afraid he wouldn't get the chance. And beyond them, through the far window, the clouds stacking higher over the headwaters, bruise-dark, patient, closer than any forecast had promised.

He lit the stove. He put water on to boil. And he found, to his own surprise, that his hands had stopped shaking—that somewhere between the boarding and the boiling, some old instinct in him had made its ruling, the way it had ruled on storms and strangers and bad harbors for nineteen years.

*Trust him,* the instinct said. *Or at least trust that he's more afraid of the burial than the truth.*

Bram chopped onions. Outside, the first far-off thunder rolled down the gorge like a drum being carried toward them, and the river, rising already, answered.
