## 25. Fever Dreams

The fever came up the river with him, he thought afterward—or perhaps the river had always been inside him, waiting all these years in his blood like a stowaway, and the heat of the gorge had only called it out. Elias Ines lay in the narrow berth beneath the wheelhouse and dreamed of water that was not water: a black lake of it standing perfectly still in the dark of the mountain, so quiet you could hear your own heart knocking against your ribs, asking to be let out.

He was cold. That was the joke of it. The cabin sweltered, the river outside steamed under a white sky, and he shivered as though he were back down in Number Three drift with the seep coming through the timber, eleven men breathing around him in the dark, their lamps making small yellow coins on the wet rock.

"Ines." A voice. His daughter's voice, though for a long time he could not make the word *daughter* attach itself to it. "Ines, drink this."

Bram's willow-bark tea, bitter as regret. He drank because her hand was steady and because refusing her had been the whole architecture of his life and he was tired of buildings. He drank, and the fever took him again, and in the dream he was young, and the mine was singing.

* * *

You have to understand what a mine sounds like when it is happy. People think of silence, of stone. But a contented mine hums. The timbers creak in a low conversational register, the air moves through the adit like breath through sleeping lungs, and the drills—ah, the drills, three hundred feet down, tapping away at the seam like woodpeckers with a shared religion. For six years Elias Ines had known that sound the way other men knew their mothers' voices. He had gone down into the Serein Consolidated every morning before dawn with a lunch pail and a carbide lamp and a heart that was, if not light, at least load-bearing.

And then one Tuesday in the autumn of his thirty-fourth year, the mine stopped humming.

It did not roar. That was the thing no newspaper ever got right. There was no roar. There was a *pause*—a silence so total and so wrong that every man in Number Three drift stopped swinging his pick at the same instant, the way a room of musicians stops when the conductor's hand comes up. Forty seconds, maybe fifty. Long enough for old Tomás Ferro to say, quietly, in the dark, "Everybody stand away from the rib."

Then the mountain exhaled.

Water first—the seep that management had been promising for two years to fix, the seep that appeared in the ledgers as *routine dampness*—came through the lower heading not as a trickle but as a decision. Cold, black, patient. It found their boots, their knees, their waists. It put out Tomás Ferro's lamp. And above them, far above, they heard the timbers begin to talk in a new language, the language of things being asked to hold more than they were built to hold.

Eleven men went down that day. Elias Ines was the twelfth man in Number Three drift, and he lived because he had gone back for his coat—his coat, God help him, his good wool coat with the torn pocket—and the delay put him behind the others, and the collapse came down between him and them like a door closing in the face of God.

He dug for three days. He and the rescue crews, bare-handed at first until the blood made his fingers useless. He dug knowing, by the second day, that digging was a ceremony and not a hope. And on the third day, sitting in the mud with his ruined hands hanging between his knees, he heard a foreman say to another foreman, not knowing or not caring who stood within earshot:

"Company line is seepage was unforeseeable. Inspection reports are clean. Keep your mouth shut and there's a bonus in it."

*Clean.* The reports were clean because the reports had been bought. The seep had been reported—Elias himself had reported it, twice, in writing, and both times the paper had come back initialed with the notation *addressed*. Addressed. Eleven men, addressed. He had spent three days digging toward men the company had already decided to bury twice: once in rock, once in ink.

That was the disaster. But the disaster, he learned over the following year, was only the wound. The cover-up was the disease, and the disease had metastasized into everything—into the coroner's inquest, where witnesses forgot their own names; into the newspapers, which ran the company's statement verbatim under headlines about *tragic but unavoidable natural forces*; into the widows' settlement, which arrived with a signature line releasing the Serein Consolidated from all further liability, and which the widows signed because their children were hungry and hunger does not wait for justice.

Elias did not sign. That refusal cost him his job, then his house, then—more slowly, more painfully—his family. He became a man with a grievance instead of a man with a trade. He wrote letters. He collected affidavits in taverns from men too frightened to sign their names sober. He learned which inspectors could be talked to and which had already been talked to by someone with deeper pockets. And in the end, when the letters went unanswered and the affidavits gathered mildew in a trunk, he understood that the truth was a heavy mineral and it would have to be mined like anything else: claim staked, shaft sunk, years given to it in the dark.

So he went back upriver. To the old claim. To the survey records and the internal correspondence he had hidden there twelve years ago, wrapped in oilcloth, sealed in a strongbox behind the collapsed adit, because a man whose house has been burned learns to keep his important papers in the wilderness.

The fever gave it all back to him now, in fragments, out of order, the way rivers return what you throw into them—driftwood here, a drowned hat there, everything arriving eventually but never in the shape you surrendered it.

* * *

When he surfaced, she was there.

His daughter sat on the crate beside his berth with her sleeves rolled and a basin of river water at her feet, wringing a cloth the way her mother used to wring laundry—two quick twists, snap, fold. She had her mother's wrists. She had his jaw. She had twelve years of his absence written across her face in a hand he could not read, and the fever loosened something in him that twelve years of sobriety had kept nailed shut.

"You're burning," she said. "Papa. You're burning up."

"I know," he said. His voice came out like gravel poured slowly. "I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Drink."

He drank. The tea was terrible. Bram cooked like an angel and brewed like a punishment, and Elias loved him for it, the way you love anything faithful.

She pressed the cool cloth to his forehead and he caught her wrist—not hard, just enough to stop her, just enough to keep her there, kneeling in the lamplight with the boat's engine muttering beneath them like a large animal dreaming.

"Odile says two days to the claim," she said. "Maybe three, if the river keeps rising. You have to stay with us. You hear me? You have to be difficult about it. Stubborn. It shouldn't be hard—you've had practice."

He laughed, and the laugh turned into coughing, and the coughing bent him nearly double, and when it released him he was weaker and lighter both, the way a candle is lighter after it has burned.

"Mira," he said.

"What?"

"The captain. Mirabel." He watched her face do something complicated. "You two have been circling each other since the Widow's Teeth like a pair of cats deciding whether the other one is friend or weather. I want to tell you something about him while I still have the wits to say it plain."

"He hasn't told you anything," Ines said. "Has he. About himself."

"No. And I haven't told him everything either. We're a boatload of locked drawers, this crew." He closed his eyes. The fever pressed down on him like a warm hand. "But I read a name. On the manifest, when my head was swimming. I wasn't snooping. God forgive me, I don't even remember reaching for it. And the name was one I knew from another life."

"Papa—"

"Hartwell," Elias said. "Not Mirabel Hartwell. The other one. Aldous Hartwell."

The cloth stopped moving in her hands. He heard rather than saw it—the small cessation of sound, the held breath.

"The union lawyer," she said slowly. "The one from the trial. The one they—" She stopped. Recalibrated. "The one who went to prison."

"The one who went to prison," Elias agreed, "for falsifying inspection records. Which was a lie. Which was the whole point of the trial—to put a honest man's face on the crime so the dishonest faces could go home to dinner." The fever made him eloquent; he could feel the words coming up hot and unbidden, like water finding a crack. "Aldous Hartwell raised that boy. Took him in off the river when he was nine years old, no parents, no papers, nothing but a talent for reading water. Gave him his name. And when the company needed someone to hang the disaster on, they hung it on Hartwell, because Hartwell had spent two years pulling inspection reports apart in public and he was the only man in the valley they couldn't buy."

"And the boy—" Ines began.

"Grew up watching his father be destroyed by a lie," Elias said, "and ran from the name the way you'd run from a fire. Changed it. Cut it off at the root. Twelve years measuring his life in miles because miles don't ask you who you are." He opened his eyes. Her face swam above him, lit gold by the lamp, blurred by his own salt water—he was crying, he realized, without any drama at all, the way old pipes weep. "I need you to understand something, and I need you to hold it, because I may not be lucid again for a while. Maybe not ever."

"I'm holding it."

"The records at the claim. The ones I dragged you up this river to find. They don't just prove the cover-up. They prove *who tried to stop it*." His grip tightened on her wrist. "Aldous Hartwell filed a minority report. A full dissent, with his own surveys attached, warning the company the lower headings were unsafe. Six weeks before the collapse. The company buried his report and then, when the mountain did exactly what he said it would do, they used his access to the inspection files to frame him for doctoring them. The report itself—it's in the strongbox. I've carried the knowledge of it for twelve years like a stone in my boot. His own handwriting, dated, witnessed. The proof that the man they destroyed was the only innocent man in the building."

Ines had gone very still. Stillness, Elias thought fuzzily, was a Montrose inheritance too, apparently, along with the jaw.

"Why didn't you ever say anything?" she asked. Her voice was careful, level, the voice of a woman walking across ice. "Twelve years, Papa. You let everyone believe—"

"Believe what? That I was a crank? A drunk? A man who couldn't get over it?" He laughed, wet and short. "Everyone believed that anyway. And saying Hartwell's name out loud felt like—like spending money I hadn't earned. I had the location of the proof. Having the location isn't having the proof. I told myself I'd speak when I had the paper in my hand. And then the years went by, and going back meant admitting I'd run, and running is a habit that deepens like a channel." He swallowed. The fever rolled through him, and he rode it. "Your mother left me over that trunk, Ines. Not over the drinking. Over the trunk. Over the fact that I loved a dead argument more than a living family. She was right. I want that on the record, wherever records go when we die. She was right, and I was a coward with a map."

"Papa." Her voice cracked on the word, and something in him broke open along the same fault line.

"I'm sorry," he said. "For all of it. For the empty chair at every table. For teaching you that a man's word matters more than a man's presence—I got that backwards and you paid the interest. For bringing you up this river on a dying man's errand because I couldn't bear to die with the stone still in my boot." He drew a breath that hurt all the way down. "But I'm not sorry about the records. Hear me. Whatever happens. Whatever you decide when you've read them. I'm not sorry about those. Eleven men went into that mountain and came out as a paragraph, and the paragraph was a lie, and lies are the only thing I've ever been willing to bleed for."

* * *

The fever took him again after that, but it was a different fever—gentler, almost companionable, a fever that walked beside him instead of riding him. He dreamed of the mine humming. He dreamed of his wife's wrists. He dreamed of a boy on a riverboat, nine years old, learning to read the sandbar's mood, and he dreamed that in the dream he, Elias, was the one teaching him, though in life they had never met until this voyage, and the dream knew it was lying and told it anyway, because dreams are merciful that way.

He surfaced once, near midnight, to voices above him—Mirabel's low and Ines's lower, rising and falling through the floorboards like weather. He could not make out the words. He caught only his daughter's tone, and it was not angry. It was the tone of a woman rearranging the furniture of her soul to make room for something large that had just come through the door.

He surfaced once more, near dawn, and she was asleep in the chair beside his berth, her chin dropped to her chest, the basin water gray, the cloth dry in her lap. On the crate beside her lay the photograph she had carried for twelve years—the one of the two of them on the dock at Catter's Landing, him squinting, her missing a tooth, the river shining behind them like a promise neither of them had managed to keep.

He looked at her a long time. Then he reached out with a hand that shook and turned the photograph face-down, gently, the way you close a book you have finally finished.

When she woke, he knew, she would have questions. About the strongbox. About the final page he had never mentioned to anyone—the last thing in the box, the thing he had wrapped separately, the thing that was not evidence at all but a letter, written in a prison hand, addressed to a boy who had never answered it.

And he knew, with the terrible clarity the fever granted him in its brief merciful intervals, that whatever she decided when she read those ledgers—whether she defended her father's name or burned it clean in public—she was going to have to decide it soon, and out loud, and in front of the son of the man his testimony could raise from the dead.

The river rose outside, whispering against the hull. Somewhere upstream, the claim waited with its mouth full of paper.

"Ines," he whispered, to no one awake. "Ines, it's time to choose what we're carrying."
