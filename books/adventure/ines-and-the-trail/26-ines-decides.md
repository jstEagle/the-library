## 26. Ines Decides

The ledgers sat between us on the crate like a third person at the table.

I had not touched them since the night before. Neither had Mirabel, though I could feel him wanting to—the way a man wants to touch a wound he knows is his own. The lantern swung gently with the boat's motion, throwing our shadows up the bulkhead in slow arcs, and outside the rain had settled into that steady monsoon drumming that no longer felt like weather but like a condition of the world.

My father slept in the aft cabin, or something near sleep—his breathing a wet, laboring thing that stopped and started like an engine flooding out. Odile had given him the last of the fever powder an hour ago and said nothing about what that meant, which was its own kind of sentence.

"He hid the last page," I said.

Mirabel didn't answer right away. He was sitting across from me with his forearms on his knees, hands hanging loose between them, and I had learned by now that his silences were not empty. They were full rooms. You had to walk into them and look around.

"You read it," he finally said. Not a question.

"I read it." My voice came out steadier than I expected. "Your guardian. Aldous Wren. He wasn't one of them."

"No."

"He was the one who tried to stop it."

Mirabel's jaw worked once, twice. "That's what the page says."

"That's what the page *proves*," I said. "There's a difference, and you know it better than anyone on this river. Signatures. Dates. A letter in his own hand, copied before it was sent, saying exactly what happened in Eleven Mile and who ordered the tunnel sealed with men inside it. Your father didn't just take the fall for the disaster, Mirabel. He took the fall for the *murder*. And the men who actually did it—" I tapped the ledger, and the sound was very small in the lantern light, "—signed their names right here. In their own ink. Thinking no one would ever come this far upriver to read it."

He looked at the ledger the way you look at water you're about to swim. "Twelve years," he said. "Twelve years I built my life on top of a grave I thought was his shame. And now you're telling me the grave was empty all along. That he died clean. That he died—" His voice caught, and he pressed his lips together until it passed. "Do you understand what that does to a man? Every mile I ran, every name I buried, every rule I made about no pasts and no names—I did it carrying *him*. Like ballast. And now someone tells me the ballast was never lead. It was gold. And I threw away twelve years for nothing."

"Not for nothing," I said.

"Easy for you to say."

"Yes," I agreed. "It is easy for me to say. Because I'm about to do the same arithmetic in reverse."

* * *

I went aft to check on Papa while the rain thickened.

He was awake. I knew it before I saw his eyes open, because the quality of his breathing changed when he was conscious—that performative steadiness sick men adopt when they don't want their daughters frightened. Twelve years, and I still knew the difference between his sleeping breath and his brave breath.

"Ines." My name in his mouth sounded like an apology he'd been rehearsing since the gorge began.

I sat on the edge of his bunk. The mattress dipped, and his hand found mine without either of us deciding it should. His fingers were paper over bone. There was a time those hands had swung me up onto his shoulders at the county fair, and there was a longer, darker time when those same hands had signed papers I only half understood, and I had spent the whole voyage trying to decide which memory was the true one.

"The last page," he said. "You found it."

"Bram found it. Tucked behind the ore assay sheets, wrapped in oilcloth like it mattered more than everything else combined."

"It did matter more." His eyes were bright with fever, fixed on some middle distance where the past lived. "Aldous Wren sent me that copy two weeks before they killed him. I was supposed to publish. I was supposed to be the second witness, so that killing one of us wouldn't end it." A cough took him, long and tearing, and I held a cup to his lips and hated how easily my hands obeyed. "I got scared, Ines. I took my daughter and I ran, and I told myself I'd come back when it was safe, and then your mother died, and then it had been three years, and then ten, and then it wasn't fear anymore, it was just—who I was. A man who buried a dead man's truth under his own cowardice and called it a mining claim."

"Why tell me now?"

"Because you've been looking at me like you already know." He smiled, and it was terrible and beautiful, my father's smile worn down to the wire. "And because if I die in this gorge, I want the last thing I did to be honest. Even if honesty costs me the only thing I have left."

"Which is?"

"Your good opinion." He closed his eyes. "Take the page to the journalist. All of it. The ledgers, the names, the photographs. Don't protect me. God knows I haven't earned protecting."

* * *

But here is what I learned about dying men: they can grant permission and still hope you'll refuse it.

I stood at the rail afterward, in the rain, letting it soak through my shirt because I needed my body to feel something other than what my chest was doing. The gorge walls rose black on either side, streaming, and somewhere ahead the river made a sound like grinding teeth.

Pao found me there. He didn't say anything at first, just leaned beside me and offered me the dry half of his coat, which I took, because refusing kindness had gotten me nowhere on this boat.

"My father helped kill eleven men," I said. "Not with his hands. With his silence. And the man who tried to speak got murdered for it, and his boy grew up thinking his father was a monster, and ran from his own name for twelve years because of what my father's cowardice wrote on him."

Pao considered this with the unhurried attention he gave everything, even falling rivers. "And now?"

"Now I'm supposed to choose." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "Everyone keeps telling me to choose. Papa says publish. Mirabel wants to sink it. Voss wants to print it. The river doesn't care. And I'm standing here realizing I'm the only one whose whole life fits inside the decision."

"Whose side are you on?" Pao asked. Not cruelly. Genuinely asking, the way children ask, cutting straight through to the load-bearing wall.

And I opened my mouth to say *my father's*, because blood, because twelve years of resentment that was still, underneath everything, love—and what came out instead surprised me so much I heard it as if someone else had spoken.

"His," I said. "Wren's. The dead man's. The eleven in the tunnel." I looked at my hands in the dark. "I've been loyal to a ghost of my father my whole life—the one from the photograph, the one who lifted me onto his shoulders. But that man died in Eleven Mile too, in every way that counts. What's left is a sick old stranger who lied to me beautifully for a week. And Mirabel—" I stopped.

"And Mirabel," Pao said, "is family."

"He's worse than family. He's proof." I turned to face him. "Everything my father broke, Mirabel carried. If I bury these ledgers to protect Papa's name, I bury Wren twice. I bury eleven miners twice. I let the men who signed their names walk free another twelve years, and maybe forever. And I make Mirabel's whole sacrifice mean nothing." The rain drummed on Pao's coat over my shoulders, steady as a heartbeat. "I can't do that. I won't. Papa asked me not to protect him, and for once in his life he's going to get what he asked for."

* * *

I found Mirabel in the wheelhouse, of course. Where else. He stood at the wheel with the boat asleep below him, steering against a current that didn't need steering, the way he did when his hands wanted work and his heart wanted distance.

"I'm taking the page to the journalist," I said. "All of it. Papa's name included."

The wheel creaked under his grip. "Ines—"

"Don't." I stepped closer. "Don't talk me out of it, and don't thank me either, because both would insult me. Just listen. I spent twelve years hating my father for leaving. Then I spent a week learning he left because he was afraid, and discovering that fear is somehow worse than malice—it's smaller and sadder and harder to forgive. And now I find out the thing he was afraid to defend was a man who *did* defend it, and paid for it, and whose son has been paying ever since." I reached out and put my hand flat on the wheel, next to his, so we were both holding the boat together. "You are not the reason I'm doing this. But you're the reason I can. Somebody has to be Wren's witness after all. It might as well be the daughter of the coward, making it right."

Something moved through Mirabel's face like current under ice. "You'd burn your own name to save mine."

"I'd spend my name to buy back yours. There's a difference." I almost smiled. "Besides, you taught me to read water. First lesson: the river doesn't care what you were. Only what you do next."

For a long moment neither of us spoke, and the wheel held steady under four hands instead of two, and I understood that whatever this was—loyalty, alliance, the rough beginning of trust—it was stronger than anything blood alone had ever made me feel.

Then the engine room bell rang twice, sharp and urgent, and Odile's voice came down the passage, tight and strange:

"Captain! Get up here—you need to see the water!"

Mirabel and I looked at each other, and I saw my own dread reflected there, because the river had been rising all day, and the storm was still a hundred miles upstream, and the gorge had begun, very quietly, to close its throat.

We ran for the bow together.
