## 27. The Storm Arrives Early

Pao smelled the rain before the sky admitted to it.

He was on the bow watch at dawn, third day out of the Night Portage, when the wind changed its mind. For six days it had blown upriver the way it always did in the dry season, warm and steady off the lowlands, carrying the smell of dust and diesel and the green rot of the delta. Now it stuttered, faltered, and swung around—coming down from the high country instead, cold and mineral, like the breath of a cave. It smelled of wet stone. Of snowmelt gone wrong. Of a storm still two hundred miles away but already thinking about them.

He had grown up on water that did this. His grandmother used to say the river told you everything twice: once in the wind, once in the water, and if you weren't listening the first time, the second time would be too late to matter.

Pao went aft to find Mirabel without being told to. That was the thing about this crew—they were all strays, but they had learned each other's habits so well that half their communication never needed words. He found the captain at the wheel, coffee untouched beside him, watching the eastern ridgeline where the clouds were stacking themselves into something with architecture.

"You feel it," Mirabel said. Not a question.

"Wind's turned." Pao climbed onto the rail and hooked his elbow through the stanchion, the way he liked to stand, half aboard and half not. "Smells like the headwaters."

Mirabel nodded slowly. "Odile says the barometer's been falling since midnight. Faster than it should."

"How fast is faster than it should?"

"Fast enough that I don't like the answer."

Below them, through the deck grating, they could hear Bram singing to the breakfast rice, some tuneless hymn from whatever life he'd left behind, and Odile's voice answering him from the engine room in a running argument about whether cilantro was a food or an insult. Ines sat on the cargo hatch with her father's head in her lap, reading aloud from a battered novel while Elias dozed, his breathing a thin wet whistle that everyone aboard had learned to hear past and no one had learned to ignore.

It looked, Pao thought, like peace. It sounded like peace. And the wind coming down off the mountains said otherwise, in a language older than any of them.

* * *

The river began lying to them by midmorning.

That was how Pao thought of it afterward—the river, which he trusted more than any person alive except maybe three of the four people currently on this boat, started telling lies. The gauge rocks they'd been using since the Widow's Teeth, the ones Mirabel had taught him to read like a ledger—their waterlines were wrong. High by inches. Then high by a handspan. The current, which should have slackened as they left the gorge narrows behind, kept its grip on the hull like something reluctant to let go.

"Glacier melt," Odile said, wiping her hands on a rag that had given up being gray years ago. She stood over her engine well with her head cocked, listening to the cooling water intake the way other people listened to music. "Warm front riding ahead of the storm. It happens. Snowpack lets go early."

"How much early?" Mirabel asked.

"A day. Maybe two."

"And the storm?"

Odile was quiet for a moment. The engine idled beneath them, that deep patient heartbeat Pao had fallen asleep to for weeks now, and above it the river made its new sound—a fuller sound, a hungrier one.

"The last report put it ten days out," she said. "But that was four days ago, and the man who reads those reports has been wrong twice before. If the warm front's pulling the monsoon in behind it—" She shrugged, and the shrug said more than her words did. "We might have three days. We might have less."

They had planned for margin. That was the whole arithmetic of the voyage: distance divided by speed, plus the days lost to sabotage and portage, minus the days until the gorge flooded, equaling a number small enough to survive. Two days of margin, Mirabel had called it, standing at the chart table with his finger on the map. Two days, and then the Serein Gorge becomes a pipe, and we are inside the pipe.

Now the arithmetic was eating itself.

Pao watched Mirabel do the calculation again, right there on his face, the same way he did everything—privately, completely, letting nothing escape until he chose. But Pao had spent weeks learning this man's face the way he'd learned the gauge rocks, and he saw the number come back worse than before. Saw the captain look upriver, toward the drowned adit and the strongbox and Elias's dying wish, and then look back downstream, toward sense, toward safety, toward every reason to turn around.

Mirabel didn't turn around.

"Full steam," he said quietly. "And nobody tells Elias yet."

* * *

By afternoon the sky had gone the color of a bruise coming up, and the first outriders of the storm reached them—not rain, not yet, just a wind with weight to it, shoving the surface of the river into long low swells that came from the wrong direction. Pao worked the bow line through a lock, hauling crates that didn't need moving just to keep his hands busy, and found Ines beside him doing the same.

She'd gotten good with rope. Somewhere between the Widow's Teeth and the Devil's Throat she had stopped being a passenger and become crew, though nobody had held a ceremony about it. Strays didn't, in Pao's experience. You just looked up one day and realized someone had stopped asking your name because they already knew it.

"My father keeps asking why we're pushing so hard," she said, coiling line without looking at it. Her hands knew the work now. "I told him Odile wanted to make a repair. He looked at me the way he looks at bad ore samples."

"What way is that?"

"Like he can tell exactly what's wrong and is deciding whether to say it."

Pao laughed despite himself. Then the wind gusted again, cold enough to raise the hair on his arms, and neither of them laughed after.

"He'll figure it out," Pao said. "The old man reads water better than he lets on. Where do you think you got it?"

Ines was quiet a moment. "I got it from books," she said finally. "Maps. Charts. Everything on paper, where it stays put." She looked upriver, at the bruised sky. "He got it from living out here. Twelve years I thought he abandoned us for gold. Now I find out he was—" She stopped. Shook her head. "I don't even know what to think now. Records. Dead men. A cover-up. Every day there's a new version of my father and I haven't met half of them."

"Could be worse," Pao offered. "You could be me. I don't know any version of mine."

She glanced at him—quick, sideways, the way people on this boat had learned to glance at each other's locked rooms. Offering the key without demanding it be turned.

"Someday," Pao said, surprising himself, "maybe I'll tell you. About my family. Why I signed on with a captain who doesn't take names."

"Why did you?"

"Because a boat full of people who don't ask questions is the only kind of family I could afford." He grinned, and she grinned back, and for a moment the wind forgot itself and the afternoon felt almost ordinary.

Then the first drop hit the deck. Fat as a coin, cold as a grave, landing with a smack loud enough to hear over the engine. Then another. Then a dozen, scattered like thrown gravel, and both of them stood looking at the sky with the same expression, because the storm wasn't due for days, and storms didn't send scouts ahead unless the main body was close behind.

"Pao," Ines said. "How far are we from the claim?"

"Day and a half. Maybe two, if the water keeps rising against us."

"And the gorge floods when?"

He didn't answer. He didn't have to. They both did the arithmetic together, silently, and both arrived at the same impossible remainder, and the rain began in earnest, drumming on the hatch covers like fingers counting down.

* * *

Evening came down gray and drowning. They tied up in a bend where the cutbank gave them shelter from the worst of the wind, and the whole crew crowded into the cabin while the rain worked at the world outside like something patient and enormous trying to get in.

Bram fed them anyway. That was his answer to everything—fear, grief, rising water—and nobody ever argued, because his answer was always good. Stew thick enough to stand a spoon in, bread baked in the stove oven that filled the cabin with a warmth the weather couldn't touch. They ate shoulder to shoulder, elbows knocking, and for a little while the conversation stayed deliberately small: Odile complaining about a bearing, Bram defending his use of salt, Pao describing—in elaborate, entirely fabricated detail—the fish he intended to catch when this was all over.

Elias ate sitting up, propped against the bulkhead, wrapped in every blanket the boat owned. He was thinner than when they'd started. Everyone saw it; no one said it. His cough had developed a rattle at the bottom of it, like stones turning in a shallow stream, and sometimes now he drifted mid-sentence and came back seconds later with no idea he'd gone.

But tonight his eyes were clear, and they moved around the cabin—from Odile to Bram to Pao to Ines to Mirabel—with the slow deliberate attention of a man taking inventory of something he intended to keep.

"You're all lying to me," he said pleasantly.

The cabin went quiet. Rain hammered the roof.

"Elias—" Ines began.

"The river's up. The wind's turned. My daughter has been steering conversations away from the schedule for two days, and our captain has been standing at the chart table at night doing sums he doesn't like." He took a slow sip of the tea Bram had laced with everything medicinal the galley carried. "I'm dying, not stupid. There's a difference, though I admit the symptoms overlap."

Nobody spoke. Outside, thunder rolled down the valley, distant but no longer distant enough, a sound like the mountain clearing its throat.

"How much time?" Elias asked Mirabel directly.

Mirabel set down his cup. Pao watched him decide—watched the whole private war of it cross his face and resolve into something steady. Whatever else Mirabel Montrose was, whatever name he'd buried and whatever past he was still dragging behind this boat like an anchor on a frayed line, he did not lie to dying men.

"If the storm holds its new course," Mirabel said, "the gorge starts flooding tomorrow night. Day after at the latest. Once it does, the lower narrows become unrunnable, and we're trapped inside until it passes. Could be a week. Could be longer."

"And the claim?"

"A day and a half upstream. If the river lets us make that speed."

"Then we have hours," Elias said, "not days." He said it without drama, the way a man reads a gauge. "Good. I was afraid you were going to waste time being gentle with me."

"Papa—"

"Ines." He reached over and took her hand, and Pao looked away, because some things weren't meant for an audience, except that in a cabin this size everything was, and maybe that was the point. Maybe that was why Elias insisted on saying it in front of all of them. "I have spent twelve years deciding who deserved the truth and when. It nearly cost me everything, including you. I'm done rationing it." He looked around the cabin again. "Whatever happens tomorrow, or the day after—you all came up this river for a stranger with a sealed crate and a bad cough. Whatever we find at that claim belongs to all of you now. That's not sentiment. That's wages."

Bram cleared his throat and busied himself with the stew pot. Odile studied her hands. Pao found himself staring at the rain-blurred window, at the black shape of the river beyond it, higher tonight than it had been that morning, gnawing patiently at the cutbank ten feet below.

Hours. The word sat in his chest like a swallowed stone.

* * *

He took the middle watch, alone in the wheelhouse while the others slept, or tried to. The rain had settled into a rhythm rather than a rage—for now—but the river hadn't. Pao stood at the window with the dim lantern turned low and watched the water climb.

He could measure it against the mooring line. When they'd tied up at dusk, the second wrap of line had been dry. By midnight the river had taken the second wrap and was working on the third. The current in the bend had reversed along the bank, an eddy line forming where no eddy belonged, and debris rode past in the dark—branches, a fence post, half of somebody's roof, tumbling south toward the delta with the mute urgency of things that had lost their argument with the water.

His grandmother's voice, in the old language, in his memory: *The river tells you everything twice.*

It was telling them now. Loudly. And somewhere upriver in the dark, past the drowned adit and the strongbox and eleven dead men waiting twelve years for their names, the storm was still arriving—more of it, all of it, the whole weight of the monsoon walking down out of the mountains toward a gorge that was about to become a throat.

A floorboard creaked behind him. Mirabel, wrapped in an oilskin, two mugs in hand. He passed one over without a word and stood at the window beside Pao, and for a long time they just watched the water rise together.

"Third wrap by morning," Pao said.

"I know."

"We can't make the claim and beat the flood. Not both. Not at this speed."

"I know that too."

"So what's the plan, Captain?"

Mirabel was quiet long enough that Pao thought he wouldn't answer. Then he lifted his mug slightly toward the black rushing dark upstream, toward the claim, toward the truth waiting in a strongbox under a drowned mountain.

"We stop measuring in days," he said. "From here on, we measure in hours. And we spend every single one of them going forward."

Lightning flickered far upriver, silent, a white vein pulsing inside the cloud, showing him for one instant the whole length of the valley they still had to climb—and the wall of rain hanging over its headwaters, coming down on them like a verdict.

Pao drank his coffee, cold and bitter, and began counting.
