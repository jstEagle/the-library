## 28. The Claim

The river told us we were close before the map did.

It had been changing all morning—narrowing, quickening, shedding its brown patience for a colder green, the water sliding over stone instead of silt. Odile stood at the engine hatch with her head cocked like a heron's, listening to the pitch of the intake, and Pao was up on the bow with the lead line, calling depths in a voice gone quiet and formal, the way he did when he felt the river listening back. Even Bram had stopped humming. The *Vespera* moved through that last stretch of gorge like a woman walking into a church she'd been avoiding for years.

Elias lay on the deck beneath the awning I'd rigged over the stern, propped on cushions Ines kept rearranging every twenty minutes as though the angle of his body were something she could still fix. He hadn't spoken since dawn. His breathing had developed a rattle at the bottom of each inhale, a sound like water finding a low place in a hull, and none of us said what we all knew about sounds like that.

Ines sat beside him with the map across her knees. The oiled silk had gone soft as skin from handling, and she traced the route with one finger, over and over, from the mouth of the Serein to a small inked cross above the third bend of the high channel—a mark so modest it looked like an afterthought, though it had cost eleven men their lives and twelve years of silence and, if the river had its way, nearly everything else besides.

"Half a mile," I called down from the wheelhouse. "Maybe less."

Ines looked up. She didn't smile. There was no triumph in her face, only a kind of bracing, the expression of someone who has carried a heavy thing a long way and is about to learn whether setting it down will hurt more than holding it.

"Captain," Elias said.

His voice came out cracked, barely there, but it carried the way a whisper carries in a silent room. I left Odile at the wheel and came aft, crouching by him. Up close he smelled of camphor and river damp and the sweetish undertone that sickness wears like a coat. His eyes were still clear, though. That was the terrible thing about him—the fever took everything else first and left the eyes standing like lampposts in a flood.

"You should rest," I said.

"When we're there." A ghost of the old stubbornness moved through his face. "You'll want to see it before you say anything to me. Everyone does. They want to tell me it isn't real, or it isn't worth it, or it isn't what I think." His hand found my wrist. His grip was still strong; whatever the disease was eating, it had left the hands for last, the way it leaves the heart. "It's real. And it's worse than you think. Go look."

* * *

The adit announced itself before we saw it.

Pao spotted it first—a seam of wrongness in the canyon wall where the green gave way to raw rock, timber framing showing through like bone through torn flesh. We nosed the *Vespera* into an eddy behind a shelf of granite, and I went ashore with Pao while Ines helped her father sit up against the rail where he could watch.

The trail up from the waterline was forty yards of slick cobbles and old rotting planking, and then the mine opened out of the cliff face like a wound.

The lower workings were drowned. Black water stood in the adit's mouth to within a foot of the lintel beam, utterly still, so that the entrance reflected itself perfectly—an eye of timber and darkness staring back at us from the rock. Someone had carved numbers into the frame: *7*, and below it, worn nearly smooth, a word I couldn't read until I climbed close enough to touch it. *SEVEN VEINS*. Below that, smaller, newer, cut with a knife rather than a chisel: *FOR THE FAMILIES*.

The water inside was deep enough to have swallowed the main drift entirely. Whatever machinery they'd run down there—hoists, carts, pumps—was somewhere beneath that black mirror, keeping its own counsel. But the upper gallery survived. A catwalk of rusted iron ran along the cliff face thirty feet above the waterline to a second opening, higher up, dry, half-hidden behind a curtain of hanging vine. The timbers around it were silvered with age but sound, and when I put my hand on the frame I felt how deliberately it had been built. Not a working man's shoring. An engineer's.

"Someone meant this to last," Pao said beside me. He'd come up quiet as he always did, and now he stood looking at the drowned adit with his head slightly bowed, the way he looked at graves.

"Someone meant it to be found," I said.

We crossed the catwalk single file, the iron complaining under our boots, vines brushing our shoulders like fingers. Inside, the upper gallery ran back into the mountain perhaps sixty feet and opened into a chamber where the ceiling rose and gray light fell through a ventilation shaft far overhead, dim as moonlight even at midday. Our lanterns found walls of raw granite veined with quartz, and rusted rails vanishing into a flooded side drift, and—

The strongbox.

It sat against the far wall where the floor was driest, a chest of riveted steel the size of a sea chest, painted the flat industrial gray of mining company equipment. It wasn't hidden. It wasn't buried. It was simply *there*, placed with care on a bed of stacked timber to keep it off the wet stone, positioned precisely where anyone climbing the catwalk would walk straight to it. A man who wanted something lost would have done differently. Elias Ines had wanted it kept—kept safe, kept whole, kept waiting—and there is a difference between those things that I understood better than I wanted to.

The lock was a simple tumbler affair, and the key hung from a nail driven into the wall beside it, exactly at shoulder height. Twelve years on that nail. The nail had rusted into a red bloom around the key, and Pao had to work it free with his knife while I held the lantern and tried not to notice that my hands weren't steady.

Inside: ledgers. Eleven of them, bound in oilcloth, their spines numbered in a clerk's careful hand. Beneath them, wrapped separately, a bundle of photographs in waxed paper, and beneath those, a sealed envelope thick as a brick, marked in the same clerk's hand with a single word: *NAMES*.

Pao let out a breath he'd been holding a long time. It echoed strangely in the chamber, and the mountain seemed to consider it and give nothing back.

"We should carry it down whole," he said. "Don't open it here."

But I was already thinking of Elias on the deck below, measuring his remaining hours in rattling breaths, and I knew—we both knew—that some things shouldn't wait for a gentler place. There are no gentler places. There is only here, and now, and who is beside you when the lid comes off.

* * *

We brought the strongbox down the catwalk between us, Pao ahead and me behind, Bram wading out to meet us at the waterline and taking the weight without being asked. Ines met us at the gangway. She looked at the box the way you look at a coffin you already know the contents of, and then she looked past it at her father, and whatever passed across her face she got control of before it finished arriving.

They laid the strongbox on the deck amidships, and the whole crew gathered without anyone calling them—Odile wiping her hands on her rag, Bram still dripping, Pao wringing river water from his sleeve. Nobody spoke. The *Vespera* rocked gently against her lines, and the gorge rose around us on both sides, green and indifferent, and the air had that pressed, held quality it gets before weather changes its mind.

Elias had them prop him upright facing the box. He'd used the last of his strength getting his back against the mast partner, and it cost him visibly; when he finished arranging himself he was gray-lipped and trembling, but his eyes had gone bright, almost feverish with purpose.

"Open it," he said.

I lifted the ledgers out one by one and set them on the deck boards. The oilcloth crackled. Under it the bindings were swollen with damp years but intact, the pages fanning just enough to show columns of figures, initials, dates going back further than I wanted to read. Eleven ledgers. Eleven men, I thought, and didn't know yet why the number snagged in me like a hook.

"The photographs," Elias said.

The waxed paper came away in sheets. The photographs were mine-site stock, sepia going toward gray, corners soft with age. Men at the adit mouth in their Sunday clothes, squinting into sun. A muck train posed on the rails. A bunkhouse row with laundry strung between posts. Ordinary pictures, the kind any operation keeps for its investors—and then the later ones, and the room changed.

A collapsed stope, timber splintered like matchwood. Rescue crews with lamps, faces black with dust, carrying stretchers. A line of eleven rough coffins on the tramway with men standing hatless beside them, heads bowed. And the last photograph, the one that made Bram turn away and made Ines make a small sound and cover her mouth: the same adit we'd just climbed to, but with the water already rising in it, and a company surveyor's tripod set up on the bank, and men in clean suits consulting papers while the mine drowned behind them.

"They flooded it three days after the collapse," Elias said. His voice had found some reserve of steadiness I wouldn't have credited him an hour ago. "Sealed the men in with the evidence. Called it safety procedure. Called it groundwater." He coughed, wet and long, and Ines held a cup to his lips until he could speak again. "Eleven men. The ledgers show the veins were gas-cut for a year before. Showed it in the assays. They knew. Every shift report for fourteen months is in those books, and every report was signed, and every signature knew what it was signing."

"Names?" Odile asked quietly.

"In the envelope." Elias closed his eyes. When he opened them again they'd gone glassy, and I saw him fighting his own body for the right to finish. "Read them. Captain. Read them aloud. I've waited twelve years to hear them said out loud in daylight, and I find—" his breath caught, held, released— "I find I don't trust myself anymore to know which ones are true. So you'll read. And my daughter will listen. And your crew will witness. That's all a record ever needed. Someone to read it, someone to hear it, someone to remember."

I broke the seal on the envelope.

Inside were pages in that same clerk's hand—not the company's clerk, I understood suddenly, but a second hand, a private accounting kept alongside the official one. Shift rosters. Assay results with the falsified figures written beside the true ones. Payments. And names, names, names—foremen, superintendents, a district manager, men whose signatures marched down the margins like a column of ants.

I began to read.

My voice sounded strange to me in the gorge, too loud and too flat, saying dates and tonnages and parts-per-million into the green silence. The crew sat motionless. Ines knelt by her father with her hand on his arm. The river slid past the hull, patient as ever, carrying everything toward the sea whether we were ready or not.

I read through the assay falsifications. Through the vent reports marked *REPAIRED* in a hand that the private ledger proved had never ordered repairs. Through the list of warnings filed and withdrawn, filed and withdrawn, eleven times, once for each man. And then I turned a page near the end, and the next name stopped my voice in my throat like a fist.

I knew the handwriting.

Not the clerk's. Another entry, inserted alone on its own page, dated two weeks before the collapse—a formal dissent, a refusal to sign, a whistleblower's report addressed to the district tribunal. Written in a slanted, careful script I had seen every day of my childhood, on chalkboards and grocery lists and the flyleaf of every book in the house where I grew up. A script I had spent twelve years not thinking about, and that my hand nonetheless still shaped, unconsciously, whenever I wrote fast.

I knew the name signed beneath it. I had carried it secretly, proudly, shamefully, for most of my life. It was the name I had abandoned along with everything else, the name I never said aloud, the name that was not Mirabel Montrose.

The gorge was very quiet. Somewhere above us, water dripped from the catwalk iron, counting seconds.

"Keep reading," Elias whispered.

I looked at him. He was watching me with those lamp-post eyes, and there was something in his face I couldn't read—pity, or apology, or the particular exhaustion of a man who has finally set down a thing he's carried too far.

"Captain," he said softly. "Keep reading. It's the last page. It's the one that matters most."

My thumb rested on the corner of the page. The paper trembled, very slightly, in the wind coming down the gorge ahead of the storm.

Behind me, Ines said my name—or the name she knew me by—and it sounded, for the first time, like a question.
