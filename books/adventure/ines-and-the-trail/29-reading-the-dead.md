## 29. Reading the Dead

The strongbox smelled of river and rust and something older, something like the inside of a church that had been closed for a long time. Ines knelt beside it on the flat shelf of stone above the drowned adit, her knees aching against the wet granite, and lifted the ledgers out one by one as though they were sleeping things she might wake.

There were nine of them. Nine ledger books bound in cracked leather, their spines furred with mold, their corners softened by decades of damp until they felt less like books than like the ribs of some drowned animal. Beneath them lay a bundle of photographs wrapped in oilcloth, and beneath those, a tin box of loose papers gone the color of weak tea.

Elias lay propped against the rock behind her, wrapped in every blanket Bram could find, his breath coming in long, laboring pulls, like a man hauling rope hand over hand up from somewhere very deep. He had asked Mirabel to read them aloud. Not her. Not yet. She understood why—his voice was gone, spent down to gravel—and because he wanted, perhaps, to watch her face while the truth came out of another man's mouth. To know what she would do with it before he had to see.

But Mirabel had looked at the first page, looked at Elias, and then looked at her, and said quietly, "This should come from family," and handed the ledger across the fire without another word.

So it was hers now. The reading of the dead.

* * *

The rain had eased to a fine drifting mist that beaded on everything—the tarps, the ledgers' covers, the hair at her temples. Odile had rigged a lantern under an awning of sailcloth near the boat's rail, close enough to the stove's warmth, far enough from Elias's hearing or so they pretended. Pao stood watch at the gorge mouth with the field glasses, counting the minutes against the rising brown line of the river. Bram kept the fire fed and said nothing, which for Bram was a kind of prayer.

Ines opened the first ledger on her knees.

The handwriting was not her father's. That was the first shock—that the account of everything was in a stranger's hand, small and precise, the letters upright and unornamented, the numbers ruled with a straightedge. A foreman's hand, maybe. An honest clerk's. The header read: *Kestrel Deep Mine — Payroll & Production — Year One.*

She began to read aloud, because Mirabel had asked her to, because the words seemed to need air and voices to become real.

"March the ninth. Shift of forty-one men below. Output four hundred tons." She paused. "It reads like any ledger. Like arithmetic."

"Keep going," Mirabel said. He sat across the fire from her, elbows on his knees, his face half shadow. "Ledgers lie by what they leave out. Read what's there and I'll tell you what isn't."

So she read. And the omissions began to show themselves, the way a sandbar shows itself only when the water drops. Payroll entries that thinned month over month even as production climbed. A shift of thirty-eight where forty-one had been listed. Then thirty-four. Then a gap—a whole week missing, pages cut cleanly away, the stubs left behind like amputations.

"They cut the pages out," she said. Her own voice sounded strange to her, flattened, careful. "Someone cut them out and left the rest."

"Evidence gets hungry," Mirabel said. "It eats itself if you let it."

* * *

By the third ledger the entries stopped pretending. The clerk's neat hand gave way to annotations in the margins, cramped and slanting, written fast, written angry:

*Ventilation shaft three collapsed again. Told the men it would hold. It will not hold. Requested closure. Denied.*

And below that, in different ink, a colder hand: *Production schedule stands. No further correspondence on this matter.*

Ines read it twice. The lantern hissed. Somewhere above them, water dripped from the cliff in a rhythm too slow to be rain, too regular to be anything but the mountain keeping time.

"Papa," she said, turning toward the blankets. "Who wrote this?"

Elias's eyes opened. They were bright with fever, sunken, but they found her with terrible accuracy, the way a compass finds north through fog.

"Corwin Ashe," he rasped. "Mine clerk. He kept two sets of books. The clean ones went to the company. These—" his hand twitched toward the stack in her lap "—these he hid in the adit. I found them twenty years after. I was a boy here. My father died here."

"Died how?" she asked, though she already knew. She had always known; it was the shape of the silence in every house they'd ever lived in, the way her father never spoke of the Serein Gorge, the way he drank tea instead of anything stronger and counted his money twice and flinched at the sound of blasting charges.

"The eleventh of June," Elias said. "Eleven men. They sealed the level with the men still breathing rather than lose the quarter's output. Then they called it a gas pocket. Act of God." His laugh was a dry ruin of a sound. "God had nothing to do with it. Men did it. Men with names. And Corwin Ashe wrote the names down."

Ines looked down at the ledger in her lap. At the margin note in its furious, upright hand. Eleven men. Her father had carried this for twenty years—had crossed rivers and deserts and twelve years of estrangement from her—to bring these dead men their names back.

"Read the fourth book," Elias whispered. "The fourth one. There are names."

* * *

The fourth ledger held them.

Not all at once. First the slow accumulation: dates, shifts, the ventilation requests denied in triplicate, the safety reports rewritten in the cold second hand—*amended per ownership*—and then, near the middle, a page where the clerk's discipline finally broke and he simply listed them, eleven names in a column, each followed by a widow's name and a sum paid out, sums so small Ines's throat closed around them. Forty crowns for a husband. Thirty-five for a father. One entry, for a boy of sixteen who'd lied about his age to get underground, read simply: *Mother deceased prior. Paid to parish.*

She read every name aloud. It took her several minutes. Nobody moved. The fire popped. Downriver, thunder muttered in the high country like something restless turning over in its sleep.

When she finished, the silence had weight, texture, almost temperature. Pao had come down from his watch without anyone calling him and stood at the edge of the lamplight, hat in his hands. Odile leaned in the companionway, arms folded tight. Even the river seemed to lower its voice.

"There's more," Mirabel said.

He said it quietly, but Ines heard the change in him—a flattening, a bracing, the tone of a man who has seen the wave coming and is telling everyone else to hold on. She looked up. He wasn't looking at the ledger. He was looking at the last one, the ninth, which lay apart from the others because Elias had set it apart, deliberately, when they'd opened the strongbox hours ago. Its cover was darker than the rest. Its binding had been mended once with wire.

"That one," Mirabel said. "Your father saved it for last. There's a reason."

"Papa?"

Elias had turned his face away, toward the black wall of the gorge. "I should have told you on the boat," he said. "I told myself I was sparing you. I've been telling myself that for twenty years. It's the same lie every time."

Her hands had gone cold on the ledger. "Papa, what is it?"

"Read it," he said. "You'll understand better reading it than hearing me fail to say it."

* * *

The ninth ledger was different inside. Fewer numbers. More words. It was Ashe's private record, she realized within a page—not payroll but testimony, written in the manner of a man building a case he did not expect to survive to argue. Dates and denials, copies of letters, the cold hand's instructions transcribed verbatim. And threaded through it all, a name that was not a miner's name, not a company man's name, but a name that appeared again and again in the margins like a refrain:

*Aldous Wren.*

She knew the name. Of course she knew it. Mirabel had spoken it exactly once in all the miles since Port of Small Mercies—one word dropped into a night conversation about why he measured life in miles and never years—and then he had closed the subject like a door. But she had heard the way he said it. You don't say a name like that unless it built you.

She glanced up. Mirabel had gone very still. Not calm-still. Held-still, the way water holds just before it goes over the lip of a fall.

"Aldous Wren filed the first report," she read aloud, because her voice seemed to be the only thing holding the moment together. "June the third, eight days before the collapse. Full survey of ventilation shafts three and five. Recommended immediate closure of the lower levels. Submitted to ownership. Receipt signed." She turned the page. "Aldous Wren repeated the warning in writing on June the fifth. June the seventh. June the tenth."

The next page was not in Ashe's hand at all. It was a clipping, brittle as moth wing, pasted in with yellowed glue. A newspaper notice. Ines made herself read it.

*"Aldous Wren, mine inspector, found deceased at his residence on the nineteenth of June. Ruled accidental. Services private."*

Eight days after the collapse. Five days after his last written warning.

"He didn't die in the mine," Ines said. Her voice came out thin. "He tried to stop it. He tried to stop it and they—"

"Yes," Elias said from the blankets. Just that. Yes.

She looked at Mirabel then, and the whole architecture of the man rearranged itself in front of her. The rule of no names, no pasts. The way he read water instead of people. Twelve years of miles. A boy raised by a mine inspector who had been murdered for telling the truth, in a case someone had fled, a trial he'd run from—

"You're his son," she said. Not a question. Nothing in her had any questions left. "Wren's son. That's who you are."

Mirabel didn't answer right away. The firelight moved on his face and he let it, and when he finally spoke his voice was low and even and cost him visibly, like hauling on a rope.

"He took me in when I was seven. After my mother. He taught me to read water because he said a river can't lie about where it's been. He wrote letters to every ministry and every paper for six years and nobody printed a word, and then one day he stopped writing letters and started packing, and we left in the night, and I grew up learning that the truth is the most expensive thing a man can carry." He looked at the ledger. "I thought he'd failed. I thought he'd shouted into the dark and the dark ate him and that was the whole story. I ran from his name because I thought it was a story about losing."

Ines turned the final page.

The final page was in Ashe's hand again, dated nineteen years after the others, and it was short. She read it aloud and the words went through her like cold water through a broken hull:

*"If this is found: Aldous Wren was the only man in authority who told the truth. Every denial, every amended report, every sealed level bears his signature in protest or his absence in exile. He was killed for the June report. I have waited my whole life to write that sentence where it might be read. Let whoever finds this finish what he started."*

The lantern hissed. The river rose and rose.

Ines became aware, distantly, that she was crying—not the hot dramatic weeping of grievance but something quieter and worse, tears that arrived without permission, grief and vindication tangled so tightly she couldn't have said which was which. Her father had not abandoned the truth. He had spent his dying year hauling it upriver in a strongbox. And Mirabel—stubborn, guarded, mile-counting Mirabel—had spent twelve years ashamed of the one man in the whole rotten affair who had done everything right.

"It wasn't failure," she said to him. "All those years. He wasn't a man who lost. He was a man they had to kill to silence, and even that didn't work, because here we are. Reading it. Out loud. In the rain."

Mirabel put his face in his hands. She had never seen him do that. She suspected no one aboard had. For a long moment the only sounds were the fire and the water and Elias's ragged breathing, and then Bram, gruff and gentle, added wood to the flames and said, "River's up another foot since noon. Captain'll want to hear that."

* * *

They worked by lantern light after that, wrapping each ledger in oilcloth, sealing them against the wet, strapping the bundle with cord. Ines did the wrapping herself. When she finished, she did not stow the bundle in the cargo hold with the crates. She strapped it instead against her own chest, under her coat, flat and hard and warm from the fire, over her heart like armor worn on the inside.

Pao came down from the gorge mouth at a run, and the look on his face ended every other conversation.

"Captain," he said. "Downstream bend. Lanterns. Three of them. It's the Corvus Line—and they're not stopping to camp."

Mirabel rose. Whatever had broken open in him by the fire was already being packed away, folded down small, to be carried later. He picked up the field glasses, and Ines watched him become, in the space of a breath, the captain again—but a different captain than the one who'd cast off from port, one with nothing left to hide and therefore nothing left to lose.

"How long?" he asked.

"At their speed, dawn," Pao said. "Maybe sooner. River's doing half their work for them."

Mirabel lowered the glasses. He looked at Elias, at the ledgers over Ines's heart, at the black flooded throat of the gorge waiting downriver, and Ines saw him weigh it all in the old silent arithmetic—and then set the arithmetic aside.

"Then we go tonight," he said. "Wake Odile. Tell her we're running the flood."
