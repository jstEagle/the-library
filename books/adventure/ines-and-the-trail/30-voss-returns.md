## 30. Voss Returns

The ledgers were still damp when the Corvus came.

I had spread them on the cabin roof like laundry—eleven years of a dead mine's arithmetic, pages warped with river water and Elias's fever-sweat, ink bleeding at the corners where the strongbox had taken water through a seam no one had thought to check in thirty years. Ines sat cross-legged beside me with a hair dryer Bram had rigged off the boat's generator, coaxing page after page back toward legibility. The sun was doing what it could. The monsoon had spent itself overnight into a gray, rinsed-out morning, and the gorge behind us roared with everything we'd run from, forty miles of white noise that made conversation something you leaned into.

That was why I didn't hear the Corvus until Odile's hand closed on my shoulder.

"Captain," she said, and nothing else, because she didn't have to.

I came down the ladder and looked upstream and there it was: the same flat-nosed workboat we'd seen shadowing us since the Widow's Teeth, gunmetal gray, riding low with the weight of whatever Voss kept aboard instead of cargo. It came around the last bend slow, engine idling down to a mutter, and it did not do any of the things I had spent two hundred miles imagining it would do. It didn't swing to block the channel. It didn't put men on deck with rifles slung casual across their chests. It just drifted in alongside us at fifty yards and stopped, nose into the current, holding station against the flood the way only a very good pilot holds station against a flood.

Pao appeared at the rail with a boathook he had no business holding like a weapon. Bram came up out of the galley wiping his hands, which is what Bram did instead of reaching for things. Ines stood, and I saw her tuck one ledger inside her jacket, against her chest, where she'd been keeping them since the Devil's Throat. She'd strapped them there with a strip of canvas and two knots of her own devising, and she hadn't taken them off to sleep.

"He's alone," Odile said. She had the binoculars up. "One man at the wheel. No deck crew."

"I can count," I said, harsher than she deserved.

She lowered the binoculars and looked at me with the particular patience of an engineer looking at a machine that is making a sound it shouldn't. "You've been waiting for this boat since Port of Small Mercies," she said. "You've been wrong about what it would do every single day. Maybe let it surprise you once."

The Corvus edged closer. Fifty yards became thirty. And Renn Voss stepped away from his own wheel, leaving his boat to hold itself in the current—a thing you only do if you trust your hands or don't care anymore whether they drown—and raised both arms above his head, empty palms out, and turned slowly in a circle so we could see every inch of him.

No sidearm. No rifle. Not even a knife on his belt that I could see. Just a heavyset man in a soaked oilskin coat with gray coming into his beard, standing on the deck of a claim-jumper's boat with his hands open to the sky.

"Mirabel Montrose!" he called across the water, and my whole body went cold, because that was not my name, not the name painted on our transom, not the name anyone aboard except Ines had ever heard me answer to—and she had heard it exactly once, by firelight, traded for a letter burning in a coffee can.

He knew. He had known, maybe, since the first day he looked back at us from his own wheelhouse outside the gorge. Twelve years I had built a man out of miles and river-reading and a rule about names, and Renn Voss said my real name across sixty feet of brown floodwater as easily as asking the time.

"You know me," I called back. It wasn't a question. My voice came out flatter than I wanted.

"I've known you since you were nineteen years old and too stubborn to cry at a funeral," Voss said. "I investigated the Hollis case. I interviewed you twice. You lied to me both times, badly, and I liked you anyway." He paused, and the current shouldered his boat a few feet closer, and nobody aboard moved to stop it. "I never found enough to charge Augustin Hollis with anything. That has been sitting in my chest for eleven years like a stone I swallowed. Then three weeks ago a dying prospector walks into my office in Delta Bend carrying survey records he wouldn't show me, and asks me how far up the Serein a shallow-draft boat can get before monsoon, and I start putting things together."

"The Corvus Line," Pao said under his breath. "Claim-jumpers."

"That's what the Corvus Line is," Voss agreed, loud enough for all of us. "It's a company I bought four years ago with my pension money so I could move up and down this river without anybody asking why. Best cover I ever found. Nobody suspects the vultures." His mouth did something that was almost a smile and mostly wasn't. "I've been following you since the Teeth because I needed to know whose map that was before I decided what to do about it. I've had men aboard half the way who would have taken that strongbox off you at gunpoint and been paid well for it. I put them ashore at Small Mercies. They think I'm running a salvage job solo. As far as the company knows, I'm still hunting you."

Bram shifted beside me. "Why should we believe a word of that?"

"You shouldn't," Voss said promptly. "Believing things is how people drown. Check me. Send your engineer over—I'll open every locker on this boat, show you the radio log, show you the letters between me and the mining commission that I've been writing for eleven years and filing nowhere because nobody would take them." He reached inside his coat, slow, telegraphing every inch of the motion, and drew out a folded packet wrapped in oilcloth. He held it up. "Affidavits. Mine. Sworn statements about what the commission knew about the Lower Serein collapse and when they knew it, and about the two men who paid to make sure the inquiry ended where it ended. I wrote them eleven years ago and I've carried them ever since, waiting for someone to bring me the other half of the truth." He looked past me, past all of us, to where Ines stood at the rail with her father's ledgers against her heart. "Your father came to see me six weeks ago, Miss Ines. Did he tell you that?"

She didn't answer. Her jaw was set the way Elias's got when the coughing was bad and he was pretending it wasn't.

"No," Voss said softly. "He wouldn't have. He didn't trust me either. Smart man. Dying men can't afford smart, though, so he hedged—he told me about the claim without telling me what was in it, and he asked me questions about the disaster that only made sense if he already knew the answers, and I let him go because I thought: here, finally, is somebody going up that river to dig up what I couldn't. And then I found out who was banked at Delta Bend waiting for him—the actual Corvus interests, the ones whose names are going to be in those ledgers if your father found what I think he found—and I realized I'd pointed a dying man straight at the wolves." He spread his hands again. "So I followed. All the way. Waiting to see if I'd have to choose."

"And what did you choose?" I asked.

The current pushed his boat another yard. Close enough now that I could see the lines around his eyes, deeper than I remembered, the face of a man who had spent eleven years losing arguments with himself.

"I chose to come unarmed to the boat of a man I once hunted," he said, "and ask him for a partnership. Because here's what I know, Captain—Captain Montrose—that you don't yet. Ledgers in a strongbox are evidence. Evidence gets lost. Evidence gets burned, gets drowned, gets ruled inadmissible by judges who summer at lakes owned by the men in the photographs. I have watched evidence die for eleven years. What evidence needs—" he tapped his own chest, over the oilcloth packet— "is a witness who won't shut up. Me. And a story the whole country can read at breakfast. A journalist. Do you have a journalist?"

"We know one," Ines said. First words she'd given him. Her voice was steady but her hand, at the rail, was white-knuckled, and I understood why. Every word this man said was either salvation or the most patient trap ever baited, and there was no way to tell which from the outside, and she had her father's life's work strapped to her body while she decided.

* * *

We brought the boats together at mid-channel, hulls kissing with a groan of rubber fenders, and Odile went across alone because she volunteered before I could pick someone and because, as she put it, "if he's lying, I'll know, and if he's not lying, I want to see his engine room, because that boat holds station better than ours does and I intend to steal ideas."

While she was gone we held a council on the afterdeck that was less a council than five people talking over each other in the rain-mist.

"He's the investigator," Pao said. "From the trial. From Mirabel's—" he caught himself, glanced at me, went on anyway, which was its own kind of gift— "from back then. He hunted Hollis. Now he's what, sorry?"

"People change," Bram said.

"People also lie for eleven years to get close to a strongbox," Pao said.

"Both things are true," Ines said, "and we can't check either one from here, and the river's rising, and Papa has maybe two days in him, and I am not going to stand on this deck arguing philosophy while the only other person on this river who wants those names published sits fifty yards away offering us his radio." She looked at me. "Captain. You knew him. Before. What's your read?"

Everyone looked at me. I hated it. I looked downstream instead, at the flooded gorge we'd come out of, and I tried to remember Renn Voss at nineteen—big hands, tired eyes, a notebook he wrote in even when nobody was talking, the smell of cheap tobacco and ink. I remembered him leaning across the table at the second interview and saying, quietly, off the record, *kid, I don't think your guardian did what they say he did, but I need you to help me prove it, and you're not helping.* And I remembered being nineteen and so full of grief and loyalty that I'd rather burn the world down than risk betraying the man who'd pulled me out of it, and I had lied, and Voss had closed his notebook and said, *that stone goes in my chest now, not yours,* and walked out.

Eleven years later he was still carrying it.

"He believed Hollis was innocent," I said. "Back then. When believing it cost him something. Investigators don't stay sorry unless they're honest, and honest men don't buy claim-jumping companies unless they're desperate." I turned around. "But I've been wrong about men before, and the price of being wrong here is everything Ines is carrying against her chest. So we test him. Everything. Odile tears his boat apart. We keep the ledgers on our deck until every word checks. And if one thing smells wrong—"

"One thing will smell wrong," Bram said. "There's always one thing."

"Then we'll weigh it," I said. "Like everything else on this river."

Odile came back an hour later, waded across the gap between hulls with water to her knees, and stood dripping on our deck with an expression I had learned to read like weather.

"Radio log's clean," she said. "Letters to the commission are real—I recognized the commission's file stamps, they answered him twice, both times telling him the matter was closed. There's a photograph taped inside his wheelhouse of eleven miners, names written on the back in pencil. He's had it up there four years. And captain—" she hesitated, which Odile never did— "he's got a bunk made up. One bunk, made up neat, with a blanket folded at the foot. Been that way a long time, by the wear on it. Man's been living alone on that boat waiting for something."

Nobody said anything for a moment. The gorge roared behind us. Somewhere below decks, Elias coughed, long and wet, and stopped, and coughed again.

"All right," I said.

* * *

Voss came aboard at noon with his oilcloth packet and a bottle of Bram's terrible coffee in his fist like a peace offering, and the first thing he did was not look at the ledgers. That was the thing that settled something in me, though I couldn't have explained it then. He stood on our deck among strangers who had every reason to throw him overboard, and he asked about the boat—asked Odile about her injection pump, asked Pao where he'd learned to tie off a bowline one-handed, asked Bram if that was chicory in the coffee or a crime—and only when Ines brought the ledgers up out of the cabin, unwrapped the canvas, laid them on the chart table under the lamp, did he go quiet all at once, like a man entering a church.

He read for two hours. He didn't touch the pages; Ines turned them for him, wearing cotton gloves Bram produced from the galley first-aid kit. He read the shift logs. He read the maintenance reports where the timber supports were marked sound, year after year, by inspectors who had signed names that matched the names in the commission's donor lists. He read the final page, the one Elias had hidden even from his own daughter until the fever took the hiding out of him—the page in Augustin Hollis's own hand, dated nine days before the collapse, reporting the support failure in the lower gallery and demanding closure, forwarded to the commission, stamped received, stamped *no action warranted.*

And then Renn Voss, investigator, hunter, the man whose name had lived in my nightmares for twelve years, took off his glasses and set them on the chart table and cried. Not loudly. Not for long. Just stood there with his jaw working and his eyes streaming, looking at my guardian's handwriting demanding that eleven men be saved, and said, "Nine days. He gave them nine days' warning in writing, and they filed it."

"Yes," Ines said gently.

"They killed him for it and called it an accident and I spent eleven years unable to prove a word of it, and it was in a strongbox under a mountain the whole time." He put his glasses back on. He blew his nose on a bandana that had seen better decades. He looked at me, and there was no hunter left in his face at all, only a tired man holding a stone he'd swallowed a long time ago and finally, finally spitting it out. "Captain. I'm going to ask you for something strange."

"Ask."

"When we give this to the press—and we will, tomorrow, at the delta, before the river rises again or anything else on this earth can stop it—I want your guardian's name spoken first. Before the guilty ones. Let the first thing the country hears be the name of the man who tried to save them." He held out the oilcloth packet, his affidavits, eleven years old and carried the whole way. "And I want you to take these yourself. Hand-deliver them. You're the only other living soul who knows what it cost him."

I took the packet. It weighed almost nothing. Eleven years of a man's conscience, lighter than a ledger page.

Down in the cabin, Elias coughed again, longer this time, and Ines went to him, and Voss watched her go with an expression I recognized because I'd worn it for twelve years in mirrors—the look of a person measuring time not in miles or years but in breaths somebody else has left.

"His boat's faster than ours downstream," Odile said quietly, at my elbow. "And his radio reaches Delta Bend from the narrows. Ours doesn't reach past the bend." She didn't say *we need him.* She didn't have to. "Storm's regrouping in the headwaters, according to his barometer. We've got maybe a day and a half of decent water, captain. After that, the delta run turns ugly."

I looked at Renn Voss standing in my wheelhouse doorway, hat in his hands like a man at a funeral, and I thought about rules. No names, no pasts, no passengers. Twelve years of rules, and every one of them built to keep out exactly this: a stranger with a history that touched mine, stepping aboard, wanting something, offering something bigger.

"Mr. Voss," I said. "How are you at reading sandbars?"

He almost smiled. "Teach me," he said. "I've got a day and a half."

Below, Elias's coughing stopped altogether—for a moment too long—and then Ines's voice came up the companionway, tight and fast: "Captain. Captain, you need to come down here. Now."

I went, taking the stairs two at a time, the affidavits still in my hand.
