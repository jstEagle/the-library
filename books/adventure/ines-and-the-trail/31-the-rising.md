## 31. The Rising

Odile heard the storm before she saw it—a sound like far-off freight, like the whole sky clearing its throat somewhere up in the headwaters where no one lived and nothing grew but stone and cloud.

She was in the engine room at the time, elbow-deep in the coolant housing of the port engine, which had developed a rattle on the last stretch that she didn't like and couldn't yet name. The *Kestrel's* engines were old women like herself: temperamental, full of opinions, capable of astonishing endurance if you listened to them properly. This one was trying to tell her something. She had her ear pressed to the housing, one hand on the throttle linkage, when the sound came down the gorge—not thunder exactly, though it would be thunder soon enough, but a deeper vibration, felt in the deck plates before it reached her bones.

She sat back on her heels and wiped her hands on a rag that had been black for years and would be black until the end of the world.

"That's not rain," she said to the engine room, to nobody, because saying things aloud helped her think, and the engine room had never once judged her for talking to machines instead of people.

The engine rattled on, indifferent.

She climbed the ladder two rungs at a time, which her knees protested about loudly, and came out onto the aft deck into air that tasted wrong. That was the first thing. Not the light—though the light was wrong too, a bruised yellow-green pressing down through the gorge walls like something seen through dirty glass—but the taste. Forty years around water and you learned its flavors. Clean snowmelt. Silt after a dry spell. The mineral tang of a river running through limestone. This was none of those. This was wet dust and hot iron and the particular green rot-smell of a jungle being drowned.

Pao was on the bow rail, looking upstream, and he didn't turn when her boots hit the deck, which told her everything before she'd taken three steps. Pao noticed everything; it was his curse and his gift both. If he was staring at the horizon like a man watching a debt collector come up the gangplank, then the horizon deserved the staring.

"Tell me," Odile said.

"The radio caught a relay out of Kessel Bend an hour ago." His voice was flat in the way it went flat when he was frightened and refusing to be. "Monsoon cell made landfall two days early. It's sitting on the headwaters right now. They're calling for eight inches in twenty-four hours over the upper catchment."

Odile did the arithmetic without wanting to. Eight inches over the upper catchment meant the feeder creeks—the ones they'd been hopping all week, ankle-deep and laughing about them—would become rivers by morning. Meant the Serein itself, already running high and brown, would double. Triple. Meant the gorge, that long stone throat with its single channel and its forty miles of whitewater between them and open delta water, would fill like a bathtub with the tap wide open.

"When does it get here?"

"Water takes time to come down from the headwaters." Pao finally turned, and his face in that bruised light looked younger than his years, which happened only when he was scared. "Eighteen hours. Maybe less if the ground's already saturated. And Odile—" he swallowed "—the forecast said ten days. We planned for ten days."

"I know what we planned for."

The plan had been a good plan. Plans usually were, right up until the world got a vote. Ten days of margin, Mirabel had said, tapping the chart in the pilothouse with that finger of his that never quite stopped moving, enough time to reach the claim, do what needed doing, and come back down through the gorge ahead of the rise. Ten days. They'd spent six getting here, and the claim itself had eaten another two, and now—

Now there were hours.

* * *

They gathered in the pilothouse as the light failed, and it wasn't really a meeting so much as five people standing close together in a small room while the river spoke to them through the hull in a voice that had changed key since morning.

Mirabel stood at the wheel even though the boat was tied off, hands resting on the spokes like a pianist between movements. Ines was beside him with the chart spread across the binnacle, weighted at the corners with a coffee mug, a wrench, Bram's spare pipe, and a brass fitting Odile had been meaning to reinstall for a month. Elias lay in the aft cabin behind a closed door, feverish, breathing in that wet tearing way that Odile pretended not to hear because hearing it meant admitting what it meant.

"The good news," Mirabel said, and someone—Bram, probably—made a sound that might have been a laugh in a kinder century, "is that we don't have to decide whether to run the gorge in flood."

"There's a choice?" Ines asked.

"No." He tapped the chart. "There's only a choice about when. We can leave tonight, in the dark, and run the first thirty miles before the worst of the rise hits the lower gorge. Or we can wait for daylight, lose six hours, and run all forty of them in full flood."

Nobody said anything for a moment. Outside, the first fat drops began hitting the cabin roof, spaced apart, like a drummer counting in.

"Running whitewater at night," Odile said slowly, "in the rain, overloaded past every rating this hull ever had—isn't a thing people do, Mirabel. It's a thing people survive, sometimes, if the river's feeling generous."

"I know."

"And the alternative is waiting for morning and running all of it in water that'll be over the banks."

"I know that too."

"Then say it plain," she said, because she was too old and too tired for a captain who hedged, and because underneath her fear there was something else, something that had been growing in her since the night the crew voted to take this job over his objection—something that sounded almost like faith, and that frightened her more than the river did.

Mirabel looked at each of them in turn. At Ines, who had strapped the ledgers to her own chest without being asked and hadn't taken them off since. At Bram, who'd gone soft-eyed and silent ever since reading those pages, as if some ledger inside himself had also been balanced. At Pao, who nodded once, small, the way he did when he'd already decided and was just waiting for permission to say so.

"We leave tonight," Mirabel said. "Full tanks, everything lashed, everyone in their gear from the first minute. We run the Widow's Teeth in the dark and we don't stop until the gorge opens up." A pause. "If anyone wants off, this is the town for it. No shame in it. I mean that."

"You know we're not getting off," Odile said. "You knew that before you offered."

"I had to offer."

"I know." She put her hand over his on the wheel, briefly, the way you'd touch a railing in a shaking house. "It's why we stay."

* * *

Loading the *Kestrel* in the rain was an act of stubbornness disguised as seamanship.

The drops had found their rhythm by then—no longer counting in but playing, hard and constant, drumming the decks until conversation required shouting. Odile directed from the cargo deck with her oilskin hood streaming, pointing with a boathook while Bram hauled crates and Pao lashed them down with a speed that came from some previous life he'd never explained and she'd never asked about. Everything went below or under canvas. Everything got doubled lines. The spare fuel drums—half-empty now, thanks to the sabotage and the day it cost them—went amidships, low, strapped to the frame rails where they'd shift least.

Ines worked alongside them without a word, and Odile watched her check the satchel strap for the hundredth time, watched her press her palm flat against the ledgers beneath her jacket like a woman confirming a heartbeat.

"They'll get wet," Odile shouted to her, nodding at the satchel. "Everything gets wet tonight."

Ines pulled out the satchel anyway, opened it, and showed her: the ledgers wrapped in oilcloth, sealed with tar at the seams, then wrapped again in Elias's spare shirt, then in a second layer of oilcloth. "I re-did it this afternoon," she shouted back. "Twice."

"Good girl," Odile said, and meant it in the way she'd never once meant it condescendingly, and saw that Ines knew that, and something passed between them there in the rain that neither of them would ever mention again—an alliance, maybe, or just the recognition of two women who fixed things.

By full dark the boat was transformed. Low in the water—Odile could see it in the freeboard, could feel it in the way the deck moved under her boots, sluggish, heavy, a vessel carrying more than it was built to carry, which was true in ways that had nothing to do with tonnage. Five people. One dying man. A strongbox of dead men's testimony. Enough fuel to make the delta if nothing went wrong, which nothing wouldn't.

She made her last round of the engine room alone, because that was her ritual and rituals were how she kept her hands steady. Oil levels. Belt tension. The raw-water strainers cleared and the spares laid out on the workbench in a row, because changing a clogged strainer in a flooding engine room at night was the kind of job you wanted to be able to do blind, and might have to. She wrapped the spare impeller kit in wax cloth and tucked it inside her coat, against her ribs, where it would stay dry and where she could reach it without thinking.

Then she stood for a moment with her hand flat on the port engine's warm housing, the way you'd rest your hand on the flank of an animal you were about to ask something terrible of.

"Old woman," she said quietly. "One more river. That's all I'm asking. One more, and I'll rebuild you myself, every gasket, I swear it on whatever you hold holy, which is probably torque specifications."

The engine ticked as it cooled, which she chose to take for assent.

* * *

They cast off at half past nine, and the river was already rising.

Odile felt it before anyone said it—the change in the *Kestrel's* motion as the current took the hull, not the steady muscular pull she'd known all week but something quicker, hungrier, a hand that closed rather than guided. She came up to the pilothouse and stood at the rear window, and even in the dark she could read the water by its sound. Rain on rain. Current on rock. And underneath it all, from upstream, that freight-train rumble again, closer now, the storm still unloading itself on the high country, sending its whole self down toward them through every creek and crack and canyon in the watershed.

The gauges on the bank told the story plainly enough for anyone who knew how to look: the driftwood line from yesterday's high water was already submerged, and the new debris riding past them—whole trees, root-balls like torn-up hair, a section of somebody's fence traveling with terrible dignity—was coming through faster than any river should move.

"Forty miles," Mirabel said at the wheel. He said it the way other men said prayers.

"Forty miles," Odile answered, because someone had to, and because she had decided, sometime during the loading, in the rain, with her hand on the engine, that if this crew was going to do an impossible thing then she was going to be the voice that treated it as merely difficult. Fear was contagious. So was its opposite. She'd learned that in another life, on another boat, in a country she'd left for reasons she kept in the same locked room where Mirabel kept his.

The gorge walls rose on either side, black against a sky that flickered now with heat lightning, silent sheets of it, like the storm showing them previews. The searchlight carved a white tunnel through the rain and found the water beyond the bow: fast, confused, already carrying more than it knew what to do with.

"Widow's Teeth in four miles," Pao called from the bow, lashed to the rail with a line around his waist, because Mirabel had ordered it and Pao, who argued about everything, had not argued about that.

Behind them, downstream of the stern, Odile heard it—one long groan of timber, distant, followed by a splash that was much too large. Something big had just come loose upriver. A jam breaking, maybe. A bank giving way. The river rearranging itself, as it did when it rose, casually, catastrophically, the way a sleeping man turns over.

Ines appeared at the pilothouse door, soaked through despite the oilskins, eyes bright and terrified and absolutely certain. "How bad is it going to get?"

Odile considered lying to her, and rejected it, because this crew had spent the whole voyage learning the price of half-truths, and she would not add to the account.

"Bad," she said. "Worse than anything any of us has run. But boats float, girl. That's the whole secret of them. Everything else is detail."

Ines looked at her for a long moment, rain running off her chin, and then she nodded and went forward to stand beside Pao at the rail, one hand pressed flat over the ledgers, the other gripping the line.

Ahead, the gorge narrowed, and the sound changed—that deepening, that gathering roar that every one of them knew by now, the Widow's Teeth announcing themselves. Mirabel's hands settled on the wheel. Odile took her station at the throttles, both palms flat on the handles, and felt the engines answer her touch, alive, willing, holding nothing back.

"Whatever happens," Mirabel said quietly, not turning, "stay with the boat."

And the *Kestrel*, overloaded and overdrawn and carrying every secret any of them had ever tried to bury, drove her bow into the dark and the noise, and the Widow's Teeth rose up out of the rain to meet them.
