## 32. Elias's Last Mile

The fever had stopped pretending to be a visitor. It lived in him now, a tenant with squatter's rights, and Elias Ines had made his peace with the arrangement the way a man makes peace with weather—by watching it, naming it, and no longer arguing about when it might leave.

He lay in the narrow berth beneath the wheelhouse, wrapped in every blanket Odile could find, listening to the boat talk to itself. The *Serein Queen*—he'd learned her name from Pao, who spoke of her the way other men spoke of sweethearts—had a vocabulary all her own. The tick of cooling iron in the engine room. The groan of cargo lashing as the current leaned against them. Above, through the deck planking, footsteps crossing and recrossing, Mirabel's long stride, Ines's quicker one, Bram's shuffle, each as recognizable to Elias now as voices through a wall in childhood.

Twelve years he had measured himself against this journey. Twelve years since he'd walked out of that gorge with ore samples sewn into his coat lining and a name on a ledger page that would not let him sleep. He had told himself he was going back for gold. He had told Ines's mother that too, before she stopped believing it, before she stopped waiting, before she died with the photograph turned face-down on the dresser so she wouldn't have to look at his young face while she hated him.

Lies, all of it, worn smooth as river stones from handling. The truth was simpler and heavier: he had found something in that mountain worth more than gold and less spendable, and he had run from it because running was easier than carrying. And then the coughing started, and the doctor in the delta town had looked at his lungs the way you look at a flooded mine shaft, and Elias understood that the mountain had sent him a bill.

*You'll go back,* he'd promised himself. *You'll go back and read every page aloud.*

And here he was. Going back. Dying on schedule, which was, he supposed, the one appointment a man like him never missed.

* * *

Ines came down to him in the gray hour before dawn, when the river fog pressed against the porthole like a face at glass. He knew her step—he'd known it at three years old, that quick determined slap of small feet, and he knew it now, grown heavier with purpose but still hers. She sat on the stool beside the berth and took his hand without asking, which was new. All her life she had asked permission for everything, even her anger. Especially her anger.

"You're cold," she said.

"I'm honest," he said. "There's a difference. Cold comes and goes."

Her mouth did something complicated—not quite a smile, not quite the opposite. She had her mother's mouth. He had spent thirty years avoiding looking at it directly.

"The storm," she said. "Odile says the gauge rose four inches overnight. We cast off within the hour."

"Then I picked my timing well."

"Papa." His name in her voice, and underneath it, twelve years of the word she'd really wanted to say: *why*. "Don't joke. Not now."

"I've been joking for sixty-one years, girl. It's how I kept from screaming. You want me to stop now, at the end, when I finally have an audience?" He squeezed her hand, or meant to; his fingers answered him the way old rigging answers, sluggishly, with complaint. "Listen to me. There isn't much time and there's less breath, so I'm going to spend both carefully."

She leaned close. Fog-light made her face pale and young and furious with love, if love could be furious, and he thought: *yes, that's exactly what love is, the fury of it, the refusal to let go of someone even when letting go would be kinder.*

"When we reach the claim," he said, "there's a strongbox under the collapsed timber by the adit. Ledgers. Photographs. A manifest with names on it—men who signed off on shoring they knew wouldn't hold, men who counted eleven miners cheaper than eleven weeks of delay." His chest seized; he rode it out, breathing shallow, the way you breathe around broken ground. "I want you to take them. Not me. You."

"Why not you?"

"Because I won't be there." He said it plainly, the way he'd once said *the vein runs east-northeast*, plain facts of surveying. "Not past the casting-off, I think. Maybe not even that far. And even if some stubborn scrap of me makes it upriver, I can't carry anything anymore. My hands—" He lifted one from the blanket, watched it tremble like a compass needle near iron. "Look at that. Forty years a prospector and I can't hold a pan steady. So it has to be you. Everything has to be you."

"I don't know what to do with it," she whispered. "Papa, I don't know what any of this is for. You dragged us up a river toward something you wouldn't explain, and now you're handing me—"

"Names," he said. "I'm handing you names. Eleven of them dead and half a dozen living who'd rather the dead stay anonymous. That's what I'm handing you. It's heavy. I know it's heavy. I carried it wrong for twelve years and it broke everything I touched, including—" his voice caught, and he let it catch, because hiding it now would be the last lie and he was done with lies, "including you. Including your mother. I ran because I was afraid, and fear is just cowardice wearing work clothes."

* * *

The engine turned over above him—a deep mechanical cough, then a steady heartbeat—and the hull began to hum. Casting off. He felt it in the berth, in the bones the fever hadn't claimed yet: the slow lateral drift as Pao threw off the lines, the pivot, the bite of current taking them. Going upstream. Into the gorge. Into the rising water.

Mirabel came down. Elias heard him first—the captain moved quietly for a big man, but the boat announced him, a particular creak at the top of the ladder that Elias had catalogued days ago the way he'd once catalogued birdcalls. Then the man filled the doorway, hat in hand, which Elias noted. A man who removed his hat in a dying prospector's berth had manners older than his assumed name.

"Captain," Elias said.

"Mr. Ines." Mirabel crouched by the berth, forearms on knees. Up close his eyes were the color of the river in shadow, and just as hard to see the bottom of. "We're making good time. Odile says two days to the claim if the water behaves."

"The water won't behave."

"No," Mirabel agreed. "It won't."

"Good. I never trusted behaving water." Elias tried to laugh and produced instead a wet rattle that frightened Ines—he saw it frighten her, saw her hand tighten on his. He squeezed back, or intended to. "Captain. I have two requests. One for you, one for me, and I'll tell you which is which."

"Go on."

"For me: don't bury me up there. Whatever happens, whatever the storm does, take me back down. I've spent twelve years going up this river in my head. I'd as soon make the return trip in fact, even if it's only my dust doing the traveling." He paused for breath, spent it, gathered more. "Scatter me somewhere with rapids. Loud water. I want noise at my funeral, none of this whispering over a hole."

Beside him, Ines made a sound that was half sob, half laugh, and he counted it a victory.

"And the second request?" Mirabel asked.

"That one's for you." Elias turned his head on the pillow—it cost him, turning his head had become a labor requiring planning—and looked at the captain straight on. "You measure your life in miles. I've watched you do it. Every day a distance covered, a hazard passed, a ledger entry: miles behind, miles ahead. You think it keeps you safe. It doesn't. It just keeps you moving, and there's a difference between moving and living that took me sixty-one years and a death sentence to learn." He drew air, and the air hurt, and he spent it anyway. "Stop measuring life in miles, Captain. Measure it in people. In mornings where somebody laughs at breakfast. In hands you hold. In truths you stop running from. That's the only arithmetic that ever paid out for me, and I got into it late, and I'm leaving most of my earnings unspent. Don't make my mistake. Spend it while you're alive."

Mirabel said nothing for a long moment. The boat carried them northward, upstream, into the throat of the country, and the fog outside the porthole thinned toward a light that couldn't decide whether to be gray or gold.

"I'll try," the captain said at last, and his voice was rough, and Elias thought: *that's the truest thing anyone's said to me in weeks.*

"Trying counts," Elias said. "Out here, trying is most of what anybody's got."

* * *

After that, the fever took the wheel.

He surfaced and sank. Once he woke to Bram pressing a cup of willow-bark tea to his lips, muttering something about stubborn old mules, and the cook's hand shook worse than his own, which Elias found deeply satisfying. Once he woke to Odile's voice in the wheelhouse overhead, reading river soundings aloud like scripture, and Pao answering, and the two of them arguing about a channel the way scholars argue about a text, and he thought: *this is what he built. This ragged, loyal, quarrelsome family. This is what a man makes when he can't go home.* He envied Mirabel that, cleanly, without bitterness. Envy without bitterness was new to him. Dying improved him, apparently. Shame it came so late.

Once he woke to Ines asleep in the chair beside him, her head tipped against the bulkhead, her hand still holding his, and he lay still as the grave he was approaching and studied her face by lantern light. Her mother's jaw. His own stubborn chin, God help her. The lines around her eyes that hadn't been there twelve years ago, carved by his absence.

"Ines," he whispered, and she woke instantly, the way mothers wake, the way prospectors wake when the ground shifts. "Come here. Closer. My voice doesn't travel anymore."

She bent over him. Her hair fell forward and brushed his cheek, and he breathed it in—soap and woodsmoke and river—and thought he had never smelled anything better, not pine money, not rain on ore, nothing.

"Tell it all," he said. "When I'm gone. The disaster, the cover-up, the names. Don't soften it. Don't protect me. I'm past protection, and the dead don't have reputations—they have records. Let the record be true." His breath whistled. He pushed through it. "That's your inheritance, girl. Not gold. Truth. It's the only thing I ever found worth digging for, and I buried it myself, and now you have to dig it up. I'm sorry. I'm sorry I left you a shovel instead of a cradle."

"Papa—"

"Promise me."

"I promise." Her tears hit his face, warm, and he thought: *rain. Warm rain. The monsoon arriving early, right here, right on my skin.* "I promise, Papa. I'll tell it all."

"Good girl." He smiled, and felt the smile reach his eyes, and thought dimly that it must be years since that happened. "Good girl. Stronger than me. Always were."

* * *

The light through the porthole went gold, then amber, then the deep copper of late afternoon. The gorge walls must be closing in—he knew this stretch, remembered it from the other direction, twelve years younger and twelve years more cowardly. The *Serein Queen* climbed against the current, patient as a mule, and Elias climbed with her, each breath a switchback, each pause a view of something he couldn't name.

He wasn't afraid. That surprised him. He'd expected fear to be the final passenger, riding him down, but fear had gotten off miles back, somewhere around the night portage, somewhere around the moment he'd heard this crew—these strays, these runaways, these liars with kind hands—hauling his boat through black water by lantern light, cursing and singing and refusing to quit. After that, what was there to fear? He'd seen what people were. The best of them, anyway.

The fever showed him things. His wife, young, laughing at his terrible handwriting. The mine, whole and roaring, before. Eleven faces he'd memorized from a company photograph, men whose names he'd carried sewn into his coat like contraband. The mountain, green and indifferent, keeping its secrets the way rivers keep drowned men.

And once—clearly, kindly—the sandbar at dawn, the first morning, when a coughing stranger had offered an impossible fare to a captain with no name, and the captain had said no, and the stranger had shown him a map on oiled silk anyway, because hope doesn't take no for an answer. Hope just keeps poling upstream.

"Almost there," he said, though he didn't know to whom. The berth was empty except for him. Or full. Hard to say, at the end, where the line ran between.

He heard Ines come down. He heard Mirabel's boots on the ladder. He heard the river, loud now, loud as he'd asked, loud as a funeral should be.

"Ines," he said, and his voice was nearly gone, a thread, a wire singing in wind. She was there. Of course she was there. "Tell it all."

"I will. I swear it."

"Captain." The boots stopped beside the berth. "Miles and years. Both. Count both."

"I will," Mirabel said, and the voice above him was breaking, and Elias thought: *good. Break open. That's how the light gets in. Somebody smart said that once, or should have.*

The current took the boat. The mountain waited ahead, green and patient, keeping his secret one more mile.

Elias Ines closed his eyes, and for the first time in twelve years, he stopped measuring.

* * *

They cast off from the last anchorage below the claim as the sky went the color of wet slate, and the river had risen a foot since noon and was rising faster, brown water shouldering against the hull, carrying branches, carrying whole trees, carrying the smell of the storm coming down out of the headwaters like a debt collector with a warrant. Odile stood at the wheel with her jaw set. Pao worked the lines with quick silent hands. Bram stood at the rail beside the shape wrapped in canvas that had been Elias Ines, one hand resting on it, saying nothing, because cooks know when words are useless.

Ines knelt by the rail with her father's map on oiled silk folded against her heart, and Mirabel took the wheel beside Odile and looked downstream at forty miles of flooded gorge and did not measure it in miles.

He measured it in people. Four alive aboard, one dead, one truth, and no time left.

"Cast off," he said, and the river took them.
