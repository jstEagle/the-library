## 33. The Gorge in Flood

The river had stopped being a river. That was Mirabel's first thought as he took the wheel from Odile's shaking hands and looked upstream into the throat of the gorge. It was a beast now, a brown muscle of a thing, swollen with the monsoon's first fury, carrying whole trees in its grip like a man carrying kindling. The rain came sideways, needling his face, and the walls of the gorge rose on either side of them, black stone streaked with falling water, so close in places that he could have thrown a stone from the pilothouse and struck the far bank.

"Forty miles," Odile said. She had been at the wheel for six hours and her voice had gone flat and strange, the voice of someone who has looked too long at something terrible. "If the boat holds. If the channel holds. If—"

"I know the ifs," Mirabel said, and took the wheel.

Behind him the pilothouse was a chaos of soaked canvas, strapped cargo, and people. Ines sat on the engineer's bench with the oilskin packet of ledgers strapped to her chest, her arms crossed over it the way a mother crosses her arms over a sleeping child. Elias's body lay in the aft cabin, wrapped in sailcloth, because they had not had time to bury him and the river would not give them time now. Pao was forward on the bow, lashed by a safety line, reading the water with the long pole he'd used since they left the delta, calling depths back to Odile in a shorthand they had invented together over years. Bram was below, keeping the engine alive with prayer and baling wire, and the engine answered with a sound like a dying animal that refuses to die out of sheer spite.

"Channel's moving," Pao called back. "Left side's gone. The bar we marked on the way up is twenty feet under."

"Then we read it new," Mirabel said. "Same as always."

*Same as always.* He said it and heard the lie in it. Nothing was the same as always. On the way up, the Serein had been a river with moods, and he had known them the way he knew Odile's silences and Bram's humming. Now it was a river with intentions, and its intention was to keep them.

The first strainer came at the third bend.

It was a cedar, whole, root ball the size of the pilothouse, wedged between two rocks with its branches combing the current like the fingers of a drowned giant. Water poured through it in a hundred silver threads, and anything that went into those threads would be held there, pinned and rolled, until the river grew bored of it. Mirabel had seen a heron die in a strainer once, held under for an hour, surfacing and submerging, surfacing and submerging, until it stopped.

"Hard to starboard," he said. "Pao—"

"I see it." The deckhand's voice came thin through the rain. "There's a tongue, captain. Clean water on the far side of it, but it's narrow. Maybe a boat's width and a half."

"Then we go through a boat's width and a half."

He brought the *Vesper*—no. He had never named her. The boat had never needed a name, because names were things you gave to things you meant to keep, and he had spent twelve years keeping nothing. He brought her around, felt the current take her stern and try to swing it, corrected with more throttle than he liked, and ran the gap with the cedar's branches hissing over the roof of the pilothouse, close enough that a twig snapped off and spun away downstream, and then they were through, and no one spoke, because speaking would have meant admitting what had just happened.

* * *

The whirlpools began at the narrows, where the gorge pinched down and the river, denied room to spread, went down instead. Mirabel had run these waters eleven times in high water and never seen them like this. The eddies behind the rock fins had grown into slow black rotations thirty feet across, deep as the gorge was tall, and they drifted—drifted, which whirlpools were not supposed to do—wandering across the channel like drunkards.

"Starboard," Pao called. "Starboard, starboard—"

Mirabel saw it a half-second after Pao did: a black eye opening in the brown water directly ahead, wide as the wheelhouse, spinning with a sound like a drain. He threw the wheel over, felt the engine labor, felt the stern slew, and the boat passed the rim of the thing so close that the water beside the hull dropped a full foot, the whirlpool pulling at them, wanting them, and then the current took them past and the eye closed behind them like something disappointed.

"Odile," he said. "How's she sitting?"

"Taking water. Not fast." She wiped her face and left a brown streak. "The seam we cracked at Devil's Throat. Bram's on it."

Bram's voice came up through the deck plates, muffled and profane. Mirabel let it go. The cook had been baling and patching for a day and a night, and the boat was alive because of him, and a man earned his swearing.

Ines came forward and stood beside him at the wheel, one hand on the brass rail, the other still pressed flat over the ledgers. Rain had gotten under her collar and she was shivering, but her eyes were on the water, and Mirabel saw—really saw, for the first time—that she was reading it. Not the way he read it, not yet, but reading it: the tongue of clean water, the V of a submerged rock, the difference between foam that meant shallow and foam that meant violence.

"See the seam?" he shouted over the rain, pointing. "Where the current changes color?"

She nodded. "The fast water runs over the slow water. You line up the seam and it carries you through."

"Who told you that?"

"You did." She looked at him. "Week two. You said you didn't, but you did. You talk to the river when you think no one's listening."

He had no answer for that, so he gave her the wheel.

She took it with both hands, and for a while—the length of one long bend, the length of a hundred heartbeats—he stood beside her and did not touch it, and the boat went where she sent it, and the river let them.

Then the bend opened into the worst water he had ever seen in thirty years on the Serein, and the river stopped letting anyone do anything.

* * *

It was a hole the size of a church, a standing wave gone wrong, the whole channel folding back on itself in a chaos of brown water that stood up and did not fall. There was no line through it. There was no around it. The gorge walls came together and the river came down through them and there was only through.

"Can we portage?" Odile shouted.

"Not with these walls. Not with this current." Mirabel was already calculating and hating every number. "We hit the seam on the left, ride the tongue in, and we keep her square. Square or we broach, and if we broach we roll, and if we roll—" He looked back at Ines, at the ledgers on her chest, at the aft cabin where Elias rode wrapped in sailcloth. "We don't roll."

He took the wheel back. Pao came aft off the bow, hand over hand along the safety line, and took his station at the stern lines. Odile braced at the engine throttle. Bram came up out of the hold black with bilge water and said nothing, just wedged himself against the rail and held on.

"Everyone says their piece with their gods," Mirabel said. "Then we go."

The bow entered the hole and the world ended.

Not gently, not gradually. The river picked the boat up like a toy and stood it on its stern, and Mirabel saw sky, only sky, rain hammering into his upturned face, and then the bow came down and punched through the trough and the whole hull twisted, groaning, and the port side dug in, and the river began to roll them.

"Starboard! All weight starboard!"

They threw themselves to starboard—Ines, Bram, Odile, all of them slamming against the rail—and the boat hung there, on her beam, the water lapping the gunwale, a hair's width from the point of no return, and then the current relented, or the engine caught, or God blinked, and she came down square and plunged on through the tail of the hole into the churning below.

Mirabel counted heads. Odile. Bram. Ines.

"Pao."

He turned. The stern lines were slack. The safety line hung snapped where it had been cleated, torn free by the twist, and the deck where Pao had been standing was empty, streaming with rain, and the river behind them was a mile of brown chaos with no man in it that Mirabel could see.

"Pao!" Ines was already moving, already at the rail. "There—off the port quarter—"

Mirabel saw him. A dark head, an arm, gone, up again, an arm waving once and gone. The current was taking him downstream faster than the boat, toward the right bank, toward a rock fin with an eddy behind it, and beyond the eddy, if the eddy spat him out, the long calm-looking reach that was not calm at all, the reach that fed the strainer fields two miles down.

"Odile, get me close. Ines—"

"I'm going."

"You're not—"

"Teach me or watch me." She was already at the rail, pulling a line off the stern cleat, tying a bowline around her own waist with hands that did not shake, using the knot he had made her tie fifty times in calm water while she complained that it was useless. "You said the eddy behind a fin holds what the river can't. You said you throw the rope upstream of the man, never at him. You said—" her voice cracked, and steadied. "You said the river gives back what you're willing to go in after. So I'm going in after."

Every argument he had died in his throat. There was no time for any of them anyway.

"Line around your waist, not your neck. Feet up, downstream, on your back. When you feel the eddy take you, let it—don't fight it, it'll hold you where the current can't." He was coiling the throw rope as he talked, his hands doing the old work while his heart tried to climb out of his chest. "And Ines—if the river decides it wants you—"

"It won't." She swung her leg over the rail. "It's had twelve years of you and couldn't keep you either."

She went in.

The brown water took her the way it took everything, completely, and for two seconds she was gone and Mirabel's whole life funneled down to a single point of nothing—then her head came up, feet first, on her back, exactly as taught, riding high, one arm up. Pao was thirty yards downstream and closing on the eddy fast, and the eddy was already turning, already reaching for him with its slow black fingers.

"Odile—inside of the fin, as close as you dare—"

The engine roared. The boat swung in behind the rock fin, into the eddy's slack water, and Mirabel stood on the rail with the throw rope in both hands and the rain in his eyes and threw.

The coil went out flat and true, upstream of Pao's drifting body, and the rope landed across the current and began to sweep down toward him, and Pao—half-drowned, blue-lipped, gone almost beyond seeing—got one hand up out of the water and caught it.

The line came tight. The river pulled. Mirabel leaned back against it with everything he had, boots skidding on the wet deck, and Bram was suddenly there beside him, huge hands over his, and Odile had the rope made fast to the mast cleat, and the three of them hauled, hand over hand, and the river gave up its prize the way it always finally did—grudgingly, an arm's length at a time.

Pao came over the gunwale coughing the Serein out of his lungs, and Ines came in right behind him, hauled in on her own line by Bram, and for a moment the four of them lay on the deck in a tangle of rope and rain and heaving breath, and nobody said anything, because there was nothing in any language for it.

Then Pao started to laugh. It was a terrible sound, wet and ragged, and it turned into coughing, and then it turned into laughing again, and then Ines was laughing too, and Bram, and even Odile at the wheel, and Mirabel felt it take him last, a shaking in his chest that was not entirely laughter and not entirely weeping and was, he understood at last, both.

"You threw it upstream," Pao gasped. "You actually—"

"Captain said never at the man," Ines said. She was on her knees, dripping, arms wrapped around the oilskin packet, and she was looking at Mirabel with an expression he had no name for. "You said the river gives back what you're willing to go in after."

"I said that?"

"You talk to the river," she said, "when you think no one's listening."

* * *

They ran the last twenty miles in a silence that was not empty. Pao, wrapped in every dry blanket the boat owned, refused the aft cabin and stayed in the pilothouse, shivering, watching the water with the rest of them, because he said—and his teeth chattered around the words—that he had seen the bottom of the Serein up close and preferred the view from above. Odile drove with a steadiness that had gone past skill into something like trance. Bram went below and came up with hot tea that no one remembered him making, and it scalded their hands and they drank it anyway.

The gorge began, slowly, to forgive them. The walls drew back. The brown fury of the current settled into a gray, grumbling power that could be read, could be argued with, could be survived. Twice more they passed strainers and went around them with room to spare, and each time Ines called the line before Mirabel did, and each time he let her.

Near dusk the rain thinned. Not stopping—monsoons did not stop, they only changed their minds about direction—but softening, and through the rag in the clouds a bar of gold light came down and lay across the water ahead of them, and the river ran through it like a road.

"Look," Odile said quietly.

Downstream, where the gorge walls finally fell away, the water spread wide and slow and silver, and the far shore was a green line under the last light, and there was no more white water between them and it that Mirabel could see. The Serein, having tried its best to keep them, was letting them go.

"Delta town by midnight," Odile said. "If the seam holds."

"The seam will hold." Mirabel put his hand over hers on the wheel, briefly, and took it back. "You've been driving her true all day."

He looked back once, at his crew—at the stray, the runaway, the cook who patched hulls and hearts with the same wire, at the prospector's daughter with the dead man's ledgers strapped to her chest and the river's lessons written into her hands now, permanent as ink. Twelve years he had measured his life in miles, because miles asked nothing of him. Somewhere in the last forty miles, without his permission, the measure had changed.

The boat ran on into the gold light, and ahead, at the delta town's dim waterfront, Mirabel saw a figure standing on the dock with a lantern raised, waiting for them—and recognized, even at that distance, the set of the shoulders.

Renn Voss had gotten there first.
