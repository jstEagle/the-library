## 35. Daylight

The gorge let go of us the way a fist opens—slowly, finger by finger, until there was nothing holding us but water.

I felt it before I saw it. For three days the river had been a living thing under the hull, all muscle and malice, shoving the *Serein Queen* sideways, lifting her stern like a dog shaking off its coat. Then, somewhere past the last bend—the one Mirabel had called the Devil's Elbow, though he'd said it with a laugh that had no humor in it—the current changed its mind. It stopped fighting. It began to carry.

I was at the wheel when it happened. Mirabel had put me there an hour before dawn, his hands over mine on the spokes, saying nothing, just steering through me as much as through the boat. The rain had thinned to a gray whisper. And then the walls of the gorge fell away on both sides at once, and the sky opened above us like a wound healing, and there was—there was *light*. Not the bruised half-light of storm, not lantern-glow or lightning's white violence, but ordinary, patient daylight, lying flat and silver on water so calm it looked poured rather than running.

"Easy now," Mirabel said behind me, though I don't think he meant it for my hands. I think he meant it for himself. "Let her drift."

I let her drift.

The lower Serein spread out before us, wide as a lake and twice as still, braided with channels of pale sand and fringed with willows bent low from the flood. Mist stood on the water in columns, slow-turning, lit from within by the strengthening sun. Somewhere far upstream, the monsoon was still chewing the headwaters to pieces—we could hear it if we listened, a long low thunder rolled along the stone—but down here the storm had spent itself into this: mist, and light, and a river gone gentle as if ashamed of what it had done.

Nobody spoke.

For a long time nobody spoke. Pao sat on the foredeck with his knees drawn up, soaked to the skin, staring at the mist as though he'd never seen weather before. Odile leaned in the engine-room hatch with a wrench still in her hand, oil to her elbows, listening to her engine turn over smooth and sweet after everything she'd asked of it. Bram stood at the rail near me, and I could hear him breathing—not the quick shallow breathing of the rapids but something deeper, longer, the breath of a man remembering how.

And Papa lay below, wrapped in canvas, because he had died two days ago at the top of the flood, and we had run forty miles of whitewater with him lashed in the cabin because there had been no other way and no time to grieve. I want to say I thought of him first, in that first silence. I didn't. I thought of nothing. My mind was a swept floor. Twelve years of anger, six weeks of river, one night of terror—and all of it washed out of me somewhere back in the gorge, left snagged on a strainer or swallowed by a whirlpool, and what remained was just this: a woman at a wheel, a boat beneath her, light on the water.

The silence held for maybe ten minutes. Long enough for the mist to shift. Long enough for a heron to lift off a sandbar ahead of us and flap away south, unhurried, unimpressed.

Then Pao started laughing.

It began as a hiccup—a small, startled sound, like something escaping him without permission. Then it came again, and again, and then it wasn't hiccups anymore, it was laughter, real laughter, wild and helpless, and he doubled over the foredeck rail laughing so hard his shoulders shook, and I understood that it wasn't joy exactly, or not only joy. It was the body's answer to fear. It was every scream he'd swallowed coming out the other side.

Bram went next. A chuckle at first, deep in his chest, then a full rolling laugh that made the rail hum, tears standing in his eyes while he laughed, which is how I knew what kind of crying it really was. Odile slid down the hatch coaming until she was sitting on the deck plates and laughed until she had to press her fist against her mouth. Even Mirabel—Mirabel, who measured out smiles like coins he couldn't spare—leaned against the pilot-house doorpost and let out a sound I had never heard from him, low and cracked and astonished, the laugh of a man who has just discovered he is still alive and cannot quite believe the evidence.

And then I was laughing too, and I couldn't have stopped if the gorge itself had reached up and demanded it. I laughed at the heron. I laughed at the mist. I laughed at Bram's hat, which had survived the Devil's Throat intact and sat on his head at a ridiculous angle, and at the fact that we smelled like bilge and woodsmoke and river mud and none of us cared, and at the sheer stupid miracle of being upright and floating and *here*.

We laughed until the laughing broke.

It broke in Pao first—he made a sound halfway between a laugh and a sob and pressed both hands over his face, and then he was weeping, great gulping sobs, the way boys cry who were taught young not to. Bram crossed the deck and put an arm around him and didn't say anything, because there was nothing to say, and Pao turned into him like a child turning into a doorframe. Odile cried quietly, wiping her eyes on her forearm, leaving dark streaks of grease across her cheek, and kept crying, and seemed surprised by each new wave of it, as though grief were arriving in installments and she hadn't known she'd subscribed.

I gripped the wheel and looked downstream and let it come.

It came for Papa first—that was right, that was owed. I wept for the man in the photograph I'd carried for twelve years, the tall laughing stranger who had left me and become a stranger worth resenting; I wept for the dying man I'd found instead, coughing dust in a portside flophouse, holding a map on oiled silk like it was a ticket home; I wept for the father I'd gotten to know only in the last miles of his life, and for every year of knowing him I'd traded away to anger. I wept for eleven miners whose names I had read aloud by lantern light while the river tried to kill us. I wept for a whistleblower named Hartwell who had died for telling the truth, and for the boy he'd raised who had spent twelve years hiding from a name that deserved better.

And underneath all of it, shamefully, unavoidably, I wept because I was alive. Because the water was calm and the sun was climbing and my hands still worked and my heart still beat, and survival, it turns out, is its own kind of grief—it means everyone who isn't there to see it, and everything that is over, and everything you can never give back.

* * *

Mirabel came to stand beside me at some point. I don't know how long I'd been crying. He didn't touch me. He never touched anyone unless they asked, which I had learned was its own kind of courtesy.

"The delta's a day and a half," he said. "Maybe less, with this current. There's a telegraph office. Newspapers." He paused. "Voss will be waiting, if he keeps his word."

"He'll keep it," I said. My voice came out scraped raw. "He let us go when he didn't have to. Men like that keep their word precisely because no one expects them to."

Mirabel nodded slowly, watching the mist burn off the far channel. "Your father would've wanted—" He stopped. Started again. "He wanted it told. All of it. You know that."

"I know." I wiped my face with the heel of my hand. "I'm going to tell it. Every page. Even the ones that cost him." I looked at Mirabel then, and he held my gaze, and there was something in his face I hadn't seen before—or had seen only once, at night, over a burning letter. Peace, maybe. Or the beginning of it. "Even the ones that cost you," I added.

His jaw worked. "Yes," he said finally. "Those too."

Below us, the engine turned over steady and true. Ahead, the river ran wide and silver toward a horizon that had no walls around it anywhere. I put my hands back on the wheel—not because the boat needed steering, but because I wanted to feel it, the whole living weight of the water passing under me, carrying us down.

Behind us the thunder went on, far away, fading. Ahead there was daylight, and a story that needed telling, and a name I was finally ready to say out loud.

"Ines," I said—to myself, to the river, to the man wrapped in canvas below. "My name is Ines. And I'm bringing you home."

The *Serein Queen* ran on toward the delta, and the light grew, and we went with it.
