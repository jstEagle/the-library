## 36. Testimony

The delta town was called Meridian's Rest, though no one resting had ever built it. It sat where the Serein gave up its argument with the land and spread itself thin across a hundred brown channels, a town of stilt houses and tin roofs and the permanent smell of river mud and drying fish. The monsoon had followed them down the gorge like a debt collector, and it arrived the same evening they did, settling in for a long stay—rain that fell not in drops but in sheets, in curtains, in whole moving walls of water that turned the streets to canals and made the town feel less like a place than like something the river was briefly allowing.

They tied up at the public wharf under a tarpaulin that Bram rigged with the tenderness of a man dressing a wound. The *Kestrel* looked terrible. Her port rail was scarred where the whirlpool had chewed her, her deck planking gaped in three places, and the seam Odile had patched below the waterline with canvas and tallow and prayer was weeping a slow, shameful trickle into the bilge. But she was floating. After the Devil's Throat and forty miles of flooded gorge and everything else, floating felt like a kind of glory.

Ines came out of the cabin carrying the ledgers.

She had strapped them to her chest through the worst of it, wrapped in oilcloth, bound with the same cord Pao used for his rigging knots, and now she carried them the way you carry an infant or a bomb—close, careful, both hands. The rain hammered the tarpaulin above her and ran off the edges in silver ropes. She stood at the top of the gangplank and looked at Mirabel, and he understood without a word being spoken that this was the last mile of Elias's journey, the one his legs would never make, and that she wanted him to walk it beside her.

"Odile knows the address," Ines said. "The journalist. Voss gave it to her before we cleared the gorge."

"Then we'd better hope Voss wasn't lying," Mirabel said.

"He wasn't." She said it simply, and he marveled again—at her, at all of it—at how a woman who had boarded his boat under a false name twelve years of resentment deep could now speak certainty about a man who had once hunted them both like animals. "He has no reason left to lie."

Mirabel took one end of the oilcloth bundle, and together they carried Elias's dead weight of paper down the gangplank into the rain.

* * *

The journalist worked out of a room above a chandlery that smelled of tar and wet rope, and her name was Sera Quill, and she was nothing like Mirabel had imagined. He had pictured someone hungry, someone who would grab the ledgers with both hands and start counting what they were worth. Instead Sera Quill was a small, gray woman with ink-stained fingers and reading glasses on a chain, who received the bundle on her worktable as if it were a sleeping thing that might wake angry, and did not touch it for a long moment.

"Eleven men," she said at last. "The Ferrow Deep collapse. I was twenty-three years old. My father hauled ore out of that mine." She looked up at Mirabel over the glasses, and her eyes were dry but very bright. "I have spent nineteen years being told there was nothing to find. Do you understand what you've brought me?"

"Evidence," Mirabel said. "And a confession. And—" he hesitated, "—a dead man's last request."

"Tell me."

So he told her. All of it, in the order it had happened rather than the order it made sense: the dying prospector and the impossible fare, the sealed crates, the crew of strays who voted against their captain, the Corvus Line shadowing them, the sabotage, the night portage, the claim that wasn't a gold strike at all but a grave with paperwork. He told her about the ledgers—the shipping manifests, the bribed inspectors' receipts, the letters between the mine company and men whose names still sat in government offices—and he told her about the final page, the one Elias had hidden even from his own daughter until the delirium took him: proof that the whistleblower, the man who had tried to warn the eleven miners, who had written letter after letter up the chain while the company sat on the reports, was a man named Aldous Wren.

Mirabel said the name out loud and found that the room did not collapse.

Aldous Wren. The man who had taken him in off the streets when he was fourteen and feral with grief, who had taught him knots and navigation and how to be quiet inside himself, who had been tried and convicted and destroyed for a crime the ledgers now proved he had died trying to prevent. Twelve years Mirabel had run from that name, had buried it so deep he'd nearly lost it, had built a new self out of river miles and rules about passengers and the comfortable fiction that a man with no past owed nothing to anyone. And now it sat on a journalist's worktable in a delta town in the rain, in Elias Ines's careful hand, and the world went on turning.

"You're Wren's boy," Sera Quill said quietly. It wasn't a question. Voss's affidavit, which had arrived by runner an hour behind them, had named him—not the captain of the *Kestrel*, not Mirabel Montrose, but the name from the old trial records, the boy who had testified badly and then vanished. "The papers called you the ward. They never printed your real name. I can print it now, if you'll allow it."

Mirabel stood at the rain-streaked window for a long time. Below, the flooded street carried a flat-bottomed skiff past the chandlery; a woman punted it with a pole, unhurried, as if drowning towns were ordinary weather. Somewhere a dog barked at the water. Twelve years ago he had run because staying meant watching them take Aldous Wren apart piece by piece and call it justice, and he had promised himself he would never be in a room again where powerful men decided what the truth was worth.

But that was exactly what this room was. The difference—the only difference, and it was everything—was that this time the truth was winning.

"Print it," Mirabel said. "All of it. My name too."

Sera Quill wrote it down in her careful hand, and when she read it back to him—his full name, first, middle, family, the whole abandoned architecture of himself—he expected to flinch. He didn't. It sounded, he thought, like a boat coming home through fog: something that had been out there the whole time, finally audible.

* * *

Voss came at dusk, when the rain eased to a steady gray whisper.

He came alone and unarmed, as he had come to them at the bend above the Devil's Throat, and he climbed the chandlery stairs slowly, favoring a knee the gorge had given him no time to complain about. Up close, without the Corvus Line's black hull behind him, Renn Voss looked older than Mirabel remembered and more tired—a man who had spent nineteen years pulling at the same knot and had just watched someone else cut it.

He signed the affidavit at Sera Quill's table, reading every line first, correcting two dates in the margin in a precise clerk's hand. When he finished, he blotted the signature, set down the pen, and looked at Mirabel across the lamplight.

"I hunted your guardian for two years," Voss said. "I believed the case. I built half of it myself—the timeline, the motive. I handed the prosecution their best exhibit." His jaw worked. "You should hate me. It would be tidy."

"I did. For a long time."

"And now?"

Mirabel considered it honestly, the way he'd consider a sandbar at dawn—reading the shape of it, testing the depth. "Now I think you were a good man given bad paper," he said. "Same as everyone on that river. The question is what you do when you find out."

Voss nodded slowly, as if Mirabel had passed some examination he hadn't known he was sitting. From inside his coat he drew a folded packet—oilcloth again, everything on this river wrapped in oilcloth—and laid it on the table. Inside were documents: deposition transcripts, correspondence he had collected over years and never been able to use, the skeleton of the case he'd once gotten wrong, now with flesh waiting on it.

"The names go high," Voss said. "Two of them are judges now. One sits on the commission that licenses river traffic—which means, Captain, that as of this morning, you and your boat are officially unlicensed, uninsured, and invisible." He almost smiled. "Consider it a professional courtesy. Invisible boats can go anywhere."

"And after?" Ines asked from the doorway, where she had been standing with her arms crossed, guarding the threshold the way she'd guarded the ledgers. "After the story runs. After the trials, if there are trials. What then?"

"Then I retire," Voss said. "Or I don't. That's my ledger to settle." He gathered his coat, paused at the door, and added, without turning around: "Your father would have made a fine investigator, Miss Ines. Stubborn as a river. Thank you for finishing his crossing."

After he was gone, Ines stood a long time looking at the door. Then she said, "I hated him for twelve years. My father, I mean. Before I knew any of it." She laughed, short and wet. "Turns out I was hating a man for hiding the truth, and the truth was that hiding it was killing him. I don't know what to do with that."

"You put it in the story," Mirabel said. "That's what we do with it. All of it. Even the parts that don't resolve."

* * *

They gave their statements through the evening and into the night, taking turns at Sera Quill's table while the rain kept its rhythm on the roof like a patient hand. Odile described the fuel line, the sabotage, the repair. Pao described the night portage, and when he got to the part where the boat slipped its cradle and nearly crushed him against the rocks, his voice shook and Odile put her hand over his on the table and left it there. Bram described the sealed crates and his shame at opening them, and then, unexpectedly, his lack of regret: "Some things you're supposed to look at. That's how you know they're true."

Ines gave the longest statement. She told the disaster and the cover-up and the twelve years of silence, and she told the last page, Aldous Wren's page, and she told it looking straight at Mirabel, daring him to look away. He didn't. When she finished, Sera Quill asked her if she understood that printing it would mean her father's name would be attached to the cover-up alongside the men who ordered it—that Elias Ines had taken company money for nineteen years of silence, and that the ledgers showed it.

"Yes," Ines said. "Print that too. He paid for it at the end. Let the record show the whole price."

Last of all, Sera Quill turned to Mirabel. "And you? You've given me the account of the voyage. But there's a piece missing. The trial. Your testimony—you were the key witness, and you recanted on the stand. Why?"

The room went very quiet. Rain on the roof. Lamplight. Four people who had run rapids together waiting on the answer to a question twelve years old.

"Because they told me if I held to my statement, Wren would hang instead of prison," Mirabel said. "And I was fifteen, and I believed them, and I recanted, and it didn't matter—they convicted him anyway, and my words were the rope they used. So I ran. I changed my name and I made a rule: no names, no pasts, no passengers. I thought if I carried nothing, nothing could be taken from me." He stopped. Looked at his own hands on the table, scarred and rope-burned and steady. "It took a dying man and a boatful of strays and eleven dead miners I never met to teach me that a man who carries nothing is just cargo. I'm done being cargo. Print everything. Print that I lied. Print that I was afraid. Print that I'm not anymore."

Sera Quill wrote it all down. When she finished, near midnight, she set aside her pen, removed her glasses, and rubbed her eyes, and for a moment she looked less like an instrument of justice than like a tired woman holding nineteen years of other people's grief.

"It runs in three days," she said. "Wire service, every major paper on the coast. There will be men in this town within the week wanting to talk to all of you—some with lawyers, some with worse. If I were you, I'd be elsewhere when it breaks."

"We're a refit away from seaworthy," Odile said. "Maybe four days."

"Then leave on day three," Sera Quill said, and smiled for the first time, small and dry. "Captains who testify usually end up famous. Famous boats attract passengers. Passengers attract trouble." She glanced around the lamplit room—at the engineer, the deckhand, the cook, the daughter, the captain with his new old name. "Though I suspect you lot would find trouble in a millpond."

* * *

They walked back to the wharf near dawn, when the rain had thinned to mist and the flooded streets had begun, grudgingly, to drain. The town smelled washed. Somewhere a rooster was making its case to a sky still too dark to argue with.

At the head of the gangplank, Ines stopped. "You know what I keep thinking?" she said. "My father spent his last weeks trying to get these ledgers downriver. One man, one map, one boat he could barely afford. And it turns out what he really needed was a crew." She looked at Mirabel sideways. "He needed people who'd vote against their captain."

"They're insubordinate," Mirabel agreed. "I should dock their pay."

"You don't pay them."

"No," Mirabel said. "I feed them. It's cheaper and it works better."

She laughed—the real laugh, the one he'd heard maybe five times since she'd come aboard under a false name, each one costing her something and giving back more. Then she went down the plank toward the cabin, where Elias lay wrapped in sailcloth on the bunk they'd agreed to keep until burial, and Mirabel stayed at the rail a while longer, watching the mist lift off the water.

Twelve years of miles. That was how he had measured it, ever since the trial—miles upriver, miles downriver, miles as a unit of forgetting. But standing there with his name freshly spoken aloud, signed to a statement, scheduled for print, he found the arithmetic had changed without his noticing. Somewhere between the sandbar and the Devil's Throat, the miles had started adding up to something. A crew. A daughter who wasn't his and was. A dead man's request, delivered. A name he could say in daylight.

Below, in the bilge, the patched seam was still weeping. Odile would fix it properly today, and tomorrow, and however many tomorrows the refit took, and then they would cast off—day three, before the story broke, before the lawyers and the worse-than-lawyers came asking questions. Downstream lay the coast, the open water, whatever came next.

Mirabel put his hand on the scarred rail, felt the *Kestrel* breathe under him, and realized he was smiling at the empty river like a fool.

He was still smiling when Pao's shout came down from the bow—half alarm, half delight—as a familiar black hull slid out of the morning mist upstream, flying no colors at all, and began, slowly, deliberately, to turn toward the wharf.
