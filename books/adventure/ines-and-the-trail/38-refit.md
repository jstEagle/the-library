## 38. Refit

The *Serein Queen* sat in the delta town's mud like a wounded animal that had crawled home to die, and Odile Vasseur stood on the bank at first light with a wrench in one hand and a cup of Bram's terrible coffee in the other, taking inventory of everything the river had taken from them.

The list was long. The starboard seam, sprung and patched twice in the gorge, would need to come apart entirely. Two of the four planks along the port gunwale had splintered where the whirlpool had tried to eat them. The rudder post was bent three degrees off true—she could feel it in her palms just looking at it, the way you can feel a wrong note hanging in a room. The wheel needed re-seating. The boiler tubes wanted descaling. The fuel tank had a dent shaped like a boulder. And somewhere below decks, in the bilge she hadn't yet worked up the courage to open, there was a smell that told her the river had gotten further inside her boat than any river should.

She drank the coffee anyway. It tasted like scorched tin and chicory and twelve years of mornings exactly like this one, and she found, to her own surprise, that she was smiling.

"You're grinning at damage," Pao said, coming up the bank with a coil of rope over his shoulder and sawdust in his hair. He was seventeen going on forty, all elbows and watchfulness, but since the gorge something in him had settled, like sediment after a flood. "That's new."

"I'm grinning because she's still floating," Odile said. "Everything else is negotiable."

Below the waterline, the *Queen*'s hull groaned against the mooring posts, and Odile put her palm flat against the nearest piling as if she could take the boat's pulse through it. She'd been an engineer's apprentice in a shipyard once, before—before was a country she didn't visit anymore, not even in her head, not since the gorge had taught her that the past could be survived if you just kept your hands busy enough. Her hands were very busy now. That was the whole trick of her life, and she'd never said it aloud to anyone, and this morning she thought maybe she wouldn't have to.

Mirabel came down the gangplank carrying lumber.

Not bought lumber—*found* lumber, salvaged off a wrecked barge half-sunk in the shallows upstream, and he was carrying it the way he carried everything now: openly, both arms, no attempt to hide how heavy it was. Twelve years of hunching gone out of his shoulders in a single season. It was strange, watching a man set down a weight so gradually you couldn't name the day it left him.

"Yellow pine," he said, dropping the boards at Odile's feet. "Salvage rights were disputed, so I paid the disputant."

"How much?"

"Enough." He wiped his hands on his trousers. "Bram's negotiating for pitch and canvas. Ines is at the chandlery getting us a proper patch kit instead of whatever disaster we've been calling one. I want the hull opened by noon."

Odile looked at the sky, then at the water, then at Mirabel. "You want to open my hull," she said, "the day after we ran forty miles of floodwater through a gorge that has a body count."

"I want to open *our* hull," Mirabel said. "And yes. Today. Because in two weeks the monsoon swells hit the delta too, and this yard floods to the second step of that warehouse, and I'd rather fix her dry than swim."

He was right, which was annoying. He'd been right a lot lately, and it hadn't stopped being strange—the man who'd spent twelve years being carefully, exhaustingly wrong about himself turning out to have excellent judgment about everything else.

"Open her by noon," Odile said. "But I do the seam work. You hold things and hand me things and pretend that's a job."

"It's the most important job," Mirabel said solemnly. "Holding things."

Pao laughed, and the sound of it went out over the water, and Odile thought: *there it is.* That was what they'd been building all this time without knowing it—not a crew, exactly. Crews got hired and fired. Something else. Something with a hull.

* * *

They opened the hull at half past eleven, and what the river had done to them made Odile swear in three languages, two of which she'd learned from Bram and one of which she'd invented herself on the Widow's Teeth.

The sprung seam wasn't a seam anymore. It was a wound—a hand's width of separated planking with the caulking torn out in ribbons, and wedged in the gap, absurdly, a piece of driftwood no bigger than her thumb, driven in by the pressure of the flood like a splinter in a giant's skin. She pulled it free and held it up.

"This," she announced to the deck at large, "is why we don't run rapids rated 'unrunnable.'"

"We made good time," Pao offered from inside the hull, where he'd wedged himself with a mallet and a bag of oakum, speaking in echoes.

"We made *loud* time. There's a difference." But she was smiling again, and she caught herself doing it, and decided to let it stand.

The work took them the rest of the day and most of the next. It was the kind of work that argues with itself—every plank a negotiation, every fitting a small war between what the boat wanted and what the plans said—and Odile discovered she'd missed arguing. Proper arguing, the kind where nobody meant it, where the anger was just another tool in the kit, passed hand to hand like the mallet.

"You're seating that plank crooked," she told Mirabel, who was indeed seating a plank crooked.

"I'm seating it *adaptively*."

"You're seating it the way a man seats a plank when he's thinking about something else."

He paused. Looked at the plank. Looked at her. "It's crooked, isn't it."

"It's a *eulogy* for straight."

He pulled it back out, laughing, and started over, and Odile held the clamp and thought about how six months ago this man had a rule—no names, no pasts, no passengers—and how the rule had died somewhere in the gorge between the Devil's Throat and daylight, and how nobody had held a funeral for it because nobody mourned it.

Bram came aboard mid-afternoon with pitch, canvas, a sack of oranges, and news, in descending order of importance.

"The chandler says the Corvus Line's been in town," he reported, handing around oranges like communion. "Voss's boat. They're laid up at the far dock refitting too. And—" he paused, savoring it, a cook who understood timing, "—he sent over a crate."

"A crate," Odile repeated.

"A crate of copper sheeting. Marine grade. Better than anything in this yard." Bram shrugged elaborately. "There was a note. It said, quote, *'For the seam. Consider it testimony.'*"

Nobody said anything for a moment. Down in the hull, Pao's mallet had gone quiet.

"He wants the story published," Mirabel said finally. "He said so on the water. I keep waiting for the part where it turns out to be a trap."

"Maybe it's not a trap," Pao called up. "Maybe some people are just—complicated."

"Maybe," Mirabel said, in the voice of a man who had spent twelve years betting on *maybe not*, and losing, and learning. "Use the copper anyway. It's good copper."

It was good copper. Odile fitted the first sheet over the worst of the wound that evening, and the metal went down bright and clean under her hands, and she thought: *a hunter paying for the repair of the thing he hunted.* There was a sermon in that somewhere. She'd let Bram find it; he was better with words before breakfast.

* * *

On the third day, with the hull closed and caulked and the pitch still tacky, smelling like a pine forest having a fever dream, Ines came down the bank carrying paint.

Not just paint—four tins of it, white and green and a blue so deep it looked like weather, and a bundle of brushes tied with string, and an expression Odile recognized immediately because she'd seen it on her own face in mirrors for twenty years: the expression of a person about to make a proposal they've already decided everyone will agree to.

"We're repainting the nameboard," Ines announced.

"The nameboard's fine," Odile said.

"The nameboard says *Serein Queen* in letters a blind man could read from shore, and half of them are river-scoured, and—" Ines set the tins down with a click. "Also I think we should add a line underneath."

"What line?"

Ines looked at Mirabel, who had appeared from below decks holding a rag and wearing the wary look of a man who suspected a vote was happening.

"*Eleven names,*" Ines said. "The miners. From the ledgers. Painted small, underneath the boat's name. So everywhere she goes, they go."

The yard went quiet. Somewhere across the water the Corvus Line's boat knocked gently against its dock, and a gull screamed its opinion, and Odile looked at the nameboard—at the scoured gold letters, at the boat beneath them, at the people standing around it, each of whom the river had tried to kill and failed—and felt her throat do something inconvenient.

"They were going to be forgotten," Ines said, quieter now. "That was the whole point of the cover-up. Eleven men go into a mine and eleven men stay in the ledgers and nobody says their names out loud ever again. Well." She picked up a brush. "I'm saying them out loud. Every port we pull into. For as long as this boat floats."

Mirabel was the first to move. He took a tin of white paint and opened it, and that was the vote, and everybody knew it, and nobody needed to say it.

They painted through the afternoon. Odile did the lettering herself because she had the steadiest hands—twelve years of machine work will give you that—and she carved each miner's name into fresh white ground while the others watched or worked or, in Bram's case, provided commentary that was fifty percent encouragement and fifty percent oranges. Pao read the names aloud from the ledger copy as she wrote them, slowly, giving each one its full weight, and by the fourth name his voice had gone rough, and by the ninth nobody was pretending to be doing anything else.

When it was done, the nameboard read:

**SEREIN QUEEN**
*and eleven who could not be here*

Odile stepped back and looked at it until her eyes stung, and blamed the pitch fumes, and knew perfectly well that the pitch fumes were innocent.

* * *

It was that evening, with the paint drying and the mosquitoes rising off the delta in silver columns, that the question finally came, and it came the way Odile had known it would—from Mirabel, at the rail, in the low light, with the directness of a man who had run out of patience with circling.

They were all there. That was the thing Odile noticed. Nobody had arranged it, and nobody had drifted away either. Bram was at the galley door shelling something. Pao sat cross-legged on the cargo hatch. Ines stood at the rail with the river going gold behind her. And Odile leaned against the wheel housing with her arms crossed, engineer's posture, listening posture.

"Ines," Mirabel said. "We cast off in four days. Downriver, then coastal, then wherever the work is. It's a working boat. The pay is bad, the food is Bram's, and the captain has a documented history of running from nothing and toward everything." He paused. "There's a bunk aft. It's yours if you want it."

The silence lasted three heartbeats. Odile counted them; she counted everything mechanical, and heartbeats were just engines with worse documentation.

Ines looked at the bunk space aft. Then at her father's ashes, which rode in a cedar box in the pilothouse, going home to the Widow's Teeth whenever Mirabel was ready. Then at each of them in turn, cataloguing, deciding, the way she'd learned to read water—looking not at the surface but at what moved underneath.

"My father booked passage on this boat to deliver the truth," she said. "He died before he could see it delivered. I could take the ledgers to the capital myself, on the mail packet, first class, done in a week." She turned back to face them. "But the truth isn't a parcel. It's a route. It needs someone reading the sandbar's mood. It needs—" she glanced at Pao, and something passed between them that Odile pretended not to see, "—it needs a crew."

"So that's a yes?" Pao asked.

"That's a yes," Ines said. "On conditions. One: I learn the engine. Odile, you're teaching me, starting tomorrow, and I warn you I ask questions."

"Questions are fuel," Odile said, and heard her own voice crack slightly on it, and pressed on. "Good ones are high-octane."

"Two: we stop at every town big enough to have a printer. The story goes out in pieces, port by port, until no one can bury it again."

"And three?" Mirabel asked.

"Three," Ines said, and smiled, and it was Elias's smile, Odile realized—she'd seen it in the old photograph, the man made of dust, young once, hopeful once. "Three is that nobody on this boat keeps a rule about names. We've all got pasts. Mine's in a cedar box. Yours is in a newspaper now. We carry them openly or we don't carry them at all."

Mirabel considered this with the gravity of a man signing articles. Then he put out his hand, and Ines took it, and that was that—that was the whole ceremony, a handshake on a deck at dusk, witnessed by a cook, a boy, an engineer, and a river.

"Welcome aboard," Mirabel said. "Crew of strays."

"Crew," Ines agreed. "Not strays anymore, though. Strays get found. We're—" she searched for it, and Odile, watching her, found she was holding her breath, "—we're a *company*. A small one. With a boat."

"A company," Bram echoed, tasting the word. "I've cooked for companies. They tip better than crews."

Laughter went around the deck, easy and unguarded, and Odile pushed off the wheel housing and went to check the pitch one more time, because somebody had to, and because her eyes were doing the inconvenient thing again and pitch fumes were as good an excuse as any.

From the rail behind her, Mirabel's voice, quiet, meant partly for her: "You voted for her, didn't you. Before I asked."

"Engineer's prerogative," Odile said. "I read the hull. She fits."

* * *

They cast off on the fourth morning, and Odile stood in the engine room feeling the *Queen* come alive under her hands—boiler pressure climbing sweet and even, the repaired seam silent as a secret, the new copper holding, the whole vessel running truer than she had in years, maybe truer than she ever had.

Through the engine room's voice-tube, faintly, she could hear the deck: Pao casting off lines, Bram singing something obscene disguised as a work song, Ines at the rail calling out the channel markers she'd learned to read, and Mirabel at the wheel, answering her, steady as a metronome.

Odile laid her palm against the bulkhead, over the place where the wound had been, and felt only smoothness. Whole wood. New pitch. A boat that had been broken open and closed again by the same hands.

Above her, muffled by deck planks, the eleven names rode downstream into the morning, going wherever the water went, and for the first time in longer than she could measure, Odile Vasseur was not running from anything at all.

Then the voice-tube crackled, and Pao's voice came down it, quick and bright with something that was not quite alarm and not quite laughter:

"Skipper says there's a passenger manifest for the next leg. Odile—you're going to want to hear who just booked passage at the dock."
