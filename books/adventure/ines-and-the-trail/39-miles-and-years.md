## 39. Miles and Years

The Widow's Teeth at dawn looked like something the river had tried to swallow and failed.

Mirabel brought the boat in slow, against the last of the night's current, and let her drift sideways into the eddy below the first fang of rock. The granite spires rose out of the water the way they always had—black, wet, indifferent—wearing their crowns of spray like old men wearing hats they'd earned. He had run these rapids more times than he could count. He had nearly died here twice. He had learned, on this stretch of water, that fear was just another current: you didn't beat it, you read it, you leaned into it, and you came out the other side changed in ways you couldn't name until years later.

He cut the engine. The silence that followed was enormous.

For a long while he simply stood at the wheel and listened to the gorge breathe—the hiss of water over stone, the groan of the boat's hull settling, the far-off complaint of a hawk somewhere up in the cliffs where the light hadn't reached yet. The monsoon had passed eleven days ago. The river was still high, still brown-green and muscular, but it had spent its fury, and what remained was the patient, permanent kind of power that would be here long after every name on every ledger was forgotten.

That was the thing about the Serein Gorge. It kept everything and forgave nothing, and it did both without malice.

He went aft to the cabin where he'd slept—or lain awake, which amounted to the same thing—and took the tin from under his bunk.

It was a tea tin, originally. Bram had repurposed it years ago for cardamom, then for spare fuses, then for nothing, and now it held what remained of Elias Ines: a fine gray ash, lighter than it had any right to be, as if the man had finally shed the dust he'd been made of all along. Mirabel had sealed the lid with wax from Odile's stores and wrapped the whole thing in oilcloth, because Elias deserved better than damp, and because Mirabel had discovered, over the past weeks, that he was incapable of doing anything halfway anymore. That was one of the changes. There were others. He was still cataloguing them.

He carried the tin forward with both hands, the way you'd carry something living.

* * *

They had argued about this, the night after the delta town, after the journalist, after Voss's signature dried on the affidavit and Mirabel said his own name aloud for the first time in twelve years and felt the syllables land on him like rain.

Ines had wanted to scatter her father at the claim. It was where he'd found the truth; it should be where he gave it back. But Pao pointed out—gently, in the way Pao had of making an argument sound like an observation—that Elias had hated the claim. Had spent thirty years hating it, carrying it in his chest like a coal. The claim was where the dead men were. The river was where Elias had been happiest, or as close to happy as a man that sick ever got. He'd stood at the rail every evening of the voyage upstream, coughing into his kerchief, watching the water go by, and once—only once—he'd said, *This is the only road I never regretted walking.*

So: the river. And not just any water. The Widow's Teeth, because Elias had heard them before he'd ever seen them, lying half-conscious in the cabin during the first run, and asked afterward, in a voice scraped down to gravel, *What was that roaring? Like the world clearing its throat?* And Ines had told him, and he'd smiled with his whole ruined face and said, *Good. I want to hear that again someday.*

He wouldn't hear it now. But the water would carry him past it, and Mirabel had come to believe that the river remembered what people couldn't.

Odile had offered to come along. So had Pao, and Bram, and Ines most of all—she'd stood at the gangway with her arms crossed and her jaw set, ready to fight for it, and Mirabel had seen how much it cost her to lose the argument.

"This one's yours," she'd said finally, uncrossing her arms. "You carried him longest."

"I only carried him forty miles."

"You carried him for twelve years before you ever met him." She'd touched his arm, brief and hard, the way she did everything. "You know what I mean. Go. We'll hold here at the pool below. Take your time." A pause, and then, softer than he'd ever heard her: "Take all the time you need, Captain."

Nobody had called him Captain with that particular weight before. He'd almost told her then—about the letter, about the name, about the man who raised him and what the ledgers proved—but there would be time. For the first time in his adult life, there was time. That was the strangest gift of the whole terrible voyage: not the vindication, not the compensation payments already moving through the courts, not even the ledgers themselves. Time. The discovery that a man could stop running and find that the ground held.

* * *

He climbed to the bow with the tin and sat down on the deck plates, cross-legged, the way Pao meditated on slow days, though Mirabel wasn't meditating. He was listening.

The Teeth roared ahead of him, white water braiding between the black spires, and beneath the roar was the deeper sound he'd been hearing since he was a boy on a different river entirely: the sound of the world going on whether you were ready or not.

"Elias," he said.

His voice sounded strange in the open air. He realized he hadn't spoken aloud in hours, maybe longer, and that the habit of silence ran so deep in him it had its own geology.

"I don't know if you can hear me. I don't know if it matters. Odile says it doesn't, Bram says it does, and Pao says the question is wrong." He turned the tin over in his hands. The wax seal gleamed dully in the first real sunlight. "I'll tell you what I know instead, and you can make of it what you like.

"Your daughter reads water now. She pulled Pao out of the Devil's Throat eddy with a throw line and a footwork turn I taught her in one afternoon, and she did it calm, in flood conditions, with the boat yawing, and when it was done she shook for ten minutes and then went back to the wheel. She's got my rule about names—she won't say yours casually, like it costs her something—but she tells stories about you at dinner. Good ones. The ones where you're funny. She's decided those are the true ones, and I think she's right.

"The ledgers are with the journalist. Eleven families will get what they're owed. The men who signed the orders are named in print, in court filings, in places that don't burn down. Voss testified for six hours and enjoyed himself, which frightens me more than I let on. Your name is clean, Elias. Not spotless—you weren't spotless, none of us are—but clean where it counts. You told the truth the hard way, at the end, and it held.

"And the other thing. The thing you hid on the last page."

Mirabel stopped. Below him, the eddy turned the boat in a slow circle, patient as a hand on a shoulder.

Twelve years he had built his life on a foundation of ash. His guardian—Aldous Wren, the man who'd fed him, taught him knots and currents and the difference between pride and dignity—had gone to prison for the mine disaster, had died inside three years of a fever the infirmary hadn't bothered to treat, and Mirabel had fled the trial, fled the territory, fled his own name, certain to his bones that the world had proven what it was: a place where good men were fuel and bad men were fire.

And the last page of Elias's ledger said otherwise. Said Aldous Wren had been the whistleblower. Had copied the shipping manifests, had smuggled out the safety reports, had written letters to three newspapers and two magistrates, and had been caught—not guilty, but dangerous—and buried under charges that stuck because no one else would speak. Every witness who might have cleared him had been paid, frightened, or dead. Except one, who'd been fourteen years old and believed what the courtroom told him.

Elias had known. Had tracked the boy down years later, found a riverboat captain calling himself Mirabel Montrose, and booked passage—not for gold, never for gold—for delivery. Of the map. Of the records. Of the truth, hand-carried upriver to the one person who'd earned it and the one person least able to bear it.

*You wanted me to see it myself,* Mirabel said silently. *You could have told me at the sandbar. You could have told me at the port, at the rapids, any of the hundred times I almost turned back. But you knew I wouldn't believe it secondhand. You knew I'd have to read it in Aldous's own handwriting.*

*I hate you for that,* he thought, and then, honestly, *and I'm grateful, and I don't think those cancel out. I think that's just what love weighs.*

"He didn't do it," Mirabel said aloud, to the ash, to the water, to the man who wasn't there. "Your ledgers cleared him too. He was trying to save them, all eleven, and they killed him for it, and I spent twelve years being ashamed of a man who deserved a statue." He laughed—a short, cracked sound with nothing much funny in it. "I've been measuring my life in miles because miles don't ask questions. Well. I'm done."

He stood. Unscrewed the lid.

The wind off the rapids came up the gorge and touched his face, cool and mineral, smelling of wet granite and distance.

"For Elias Ines," he said, loud enough to reach the Teeth. "Who walked the only road he never regretted. May it run downhill from here."

He tipped the tin out over the bow.

The ash went up in a gray plume, caught by the wind, thinned, spread—and then the river took it, as rivers take everything, without ceremony and without refusal. A faint ghost of it hung above the water for a moment, turning in the eddy's slow wheel, and then the current leaned in and drew it toward the rapids, toward the roar, toward the white throat of the Widow's Teeth where the world clears its throat, and it was gone.

Mirabel stood at the bow until his hands stopped shaking.

Then he screwed the empty lid back on, because Bram would want the tin for cardamom, and went aft to start the engine.

* * *

Below the pool, the crew waited on the deck of the moored skiff they'd towed this far—no, not waiting, he corrected himself as he came alongside. Working. Odile had the skiff's little outboard torn down to its gaskets on a tarp, lecturing Pao about fuel lines with the fervor of a woman who'd had one cut out from under her once and intended that history never repeat. Bram was cooking something on the skiff's single burner that smelled like cardamom and onions and forgiveness. And Ines stood at the stern, facing upstream, watching for him.

She saw the empty tin in his hand. She didn't ask. She just nodded, once, the way river people acknowledge a thing done properly, and then she said, "Bram made breakfast. It's either the best thing he's ever cooked or a war crime, and he won't tell us which."

"It's both," Bram called. "Sit down, Captain. You look like a man who's been talking to ghosts."

"I have."

"Did they talk back?"

"They listened." Mirabel tied off the line and stepped aboard, and the skiff rocked under his weight, familiar as a heartbeat. "That's more than I usually get."

They ate on the water, drifting, the gorge falling away behind them and the long green valley opening ahead, terraced fields steaming in the post-monsoon sun, a kingfisher stitching the surface ahead of the bow. Nobody talked much. It was the good kind of quiet, the kind Mirabel had spent twelve years mistaking for the only kind, and he ate his eggs and listened to Odile argue torque specifications and Pao hum something tuneless and happy and felt the last cold stone in his chest—twelve years of it, a lifetime of it—finally, finally come loose and go downstream.

Afterward, Ines came and stood beside him at the tiller.

"My father used to say a man ought to measure his life in something honest," she said. "Miles, years, debts paid. He said most people pick money, and money lies."

"He was right about that."

"He was right about very little else." She smiled, though, so Mirabel knew the wound had scabbed. "What will you measure in? Now?"

Mirabel considered the question the way he considered a rapid: reading it whole before committing.

"Miles and years," he said at last. "Both. Miles for the body, years for everything else. And—" he glanced back at the skiff, at Odile's grease-black hands and Pao's humming and Bram threatening the eggs with more cardamom, "—passengers. I'm done with the rule about passengers. I think we take them. The strays, the runners, the ones with no names worth keeping. Somebody took me once. Took all of us, one way or another. It's the only debt I know how to pay."

Ines looked at him for a long moment. Then she reached over and put her hand flat on the tiller beside his, not taking it, just sharing it, the way you share a wheel in rough water.

"Then we'll need a bigger boat," she said.

"We have a bigger boat. She's held together with prayer and Odile's spite, but she floats."

"She cracks a seam every third rapid."

"So do I. We refit each other." He eased the throttle forward and the skiff gathered way, sliding out of the eddy into the main current, and the valley opened before them mile after green mile, the river running smooth and deep and sure of itself. "Besides. Word travels on this river faster than water. By the time we're home, there'll be somebody on the sandbar waiting for us. There always is. There always was—I just used to pretend I couldn't see them."

"And now?"

Mirabel watched the current bend ahead, silver where the sun hit it, dark where it deepened, and read it the way his guardian had taught him, the way Elias's ledger had freed him to, the way he would teach it to whoever came next: not as an obstacle, not as an escape, but as a road.

"Now I wave," he said, and opened the throttle, and ran downstream toward morning.
