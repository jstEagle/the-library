## 40. The Trail Continues

*Pao*

The sandbar had moved again.

Pao saw it from the wheelhouse steps before the sun cleared the eastern ridge—a pale tongue of silt where last season there had been open water, curling downstream like a finger beckoning or warning, depending on how you read it. He stood with his tea cooling in his hands and let the river tell him what it wanted to tell him. The current ran lazy on the far side, braid-marked, the color of weak coffee. On the near side, where the new bar shouldered up out of the shallows, the water folded over itself in long slow wrinkles, the way cloth does when something solid moves beneath it.

A year ago he would have seen nothing but water. Now he saw the bar's mood, and the bar's mood was *shifting*, which meant the channel below Widow's Teeth was shifting too, which meant somebody ought to say so before they were hard aground with a boatload of pilgrims and mail sacks.

He found Mirabel at the rail amidships, watching the same water, which was no surprise. Mirabel always watched the water first and the world second.

"Bar's moved," Pao said.

"I know."

"You always know."

"That's the trick of it," Mirabel said, not looking away from the river. "You only get to know if you've been looking all along."

Pao grinned and drank his tea. Below them, on the foredeck, the morning passengers were arranging themselves the way new passengers always did—the nervous ones near the life rings, the romantic ones at the bow with their faces into the wind, the one young man in the patched coat who sat apart from everyone with his knees drawn up and his eyes moving constantly, cataloguing exits, counting strangers' hands for weapons. Pao knew that posture. He'd worn it himself, two years back, when he'd come aboard this boat with everything he owned in one bag and no intention of telling anyone why.

The young man's name was Teo. He'd paid his fare in small, worn notes and given a hometown that Pao was fairly sure didn't exist.

Pao didn't ask. That was the rule, and the rule had become the whole architecture of the boat: no names unless offered, no pasts unless volunteered, no questions about the road behind because everyone aboard was running from something and the running was nobody's business but their own. It wasn't secrecy, exactly. It was more like courtesy between wounded animals.

The whistle sounded. Odile, down in the engine room, letting them know she had steam. Ines appeared at the wheelhouse door, hair tied back, Elias's old brass compass hanging at her throat on a cord, and she looked at the water the way her father used to look at maps—with hunger, and with grief, and with love, all three braided together like the channels below the bar.

"Channel?" she called up to Pao.

"West of the new bar," Pao said. "But it'll be different by afternoon. It always is."

Ines smiled. "Then we'll read it again this afternoon."

She went inside and took the wheel, and the *Serein Queen*—they'd repainted the name that spring, gold leaf on fresh green, Bram insisting the old name deserved better than the faded letters it had been wearing—took the current, and the day began.

* * *

*Odile*

Odile felt the river through the deck plates before she heard anything worth hearing.

That was the thing people never understood about engines and water: they spoke the same language. A change in the current came up through the hull as a change in vibration, a subtle flattening of the rhythm under her palms when she braced them against the housing, and by the time the pilothouse noticed the eddy line or the boil, Odile had already known for half a mile that the river was thinking about something.

This morning the river was thinking about the bar. She could feel the boat's stern wander a degree or two off true, the propeller biting water that couldn't decide its own depth. She adjusted the throttle without being asked—she and Ines had developed a wordless conversation over the telegraph, two clicks meaning *I feel it*, three meaning *give me more*, silence meaning *we're fine, stop worrying*—and the *Queen* slid west of the shoal like a needle finding the groove of an old record.

One year. She thought about it while she wiped down the brass, which was the kind of work that freed the mind for wandering. One year since the gorge in flood, since the night run that should have killed them all, since she'd stood in this engine room with water over her boots and the fuel line patched with a strip of canvas and prayer, driving forty miles of whitewater at full steam because a dying man's daughter had strapped a bag of ledgers to her chest and refused to let the river have them.

They'd rebuilt the engine twice since then. Rebuilt most of the boat, honestly. But the bones were good bones, and Odile had come to believe that boats, like people, carried their history in their frames. This hull remembered the flood. You could see it in the new planking, paler than the old, a scar-line running the length of the starboard side where the rock had kissed them in Devil's Throat. Passengers asked about it sometimes. *What happened there?*

Bram liked to tell them it was a whale. Odile told them the truth, or the shape of the truth: *We were carrying something heavier than cargo, and we got it through.*

At noon they made the landing at Coriander Bend, the little market town that hadn't existed on any map worth trusting until the mine settlements brought money and attention upriver. Now there was a dock, a warehouse, a woman named Sefa who sold oranges and ice and gossip in roughly equal measure. New passengers came aboard here—a surveyor, two sisters going to visit an aunt, a government clerk with a briefcase chained to his wrist—and Teo, the quiet one in the patched coat, helped carry luggage without being asked, watching the gangplank the whole time like he might bolt.

Odile caught him at the rail afterward, handed him a rag, pointed at the smudge his boots had left on fresh paint.

"You live aboard now," she said. "You can clean."

He took the rag. His hands shook, just slightly, and he looked at her with the wariness of someone waiting for the trap to close.

"Why are you being kind to me?"

"Because someone was kind to me once, when I didn't deserve it and couldn't explain why I needed it." She shrugged. "It's how the boat works. You'll figure it out."

He looked down at the rag in his hands like it might be a test he could fail. Then he bent to the paint, and his shoulders came down half an inch from around his ears, and Odile went below to check her bearings and thought: *another one.*

They were never going to run out of strays. Thank God for that.

* * *

*Bram*

Bram fed thirty-one people that night and considered it a modest evening.

The dining saloon had been expanded that winter—one wall taken out, new tables built from the old cargo decking, so that the wood under the plates had already been down the gorge once and come back. He liked that. Food tasted better on wood with history. He'd served on liners where the tables gleamed and the guests chattered and nobody knew anybody's story, and he'd served in prison kitchens where the beans were thin and the stories were thick, and this—this crowded, lamp-lit, elbow-to-elbow room full of strangers becoming acquaintances becoming something else—was the best of every world he'd ever cooked in.

The clerk with the chained briefcase sat next to the sisters and by dessert was showing them photographs of his mother's garden. The surveyor argued happily with Ines about channel markers until Bram refilled both their glasses and the argument dissolved into laughter. And Teo, the stray, ate standing up at first, near the door, the way you eat when you're not sure the meal is really yours—until one of the sisters pulled a chair out and said, "Sit, you'll spill," and something in the boy's face cracked open like river ice in spring.

Bram watched from the galley doorway, towel over his shoulder, and felt the old ache that was not quite pain and not quite joy.

He'd peeked in those crates once. Sealed crates, a dying man's crates, and curiosity had won, and what he'd found had nearly torn the boat apart—not the contents themselves but the suspicion, the fraying trust, the terrible arithmetic of wondering who among them might be bought. He'd spent a long time after the flood making amends that nobody had asked for. Cooking harder. Listening better. Learning that loyalty wasn't a thing you declared; it was a thing you did with your hands, daily, whether or not anyone was counting.

Now the ledgers were gone—given away, published, read aloud in courtrooms by men with clean voices—and the names in them had become sentences and settlements and a memorial stone at the minehead with eleven names cut deep enough to outlast weather. And the boat carried mail and pilgrims and families and the occasional fugitive, and every sealed crate that came aboard now got opened and inspected, because Bram insisted, and Mirabel had laughed and agreed, and that was its own kind of penance and its own kind of peace.

After dinner, Teo appeared in the galley with his dish, washed it without being told, and lingered.

"The captain," the boy said carefully. "People say he used to be someone else. Before."

"People say a lot of things."

"Is it true?"

Bram dried his hands slowly. Outside the galley window, the river ran silver under the rising moon, and somewhere aft, Mirabel's voice drifted across the deck, low and patient, teaching.

"It's true he used to be someone else," Bram said. "But then, so did I. So did you, probably. The question isn't who you were." He nodded toward the window, toward the voice. "It's who you're becoming, and who's helping you get there. Go on. He's waiting for you."

Teo went. Bram turned back to his pots, and hummed.

* * *

*Ines*

Her father had taught her to read maps. Mirabel had taught her to read water. Between the two educations, she had learned that they were the same education—that a map was only a river's memory written down, and the river itself was a map forever revising itself, and anyone who trusted either one completely deserved the sandbar they found.

She stood the evening watch alone, as she did most evenings now. The wheel spoke to her palms in small tremors; the compass swung its slow arc; the banks unspooled past the windows, darkening from green to black, lit here and there by farm lanterns and the occasional fishing skiff whose occupants raised a hand as the *Queen* passed, because they knew her now, the way you know a neighbor's dog or a church bell. A year of runs up and down this reach. A year of landings and departures, of learning every bend's temper in high water and low.

She missed him every day, and the missing had changed shape. At first it had been a wound—Elias dying at dawn on the third day of the flood run, his hand in hers, his last words split between her and the man at the wheel: *Tell it all,* for her. *Stop measuring in miles,* for Mirabel. For weeks afterward she had expected to hear the cough, had turned at every deck-board creak. Now the missing was quieter, more like ballast. He rode low in the hull of her days and kept her steady.

The ledgers had done their work. Eleven families compensated; four men convicted; two dead before trial, which she tried not to think of as an escape. Her father's name had been dragged through the mud of the hearings—*the prospector who sat on the truth for twelve years*—and then, when the final page surfaced, the page he'd hidden even from himself, dragged back out again: *the man who gathered the evidence at all.* History couldn't decide what to call him. Ines had decided. She called him Papa, and she told it all, exactly as asked, and some nights telling it was the only way she slept.

Footsteps on the stair. Mirabel, coat collar up against the river damp, a thermos of Bram's chicory in each hand. He handed her one without asking, leaned against the wheelhouse frame, and looked out at the water with the expression she'd come to think of as his listening face.

"Bar'll be different tomorrow," he said.

"West channel narrows after the rain upstream."

"Mm." He glanced sideways at her, and there was the other thing in his face, the thing that had arrived sometime over the winter and stayed: pride, undisguised, unashamed. "You could take her down alone, you know. Any reach. Any season."

"I know."

"Soon you'll want your own boat."

"I have a boat."

"A boat you crew. Not a boat you command." He said it gently, testing the weight of it, the way he tested everything—currents, silences, other people's readiness.

Ines watched the dark water slide by and thought about the question underneath the question. "Not yet," she said. "There's still things I need to learn. And—" she hesitated, then said it plainly, because plainness was the house style aboard now, "—and I don't want to leave yet. This is the first place I've ever stayed on purpose."

Mirabel nodded slowly, and drank his chicory, and said nothing, which from him was agreement, blessing, and thanks all at once.

Below and aft, lamplit on the afterdeck, he'd left a lesson unfinished. She could hear Teo's voice, hesitant, asking a question. She could hear the pause before the answer—the pause that was itself the teaching.

"Go on," she said. "Your student's waiting."

"He's not my student. He's the river's. I just translate."

"Then go translate, Captain."

He went, and Ines held the wheel alone in the warm dark, and the river ran on, revising its map, keeping its moods, keeping, too, whatever it keeps—the cargo, the years, the dead, the names—and giving back, if you learned to ask correctly, exactly what you needed.

* * *

*Mirabel*

The boy sat on the afterdeck hatch with his knees drawn up, and when Mirabel settled beside him he didn't flinch, which was progress. Two weeks ago he'd have been over the rail.

"Show me again," Teo said. "The sandbar. How you knew."

So Mirabel looked at the water, moon-silvered and slow, and began the way he always began—not with the answer but with the looking.

"Don't watch the water," he said. "Watch what the water does. See the wrinkles above the bar? Current folding over something shallow. See how the driftwood lines up along that seam? Debris marks the border between fast water and slow, and the border is where the channel lives. The river writes everything down. Most people just don't read."

"And the mood? Pao said you read the bar's mood. Bars don't have moods."

"Everything has a mood." Mirabel let the silence sit a moment, because silence was part of the lesson. "A river after rain speaks differently than a river in drought. A bar that's building talks in one voice; a bar that's washing out talks in another. You learn the voices by listening for years, and then one morning you realize you can't remember not knowing them." He turned to look at the boy directly. "Same with people. Same with yourself."

Teo was quiet for a long time. The paddlewheel turned its steady revolution behind them, patient as a heart.

"I did something," the boy said finally, to the water, not to Mirabel. "Before. Something I can't undo. That's why I gave a false town. Why I paid in small bills. Why I—"

"Stop." Mirabel said it softly. "You don't owe me the story. Nobody aboard will ever ask you for it. That's the rule."

"But you—you had a rule like that once, and you broke it. Everyone says so. The captain who hid for twelve years and then gave his real name to a journalist on the delta docks." Teo's voice wavered between accusation and awe. "If hiding doesn't work, why do it? If telling works, why did you wait?"

Mirabel looked downriver, toward the distant dark where the gorge waited, forty miles of whitewater that had taken a boat's seams and a man's life and had given back, somehow, everything that mattered. He measured the answer not in miles but in years, which was the whole point, which was the lesson he'd promised a dying friend he'd learn.

"Because I thought the past was a cargo," he said. "Something you could seal in crates and stack in the hold and never open, and the boat would float anyway. It floats for a while. Then the river rises." He shrugged. "I waited because I was afraid. I told because I finally had people worth being afraid for. When you find yours—and you will, probably on this boat, probably sooner than you want—you'll understand the difference between hiding and peace. One looks like the other from a distance. Up close, only one of them lets you sleep."

Teo absorbed this with the gravity of the very young and the very tired. Then: "Will you teach me the rapids? Someday? The Teeth?"

"Someday." Mirabel stood, joints announcing themselves the way they did now, another year's honest mileage. "First you learn the sandbar. Then the eddy. Then the seam. The river teaches in order, and it doesn't skip."

He left the boy at the rail, chin lifted, reading the wrinkles over the bar, getting it wrong and getting it wrong and—there—getting it right, a small correction of the eyes that Mirabel felt in his chest like a struck bell.

At the wheelhouse, Ines had the watch. Through the glass he watched her stand easy at the wheel, Elias's compass glinting at her throat, and he thought: *twelve years I measured in miles, and it took one season to learn to measure in years, and it took one girl at a wheel to teach me that the two were always the same measurement.* Miles and years. Distance and time. The trail and the life. You didn't choose between them. You ran them together, at speed, through the gorge, holding what mattered strapped to your chest.

Tomorrow they'd make the landing at Millbrook, where a letter waited—he could feel it, the way you feel weather—from the historical society wanting to mark the route, wanting a ceremony, wanting *him*, the famous captain of the famous run, to say words over a plaque. He'd go. He'd say few of them. And then he'd come back to the boat, because the boat was the true monument, and the tradition was the true plaque, and the tradition looked like this: a stray at the rail learning the water, a daughter at the wheel learning the years, a crew of strays-turned-family keeping the engines and the pots and the watch, and the river ahead, revising its map, keeping its moods, calling them on.

Ines opened the wheelhouse door. "Landing at first light?"

"First light," Mirabel said. "And Ines—wake me when the bar comes into view. I want to hear what our new reader says it's doing."

She smiled, and the wheel answered her hands, and the *Serein Queen* ran on into the dark with her lamps burning and her hold full of ordinary cargo and her decks full of extraordinary company, down the long bright trail of the river, which continues.
