## 1. Miles, Not Years

The tide was going out and so, in a manner of speaking, was I.

I'd been measuring my life in miles for twenty years now — miles of water under the hull, miles of cable paid out over the side, miles between me and whatever town had last asked me questions I didn't want to answer. Miles are honest. Years lie. A year can pass while you stand still, and standing still is how men like me get found.

The *Magpie* sat at anchor two miles off a stretch of coast that didn't have a name on any chart worth owning. Gray water, gray sky, gray rock inland with a fringe of black spruce like a beard nobody trimmed. It was October, which meant the water was cold enough to kill you in twenty minutes and clear enough to show you everything you'd find down there before it did. Good working conditions, if your idea of work was what mine was.

I checked my gauges twice, spat into my mask the way you do, and went over the side without ceremony. Ceremony is for people who expect someone to notice.

* * *

The wreck lay in forty feet of green-gray nothing, which is shallow by wreck standards and deep enough by every other kind. She'd been sitting on the sand for longer than me — decades, maybe more — her hull furred with kelp and mussel beds, her superstructure collapsed inward like a rib cage after a bad argument. No name on the stern that I could read through the growth. No registration numbers. Just steel and silence and the slow business of the sea taking back what it had been lent.

I didn't ask who she was. That's rule one, and rule one has kept me alive and unindicted for two decades: *never ask who owns what you pull up.* The men who hire me don't want curiosity. They want results, delivered quiet, no receipts. In exchange they pay cash and never learn my face. It's not a friendship. It's better than a friendship. Friendships come with obligations, and obligations come with people standing at your funeral wondering why you never told them where you lived.

There was brass down there. I could see it from ten feet out — fittings, valves, a ship's bell half-swallowed by anemones. Brass doesn't rot. Brass waits. There was something else too, deeper in the hold, shapes under tarps that had gone stiff as bark, but I wasn't there for mystery. I was there for metal, and metal I took.

An hour of work. Knife, pry bar, lift bag. The bell came free with a groan that traveled through the water like a rumor through a small town, and I felt it in my teeth. Somewhere below me, something shifted in the hold — sand settling, probably, or a bulkhead finally giving up its argument with gravity. Probably.

I've learned to live comfortably around *probably*. It's the only neighbor I keep.

* * *

Surfacing is the part of the job I like least. You spend an hour in a world where nobody talks, where your own breathing is the loudest thing for miles, and then you break the surface and remember that the world up here is full of eyes. Most of them are harmless. Most.

I came up beside the *Magpie*, hauled myself onto the transom step, and started pulling gear, and that's when I saw her.

A tender. Navy gray, low-slung, sitting beyond the buoy line like a cat outside a mouse hole — close enough to watch, far enough to claim it wasn't watching. No flag flying. No wake. Just a dark shape holding station in the long afternoon light, and a glint off glass that might have been binoculars or might have been nothing.

I stood very still, dripping on my own deck, and did the arithmetic. Two miles. My boat does eight knots loaded. Theirs does thirty without trying. If the math ever became relevant, I would lose it, and losing math contests at sea tends to end with drowning or paperwork, whichever comes first.

So I did what I always do when the world looks at me: I made myself boring. I stowed my gear with the unhurried competence of a man with absolutely nothing to hide because he has carefully arranged his life to contain nothing at all. I coiled line. I wiped down the regulator. I even, God help me, whistled a little — three notes of something tuneless, the way a man whistles when his conscience is as empty as a summer harbor.

The tender held position for another ten minutes. Then it turned, slow and deliberate, the way a man turns away from a window he's decided isn't interesting, and moved off north along the coast until the haze took it.

I sat down on my cooler with a beer that had gone warm in the sun and thought about it, the way I think about most things — briefly, and sideways, and never head-on.

Could be coincidence. Tenders patrol. Coastlines are long and navy boats have to be somewhere. Could be they were looking for smugglers, or poachers, or a lost drone, or any of the hundred things governments lose off their own shores in any given month. Could be.

But tenders don't drift. And that one hadn't been drifting. It had been *stationed* — holding a point in the water relative to a fixed reference, and the only fixed reference within five miles was me.

I finished the beer and let the thought sink like a stone I didn't have to pick up. Rule two, right behind rule one: *a problem you can't solve today is a problem you can drink past tonight.*

* * *

That night I anchored in a cove whose name I never learned and cooked rice and tinned sausage on a single burner, eating out of the pot because plates are just dishes waiting to be washed and washing dishes is a thing people with addresses do. The cove was deep and dark and smelled of cold salt and pine rot. A loon called somewhere across the water, that sound like laughter played backwards, and I thought, not for the first time, that this was freedom.

This is what I mean about years lying. Because if you'd asked me — and nobody ever asked me anything, which was rather the point — I'd have told you I was free. Free as a man can be. No lease, no ledger, no last name that anyone could trace to anything. Twenty years since I'd signed anything but a receipt for salvage fees, and those in names that weren't mine. I owned a boat, a set of tools, a duffel bag, and a diving record that officially belonged to a dead man, which is a long story involving a contract dive I was paid to forget, and I had forgotten it, mostly, except at three in the morning when the wind came around to the north.

Freedom. Sure. I had all of it. I could go anywhere, be no one, owe nothing.

It occurred to me, sitting there in the dark with the loon laughing its backwards laugh, that "anywhere" and "nowhere" are the same place if you stay in motion long enough. That a man who measures his life in miles is a man who's decided he'll never arrive anywhere, because arriving means being seen, and being seen means being known, and being known — well. Being known is how you end up owing somebody. And owing somebody is how you end up on a manifest, and manifests, in my experience, are lists of people other people have decided not to worry about anymore.

I didn't know that yet, not really. I knew it the way you know a word in a language you don't speak — the shape of it, the weight, none of the meaning. The meaning would come later, forty feet down, in a hold full of sealed paper and a boy who should have frozen to death and didn't. But that night all I knew was the warm beer, the cold stars, and the particular silence of a coastline with nobody on it.

It was a good silence. I told myself it was a good silence. I'd been telling myself that for twenty years, and I'd gotten so fluent at it that I almost believed it, the way you almost believe a compass needle when it points somewhere convenient.

Almost.

* * *

Morning came up gray and flat, the sea like poured pewter, and I weighed anchor and ran south along the shore toward the rendezvous point where the fixer's man would meet me for the handoff. The fixer — I knew him as Marsh, though I'd have bet money that wasn't his name either; we were a matched set, him and me, two men wearing borrowed labels — had a new job teed up. Quiet one. Off the books even by our standards, which was saying something, because our standards were already subterranean.

"Recovery job," he'd said, three weeks earlier, across a table in a diner where the coffee tasted like it had been brewed during a previous administration. "Government survey team worked a site up the coast. Packed some expensive equipment in. Then they walked off the job." He'd smiled with exactly half his mouth. "Client wants the equipment back. Doesn't much care about explanations."

"And the team?" I'd asked, because even I break my own rules occasionally, just to feel the edge.

"What team?"

Right. What team. That was the whole conversation, and I'd taken the job anyway, because the number was good and the season was ending and winter money doesn't grow on trees. It grows in wrecks, if you're willing to go down and get it and not ask what else is down there getting it with you.

So I ran south with the morning fog burning off the water in rags, and the *Magpie* pushed her blunt little bow through the swells, and I felt — I want to be honest here, since honesty costs me nothing on paper — I felt fine. Better than fine. The engine drumming under my boots, the coast sliding past green and empty, the whole day unwritten ahead of me. This was the life. Nobody's boss, nobody's friend, nobody's problem. Twenty years of miles, and every one of them mine.

The rendezvous was a notch in the cliffs where the water went flat and green and deep. Marsh's man was late, which was unlike him, and when he finally came putt-putting out of the fog in a skiff, he was alone and moving fast and not looking at me, which was also unlike him.

"Galloway." He said the name like he was testing whether it fit. It didn't; it wasn't mine. "Change of plans."

"I don't do changes of plans."

"You'll do this one." He handed over an envelope, thick, and a folded sheet of paper. "New site. Same client. Survey team left equipment at this location too." He paused, and for the first time in six years of doing business, the man looked at me like I was a person instead of a vending machine. "Bram. The camp's still set up. Boots by the fire. Coffee in the pot. Nobody's touched it in nine days."

I looked at the coordinates on the paper. I looked at the envelope. I looked at the fog, which was coming back in off the water like it had forgotten something.

"Coffee goes stale," I said.

"Not that fast," he said, and turned his skiff around, and left me alone with the envelope and the coordinates and the growing understanding that I had just been hired to dive a wreck that the government had walked away from — walked away from boots, coffee, radios, everything — and that somewhere north of me, a navy tender that didn't drift was holding station over water that had no reason to interest anybody.

I should have burned the paper. Twenty years of rules said burn the paper, take the envelope, run south, measure another thousand miles.

Instead I opened the throttle and turned north, telling myself it was about the money, which was true, and that money was the only thing I feared losing, which was the biggest lie I'd told myself yet — and I'd told myself some beauties.

The *Magpie* ran north into the fog, and the fog closed behind us like a door.
