## 2. The Survey Team That Wasn't

The fixer's name was Marisol Quintero, and she paid in cash the way other people paid compliments—quickly, and without meaning anything by it.

We met at the Rusty Anchor, which was neither rusty nor an anchor, but a diner two towns up the coast where the coffee tasted like it had been filtered through a work glove and nobody asked questions because asking questions would have required effort. She took the booth facing the door. I took the one facing her. Twenty years of salvage work teaches you a few things, and one of them is that you always let the money sit with its back to the wall.

"Recovery job," she said, sliding an envelope across the formica like she was dealing cards to herself. "Government survey team was working a site up past Cape Merrow. They walked off the job."

"People do that."

"These people left their equipment."

"That's a lot of equipment."

"It's a lot of equipment." She didn't smile. Marisol never smiled during business; smiling was for after, when the check cleared and nothing had gone sideways. "Sonar arrays, sediment corers, a couple of hard cases of instruments I couldn't pronounce if you held my family hostage. Client wants it recovered before the weather turns. Quietly."

"Who's the client?"

"Bram." She said my name the way a teacher says a student's who's about to fail a spelling test. "You know better than that."

She was right. I did know better than that. Rule one, learned in my first year on the water when I was young enough to think rules were suggestions: never ask who owns what you pull up. Ownership is a story people tell after the fact. The water doesn't care whose name is on the manifest. It just gives things back when it's good and ready, and whoever's holding the net when that happens is, for legal purposes, a fisherman.

"How much?"

She told me. It was enough to make the winter comfortable and then some—enough that I'd stop doing the math on whether the *Maren* needed a new impeller or just a new life. Enough that I should have been suspicious, because in my experience the jobs that pay too well are the ones where somebody's buying your silence along with your labor, and silence is the only thing I've ever had in surplus.

"Half now," she said. "Half on delivery. Site coordinates are in the envelope. You've got nine days before the client considers the equipment abandoned and writes it off."

"And if I find the survey team?"

Marisol looked at me for a long moment. Outside the window, a gull was harassing a dumpster with the persistence of a debt collector. Inside, the fry cook scraped the grill with a sound like fingernails on the underside of the world.

"You won't," she said. And then, quieter, in a voice I'd never heard her use before—a voice with actual weather in it—"And if you do, Bram, don't talk to them. Just get the gear and go."

I want to be clear about what I did with that sentence, because I've replayed it enough times since. I filed it under *clients being dramatic*. Government contractors love their drama; it justifies the invoices. A team walks off a job, the client gets twitchy, everybody starts talking in code at diners. I'd heard versions of this before. Once it was a pipeline inspection crew that turned out to have quit over unpaid overtime. Once it was a marine archaeologist who'd faked his own disappearance to escape a marriage. People leave. It's the one thing people are reliably good at.

So I took the envelope, shook her hand, and drove home to load the boat.

* * *

Cape Merrow sits forty miles north of my moorage, a fist of black basalt sticking out into the Pacific like it's daring the ocean to try something. The road up there dies about six miles short of the cape itself, which means the last stretch is a game trail that used to be a logging spur, which means everything going in or out goes on someone's back or by someone's boat. Good spot for a survey camp. Bad spot for leaving in a hurry.

I anchored the *Maren* in the lee below the cape and went ashore at first light with a pack frame, a pry bar, and the particular mood of a man expecting to haul several hundred pounds of electronics down a goat path for money. The morning was the kind of gray that isn't weather so much as a decision the sky keeps deferring. Fog sat in the trees like it was paying rent.

The camp was three hundred yards up from the landing, tucked into a shelf of flat ground between two cedars. I smelled the woodsmoke before I saw it—that sweet, settled smell of a fire that's been burning down for hours—and I remember thinking, *huh, they must have come back for something,* because the alternative hadn't occurred to me yet.

Then I came around the cedar and saw it, and I stopped walking, and I stood there for what I'd later estimate was a full minute doing absolutely nothing, which for me is roughly equivalent to screaming.

The camp was intact.

Not ransacked-intact. Not abandoned-in-a-hurry intact. *Intact* intact. Two dome tents staked properly, guylines tensioned, rainflies on. A folding table with a laptop under a waterproof cover, weighted down against wind. A drying line with socks on it. Socks. Somebody's socks, hung out to dry, still hanging, patient as laundry gets.

And the fire. The fire was the part that got me. There was a pot on the grate—a dented aluminum thing, the kind every field crew on earth owns at least one of—and when I lifted the lid, there was coffee in it. Half full. Cold, obviously, days cold, a skin of mold starting its slow land claim on the surface. But half a pot. Nobody makes half a pot of coffee. You make a whole pot, you drink what you drink, and if you're leaving, you dump the rest on the fire. That's not even etiquette. That's physics. Standing liquid is a thing you deal with.

Unless you didn't plan on leaving.

I set the lid down and made myself walk the site the way I'd walk a wreck. Systematic. Left to right, top to bottom. It's funny—the same discipline that serves you fifty feet down works fine at sea level, except underwater there's usually a body to explain things, and bodies are honest. Bodies tell you what happened. Camps just stand there being wrong at you.

Boots by the fire. Two pairs, plus a pair of rubber deck boots by the tent flap. Four sets of footwear for—I counted sleeping pads—four people. Four pads, four bags, four mess kits, four of everything, all of it present, none of it packed. A paperback novel face-down on a crate, spine cracked, bookmarked with a pencil. A radio on the table, antenna up, powered off. Not dead—off. Switched off, the way you switch something off when you're stepping away for a minute, not the way you strip it for parts or smash it so it can't be tracked.

I checked the ground. This is the part I keep coming back to, the part I've tried to price a dozen ways since and can't. The trail in from the landing was soft from three days of drizzle. My own boots were cutting six-inch prints in it. The camp's approach path showed traffic—heavy traffic, boot prints overlapping, some of them weeks old by the weathering, some fresher. Going in.

Going in.

I walked the perimeter twice, then widened it, quartering the brush like I was looking for a dropped wrench. Nothing. No trail leading out. Not a faint one, not an old one, not one heading toward the logging spur or down to a second landing or anywhere. Four people had walked into that camp carrying enough gear to live for a month, and as far as the ground was concerned, they had then evaporated.

There are explanations. I ran through them that morning, standing in the fog with my hands in my pockets, and I'll run through them again now, because listing them is how I keep the hair on my arms lying down. Helicopter extraction—possible, except the canopy's too tight for a helo within a quarter mile, and I found no rotor-wash sign, no trampled clearing, no skid marks. Boat pickup from a different cove—possible, except why hike everyone out overland and leave four grand in boots behind? They walked out light and fast and someone collected them elsewhere—possible, except the terrain funnels. There are exactly two ways off that shelf, and I walked both, and both were empty of tracks.

Or—and here's the explanation I didn't put into words until I was back on the boat, because some thoughts need salt air between them and you—or nobody left at all.

I got to work. That's the thing about me that I've always counted as a virtue and am beginning to suspect is something closer to a symptom: whatever's happening, I work. Fear, grief, confusion—it all goes in the hold and I keep hauling. So I hauled. Sonar array, broken down and crated. Corer, in two pieces. The instrument cases, which weighed like they were full of lead shot and secrets. I worked six hours straight, running loads down to the dinghy, and the whole time the camp watched me with that terrible domestic patience. The socks dried in the fog. The coffee grew its civilization. At one point I caught myself talking—not to myself, I talk to myself all the time, that's just maintenance—but to the camp. Out loud. Telling it I wasn't the one who'd done whatever had been done. Explaining myself to four missing strangers and a pot of moldy coffee.

That's when I knew the job had already stopped being a job. Jobs don't make you apologize to cookware.

* * *

Last load of the day was the hard cases. Marisol had said instruments she couldn't pronounce; I'll say the same, mostly because the labels had been removed, which tells you more than any label would have. Professional gear gets labeled. De-identified gear gets de-identified by people who plan on denying it exists. I stacked the cases on the pack frame, cinched it down, and gave the camp one last look on my way out—the way you look back at a wreck you've just stripped, except wrecks don't have bookmarks in their novels.

The pencil was bothering me. I went back for it. Don't ask me why; I've decided it's the kind of question that doesn't improve with answers. I picked up the paperback—some western, dog-eared to death—and the pencil, and I stood there holding a stranger's reading habits in my hand, and that was the moment the crack went through.

Not a big crack. Not a dramatic one. Just a thin fault line running through the concrete of me, the way ice cracks on a pond—you don't see it happen, you just hear it, somewhere under your feet, and afterward you know the surface isn't to be trusted.

Because here's what I understood, standing in that camp with the fog coming in off the water like the tide had gotten lost: somebody had been sitting right where I was standing, reading that book, drinking that coffee, hanging up those socks. Four somebodies. And whatever came for them hadn't given them time to be afraid, or had been the kind of fear you can't run from, or—

No. I shut that door. I'm good at shutting doors; it's most of what I do.

I carried the last load down to the boat in the dark, made two trips because I wouldn't leave the cases overnight, and pushed off from the landing at 2100 with the camp's smoke smell in my jacket and the envelope's advance riding heavy in my pocket. Heavier than paper should weigh.

Out past the buoy line, I killed the running lights and sat in the cockpit with a beer I didn't open, looking north at the black shape of the cape. Somewhere up there was a pot of coffee that proved four people expected to come back. Down here was a boat full of equipment that proved somebody very much wanted to be sure they hadn't left anything behind worth reading. And between those two facts was a gap, and the gap was shaped like four men, and no amount of salvage arithmetic was going to fill it.

Here's my rule, rule one, the one that's kept me fed and free and alone for twenty years: never ask who owns what you pull up. It's a good rule. It has kept me alive. But rules are built for the kinds of jobs where the worst thing at the bottom is a body, and bodies, like I said, are honest.

There was no body in that camp. There was worse. There was *nothing*, and nothing doesn't explain itself, and nothing can't be priced.

I was halfway through deciding to call Marisol in the morning and give the money back—which I have never once done, not in twenty years, not for any reason, because giving money back is how you become a person who knows too much loudly instead of quietly—when I saw the light.

It came from the wreck site itself, out beyond the shoal where the sonar charts showed the anomaly the survey team had been studying. A green-white glow, deep down, steady, pulsing slow. Like a beacon. Like something down there saying *here, here, here.*

Except the survey team's gear was all in my hold. Every case, every array, every instrument Marisol couldn't pronounce. Which meant either the client had more equipment on that wreck than they'd told me—

—or whatever was glowing down there had never belonged to the survey team at all.

I sat in the dark and watched it pulse, and for the first time in twenty years of measuring my life in miles, I wanted very badly to be measuring it in something else. Anything else. Years, maybe. Whatever those were.

Then the glow went out, all at once, like a held breath released, and the water off Cape Merrow was just water again, black and indifferent and full of whatever it was full of.

I started the engine. And that's when I heard it—thin, high, carried across a mile of cold water from the direction of the shore.

Someone was whistling.
