## 3. A Boy in the Hold

Here is what nobody tells you about hiding in a sunken ship for three days: it's not the cold that gets you. The cold is honest. The cold just sits on your chest like a cat and stays there, and you can negotiate with a cat. What gets you is the sound.

A wreck talks to itself all night. Metal contracts, water finds new ways through old seams, and somewhere below decks something heavy shifts a few inches every few hours, like the ship is turning over in its sleep trying to get comfortable. After three days I'd named the sounds. The groaner was the hull settling. The tapper was a loose pipe in the corridor outside my crate. And the listener — the long, wet, patient silence between sounds — that one was me, listening for footsteps that weren't mine.

I want to be clear that I'm not brave. People who knew me back then would laugh if you called me brave. I was fourteen years old and I had run from exactly two things in my life: the instruments on that survey team, and now whatever was coming down through the water toward this hold with a dive light and a very calm way of moving. Running is a skill. I'd argue it's the oldest skill there is. Everything else — courage, loyalty, standing still — those are things you learn later, usually from someone who's better at running than you are and got tired of it.

But I'm getting ahead of myself. Let me tell you how I ended up living inside a dead ship off a coast whose name I still won't say out loud, because some habits keep you alive even after they stop being necessary.

* * *

The survey team picked me up in a harbor town six days before everything went wrong. I'd stowed away on their charter boat the way I stowed away everywhere — small, quiet, and useful enough once discovered that throwing me overboard became more trouble than keeping me. That's the whole trick of being a stowaway. You don't hide well. You hide *just* well enough, and then when they find you, you make yourself worth the ration of food. I could read charts. I could run a skiff. I knew the coastal range better than any of them because I'd grown up in its shadow, walking ridgelines since I was eight, learning which valleys held weather and which ones let it pass through.

The team lead was a woman called Dr. Voss, and she treated finding me like a paperwork problem rather than a person problem. She wrote down my name — Isandro Reyes — in a notebook, asked if my parents were looking for me, and when I said no, she wrote something else down and closed the book. I've thought about that notebook a lot since. I think whatever she wrote second was the reason I'm alive. I think she decided I was already nobody, and nobodies don't get collected.

They were surveying the wreck. That was the official story, and I believed it right up until the night the instruments started screaming.

Not alarms. Screaming. There's a difference, and everyone on that boat heard it. Alarms are designed by people who expect trouble. This was different — the magnetometer and the sonar rig both making sounds they weren't built to make, high and ragged, like dogs that had seen something in the dark that dogs shouldn't be able to see. Dr. Voss stood on the deck holding a handheld unit against her chest with both arms, the way you'd hold a baby or a bomb, and the needle wasn't swinging anymore. It was pinned. Pressed flat against the stop like it was trying to escape the dial entirely.

"Readings," she said, to nobody, in a voice I didn't like. "We're getting readings."

That was the last conversation I wanted to overhear. By morning they were talking about going down into the hold itself, and about manifests, and about a word I didn't know yet but learned fast: *sanitization*. I heard it through the bulkhead at two in the morning, said quietly, the way people say things they've already decided. So I did what I do. I ran. Down into the wreck instead of away from it, because away was open water and a coastline full of roads, and down was dark and terrible and *theirs*. Nobody searches the place they're afraid of.

I took the compass on my way past the survey gear. Not because I understood it. Because it was brass and beautiful and it had been sitting in Dr. Voss's kit like something she was afraid of, and I figured anything a scientist is afraid of is worth stealing.

It hasn't pointed north once since I took it. But that's another chapter.

* * *

Three days in the hold. Three days of canned peaches from the survey stores and meltwater caught off the ribs of the hull, and the sounds, and the listener. On the third day, the footsteps came.

I knew immediately they weren't navy. Navy moves in rhythm — boots together, lights sweeping in patterns, the whole machine confident of its own size. This was one man, alone, moving like water finding its level. Slow where the structure was weak. Fast where it was strong. He came down through the collapsed section aft without using his light more than twice, which told me he'd been in wrecks before, lots of them, and that he trusted his hands more than his eyes.

I pressed back into the gap between the crates where I'd made my nest — a survey equipment case and two sealed cargo crates forming a pocket just big enough for a boy who hadn't eaten properly in a week. My heart was doing something embarrassing. I put my hand over it, as if that helps, as if hearts respond to management.

The light found the crates first. Then the floor. Then, with the terrible patience of a man who has done this exact thing many times, it swept up and stopped on me.

Here's what I looked like, I imagine: a half-frozen kid wedged into a hole in a dead ship, wearing three layers of stolen survey gear, holding a peach can in one hand like a weapon and absolutely fooling no one. Here's what he looked like: big, gray-stubbled, older than my father would have been, with eyes that did the same thing mine were doing — measuring the exits.

He didn't yell. That's the first thing. He crouched down slowly, set the light on the deck so it threw our shadows tall against the crates, and looked at me the way you look at a wild thing you don't want to spook.

"You've been eating the survey stores," he said. His voice was rough, unhurried. "Peaches. I saw the cans."

I said nothing. Silence is free and it never lies for you.

"There's coffee in their camp," he went on. "Still in the pot. Boots by the fire." He tilted his head. "You know anything about that?"

"They left," I said. My voice came out cracked from disuse. "Or they were taken. Depends which night you're asking about."

Something moved across his face — not surprise exactly. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man hearing an answer he'd already guessed and hoping he'd guessed wrong.

"And you hid," he said. "Down here. In the dark. In the one place nobody sane would go."

"It's worked out so far."

He almost smiled. It didn't survive the trip to his mouth, but I saw it start. Then he did something strange. He reached up and knocked twice on the crate beside him — knuckle on steel, a dull hollow sound — and waited, like he was asking the ship's permission.

"How long have you been down here?" he asked.

"Three days."

"On peaches and meltwater."

"And spite," I said. "Spite keeps."

That time the smile made it all the way. Brief, crooked, gone again. He unclipped something from his belt — a foil blanket, the crinkly kind — and tossed it to me without ceremony, the way you'd feed a stray you'd decided not to kick.

"Wrap up. We're leaving in ten minutes."

"I'm not going anywhere with you."

"Sure you are." He was already turning away, checking the corridor, and he said it over his shoulder like it was a fact about the tide. "Navy tender's been sitting past the buoy line since yesterday. They burned the camp this morning. Whatever they're here to erase, they're starting with paper and ending with people. You can stay in this hold and be a footnote, or you can come with me and be a problem. I recommend problem. Footnotes don't get rations."

I should say, for the record, that I considered staying. I want that on the record, because later — much later — people would talk about me like I was brave from the beginning, and I wasn't. I sat there in my peach-can fortress and genuinely weighed it. Staying meant the dark I knew. Going meant a stranger, and strangers are the most dangerous weather there is.

But then I looked at him again, really looked, and I saw it. The thing I recognized. He stood the way I stood. Weight low, back never fully turned to an opening, hands always knowing where the exits were. He counted things — I watched his eyes flick around the hold, touching each crate, each shadow, each route up and out, the way mine did. He wasn't comfortable anywhere. He was just less uncomfortable here than most places, and he'd built his whole life on that arithmetic.

I knew that posture. I'd invented it independently, at eight years old, in a house where footsteps on stairs meant either dinner or danger and you learned to tell which by the rhythm. Two survivors, recognizing each other's survival posture. It's not friendship. It's not trust. It's more like two animals meeting at a waterhole at dusk, each one thinking: *you're scared too. Good. Scared things don't do stupid generous things.*

Except he did do a stupid generous thing. He'd given me the blanket first and asked questions second. That order bothered me for days afterward.

* * *

His name was Bram Galloway, though I didn't learn that until we were on the surface, and I didn't learn anything else true about him for weeks. What I learned in the first hour was his rules, delivered in a flat recitation while he rigged the ascent line, not looking at me, like a man reading terms of service:

"One. Don't ask who owns what we pull up. Two. Don't give your real name to anyone with a clipboard. Three. Never sleep deeper than you can run from. Four—" he paused, checking the line, "—don't touch the compass."

My hand, which had drifted toward my pocket without my permission, froze.

"You know about it?" I said.

"I know you keep checking it every ninety seconds like it's a wound." He glanced at me then, one look, gray eyes in a gray face. "And I know Dr. Voss kept it wrapped in cloth in a case lined with cork, which is not how you store a compass. That's how you store something you're afraid of."

"She said it was miscalibrated."

"Mm." He finished the knot he was tying. "People say 'miscalibrated' about a lot of things they can't explain. Weather. People. Me." He jerked his chin toward the ascent line. "You first. Clip in, breathe normal, exhale on the way up, and if you panic, grab the line and don't let go. I'll be behind you."

"Why behind me?"

"Because if something takes you, I want to see it coming."

It was, I would come to understand, the closest thing to tenderness Bram Galloway was capable of offering a stranger: the promise to watch you die. I clipped in. I breathed normal. And I rose out of the hold of that ship, through forty feet of green-gray water, past the ribs of a vessel that had carried people the world had decided to forget, and I did not look back down. Looking back down felt like something the compass would notice.

* * *

The surface was worse than the deep. That's the joke of it — you spend three days hiding from the world and then the world is just *there*, enormous and lit, the tender a smudge on the horizon pretending to be nothing, the burned camp still smoking faintly on the headland like a candle somebody forgot. Bram's boat — the *Marguerite*, though he only ever called it "the boat," the way you'd refuse to name a pet you planned to abandon — smelled of diesel and old rope and, underneath it, faintly, of someone who lived alone and had gotten good at it.

I sat in the cockpit wrapped in the foil blanket, shivering in the honest way, the way you shiver when the adrenaline leaves and your body presents its invoice. Bram stood at the wheel and put distance between us and the wreck without hurrying. A man who hurries, I'd learn, is a man who thinks speed will save him. Bram believed in geometry, not speed. Angles, distances, the curve of the shoreline between you and the thing you feared.

"The survey team," I said, after the headland had swallowed the smoke. "The ones who vanished. Do you think they're—"

"Don't." He said it mildly, eyes on the water. "Rule five. Don't speculate about people you can't help. It costs energy and pays nothing."

"That's a terrible rule."

"It's kept me alive twenty years."

"Yeah, well." I pulled the blanket tighter. "Kept me alive fourteen, and I speculate constantly. Maybe it's a team effort."

He didn't answer. But I caught it in the reflection of the wheelhouse glass — the almost-smile again, starting and dying, and this time I understood it wasn't amusement. It was resistance. He was resisting the smile the way he resisted everything: names, questions, boys in holds. Somewhere in that man was a door welded shut, and I was too cold and too young to care, so I did what I always do with welded doors.

I talked anyway.

"Her name was Dr. Voss," I said. "The team lead. She wrote my name in a notebook and then wrote something else under it and closed the book. I think the second thing is why they left me behind."

Bram's hands went still on the wheel. Just for a second. A hitch, small as the tapper pipe in that corridor, and then smooth again.

"What do you mean, left you behind?"

"I mean they didn't collect me." I watched the water slide by. "Whatever happened to them — whatever word you want to use — they did it to themselves, on purpose, and they knew it was coming, and they *left me*. Because I was already nobody. Because I didn't count." I shrugged, and the shrug cost more than I let it. "So either I'm the luckiest boy on this coast, or I'm the only witness they forgot to erase. And I've been sitting in a shipwreck for three days deciding which one."

The engine hummed. The tender smudge stayed on the horizon, patient as the listener.

Bram finally spoke, and his voice had changed — flatter, quieter, the voice of a man reading his own terms of service back to himself and finding a clause he didn't remember agreeing to.

"Rule five is suspended," he said. "Tell me about the notebook."

And I would have — I opened my mouth to — but that was the moment the compass woke up.

I felt it before I heard it, a twitch against my hip like a fish taking bait, and when I pulled it out, the needle wasn't pointing at the wreck behind us, the way it had pointed for three days, steady and mournful as a dog at a grave. It was swinging. Wide, slow arcs, port to starboard, starboard to port, like it was searching the horizon for something it couldn't name.

Then it stopped. Dead center. Pointing straight at Bram Galloway.

He looked at it. I looked at him. Neither of us said anything, because there was nothing to say that wasn't a question, and we were both, at that moment, professionals at not asking questions.

The needle trembled once, twice — and settled, firm and certain, on the man at the wheel, as if the compass had just decided something about him that he hadn't decided about himself yet.
