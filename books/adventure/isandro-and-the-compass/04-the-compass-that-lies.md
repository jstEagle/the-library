## 4. The Compass That Lies

The fire had burned down to a red eye by the time I worked up the nerve to show him.

Bram sat with his back against the hull of the overturned skiff, boots crossed at the ankle, cleaning a dive knife with the kind of patience most people reserve for prayer. He'd been doing it for an hour. The blade was clean. He kept going anyway, because his hands needed something to do that wasn't shaking, and he'd decided I wasn't allowed to see that.

I'd been watching him since the wreck. Not in a creepy way. In a *taking inventory* way, which is what you learn to do when you're small and stowaways don't get second chances. Bram Galloway moved like a man who'd already counted everything in the room and found it all slightly disappointing. His boat. His gear. His coffee, which he drank black and bitter and without any ceremony whatsoever, like flavor was a luxury he hadn't budgeted for.

He was also, I was almost certain, the first adult in three years who hadn't lied to me about something important. He just hadn't told me anything at all, which felt — weirdly — more honest.

"I know you went through my bag," I said.

His hands didn't stop. "Did I."

"Yesterday. While I was asleep on the bench. You checked the side pocket twice."

"You snore like a bilge pump," he said. "I was considering throwing you overboard. Wanted to make sure there wasn't a rope involved."

"There's no rope."

"Mm." He tested the edge against his thumb, frowned at nothing, and set the knife down. "So why are you telling me?"

Because here's the thing about secrets: they get heavy. And I'd been carrying this one since the hold of that ship, through two days of cold and one night of listening to Bram breathe in a rhythm too controlled to be real sleep, and the weight of it had started pressing on my ribs like water pressure. Down there, thirty meters under gray-green water, I'd learned that a secret can drown you faster than the sea if you hold it too long.

Also — and I want to be honest about this, because honesty is the whole point of what happened next — I wanted him to tell me I was crazy. I wanted a grown-up to look at it and say *kid, that's a broken compass*, and then I could go back to being a boy who'd found junk in a dead ship instead of a boy carrying proof that the world had a seam in it somewhere.

I took it out of my jacket.

The brass caught the firelight and threw it back warm, the way brass does, like it's showing off. It was smaller than my palm, heavier than it looked, the case worn smooth at the edges where decades of thumbs had rubbed it. Someone had scratched a number into the base — 7-1-4 — and someone else had tried to scratch it out. Underneath the glass, the needle lay perfectly still.

"That's not yours," Bram said immediately.

"Nope."

"Where'd you get it."

"The chart room. Second deck. There was a drawer that was locked and the lock was—" I shrugged. "Old. Everything down there is old. It came open like it was waiting."

Bram looked at the compass the way he'd looked at the survey team's camp — like something that didn't fit the arithmetic of the world, and therefore had to be wrong, because the world couldn't be. "Put it away."

"It doesn't point north."

"Kid—"

"I'm not a kid. My name is Isandro."

"*Isandro.* Put it away."

But he didn't look away from it. That was the thing about Bram Galloway that took me weeks to understand and that I understood completely in that moment: he could order things to stop existing all he wanted, but he never once managed to stop looking at them. Curiosity, for Bram, was a disease he treated with denial, and denial only ever worked until sundown.

I held the compass flat on my palm and turned slowly, watching the needle.

It didn't waver. North was behind me, toward the water, toward the wreck sitting on its shelf of silt with its sealed holds and its manifests full of names nobody was supposed to read. The needle didn't care about north. The needle pointed at the ship like a dog pointing at a grave.

"Watch," I said. "I'll turn around."

I did. The needle swung — smooth, deliberate, no jitter, none of the lazy overshoot a cheap compass does — and settled again. Still pointing at the wreck. Through rock, through forest, through me standing between it and itself.

Bram set down his coffee. Slowly. Like sudden movements might count as belief.

"What is it," he said. Not a question. A demand wearing a question's clothes.

"That's what I'm trying to find out. I've had it two days. It hasn't pointed north once. And it's not broken, because—" I hesitated, then decided to spend the whole coin. "Because when Odile—"

No. Wait. I'm getting ahead of myself. Odile comes later. At this point in the story it was just me and Bram and a fire on a shingle beach with the tender's searchlight sweeping the fog out past the point every forty seconds, patient as a lighthouse that hated us.

"Because when what," Bram said.

"When I think about certain things, it moves." I swallowed. "Okay. Here's the part where you decide I'm insane. When I hold it and I think about my dad, the needle pulls harder. Like — you know how a magnet feels over iron? It gets *eager*. And when I think about getting off this coast, getting away, starting over somewhere the navy's never heard of—" I turned my hand over. The needle sagged, confused, swinging in slow half-circles like it was searching a room for furniture that had been removed. "It doesn't know where to go. Because I don't, really. But it always comes back to the wreck. Always. No matter what else I want."

Bram was quiet for a long time. The fire popped. Out on the water, the searchlight made its patient pass.

"And your father," he said finally, very carefully, "is connected to the wreck how?"

I hadn't told him that part either. So I told him. About the manifest pages I'd seen in the chart room before I hid — the ones listing passengers, actual names, dates going back further than I'd been alive. About finding our family name in a margin note in my mother's handwriting, which she must have left with the survey team, which meant she'd been *here*, which meant everything she'd told me before she died was true. About how I'd stowed away with the survey team not to guide them but to find out what they were guiding themselves toward.

Bram listened without moving. When I finished, he picked up his coffee, considered it, and put it down again untasted.

"So it points at what you're afraid to lose," he said.

"How do you figure?"

"Because it's not pointing at the wreck, Isandro. It's pointing at whatever the wreck has that you're scared of losing. Your mother's handwriting. Your father's name. Proof she wasn't lying." He said it flatly, like a man reading coordinates off a chart. "Compasses don't want things. People do. Whatever this thing is, it's reading *you*."

"That's worse," I said.

"Yeah," he agreed. "It is."

* * *

Here's what I should have done: dropped it in the fire. Or thrown it in the sea, which would have been poetic and also probably futile, given that the thing seemed to have opinions. Instead I did what I always did with dangerous things, which was hold onto them tighter, because everything I'd ever loved had been taken away by people who were better at letting go than I was.

"Bram," I said. "Hold it."

"No."

"Just hold it. One second. Tell me what it does."

"Nothing. It'll do nothing, because it's a hunk of brass and you're a tired kid projecting onto it, and tomorrow we're going to argue about something normal like food." But his eyes were still on it, and I watched him lose the argument with himself in real time — watched twenty years of practiced indifference come apart over four ounces of metal, like rust working through paint.

"It won't bite."

"Everything bites. That's the first rule of salvage." He wiped his palms on his thighs — actually wiped them, like he was about to handle something live — and then, instead of taking it, he stood up and walked to the tideline. Stood there with his back to me, shoulders squared against the fog, the way men stand when they're arguing with the ocean.

I brought it to him anyway. Because I was fifteen and stupid and lonely, and because some part of me had already decided that if this man was going to be the one who got me off this coast alive, he deserved to be as scared as I was. Misery loves company. Fear loves it more.

I held it out. "One second."

"Don't."

"One—"

"*Don't.*" He turned around, and his face did something I hadn't seen it do yet. Not anger. Something older and tireder. "You want to know why I work alone? Why I dive wrecks nobody names and ask no questions about who owns what comes up?" He jabbed a thumb at the compass. "Because questions like that one have a price tag, and I've already paid it once. I spent twenty years making sure nobody could take anything from me that mattered. Boat, gear, money, name — all replaceable. All *priced*. You know what that buys you?"

I didn't answer.

"Freedom," he said. "Cheap, ugly freedom. And I'm not holding that thing, because the second it tells me what I'm afraid to lose, I stop being free. You understand? The moment you have something—" and here his voice did a strange hitch, like a rope going taut, "—you're done. Somebody can use it. The navy, the contractors, whoever burned that camp. They don't need to catch you. They just need to know what you'll turn around for."

The searchlight swept the fog behind him. For one rotation, it lit him up silver, and I saw that his hands were trembling, and that he knew I could see it, and that he'd decided to let me see it, which from Bram Galloway was practically a signed confession.

"So no," he said, quieter now. "I'm not touching it. And you shouldn't either. But you will, because you're fifteen and you've got nothing else, and that's exactly how these things get their hooks in."

He was right about all of it. That's the part that hurts to write down. He was right, and I handed him the compass anyway, because I was fifteen and cruel in the way only lonely kids are cruel — which is to say, accidentally, and to the people trying to help us.

I pressed it into his palm.

* * *

For maybe three seconds, nothing happened. Long enough for Bram to start breathing again. Long enough for him to give me a look that said *see? Nothing. Brass. Junk.*

Then the needle woke up.

It didn't swing. It *rose* — lifted off the pivot like a sleeping animal lifting its head, quivering, straining against the glass toward something south and inland, something that wasn't the wreck, wasn't the water, wasn't anywhere we'd been. And it pulled so hard the whole compass trembled in his hand, buzzing faintly against his skin like a trapped wasp.

Bram stared at it.

I stared at Bram.

"Well," I said. "What's south?"

"Nothing," he said automatically. Then, correcting: "The highway. Kettle Bridge. A fuel depot. Nothing."

The needle strained. Buzzed. Pointed.

And Bram Galloway — a man who measured his life in miles specifically so he'd never have to measure it in anything that mattered — went white around the mouth and closed his fist around the compass like he could smother it, and shoved it back at me, and said, in a voice sanded down to almost nothing:

"We move at first light. Before the tender's next sweep. South."

"South where?"

He didn't answer. He was looking at his own empty palm like it had betrayed him, and out past the point the searchlight swung around again, and this time it paused — just for a heartbeat — on the shingle where we stood, before sliding on into the fog.

"They know we're here," Bram said. "Pack your bag, Isandro. We're leaving tonight."
