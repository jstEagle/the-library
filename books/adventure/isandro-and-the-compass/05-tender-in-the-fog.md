## 5. Tender in the Fog

I've been woken by bad weather, bad engines, and bad company more times than I can count, but I'd never been woken by silence before. That's what did it—the absence of the gulls that had been working the tideline all week like a union crew on overtime. When the birds go quiet, something bigger than them has arrived.

I came up out of my bunk with the knife already in my hand, which is a habit and not a comfort, and looked through the porthole at a fog so thick it seemed the sea had been erased overnight and replaced with gray felt. And sitting in that felt, maybe two hundred yards off my stern, was a tender.

Not the navy tender from the buoy line, the one that had watched me for three days like a cat watching a mouse hole. This one was bigger, grayer, and flying no colors at all, which on the water is its own kind of flag. No running lights. No wake churn. Just a shape where a shape shouldn't be, close enough now that I could hear the low diesel mutter of her idling, the sound of an engine being kept warm rather than used.

I dressed without turning on a lamp. Twenty years of salvage work teaches you to dress in the dark, to know where your boots are by feel, to move through your own boat like a rumor. I put water on for coffee because I refuse to meet my own execution uncaffeinated, and while it heated I took inventory.

The camp ashore was still standing—I could see the faint orange smudge of the survey team's fire pit through the fog, or thought I could. The boy was aboard, asleep in the forepeak, curled around that brass compass like it was a heat source. The recovered equipment sat lashed under tarps on deck: cases stenciled with numbers instead of names, instruments wrapped in wool blankets like infants.

And there it was, the whole problem laid out in one glance. I'd spent twenty years making sure nobody could look at me and see anything worth looking at. Now my deck looked like a government storeroom and my boat had a witness sleeping in it.

The coffee boiled. I drank it standing up, watching the tender, waiting for it to do what tenders do—come alongside, hail me, ask questions I'd answer with the flat half-truths that had kept me solvent and anonymous since I was thirty. But it didn't come alongside. It just sat there, and after a while I understood that it wasn't waiting for me. It was waiting for daylight, or for something on shore, or simply for its own schedule, and my boat was beneath its notice except as a line item.

That should have been a relief. It wasn't. Being beneath notice had been my religion for two decades, and I knew the feeling of being noticed anyway the way you know a hand on the back of your neck.

* * *

Full light came up gray and sourceless, the sun just a brightening behind the fog. That's when the boats started.

First a rigid inflatable, running quiet from the direction of the tender toward shore, carrying six men in civilian gear—olive jackets, no insignia, the kind of clothes bought in bulk by people who don't want their people to look like anything. Then a second boat. Then a third. They beached at the cove below the survey camp and began moving uphill with the unhurried efficiency of men who have done this before and will do it again next week somewhere else.

I got the binoculars off the shelf above my chart table. My hands were steady. I noted that the way you note someone else's heartbeat—clinically, from a distance, as if it belonged to a stranger.

They didn't search the camp. That was the first thing I understood, and understanding it dropped my stomach through the hull. Searching is what you do when you're looking for answers. These men went straight to work like they already had every answer and were only erasing the questions. Two of them pulled the tents down and folded them with parade-ground precision. One walked the perimeter with a device I couldn't identify—not a detector, more like a reader—and marked things with little flags. The last two built up the fire pit into something industrial, feeding it the camp's own stores, and then, when that wasn't enough, cans of something from a case they'd brought with them.

The smoke when it caught wasn't wood smoke. It was chemical and white and it climbed straight up in the windless air like a signal flare announcing nothing to no one.

Boots by the fire. Coffee in the pot. No tracks leading out. I'd stood in that camp four days ago and felt the first crack run through my indifference, and now I watched the crack get filled in with fire and accelerant and folded canvas, watched the evidence of whatever happened to those people go up in smoke that would be gone by noon.

They weren't lost. I'd known it in some cellar of myself since the first night, the way you know a storm is coming from the ache in old bones. But knowing and watching are different animals. Those people hadn't wandered off, hadn't drowned, hadn't deserted. They'd been *collected*—gathered up along with their readings and their instruments and their boots, processed, removed. The camp wasn't being cleaned because it was dirty. It was being cleaned because it existed.

And I was standing on the only other thing in fifty miles that existed.

* * *

"Are they burning it?"

The boy's voice behind me, hoarse with sleep. I hadn't heard him come up the companionway, which bothered me more than the tender did. A fourteen-year-old shouldn't be able to move quieter than a man who's made his living not being heard.

"Burn barrel," I said. "Camp cleanup."

"That's not cleanup." He wedged into the space beside me at the wheel, small and sharp-eyed, hair flattened on one side. He had the compass in his fist. He always had the compass in his fist. "Cleanup is when you take the trash out. That's when you make sure there was never any trash."

I looked down at him. Somewhere between the wreck and this morning he'd stopped being cargo and started being a person, and I resented the timing, because it's much easier to make cold decisions about cargo.

"They'll come for us next," he said.

"They might come for the equipment."

"You know that's not true." He said it without accusation, which was worse. "You've known it since the buoy line. You just keep hoping if you stand very still, you'll turn back into furniture."

"I'm going to pretend you didn't say that, on account of you're a minor and I'm an adult who doesn't take life advice from minors."

But he was right, and we both stood there letting him be right, watching the white smoke climb. Below us the tide sucked at the pilings. Above us the fog held the tender like a hand holds a card it hasn't turned over yet.

One of the men on shore broke away from the others and walked down to the beach. He stood facing the water for a long moment, and even through the binoculars, even across the gray distance, I felt the weight of his attention swing out over the cove like a searchlight. He raised something—a radio, a phone, a hand—and spoke into it.

Then he pointed. Not at the camp. At my boat.

* * *

Here's a thing about terror that the movies get wrong: it isn't loud. It doesn't come with music. Real terror, the kind that arrives without drama, feels almost like administrative inconvenience. A form being filled out somewhere. Your name typed into a field. The world quietly deciding what you are, and you finding out after the decision has been made.

The second inflatable came across the cove at a working pace, not rushing. Four men this time. One of them held a clipboard, and I swear on my mother's grave—which is a lie, because my mother is alive and living in a place she thinks I don't know about—that clipboard frightened me more than any gun I've ever seen on the water. Guns mean the moment is still undecided. Clipboards mean it's been decided and you're just paperwork now.

"Get below," I told the boy.

"No."

"It wasn't a negotiation."

"I know," he said. "It was a suggestion, and I'm declining it." He planted himself against the wheelhouse door with his chin up and his knuckles white around the compass, and I had a sudden, unwelcome image of what he must have survived alone in that hold—dark, cold, listening to instruments scream—and understood that a kid who'd done that wasn't going below decks because a stranger told him to.

So I opened the wheelhouse door myself and stepped out onto my own deck like a host greeting guests he hadn't invited.

The inflatable bumped alongside. Hands caught my rail. The man with the clipboard came up first—he was maybe forty-five, square-faced, wearing a jacket that cost more than my winch, and he had the particular blankness of a man whose face has been professionally sanded smooth. Behind him came two big ones who stood at the rail doing the thing big men do, which is take up space, and one younger one who stayed near the boat and kept his eyes moving, checking sightlines, counting exits. I filed that one away. The watchers always matter more than the muscle.

"Morning," I said.

The clipboard man consulted his clipboard, which struck me as either theater or genuine bureaucracy, and both possibilities were bad. "Vessel owner?"

"That depends who's asking."

He looked up. For a second something moved behind the sanded-smooth face—not menace exactly, more like mild professional regret, the look of a man who'd hoped this would stay easy. "Mr. Galloway. The vessel is the *Maren K*, forty-one feet, steel hull, registered out of—" he read it, though I'd have bet money he knew it by heart, "—Port Anselm. Is that correct?"

Nobody had called me Mr. Galloway in eleven years. On the water I was Bram, or hey-you, or nothing at all. Hearing my name in that flat voice was like hearing a doctor say a word you'd only ever seen written down. They hadn't followed the trail to me. They'd started with it.

"That's the boat," I said.

"We're placing a hold on it. Pending resolution of an ongoing matter." He handed me a paper. It was real paper, real ink, a seizure notice with a case number and a statute citation and a signature block signed with initials. It listed my boat, my engine serial number, my dive gear, and—my eyes snagged here—"miscellaneous recovered materials, quantity unknown." Quantity unknown. They knew I'd been down at the wreck. They'd known the whole time. The tender beyond the buoy line hadn't been watching me; it had been *logging* me.

"You can appeal through the regional office," the clipboard man said, in the tone of a man reading the nutrition label off a cereal box. "You're free to go ashore. Personal effects can be collected within seventy-two hours."

"And if I'm still aboard in seventy-three?"

He finally looked at me fully, and I gave him credit for not smiling. "Then you'll be aboard a government vessel, Mr. Galloway, and we'll have a different conversation."

Behind me, inside the wheelhouse, I heard the boy shift his weight against the door. Keep still, kid. Keep being furniture.

"The survey team," I said. "The one that walked off the job. Any word?"

The smooth face didn't change, but the two big men at the rail changed—one shifted his grip, one glanced at the clipboard man and away again, quick as a fish turning. People think lies live in faces. Lies live in the people around the face.

"There's no survey team," the clipboard man said.

"Right," I said. "Whose coffee was in the pot, then?"

For a moment the only sound was the water slapping the hull and the tender's idle carrying across the fog. Then the clipboard man tore a carbon copy off his pad and held it out. "Seventy-two hours," he said. "Don't make it complicated. Complicated is bad for everyone, especially men with your history."

My history. I kept my face doing nothing, which is the one skill I've practiced harder than diving. Whatever file they had on me, it was thick enough to reference casually to a stranger, and thin enough that they hadn't simply arrested me. Which meant they wanted something from me besides my presence. Which meant the boat, the gear, the equipment under the tarps—they wanted me to lead them to whatever piece of this was still missing.

Or they wanted the boy, and didn't know he was aboard.

Both thoughts arrived together and shook hands.

* * *

They pulled away the way they'd come, unhurried, and the fog took them, and the tender's diesel note faded until I couldn't tell it from the blood in my ears. The smoke over the camp had thinned to a smudge. By afternoon there'd be nothing up there but scorched rock and trampled ground, and by tomorrow the tide would start working on the beach prints, and by next week the cove would look like nobody had ever been foolish enough to care about anything.

I stood at the rail holding a piece of paper that said my boat belonged to the government now, and I did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding since dawn.

Option one: comply. Go ashore with my duffel, walk inland, catch a bus, become a new name in a new port town, start the miles over. It was the play I'd run before. It was the play I was built for. The fine print of my whole adult life: never ask who owns what you pull up, never be owned yourself, never carry anything heavy enough to slow you down.

Option two was standing behind me in the wheelhouse doorway, holding a brass compass that pointed at a burned camp full of people who'd been collected like evidence.

"They're giving us seventy-two hours," the boy said. He'd heard everything; of course he had. His voice was steady but his eyes had gone glassy-bright, and he was gripping the doorframe hard enough to blanch his knuckles. "Bram. They gave us seventy-two hours. That means they think we need it. That means they don't know everything yet."

"Kid—"

"The manifests are still down there." He said it fast, like a man stepping off a ledge. "In the hold. Under the false decking. Nobody's found them because nobody knew to look, and if we don't go back, those names stay down there forever, and everyone they burned today stays burned, and—" He stopped. Swallowed. Held the compass out between us like an argument. The needle inside the glass was trembling, swinging, trying to decide, and for the first time since I'd met him it wasn't pointing at the wreck.

It was pointing at the open water east of us. Inland. Toward the range.

I looked at the seizure notice in my hand. Case number. Statute. Initials. Seventy-two hours before my boat stopped being mine and I stopped being invisible, and the truth—the plain, ugly, twenty-year truth—was that part of me wanted to sign the paper, shoulder the duffel, and let the *Maren K* sink into the ledger like everything else I'd ever let sink.

I looked at the boy instead. He was watching me with the terrible patience of someone who has already lost everything once and knows how it's done.

"Get the dive bags," I heard myself say. "We go back down at slack tide. And then, kid—we run inland."

The needle steadied, pointing east, like it had been waiting all morning for me to catch up.
