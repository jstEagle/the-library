## 6. What the Wreck Carried

The compass pointed at the wreck, and Bram said we were leaving anyway.

I want that on the record. Whatever else happened down there, whatever we found in the dark hold of a ship that wasn't supposed to have a hold at all — it started with me arguing and Bram pretending not to hear me, which is how most of our conversations went in those first days. He was already packing the boat when I found him, folding his gear with the economy of a man who'd practiced leaving so many times it had become a kind of prayer.

"They're coming back," I told him. "The navy. You saw what they did to the camp."

"I saw smoke." He didn't look up. "Smoke means leave."

"So we're just going to run?"

"That's generally what running is, kid."

I stood there holding the compass in both hands like it might warm me, which it wouldn't, because brass holds cold the way some people hold grudges. It sat dead still in my palms, needle fixed on the water behind us, on the gray swell where the wreck lay forty fathoms down with its secret still sealed inside it.

"It's pointing," I said.

"I can see that."

"You said it points at what you're afraid to lose. Mine points at the wreck. So maybe the wreck isn't done with us. Maybe—"

"Maybe nothing." Now he looked up, and his eyes had that flat shine they got when he was scared and converting the fear into something more useful, like irritation. "Isandro. Listen to me. There are two kinds of people who go back for one last look. The first kind get away with it. The second kind become a story other divers tell each other over beers." He zipped the bag shut. "I've never met the second kind. That's the point. They don't come back to tell it."

But here's the thing about Bram Galloway that took me weeks to understand and years to forgive: when he finished explaining why we shouldn't do a thing, he always left a silence afterward. A door-shaped silence. And if you were stubborn enough to stand in it long enough, he'd sigh like the weight of the whole Pacific had been assigned to him personally, and he'd start unzipping the bag again.

He sighed. He unzipped.

"One dive," he said. "Forty minutes bottom time, no heroics, no touching anything you don't show me first. And if I say ascend, you ascend, even if the wreck itself grows legs and asks nicely."

"Okay."

"And Isandro—" He stopped, and for a second something moved behind his face that I couldn't name yet, though later I'd know it as the look of a man watching himself break his own rules and finding, to his horror, that it doesn't even hurt. "You don't have to do this. Whatever your father left down there, it'll keep. Dead things keep."

"That's what I'm afraid of," I said.

He didn't answer that. He just handed me my mask, and we went out into the fog to commit the dumbest, most necessary mistake of our lives.

* * *

Descending through cold water is like being erased slowly, layer by layer. First the light goes, then the color, then the sound of your own breathing becomes the only proof you exist. By thirty meters I was a pair of hands and a heartbeat, and the wreck rose up out of the green-black below us like a mountain range nobody had bothered to name.

Bram went first along the hull, his light cutting cones out of the murk. The ship was bigger than I remembered from my one terrified visit — or maybe I was bigger now, braver, or stupider; the three feel identical at depth. We came around her stern where the name had been ground off, not weathered off, *ground*, chiseled away by someone who wanted her anonymous even in death, and Bram ran his glove along the gouged steel and shook his head in a way that meant a whole paragraph he wasn't going to waste air on saying.

We dropped through the cargo hatch into the hold.

I'd been in there before, hiding, half-frozen, listening to the survey team's instruments scream through the hull above me. But that had been survival, and survival doesn't let you look at anything. This time I actually saw the place. Crates stacked in careful rows, all stamped with the same shipping code. Machinery under tarps. And against the far bulkhead, set apart from everything else like it was embarrassed to be there, a bank of small steel cabinets — filing cabinets, bolted to the deck, in the cargo hold of a ship that officially carried nothing worth filing.

Bram's light found them and stopped moving.

"Huh," he said, which from Bram was a shout.

The cabinets weren't locked. I think that's what got to him most, later — that nobody had bothered to lock them, because who would ever come looking? Who dives a grave to read its paperwork? The drawers slid open with a squeal that sounded almost human, and inside were envelopes. Hundreds of them. Oilcloth-wrapped, wax-sealed, each one numbered, each one heavy in that particular way paper gets when there's a lot of it and it's been waiting a long time.

Bram held one up to his light. The seal had a government stamp on it, the kind of eagle-and-anchor emblem that means *this document does not exist*. His lips moved as he read the header. Then they stopped moving entirely, and for about ten seconds the only thing alive in that entire drowned hold was the sound of my regulator and the small frantic drumming of my own heart.

"What?" I asked. My voice came out as bubbles.

He turned the envelope so I could see it. Typed across the face, beneath the classification stamp:

**MANIFEST OF PASSENGERS — FINAL. DO NOT RECOVER.**

"Do not recover," Bram repeated. His voice through the comm was very quiet. "You know what that means? That means somebody wrote instructions to themselves. Somebody knew this ship was going down with these aboard, and they wrote themselves a note not to be a hero about it."

"Passengers," I said. "This ship was supposed to be empty."

"Yeah." He was already opening the envelope, working the wax with his knife, careful as a surgeon. "Yeah, I know."

Inside was a typed list. Names. Dates of birth. Next of kin. And beside every single name, in the same column, the same notation, repeated down the page like a litany:

**STATUS: PRESUMED DROWNED — [DATE, 31 YEARS PRIOR].**

Thirty-one years. These people hadn't died on this ship. They'd died — officially, legally, bureaucratically — three decades before she ever sank. Bram's light trembled, just slightly, and I realized his hands were shaking, and I realized I'd never seen Bram Galloway's hands shake, not in rough water, not with a hook through his calf, not once.

"Bram?"

"These names." He cleared his throat. "Some of these names are on memorials. I've seen them. Coastal towns, little plaques, *lost at sea*. Whole communities wrote letters home to people who were already—" He stopped. Started again. "There's a woman on this list with three kids listed as next of kin. Kids who grew up thinking their mother drowned. Who lit candles for her. Who are probably grandparents now."

The water felt colder suddenly. Or maybe that was just the truth getting in.

"Why?" I asked. "Why fake drowning them? Why put them on a ship and sink—"

And then I stopped, because I understood, and understanding is the worst thing that ever happens to you at forty meters in the dark. You don't fake-drown people who volunteer. You fake-drown people the state has decided are *expendable* — dissidents, witnesses, the wrong kind of loud — and then you give their families a body of water to grieve instead of a truth to demand. The ship wasn't a tragedy. The ship was a filing system. The ocean was the shredder, except somebody upstairs had gotten sentimental, or careless, or afraid, and ordered the manifests preserved instead of burned, and then the ship had gone down anyway, and the whole apparatus of erasure had sunk with its own evidence inside it.

"They were going to destroy these," I said. "That's why the survey team came. Not to study the wreck. To confirm the manifests were gone."

Bram was silent for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had changed — flatter, harder, the voice of a man doing arithmetic he hates.

"The team didn't vanish because they found treasure, Isandro. They vanished because they found *this*. And whoever sent them needed to know whether the past stayed buried." He looked at the open drawer, at the hundreds of sealed envelopes, at the dead demanding their accounting. "It didn't."

We filled his dive bag until the zipper strained. Manifests, dozens of them, wrapped in oilcloth, strapped flat against his chest where they wouldn't foul his rig. I took a stack too, because my arms were shaking and I wanted to carry them anyway. Grief is heavy, but it's lighter than guilt, and I was learning the difference at forty meters in the dark.

As we rose through the hatch, my compass — stowed in the pouch on my vest — gave one slow, deliberate swing. Away from the wreck. Toward the surface. Toward the east, and the mountains, and whatever came next.

For the first time since I'd taken it, the needle wasn't pointing backward at the dead.

It was pointing forward, like it finally had somewhere to be delivered.

* * *

We surfaced into fog so thick the boat was a rumor. Bram hauled us aboard, stripped his gear with shaking hands, and then sat down on the deck among the wet oilcloth bundles and just — looked at them. For a long time. The fog dripped off the rigging. Somewhere beyond the buoy line, the navy tender was presumably out there being patient and terrible.

"My father's name is in there," I said. "Somewhere in those pages. I haven't looked yet. I'm scared to look, and I'm scared of why I'm scared."

Bram nodded slowly. He picked up one bundle, turned it over in his hands like he was weighing it, and I watched twenty years of *never ask who owns what you pull up* die on his face. Because you can't not-ask anymore. Not now. Now the question wasn't whose salvage this was.

Now the question was who these people belonged to — and the answer, for the first time in thirty-one years, was *themselves*.

"We can't run with this," Bram said quietly. "Running means burying it again, and I've spent my whole life being good at burying things, and I am telling you, kid, I cannot bury *this*." He looked up at me, and his eyes were wet, and he didn't pretend otherwise, and that was somehow the scariest part of the entire day. "They put these people in the water twice. Once with the ship, and once with a lie. Somebody's going to have to pull them out both times."

The foghorn of the tender moaned, distant, closer than yesterday.

Bram stood up, wiped his face with the back of his hand, and started the engine.

"East," he said. "Inland. Before they close the water."

And the compass in my pocket swung toward the mountains like it agreed.
