## 7. Upcountry

The *Maren B* had been mine for eleven years, and I sank her in forty minutes.

Not literally — I'm not a sentimental man, and even if I were, you don't sink a boat to spite a tender. You strip her. Registration papers into the woodstove first, then the logbooks, then the charts with my handwriting all over them like a confession. Isandro watched from the companionway while I pulled the plates off the sonar unit and dropped the whole assembly into a mesh bag meant for scallop drag. He didn't ask questions. That was the first thing I liked about him, though I'd have denied liking anything about him at the time.

"They'll find the hull," he said.

"They'll find a hull." I fed the last of the paperwork to the fire and watched it curl. "A derelict, abandoned by persons unknown. Happens every winter. The navy will write it up as salvage and auction it to some fisherman who'll spend three years wondering why the bilge smells like diesel and bad decisions."

"And us?"

"Persons unknown." I killed the fire, opened the seacocks, and stepped off onto the dock without looking back. Looking back is how you get caught. Also how you get sentimental, and sentiment is just ballast you can't jettison.

We left her taking water at the mooring in Miller's Inlet, which was the best I could do on short notice. By morning she'd be on the bottom in twelve feet of cold black water, and by afternoon some kid in a skiff would be claiming he'd found her, and by evening the story would be about the boat and not about the boy standing next to me wearing a dead surveyor's parka two sizes too big.

I'd like to tell you I had a plan. What I had was a direction: east and up, toward the coastal range, toward a pass called Saddleback that I knew only from a chart I no longer owned and a trucker I'd once bought fuel from at 3 a.m. The pass crossed out of the navy's jurisdiction and into somebody else's, and past that there was a highway, and past that there was anywhere that wasn't here. It wasn't much of a plan. But plans are like anchors — mostly they're for making you feel like you've done something.

The compass rode in Isandro's inside pocket, and I made a point of not thinking about it, which took roughly all my concentration.

* * *

Here's what nobody tells you about walking away from your entire life: it's boring. Ninety percent of it is putting one foot in front of the other on a logging road that smells like cut spruce and diesel, carrying more weight than your knees signed up for, listening to a teenager breathe.

Isandro talked anyway. Not constantly — he had survival instincts, whatever else you could say about him — but enough. Enough that by the second day I'd learned his mother worked cannery shifts, that he hadn't seen her since March, that he'd stowed aboard the survey vessel because the pay rumor said they needed a local guide and he needed to be anyplace that wasn't where he was. He told me these things the way kids hand you things they want you to hold but not examine.

"Rule one," I said somewhere around mile twenty, mostly to shut him up. "You don't talk about where we came from. Not the wreck, not the camp, not the tender. If someone asks, we're brothers. We're going over the pass to work the orchards before frost."

"I'm fourteen."

"You're small for fifteen. Orchards take small people; it's why they hire teenagers." I adjusted the strap of the gear bag. "Rule two: we don't walk ridgelines when we can walk draws, and we don't walk roads when we can walk beside them. Roads are for people who want to be found."

"Rule three?"

"There's no rule three yet. I'm building the list as we go."

He nodded, serious as a deacon, and then within the hour he broke rule one by telling a gas station attendant in Kettle Flats an elaborate story about our grandmother's apple press, complete with details he invented on the spot — the crooked handle, the year the wasps got into the crop — until the old woman behind the counter was laughing and pressing a paper sack of bruised fruit into his hands and refusing payment.

I stood there holding money I no longer needed to spend, watching this happen.

"That was rule one," I said when we were back on the road.

"She looked hungry for company," Isandro said. "Did you see her hands? She's alone out here. You can tell."

"So now we're running a charity operation."

"We're running *somewhere*," he said, "and she gave us apples. That's not charity, that's trade." He bit into one, considered it, and handed me the rest without being asked.

That was the thing about him, and I want to be honest about how much it irritated me: he kept breaking my rules in exactly the ways that worked. Rule two said avoid contact. He stopped to help a woman whose trailer hitch had come loose outside Alder Fork, and she turned out to know which forest service gates were locked this season and which weren't. Rule one said no names. He gave ours as "Bram and Sandy, brothers," which was close enough to true to be untraceable and warm enough to be believed, and I realized somewhere around the third kindness that I'd spent twenty years perfecting a technique for moving through the world without leaving prints, and this kid had a technique too: he left prints everywhere, deliberately, little warm ones, and somehow they never pointed back at him.

It shouldn't have worked. It worked.

* * *

On the third night we slept in the shell of a fire lookout that some budget line had murdered a decade ago — just the tower legs and a plank platform and a view of the range going blue and then black under a sky with too many stars in it for comfort. I built a small fire in a ring of stones some previous traveler had left, small enough to pass for nothing from the valley floor, and heated beans, and we ate in the kind of silence that isn't empty.

"The compass pointed at the wreck," Isandro said, after a while. "Back at the inlet. All night, the whole time we walked away, it pointed backward."

I put another bean in my mouth and chewed it thoroughly, buying time.

"It doesn't anymore," he went on. "Since yesterday morning it points—" He stopped. Started again. "It points along our route. Ahead of us. Like it knows where we're going."

"Compasses don't know things."

"This one does."

"Then it knows we're heading for a pass, same as me. Congratulations to the compass on basic map reading." I scraped the can clean and set it down harder than necessary. "Put it away."

"You're scared of it."

"I'm *priced* of it," I said, before I could stop myself. "There's a difference. Scared means you run. Priced means you figure out what it costs and whether you can afford it. Everything costs something. A compass that reads minds costs plenty."

He was quiet for a moment. Then, in the tone of someone laying down a card he'd been holding: "What are you afraid of losing? If you held it. What would it point at?"

"Nothing. That's the trick of it. Nothing to lose, nothing to point at. It's the cheapest way to live there is, and I recommend it."

He looked at me across the fire with an expression I recognized, God help me, because I used to wear it — the look of a person doing arithmetic on someone else's lie and getting a remainder that wouldn't come out even. Fourteen years old and he already knew that men who say they have nothing to lose are reciting a eulogy.

"Get some sleep," I said. "We hit the switchbacks at first light. Snowline's low this year. I can smell it."

* * *

The snow came early, of course. It always does, in stories and in mountains, right when the schedule says it can't afford to.

We were on the lower switchbacks when the first flakes came down, fat and slow, the kind that mean business later. I checked our position against the sun, such as it was, and did the math I didn't like: two days to the pass at our pace, maybe three if the weather closed, and the weather intended to close. Behind us and below, faint through the trees, I heard it — or thought I heard it, which amounts to the same thing in the mountains — the distant insect whine of engines on the forest road. More than one.

"Rule four," I said.

"You said there was no rule three."

"There is now. Rule three: when I say move, you move, and you don't ask where. Rule four: if we get separated, you go up. Always up. People search valleys. Nobody searches above treeline unless they're crazy, and the navy hires sane people, it's cheaper on the insurance."

"And if you don't come?"

I tightened the strap on the gear bag and didn't answer fast enough, and he saw it, and something passed between us that neither of us named — the understanding that "if you don't come" was a real question with real odds attached, and that I had just, without meaning to, become someone whose absence would cost him something.

"Then you go up," I said finally, "and you keep going, and you don't wait. That's the whole rule. That's rules five through fifty."

"That's a terrible rule."

"It's the only rule that keeps people alive."

"No," he said, adjusting the dead surveyor's parka around his shoulders, and there was a stubbornness in his jaw that I would learn to respect and dread in equal measure. "The rule that keeps people alive is that nobody gets left. You had it backwards. You've had everything backwards, I think. That's okay. I'm patient."

Above us the snow thickened, and below us the engine sounds faded or didn't, and ahead the pass waited under its white lid like a question nobody wanted asked. The compass, I didn't check, but I knew — I could feel it the way you feel weather coming — was pointing forward, steady as a pulse, toward whatever it was we were walking into.

And for the first time in twenty years of miles, I noticed I wasn't counting them anymore.

* * *

We made the tree line by dark, which was either good navigation or bad luck, depending on how you felt about sleeping at ten thousand feet in October. There was a miner's cabin — half a cabin, really, one wall gone to rot, the rest holding — tucked against a granite shoulder, and I called it home for the night the way you call a punch thrown: too late to matter.

Isandro was asleep in minutes, the way the young sleep, completely, like falling. I sat up keeping the watch I'd kept for twenty years, alone in the doorway of a dying building, listening to the snow tick against the rocks.

Except I wasn't alone. That was the new arithmetic. There was a boy breathing six feet behind me whose father's name might be sitting in a sealed hold at the bottom of an inlet, and a brass instrument in his pocket that claimed to know what I feared, and somewhere down in the dark two sets of headlights were working the forest road methodically, patient as tide.

I'd spent two decades learning how to disappear. Nobody had ever taught me what to do with someone worth appearing for.

The snow kept falling. Somewhere below, the engines stopped — or went quiet, which is worse — and I sat in the doorway of the broken cabin and began, reluctantly, against every instinct I owned, to build a list of rules for keeping two people alive instead of one.

Rule one, I decided, was going to have to change.
