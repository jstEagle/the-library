## 8. The Ferryman's Daughter

**Bram**

The river found us before we found it.

You could hear it for a mile through the trees—that low, muscular mutter of water moving with somewhere to be—and then the forest simply gave up and there it was: the Kestrel, running high and gray-green with snowmelt, wide as a highway and about as interested in conversation. On the far bank, a cable sagged between two timber towers, and hanging from the cable was a flat-bottomed scow the size of a barn door.

A hand-painted sign leaned against a stump at the landing. FERRY — 2 DOLLARS WALK-ON, 5 WITH PACKS, 10 IF YOU'RE TROUBLE.

"Somebody's got a sense of humor," Isandro said.

"Somebody's got a mortgage," I said. "Humor costs extra."

I'll admit I'd been watching the boy all morning, and not just because he was my responsibility now, God help me. It was the way he walked. Two days inland from the coast, two days since we'd burned our own boat's papers and buried our own tracks, and most people would've been hunched and hunted by now. Isandro walked like the ground owed him money. He kept checking the compass in his pocket—not reading it, exactly. Consulting it. The way you'd check on a sick relative.

"Which way's it pointing?" I asked, because apparently I'd become the kind of man who asked that question over breakfast.

"Across," he said. "It's been pointing across since last night."

"Meaning what you're afraid of losing is on the other side of a river you haven't seen yet."

"Meaning I don't know." He said it without drama, which somehow made it worse. "It pointed at the wreck for weeks. Then at you. Now it points east of north, steady, like it knows something."

I didn't care for that. Compasses aren't supposed to know things. Compasses are supposed to be dumb iron and honest needles, the one piece of equipment in a diver's kit that never lies to you, because it can't. This one had opinions. I'd have thrown it in the Kestrel myself if it weren't the only map either of us had.

The ferry landing was empty but lived-in. A woodpile stacked with the fussy precision of someone who expected to be old soon. A kettle on a hook over a cold fire ring. A dog—some rangy shepherd mix with gray around its muzzle—rose from beside the woodpile, considered us at length, and decided we were beneath barking.

"You want the crossing or not?" said a voice from up the path. "Because the light's going in about three hours, and I don't run the wire after dark. Not this time of year, not with the water up."

She came down through the alders carrying a coil of rope over one shoulder like it weighed nothing, which told me she'd been doing it her whole life. Mid-twenties maybe. Dark hair cut short and practical, work jacket gone soft as cloth can get, hands that had rope calluses where other women had rings. She looked at me the way dockhands look at weather—assessing tonnage, estimating trouble.

"Two walkers, one pack," she said. "Seven dollars."

"We've got four," I said, "and a question."

"Everybody's got a question. Questions are free. Crossing's seven."

"The road behind us," I said. "You see anybody come through? Government types. Civvies with new boots and no dirt on them."

Something moved behind her eyes. Not fear—I knew fear when I saw it, wore it daily like a coat. This was older and more tired than fear. Recognition.

"How many?" she said.

"Six that I counted. Maybe eight."

"And they were asking about—" she stopped herself. Looked at Isandro. Looked at me again, longer. "Get on the boat," she said. "Both of you. Now."

"That wasn't the arrangement—"

"The arrangement changed when you opened your mouth. Get on the boat or get back in the trees, but don't stand on my landing being followed."

We got on the boat.

* * *

**Isandro**

Her name was Odile Marchetti, and she ran the ferry the way Bram ran his dives: alone, precisely, and like she expected the world to try something.

The scow rode a trolley on the main cable, and she worked it with a hand-cranked winch and a steering sweep, reading the current out loud to herself—*heavy on the starboard quarter, hold her, hold*—in a voice so calm it sounded bored. The river shoved at us the whole way across. Water came over the toe rails in cold ropes. Bram stood braced with his feet apart like he was on a deck he trusted, which was a lie, because I'd seen him look at the cable anchors twice and do arithmetic with his face.

Halfway across, Odile said, without turning around: "The men you're running from. They have a boat with an outboard, tan canvas over the console?"

Bram went very still. "You've seen them."

"I've seen them ask questions at the gas station in Millbrook yesterday. And I've seen their kind before." She hauled the winch another quarter turn. "Twenty years ago, I saw their kind before too. Different trucks, same boots."

The far landing came up fast. She threw a loop over the mooring post, snugged it, and only then turned to face us, arms crossed on the sweep handle.

"Now. You're going to tell me who you are, and you're going to tell me straight, because I've spent my whole life hauling liars across this river and I can smell one coming upstream."

"We're nobody," Bram started.

"Try again."

And here's the thing I keep learning about Bram Galloway, the thing that doesn't fit on any chart: he lies beautifully to everyone except when it matters, and then he can't lie at all. He looked at this woman he'd known for ten minutes, and whatever was in his face made her go quiet before he even spoke.

"There's a wreck off the coast," he said. "Old transport. Sank forty-some years back. Official story says it went down empty, running light, crew lost. That's not true. There were passengers. A few hundred of them. People the government had already written off as dead on paper years earlier, so sinking them for real was tidy bookkeeping." He swallowed. "There's a manifest. Names. We've got copies. The navy wants the names to stay lost, and they're willing to lose a couple more people to keep it that way."

Odile didn't move. The river talked underneath us, patient as anything.

"Why are you telling me this?" she said finally. "You don't know me. You met me twenty minutes ago. Smart runners don't hand their secrets to ferrymen."

"No," Bram agreed. "Smart runners don't."

"So why?"

He didn't answer. I did, because somebody had to, and because I'd watched him fail to answer and understood why. Some things you can't say while looking at the person they might break.

"Because your father's name is on it," I said.

* * *

**Bram**

I want it on the record that I told the boy not to say that.

Not in those words—I hadn't known it myself yet—but I'd felt it building in him ever since the river, the compass twitching in his pocket every time Odile leaned on the sweep. He'd been doing the math the whole crossing, the terrible arithmetic that compass does, pointing at what its holder fears losing, and I'd watched him arrive at the answer and watched him decide to say it out loud anyway, because kindness is the one rule of movement he has never once managed to follow.

Odile Marchetti stood very still on her own boat, in the middle of her own river, and said, "What did you just say?"

"The manifest," Isandro said gently, the way you talk to someone standing on ice. "Your father. He was a fisherman, right? Out of Millbrook? Listed drowned in—"

"Don't." Her voice cracked like a whip over water. "Don't you dare stand on my ferry and recite my family to me like a—like a cargo list."

"He's on it," Isandro said. "Emile Marchetti. Page three. I've read it so many times I know the pages. I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. But he's on it, and he didn't drown fishing, and somebody decided you'd never know, and we're carrying the proof, and those men behind us are carrying gasoline."

The silence went on long enough that the current swung the scow a degree on its mooring and she had to correct it without thinking, hands moving on their own while the rest of her stood somewhere else entirely. Forty years of grief, I thought, sitting in a body like ballast. I know that weight. I've dived with it.

"My mother died believing he was lost in a squall off the Kestrel bar," Odile said at last, flat and precise, each word set down like a stone. "She lit a lamp in the window every night for eleven years. Eleven years, until the arthritis took her hands and then everything else. For a *squall*." She looked up, and her eyes were dry, which frightened me more than tears would have. "Show me."

Isandro took the folded pages from the oilskin wallet inside his jacket—the real ones, the ones we'd risked the dive for—and handed them across. I watched her read. I've watched a lot of people read a lot of documents, salvage claims and insurance riders and once, memorably, a coroner's report with my own name misspelled in it, and I can tell you that paper is never just paper. Paper is a door. You watch a person read and you can see which doors open and which doors they nail shut.

She read page three. She read it twice. Her thumb stayed on one line the whole second pass, pressing into the paper hard enough to blanch it, as if she could hold the name in place by force.

Then she folded the manifest along its existing creases, exactly, carefully, the way you fold a flag, and handed it back, and said:

"The winch needs oiling and the dog needs feeding, and then I'm coming with you."

"Whoa," I said. "No. Absolutely not. We appreciate the crossing, the seven dollars stands, but this isn't a—"

"A what? A club?" She was already off the boat, striding up the landing, talking over her shoulder. "You think I'm asking to join? I'm telling you the route. You're heading for the range, and you're heading for it on foot, and you don't know these valleys, and there's exactly one way across the Kestrel headwaters that doesn't end with you swimming in May meltwater, and I'm the only living soul who's worked that crossing since my grandfather built the lower tower. So it's not a request, and it's not a favor, and you can stop making that face, diver, because I've hauled drunk loggers and armed poachers and one federal fisheries officer across this river, and you three are the least trouble I've carried all year."

"She's coming," Isandro said to me, delighted, insufferable.

"She's coming," I agreed, because I'd been in the water long enough to know a riptide when I was caught in one.

* * *

**Isandro**

We left at first light, because Odile said the contractors would check the ferry landing by mid-morning and she wanted to be a rumor by then. She left the ferry locked and the sign flipped to CLOSED FOR SEASON, which she said would buy a day, maybe two, since nobody questioned a ferry operator's judgment about ice-out.

Before we left she did one strange thing. She went to the woodpile, pried up the bottom course, and took out a tin box. Inside was a photograph—a young man on the deck of a fishing boat, laughing at whoever held the camera, one hand shading his eyes—and a lamp key on a chain.

"For the window," she said, when she caught me watching. "After she died, I kept lighting it. Stupid habit." She closed the tin and put it in her pack, next to the water bottle, where a person keeps things they need often. "Not stupid. Just unfinished."

We climbed away from the river through cedar and slide alder, Odile setting a pace that made both of us ashamed of ourselves. By noon we were above the valley fog, and she'd renamed half our gear (*that's not a pack, that's a coffin with straps*) and reorganized all of it, and argued Bram out of his planned route through the gorge on the grounds that the gorge floods in spring and the contractors knew it too.

"You argue like a tugboat," Bram told her.

"You navigate like a tourist," she said, and he almost smiled, which for Bram is roughly equivalent to a marriage proposal.

That evening, camped under an overhang with the Kestrel's tributaries talking below us, I checked the compass out of habit. It had settled overnight into a fixed bearing, steady as a preached sermon: south by east. Toward the low country. Toward towns, roads, records.

"What's it saying?" Odile asked. She'd taken the news of the compass with remarkable calm—*my father's ghost is on a manifest and your pocket weather vane points at grief, sure, fine, I once ferried a piano*—but she watched it now with narrowed eyes, the way you watch weather that hasn't decided yet.

"Toward Alder Creek," I said. "I think. There's a regional archive there. Bram won't talk about why I think that."

"I'm standing right here," Bram said from the fire.

"You're pretending to sleep."

"I'm resting my eyes strategically."

Odile laughed—a short, surprised bark, like it had escaped custody—and then went quiet, feeding a stick to the flames. "Alder Creek has a records office," she said slowly. "My mother used to order land maps from it. Small place. One clerk, mostly." She looked up, and the firelight did something complicated to her face. "If those names get entered into public record—if they're read, filed, indexed—then they happened. Officially. Forever. Nobody can burn a filing cabinet in a town full of witnesses without it becoming a bigger story than the files."

"That's the idea," Bram said quietly.

"Then we should know something," Odile said, "before we commit to it. Because I've been thinking about those six men with the clean boots, and here's what bothers me. They weren't following you. Not really. They were following the *route*. Like they already knew where you'd have to go." She poked the fire. "Somebody drew them a map."

The compass sat in my palm, needle steady, patient, pointing south toward a small town with one clerk, and for the first time since the wreck I wondered whether it was guiding us—or baiting us.

Above the ridge to the north, faint but unmistakable, an engine droned. Then another. Holding formation. Moving slow, the way you move when you're not searching anymore.

When you've already found.
