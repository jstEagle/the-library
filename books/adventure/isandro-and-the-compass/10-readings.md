## 10. Readings

The fire lookout sat on a bald knob above the tree line like an old man on a park bench—legs splayed, paint peeling, watching everything and judging nothing. Odile found it by accident while scouting water, and Bram grumbled the whole climb up about sightlines and smoke and how anything that could be seen from could be seen *to*, which was Bram-speak for *I don't like it here* and also, apparently, for *we're staying anyway because my knee has opinions now.*

So we stayed.

The cabin at the top was small enough that three people in it meant negotiation. There was a steel table bolted to the floor, a map case full of charts so old they showed roads that had washed out before I was born, and a logbook from 1987 where some long-gone watcher had recorded, day after day, entries like *Clear. Nothing.* and *Smoke NE of Miller Creek. Nothing.* Two hundred pages of nothing. I read half of them the first night and decided whoever he'd been, I understood him completely. Some jobs are just permission to watch the world without being part of it.

Bram would have loved it here, I thought. Then corrected myself: Bram already did love it here. He just hadn't admitted it yet.

The compass lived in my jacket's inside pocket, against my chest, where I could feel it through the fabric like a second heartbeat. It had been pointing at Odile since Kettle Bridge. Before that, the wreck. It had never once pointed north, which meant every map I'd ever learned to read was useless to it, or it was useless to every map, and I hadn't decided which insult fit better.

Here is what nobody tells you about a thing like that: you stop trusting your own eyes. You hold it level and watch the needle swing toward a person—a girl carrying firewood, for God's sake—and part of your brain says *magnetism, anomaly, cheap brass,* and the other part says *it knows something you don't,* and the two parts fight until you're too tired to think at all.

I lasted four days.

* * *

It was Odile who gave me the opening, though she didn't know it. She'd spread her father's copy of the manifest page—the one she'd taken from the wreck's strongbox, the one with REYES, E. printed in a clerk's indifferent type—flat on the steel table under the lamp, smoothing it over and over like she could iron the dead back into shape.

"These were typed," she said. "Typed means filed. Filed means copies."

"Carbon paper," Bram said from his bunk, not opening his eyes. "Triplicate, if the navy was feeling thorough."

"So somewhere there are more." Her finger traced the column of names without touching them, the way you'd trace a scar. "Somewhere this exists twice more, and both times somebody signed off on it. Somebody read these names and stamped them *lost at sea* when they weren't even—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "When the state knew exactly where they were."

Bram said nothing. He was very good at nothing. Twenty years of practice, probably.

I lay in my bunk with the compass against my ribs and felt it do something I hadn't felt before.

Not move. Not spin. More like—lean. The way a dog leans into a door it wants opened. A pressure, faint and directional, not toward Odile this time but toward the paper itself, toward the names, and past the names toward—

I sat up.

"What," Bram said. He hadn't been asleep. He was never asleep. I was starting to suspect sleep was another thing he'd traded away along with his name.

"Nothing," I said, which was my first lie to him, and it went down easier than I wanted it to.

* * *

The next morning Bram and Odile went down to the creek to run the filter and argue about rations—arguing about rations was their love language, I'd figured out; it meant things were stable—and I stayed behind with the map case and the compass and the specific kind of quiet that only exists in buildings built for one person to be alone in.

I told myself I was just going to look at it.

I took it out and set it on the steel table. Brass case, glass clouded at the edges, needle dark as a splinter. On the table, away from my body, it didn't point at me anymore. It pointed south-southwest, steady as a fence post, out over the charts and down through the floorboards and the mountain and whatever lay beyond.

Toward the wreck? We were two days inland and climbing. Could a thing point through rock?

I got out the old survey charts. Found our position by matching ridgelines—thank you, Mr. Halloran, grade eight geography, the one class where you said the teacher's name out loud in your head for the rest of your life. Drew a line along the needle's bearing with a pencil stub I found in the map case.

The line ran through the coast. Through the bay. Through the wreck, near enough that pencil-width forgave the difference.

Okay. Okay. So it pointed home when I wasn't holding it. That was almost normal. That was almost *instrument behavior.* I could file that. I could put that on a shelf next to *the needle trembles near Odile* and pretend the shelf wasn't buckling.

Then I put the manifest page on the table.

I want to be honest about what happened, because later—when it mattered, when people asked—I needed to have told myself the truth at least once. I didn't plan the experiment. But I didn't not-plan it either. I saw the page folded in Odile's pack where she'd left it drying, and I thought *just to see,* and "just to see" is the sentence every disaster in history has started with.

The moment the paper crossed onto the table, the needle moved.

Not swung—*shivered*. Like a tuning fork struck against its twin. It held its bearing but it vibrated, fast and fine, humming against the pivot in little ticks I could feel through the table if I pressed my palm flat. And the closer I brought the manifest, the harder it shook, until the page was six inches from the compass and the needle was practically dancing, straining, leaning into the paper like—

Like a needle to ore. That was the phrase that came to me, standing alone in a fire lookout with my heart going like a trapped bird. My grandfather had prospected for two seasons before the mine took him, and he used to say a good needle over a good vein doesn't point, it *wants*. It wants so bad it shakes.

The compass wanted the manifest.

No. I made myself sit with it, because the easy version was wrong. It wasn't the paper. Paper is paper. I waved a page of the 1987 logbook over it—nothing. A chart—nothing. Even the corner of the manifest itself, torn blank, nothing.

It was the names. The typed names, the filed names, the names that existed in triplicate somewhere in the world, waiting in some drawer to be read or destroyed. The compass didn't care about the object. It cared about the *where*. Somewhere out there under that bearing line, other copies of these names sat in the dark, and the compass knew it the way water knows downhill.

It wants the names delivered somewhere, I thought. And then, colder: Or it wants them kept from somewhere. I couldn't tell which. Wanting and warning feel the same from the inside.

I did what any reasonable person does with a discovery that rearranges the furniture of the universe.

I did it again.

* * *

By the time their voices came up the trail I had a whole grid scratched into the top of the steel table—distances, angles, tick-rates, the works, written in pencil on the back of a 1987 chart of *nothing*. I'd learned three things:

One: the reaction scaled with proximity, but not evenly. At arm's length it was a murmur. At six inches, a shout. Whatever the mechanism was, it wasn't magnetism, because I turned the manifest every direction and the needle didn't care about orientation, only distance. It wasn't reading the paper. It was reading *through* it, to wherever its twins lived.

Two: when I covered the manifest with my hand, the shaking stopped. Completely. Instantly. Like the names needed a witness to count. I don't know how else to say it. Hidden names weighed nothing. Seen names pulled.

Three—and this is the one that still sits wrong in my chest—when I held the compass itself, bare-handed, over the page, the pull changed direction. It stopped pointing at the horizon and pointed at *me*. At my hand. At the skin touching the brass.

For four days it had pointed at Odile. For weeks before that, at the wreck. Now, holding it over the names, it pointed at Isandro Reyes, son of Eduardo, line forty-one of a list the government called drowned.

I set it down very carefully, the way you set down something that might be asleep.

That's when the ladder creaked.

* * *

Bram came through the hatch first, which was usual, and stopped, which was not. He had a way of reading rooms that I think was really just reading people, and he read me in about a second and a half—the chart on the table, the manifest squared beside it, the compass sitting between us like a confession.

"Odile," he said, without turning around. "Take the water down slow. Check the tie-off."

"I checked the tie-off an hour—"

"Check it again."

Her boots went down the ladder. She knew. Everybody always knew. That was the thing about Bram's silences—they came with subtitles.

He didn't touch anything. He stood with his hands in his pockets and looked at the table the way he must have looked at wrecks: assessing, pricing, deciding what was worth the risk of reaching for.

"How long," he said.

"An hour. Maybe less."

"And?"

And. He wanted data. Fine. Data was safer than the other thing I wanted to talk about, which was what it meant that the needle had turned on me.

"It reacts to the manifest. Only the manifest. Distance-based, not magnetic—it doesn't care about orientation. When I cover the names, it stops. When I uncover them, it pulls." I swallowed. "There are carbon copies, Bram. Triplicate. It's not pointing at the paper. It's pointing at wherever the other copies are. Southwest. Down through the ridge."

He was quiet long enough that I heard the wind change pitch outside, working itself up toward weather.

"You drew a bearing," he said finally. Not a question. He'd seen the pencil line.

"On the back of the '87 chart. It's—"

"I know what's on the back of the '87 chart. I've read all of it. Twice." He came closer, and I braced for shouting, because that's what adults did in my experience when you broke rules they hadn't gotten around to making yet. Instead he picked up the compass, turned it over once, and set it back down with a gentleness that scared me more than shouting would have. "You held it."

"Yeah."

"Over the page."

"Yeah."

"What did it do."

I could have lied again. The lie was right there, pre-cut, easy. *Nothing much. Same as always.* And maybe he'd have believed it, because I was getting good at lying, which is a horrible thing to get good at when you're fifteen and the person you're lying to is the only reason you're warm.

But the needle had taught me something in that hour, something I couldn't unlearn: hidden things weigh nothing. Covered names have no pull. It was only the ones brought into the open that moved the world.

"It pointed at me," I said. "At my hand. While I was touching it over the names."

Something crossed Bram's face then that I'd never seen on it. Not fear exactly—he was too well-built for fear, the way a seawall is built for storms—but recognition. The look of a man seeing a wave he'd been telling himself wouldn't come.

"That's why you don't experiment," he said quietly. "It's not a tool, Isandro. Tools wait for instructions. This thing *negotiates*. Every time you use it, you're not learning what it does. You're teaching it what you'll give it."

"It's a compass. It doesn't take."

"Everything takes." He said it flat, no drama, a man quoting his own invoice. "You think I don't know? I spent twenty years taking contracts that took pieces of me and called it freedom because I set the price myself." He tapped the table once, beside the compass, not touching it. "This morning it points at Odile. Tonight it points at you. Ask yourself what it's mapping, kid. It's not finding what we fear losing. It's *keeping score*."

The word landed hard because it was true, and we both knew it, and neither of us said the rest: keeping score of what? What was I afraid of losing? I'd boarded a government survey boat as a stowaway with a duffel bag and a grudge. I'd already lost everything there was to lose.

Except I hadn't, had I. Because the needle had turned, and things only turn when the target moves.

"No more experiments," Bram said. "Not readings, not tests, not 'just to see.' It goes in the bottom of my kit, wrapped, and it stays there until Alder Creek or wherever we end up, and then it goes to someone whose job is instruments, not kids." He held out his hand.

And here's the part I've turned over a thousand times since. I picked it up first. Just to hand it to him—that's all, lift and pass—but the moment my skin met the brass, the needle woke and swung, smooth and certain, and settled pointing directly at Bram Galloway.

Not at the manifest. Not southwest toward the buried copies.

At him.

Bram looked at it. Looked at me. And I watched twenty years of practiced anonymity fail to find anywhere to hide on his face.

"Give it here," he said, and his voice was perfectly level, and his hand was not.

I gave it to him. He wrapped it in the oilcloth from his kit without another word, stowed it deep, and went out to check the tie-off himself, twice, though Odile had already done it.

I sat alone in the lookout with my grid of numbers and the 1987 man's two hundred pages of *nothing*, and I understood, finally, what the compass had been doing all along. It had never been pointing at what we feared losing.

It had been pointing at what was about to be taken.

Outside, far to the southwest, under the ridge line, thunder rolled—except the sky in that quarter was clear blue, and the sound came again, lower, rhythmic, and Bram appeared in the doorway with his face gone the color of the fog off the wreck.

"Helicopters," he said. "More than one. Coming up the valley."
