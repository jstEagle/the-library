## 11. The Man Who Measures Miles

The boy asked me last night, somewhere past the second river crossing, why I counted miles instead of years. He asked it the way kids ask things—sideways, while doing something else, in this case trying to dry his socks over a fire that was mostly smoke and ambition.

"Because miles are honest," I said. "A mile is a mile no matter how bad the year was."

He looked at me like I'd handed him a coin from a country that didn't exist, and then he went back to his socks, and I thought that would be the end of it. Kids don't press. They file things away like magpies.

But it's been sitting on my chest since then, that question, and I've learned—recently, against my will—that when something sits on your chest long enough, you either cough it up or drown. So here it is. The story I've never told anyone, told now to a fire and a sleeping boy and a woman keeping watch who is pretending not to listen, which is its own kind of kindness.

Twenty-two years ago I had a name people used out loud.

* * *

I was twenty-six, and I was good. Not competent-good, not steady-paycheck-good—I was the diver other divers called when the job had teeth. Cold water, deep water, water with history in it. My name then was Bram Galloway, same as now, but back then it meant something specific: it meant you could hand me a contract with a hazard clause three pages long and I'd sign it without reading past the rate.

The fixer who brought me the job was named Corliss. Small man, expensive coat, hands that never fully warmed up. He'd brokered four jobs for me before, all salvage, all clean. This one he described as "recovery," which should have been my first warning. Salvage pulls things up. Recovery puts things down.

"The site's a freighter off Cape Verity," he said, sliding a photograph across the table of a dockside bar where the bartender knew better than to look at us. "Sank eleven years ago. Officially she went down light—ballast, machinery, nothing worth the insurance company's time. Unofficially, there's a compartment aft that wasn't on her registry."

"What's in it?"

"That's not your question."

"It's always my question."

Corliss smiled the way men smile when they're deciding whether you're an asset or a liability. "Your question is depth, bottom conditions, and whether you can get into a sealed compartment at forty fathoms without cutting torches, because torches make noise and noise makes paperwork. Can you?"

Forty fathoms. Two hundred forty feet, on mixed gas, alone, into a compartment nobody would admit existed. The rate he quoted was triple anything I'd earned in a season. I signed. I want to be clear about that part—I signed with my eyes open to everything except the one thing that mattered, because I was young enough to believe that what I didn't know couldn't bill me later.

* * *

The dive itself was beautiful. That's the obscene part, the part I still can't square. Three days of prep, a borrowed tender anchored off a coastline so empty the charts just gave up and drew squiggles, and then down through water that went from green to black like a door closing.

The freighter sat upright on the silt, her hull furred with twenty years of growth, and she was—there's no other word for it—peaceful. Wrecks always are, at first. The sea does a kind of embalming. It's only when you start opening things that the dead remember they have opinions.

The unregistered compartment was aft, below the engine room, accessible through a maintenance trunk that someone had welded shut from the outside after the ship went down. Welded underwater. Which meant somebody had come back. Somebody had cared enough about what was inside to seal it twice—once with steel, once with silence.

My instructions were simple: enter, confirm contents, place a marker beacon, exit. Do not remove anything. Do not photograph anything. Confirm and mark. Even at twenty-six I understood that "confirm and mark" was the language of men planning a return visit they didn't want witnessed.

The welds were old but shallow—done in a hurry, by someone working cold and scared. I got through the seam with a hydraulic grinder in ninety minutes of bottom time that felt like ninety minutes inside a struck bell. And then I was through, into a space maybe thirty feet long, and my light found the cargo.

Crates. Dozens of them, stacked with care, stenciled with numbers instead of names. And between the crates, lashed to the bulkheads because whoever loaded them expected rough handling—personal effects. Suitcases. A child's coat folded on top of one crate like it had been set down for a moment and never picked back up.

I'm a salvage diver. I've pulled up engines, ore, ordnance, safes, and once—and this is true—a grand piano, which is a story for a different night and a better bottle. I know cargo. Cargo doesn't come with coats folded on top of it.

I opened one crate. Just one. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth, were documents—waterlogged, fused into bricks, but legible at the edges. Manifests. Passenger manifests. Names, ages, ports of origin. A transport manifest for a ship that officially carried ballast.

Eleven years earlier, a vessel full of people had gone down off Cape Verity, and the state had written them off as machinery, and then someone had come back and welded the door shut on the whole arithmetic of it.

I did what the contract said. I confirmed. I marked. I ascended.

And then I made the mistake that cost me my name.

* * *

I took a photograph.

One frame, shot through the open seam before I resealed my entry cut with a patch plate—the professional thing to do, leave the site as found. But my camera was in my hand and the child's coat was in frame and I thought, in the arrogance of twenty-six, that evidence was a form of protection. That a man holding proof is a man who can't be disappeared.

I surfaced, I decompressed through the long gray hours hanging on the line, and I came aboard and Corliss was waiting on my deck with two other men I'd never seen, and the look on his face told me the photograph had already stopped being mine.

Here's what I understand now, twenty-two years and too many miles later, that I didn't understand then: they weren't angry. Anger implies surprise. These men were performing a procedure. I was a variable that had produced an unexpected output, and the procedure for unexpected outputs was well established.

They took the camera. They took the film. Then Corliss sat down across from me in my own wheelhouse, poured two glasses of the cheap whisky I kept for celebrating, and explained my future to me.

"You're going to report the site unrecoverable," he said. "Compartment flooded, structure collapsed, access impossible. You're going to say it convincingly, because you're a convincing man. In exchange, you keep your certification, your boat, and your health. There's a naval review board in six weeks that will ask you questions, and you'll answer them the way we've discussed, and then this will be over."

"And if I don't?"

He didn't threaten me. That's what I keep coming back to, all these years. He never once threatened me. He just said, "Bram. Look at this honestly. Who do you tell? A newspaper? They'll run it for a week and then their lawyers will get a letter. The families? We've already spoken to the families—or rather, to the absence of families, because these people were chosen partly for having few. You have a photograph of a coat. We have a government. Go home, sleep, and be sensible."

And the worst part—the part I've never said out loud—is that for about ten seconds, I was sensible. I looked at the arithmetic and it was airtight. One man, one photo, one state. The math didn't just favor them; it was them.

Then I thought about the coat. Folded. Someone had folded it, set it down gently in a hold full of people the state had decided were cargo, and I thought: if I say unrecoverable, then that fold is the last act of tenderness those people ever get, and it'll be welded shut behind steel until the hull rusts through, and no one will ever know it happened.

"I'll say it," I told Corliss. "Unrecoverable. Collapsed. Impossible."

He smiled and stood to go, and I said, "But I want it on record between us—you and me, no paper—that I know what's down there. I want you to carry that around. Not as a threat. As weight."

Corliss paused at the door of my wheelhouse, and for one moment the broker's polish dropped and I saw the tired functionary underneath, a man who also carried things he'd been paid to forget.

"Everyone says that," he said quietly. "It doesn't make the steel any thinner."

* * *

The review board lasted a day and a half. I testified that the compartment was structurally compromised beyond safe entry—which was even half true, since I'd had to cut my way in. A commander with a face like a closed ledger thanked me for my service to maritime safety. My name went into a transcript that was sealed, and my reputation went into a smaller, quieter box: reliable, discreet, asks no questions.

That last one became my brand. I built it deliberately, stone by stone, the way you build a wall when you've learned that windows let things in.

Stone one: I stopped diving wrecks with histories. Freighters, yes. Ore barges, yes. Anything with a passenger list, no. Divers noticed. Divers talk. Within two years the story was that Galloway was superstitious, and superstition is a respectable flaw—it costs you nothing but certain jobs, and those were the jobs I didn't want anyway.

Stone two: I stopped keeping a home address. Not because they were hunting me—they weren't; I was far more useful free than gone—but because a man with no address can't be summoned, and a man who can't be summoned starts to believe he's free. It's a lie, but it's a load-bearing lie, and I lived in it for two decades.

Stone three, the keystone: I started counting miles. Distance is the one measurement that never argues with you. A year can be wasted, stolen, ruined. A mile is just a mile—swum, sailed, walked, it adds up the same, indifferent and clean. I measured my life in nautical miles logged, bottom time accumulated, feet descended. Numbers that went up and never asked me what they were for.

People assumed it was eccentricity. Old divers are allowed their rituals. Nobody guessed the ritual was a coffin I was building for myself at a comfortable pace, nailing in one plank per season.

* * *

Here's the thing about walls, though, and I learned this only weeks ago, freezing on a mountainside next to a boy who keeps being kind at tactically inappropriate moments: a wall doesn't just keep things out. It keeps you small. Every stone I laid made the space inside more manageable and less livable, until the space was exactly the size of one man and his boat and his rules, and I called that freedom because the alternative was calling it what it was.

Corliss died eight years ago—heart, ordinary, unremarkable, the kind of death the state grants its functionaries. I read the obituary in a trade paper and felt nothing, and I counted that nothing as a victory. Another stone.

But the wreck off Cape Verity never left me. I've dreamed it maybe two hundred times: the seam opening under my grinder, the light spilling in, the coat. Always the coat. In the dreams I sometimes pick it up, and it's warm, which is impossible, and I wake with my heart going like a bilge pump and I lie there telling myself it's just nitrogen memory, just old gas in old blood, nothing down there but steel and silt.

Except it isn't nitrogen memory. It's testimony. I've been carrying a deposition for twenty-two years, sworn to no one, filed nowhere, and I called that discretion. I called it professionalism. I called it survival.

What I actually did was exactly what Corliss paid me to do. I forgot on schedule. I forgot so thoroughly that when Isandro held that brass compass in the wreck's hold and it swung toward the crates—toward the manifests, toward the names—I felt something worse than fear. I felt recognition. Because the compass does what I refused to do. It points at the thing you most fear losing, and for twenty-two years the thing I most feared losing was the knowledge of what I'd helped bury. Losing it was the deal. Forgetting was the fee. And I paid it every single day, in miles, because miles were the only currency that didn't remind me of the purchase.

* * *

The fire has burned down to a red argument with the dark. Odile has heard all of it—I can tell by the way she isn't looking at me, the careful stillness of someone giving a wound room to breathe. Isandro is asleep, or performing sleep, socks abandoned in a tragic wet heap.

I should stop there. That's the whole story: dive, discovery, photograph, procedure, wall. Twenty-two years compressed into one bad night and two decades of deliberate forgetting.

But there's an epilogue, and the epilogue is why I'm telling this now instead of taking it into whatever grave the navy has probably already started drafting for me.

Three days ago, in the hold of that wreck—the same wreck, or its twin, and I no longer believe in coincidence at that scale—I watched a sixteen-year-old stowaway unwrap oilcloth from a waterlogged brick of paper and read a name off a manifest, and then another, searching, searching, and I knew before he said it what name he was looking for, because I've seen that search before. I ran it myself, silently, in that sealed compartment at forty fathoms, running my finger down a fused page looking for a name I could carry out. I never found one. I told myself that made it easier.

It didn't. It just meant I carried all of them.

So here's what I've decided, here in the dark, above the snowline, with contractors somewhere below us and the compass ticking its quiet mad arithmetic in the boy's pocket: the wall comes down. Not all at once—I'm too old for demolition, and some stones are load-bearing if I want to keep us alive. But the keystone goes. The rule that says never ask who owns what you pull up, never ask what the cargo means, never let the dead become your problem.

Because they are my problem. They have been since I was twenty-six and signed a contract with my eyes open to everything except the one thing that mattered. I was paid to forget, and I collected in full, and the interest has compounded every mile since.

Somewhere south of here there's an archive with a filing desk, and somewhere north of here there are men who burn camps, and between those two facts lies a stack of manifests that have waited eleven years longer than they should have—twenty-two, if you count from Cape Verity—for someone to stop measuring the distance and start closing it.

Isandro stirred in his sleep and murmured something. The compass, I noticed, had worked its way out of his jacket pocket and lay on the ground between us, needle trembling, settling, finding its bearing.

I didn't touch it. But I looked at where it pointed, and for the first time in twenty-two years, the direction didn't frighten me.

It pointed at him. And tomorrow we'd walk toward whatever that meant—together, into weather that wanted us dead, carrying names that somebody powerful wanted forgotten.

I'd spent my whole life learning how to disappear.

Time, finally, to learn the other thing.
