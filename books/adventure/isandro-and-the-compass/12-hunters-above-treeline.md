## 12. Hunters Above Treeline

My father used to say the mountains don't hate you, they just don't know you're there. He said it like it was a comfort. Twenty years of running a ferry crossing will teach you a certain kind of patience, and patience teaches you that most men who talk about nature's indifference have never had to pull a drowned boy out of cold water and explain to his mother why the rope was frayed.

I'd known Bram Galloway for four days when the contractors found us, and I already understood him better than I understood most people I'd ferried for decades. He counted everything in miles. Distance to the pass. Distance to water. Distance, I suspected, between himself and whatever he was afraid of feeling. Isandro counted in names — the ones on the manifests, the one he hadn't found yet, his father's. And me? I counted in what I could carry.

That morning I was carrying too much.

We'd come up out of the river valley before dawn, following an old mining track Bram swore was on no map worth printing. The trees had been thinning all morning, pines giving way to scrub giving way to nothing at all, and by midday we stood at the bottom of a scree slope that went up forever — a river of broken gray stone, thousands of loose slabs stacked by some ancient landslide, waiting for gravity to remember them.

"Two hours up," Bram said, squinting against the glare. "Then the shoulder, then tree line again on the far side. We move quiet and we move steady."

"Quiet," Isandro repeated, like he was testing whether the word would hold his weight.

"You've got hiking boots," Bram told him. "The mountain doesn't care about your feelings."

"The mountain and my father have that in common," Isandro said, and started climbing before either of us could decide if he'd meant it as a joke.

I went last. That was my position in this little expedition — rear guard, pack mule, the adult whose job it was to notice things. Odile the ferrywoman, noticing things. It's how I'd spotted the navy tender burning our camp from across the bay. It's how I'd known the corporal at Kettle Bridge was lying about the road being closed for culvert work, because I'd driven that road every summer of my life and there wasn't a culvert within twenty miles of it. Noticing is not a gift. It's just the habit of assuming the world is trying to kill you and reading its correspondence accordingly.

So when I heard the sound — a single stone clicking against another stone, down and behind us, where nothing should be moving but wind — I stopped.

"Bram."

He didn't turn around. Good man. Turning around tells whoever's watching you that you've noticed them. He just tilted his head, listening, and I watched his shoulders change — the way a man's shoulders change when he stops being a hiker and becomes something older and harder.

"How many?" he asked quietly.

"One. Maybe two. Below us, maybe three hundred yards." I kept my voice flat. "They're good. They waited until we were committed to the slope."

"They're not military," Bram said. "Military announces itself. These are the other kind."

"The kind you used to be?"

"The kind I used to work alongside." He finally turned, and his face did something complicated. "Contractors. Private. They get paid by the result, so they'll be patient and they won't shoot unless shooting is cheaper than not shooting."

Isandro had climbed ten feet above us and stopped, one hand on a slab of rock, looking back down the way we'd come with that expression he had — the one that made me think of my father's dog watching a door it knew someone would eventually walk through. Patient. Certain. Young enough to still believe certainty was a virtue.

"What do we do?" he asked.

Bram looked up the slope. Then down. Then at me, and for the first time since I'd joined them, he looked like a man asking rather than telling. I decided right then I liked him better this way, which was unfortunate, because liking people is a liability above treeline.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about scree: it is alive. Every step shifts. The whole slope is a argument the mountain has been having with itself for ten thousand years, and when you walk on it, you join the argument. Loose stone sings under your boots — a high, chattering whisper that carries further than you'd believe. A hundred climbers can cross a scree field quietly if they're careful. One climber in a hurry sounds like an avalanche rehearsing.

Bram's plan was simple, which is to say it was terrible and also the only plan available. We couldn't outrun professionals downhill — going down scree fast is how you break an ankle and then die of exposure while feeling very stupid. We couldn't hide; there was nothing to hide behind but stones, and stones are honest about what they conceal. So we would climb. Fast, loud, deliberately loud, straight up toward the shoulder, and cut east along the ridgeline where the slope dropped into forest and the forest would swallow us.

"Loud?" Isandro said. "You spent two days teaching me to move quiet."

"That was for roads and trails," Bram said. "This is different. On scree, noise is weather. Everybody makes it. If we sound like three ordinary hikers panicking uphill, we're boring. If we go silent, we're interesting, and interesting gets investigated."

"And if they're faster than us?"

"Then we're having a different conversation." Bram checked the compass — Isandro's compass, the brass thing that pointed at fear instead of north — where it rode in the boy's inner pocket. "Whatever happens, that stays with you. You hear me? Whatever happens."

I should have paid more attention to the way he said *whatever happens*. Twice. Like a man laying a rail ahead of a train he could already see coming.

We climbed.

Scree climbing is a rhythm: plant, test, commit, push. The stone slides and settles and you ride it. Within minutes we were all breathing like bellows, and the slope sang beneath us exactly as Bram intended — a broad, meaningless chorus of shifting rock that any tracker would file under *hikers, fleeing, unremarkable*. I let myself believe it might work. Believing things is another liability of mine. My father called it optimism. My mother called it the family disease.

The first shot came off the rocks below us like a whipcrack, and the bullet hit stone somewhere to our left and screamed away into the sky.

Nobody screamed. I want that recorded somewhere, in whatever ledger keeps accounts of such things: nobody screamed. Bram grabbed Isandro's collar and shoved him sideways behind a house-sized boulder, and I flattened against the slope myself, heart slamming, and the three of us lay among the stones like lizards while the echo of the shot rolled up the valley and died.

"Warning shot," Bram breathed.

"You're sure?"

"Military gives warnings. Contractors give invoices." He peered around the boulder, low, careful. "One man visible. Rifle. He's not advancing. He's herding."

"Herding us where?"

"Away from the ridge. Down-slope, toward the gully." His jaw worked. "There'll be two more in the gully. Classic box. They want us alive — dead men can't tell you where the manifests are, and they need to know if copies exist."

"So we do the opposite of what they want."

"We do something they haven't priced in." He looked at me then, really looked, and I realized he was doing arithmetic with my life in it, and I hated him for it, and I respected him for it, and both those feelings arrived holding hands like twins.

"I ran a ferry for thirty years," I said. "Water does what you tell it if you understand what it wants. Stone's the same. What does this slope want?"

Bram blinked. Then — and I'll treasure this until I die — he smiled. Small, crooked, real.

"It wants down," he said. "Everything here wants down. Including them, once they start moving."

* * *

I'll describe what we did, and it will sound insane, because it was.

Above us, maybe forty yards, the slope steepened into a band of larger slabs — car-sized plates of rock sitting on the loose stuff below like dishes on a badly loaded shelf. Gravity's pantry. Bram had seen it on the way up; so had I; so, presumably, had the contractor with the rifle, which is why he was confident herding us away from it. Nobody starts an avalanche they're standing under.

But we weren't standing under it. We were beside it. And the man with the rifle was below it.

"Odile kicks the lowest slab," Bram said. "Hard, angled left. It goes, it takes the next one, the shelf lets go. Everything comes down the fall line — there—" he pointed to a shallow chute forty feet to our right, "—and misses us by a margin I'd call generous if I weren't betting my life on it."

"And them?"

"They're professionals. They'll read it and run perpendicular." He paused. "Mostly."

"*Mostly.*"

"I'm not going to lie to you, Odile."

"No," I said. "You're going to make a boy kick a mountain and hope the math holds." But I was already climbing toward the slabs, because the truth was I'd made my peace with the math the moment I saw my father's name on that manifest. Elias Ferreol, ferry operator, listed as drowned, 1971. The state wrote him off in ink and moved on. Somebody had to be the kind of person who didn't move on.

Isandro followed me. Of course he did. I told him to stay put and he came anyway, light-footed across the singing stone, and crouched beside me at the base of the shelf with his jaw set in a way that was going to get him killed someday or make him magnificent, possibly both.

"I can kick harder than she can," he said to Bram, over my objection.

"This isn't about strength, it's about—"

A second shot cracked off the slope, closer, and a chunk of stone spalled off the boulder shielding Bram and stung his cheek. Below, the rifleman adjusted his position, starting to move laterally, patient as tide. Behind him, half-hidden by heat shimmer and distance, I caught movement — a second figure working along the gully line. The box was closing.

"Isandro," Bram said, and something in his voice changed — dropped all its gravel, went young and urgent. "Left foot. Angled. All of you, now."

The boy kicked the mountain.

For one heartbeat, nothing. The slab sat there, ancient and contemptuous. Then it sighed — there's no other word — a deep grinding exhalation, and slid, and took its neighbor with it, and the neighbor took the whole shelf, and the slope above us turned liquid.

I've been in rapids. I've been in a ferry when the cable parted in floodwater. Nothing prepared me for the sound — a roar like the mountain clearing its throat after a thousand years of politeness — or the sheer animal intelligence of the thing, stone flowing downhill in a braided white-gray torrent, dust boiling up around us, the world reduced to noise and shaking and the primitive certainty that the earth had finally noticed us and disapproved.

We ran. Perpendicular, as Bram had promised the professionals would — cutting hard right, away from the fall line, while the avalanche poured past through the chute like a freight train derailing in slow motion. Dust swallowed everything. I lost sight of Bram. I lost sight of Isandro. I lost sight of my own boots. I ran on faith and fury and the memory of my father's hands on a tiller, and then the roar began to die behind us, settling into rumbles, into clicks, into silence and the soft grey snowfall of pulverized stone.

I turned in the choking haze.

Bram was ten feet away, on his knees, coughing, waving dust from his face. Alive. And Isandro—

Isandro wasn't there.

* * *

We found him fifteen feet downslope, at the edge of the debris fan, and for one endless moment I thought the mountain had taken its toll and I would spend the rest of my life carrying the weight of a boy I'd helped kick a hillside onto.

Then he groaned, and I have never been so grateful to hear anything.

His leg was pinned under a slab the size of a stove — not crushed, thank God, but trapped, the edge resting across his calf where he'd gone down. And here is the thing I will tell my grandchildren, if I live to have any and if I ever learn to tell stories that aren't about loss: when we reached him, his arms were wrapped around his own chest, crossed, protective, and inside his jacket the brass compass was intact. He'd fallen protecting it. In the middle of an avalanche, with half a mountain coming down around him, sixteen years old and terrified, his body had chosen the compass over his own face — I could see the scrape along his cheekbone where he'd turned the impact away from his chest, away from the brass and the glass and the needle that pointed at what you couldn't bear to lose.

"Foolish," Bram was saying, hauling the slab off him with a leverage bar from his pack, his voice wrecked and rough. "Foolish, foolish — you drop the pack, you drop the compass, you keep your hands free—"

"No," Isandro said. Simply. Like correcting a sum.

Bram stopped. Looked at him. The boy's leg was bleeding, his face was grey with dust and pain, and he stared back at Bram with the absolute calm of someone who has already survived the worst thing and knows the difference between important and essential.

"It points at what you fear losing," Isandro said. "You wouldn't touch it. Fine. But it's mine to lose, and I'm not losing it."

Bram knelt there in the settling dust, holding the boy's ankle, and I watched something happen to his face that I suspect hadn't happened in twenty years. The wall didn't come down — walls like his don't come down, they erode — but a piece of it went, right then, a whole course of stone sliding loose, and what showed underneath was naked and startled and human.

"You could have died," Bram said.

"Yeah." Isandro winced as Bram probed the leg. "Could've died keeping it safe, too. At least this way it was my choice."

"Your ankle's sprained, maybe fractured. You can't walk fast."

"Then carry me," the boy said, "or leave me, but the compass goes where I go."

And Bram Galloway — twenty years of miles, twenty years of never carrying anything he couldn't drop — gathered Isandro onto his back without another word, adjusting the boy's weight with the competence of a man who had hauled drowning strangers out of cold water in worse places than this, and stood up.

I picked up the fallen packs. Mine, Bram's, Isandro's. Three packs, one injured boy, one impossible compass, and a ridge line still between us and the trees.

Below us, through the thinning dust, I could see the contractors regrouping at the edge of the debris field — two figures now, the third presumably buried or withdrawn, their herding plan converted to rubble. They'd have to climb around the slide. It would cost them an hour.

It had cost us nearly everything. Isandro weighed on Bram's shoulders like a debt. The boy's blood dotted the grey stone in small bright coins. And when I glanced at Isandro's jacket, where the compass rode against his chest, I saw the needle through the worn fabric's gap — no longer pointing back toward the wreck, back toward the sea, back at the dead.

It was pointing at Bram.

Steady as a plumb line. Steady as something that had finally found what it was looking for.

I said nothing. Some cargo you don't announce. But I walked behind them up the slope, rear guard, noticing things, and I noticed that Bram Galloway carried that boy like he'd been carrying him for years — and that he hadn't yet looked down at the compass, and hadn't felt it change, and that the knowing of it was going to land on him like the avalanche had landed on the shelf.

The trees were close now. Dark and forgiving.

Behind us, faint over the rattle of settling stone, I heard the contractors' radio crackle, and a voice, clipped and professional, say words I couldn't make out — except one, repeated twice, cold as the snowline waiting above:

*"Alder Creek."*

They knew where we were going.
