## 13. The Hermit of Cold Ledge

The man at Cold Ledge heard us coming two hours before we arrived, which told me everything I needed to know about him before I ever saw his face.

We'd been climbing since dawn, following a trail that wasn't a trail—just a suggestion of passage through the pines that Odile's map called a fire road and the mountain had long since reclaimed. Isandro was limping. The fall on the scree slope had turned his ankle the color of a storm cloud, and he was carrying it with the particular stubbornness of a boy who'd decided that complaining was a debt he refused to owe anyone, least of all me.

"Rest," I said, for the fourth time.

"I'm fine."

"You're walking like a crab with a grudge."

He glared at me and kept walking. I let him. There are arguments worth having and arguments that are just weather, and I've gotten better at telling them apart, mostly because of these two.

It was Odile who spotted the smoke first—a thin gray thread rising off the shoulder of the ridge ahead, too small to be a settlement, too steady to be a campfire. She looked at me. I looked at her. Neither of us said what we were both thinking: smoke means people, people mean risk, but cold nights above the snowline mean worse.

"We knock," she said. "Politely."

"And if they're contractors?"

"Then we're three hikers who lost our way." She smiled, and it didn't reach anywhere near her eyes. "I've been somebody else before. It wears fine."

The cabin sat in a notch of granite like something the mountain had coughed up and then decided to keep. It was built low, roofed in shakes gone silver with age, with a woodpile stacked against one wall in an arrangement so precise it could have been surveyed. A garden plot, half-harvested. A rain barrel. And strung between two dead snags, a line of antennas—wire dipole after wire dipole, sagging under their own weight like laundry hung out by a ghost.

I stopped looking at the antennas and started looking at the door, because the door had opened, and there was a man standing in it holding a shotgun with the bored competence of someone who'd held one many times and never once enjoyed it.

He was maybe seventy. Big through the shoulders in the way of men who used to be bigger, wearing a wool shirt that had been mended more times than most boats get repainted. His eyes went over us the way mine go over a wreck site—methodically, without sentiment, cataloguing threats and salvage in the same pass.

"Hikers," he said. It wasn't a question. It was a verdict he hadn't finished writing yet.

"Lost ones," Odile said.

"Nobody gets lost on my ridge. The ridge is very clear about where things are." He shifted the shotgun—not aiming it, just reminding it existed. "You came up from Kettle Bridge side. That roadblock come down?"

"It did," I said.

His eyes flicked to me, and something moved behind them—recognition, or its cousin. "You're the diver."

I didn't answer, which was an answer.

"The fixer talks," the man said. "Even when you pay him not to. Especially then." He stepped back from the doorway and lowered the gun to rest against his thigh. "Well. Come in before the weather does. And mind the cat, she bites on principle."

* * *

The cat was a gray thing with one ear chewed short, and she bit Isandro on principle almost immediately, then forgave him within the minute and installed herself on his lap as though the biting had been a formality required by her union. Isandro sat very still, hand resting on her back, and I watched some of the tension go out of his shoulders for the first time in two days.

The inside of the cabin was a library that had suffered a shipwreck. Books everywhere—shelved, stacked, doubled as furniture. But it was the walls that told the real story. Charts. Naval charts, survey charts, coastal charts going back decades, pinned and annotated in a small, furious hand. And among them, photographs. Ships. Men in uniform. A younger version of our host shaking hands with an admiral whose name I didn't know and whose face I'd seen on recruitment posters.

"Cold Ledge," Odile said, reading the name stenciled on an old sea chest. "That's a station designation."

"It's a rock," the man said, pouring coffee into four tin cups without asking whether we wanted any. "Off the north coast. There was a listening post on it, once. I was the archivist attached to it." He handed the cups around. "Emory Vance. Twenty-six years keeping the paper honest. Or trying to."

"You don't keep paper honest," I said. "You keep it filed. Honest is a different job."

Vance looked at me for a long moment, and then he laughed—a dry, rusted sound, like a hatch opening after years shut. "Oh, you'll do. Sit down, diver. You look like a man who's been asking himself questions he can't afford."

"He has," Isandro said.

"Traitor," I said. The cat purred.

Odile set her cup down untouched, which I took as a professional courtesy to her own suspicion. "Mr. Vance. We came up this ridge because the navy burned a camp and tried to seize a boat, and because a boy here is carrying a compass that doesn't point north, and because somewhere below us there's a wreck full of names nobody wants read aloud. If any of that sounds like your business, now's the time to say so."

Vance had been lowering himself into a chair. He stopped halfway, and for a moment he looked every year of his age and then some—the weight of twenty-six years of filing settling across his shoulders like ballast.

"A compass that points at what you fear losing," he said quietly. "Yes. I've heard of those. Not that one specifically. But instruments that were never meant to work, working anyway—that I know about." He drank his coffee like it was medicine, which it probably was. "Show me the manifests."

Isandro looked at me. I looked at the fire. Then I nodded, because at some point in the last week I'd apparently become a person who made decisions with other people instead of alone, and nobody had warned me.

Isandro dug the sealed packets from the waterproof bag and laid them on the table. Vance didn't touch them at first. He bent close, reading the seals, the stamps, the routing codes, and I watched his hands start to shake—not much, just at the fingertips, the way rigging trembles before it parts.

"These were supposed to be ash," he said. "These exact documents. Serial range 4400 through 4460. I filed the destruction order myself."

* * *

He told it slowly, the way old men tell things they've rehearsed being angry about for years, each sentence worn smooth from handling.

The transport—she'd been a conversion, a troop ship refitted for what the paperwork called "administrative relocation"—had gone down in a storm thirty-odd years ago with sixty-one souls aboard. Officially. Sixty-one souls, officially drowned, officially mourned in a single paragraph on page eleven of a regional paper, officially forgotten.

Except the transport hadn't sunk. Not the way they said.

"She was scuttled," Vance said. "Deliberately. With the passengers aboard. Because the passengers were the paperwork problem. Dissidents, debtors, witnesses, the sick, the inconvenient—people the state had already written off on other ledgers, and who were about to become visible in ways that embarrassed important men. So they were loaded aboard for a 'relocation crossing' that was always going to end at the bottom." He tapped the manifests with one finger, gently, the way you'd touch a wound. "And then the storm came early, and the scuttling crew panicked, and the ship went down twelve miles off her intended position, in water deep enough that recovery was impossible—but shallow enough that someday, someday, someone might find her."

"So they waited," Odile said.

"They waited thirty years for the technology to catch up. Remote survey, deep salvage, the works. And when it did, they sent a team to do one job: confirm the cargo was unrecoverable. Confirm the manifests had gone down with her and rotted past reading. Get readings, take photos, file a report saying the evidence no longer exists." Vance's mouth twisted. "That was the mission brief I saw cross my desk before I left the service. I was already out by then—retired sideways, you'd call it, after I asked one question too many about the original sinking. But old friends still send me things. Old habits."

"But the manifests survived," Isandro said. His voice had gone careful, the way it does when he's holding something fragile. "They're in sealed cases. Waterproof. We found them in the hold, behind a false bulkhead."

Vance closed his eyes. "Behind a false bulkhead," he repeated. "Of course. Someone aboard hid them. Someone knew." He opened his eyes and looked at Isandro, and whatever he saw in the boy's face made him gentler. "Your father was aboard."

"My father was aboard," Isandro said. "I came to find his name. I found it. Page three." He said it flat, factually, and it was the flattest, most factual voice I'd ever heard break a little in the middle.

Nobody rushed to fill the silence. Odile put her hand over his on the table. The cat, sensing the gravity, bit him again, lightly, and he almost smiled.

"And the survey team?" I asked. "The one that walked off the job? Boots by the fire, coffee in the pot, no tracks?"

Vance's face did something complicated. Pity and horror sharing a small space. "There was never a survey team that walked off, son. There was a survey team that got collected. They confirmed the manifests were intact and readable—which was the opposite of their orders—and they reported it honestly, because one of them was honest. And honesty, in that chain of command, is a security breach." He stood, joints cracking like green wood, and went to a shelf, and pulled down a battered logbook. "The tender that visited you? Same vessel class as the one that picked them up. I've tracked her movements on the radio bands for years. She doesn't patrol. She tidies."

"Tidies," Odile repeated.

"Burns camps. Seizes boats. Collects witnesses." He set the logbook on the table, open to a page of dense entries. "I've been documenting her for six years. Times, bearings, signal signatures. Nobody upstairs wants it, because everybody upstairs either signed off on the sinking or signed off on the cover-up, and the two groups overlap considerably." He looked around at the three of us—at the boy with the broken compass-heart, at the woman with her father's name on a manifest, at me, sitting there like a man who'd wandered into a story he'd spent twenty years avoiding. "So I kept the paper honest. Alone. On a rock. For six years."

"That's not living," I said. "That's waiting."

"Yes," Vance said simply. "It is. Thank you for noticing."

* * *

We stayed the night because the weather decided it for us—the first real snow of the season came down the ridge an hour after full dark, fat flakes falling straight and silent, erasing the world beyond the windows. Vance fed us stew that tasted like forty years of solitude and salt pork, and Isandro slept by the fire with the cat on his chest and the compass bag tucked under his arm like a child holds a stuffed animal, and Odile sat up late with Vance over the logbook, two archivists of different denominations, comparing notes in low voices.

I couldn't sleep. I went out onto the lean-to porch with my cup and watched the snow fill in our tracks, and after a while Vance came out and stood beside me, and neither of us said anything for a long time, because men like us communicate primarily through strategic silence.

"Twenty years measuring miles," he said finally. "That's what the fixer said about you. Never stays anywhere. Never keeps anything. Never asks who owns what he pulls up."

"Sounds about right."

"And now you're running escort for a boy and a box of dead men's names, up a mountain, toward a pass, in winter." He blew steam off his cup. "What happened?"

I thought about lying to him. I'm good at it; it's one of my few marketable skills. But the snow was coming down straight and honest, and the man had spent six years alone keeping faith with the dead, and it seemed like a debt of respect.

"I touched the compass," I said.

Vance went very still.

"It pointed at the boy." I turned the cup in my hands. "Not the boat. Not my secrets. Him. And I've been trying to figure out what to do about that ever since, and the only thing I've come up with is keep walking and hope the needle changes its mind."

"It won't," Vance said. "Instruments like that don't lie. People lie to instruments." He drained his coffee. "Get some sleep, diver. Tomorrow I'm coming with you."

I laughed. "You're seventy years old with a bad hip and a cat."

"I'm seventy years old with a bad hip, a cat, and the only complete paper trail connecting that tender to the collection of the survey team and the original scuttling order." He turned and looked at me, and his eyes in the porch light were bright and hard and absolutely certain. "Six years I've waited for someone to carry this somewhere it counts. You're not leaving my life's work on this mountain to burn like that camp did. Pack the logbooks."

* * *

We left at first light, four of us and a cat, moving south through knee-deep snow toward the pass, with Vance navigating by landmarks he'd memorized from charts of terrain he'd never walked, which should have been absurd and somehow wasn't. He moved slow but he moved true, and when Isandro stumbled on the buried trail, it was Vance's arm that caught him, easy as breathing, like the old man had been catching people his whole life and had merely been out of practice.

By midday the sky behind us had cleared, and I looked back once, the way you do, and saw what I'd been afraid I'd see: far down the valley, on the road we'd abandoned, a vehicle crawling, patient as a tide, kicking up a thread of dust that no snowfall would quite explain.

They weren't behind us anymore.

They were below us. And the pass was still two days away, and the compass in Isandro's pocket had begun, very faintly, to tremble.
