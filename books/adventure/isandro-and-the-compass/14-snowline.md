## 14. Snowline

The snow came the way bad news comes — early, sideways, and pretending to be nothing at first.

We were still a full day below the snowline when Odile spotted it: a gray smear on the northern ridgeline that I took for cloud and she took for weather. She was right. She was usually right about things like that, which was one of the more annoying features of her personality. Bram squinted at the ridge for a long time, said nothing, and then started walking faster. That was his version of announcing a crisis. Some men shout. Bram Galloway simply increased his pace by fifteen percent and let you do the math.

"Storm's early," Odile said.

"Storms are always early," Bram said. "Weather doesn't respect your schedule. Neither does anything else."

"You're a wellspring of comfort."

"I'm a wellspring of accuracy. Comfort's extra."

I was in the middle, as usual, carrying the compass in my inside pocket where it sat against my chest like a second heartbeat that had opinions. It had been behaving strangely since Cold Ledge — not spinning, not lying, just *leaning*. All morning its needle had drifted off Odile, off the manifests wrapped in oilcloth in her pack, and settled into a steady, stubborn bearing that I didn't need brass and glass to interpret.

It pointed at Bram.

I hadn't told him. There didn't seem to be a good way to open that conversation. *Hey, the magic compass says you're the thing I'm most afraid of losing* is not small talk, and we weren't a small-talk group. We were a walk-fast-and-don't-die group. But all day the needle held its line like it was welded there, and every time Bram stopped to check our backtrail — which he did constantly, with that little half-turn of his shoulders, a man so used to being alone he'd turned checking for pursuers into a nervous habit — the needle swung with him, faithful as a dog.

By mid-afternoon the gray smear had become a gray wall, and the temperature dropped like something falling off a cliff, because that's essentially what it was. The air went thin and metallic. My head, which had been bothering me since the scree slope — since I'd taken that fall protecting the compass and cracked my elbow and my dignity in roughly equal measure — started bothering me in a new register. Not pain exactly. More like someone had replaced the inside of my skull with wet wool and a low-grade fever with ambitions.

"We need shelter," Odile said. "Real shelter. Before dark."

"There's nothing real out here," Bram said. "There's only less fake."

But he'd already seen it. I saw him see it — a black seam in the hillside above us, maybe four hundred yards up through the pines, too straight to be natural. A mine adit. Old workings, probably abandoned before either of us was born, the kind of hole men dug into mountains when they still believed mountains owed them something.

Bram looked at it the way he looked at everything: like he was pricing it. Then he looked at me, and whatever he saw made the price irrelevant.

"Can you make four hundred yards?" he asked.

"I can make four hundred miles," I said, which was a lie, and he knew it was a lie, and he let me have it anyway, which was somehow worse than calling it.

* * *

The adit was maybe eight feet high and went back forty feet into the rock before ending in a wall of collapsed rubble. Someone had worked it with hand steel a century ago and quit angry — you could see the half-finished drill marks in the face. There was no timber left worth burning except a few rotten lagging boards near the entrance, and a rusted ore cart sitting on rails that ended abruptly at the collapse, as if the mountain had simply decided, mid-transaction, that it was done giving.

Bram got a fire going with a efficiency that bordered on insulting. He built it small and deep, back against the side wall where the smoke would curl along the ceiling and out the mouth instead of announcing us to the whole valley. He stacked rocks behind it to throw heat forward. Twenty years of living outdoors had compressed themselves into those ten minutes of work, and I watched him do it and thought, not for the first time, that there was an entire person inside Bram Galloway that he kept folded up small enough to fit in a pocket, same as the compass.

Odile unpacked food. We ate in the firelight — beans from a tin, hard bread, the last of the cheese she'd traded for at Kettle Bridge what felt like a geological era ago. The storm arrived outside in earnest while we ate, wind rising through the pines with a sound like the whole forest exhaling, and then the snow came down thick and silent beyond the adit's mouth until the world ended about six feet past the firelight.

It should have felt safe. Enclosed, warm, hidden. Instead I felt worse by the hour. The fever climbed. My teeth chattered even though I wasn't cold anymore — or rather, I was cold somewhere deeper than the fire could reach, some interior cold that had been accumulating since the wreck, since the camp, since the night I'd run into the trees while men with instruments screamed at readings only they could hear.

"Isandro." Odile's voice, quiet. "You're gray."

"I'm always gray. It's my best color."

"You're shaking."

"It's cold."

"It's not that cold," Bram said. He'd moved without me noticing, the way he did, and now he was crouched in front of me with the firelight doing things to his face that made him look older and younger at once. He put the back of his hand against my forehead, and his hand was rough and cool and I leaned into it before I could stop myself, the way a dog leans, without any dignity whatsoever.

"Fever," he said.

"Thank you, doctor. Truly, your diagnostic gifts—"

"Shut up and lie down."

There was a sleeping roll and there was a wall of rock and there was Bram's shoulder, eventually, because I couldn't get warm and because neither of them said anything when I ended up leaning against him, which was its own kind of mercy. Odile sat across the fire with the manifests in her lap, reading the names again by firelight, the way she did most nights. Her father's name was in there. Somewhere between page twelve and page nineteen, typed in a government font by a government clerk who had never met him, filed under a word like *expendable* that nobody ever actually writes but everybody understands.

The fever did something to me. I want to be honest about that, because everything else in this story I've tried to tell straight, and this part matters. The fever loosened something. All day the compass had been pointing at Bram, steady as scripture, and all day I'd been carrying two weights — the brass in my pocket and the truth in my chest — and the fever decided I was done carrying both.

"I stowed away," I said.

The fire popped. Wind moaned at the adit mouth. Nobody spoke.

"On the survey boat," I said. "The *Corvina*. You know that. You found me in the hold. But you don't know why."

Bram shifted slightly. Not pulling away. Settling. Making himself into something I could lean harder against if I needed to.

"My father worked the ferries," I said. "Before. You've seen the manifest names — they're all ferry people, coast people, people who lived on the water. My father ran the evening crossing out of Puerto Sombra. Eleven years ago the state retired that route. 'Consolidation,' they called it. They gave the families a settlement and a story: the old boats were unseaworthy, the route was a money pit, everyone was retrained, relocated, fine, fine, fine. Except my father didn't relocate. He wrote letters. He organized the other families. He stood outside the transport office with a sign until the police explained, politely, repeatedly, that standing was no longer permitted."

I heard my own voice going flat and fast, the way it goes when I'm reciting something I've rehearsed in the dark too many times.

"And then one winter the letters stopped coming. And then the man who delivered them stopped coming. And then the whole town acted like he'd been a rumor. My mother said he'd gone inland for work. My mother said a lot of things. She died believing at least three of them."

Odile had stopped reading. The manifests lay closed in her lap. In the firelight her face was very still.

"When I heard a survey team was going out to the coast," I said, "a government team, with instruments, to a wreck site nobody would name — I didn't know it was *the* wreck. I swear I didn't. I just knew it was a government boat going somewhere secret, and I had learned by then that governments keep their secrets in the same place they keep people like my father. So I hid in the hold. Among the crates. Which is funny, right? Because that's where you found me later. Same crates. I'm apparently a boy who belongs in holds."

Nobody laughed. It hadn't really been a joke.

"The team found the wreck on the fourth day," I said. "And the third night, before that, I heard them talking. Two of them, on deck, smoking. One said the manifests were supposed to have gone down with her. Burned, sealed, sunk — triple insurance. And the other one said, 'Then why are we here?' And the first one said, 'Because somebody upstairs just found out they weren't.'"

My hands were shaking. I watched them shake with a kind of distant interest, like they belonged to a boy in a story.

"They weren't destroyed," I said. "Eleven years of telling ourselves they were lost with the ship, and the paper survived. Paper always survives. That's the joke of it. People sink; paperwork floats. So they sent a team to confirm, and the team confirmed, and then the team walked off the job — boots by the fire, coffee in the pot — because the job was never surveying. The job was confirming a destruction that never happened, and then becoming part of the correction."

"That's when you ran," Bram said. Quietly. Not a question.

"That's when I ran. Into the hold. Where I stayed until a salvage diver with a rule about not asking questions found me anyway." I finally turned and looked at him. His face was unreadable, which was normal, but his eyes weren't, which wasn't. "I took the compass off the chart table in the wheelhouse. First thing I grabbed. I don't even know why. It was just — it was the only thing on that boat that looked like it told the truth."

"And it's been pointing at the wreck ever since," Bram said. "At the manifests. At the names."

"At what I was afraid of losing," I said. "Which was never the paper. It was—" My voice caught, and I hated it, and I pushed through. "It was finding out I was right. That he was on it. That the story was a story. Because as long as I didn't know, he could still be alive somewhere inland, working some other ferry, too busy to write. As long as I didn't look, he was still possible."

The wind hit the adit mouth and the fire flattened and sprang back.

"But I looked," I said. "Page fourteen. Third name in the second column. *Tomás Reyes, mate, age 44.* Age 44. They wrote down how old he was. They knew his birthday. Somebody typed that. Somebody's hands made the letters R-e-y-e-s and then that person went home and ate dinner."

Odile made a sound — small, involuntary — and I understood it completely, because her father's name was in the same column, pages away, and she had read it fifty times and it had never once gotten easier.

"So that's my confession," I said. "That's why I'm here. Not adventure. Not accident. Arithmetic. I did the math on what I could survive knowing, and I was wrong, and now I'm feverish in a hole in a mountain with a stolen compass and two people who should have left me on the beach."

Silence. The good kind, the kind that isn't empty.

Then Bram said, "You know what I did, the year I lost my name?"

I blinked. Of all the possible responses, I had not priced that one.

"I took a contract," he said, staring into the fire. "A dive I shouldn't have. Deep, wrong equipment, wrong employer. And when it went bad — when a man died down there because I followed instructions instead of my own read of the water — I was paid to forget it. Actual money, actual papers, a new registration. They bought my silence and I sold it, and I told myself for twenty years that I'd made a trade. Clean transaction. Miles, not years. Never ask who owns what you pull up." He turned his head and looked at me, and the firelight moved in his eyes. "And then a half-frozen kid in a cargo hold asks me one question — just one, about whether the navy will come back — and I hear myself answer honestly, and I realize I haven't answered anything honestly in twenty years. You broke my rule without even trying. You've been breaking all my rules since the beach. Being kind. Asking names. Caring about outcomes."

"That sounds like your problem," I managed.

"It is," he said. "It became my problem on the beach." He paused. "Your father's name gets read somewhere public. That's not a hope, it's a plan. We're not running east to disappear. We're going south, to the archive, and those names go into the record where no amount of money buys them back. That's the whole mission now. You understand? Not escape. Delivery."

Something in my chest cracked — not broke, cracked, the way ice cracks before it becomes water. I think I said okay. I think I might have cried a little, feverishly, undignified, and I think Bram pretended not to notice, which was the kindest thing anyone has ever done for me.

* * *

Later — much later, the fire burned to coals, Odile asleep sitting up with the manifests still in her lap like a woman holding a sleeping animal — I checked the compass one more time. Fever makes you do ritual things. I eased it out of my pocket and opened the lid a crack, expecting the needle to waver, to drift toward the wreck, toward the names, toward the dead.

It pointed at Bram.

Steady. Unhurried. Certain. He slept propped against the adit wall with his arms crossed, a man who had spent twenty years making sure he was the kind of person nobody would miss, and the little brass needle disagreed with his entire biography. It pointed at him the way it used to point at the wreck: at the thing I could not afford to lose.

I closed the lid and held the compass against my chest and listened to the storm bury the world.

Outside, far below us in the white dark, I thought I heard it — faint, rhythmic, wrong for the hour: a siren, or a vehicle, or just the wind lying to me. I decided it was the wind.

I decided wrong.
