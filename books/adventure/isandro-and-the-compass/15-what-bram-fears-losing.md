## 15. What Bram Fears Losing

The fever broke sometime in the small hours, and I knew it the way you know a storm has passed—not by any single thing, but by the quality of the silence. The wind had quit hammering the mine adit. The drip at the entrance had slowed to a metronome instead of a drumroll. And Isandro's breathing, which had been a shallow rasp all night, evened out into something that sounded like an actual boy sleeping instead of a boy being slowly spent.

I should have slept myself. Twenty years of diving had taught me to take sleep like salvage—whenever it presented itself, no questions asked. But I sat there on the cold rock with my back against the timber frame and watched him instead, and I couldn't have told you why, except that watching felt like work I'd been avoiding.

The kid had confessed something to me the night before, half-delirious, firelight making his face flicker between child and old man. He'd stowed away on the survey team's boat because of a name. His father's name. He wanted to find it on a manifest in a sunken hold, because finding it meant the state had at least bothered to write it down somewhere, even if only to erase it later. That was the bar. That was what a boy hoped for from his government: that his father existed enough to be officially unpersoned.

I'd heard worse confessions. I'd told worse ones myself, though not sober and not lately. But here was the thing that kept me sitting up: when he said it, the compass—the brass thing he kept wrapped in a sock in his jacket pocket—had been lying on the flat rock between us, and its needle hadn't wavered. It pointed at me. Steady as a weld bead. It had been pointing at me since the scree slope, since the fall, since the night I carried him down off that mountainside with his ankle ballooning and his teeth chattering out apologies.

I'd told myself it was a malfunction. Instruments fail; that was practically my religion. A compass is a magnetized needle floating in fluid, and fluid freezes, and housings crack, and magnets get demagnetized by proximity to ore bodies—and we were sleeping in a mine, for God's sake, surrounded by more iron than a shipbreaker's yard. There was a perfectly reasonable explanation, and I clung to it the way a drowning man clings to a crate marked FRAGILE.

But Isandro, feverish, had looked at me across the fire and said, "It's not broken, Bram. It's never wrong. It just tells you what you already know and don't want to."

And then he'd slept, and I'd sat with the thing lying there between us like a lit fuse.

* * *

Morning came gray and thin through the adit mouth. Odile was already up, doing her quiet inventory of our supplies the way she did every morning—counting calories like a woman balancing a ledger she suspected was cooked. She caught my eye over the top of the ration tin and raised one eyebrow. Odile could conduct entire conversations with her eyebrows. This one said: *You didn't sleep.*

"I slept," I lied.

Her other eyebrow joined the first. *Liar.*

"He's better," I said, nodding at Isandro. "Fever's down."

"Good." She set the tin down. "Because we can't stay here past tomorrow. Storm gave us a day, maybe two. After that the contractors pick up the trail or the snow does it for them."

"I know."

"Do you?" She glanced at the compass on the rock, then at me, and something in her face softened by a degree. "Bram. It's been pointing at you for three days. You've walked around it, stepped over it, turned your coffee mug so the handle faces away from it. I've watched you do it. You treat that thing like a snake you're hoping will molt."

"It's a piece of junk with delusions."

"Sure." She picked up the compass, weighed it in her palm like she was pricing it at a market stall, and held it out to me. "Then holding it won't cost you anything."

Everything about my training said take it. Twenty years of salvage taught you that fear of an object was superstition, and superstition was how divers died—hesitating at the wrong moment because some feeling told them to. You touched the thing. You assessed the thing. You made the thing give up its information. Fear was just data arriving early.

My hand wouldn't move.

Odile waited. She was good at waiting; ferry people are, I think. You spend your life watching water decide things, you learn patience. She stood there with the compass extended and the gray light coming in behind her and Isandro breathing slow and even ten feet away, and she let the silence do her arguing for her.

"The boy almost died protecting that," she said finally, quietly. "He took a forty-foot slide on scree rather than let go of it. And you won't take it out of my hand." Her mouth twitched, not quite a smile. "For a man who claims not to believe in anything, you're very devout."

I took it.

The brass was cold—that was the first thing, the mundane thing, the relief of it. Just metal. Dinged along the bezel where it had hit rock. Someone's initials scratched inside the lid, worn nearly smooth: R.M.R., maybe, or R.M.P., letters going soft the way letters do when a thumb worries them for years. It had been held before. It had been loved before, or at least relied upon, which in some lives amounts to the same thing.

The needle swung.

Not wildly—it wasn't dramatic, wasn't the spinning circus act Isandro described from the reading room at the lookout. It moved the way a heavy door moves on good hinges: deliberate, certain, settling. And then it stopped, and it pointed—

At nothing.

That was my first read, anyway. I turned a slow circle, checking against the adit mouth, against the timber line, against the seam of quartz in the wall that I'd been using as a reference point all night. The needle tracked me. Wherever I faced, it corrected, patient as gravity, until I understood that it wasn't pointing at a direction at all.

It was pointing at a person. And the person was behind me.

I turned around.

Isandro was awake. Had been for who knows how long—long enough to watch me take the compass, long enough to see me turn circles like a man looking for a exit in a room with no doors. He was propped on one elbow, gray-faced but clear-eyed, and he watched me with an expression I recognized because I'd worn it myself, once, standing on a dock deciding whether to board a boat I'd sworn I was done with.

"Bram," he said. His voice was still rough from the fever. "It's okay."

"It's broken," I said.

"Yeah," he said. "You said that about yourself once, too. First night. You thought I was asleep."

* * *

Here's what I know about fear, professionally speaking. I've been afraid at two hundred feet on a wreck with a fouled line. I've been afraid in black water when my light died and the only sound was my own regulator and the distant groan of a hull settling. I've been afraid of men with clipboards and badges, which is a different flavor—colder, longer-lasting. Fear of dying is simple. Your body handles it for you. Fear of men is manageable, because men can be outrun, outwaited, out-lied. I had built an entire life on the principle that everything frightening falls into one of those two categories, and that a man who owned nothing and owed nothing and answered to nobody could keep himself permanently outside the reach of both.

I stood in that mine adit holding a brass compass that had just dismantled the whole architecture, and I understood that there was a third category, and I had been living inside it for weeks without noticing, the way you live inside weather.

The needle pointed at Isandro.

Not at my boat—my boat was a burned hulk on a beach two hundred miles behind us, and I'd felt surprisingly little about that. Not at my freedom, whatever that word meant anymore; freedom, I was beginning to suspect, had mostly been a brand name for loneliness, and I'd bought it retail for twenty years. Not at my secrets, though God knew I had enough of them buried in enough jurisdictions to stock a small cemetery. The compass ignored all of it. All the cargo I'd spent a lifetime keeping secured and lashed down. It went straight past every hatch to the one compartment I hadn't known was loaded.

A half-frozen stowaway with a busted ankle and a dead father's name.

"That's not—" I started, and stopped, because finishing the sentence out loud would make it real, and I'd built a career on not making things real out loud. "The needle's dragging. Magnetization. There's iron everywhere in here—"

"Bram." Isandro sat up fully now, wincing at the ankle, refusing to look at the ankle. "When it pointed at the wreck, I kept telling myself the same thing. That it was stuck. That it was grief playing tricks with a magnet. You know what I figured out?" He took a breath. "It doesn't show you anything new. It shows you the thing you check for in the middle of the night. The thing you'd go back for. Everybody has one. Most people just don't have to look at it."

"And yours was the wreck."

"Mine was my dad." He said it simply, without drama, the way you'd report a depth reading. "The wreck is just where they put him."

Odile had drifted toward the adit mouth, giving us the room with the practiced grace of someone who understood that some conversations need fewer witnesses, though I noticed she didn't leave. Odile never entirely left. That was apparently a feature of this new arrangement I'd gotten myself into—people stayed within earshot. People expected things. It was like acquiring ballast you couldn't jettison.

"So now you know," Isandro said. "What are you going to do about it?"

"What am I—" I laughed, and it came out wrong, too loud in the stone room. "Kid, it's a compass. Knowing what it says doesn't obligate me to anything. It's not a contract. It's a needle floating in fluid."

"Sure," he said. "And a manifest is just paper, and a name is just ink, and a witness is just a person who happened to be standing there." He looked at me with those eyes that had gone old too fast. "You taught me the rules, Bram. Rule one: never ask who owns what you pull up. But you also taught me rule four, that night on the boat, remember? When I asked why you always log your dives even though nobody pays you to."

I remembered. I'd told him: *because a thing that isn't recorded didn't happen, and I refuse to be a man nothing happened to.* I'd said it like a joke. Nothing I'd said in twenty years had been less of a joke.

"You log what's true," Isandro said. "Even when it costs you. Especially then. That's rule four." He nodded at the compass in my hand. "So. Log it."

The adit was quiet except for the drip. Somewhere above us, snow was doing whatever snow does when nobody's watching—piling up, sealing passes, rewriting the map. Somewhere below us, men with contractor lanyards and government silence were working their way up a trail. And I stood in between, holding a dead man's compass that was telling me the one truth I'd arranged my whole life to avoid hearing:

I had something to lose now. Something specific. Something with a name and a chattering way of talking and a stubbornness I recognized like looking in a mirror, and a fever that had nearly taken him while I sat three feet away doing nothing, because doing nothing was the only skill I'd ever mastered completely.

Do you want to know the honest arithmetic of it? The navy terrified me less. The navy was a known quantity—a big dumb animal that would burn and seize and collect, but only ever threatened the things I'd already written off. My boat. My license. My name, such as it was. The navy could take my future, and I'd been living without one so long that losing it would barely change my wardrobe.

But this. This feverish kid looking at me like I was an answer to a question he'd been asking since a sunken hold swallowed his father—this I could lose. And losing it would not leave me where I started. That was the trap nobody warns you about. You spend years believing you can't lose anything because you own nothing, and then you find out that the things you can't afford to lose aren't things at all, and they don't come with manifests, and there's no insurance, and no amount of miles puts them beyond reach, because they're not located anywhere. They're located in you.

I closed the lid on the compass. The click of it sounded final, like a logbook entry.

"Rule four," I said.

Isandro smiled. Small, fever-faded, but real. "Rule four."

* * *

We broke camp an hour later, moving fast and quiet through the white world outside. Odile led—I'd learned to trust her mountain sense the way I trusted my water sense, each of us fluent in a medium the other only survived in. Isandro managed on the bad ankle with a pole I'd cut for him and a ferocity about not being carried that reminded me unpleasantly of myself.

The compass rode in my jacket pocket now. Isandro had handed it over without ceremony that morning, the transfer neither of us commented on, both of us understanding exactly what it meant and neither willing to say it in words, because words were Odile's department and mine were actions.

Twice on the climb I stopped, ostensibly to check our backtrail, and took it out. Both times the needle settled instantly, patiently, on the boy limping ahead of me through the snow.

The second time, Odile dropped back beside me. She didn't look at the compass. She looked at me, and her eyebrows conducted a brief seminar.

"Don't," I said.

"I didn't say anything."

"Your eyebrows said plenty."

"They're concerned," she said, "that a man carrying a compass like that might start making decisions based on what it tells him. Like, say, trading his own escape route for a boy's testimony somewhere up ahead. Hypothetically."

"Hypothetically," I said, "that would be a stupid trade."

"Mm." She lengthened her stride to catch up with Isandro. Over her shoulder: "For what it's worth, the ferryman's daughter thinks it would be the first smart trade you ever made."

Ahead, the trees thinned toward the pass, and beyond them the sky had that bruised look it gets before weather turns mean again. And in my pocket, steady as a heartbeat, the needle pointed at everything I had left to lose—which is to say, forward, up the mountain, into the white, wherever he was going.

I followed it.
