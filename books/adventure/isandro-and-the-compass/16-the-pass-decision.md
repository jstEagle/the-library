## 16. The Pass Decision

My father used to say a river crossing is just a decision you make with your whole body. You can stand on the bank all day weighing it, but the moment your boot touches water, you've already chosen. I thought about that a lot over the next two days, because we spent them standing on the moral equivalent of a riverbank, and nobody wanted to put a boot in.

We'd come down off Cold Ledge in the grey hour before dawn, the hermit—his name was Anselm Voss, though he answered to "the archivist" the way a dog answers to a name it resents—pressing photocopied pages into Isandro's jacket like a man handing off a live grenade. *The manifests weren't destroyed,* he'd said. *They were supposed to be destroyed. That's why there was a survey team at all.* And then he'd shut his door on us, which I respected. There's a kind of courage in opening your door to strangers and a different kind in closing it again before they get you killed.

Now we were camped in the lee of a granite shoulder maybe two hours below treeline, and the weather had opinions.

I'll describe the situation the way I saw it from my side of the fire, because Bram will tell you his version later and his version always makes him sound more decisive than he was. The truth is that for most of that morning, none of us decided anything. We sat and watched the clouds come down the valley like something being poured.

* * *

Here's what we knew, laid out as neatly as I could lay it in my head while Isandro slept against a pack and Bram pretended not to be watching him sleep:

One: the contractors were below us. Not behind—below. We'd seen their headlamps in the pre-dawn dark, three points of light moving up the drainage we'd descended, patient as toll collectors. They weren't chasing us anymore. Chasing implies you don't know where someone's going. They knew. There were exactly two places three people and a boy could be headed in this country: east through the high pass into the empty interior, where you could vanish so thoroughly your own mother would need a divining rod; or south, down the long valley to Alder Creek, where the regional archive sat in a converted grange hall and took filings from anyone with documents and a pulse.

Two: the weather was closing the high route. Bram had read the sky the way I read water, and what he read made him quiet, and Bram quiet is quieter than most men's shouting. "Snow by tomorrow night," he said. "Real snow. The pass goes white and stays white till spring."

Three: Isandro had a fever of a hundred and one and was pretending he didn't, and the manifests—the actual paper, forty years of names the state had declared expendable—were wrapped in oilcloth inside his jacket, against his chest, like a second heartbeat.

"So," I said, when the silence had gone on long enough to develop its own weather. "Somebody say it out loud."

Bram looked at me across the fire. He has a face built for saying nothing—I've watched him talk his way past a roadblock using nothing but boredom and a fake fishing license—but even he couldn't pretend he didn't know what I meant.

"East or south," he said.

"There it is."

"East, we disappear." He turned a stick in the coals without any interest in burning it. "Cross tonight, beat the snow, drop into the interior. Nobody finds us. Nobody finds the manifests either. They sit in a jacket until the jacket rots, and then they're ash, and forty years of dead people stay exactly as forgotten as the government intended."

"And south?"

"South, we walk into a town with a navy car already parked outside the archive, hand the papers to a clerk who may or may not still work for us, and pray." He said *pray* the way a man says a word he doesn't believe in but has run out of alternatives for. "And if it works, the names go into public record. Filed. Stamped. Real. Something that can't be un-filed no matter how many tenders they send."

I fed a branch to the fire and watched it catch. "You've been counting the miles to Alder Creek since Cold Ledge," I said. "Don't tell me you haven't."

"I count everything. It's a disease."

"Bram." I waited until he looked at me. "You already know which way it is. You've known since Voss handed over those copies. You're just waiting for one of us to make you say it, because saying it means you're the kind of man who walks toward the navy instead of away from it, and you've spent twenty years being the other kind."

He didn't answer right away. Below us, somewhere down the drainage, I imagined I could hear the contractors' boots on scree, though it was probably just the wind doing its impression of boots. Fear does that. It fills in sound effects.

"The last time I chose witness over disappearing," Bram said finally, "I lost my name, my record, and eleven years of my life. I got very good at not being seen. It kept me alive. It's the only thing that's ever kept me alive." He looked at Isandro, asleep, feverish, one hand curled over his jacket where the papers were. "And now I'm standing on a mountain arguing myself out of the only skill I have, because a kid I've known three weeks won't survive me abandoning him, and neither—" he stopped. Started over. "Neither will I, actually. Just slower."

"That's the most words you've said since Kettle Bridge," I told him.

"Don't get used to it."

* * *

Isandro woke mid-afternoon, and we told him. All of it. The headlamps below, the snow coming, the two roads out of the mountains. I expected him to argue for the archive—he'd stowed away on a government survey boat to find his father's name; the boy had never once in his life chosen the invisible road—but he surprised me. He sat with it. Feverish, shivering under both our coats, he sat with the choice like it was a stone he was turning over.

"If we go east," he said slowly, "we live."

"Probably," Bram said.

"And the names die with us. My father stays drowned twice. Once in the water and once in the record." His jaw worked. "There's a woman in Alder Creek who runs the grange hall coffee urn for archive days. Odile told me. She's seventy if she's a day, and she files things because she thinks names should go somewhere after people do." He looked up, and his eyes were too bright with fever, and absolutely steady. "If she can do that every month, I can walk four days downhill."

Bram closed his eyes for a second. When he opened them, something had finished settling in him, like sediment clearing in water.

"South, then," he said. "God help us. South."

* * *

But—and here's where my father's river-crossing wisdom comes back around—we didn't move yet. Because moving south meant descending into the same drainage the contractors were climbing, and Bram refused to walk us down the throat of that.

"We split the difference," he said, spreading his map on a flat rock and weighting the corners with stones. "There's an old mining cut here—" his finger traced a line west of our position, contouring along the shoulder of the mountain rather than dropping into the valley— "traverses above their route, comes back to the valley floor below the tree line, maybe six miles short of where they are now. We cross above them. They never see us."

"And if the traverse is washed out?" I asked. I'd ferried enough loggers and miners to know how mining cuts age. Like everything else in these hills: badly.

"Then it's washed out and we come back and take the valley and deal with what's in it." He rolled the map. "It buys us a night's lead. We move at first light."

That night I took first watch, which suited me. I like watch alone. The fire down to embers, the boy breathing in that thin whistling way fevers give you, Bram sleeping the way he does—on his side, facing the slope, one hand open near his knife like a man who'd made peace with waking up badly.

I sat with the compass.

Isandro had left it out of his jacket while he slept—it had started pointing at Bram, steady as a sermon, and he said it made his chest hurt to carry it. So it sat on a rock between us, brass case catching ember-light, needle swinging lazily and then settling, always settling, on the sleeping shape of Bram Galloway.

I'd watched it point at the wreck for Isandro. At Odile—that was me—for one terrible minute at Kettle Bridge, when the corporal was leaning into the truck window and the compass was telling everyone what I hadn't said aloud yet: that my father's name was on that manifest, that I wasn't helping these two out of charity, I was helping myself. The compass doesn't lie. That's the whole horror of it. It just holds up whatever you've buried and lets it gleam.

So I sat there looking at it pointing at Bram, and I thought about what it meant. For Isandro it pointed at the wreck because the wreck held his father's name—what he feared losing was the chance to find it. For me, that day on the bridge, it pointed at me because I feared losing the last piece of my father I'd ever hold. But Bram? Bram owned nothing. That was his entire philosophy, stated often enough to be scripture: own nothing, owe nothing, be seen by no one. A man like that shouldn't have anything for the compass to find.

And yet the needle lay against his sleeping body like it had found true north at last.

Either the compass was broken, or Bram Galloway had something left to lose and hadn't admitted it to himself yet. I knew which bet I'd place. I'd seen how he checked on Isandro during the night watches—pretending to check the weather, checking the boy. I'd seen him give up his dry socks without being asked. Twenty years of invisibility, and the man was coming apart at the seams into something almost decent, and it scared him worse than the navy did.

"You going to stare at that thing all night?" Bram's voice, low, from the bedroll. Not asleep after all. Of course not.

"Just taking readings," I said, and heard him huff—almost a laugh. Isandro's word, borrowed. Our little joke that wasn't a joke.

"It's wrong about me," he said, after a while. "Whatever it thinks it's pointing at."

"Sure," I said. "Compasses are famous for being wrong."

He didn't answer, but I felt him turn over, and I felt the needle swing a half-degree to follow him, faithful as a dog, and I thought: *you poor stubborn man. You're going to walk into that archive and be seen by everybody, and it's going to save your life.*

* * *

First light came up the color of dishwater. We broke camp fast and quiet and took the traverse.

I'll say this for the mining cut: it went. Narrow, crumbling at the edges, a sheer drop on the left that made my ferryman's hands sweat on the rock even in cold air—but it went, contouring along the mountain's shoulder with the valley falling away beneath us. By mid-morning we were a thousand feet above the drainage, moving single file, Isandro between us because Bram had decreed it and because the boy's fever made his footing soft.

That's when I looked down and saw them.

Three figures on the valley trail below, small as chess pieces, stopped. One of them had binoculars raised—not at us, we were too far, too high, folded into the rock—but sweeping the slopes ahead of them. Reading the mountain the way Bram read sky. Looking for exactly the traverse we were standing on.

"They'll find the cut," Bram said quietly. He'd seen them too. "Maybe today, maybe tomorrow. Then they'll know we crossed above them, and they'll stop climbing and start driving. Every road into Alder Creek has a car on it by tomorrow night."

"Then we'd better be faster than cars," I said.

"On foot?" He almost smiled. "No. We'd better be smarter than cars. There's a logging spur off the old mill road, mile and a half past the valley mouth. If it's still passable, it cuts ten miles off the descent and dumps us behind the grange hall." He paused. "If it's not passable, we're walking down the main road past whoever they've parked on it."

"Wonderful," I said. "Two options again. I was hoping for a third."

"There's always a third," Bram said. "It's just usually worse."

Below us, the man with the binoculars lowered them, conferred with the others, and the three chess pieces resumed their climb—upward, away from Alder Creek, hunting the high route we'd already abandoned. We had bought our night's lead. Now we had to spend it.

Isandro, who had heard none of this over the wind, tugged my sleeve. "Are we close?" he asked. Not to the valley—to the archive. To the filing desk. To the moment when his father's name would be spoken into a record that couldn't swallow it back.

"Four days," I said. "Weather willing."

He nodded and touched his jacket, where the papers rode, and behind us the clouds came down another hundred feet, and somewhere below, unseen, the roads were filling up with men who wanted the names to stay dead.

We put our boots to the mining cut and started down toward the valley, toward the mill road, toward the town where a seventy-year-old woman kept a coffee urn warm for anyone who believed names should go somewhere after people do.

The snow followed us down like it had made the same decision we had.
