## 17. Whiteout

The mountain gave us one good hour and then took it back with interest.

We came off the mining cut onto the shoulder of the ridge just after first light, when the sky was the color of dishwater and the wind hadn't yet decided which direction it hated us from. Odile led. She'd grown up on water, she said, and a ridgeline was just a river standing still—same rules, read the current, respect the drop. Isandro walked between us with the manifests wrapped in oilcloth against his chest like a second heartbeat. I brought up the rear, because that's where the person who trusts nobody belongs: last, watching everyone else's back trail, counting the ways this could go wrong.

I'd counted eleven by noon. The twelfth arrived at half past.

It came as a sound first—a long, low exhale from the west, like the range clearing its throat—and then the light went strange, flat and sourceless, the kind of light where shadows stop meaning anything. I've seen that light underwater. It's what the sea does right before it stops being a place and becomes a condition.

"Snow," Odile said, unnecessarily. Snow was already coming down in fat, wind-driven clots, thick enough to taste.

"Southern crossing," I said. "We keep moving or we camp at nine thousand feet in an open bowl with three contractors somewhere behind us reading our tracks like a menu."

"Charming options," Odile said. "Lead on, Galloway."

I led on.

* * *

Rope work is honest work. That's the thing I've always liked about it. A rope doesn't care who you were or what you're running from; it only cares whether you tied your knots right and whether you told the truth about how much you weigh. Twenty years of salvage taught me knots the way priests learn scripture, and for four hours on the southern shoulder of that ridge, knots were the whole of my religion.

We roped up at the first icefield—fifty feet apart, me on point, Isandro anchored in the middle because he was the cargo and cargo rides protected. I ran the line through my hands and called the holds the way I'd call them over a hard wreck: "Shorten up. Two steps left. Trust the picket, not the snow."

Isandro learned fast. Faster than I liked, honestly, because a boy who learns fast starts believing the mountain is on his side, and no mountain is ever on anybody's side. But he placed his feet where I placed mine, kept the rope neither slack nor strangling, and when I looked back once and caught his eye through the driving white, he gave me a thumbs-up with a mitt the size of a dinner plate, and something in my chest did a thing I chose not to examine.

Odile, meanwhile, had opinions.

"Your knots are ugly," she shouted over the wind, some ninety minutes in, while I anchored her across a bullet-hard stretch of blue ice.

"They hold."

"So does rebar, but you don't hang art on it."

"Keep your weight downhill," I said, "and save your criticism for the archive."

She crossed grinning, which I noted was a bad sign. People grin on mountains the way they sing in thunderstorms—right before nature reminds them of the pecking order.

The wind rose through the afternoon the way debt does: gradually, then all at once. By three o'clock we were leaning into it at forty-five degrees, and by four the world had shrunk to a circle maybe thirty feet across, with the three of us at the center and nothing else anywhere, ever. Whiteout isn't darkness. Darkness at least has the decency to be consistent. Whiteout erases the horizon, erases the slope, erases up and down until your own boots look like they're floating on milk. I've had clients pay me to dive in zero visibility, feeling my way along a hull by touch and prayer, and I'll tell you straight: I'd take the black water. At least down there you know which way the surface is.

Up here, I wasn't sure anymore. And I was the one with the compass experience, metaphorically speaking.

"Talk to me," I called back. "Both of you. Constantly."

"Why?" Isandro's voice came thin and wind-shredded.

"Because if you stop answering, I'll know you've gone down. And because voices carry direction better than anything else in this soup. Keep talking."

So they talked. Odile told some rambling story about her father ferrying a prize pig across the river and the pig getting seasick, and Isandro laughed—the real laugh, the one that cracked his voice in the middle—and I listened to them both and steered by their noise, and I want it on the record that I have navigated by sextant, by dead reckoning, by the smell of kelp on a night tide, but I have never navigated by the sound of a teenage boy laughing at a pig joke, and it worked, and I don't know what that says about my life.

* * *

The cornice didn't announce itself. Cornices never do. That's their whole personality.

We'd traversed below a broad roll of slope for the better part of an hour, and I didn't like it—I could feel the wind-loaded snow above us like a held breath—but the alternative was a descent chute full of rockfall debris, so I made the call every diver makes: take the known hazard at a careful pace rather than gamble on the unknown one. I put extra distance in the rope. I moved us one at a time. I told myself the temperature had been cold enough, long enough, that the load would hold till we cleared the roll.

I was wrong by about forty minutes.

It happened to Odile. Of course it happened to Odile. She was in the lead by then—we'd rotated because my shoulders were shot and she claimed, correctly, that she read snow better than I did—and she was maybe fifteen feet ahead of me, testing the slope with her pole, when the entire world above her let go.

There was no dramatic crack. There was a soft whump, the sound of a mattress turning over, and then the slope ahead of her simply wasn't there anymore—it was moving, a silent white avalanche of cornice snow pouring down the lee side of the ridge, and Odile went with it, pole windmilling, face registering not terror exactly but outrage, the expression of someone who cannot believe the river has done this to her.

"ROPE!" I screamed, and threw myself flat, and jammed my axe into whatever would hold, and the line snapped taut and nearly took my arm out of its socket.

Here is what nobody tells you about holding a falling person on a rope: you don't hold them. Not really. Physics holds them, if physics feels like it, and you are mostly a piece of friction with opinions. I got my boots dug in, got the rope wrapped twice around my axe shaft, and leaned back into the slope with everything twenty years of hauling gear bags up ladders had built into my back, and the rope burned through my gloves and into my palms, and Odile swung out over the void like a pendulum at the end of the world.

And Isandro—God help me, Isandro—came forward instead of back. Fourteen years old, fever-scarred, the boy I'd spent two weeks teaching to be small and quiet, and he dropped onto the snow beside me, wrapped his mitts around the rope behind mine, and leaned back with all hundred and thirty pounds of him, and yelled, "I'VE GOT HER," in a voice that believed it.

He didn't have her. Neither of us had her alone. Together we had her, barely, inch by screaming inch, while the cornice snow finished its long white sigh below and settled into silence, and Odile dangled over a drop I couldn't see the bottom of, and said, in a perfectly level voice, "If either of you tells anyone I screamed, I will deny it."

"You didn't scream," Isandro panted.

"I screamed internally. Extensively."

We hauled. Hand over hand, boots punching through crust, my palms leaving red prints on the rope that the cold turned brown before they hit the snow. It took ten minutes or an hour; time goes strange when you're pulling someone back into the world. When her boots finally crested the lip and she rolled onto solid slope and lay there breathing hard, Isandro fell on her the way boys fall on dogs, hugging her too hard, and she let him, and I sat back on my heels and shook, quietly, where I hoped neither of them could tell.

"Your knots," Odile said finally, from inside the hug, "are officially forgiven."

* * *

We camped that night in a hollow behind a spine of granite, because stopping was suicide and moving was also suicide and I picked the suicide with shelter. I dug a platform into the drift, we got the tent up in conditions that would have made a tent salesman weep, and we crowded in with the stove hissing and the wind trying its key in every seam.

My hands were ruined. The rope had opened both palms in lines, neat and deep, like the mountain had decided to measure me. Odile dressed them without a word, winding gauze with the same competence she brought to engines and ferries and, apparently, to dangling men, and Isandro held the flashlight and watched my face while she worked, and I realized he was checking whether I was scared.

"Ask me tomorrow," I said.

"I didn't say anything."

"You were going to. Everybody wants to ask the diver if he gets scared." I flexed my bandaged fingers. "Answer's yes. Today especially. Today I was scared the way you're supposed to be scared—usefully."

"What's usefully?"

"Scared enough to double-check the anchor. Not so scared you freeze." I nodded at his jacket, where the manifests rode. "You've been carrying those since the mine. You afraid of them?"

He thought about it seriously, the way he thought about everything, which is one of the things about him that had stopped annoying me sometime around chapter twelve of our acquaintance and started mattering to me in a way I declined to name even privately.

"No," he said. "They're heavy, but they're not scary. They're just... waiting. Like they know where they're going and they're patient about it."

"That's not how paper works."

"That's how these papers work." He settled back against his pack. "Bram?"

"Yeah."

"When Odile went over the edge—you didn't hesitate. You didn't do the math. I saw your face. You just went."

I looked at the stove instead of at him. "Math was already done," I said. "Did it weeks ago. Somewhere around Kettle Bridge, probably. You two were preloaded."

Odile, zipped into her bag, said sleepily, "That's the nicest thing you've ever said, and it sounds like an invoice."

"Go to sleep."

* * *

Morning came gray and quieter, the storm spent, the world remade into clean unbroken white with no tracks on it anywhere—including ours. The wind had erased us completely. For the first time since the tender, nobody alive knew where we were, and I stood outside the tent breathing the razor air and felt something I hadn't felt in twenty years of professional invisibility: I didn't want to be lost. I wanted to be found—by the right people, at the right desk, in a town with a coffee urn and a filing clerk, where these names could stop being cargo and become record.

"Visibility's holding," Odile said, crawling out beside me. "Six hours of travel puts us on the saddle. After that it's all downhill to Alder Creek."

"And the contractors?"

"Behind us, buried under a foot of fresh, reading blank snow." She smiled, then didn't. "Unless they're ahead of us."

"Nobody's ahead of us. Nobody sane crosses that saddle before us."

Which is when Isandro crawled out, zipped and pale and squinting into the east, and said, "Then who's that?"

Downslope, maybe a mile out, at the edge of visibility where the white world dissolved into gray, something dark was moving. One figure. Then two. Then a third, cresting a rise we ourselves had meant to crest by noon, walking—not hurrying, not floundering—walking with the unhurried confidence of men who knew exactly where we were going, because they'd read the same map we had, and had decided the best place to catch a family was at the top of the last climb, where there was nowhere left to run but down.

The compass, in Isandro's pocket, I would learn later, had been trembling since dawn.

"Well," Odile said, cracking her knuckles inside her mitts. "Race them to the saddle?"

I looked at my bandaged hands, at the boy with the papers, at the woman who'd come back over the edge for us, and I heard myself say the least Bram Galloway sentence of my entire life:

"No. We make them race *us*."
