## 18. The Archive at Alder Creek

Alder Creek smelled like woodsmoke and wet wool and, faintly, like the inside of a library nobody had lied in yet. I want to remember that. The smell before.

We came down off the ridge at first light with our boots wrapped in burlap because Bram said the last two miles of any approach are where you get stupid. Odile's hands were still shaking from the cornice—she kept flexing them like she was testing whether they belonged to her—and Isandro, that's me, I was carrying a canvas satchel against my chest like it was a baby. Inside the satchel: forty-one pages of sealed manifests. Forty-one pages of people the state decided were already dead before they died. My father's name was on page nine. I'd read it so many times by then that the ink had started to feel like something I'd written myself.

The compass sat in my pocket doing nothing. It had gone quiet after the mine, after the fever, after it started pointing at Bram instead of the wreck. Quiet like a held breath.

"Rules," Bram said, crouched behind the last stand of alders, looking down at the town the way he looked at everything—like he was pricing it. "We're three salvagers who found papers floating. We don't know each other's histories. We don't use names unless someone makes us."

"You've used my name twice since breakfast," I said.

"I'm allowed. I'm the adult."

"You're the oldest," Odile said. "That's different."

He gave her a look that would have killed a weaker friendship, and she smiled at him, and something in his face did that thing it did lately—the thing where the wall got a window in it. Twenty years of miles, and here he was walking into a town to be seen on purpose. I don't think he knew yet that he was going to offer himself up. I think some part of him had been packing for that trip since the tender burned our camp. But that's getting ahead of the story, and Bram always says the story gets ahead of you exactly once, and that's the time it kills you.

* * *

The archive was a stone building with a green door, sitting across from a church and a feed store like it was daring anyone to take it seriously. A brass plaque said REGIONAL RECORDS — ALDER CREEK DEPOSITORY, and under that, smaller: EST. 1911. BY APPOINTMENT OR DURING PUBLIC HOURS. Public hours had started twenty minutes ago. There was one clerk visible through the window, a woman with gray hair pinned up like a flag of surrender, and there was—

There was a navy car parked in the alley beside the building. Unmarked except for the way unmarked government cars are always marked, if you've spent enough time being watched by them. Clean, dark, engine ticking as it cooled. Parked in the alley, not the street. People who park in alleys aren't visiting. They're waiting.

Bram saw it the same second I did. His hand found my shoulder—not pushing, just landing, like an anchor deciding where it wanted to be.

"Change of plan?" Odile asked quietly.

"No," Bram said. "Accelerated plan." He looked at the green door for a long moment. "Navy got here first. Means they haven't gotten the manifests destroyed yet, or they wouldn't need to sit on the clerk. Means we still have a window." He turned to me. "How many pages?"

"Forty-one. Plus the cover sheet with the transport's registry number."

"And the filing fee?"

I patted my pocket. "Eleven dollars and sixty cents and a compass worth more than this town."

"Don't tell them about the compass."

"Bram. When have I ever told anyone anything?"

He looked at me. "Constantly. You narrate your own life like it's a radio serial."

"That's called being honest."

"That's called being a target." But he almost smiled when he said it, which was new, and which scared me more than the navy car did, honestly.

* * *

Inside smelled like paper dust and radiator heat. The reading room was long and narrow, oak tables worn pale at the elbows, green-shaded lamps burning even though daylight came through the windows fine. Two men sat at the far table. Civilian clothes, wrong posture. One had a newspaper he wasn't reading; the other had his hands folded around a coffee cup he never drank from. I'd seen that exact tableau before, outside a chandlery in Port Merrow, the day before the survey team stopped existing.

The gray-haired clerk stood behind her counter, and when she saw us come in, something crossed her face too fast to name. Fear, maybe. Or apology.

"Public hours," Bram said pleasantly, loud enough for the whole room. "Wonderful. We're here to file documents."

The clerk's eyes flicked toward the two men and back. "Filing requires... the registrar handles intake. He's not in until—"

"The plaque says public hours," Bram said, still pleasant, still loud. "It also says by appointment. We didn't make an appointment, so we'll take public hours. Section twelve, depository of record, right? Anything filed during public hours enters the public record same day. That's the law. I read it on the train." He hadn't read it on the train. He'd read it on the hermit's battered copy of the municipal code, by lamplight, at Cold Ledge, while the rest of us slept. Bram memorized things the way other people prayed.

One of the men at the table unfolded himself and stood. Big, but big in the way of someone who'd been told his size was sufficient and believed it. "Sir, this facility is undergoing an inventory audit. You'll need to come back next week."

"Audit," Bram repeated. "Of a public archive. During public hours. With no posted notice." He strolled toward the counter like a man with nowhere better to be, which was a lie so practiced it had become true. "Ma'am, I'd like to file a document. It's a salvage recovery—maritime records, section twelve. Here's the fee." He put eleven dollars and sixty cents on the counter, counted out, exact.

The clerk looked at the money. She looked at the man standing now, and his partner rising behind him. And then she did the bravest small thing I have ever witnessed, and I include mountain rescues and flood sirens in that count. She opened the intake ledger, picked up her pen, and said, in a voice that shook only a little:

"Name of filer?"

* * *

What happened next happened fast, the way bad weather happens fast.

The big man moved toward the counter. Bram stepped sideways—not blocking, just present, the way a rock is present in a river. "Careful," Bram said, conversational. "Interfering with a lawful filing is a federal offense. I'd hate for you to commit one in front of witnesses." He gestured at the windows, the street beyond, a woman walking a dog, a boy delivering bread. "Nice town. Lots of eyes."

"We can do this the easy way," the man said.

"I've done the easy way," Bram said. "Twenty years of it. It's overrated."

And that's when I understood—I mean really understood, in my stomach—that he wasn't talking about the filing anymore. He'd walked into this room knowing how it would go. He'd counted the men, counted the exits, counted the minutes until the town filled up with its morning errands, and he'd walked in anyway, because the math only worked if somebody stood between those two men and the counter, and Bram Galloway had apparently decided, somewhere on a scree slope or in a snowed-in mine or maybe twenty years ago in water I'd never see, that the somebody was going to be him.

"Isandro," he said quietly, without turning his head. "Counter. Now."

I went. My legs felt borrowed. I put the satchel on the counter and the clerk's breath caught when she saw the seal stamps—the state seal, broken properly, chain of custody documented in Odile's careful hand all down the margins. The hermit had taught her the forms. An archivist's revenge is paperwork.

"What am I filing?" the clerk asked.

"Sealed manifests, transport *Corvane*, recovered from a wreck site in coastal waters," I said. My voice cracked on *Corvane*. Page nine. My father. "Names of passengers listed as lost with all hands. They weren't all hands. Some of them were put there. And the state has known for—" I stopped, because my voice had left entirely.

"Recovered how?" the big man demanded, coming closer. "By whom? Those are stolen government documents. That boy is a thief."

"Yeah," Bram said. "About that."

* * *

Here is the thing I will carry longer than the cold carried me, longer than the scar on my palm from the wreck grating. Bram turned around. He faced the two men fully, squared up, hands loose at his sides—a diver's stance, balanced for water that isn't there—and he said, clear and level, so the clerk writing in the ledger could hear every word:

"The documents were recovered by me. Salvage diver, twenty years licensed. The boy is my deckhand. He touched nothing. Whatever you think was stolen, I stole it. I broke into the survey camp, I dove the wreck, I cut the seals. Him, the woman who came in with us—they were hired labor. Hired by me. Every decision was mine."

"That's not—" I started.

"Isandro." Just my name. But the way he said it closed my mouth like a hatch.

The clerk's pen scratched. *Filed by:* and Bram gave his name—his real one, the one he'd buried under twenty years of miles, the one he'd told us once, drunk on nothing stronger than exhaustion, that he'd traded away so thoroughly he sometimes forgot it. He spelled it for her. He watched her write it down like a man watching his own grave get dug, and he did not flinch.

"Entry into the public record is effective immediately," the clerk said. Her voice had steadied. Maybe courage is contagious. Maybe she'd spent thirty years stamping deeds and marriage licenses and waiting for one day when the paper mattered. "Certified copies available upon request. The original is retained in perpetuity." She stamped the ledger. The sound it made was very small and very enormous, like a single nail going into a very large coffin.

"In perpetuity," the big man repeated flatly.

"Her words, not mine," Bram said. "But yes. You can burn this building down, and the record exists. It's been entered. There's a certified copy procedure, there's a notice requirement, there's a whole apparatus now. Congratulations. You've made it official." He smiled, and it was the worst smile I've ever seen on him, all teeth and no warmth, the smile of a man selling his own hide and naming his price. "So the question isn't whether the names survive. The question is what you're going to do about the thief standing in front of you. And I'd think hard, because this town wakes up slow but it wakes up loud, and my associates—" he nodded at Odile, who had positioned herself by the door with the casualness of a woman guarding it, "—know journalists. Plural. With appetites."

Nobody moved. The radiator ticked. Somewhere outside, the dog-walking woman passed again, and the world continued being ordinary, which suddenly seemed like the most precious thing in existence.

The two men exchanged a look. It was the look of professionals recalculating. Then the big one said, "You'll be hearing from us," which is what cowards say when the arithmetic turns against them, and they walked out. The navy car sat in the alley another ten minutes—we watched it through the window, all three of us, nobody breathing much—and then it pulled away, unhurried, the way a predator leaves when it's decided today isn't the day but hasn't ruled out tomorrow.

* * *

Afterward, the clerk—her name was Mrs. Vantol, she told us, thirty-one years at the depository—slid the ledger across the counter so we could see the entry. Bram Galloway, filer of record. Date, hour, minute. In perpetuity.

"You didn't have to do that," I said. My voice was still wrecked. "Say it was you. I carried them. I found them. It should be—"

"It should be nothing," Bram said. "It's paper. Paper doesn't care whose name is on the line. But charges attach to a name, and I've got twenty years of being nobody to spend, and you've got—" he stopped. Looked at me. Started over, quieter. "You've got a father on page nine, and a life that hasn't happened yet. Mine mostly has. It's not sacrifice, kid. It's accounting."

"That's the biggest lie you've ever told," Odile said from the door, "and I once heard you tell a corporal you were a fish buyer."

"Fish are bought," Bram said. "It's plausible."

Mrs. Vantol cleared her throat. "There's a procedural matter," she said, and her face had gone strange again, that fear-apology look from the beginning. "The men who were here before you. They weren't only waiting. They filed a request. A destruction order—for certain records. Transport-related. It's signed by a regional director." She pulled a folder from under the counter. "It's dated tomorrow. Which means tonight, after close, they have legal authority to remove and destroy anything matching the description. Including—" she tapped the ledger gently, "—well. Not including this. This is entered. But the underlying registry files, the *Corvane*'s original records, the corroborating materials? Those are still vulnerable. And there's a courier scheduled to collect at six a.m."

Odile and I looked at each other. Then we both looked at Bram.

Bram looked at the ledger entry for a long time. At his own name, sitting there in a public book, in a public room, in a town full of people who by tomorrow would know exactly who he was and what he'd done. Everything he'd spent twenty years avoiding, achieved in one stroke, permanent as a tattoo.

"Well," he said finally, rolling his shoulders like a man diving into cold water, which is the only kind of dive he'd ever known how to make. "Then we've got until six a.m. to figure out how to keep a town awake."

Outside, far off toward the ridge, a sound rolled down the valley—long, wavering, unmistakable. Odile's hand went to her mouth.

The flood siren. Somebody was already sounding the alarm.
