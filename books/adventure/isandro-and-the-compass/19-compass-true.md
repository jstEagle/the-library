## 19. Compass True

I've spent twenty years learning how a room reads you. A bar, a dock office, a customs shed—every room has a way of looking, and the trick is to be furniture. Be the thing people's eyes slide off on their way to something interesting. I'd been furniture so long I'd forgotten what it felt like to be looked at.

The reading room at Alder Creek made me look like a man with a name.

It was a small municipal building, the kind built by towns that wanted to seem more important than they were—stone facade, brass fittings gone green, a clock over the door that had been wrong since before either of us was born. Inside: oak tables worn pale at the elbows, a smell of cold paper and floor wax, and a clerk named Mr. Pell who had spent the morning being polite in the specific way of a man who has been told what will happen if he isn't.

The navy had come through hours ahead of us. We knew because the coffee in his pot was fresh and he wouldn't meet Odile's eyes, and because there was a gray sedan parked across the street with two men in it who had been very still for a very long time. Stillness is its own kind of uniform. You can't buy that kind of patience; it's issued to you along with the rest of your gear.

Isandro sat at one of the tables with the manifests wrapped in oilcloth inside his coat, both hands flat on the wood like a boy keeping himself from fidgeting. The compass rode in his pocket. He'd told me on the walk down from Cold Ledge that it had gotten heavier. Not physically—he checked, he said, weighing it in his palm like a fish—but some other way. Like it knew we were close.

"Mr. Galloway," Pell said, "if this is about a salvage claim, the regional office handles those in—"

"It's not about a claim." I set my hands on his desk, palms down, so he could see they were empty. Twenty years of diving teaches you what empty hands are worth. "It's about filing documents into the public record. Which is a service this office provides. To anyone."

"Anyone," Pell repeated.

"Anyone."

He looked at me then, really looked, and I watched him do the arithmetic every compromised man does: which side of this am I going to end up on, and which side will remember me longer. I didn't blame him for the arithmetic. I'd have done it myself, once. What I did instead was lean in close enough that only he could hear.

"There's a transport at the bottom of the water near my mooring," I said quietly, "carrying sixty-one people your government listed as drowned forty years ago. Drowned before they ever boarded. These are the manifests. They're going in your drawer today, Mr. Pell, and in twenty years when somebody writes the book about this, the question won't be whether you filed them. It'll be whether you helped burn them."

That's not the speech I'd planned. I'd planned nothing—I've never trusted plans past the first complication. But I watched his jaw work, and I watched him glance at the window, at the sedan, and I understood that fear doesn't pick sides. It just picks a direction.

The door opened behind us.

Not the contractors. Worse, in a way—a woman in a good coat with a navy lanyard under it, flanked by the two men from the sedan now wearing their stillness indoors. She smiled at Pell like they were old friends at a funeral.

"Mr. Pell. There's been a clerical error regarding materials reported missing from a government survey. We're here to collect them before they cause anyone embarrassment."

Embarrassment. That was the word they'd chosen. Sixty-one names reduced to a stain someone should dab out before company arrived.

"They're not collected yet," I said. "They're not anything yet. They're paper on a table in a public building, and the second they're entered into the record, they're history, and history's the one cargo nobody salvages."

The woman turned her smile on me, and it was a good smile, professional, the kind that's been drilled. "Bram Galloway. Salvage diver. Unlicensed work on restricted sites. We have a file on you that would curl your hair."

"You'd be surprised what's already curled it."

* * *

Here's the thing about holding a door: it's the least glamorous job in any fight, and it's the one everything else depends on. Somebody has to be the wall while somebody else becomes the story.

Odile had slipped out during the talking—she was better at leaving rooms than any of us, ferry blood, always knowing when the current turns—and I hadn't liked letting her go, but the plan, such as it was, needed the street. Isandro needed the crowd. And the crowd needed a reason to exist.

Because that was the whole shape of it, the thing I'd finally understood somewhere on the mountain: you can't win against men like these by running or hiding or even fighting. You win by making witnesses. A secret dies in public. That's all. That's the entire weapon. Sixty-one dead people needed one crowded room and a filing stamp, and everything we'd bled through—the scree slope, the mine adit, the whiteout, the hermit's cabin smelling of kerosene and old grievances—was just the long walk to a clerk's drawer.

The contractors moved first. Of course they did. Men paid by the hour don't wait for the plot.

The taller one came around the table toward Isandro with the unhurried confidence of somebody who'd done this in countries without clocks, and I did the only thing I've ever been genuinely excellent at: I put my body where it wasn't welcome. Shoulder into his chest, boots planted on the oak floor, twenty years of mass and stubbornness between him and the boy.

"Galloway—" the woman started.

"I'm the salvage thief," I said, loud enough for the whole room. "Whatever's missing, whatever's stolen, whatever's embarrassing—it's me. One man. Write it up however you want. But those papers go through that door over everybody's objections, or this town learns today what its navy does with paperwork."

The tall one hit me. I want to say I took it well. I took it mostly well. There's a technique to absorbing a punch when falling down is the last thing you can afford—turn with it, keep your feet under the noise—and I got an elbow up and bought us another thirty seconds, which is the currency everything runs on.

And then the world outside went mad.

* * *

The flood siren.

Alder Creek sits in a river valley and keeps a siren for the spring melt, a thing that hasn't sounded in living memory except for tests. It started as a low moan that climbed into a scream, and I heard it through stone walls and thought, absurdly, of the wreck—of sonar pings, of the sound the instruments made when the survey team took their readings, the screaming Isandro described. The town's own voice, raised at last.

Every head in the reading room turned toward the windows. And through them we saw it: people coming. Shopkeepers, schoolkids, a woman still holding a half-waxed floor buffer from the hardware store, the volunteer fire crew jogging in their yellow coats because a siren means duty even when it means confusion. Odile stood at the base of the siren tower, hand still on the lever, and she wasn't hiding anymore either. She was standing in the open, in plain view, daring the town to look away.

"That's not a flood," the navy woman said flatly.

"No," I agreed, spitting blood onto their nice floor. "It's a witness pool."

Pell rose from his desk. I'll remember this longer than the punch: a small round man in a cardigan, terrified, doing the arithmetic one final time and arriving, God help him, at the right answer. He unlocked the filing cabinet himself. He set out the forms himself. He stamped the date line with a shaking hand and said, in a voice that cracked but carried, "Public record requires submission in the presence of—of members of the community. Please form a line."

A line formed. That's the part that still gets me. Nobody voted. Nobody debated. A line formed, because a line is what a town does when it decides, all at once, which side of the glass it's on.

Isandro walked through it.

I held the door—literally, one boot wedged against it, one eye on the contractors, who had gone very quiet in the way of men recalculating. The tall one looked at the crowd, at the fire crew, at the woman with the floor buffer, and did the math professionals do: the job was no longer worth the optics. He stepped back. His partner followed. The navy woman stood alone in a room filling with her own citizens, and for the first time since the tender appeared beyond my buoy line, I watched invisibility flow the other direction. She became the furniture.

Isandro unwrapped the oilcloth at the front table. His hands shook. He'd carried those pages through snow and gunfire and fever, and now he laid them down gently, like setting something to rest, and read the first name aloud.

Then the next. Then the next.

The compass came out of his pocket while he read—I saw him take it out, maybe without deciding to—and it went wild. Spinning. Whipping around its pin like a live thing caught under a glass, needle blurring, pointing at everyone and no one, at the crowd, at the filing cabinet, at the door, at me. All that weight it had been carrying, all that wanting, and now the thing it wanted was happening in real time, six inches from its face, and it couldn't choose a direction because every direction was true.

The names kept coming. People in the line began saying them after him—repeating them, the way you repeat a vow. An old man near the window went gray when he heard one particular name and sat down slowly in a chair like his legs had been unplugged. Odile came in from the cold, cheeks burning, and stood beside Isandro and said the names of her father's row with him, word for word, because she'd memorized them on a river, in the dark, months ago.

Stamp. Stamp. Stamp. Pell's stamp fell like a slow heartbeat. Filed. Entered. Witnessed.

And somewhere around the fortieth name, the compass stopped spinning.

It didn't settle north. It didn't point at the wreck, or at Odile, or at me, or at anything in that room full of things a boy could be afraid to lose. It swung once, twice, slower each time, like a pendulum running down, and then it lay still in Isandro's palm, needle level, pointing nowhere at all. Needing nothing. Whatever it had been for, whatever it had wanted delivered, it was delivered. Sixty-one names, public record, stamped and witnessed and irreversible.

Isandro stared at it. Then he laughed—one short, wet, disbelieving sound—and closed his fist around it, and finished the list.

* * *

The navy woman left before the last name. No drama. She paused at the door I was still holding, looked at me with something that wasn't quite respect and wasn't quite threat, and said, "This isn't over, Galloway. Filing paper is easy. Keeping it filed is another matter."

"Then you'd best hope," I said, "that the whole town heard every name. Because I'm done being hard to find."

She left. The contractors' sedan pulled away with the wipers going, though it wasn't raining. The siren wound down to silence, and in the ringing quiet afterward, Alder Creek did what small towns do: it made coffee, and asked questions, and didn't leave.

Pell locked the cabinet with the manifests inside and handed Isandro the receipt—an official document, typed, numbered, bearing a government stamp—like handing him a sword hilt-first. The boy took it in both hands. He was crying now, quietly, the way kids cry when they finally stop having to be brave, and Odile had her arm around his shoulders, and I stood in the doorway of a public building in a town I'd known for four hours, holding a door that no longer needed holding, and realized I didn't know what to do with my hands.

Twenty years, I'd measured my life in miles. Distance traveled, distance kept, distance between me and everything that could cost me. Standing there, I tried to count the miles from that wreck to this room and couldn't make the number mean anything. Because the number that mattered was smaller. It was the width of a reading room. It was the distance between a scared kid and a filing desk. It was how far a town will walk when a siren sounds and someone finally tells it the truth.

The old man by the window rose, crossed the room, and shook Isandro's hand with both of his. Said a name—not a manifest name, his own—and thanked him in a voice like gravel underwater.

Outside, the light was failing, and down the street, past the siren tower where Odile's handprint was still on the frost of the lever, a figure stood at the edge of the lamplight watching the municipal building. Not navy. Not contractor. Someone else entirely—someone who had been waiting for exactly this moment, and who now turned and walked unhurriedly toward the one place in Alder Creek none of us had thought to guard:

the archive annex, where the originals were kept.
