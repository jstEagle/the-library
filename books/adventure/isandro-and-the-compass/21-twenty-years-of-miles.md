## 21. Twenty Years of Miles

The fine came to four hundred dollars and the cost of a certified letter, which struck me as the cheapest I'd ever been. Twenty years of staying invisible, two decades of measuring my life in miles so no one could ever put a price on me, and in the end the state priced me at four hundred dollars plus postage. Odile framed the invoice. She hung it over the workbench in the boat shed like a diploma.

"Salvage thief," she said, reading it aloud for maybe the hundredth time, savoring it. "Reduced to a fine. You should be insulted."

"I am," I said. "Deeply."

"You're not. You're pleased as a cat in cream." She tapped the paper. "They couldn't make anything stick because you made sure everything stuck to you instead. That was the whole point of your little performance at the archive, wasn't it? Sole salvage thief. One man, one crime, everyone else clean."

I didn't answer that, mostly because she was right and partly because my hands were busy. There's always something to do with hands in a boat shed, and I had learned over the winter that busy hands are how a man survives having nothing left to hide behind. The *Marta June* — my boat, seized in October, returned in March with a navy inventory sticker still on the wheelhouse glass — sat on the cradle outside with her hull stripped to bare wood, and every plank of her needed my attention more than my conscience did.

That's the thing nobody tells you about being seen: afterward, there's nowhere to put yourself. For twenty years I had been a man shaped exactly like the space he occupied, and now the space had changed and I hadn't. Alder Creek was too small for anonymity and too stubborn to care about my history. Mrs. Halloran at the diner called me "the diver" like it was a first name. The clerk from the archive — the one who hadn't been compromised after all, just scared — nodded to me on the street now, slow and deliberate, the way you nod to someone who held a door for you once and might hold it again.

And Isandro. Isandro followed me around like a shadow that asked questions.

"Show me the gauge again," he said, coming into the shed with his boots leaving snow slush on my clean floor, which he knew I hated, which was why he did it.

"You were here yesterday."

"The gauge again anyway."

I handed it to him. He turned it over, checked the face, thumbed the bezel, and looked at me expectantly. This had become our liturgy. He'd learned the pressure gauge, the depth tables, the knot board, the parts of a regulator in order of how they fail. What he wanted wasn't the information. What he wanted was the standing appointment — the fact that there was a place where somebody expected him at a certain hour and would notice if he didn't come. I recognized the hunger because I'd spent twenty years mistaking its absence for freedom.

"Max working pressure," I said.

"Two thousand five hundred psi. Never trust a gauge that reads zero on a full tank, because then it's the gauge lying or the tank empty and you don't get to pick which."

"And?"

"And you check it twice. Once before you're wet and once when you can still turn back." He set it down carefully. "Bram. When do I actually get in the water?"

I'd been putting this off since January with the excuse of ice, then cold, then runoff, and by April I was out of excuses and we both knew it. The inquiry had opened the wreck site under protection — divers in government rubber going down to where I'd found him half-frozen among the crates — and the boy had watched from the seawall every single day, hands in pockets, saying nothing. A kid who stowed away on a survey ship to find his father's name, who walked manifests through a crowd while contractors closed in, and he stood at a railing like it was a church service he wasn't allowed to take communion at.

"Sunday," I said. "The cove, not the wreck. Wreck's off limits until the recovery team clears it, and I'm not losing my certification teaching a minor to dive illegally."

"That's the only reason?"

"No," I said. "The other reason is I've never taught anybody anything and I'm afraid I'll be bad at it."

He considered that with the gravity he gave everything, then said, "You taught me knots. You taught me the compass work. You taught me how to read water before I cross it." He shrugged. "You're bad at admitting you're good at things. That's different."

Out of the mouths of teenagers. I went back to sanding and let him sweep the floor, badly, and neither of us said anything else, and it was — I want to be honest here, because honesty is the whole tax I'm paying on that fine — it was the best hour of my day. It was the best hour of most of my days now, and some mornings I woke up in the room above the shed that Odile rented me and felt the old reflex fire, the one that said *move, go, put miles between you and whatever knows your name*, and then I'd hear the kettle downstairs, or Isandro's boots on the stairs, and the reflex would die without ever becoming a decision. Stillness, I was learning, isn't the absence of running. It's running that keeps getting interrupted.

* * *

The hearing itself took forty minutes on a Tuesday, in a county courtroom that smelled like floor wax and old radiators. The inquiry had gone up the chain all winter — the manifests filed at Alder Creek, read into record, names spoken aloud in open session until the state ran out of ways to call them clerical errors — and what trickled down to me was a charge of unauthorized salvage reduced to a civil penalty, plus a suspended license that would be reinstated "upon review," which is bureaucratic for *when we stop being embarrassed*.

My lawyer was a public defender named Cho who had taken my case pro bono and out of pure fascination. "Do you understand," she'd asked me in February, "that you confessed to everything? Voluntarily? In writing?"

"It seemed efficient."

"People spend fortunes avoiding what you did for free."

"They do," I agreed. "I used to be one of them."

In the courtroom, the navy sent a lieutenant with a folder full of nothing, because everything worth hiding had already been read aloud by a sixteen-year-old in front of witnesses and flood sirens. The judge — an old woman with reading glasses on a beaded chain, who had clearly been briefed on the newspapers and disapproved of them — looked at me over those glasses for a long moment.

"Mister Galloway. You recovered equipment from a restricted site, obstructed a federal operation, and harbored a minor." She let that sit. "You also, according to testimony I am not permitted to fully credit, preserved evidence that this court has been told is of significant public interest. The state has elected to reduce the charges. Do you have anything to say before I impose the penalty?"

I thought about it. I thought about twenty years of rehearsed silence, of the practiced blank face, of the contract dives I was paid to forget. I thought about a botched job years ago where a man drowned and I took money to have no name and no record, and how I'd built a whole life out of that transaction, miles instead of years, nothing owned, nothing owed.

"I'd like to say something," I heard myself say.

Cho's pen stopped moving.

"When I took that contract," I said, "I was told not to ask who owned what I pulled up. And I didn't ask, for a long time, because not asking felt like freedom. It wasn't. It was just loneliness with better weather." The courtroom was quiet enough to hear the radiator tick. "What was on that wreck were people the state decided not to count. I know what it is to decide not to count. So if the question is whether I regret trespassing on their grave to bring their names up — no, ma'am. I regret the twenty years I spent practicing not seeing."

The judge looked at me for a while longer. Then she said, "Four hundred dollars," in a tone that suggested she was fining me for the speech rather than the salvage, and banged her gavel like she was closing a lid on something.

Outside, Odile was waiting on the courthouse steps with Isandro, and the boy was grinning like he'd personally argued my case. "You gave a whole speech," he said. "In court. You hate talking."

"I hate lying," I said. "Turns out talking's fine when it's true."

Odile shook the newspaper she'd brought — regional edition, the story buried below the fold but present, *Diver Fined in Wreck Records Case; Inquiry Continues* — and folded it into her coat pocket like she was keeping it for a scrapbook. "Twenty years of miles," she said, "and it ends with a parking ticket."

"It doesn't end," I said. "It parks."

She laughed, and Isandro laughed, and we walked down to the water together, three people who six months ago had been strangers with separate reasons to run, and nobody ran anywhere.

* * *

Spring came to Alder Creek the way it comes to coast towns — sideways, with rain, then all at once. The boat shed filled up with other people's projects. Word travels strangely in small places: the diver who stood up to the navy became, locally, just the guy who's good with fiberglass, and the work arrived the way work does when you stop hiding from it. A gillnetter with a soft transom. A skiff that had been upside down in someone's yard for a decade. Mrs. Halloran's late husband's rowboat, which she brought in wrapped in a tarp and wouldn't explain, and which I rebuilt over three weeks without asking, because some jobs are prayers and you don't interview the praying.

Isandro worked mornings at the shed and afternoons at school, where Odile had enrolled him through a combination of charm, forged residency documents she was unrepentant about, and the inquiry lawyer's phone number. His father's name — Reyes, Tomás — had been read into the record in the third week of hearings, and I'd been beside him when it happened, close enough to feel the breath go out of him like a line going slack. He didn't cry in the session. He cried later, in the shed, holding a sander he wasn't using, and I stood next to him and said nothing, because there are times when saying nothing is the whole job, and twenty years of solitude had at least taught me that much.

On Sundays we dove the cove. I rigged his gear myself, checked it twice, made him recite the checks back, and took him down in twelve feet of green water over eelgrass and old pilings, where the light came down in columns and the crabs regarded us with the indifference of landlords. He was a natural in the way that broke my heart a little — easy in the water, unhurried, breathing like the sea was a room he'd always known. Kids who grew up afraid learn to move quiet, and quiet translates.

"Bram." His voice through the regulator, muffled and delighted. "There's a bottle down here. It's got a note."

It did. Some kid's message from decades back, ink faded to a ghost, cork fused with salt. We brought it up and dried it out on the workbench and never could read more than *to whoever* and what might have been a name starting with M. Isandro wanted to throw it back in with a new note inside. So we did. His said: *We found yours. We're still here.*

That's the kind of thing I would have mocked, two years ago. I want that on the record. Two years ago I'd have called it sentimental garbage and meant it, because meaning it kept the walls up. Now I keep a bottle's answer in a boat shed and sleep fine.

* * *

The compass sits in the drawer of the workbench, second one down, under the spare gauges. Inert. It has been inert since the day at the filing desk — needle wandering, settling, pointing at nothing, needing nothing, whatever current it ran on finally discharged by a stack of manifests entered into public record. Isandro checks it sometimes, the way you check a scar. He picks it up, watches the needle drift without conviction, and puts it back.

"It's just brass now," he said in April, turning it under the work lamp.

"Yeah."

"Do you think it was ever magic? Or do you think it was always just — grief, with a bearing?"

I thought about it longer than he probably wanted me to. "I think most things that look like magic are just attention," I said. "Something paying attention to what matters. The compass paid attention. Now the record pays attention. Same function, worse special effects."

He smiled at that, and put it away, and that was the last metaphysical conversation we had about it, though I notice he still taps the drawer twice when he passes, some private superstition, gratitude with a knock code.

The inquiry released the wreck site to open recovery in May. They asked me to consult — me, Bram Galloway, whose name hadn't appeared on a document anyone wanted in twenty years — and I said yes before the sentence was finished, which frightened me more than the contractors ever had. Saying yes fast turns out to be a habit you build like any other. First yes costs blood. The rest get cheaper.

* * *

June, and the *Marta June* went back in the water.

Odile ran the winch. Isandro handled lines. Mrs. Halloran brought sandwiches and commentary. Half the town drifted down to watch, because in Alder Creek a relaunching is an event and I had stopped pretending I wasn't part of the town, which was the real relaunching, the boat was just the ceremony. She sat on the water like she belonged there, which she does, which I suppose I do now too, and I stood at her wheel with the inventory sticker peeled off the glass and the adhesive ghost still showing, and decided to leave the ghost. Let it show. Evidence of where she'd been. Evidence of where I'd been.

"Where first?" Isandro asked, at the helm beside me, fourteen now by his best guess, which is what you say about a kid whose birthday is a guess.

"Cove," I said. "Then, when you're certified—" I stopped. Corrected myself. "When you're ready. The wreck. Both of us. There are men down there the state left, and somebody who knows the layout should walk the recovery team through it."

"Somebody," he repeated, catching me doing it, catching me hiding inside the passive voice the way I used to hide inside miles.

"Me," I said. "I should. I will."

He nodded, satisfied, and put his hand on the wheel next to mine, and we idled out past the buoy line — past the exact spot where, two years ago, a navy tender had sat watching me and I had pretended not to see it, invisibility mistaken for freedom — and I looked back once at Alder Creek getting smaller, and it didn't look smaller. It looked like the center of something. It looked like the place the compass would have pointed, if the compass had ever been mine.

That night, moored at the dock, I did the accounting I'd avoided for twenty years. Not money. Years. I counted them honestly for the first time: the lost ones, the bought-and-forgotten ones, the winter of the wreck, the spring of the fine. And then I counted forward, which was new — seasons ahead, a boy to teach, a boat to keep, a town that knew my name and used it kindly. Miles, I realized, had never been a life. They'd been a ledger of everything I refused to stay for.

The harbor light swung on its mast. Somewhere below the green water, the wreck waited with its dead, patient as archives, and come morning we'd go down to them openly, with paperwork and witnesses and a boy who needed to touch the rail his father leaned on. I banked the stove, wrote the day's hours in the logbook — actual hours, logged like a man who expects more days — and climbed the stairs to bed above a shed full of other people's boats, in a town that had fined me four hundred dollars and given me back everything else.

For twenty years I measured my life in miles.

Tonight, for the first time, I measured it in seasons — and found I intended to be here for all of them.
