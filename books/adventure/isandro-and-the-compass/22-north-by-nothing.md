## 22. North by Nothing

**Bram**

The navy sent a boat to protect us. I want you to sit with that for a second, because I did, standing on the deck of the *Marta June* at six in the morning while a gray-hulled tender idled two hundred yards off my stern with its name painted on and everything—*HMCS Alder Bay*, or some such—and a lieutenant with a clipboard asking me politely if I'd like an escort.

Twenty years of diving wrecks nobody would admit existed, and now they were offering me a police escort. The world had gone soft in the head over one winter.

"Escort's fine," I said. "Just keep your distance. You'll spook the fish."

The lieutenant smiled like he wasn't sure whether I was joking, which was fair, because neither was I.

We'd come back north on the first week the coast stopped threatening ice. Odile had the wheel, because she always had the wheel when there was actual steering to be done, and Isandro was forward with his arms folded on the rail watching the water go by like it owed him money. Which, in a way, it did. Somewhere under that gray-green surface lay a transport full of people the state had written off with a pen stroke forty years ago, and somewhere under that same surface was a boy's father, and we were going down to bring them home.

Not all of them. Not even most of them. The inquiry divers had been at it since February, working the wreck in shifts, cataloguing, photographing, doing everything properly and slowly and by the book. What we were doing today was different. Today we were going down as family, not contractors. There's a difference, and everyone aboard felt it, though only Odile said it out loud.

"They're not cargo," she'd told the liaison officer on the dock, a woman named Prentiss who had the good grace to look ashamed of her own institution. "They're passengers. They've been waiting long enough."

Prentiss had nodded and written something on her clipboard, and I'd thought: twenty years ago that sentence would have gotten us all arrested. Now it got us a safety briefing and a laminated card with the emergency channel on it. Progress, I suppose, is just the speed at which the truth stops being dangerous to the people who own it.

The compass sat in its canvas bag under the wheel, where it had lived since November. Inert. Dead weight. It pointed nowhere now—or everywhere, depending on how sentimental you wanted to get about a needle. We kept it aboard anyway. Ballast, Isandro called it, and he wasn't entirely joking. Some things you carry not because they work but because of what they cost you to learn.

I checked my gauges twice, out of habit, then a third time because Odile was watching and it made her feel better. The tender held station behind us, close enough to see, far enough to pretend it wasn't there. Like most protection, I suppose.

"Bottom time's ninety minutes," I told them. "Nobody works past their air. Nobody chases anything into the wreck. We go in, we do what we came to do, we come up." I looked at Isandro. "Say it back."

"We go in, we do what we came to do, we come up." He didn't roll his eyes, which was growth. Or maybe he understood that this time the rules weren't about hiding. They were about coming back.

I went over the side first. That much hadn't changed. Somebody has to be first down and last up, and after twenty years I'd stopped pretending it was anyone but me.

* * *

**Isandro**

The water in spring is different from the water in autumn. In autumn it had felt like a lid closing. In spring it felt like a door opening—cold still, cold enough to bite through the suit, but light came down further, and the kelp near the shallows had gone bright green, new growth, the sea doing its yearly pretending that nothing bad ever happens in it.

My dad is at ninety feet, in a compartment the inquiry divers call Compartment C, because governments can't say a room without numbering it. They found him in January. His name is Tomás Reyes, and for four months it was a line item in an evidence log, and last Tuesday a woman in a gray room read it aloud along with three hundred and eleven others, and my mother cried in the third row, and I didn't cry until afterward, outside, where nobody official could see, because apparently I'm my father's son.

Today we weren't going to Compartment C. That part was done. Today we were going where the manifests had been found—the sealed strongroom aft—and we were bringing up the last of the personal effects. Shoes. Rings. A child's tin whistle that the catalogue described as "item 4471, metal, corroded," and which Odile had insisted be listed from now on as "a whistle."

"You don't catalogue a boy's whistle like a bolt," she'd said, and Prentiss had written it down, and that was that. Small victories. The whole winter had been made of them, stacked like stones.

Bram swam ahead of me, big and slow and sure, the way he does everything, like the water had agreed years ago to cooperate with him personally. I used to think he moved like that because he didn't care about anything. Now I know it's the opposite. He moves like that because he cares so hard he's built a whole way of walking to hide it.

He paused at the strongroom hatch and looked back at me, and even through the mask I could see him checking—air, position, my face—all three, in that order, which told me something about the order.

I gave him the okay sign. He gave it back. We went in.

The strongroom was exactly as I remembered it, except emptier, the shelves picked clean by the inquiry teams, the manifests themselves long gone into evidence bags and eventually into the public record, where they could never again be un-known. But the walls were still there. The little brass plaque was still there: PROPERTY OF THE MINISTRY OF TRANSPORT. AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL ONLY. Forty years of authorized personnel, and not one of them had opened this door until people who weren't authorized at all came along.

That's the thing I learned this year, if I only get to learn one thing: the lock was never the problem. The problem was that everybody with a key had agreed not to use it.

Odile handed me the effects bag and I worked shelf by shelf, slow, careful, saying the numbers out loud into my recorder the way they taught us, except I added names where I knew them. Item 4471, a whistle. Item 4488, a wedding ring, engraved, R.M. Item 4502, a pocket watch, stopped. I said them like a litany, because that's what it was. Bram hung back by the hatch and let me, and didn't hurry me, and when my hands started shaking he put one of his own on my shoulder, underwater, weightless, and left it there until they stopped.

We came up into gray light and the smell of diesel and coffee, and Odile hauled us aboard, and the tender stood off at its polite distance, and nobody shot at us, and nobody burned anything down, and I stood dripping on the deck holding a bag of other people's small precious things and thought: this is what safe feels like. I'd forgotten. I'd been fourteen the last time I felt it, before the survey team, before the compass, before everything.

Bram poured coffee into three tin mugs and handed mine over without being asked. He knows how I take it now. Black, too sweet. He never comments on the too sweet. That's another thing I learned this year: love is mostly noticing things and not making a speech about them.

* * *

**Odile**

My father's ring came up in March with the second inquiry dive. R.M.—Rafael Mercier, though everyone called him Ferry, because he ran the crossing at Kettle Bridge the way his father had and his grandfather before that, and because men who spend their lives carrying people across water deserve a name that says so.

I wear it on a cord around my neck now. It doesn't fit any finger I have. It wasn't meant to fit me. It was meant to fit a life, and the life is over, and what's left is the wearing.

People ask sometimes—Ismay at the archive, mostly, because Ismay asks everything—whether it's strange, diving the wreck that took him. And I tell them the truth, which is that the wreck didn't take him. Men took him. The wreck is just where they put him. The sea didn't kill my father; a signature did. Once you understand that, the water stops being the enemy. The water is just the place where the abandoned things wait, patient as stone, for somebody to come back.

And we came back. That's the whole story, really. Everything else—the compass, the pass, the whiteout, the siren, Bram holding that reading-room door like the last man at a bridge—is detail. The story is: they threw people away, and three nobodies and a brass needle refused to let the throwing-away stand.

This morning, on the ride out, Isandro asked me if I was scared. Not of the dive—he knows I don't scare at dives anymore—but of the day itself, the going-down-again-ness of it. And I told him no, and that was true, and then I told him why, which was also true: because fear needs a future, and I've stopped having one shaped like running. My future looks like Kettle Bridge, where I've taken over the ferry lease, and like Alder Creek on market days, and like this deck, and like these two stubborn men I collected in the woods one terrible autumn. Fear wants you moving. I'm done moving.

Bram surfaced last, the way he always does, and climbed aboard looking like a man who'd been arguing with the ocean and won on points. He stood at the rail a moment, looking back down at the spot where the wreck lay, and I watched him do the thing he does—counting, measuring, some old arithmetic running behind his eyes—and then, for the first time since I've known him, he stopped. Just stopped. Looked at the water. Looked at us. Let the number go.

"Fuel's half," he said. "We'll make the harbor by dark."

It sounded like nothing. It was everything. Twenty years of miles, and the man finally bought himself a place.

* * *

**Bram**

Here's what I know about ballast: it's the weight you carry so the boat rides true. Too little and you capsize in weather. Too much and you sit low and slow and every mile costs double. A man spends his whole life tuning it, and most men tune it wrong—they throw off everything heavy, everything that loves them back, and then wonder why they heel over in the first real blow.

The compass sits under the wheel now, in its bag, in a wooden crate Odile lined with felt because she has ideas about dignity that extend to objects. It hasn't pointed at anything since the reading room. Isandro checks it sometimes, the way you check on an old dog that's gone blind—not hoping, just keeping company. Last week he brought it up on deck and set it on the hatch cover in full sun and watched the needle drift lazy circles, pointing at gulls, at clouds, at nothing, needing nothing, and he laughed, actually laughed, and said, "It retired."

Good. Retirement's underrated. I'm considering it myself, in the sense that I have considered it and rejected it and will continue rejecting it weekly, because a man can stop running without stopping working, and there are boats in Alder Creek harbor that need a diver's eye, and there's a boy—no. Not a boy anymore. Sixteen in April, shoulders like a young oak, certified in open water with a card the instructor signed with genuine relief. There's a young man who needs hours on the line, and I'm the only fool on this coast patient enough to give them.

We raised the wreck site at noon and the *Alder Bay* fell in behind us like a shadow that had learned manners. Prentiss herself came across by launch for the afternoon run, stood on my deck drinking coffee she pronounced "adequate," and watched Isandro and Odile work the lift bags with the ease of people who'd done it a hundred times, which they had, because I'd made them do it a hundred times, because competence is the only inheritance worth leaving anyone.

"Your testimony closed the file," she said to me, apropos of nothing, watching the kids work. "You know that. The manifests were the evidence, but it was your statement that made the panel believe them."

"I talk good," I said.

She almost smiled. "You could have a career in this. Consulting. Institutional reform. There are other wrecks, Galloway. Other files."

"There are," I agreed. "And other people to find them. I found mine." I nodded at the deck, at the two of them arguing cheerfully about knot choice. "Took me forty years and a haunted compass, but I found mine."

She left before the last lift broke water, and I didn't watch her go, because I was busy watching something better: Isandro guiding a small bundle up the last fathom with both hands, gentle, the way you carry a sleeping kid, and setting it on deck between us, and the three of us standing around it in the late light while the gulls yelled their opinions overhead.

Inside the bundle: a tin whistle, a wedding ring already delivered to its widow in February, a pocket watch stopped at 11:42, and forty years of waiting, done.

We buried the ashes of it properly, later, ashore, in the little cemetery above Alder Creek where the inquiry paid for stones with real names on them, all three hundred and twelve, in order, no line items. Isandro read his father's name himself. Odile read her father's. I read the ones nobody claimed, all sixty-one of them, because someone should, and I've spent my whole life being someone nobody claimed, and it turns out that qualifies you.

Afterward we walked down to the harbor in the blue hour, and the *Marta June* rode at her mooring riding high and easy, ballast trimmed right, and Isandro asked me the question he asks every few weeks now, the one I used to dodge:

"How many miles this year?"

And I did the thing I do now instead of dodging. I thought about it honestly. About the run north in October with the navy burning our wake. About the pass, the whiteout, the rope, Odile's hand out of the snow. About the reading room, the siren, the walk to the filing desk through a crowd of witnesses. About February, and March, and this morning, and tonight.

"None that mattered," I said.

He grinned, because he's learning to translate me. Then he said, "So what are you counting?"

I looked at the harbor, and the hills going gold behind it, and the ferry landing Odile was already scheming to expand, and the boy beside me who'd walked into my hold half-frozen and walked out owning a third of my heart, and I gave him the only answer that's ever been true:

"The seasons. One down. Next one starts tomorrow."

And for once, the needle in my chest pointed exactly where I stood.

*The end.*
