## 1. The Thief Who Wasn't

The salt flats lied about distance, and Elodie Yarrow had spent twenty years learning not to believe them.

She lay flat on her belly at the edge of the white, chin in the crust, and watched the far shimmer where the world bent like glass over a flame. Something moved out there. Not heat-shimmer—shimmer didn't have a gait. Shimmer didn't stop and stagger and stop again.

A horse. A bad one, by the way it moved, dragging a leg or a grudge or both. And leading it, small against the glare, a figure that walked like someone who had learned to make himself cheap to look at.

Elodie spat grit and did the arithmetic she always did before committing to anything: two hours' water left in her skin, six hours back to the waystation if she turned around now, and a sun overhead with no opinions and no mercy. The flats offered exactly one shade in a hundred miles, and it was attached to her hat brim.

"Stupid," she told herself conversationally. "Definitely stupid."

But the code was the code, and the code said a stolen horse was a debt owed to somebody, even if that somebody was a caravan master who watered his animals out of a shared trough and beat them for drinking first. She'd tracked worse debts across worse ground for less reason than principle. Principle was mostly what she had left, and unlike water, it didn't run out—though some mornings she suspected it evaporated at roughly the same rate.

She rose in a crouch, checked her boot prints behind her—soft sand at the flat's margin held a print like a signature, and she'd taught three people to read hers and hated every one of them for it—and started down onto the white.

* * *

An hour later she'd closed half the gap, and the figure hadn't once looked back.

That bothered her more than the horse did. A thief who doesn't check his backtrail is either very good or very dead already, and this one wove a drunkard's line across the salt, stopping every fifty paces to lean on the animal's neck. The horse, she saw as she came closer, wasn't stolen so much as rescued—it was a swaybacked gray with rope burns on its muzzle and ribs you could play music on, walking on three legs and a promise. Nobody stole a horse like that. You couldn't sell it. You couldn't ride it hard enough to escape anyone. It was the kind of horse you took because leaving it was unthinkable, which made it less a theft than an argument with the world.

The boy—because up close it resolved into a boy, fourteen maybe, fifteen, all elbows and hollows under a coat cut for a grown man—finally heard her at forty paces. He spun, put himself between her and the horse, and raised a knife.

It was a table knife. The handle was wrapped in twine that had been rewound so many times it had become mostly knot. He held it the way he'd seen men hold knives in stories, arm cocked, blade out, everything about the pose screaming that he had never once done this before and knew it.

Elodie stopped walking. She let her hands hang loose and open, which in her case was not the reassurance it might have been, since her hands were the most dangerous things within fifty miles. But she kept them still.

"That's a butter knife," she said.

"It's a knife."

"It's for spreading things." She tilted her head. "What are you planning to spread?"

The boy's eyes flicked—to the horse, to the horizon behind her, to the water skin at her hip. That last flick lasted a half-second too long, and Elodie felt something old and tired turn over in her chest, the way a hearth log turns when you've been poking it too long.

"When were you last watered?" she asked.

"I'm fine."

"That's not what I asked."

"I'm *fine.*"

His voice cracked on the second *fine*, which is the desert's way of settling arguments. Elodie unslung her skin, walked forward slowly—close enough now to see the salt burn on his lips, the white crust in the corners of his mouth, the tremor in his knees that had nothing to do with fear—and held it out.

He stared at it like it might be a trap. Everything, she supposed, probably was, from where he stood.

"Take nothing you can't carry," she said. "That's the first rule of the flats. You can carry this. Drink."

He drank like a starving thing drinks, which is badly, gulping until she put a hand on the skin and eased it down. "Slow. Slowly. Your stomach will cramp and then I'll be carrying you, and you're heavier than you look."

"I'm not heavy at all," he said, with the wounded dignity of the young, and swayed.

She caught him by the collar. "Sit down before the ground decides for you."

* * *

They sat in the meager shadow of the horse—the gray had lowered its head over them like a broken awning, whether out of affection or the simple desire to lean on something, Elodie couldn't say—and she fed him strips of dried goat and hardtack softened in a splash of water, watching him eat with the clinical attention she gave all problems.

"So," she said. "The horse."

"I found him."

"You found him."

"He was tied up outside the Salt Larder station. Tied short, no water bucket, in the sun. For two days." The boy's jaw set. "I waited to see if anyone would come for him. Nobody came. So I untied him."

"And the station master called it theft."

"The station master called it a lot of things." There was something underneath the boy's anger, older than this morning, older maybe than the boy. "He sent a rider after me. I've been listening for hooves since dawn."

Elodie chewed on that along with her own strip of goat. The Salt Larder was a mean little watering hole run by a mean little man named Corb, who charged travelers double for shade and had once tried to charge *her* for looking at his well. If Corb had tied a horse out in the sun to die slow rather than feed it, that wasn't a story she found hard to believe. It also wasn't a story that changed anything. A taken horse was a taken horse; the whole architecture of the Escarpment ran on the difference between taking and asking, and rangers existed to keep that wall standing, even when the wall was stupid.

Especially when the wall was stupid. That was the part nobody thanked you for.

"What's your name?" she asked.

The boy went still. Not startled-still. *Practiced*-still, the stillness of someone who had answered that question wrong before and paid for it.

"Don't have one," he said.

"Everyone has one."

"Not everyone keeps theirs." He looked at her sideways, and there was a sharpness in him under the starvation, a whetstone glint. "You're a ranger. Rangers don't use names. Everyone knows that. You take nothing you can't carry and you leave no name behind. Everybody on the flats knows the code, even if they don't live it."

Elodie blinked. It wasn't often the code came back at her out of the mouth of a child in a dead man's coat.

"Where'd you learn that?"

"Listening." He shrugged, which cost him something, given how little of him there was to shrug with. "Caravans talk. Station men talk. Rangers talk least of anybody, but they talk. You're the one they call—" He stopped himself, almost smiled, caught it. "You don't have a name either. They just call you the Yarrow woman. Or the one who finds things."

"They call me worse."

"I know. I've heard that too." And there it was again, that flicker of humor, dry as the ground they sat on, buried deep but alive. Starving boys weren't supposed to be funny. It unsettled her more than the knife had.

She studied him while he ate. Salt-burned, half-starved, dressed in a coat that had belonged to somebody bigger and deader. Hands that were clever despite themselves—she'd watched him break off the hardtack and divide it without being asked, one piece set aside on his knee for the horse, who lipped it up with the tenderness of an old man accepting charity. He'd divided it *before* feeding himself. She'd seen grown men fail that test their whole lives.

"Why the horse?" she asked. "You could've walked away. Nobody would've known."

"I'd know." He said it simply, without drama, the way you'd state the weather. "And he'd know." He patted the gray's neck. "That's enough witnesses for me."

Elodie looked out at the flats, at the light going long and copper toward evening, and felt the weary weight settle onto her shoulders that always settled there at moments like this—the weight of knowing what the right thing was going to be before she'd agreed to it. Twenty years of turning people out at the edges of her life. Twenty years of *not my problem*, which was the ranger's true prayer, unsayable and constant. Every person she'd ever turned away had been a problem that stayed solved. That was the point of the code. The code kept the family small and the graves shallow and the ledger empty.

But the boy had saved half his bread for a dying horse.

"Well," she said, and sighed, and hated how audible the sigh was. "Here's how it stands. Corb will send riders after that horse, and they'll find your tracks, and they'll follow them, and they'll find you, because your tracking is terrible. Truly awful. You walk like a man being chased by bees. You stopped twice to rest in open ground. You camped in a hollow that funnels wind, which means you froze last night, and you'll freeze tonight, and the night after that until you die of it or someone eats you."

The boy absorbed this inventory of his failures with a scowl that wanted very much to be argued with and couldn't find the footing.

"But," Elodie continued, "you picked the right horse to steal. A lame gray nobody wants is the only animal in the Escarpment that could carry you out of here without Corb's riders catching you, because nobody chases a horse worth less than the chase. And you rationed your water by licking stones instead of drinking, which is disgusting, but it's *informed* disgusting—you knew the sun would take more from a wet mouth than a dry one. Somebody taught you that, or you figured it out alone, and either way it means there's a tracker in you trying to get out."

"I'm not going anywhere with you," the boy said automatically, the way people say *I'm fine*, meaning the opposite.

"No," Elodie agreed. "Of course not. You're going to sit here and die of exposure with a horse you love, out of principle. Very noble. I'll mention it to whoever finds the bones."

Silence. The horse snorted, which sounded suspiciously like commentary.

"I want terms," the boy said at last.

"Everyone does."

"I eat same as you do. Same portions. I'm not a charity."

"You'll eat same as me, which is to say not much, because I travel light. Take nothing you can't carry."

"And I keep my name to myself. All of it. Whatever it is."

Elodie nodded slowly. "Leave no name behind. That's the second rule. You'll fit right in—we're all nobody at my camp. Best collection of nobodies on the Escarpment. Quartermaster who won't say where she learned numbers. A medic who flinches at church bells. A scout who talks to rocks because rocks listen better than people." She stood, brushing salt from her knees, and offered him her hand. "There's room for one more nobody. Pay is water, silence, and the occasional lesson in not walking like a man chased by bees."

The boy looked at her hand for a long moment. Then past her, at the flats, at whatever life he was choosing to bury out there in the white. When he took her hand, his grip was stronger than his body had any right to supply.

"Isandro," he said.

Just like that. No ceremony, no hesitation—and Elodie realized, with a small cold shock, that it wasn't carelessness. He'd decided. Somewhere between the butter knife and the bread, he'd decided she was safe to know it, and having decided, he committed the way he'd committed to the horse: completely, without checking his backtrail.

"Isandro," she repeated, tasting it. "That's a river name."

"Is that a problem?"

"Rivers," Elodie said, swinging up into her saddle and holding a stirrup for him, "have a habit of getting where they're going. No matter what's in the way." She pulled him up behind her, and the gray fell into its three-legged shuffle, northward, toward the dark line of the Escarpment rising out of the dusk like a held breath. "We'll see if that's a virtue or a curse."

Behind them, the salt flats went on lying about distance to no one at all.

And somewhere south, faint as a lie, Elodie thought she heard hooves—Corb's riders, coming too late, chasing a debt that had already dissolved into the dark. She didn't look back. Looking back was for people with names to protect.

She had a feeling, though—a ranger's feeling, the kind that had kept her alive through twenty years of bad country—that the name riding behind her, warm and stubborn and utterly unguarded, was going to cost her more than any name she'd ever left behind.

She was right. But that, as Isandro would learn to say, is another chapter.
