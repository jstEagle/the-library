## 2. Crew of No Names

The camp didn't exist, and Isandro had learned to keep it that way.

It sat in a fold of the Escarpment where the rock shrugged off the wind and a seep of water ran cold and constant out of a crack in the stone—too small to appear on any surveyor's map, too reliable to be an accident. Someone had found it long before Isandro was born. Someone had decided it would never be written down. The way you learned about it was the way he had learned about it: Elodie Yarrow's hand on your shoulder, walking you in blindfolded on the third night, saying only, *If anyone ever asks, you were never here.*

He'd been with them four months now. Long enough to stop counting days and start counting seasons. Long enough to know that the rule wasn't cruelty—it was architecture. A crew with no pasts was a crew with no handles. Nobody could grab you by your history and shake.

And nobody could grab him either, because as far as the world knew, Isandro didn't have one. He was the boy who'd tried to steal water from Elodie Yarrow on the salt flats and gotten hired instead of hanged. That was his whole story, and everyone in camp treated it like a treasure.

* * *

Bex ran the stores the way a spider runs a web—everything connected to everything, nothing wasted, nothing missing without her knowing within the hour.

She was a broad, gray-streaked woman with forearms like dock rope and a voice pitched low enough that you leaned in to hear it, which Isandro suspected was deliberate. Bex did not waste volume. She kept the ledger of their supplies in her head and a backup copy in a ciphered book she slept with under her bedroll, and she doled out water, powder, salt, and boot leather with the impartiality of rain.

"Boy," she said this morning, not looking up from the scale, "you've been drinking heavy."

"It's hot."

"It's always hot. That's the desert." She tapped the water tin. "You get two liters working days, three if you're climbing. You drank four yesterday."

"I gave half my second liter to the mule."

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth—the closest thing to approval Bex issued in writing. "Then you're down to one and a half today, and I'll pretend I didn't hear about the mule, because if the others find out you're soft-hearted they'll all start giving away rations and I'll have a camp full of skeletons with good reputations." She pushed the tin across the crate toward him. "Drink it slow. Slow is a skill."

Isandro had decided early that Bex was the crew's spine. Not its heart—that was Tamsin—and not its temper, which belonged jointly to Ovid and Ferro depending on the day. But a body doesn't stand up without a spine, and nobody in this camp stood up hungry or thirsty or unshod unless Bex had decided there was a reason worth standing for.

"You ever been married?" he asked once, because he was fourteen and stupid in the specific way fourteen-year-olds are stupid.

Bex had looked at him for a long moment. "I've been a quartermaster longer than most marriages last," she said. "Count what that tells you and don't ask again."

* * *

Ovid lived at the edge of camp, in a shelter he'd built himself from pack-frame poles and sun-bleached canvas, and everything inside it was either a tool or a trap.

That was not an exaggeration. Isandro had learned to announce himself from ten paces out, because Ovid strung tripwires the way other men hung laundry, and the one time Isandro had crept in quiet—showing off, wanting to prove he could move silent—he'd set off a clacker alarm made from a bent spoon and a shell casing and Ovid had come out of his tent holding a knife and looking genuinely disappointed in him.

"Silence isn't sneaking," Ovid said, resetting the trigger. "Sneaking is being expected. Silence is just noise that hasn't happened yet."

Ovid was thin and quick and somewhere between forty and sixty, a range he maintained through sheer refusal to discuss it. His hands were a map of old burns. He taught Isandro knots first, then fuses, then how to listen to a charge before you lit it—"a charge talks to you, boy, it hums when it's happy and clicks when it's lying"—and never once explained why a demolitions expert was living in a hole in the Escarpment tracking horse thieves for water money.

"You blow bridges?" Isandro asked him in the first month. "Like, for work?"

"I unbuild things," Ovid said. "Building is easy. Any fool with rope and confidence can build. Unbuilding takes patience." He held up a fuse and sighted along it like a man reading scripture. "Most of what people call destruction is just impatience with a flame attached."

Isandro understood, even then, that this was a man telling you his whole life story while telling you nothing at all. He'd stopped asking after that. It was part of belonging: you took what people offered and you didn't rattle the tin to see what else was inside.

* * *

Tamsin stitched.

That was how Isandro thought of her, though she was the medic and the stitching was only the smallest part. She stitched cuts and she stitched canvas and she stitched, once, in front of him, a wound in Ferro's shoulder that should have killed him, humming a tuneless little song through the whole thing while Ferro swore with impressive creativity.

She was small and quick-fingered and older than she moved, and she kept her medicines in labeled tins that she reorganized nightly, a ritual Isandro came to understand was less about order than about having something to do with her hands when the memories got loud.

Tamsin was the one who'd checked him over the day he arrived—salt-cracked lips, feet like raw meat, ribs you could play like a xylophone. She'd fed him broth in stages, scolding gently between spoonfuls. "Not so fast. Your stomach's forgotten how to be grateful."

"What happened to your hands?" he'd asked, because her knuckles were scarred in a way that didn't match the rest of her.

"They got in the way of something," she said, and changed the subject by handing him a second bowl.

But Tamsin was also the one who talked to him. Not about herself—never about herself—but about the world. About which plants lied about being edible. About how to splint a leg using a rifle and a belt. About why you always told someone where you were going, even in a camp of five, because "the desert forgives nothing and forgets no one, and neither do I."

Once, half-asleep by the fire, Isandro had mumbled a question about where she'd learned medicine—in a city? an army? a hospital?—and the whole camp had gone quiet in a way he felt more than heard. Tamsin had smiled at him across the flames, warm as banked coals, and said, "Ask me something easier. Ask me what's in the blue tin."

He never asked again. But he noticed, after that, that the rule had a shape: *each of us erased ourselves, and the price of the erasing is that none of us gets to be curious.* Everyone except him. He was allowed no past because he'd never had time to make one worth hiding. The others had histories the way the canyon had water—present, essential, and completely underground.

* * *

Ferro was the hardest to know and the easiest to be around, which Isandro eventually understood was the same trick done twice.

He was tall and laconic and moved like the ground owed him money, and he could read sign—a broken twig, a shifted pebble, a bird flying wrong—like other men read letters. He'd taken Isandro on as a project almost immediately, mostly, Isandro suspected, because Isandro was terrible at it and Ferro enjoyed teaching terrible students, the way some men enjoy whittling.

"Again," Ferro said, crouched over a stretch of sand that looked, to Isandro, exactly like every other stretch of sand in the world.

"Rabbit?"

"Coyote. Rabbit prints cluster. Coyote travels with purpose." Ferro touched a depression with one finger, barely. "See the stride? It's thinking about something. Everything out here is thinking about something. Your job is to figure out what."

"How do you know it's thinking about something?"

Ferro looked up at him with an expression of profound, weary patience. "Because it's alive, boy. Same as you. Now walk it out. Where's it going?"

Isandro walked the track out twenty paces until it vanished into rock, and Ferro nodded once, which from Ferro was a parade.

Ferro had a scar that ran from ear to collarbone, and when Isandro had asked about it in week one, Ferro had said, "A door," and gone back to cleaning his rifle, and Isandro had understood that *a door* was both the entire answer and a locked one.

What Ferro did give freely was the country itself. Every patrol became a lesson delivered in grunts and single sentences, and Isandro hoarded them. Where water hides in canyon country. Why you never camp in a wash. How to tell a lie in tracks—because people could fake sign, but the desert couldn't, and learning to spot the difference was learning to spot a liar.

"You'll be better than me someday," Ferro said once, apropos of nothing, watching Isandro read a trail at dusk.

"No I won't."

"Mm." Ferro adjusted his hat brim. "That's the first smart thing you've said. Maybe you will."

* * *

Evenings were the best part, and Isandro guarded them fiercely.

The crew cooked together over a low fire tucked into a rock hollow so the light wouldn't show. Bex portioned. Ovid built the fire with an economy that bordered on showing off—one match, every time, held aloft afterward like a trophy. Tamsin told stories that weren't about anything, folk tales and traveler's nonsense, and everyone pretended not to notice that none of them were true and all of them were comforting. Ferro said maybe six words and somehow they landed at exactly the right moments.

Elodie sat slightly apart, as captains do, close enough to be warmed and far enough to stay unreadable. But she watched Isandro the way she watched weather.

Tonight the talk turned, as it sometimes did, to the trade—petty thieves, runaway debtors, a caravan master who'd shorted a well-digger and needed finding. Small work. Quiet work. Work that paid in water and silence, exactly as advertised.

"The Marrow brothers hit a caravan two days east," Bex said, updating the mental ledger. "Took grain and a rifle."

"Ferro can find them by Thursday," Ovid said.

"Thursday's optimistic," Ferro said.

"Thursday's Thursday," Ovid said, and the argument proceeded along these lines for several minutes with great seriousness and zero heat, and Isandro sat in the middle of it feeling something he hadn't let himself feel since before the flats: safe.

That was the thing nobody warned you about a family built on secrets. It worked. It worked beautifully. Five people who owed each other nothing but tomorrow, and gave each other everything anyway. No names meant no debts from before, no grudges imported, no one's uncle calling in favors. Every day started clean.

He just had to keep the one rule. Don't ask. Don't wonder out loud. Don't rattle the tin.

He was good at it. He was getting better. And if sometimes, falling asleep to the sound of Ovid checking his wires and Tamsin humming her tuneless song, he wondered—not about them, but about himself, about whether a boy with no past was really free or just empty—he kept that wondering folded up small, like a letter he'd never send.

* * *

It was nearly full dark when Elodie came back into camp from the outer picket, moving quiet, and Isandro only knew she'd returned because the quality of the silence changed.

She crouched at the fire, accepted a cup from Bex without a word, and drank. Then she reached into her coat and set something on the flat rock beside her: a letter, sealed, heavy paper, wrong for the desert. Paper like that cost more than a mule.

Nobody asked. That was the rule. But everybody looked.

Elodie looked at the seal a long time—longer than a captain looks at anything—and Isandro, watching from across the fire, saw something cross her face that he had never seen there before. Not fear, exactly. Recognition. The look of a woman reading her own name in a place names aren't supposed to go.

"Bex," she said quietly. "Tomorrow we move camp."

"Trouble?"

"No," Elodie said, and fed the letter to the flames without opening it, and watched it burn with those steady gray eyes, and Isandro saw her hands were not quite steady at all. "No trouble. Just an old debt."

The fire ate the paper. Nobody spoke. And Isandro, fourteen years old and finally belonging somewhere, understood for the first time that the rule cut both ways—that the woman who'd taken him in had a past too, and it had just found her, and whatever it wanted, it had made her hands shake.
