## 3. Water Ledger

Bex kept the books the way other people prayed: daily, privately, and with a firm belief that someone upstairs was checking the arithmetic.

The waystation sat in a fold of rock where two trade paths crossed and pretended they hadn't. Officially it was nothing—a wind-scoured shelf of stone with a cistern, a shade awning stitched from four different awnings, and a stove that Bex had personally coaxed back from the dead three times. Unofficially, it was the best-informed watering hole on the eastern Escarpment, and information, properly weighed and portioned, was worth more than water.

Water you could find if you knew where to dig. Knowledge of where to dig—that was the whole economy.

"Two fingers of route talk for a full skin," Bex said to the caravan master, a broad woman named Okku who smelled of cardamom and camel. "The Salt Ladder crossing is out. The flash scoured the lower steps last month. You'll want the northern bench instead."

"The northern bench costs a day."

"The Salt Ladder costs everything, if you're on it when the sky decides otherwise." Bex turned the ledger toward her, though not far enough that she could actually read anything. The pages were written in Bex's own shorthand, a crabbed cipher of her own invention that had survived two attempted thefts and one very determined goat. "A day is cheap."

Okku grumbled, paid, and left happier than she'd arrived, which was the mark of a fair trade. Bex entered the transaction in her neat columns: *One skin, one route. Okku's line, eleven head, bound south. She'll tell three people about the bench; those three will each pay us a question within the month.* The ledger didn't just record what they'd sold. It recorded what the sale would grow into. That was the trick nobody else on the Escarpment had figured out—knowledge wasn't a thing you spent. It was a seed.

Behind her, Ovid was supposed to be repairing the cistern pump and was instead disassembling it entirely, which by Ovid's standards counted as repairing.

"If that thing explodes," Bex said without looking up, "I'm billing you for the awning."

"It won't explode." A pause, rich with honesty. "Probably. I found something interesting in the valve housing."

"Everything you find is interesting. That's how the last stove died."

"This is *genuinely* interesting." He held up a small disc of corroded metal, green with age. "Someone built a pressure trap into this pump. Old work. Pre-war, maybe. If we'd forced the handle, it would have—" he made a small gesture like a bird taking off, then reconsidered, "—like a bird taking off, but with more shrapnel."

Bex set down her pen. "How old?"

"Older than us." Ovid turned it in his fingers, and his voice did the thing it always did around old mechanisms—the flippancy draining out, leaving something almost reverent. "Somebody wanted this water defended. And then somebody else wanted it forgotten so thoroughly they buried the defense inside the plumbing."

"That's the Escarpment for you," Bex said. "Everything here is either hiding or hunting."

She wrote it down anyway. *Cistern trap, disarmed. Note: check every fixture at every station before touching.* Her ledgers were full of such notes—twenty years of small warnings, small repairs, small knowledge accumulating into the closest thing the crew had to an institution. Elodie was the captain because someone had to decide. But the ledgers were the crew's memory, and everyone knew it, even if no one said it. People who erased their pasts needed somewhere to keep the parts worth keeping.

Tamsin came up from the shade of the overhang where she'd been sorting stores, shaking sand out of a bandage roll with the particular violence she reserved for supplies that hadn't been stored correctly. Which meant Isandro had stored them.

"Where is he?" she asked.

"Out with Ferro," Bex said. "Learning the difference between a trail and a story about a trail."

"He stacked the gauze under the water skins again. If a skin sweats, the gauze wicks, and then I'm wrapping wounds in damp cloth."

"I'll enter it in the ledger as an official complaint."

"You do not need to—" Tamsin stopped, caught the look on Bex's face, and huffed. "Fine. Enter it. At least someone in this crew respects documentation."

That was Tamsin all over. She claimed to despise the books and read them more carefully than anyone. Bex had caught her twice, late at night, going through old entries with a lamp turned low—not auditing, exactly. Visiting. There were entries in there from before Isandro, from before the current shape of things, and Bex had never once asked what Tamsin looked for in them. Some questions you didn't ask in a family built on not asking.

* * *

Isandro and Ferro came back at dusk, and Bex heard them before she saw them, because Isandro was talking. The boy talked the way water ran—constantly, finding every crack, wearing grooves into silence whether the silence wanted them or not.

"—and Ferro says the tracks were three days old, but the dung was still soft at the edges, so really they were two days old, and the difference matters because—"

"Because a trail lies," Ferro said. Ferro could make six words sound like a complete lecture. He dropped onto the stones by the fire, folded his long frame down, and accepted a bowl of stew without appearing to notice he'd been given one. "Dung doesn't."

"Dung doesn't lie," Isandro repeated, solemn as scripture, and then ruined it by grinning. He was sixteen now, all elbows and appetite, and he ate like the desert might take the bowl back. Two years since the salt flats, and he still ate like that. Bex had stopped finding it heartbreaking and started finding it funny, mostly. Mostly.

"How was the lesson?" Bex asked.

"He made me track him," Isandro said. "For four hours. In circles. He circled, Bex. Who does that?"

"A scout," said Ferro, "who wanted dinner."

Bex laughed and opened the ledger to the day's final page. *Stores drawn: stew for five. Gauze misfiled (see complaint). One pump trap, disarmed. One apprentice, improved.* She liked writing that last kind of entry. It was the only column in any of her books that reliably grew.

Elodie came out of the dark sometime after full night, the way she always did—no announcement, just suddenly present, as if she'd been standing at the edge of the firelight for an hour deciding whether to join it. She usually decided yes. Tonight she stood a moment longer than usual, and Bex noticed, because noticing was Bex's whole profession.

"Roads quiet?" Elodie asked.

"Quiet enough. Sold the northern bench to Okku. We'll get follow-on questions inside a month." Bex hesitated, then added, because the ledger would want it noted: "Ovid found a trap in the cistern pump. Old. Deliberate."

Something moved behind Elodie's eyes—quick, unreadable, gone. "This station?"

"This station."

"Then we don't drink from it until Tamsin's checked the main flow." She sat, took a bowl, and ate with her back to the rock, facing the dark. Captain habits. Bex had stopped counting years ago how many of Elodie's habits were comfort and how many were doctrine. Probably they'd merged long ago, the way a river merges with its silt.

It was a good evening, by their standards. Ovid told a story about a demolition job gone wrong that got funnier the more times he told it, because each retelling added one more improbable detail and subtracted one more consequence. Tamsin corrected his geography twice. Isandro laughed until he had to lie down. Ferro watched the dark and let the corners of his mouth suggest that he was having a fine time, which for Ferro was effusive.

Bex balanced the day's columns by lamplight and felt the specific contentment that only came of numbers agreeing with each other. It wouldn't last. Nothing on the Escarpment lasted. But that was tomorrow's problem, and tomorrow's problems went in tomorrow's ledger.

* * *

The courier came three days later, and Bex knew he was trouble before he crested the rise, because trouble always arrived at the pace of someone being paid by the hour.

He was a lean man in company gray, riding a good horse badly, with the sweat-stained confidence of a man carrying paper that he believed made him important. He reined in at the edge of camp with the theatrical carelessness of someone who wanted to be seen arriving.

"Looking for the rangers," he announced, to the camp in general, which was his first mistake. Nobody answered. His second mistake was continuing. "I've a delivery for—"

"For whom?" Bex rose from the ledger, pen still in hand, and gave him the full weight of her quartermaster stare. It was a good stare. She'd practiced.

The courier faltered, recalibrated. "There's no name on it. Just a description of the camp." He produced a letter—heavy paper, sealed with wax, the seal pressed with a device Bex didn't recognize and then, a half-second later, recognized completely, and wished she hadn't. A stylized V, struck through with a blade.

Her stomach performed a slow, professional turn.

"No name?" she said, keeping her voice level, bored, the voice of a woman who handled forty letters a week. "Then it goes in the dead file. We don't accept anonymous post. Costs too much to be curious."

"It's paid delivery," the courier said. "Double rate."

"And yet still anonymous." She held out her hand anyway—because refusing outright would tell him everything, and taking it told him nothing—and he laid the letter in her palm with a flourish, as if handing over a crown rather than a problem. "Wait there," she said. "If it's refused, you'll carry it back."

She walked the twenty paces to the fire as slowly as her legs allowed, which was not very slowly at all, and put the letter down on the flat stone beside the ledger without opening it.

Elodie looked at it for a long time.

The camp went quiet in stages. First Ovid, who could smell a moment the way he smelled unstable compounds. Then Tamsin, whose hands stilled over the bandage roll. Then Isandro, who read the room these days better than he read trails, and who nudged Ferro, and received a look that said *watch.*

"Burn it," Bex said quietly. "Unread. That's cleanest."

Elodie didn't move.

It was a small thing, afterward—the kind of detail Bex would enter in the ledger and hate herself for entering. But she saw it, and so did everyone, because everyone was watching, because Elodie Yarrow did not hesitate over mail. Elodie Yarrow burned things without reading them the way other captains signed orders: routinely, decisively, without ceremony.

She picked up the letter. And her hands shook.

Not much. Barely a tremor, there and gone, like heat shimmer off stone. But Bex had watched those hands for fifteen years—steady on a rope, steady on a wound, steady on the ledger line when the numbers went bad and stayed bad. Those hands did not shake. Those hands were the steadiest thing on the entire Escarpment, and Bex felt the tremor land in her own chest like a dropped stone hitting still water.

Elodie carried the letter to the fire. She held it over the flames without breaking the seal. The wax bubbled first, the bladed V softening, sagging, losing its edges. Then the paper caught, and she held it until the fire had all of it, and dropped the last black corner into the coals.

"Dead file," she said. Her voice was perfectly level. That was somehow worse than the hands.

Nobody spoke. Isandro opened his mouth—of course he did—and Ferro's hand landed on his shoulder with the gentle inevitability of a landslide, and the mouth closed.

The courier waited an hour, grew bored, and rode out unpaid, muttering about double rates. The evening resumed its shapes: stew, dishes, watch rotations. Bex entered the day in the ledger. *Courier, company gray, one letter, refused unread. Seal: V.* She stared at that last entry for a long while. Then she closed the book.

Later, when the others slept, Elodie came and sat across the coals from her.

"You saw," Elodie said. Not a question.

"I see everything. It's my job."

"She's coming herself, next. A letter means she's testing the water. Sable never tests water she doesn't intend to cross."

"Sable," Bex repeated, tasting the name. Fifteen years, and Elodie had never once spoken it aloud in her hearing. "You know I'll have to write that down."

"I know." Elodie fed a stick to the coals and watched it catch. "Write it small."

Bex looked at her captain—at the woman who had taught her that a ledger was a promise you made to the future, that every entry was a debt someone would come to collect—and understood, with the cold clarity of a well-kept account, that whatever was written next would cost them all.

She opened the book. She wrote it small.

Far to the north, she would think later, a rider was already on the road.
