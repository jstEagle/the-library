## 4. The Rival's Terms

The border town of Corrow had two claims to fame, and both were lies. The first was that it sat on the edge of the Escarpment, which was true only if you squinted past the forty miles of scrub and salt between the town and anything resembling an edge. The second was the sign over the Drowned Ox saloon, which promised COLD BEER in letters painted so long ago that the paint had given up its C and its D, leaving a sign that read, with a kind of accidental honesty, O L BEE.

Elodie Yarrow liked Corrow anyway. It was the kind of town where nobody looked at your face twice and everybody looked at your horse once, and that was the correct order of priorities for a border settlement. She'd left the crew at the waystation two days east with instructions to water the stock, count the canteens, and under no circumstances let Ovid "improve" the well pump again. The last time he'd improved a well pump, it had worked beautifully for six hours and then launched a bucket through a caravan master's tent at what Bex still described, with grudging admiration, as "ballistic velocity."

She'd come to Corrow because of the courier. Because of the letter she'd burned unread, and because her hands had shaken doing it, and because Bex had seen, and Bex never forgot anything except, pointedly, the debts people owed her.

A burned letter was an answer. But Elodie had been a ranger long enough to know that some questions deserved to be asked twice.

The Drowned Ox was doing its mid-afternoon business, which was to say four drovers playing cards with the grim concentration of men who had already lost and were now playing for the privilege of losing more, and a barkeep polishing a glass that had been clean since the previous administration. Elodie took a table in the corner with her back to the wall and ordered the O L BEE, which arrived cold, which surprised her, and tasting faintly of the barrel, which did not.

She was halfway through it when the door opened and the temperature of the room changed.

It wasn't the heat—though Sable Vane carried heat with her the way some women carried perfume, a shimmer of the desert that seemed to arrive a half-step ahead. It was that the card game stopped. The drovers didn't look up, exactly. They just went very still, the way rabbits go still, and Elodie felt her spine straighten without her permission, some old animal part of her recognizing a predator before her mind caught up.

Sable Vane crossed the saloon floor like she owned it, which was her particular genius. She was dressed better than anyone in Corrow had a right to be—dust-colored silk under a long coat, a hat that had never once been caught in the rain—and she was smiling before she reached the table, a smile with a great deal of charm in it and, if you knew where to look, no warmth whatsoever.

"Elodie Yarrow." She said the name softly, and Elodie's skin prickled, because that was the game, had always been the game. Sable knew her name. Sable was the only person alive who used it.

"Sable." Elodie didn't rise. "You're a long way from anywhere."

"I'm precisely where I intend to be. That's the difference between us, I've always thought." Sable sat down uninvited, draped herself against the chair back, and gestured at the bottle. "Another of those. Two." She caught the barkeep's eye without raising her voice, which was another of her genius's many facets. Then, to Elodie, pleasantly: "You look well. The desert agrees with you. Or you've simply stopped fighting it, which amounts to the same thing on you."

"You look expensive."

"I am." Sable laughed, and it was a good laugh, and that was the worst thing about her. "God, look at us. Ten years and we open with insults. Some things hold up better than the sign on this establishment."

"Twelve."

"Twelve." Sable turned the word over like a coin she was checking for shaving. "You've been counting."

"I count everything. It's the job."

The beers arrived. Sable paid with a coin that was worth more than the entire bar's till and waved off the change. She wrapped her hands around her glass and didn't drink, and Elodie recognized that too—the performance of ease over a body that wasn't easy at all. Sable Vane was nervous. Sable Vane had crossed the Escarpment, hired or bought her way through three territories, and walked into a saloon famous for nothing, to sit across from a woman who'd burned her letter, and she was nervous.

That, more than anything, made Elodie listen.

* * *

They had worked together once, in the years before either of them had a reputation, back when the Escarpment rangers were less a company than a habit—a handful of trackers who'd learned the same hard lessons from the same hard teachers and hadn't yet learned the hardest one, which is that the desert keeps everyone eventually.

Sable had been the better shot. Elodie had been the better reader—of tracks, of weather, of people, though the last skill had conspicuously failed her where Sable was concerned. For two years they'd run the high trails together, splitting water and wages down the middle, sleeping back to back in the cold, and it had worked the way good partnerships work, which is to say neither of them could have said why.

And then, one autumn, Sable had taken a job Elodie wouldn't, and come back from it with new boots and a closed mouth, and something between them had gone quiet in a way that never un-quieted. Elodie had asked her once, directly, on a ridge above the Anselm, what the job had been. Sable had looked at her with something Elodie had spent twelve years refusing to name—hurt, maybe, or its cousin, shame—and said, "You don't want to know, Elodie. That's the difference between us. You never want to know."

Then she'd ridden south, and Elodie had let her go, and the not-knowing had hardened into a scar tissue of grievance that both of them had kept polished and warm, the way you keep anything that reminds you you're still alive.

"You've heard from my courier," Sable said now.

"I burned it."

"I know. I paid the man extra to confirm." Sable smiled at her beer. "I'd have been disappointed otherwise. You never read my letters. Even at the beginning. I'd write you actual correspondence—news, jokes, once a recipe—and you'd hold the page over the camp stove like it was infected."

"Paper tells you who wrote it. I knew who wrote it."

"And yet here I am." Sable leaned forward. The charm stayed on, but underneath it, something focused, something hungry. "Here I am, Elodie, because the job matters, and because you're the only crew on the Escarpment that can do it, and we both know it, and we're both going to sit here and pretend that's an easy thing for me to say."

"Say it anyway."

"A survey team. Three weeks, maybe four. Up the River Anselm from the last ford, mapping the channel to the Concession line. Not past it. To it." Sable held up a hand. "I know. I know what that word does in your camp. I'm not asking you to cross the line. I'm asking you to walk to it, look at it, and turn around. Water levels, channel depth, fordable points. My clients are a water concern out of the coast. They hold permits—valid permits, Elodie, I checked them myself, I know you'll check them anyway—and the government wants the survey done before the permits come up for renewal. That's all. That is genuinely all."

Elodie drank her beer and said nothing, because Sable hadn't finished, and Sable always finished.

"The pay," Sable said, "is four hundred a head. Plus two hundred for your captain, and water rights for the crew for a year—clean rights, registered, in whatever names you like. Names you like, Elodie. Think about that. A ledger entry that says exactly what you want it to say."

The number landed in the room like a dropped anvil. Four hundred a head was more than the crew made in a good year, and the crew had not had a good year. Bex's books had been bleeding since the spring, quietly, the way Bex did everything, but Elodie had seen the figures. Water rights, registered—Tamsin could stop buying medicine off smugglers. Ferro could stop sleeping in his boots. Isandro could eat like a growing boy instead of like a growing boy on a ranger's rations, which was to say like a small, dignified famine.

Sable watched all of this cross Elodie's face, because she was very good at watching, and Elodie hated her a little for it.

"And the insult," Elodie said. "There's usually an insult. You've done the charm and the money. Where's the part where you remind me what I am?"

Sable's smile flickered—genuine, for half a second, which from Sable was worth more than the coin she'd overpaid the barkeep. "You haven't changed."

"You have. You got worse at hiding it."

"The insult," Sable said, sitting back, "is the job itself. I'm not a fool. You'll take it apart tonight with your people, and Bex will find the arithmetic, and Ovid will find the chemistry, and Tamsin will find the reason not to, and you'll sit there in the middle of all that competence knowing that the one thing nobody can find is what my clients actually want with a river survey they could have bought from anyone cheaper." She turned her glass slowly on the scarred wood. "That's the insult, Elodie. I'm asking you to trust me, and we both know what I am."

It was so close to honesty that Elodie almost missed the thing underneath it. Almost. Twelve years of not-trusting Sable Vane had made her very good at exactly one thing.

"You said *my clients*," Elodie said. "But the letter was sealed with your mark, not a company's. And you said you checked the permits *yourself*. Sable, you don't work for a water concern. Water concerns don't need you. You work for whoever's scared enough to hire their own kind."

For the first time, Sable Vane looked tired. It lasted only a moment, and she covered it with the smile, but Elodie had seen it, and they both knew she'd seen it, and that was its own kind of wound.

"Everyone's someone's own kind," Sable said. "That's the first thing the Escarpment teaches and the last thing anyone learns." She stood, dropped a second coin on the table—far too much, deliberately, a small flourish of contempt or apology, with Sable it was impossible to say—and settled her hat. "The survey team assembles at the last ford in nine days. Pell's the engineer's name; he'll have the papers. If you're not there, I hire the Marrow brothers, and they'll lose two men and a third of the porters to the canyon, and I'll have to live with that, and so, in a smaller way, will you." She paused at the edge of the table. "For what it's worth—and I know what it's worth, which is nothing—I'm glad you burned the letter. It means you still think before you open things."

"Sable." Elodie didn't stand. "Why do you need us? Truly. You've got the money to hire anyone."

Sable looked down at her for a long moment, and the saloon noise seemed to recede, the card game, the barkeep, the O L BEE sign outside all of it fading the way the desert fades when a storm builds at the far edge of it.

"Because whoever else I hired," she said quietly, "wouldn't know what the Anselm is. And someone has to know what it is, Elodie. Before this is done, someone has to actually know." 

Then she was gone, the door swinging, the heat of the street swallowing her, and Elodie sat alone with two beers' worth of coins and a decision that had just gotten heavier instead of lighter.

* * *

She rode back to the waystation in the long gold light of late afternoon, letting the horse pick its line while she turned the conversation over, again and again, like a stone with something underneath it.

*Someone has to know what it is.*

The Anselm was the river that had carved the Escarpment—every child on the flats learned that much. It rose somewhere in the high country and fell off the escarpment edge in a series of falls that no map agreed on, and above the falls it ran through country that didn't appear on maps at all, because maps of that country had a way of getting their cartographers talked to, and then talked about, and then not talked about ever again.

The Concession.

The crew didn't say the word. That was the rule that predated all the other rules, the one rule that had never been written down because writing it down would have meant saying it. Elodie had inherited it along with the crew itself, from the founders, from the two grim old rangers who'd built the company and built the silence into its bones the way you build a keel into a boat. *We don't go north of the falls,* old Marram had told her, the only time she'd ever asked. *Not for pay, not for water, not for blood. Some ground you don't walk on twice.*

She hadn't asked what *twice* meant. She'd been young, and the crew had been warm, and belonging had seemed worth the price of one unasked question. Twenty years on, she was still paying it, in installments, and the interest was Sable Vane sitting in a saloon saying *someone has to know* with tired eyes.

The waystation came into view at dusk, cook-smoke rising straight in the windless air, the horses a dark congregation at the rail. Ferro's silhouette detached itself from the rocks before she was within a quarter mile—he'd heard her coming, of course, Ferro heard everything coming—and by the time she dismounted, the whole crew had arranged itself around the fire with the studied casualness of people who had absolutely been waiting.

Bex had the ledger open on her knees. Tamsin was mending a pack strap that didn't need mending. Ovid was whittling, which meant he was thinking about explosives, which was how he relaxed. And Isandro sat cross-legged nearest the flames, seventeen and all elbows, watching her with the naked, unguarded hunger for information that was the one thing about him no amount of ranger training would ever sand down.

"Well?" Bex said.

Elodie sat down heavily, held her hands to the fire, and told them all of it. The saloon. The offer. The numbers, which made Ovid's knife stop moving and Bex's pen stop writing. The permits, the nine days, the last ford. She told them about *my clients* and the sealed letter and the tiredness under the smile, and she told them the last part last, the way you save the stone for the bottom of the stew pot:

"She said someone has to know what the Anselm is. And then she left."

The fire cracked. Somewhere out in the dark, a coyote made its small opinion known.

"Four hundred a head," Bex said at last, in the voice she used for inventory, which was how she handled everything that frightened her. "Plus two hundred. Plus registered water rights."

"I heard the numbers, Bex."

"I'm saying them out loud so they're on the record. Someone has to count everything. It's the job." But her pen didn't move.

Tamsin set down the strap she wasn't mending. "She said the Concession line. Not past it." Her voice was careful, flat, the voice she used for wounds. "But Sable Vane has never in her life walked to a line and stopped at it. I knew her a year, Elodie, before you did, in the coast garrisons. She stops at a line only when there's something on the other side she wants you to fetch."

"I know."

"And the timing." Tamsin's eyes were steady across the fire. "Permits coming up for renewal. On *that* ground. After twenty years of nobody touching it. That's not a survey, Elodie. That's a door being oiled."

"I know that too." Elodie rubbed her face. "Which is why we vote. Tonight, all of us, like always. And Isandro—" the boy started, and she raised a hand, gentle, "—you get a voice. You don't get a vote yet. That's the rule until your second year. But I want to hear you anyway."

Isandro looked around the fire—at Bex, at Tamsin, at Ovid, at Ferro, who had appeared from the darkness at some point and was leaning against the water barrels with his arms folded—and Elodie watched him understand, with a jolt she could almost see, that this was the question. Not the money, not the river. This. The thing the crew had been built around, the silence shaped like a place, and Sable Vane had just walked into their camp carrying a lantern.

"The Marrow brothers," Isandro said slowly, "would lose two men and a third of the porters."

"Sable's arithmetic," Elodie said. "Take it with salt."

"But if it's even half true." He looked into the fire, jaw working. "Then saying no doesn't keep us clean. It just means it happens where we can't see it." He stopped, hearing himself, and glanced up, suddenly uncertain. "Is that—is that a reason?"

Elodie felt the question go through the whole crew like a current through water. It was a reason. It was the worst kind of reason, the kind that made a hard thing feel inevitable, and every one of them knew it, and every one of them was silent, and the fire burned, and nine days ticked down somewhere in the dark like water dripping into an empty canteen.

"Vote's at dawn," Elodie said, and reached for the coffee pot, and no one moved, and the night got very long.
