## 5. Sworn Off

Tamsin knew the vote was coming the way she knew a fever was coming in a patient—before anyone said a word aloud, before the first symptom showed. It was in how Elodie set out six cups instead of five at the evening fire, and then stood looking at them as if one of the cups had insulted her. It was in Bex sharpening a pencil that didn't need sharpening. It was in Ovid humming, which he only did when he was nervous or had recently buried something explosive, and there was no reason to bury anything explosive.

The courier's letter was three days dead by then. Elodie had burned it unread at the waystation, and everyone had watched her hands shake, and nobody had said anything, because that was the crew's whole religion: you didn't say things. You carried them.

But Sable Vane did not accept being ignored. The second letter had arrived that morning, delivered not by courier but by a boy on a fast mule who'd been paid to ride until he found them, which meant Vane had spent real money, which meant she wanted an answer badly enough to pay for it.

Tamsin read the letter twice by firelight while the others pretended not to watch. She was the medic; people handed her things and expected her to be steady about it. It was a reasonable expectation most days. Her hands were steady now. It was everything behind her hands that wasn't.

*Elodie,* it began, no honorific, no warmth, just the name like a stone set down on a table. *The offer stands. Escort up the Anselm to the Concession line. Survey team, ninety days, paid in water rights scrip convertible anywhere. You know I wouldn't ask if anyone else could do it. You know why.*

*You know why.* Two words doing the work of a hundred.

Tamsin folded the letter along its original creases and looked around the fire. Isandro was mending a sandal with the fierce concentration of a boy who'd learned that useful hands were welcome hands. Ferro was cleaning his rifle he'd already cleaned. Ovid was humming. Bex had moved on from the pencil to re-counting a cash box whose total never changed.

"Well," Tamsin said. "She's back."

Nobody asked who. That was the thing about this crew—they'd all been carrying the same ghost for twenty years, and ghosts travel light but they don't travel alone.

* * *

Tamsin had been twenty-six when she rode into the Concession country the first time, and she'd been someone else entirely, though she could no longer remember the other woman's name, which was either the cost of her new life or its whole point, depending on the hour.

She remembered the camp, though. She remembered the smell of the infirmary tent—carbolic and canvas and the particular sweetness of gangrene starting—and she remembered the miners' faces, because a medic remembers faces even when she burns the rest of the file.

There had been two hundred and eleven miners in the Concession camps that spring. Tamsin knew the number because she'd counted the ledgers afterward, sitting in a tent with Elodie and Bex and the two founders whose names none of them spoke anymore, copying figures by lamplight while men with company badges waited outside to make sure the arithmetic came out right.

The paperwork had been beautiful. That was what she'd never been able to explain to anyone since—the beauty of it. A massacre is a messy thing, all panic and bad aim and men running into ravines, but the document that followed it was clean as surgery. *Reclassification of mineral claims. Voluntary abandonment of holdings. Compensation disbursed per schedule.* Signatures in three colors of ink. A magistrate's seal pressed so firmly into the wax it left a crater.

"It makes it look legal," the older founder had said—not their founder, one of the company's—and Tamsin had understood, in the way you understand a bone is broken before you feel the pain, that *legal* and *true* were two different countries, and she was standing on the border with a satchel full of forged passports.

They hadn't killed anyone, the four of them. They hadn't fired a shot. They'd carried paper. They'd made two hundred and eleven deaths into a bookkeeping entry, and they'd been paid for it—not in money, exactly, but in something better: erasure. New names. A code. A life off every map, where no one from the Concession country would ever find them and ask them what they'd done.

Tamsin had told herself for twenty years that they'd had no choice. Some nights she almost believed it.

* * *

"Say it plain," Bex said, when the fire had burned down to coals and Isandro had been sent to check the picket line—a mercy, Tamsin thought, more than a chore. The boy needed to not hear this, and Elodie needed him gone before she could say what she was going to say.

Elodie turned the second letter over in her fingers without opening it again. She didn't need to. Tamsin suspected she'd memorized it in the first reading, the way she memorized tracks.

"Sable Vane wants us to escort a survey team up the Anselm," Elodie said. "To the Concession line."

Silence. Even Ovid stopped humming, which was how you knew it was serious; Ovid's humming was load-bearing.

"The Concession," Ferro said flatly. He didn't make it a question. Ferro rarely wasted words on questions when a statement would do the damage.

"There's a survey team. Permits. Ninety days." Elodie's voice was level, captain-level, the voice she used for weather reports and casualty counts. "And before anyone says it—I know. I know what we swore."

"Do you?" Tamsin asked, and heard her own voice come out sharper than she'd intended.

Everyone looked at her. Tamsin was the one who patched people up and told terrible jokes while she did it, the one who kept a jar of candied ginger for shock victims and small children and occasionally captains. She was not the one who sharpened her voice.

But twenty years was a long time to hold a splinter, and tonight her hand was near it.

"I know what we swore," Elodie repeated, quieter. "I also know we're down to our last reliable water cache, Bex has been trading route knowledge at rates that would embarrass a charity, and winter comes early on the high mesa. This job pays enough to keep us fed for two years."

"Two years," Bex murmured. Bex could make numbers sound like scripture.

"And it's Vane," Ferro said. "Which means it's a knife with a handle shaped like a job."

"All true," Elodie said. "All of it. So we vote. Like always." She looked around the fire, and Tamsin watched her captain put on composure the way other people put on a coat—one arm, then the other, buttoned to the throat. "But we vote knowing everything. Not the version where we pretend it's just a long walk up a river."

Ovid cleared his throat. "For those of us who joined after," he said carefully—he'd been with them twelve years and still said *after* like a man stepping around a hole in the floor—"what exactly is it we're pretending not to know?"

Tamsin closed her eyes briefly. Of course. Ovid had never been told. Neither had Isandro, and Isandro was safely out of earshot, which meant the question would land on her, because it always landed on her. Elodie carried the guilt; Bex carried the books; Ferro carried the guns. Tamsin carried the telling.

"You've noticed," she said, opening her eyes, "that none of us will take work south of the Anselm headwaters. You've noticed we go forty miles around a stretch of map that adds four days to any crossing. You're too polite to ask why, and I've always loved you for it, but you're asking now."

"I'm asking now," Ovid agreed gently.

So she told him. Not all of it—never all of it—but the shape of it. The mining camps. The spring twenty years ago when the company decided water rights were worth more than the men holding the claims. The two hundred and eleven. The paperwork, clean as surgery, that turned slaughter into surrender. And the four young rangers—young, stupid, cornered, complicit—who carried that paper out of the canyon and were paid in new names.

"We didn't pull a trigger," she finished. "We held the pen steady while someone else signed. And then we built a life where nobody could ever ask us about it. That's the taboo. That's the sworn-off. It's not the place, Ovid. It's the mirror."

The fire popped. Somewhere out on the picket line, Isandro's voice drifted back, low and easy, talking to the horses the way he talked to everything, like the world was mostly good if you were patient with it.

Ovid was quiet a long moment. Then: "And going back would mean—what? Being seen? Being found?"

"Being *there*," Tamsin said. "Walking ground where we helped bury the truth under a magistrate's seal. With a company survey team. Working for whoever holds those permits now, which I'd bet my last jar of ginger is the same company, wearing a different hat."

* * *

They voted the way they always voted: stones in a tin cup, passed hand to hand, so no one had to say their piece aloud and no one could later claim they'd been pressured. White stone for taking the job. Black for refusing.

Bex went first. White. "Numbers are numbers," Bex said, not apologizing. "We starve elegantly or we eat compromised. I choose eating. I'm sorry, Tamsin. I'm sorrier than the vote shows."

Ferro went second. Black. He didn't explain. Ferro's explanations were usually delivered later, at range, in caliber.

Ovid went third. He held his stone over the cup for a long time, turning it. "I came to this crew because it was the first place I ever felt clean," he said. "Whatever you all did before me, you've spent twenty years paying it forward in the only currency you had—finding lost people, returning stolen things, keeping your hands empty. That counts for something. But I can't walk into that canyon as hired muscle for the heirs of whatever happened there." Black.

Three votes in, it was two-one for refusal, with two to cast. Tamsin took the cup. Her stone was black before her fingers closed around it; she'd palmed it from her kit pouch an hour ago, because some part of her had known since the waystation, since Elodie's shaking hands, that this night was coming, and Tamsin prepared for fevers before they broke.

"No," she said simply. "Not there. Not for her. Not for anyone." She looked at Elodie across the fire. "You weren't there. You don't get to spend the dead to buy groceries."

"Tamsin—"

"No. Let me finish, because I've been finishing this speech for twenty years in my head and it deserves its moment." Her voice cracked, and she let it. "Old wounds don't scar, Elodie. Everyone says they scar. They don't. They just wait. They sit there under the skin, quiet, and then somebody offers you money to walk back over them, and you find out they were open the whole time. I have dressed the wounds of strangers for thirty years. I know a wound that isn't healed when I see one. This crew is one."

Elodie took the cup last. She sat with it in both hands, and Tamsin watched her captain's face do something rare: nothing at all. Perfect stillness, the stillness of deep water.

Then Elodie tipped her hand, and the white stone clicked against the tin.

"Bex," she said. "You're outvoted with me. Three-two, it's no."

Bex exhaled. Ferro began reassembling his rifle with the air of a man closing a ledger. Ovid picked his humming back up, softly, a tuneless little melody that meant relief.

But Tamsin kept watching Elodie, because Tamsin had spent thirty years watching people's faces for the thing they said underneath the thing they said, and what she saw there was not relief.

It was hunger. Careful, banked, ashamed of itself—but hunger. Elodie Yarrow wanted to go back to the Concession. Had maybe always wanted to go back, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. And if Sable Vane wrote one more letter, offered one thing more—

Elodie caught her looking. For a heartbeat, captain and medic regarded each other across the coals, and neither lied.

"You'll tell me if it changes," Tamsin said quietly. It wasn't a request.

"It won't change," Elodie said.

Behind them, from the dark of the picket line, came the soft crunch of boots, and Isandro's voice, bright with curiosity and the terrible timing of the young: "Vote's done? What'd we decide?"

Four rangers looked at one another around a dying fire, a family built on silence, and Elodie smiled the smile she used for border guards and said, "Nothing, kid. Just whether to fix the east fence."

Tamsin reached for the ginger jar and said nothing at all, and hated herself a little for how good at it she'd become.
