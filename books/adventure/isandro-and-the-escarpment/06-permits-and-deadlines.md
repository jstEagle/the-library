## 6. Permits and Deadlines

Ovid had a theory about paper.

Explosives were honest. A charge was either cut correctly or it wasn't, and if it wasn't, the rock told you so immediately and often memorably. Paper was different. Paper lied the way water lied—softly, patiently, wearing away at whatever you thought was solid until one morning your footing was gone and you were somewhere you never meant to be.

So when Bex slid the leather folio across the table at the Salt Lark waystation and said, "You're the one who reads fine print," Ovid felt the particular dread he always felt around documents, which was the same dread he felt around fuses that someone else had spliced.

"Why me?"

"Because Tamsin reads everything like it's sick and needs diagnosing. Ferro reads nothing. Isandro reads fast but believes what he reads." Bex tapped the folio once with two fingers. "And because whoever drew this up wanted people to stop reading by page three."

The common room of the Salt Lark was nearly empty at midmorning—a caravan master snoring in the corner, the station keeper's daughter doing sums behind the counter and getting them wrong with great confidence. Good. Ovid liked witnesses to be elsewhere when he worked.

He opened the folio.

* * *

The first thing he noticed was that there was too much of it. Eleven pages for what should have been four. The opening pages were a thicket of recitals—"WHEREAS" appeared eleven times in the first paragraph alone, each one a small ceremonial drumbeat announcing that something important was about to happen—and then, buried on page five, in type small enough to be an apology, the actual terms.

Ovid read the way he laid charges: slowly, twice, and with his hands flat on the table so he wouldn't start gesturing.

"The Anselm Concession," he murmured. "Renewal instrument. Holders: the Meridian Water and Land Company." He looked up. "Bex. Who are Meridian?"

"Nobody." Bex sat across from him, arms folded, watching the door out of habit. "That's the point of companies. They're nobodies with letterheads. Keep going."

He kept going.

The concession covered the upper Anselm—the river's whole run through the canyon country, from the Escarpment rim to the source springs inside the old forbidden ground. Ninety years ago some colonial governor had granted it to a mining concern; twenty years ago the mines had stopped, violently, in the way everyone knew and nobody said; and now here was a renewal instrument, drafted fresh, sitting in a folio that Sable Vane's courier had left with a caravan broker who owed Bex a favor.

Ninety days. That was the number that mattered. The renewal lapsed in ninety days, and after that the crown reverted the land—unless the holders could show, per clause fourteen, subsection six, "continuous beneficial use and documented improvement of the granted waters."

Ovid read that phrase four times.

"Documented improvement," he said aloud, tasting it.

"Sounds like a compliment," Bex said.

"It's not. It's a shovel." He turned the folio around and put his finger under the line. "Beneficial use means they have to be using the water. Documented improvement means they have to be building things—diversions, weirs, pumping stations, storage. If they can show the surveyor's office a river that's been fenced, measured, and put to work, the renewal is nearly automatic. If they can't—if the land reverts wild—the water rights go back to the commons and every town downstream gets a claim."

"So they need the survey."

"They need more than the survey." Ovid pulled the folio back and kept turning pages, because there was a schedule annexed at the end, and schedules were where intentions lived. "Look at this. Milestone one: hydrographic survey of the main channel. Milestone two: site assessment at—" he squinted at the coordinates, did the mental arithmetic of latitude against the maps pinned in his head from a hundred caravan briefings, and felt the hair on his forearms rise— "at the source springs. Milestone three: 'clearance of legacy obstructions.'"

"Legacy obstructions."

"That's what it says."

"In canyon country," Bex said slowly, "what would you call a legacy obstruction?"

Ovid thought about it the way he thought about all demolition problems: what's there, what do they want there instead, and what has to come out in between. Homesteads. Fences. Wells. Graves, if graves got in the way of a pump house, though no schedule ever wrote the word *graves*. "Anything that isn't theirs," he said finally. "Which, given the history, could be quite a lot."

Bex was quiet for a moment. At the counter, the station keeper's daughter announced her sum to the empty room, got it wrong again, and started over without shame. Ovid envied her a little. His own sums never let him start over.

* * *

He worked through the afternoon, and by late afternoon he had filled three sheets of his own paper with figures, because the thing about cold arithmetic is that somebody has to be cold enough to do it.

Here was what the numbers said.

The Anselm ran a hundred and forty miles from source to the escarpment towns, and fed perhaps forty thousand people along the way—not counting the seasonal camps, the caravan wells it recharged, and the flood-plain gardens that turned green every spring like a rumor of a better country. Whoever held the concession held the right to take water from any point upstream of any settlement. Clause nine gave them priority of use. Clause twelve let them sell surplus rights to third parties. Clause seventeen—

Clause seventeen was the one he read standing up.

It permitted the holders, upon renewal, to "consolidate and extinguish" subordinate claims within the grant. Extinguish. As if water claims were candles. As if the towns downstream were so much wax.

Ovid walked to the window and looked out at the Salt Lark's water tank, a dented iron thing on a timber frame, sweating in the heat. A lizard was drinking from the overflow drip with the focused devotion of a creature that understood exactly what water was worth. Everything out here came down to it. Wages were paid in it. Wars were fought with less cause than a good well.

"Ninety days," he said to the glass.

Then he went back to the table and did the cruelest sum of all, the one he'd been avoiding: what the crew stood to make. Vane's offer hadn't named a figure yet—that was coming, offers always came dressed last—but escort work upriver for a company with this kind of paper behind it would pay more than the crew made in two ordinary years. Two years of water and silence, as Elodie put it. Enough to fix the camp roof. Enough to buy Ferro a horse that didn't hate him. Enough to stop counting canteens before every long haul.

And all it would cost was ninety days of helping these people document their improvements.

He sat back down and looked at his three sheets of figures until they blurred.

"Bex," he said. "There's a version of this where we're just guides. There's a version where we're witnesses. And there's a version where we're the shovel." He tapped clause fourteen. "The question is whether we get to choose which one we are, or whether it's already been chosen for us."

"You've been talking to Tamsin."

"I've been talking to paper. Tamsin just agrees with it."

* * *

Elodie came in at dusk, dusty from the border road, with Isandro a half-length behind carrying her saddlebags and trying to look like he hadn't been asking questions the whole ride. Ovid watched the boy clock the folio on the table, clock Ovid's face above it, and wisely go find work at the far end of the room.

"Well?" Elodie said. She didn't sit. Elodie rarely sat indoors; Ovid suspected she distrusted furniture the way she distrusted ledgers, as a technology for trapping people.

"It's real," Ovid said. "The permit, I mean. It's genuine—or forged by someone very good, and if forged, forged by the same hand that will judge whether it's genuine, which amounts to the same thing." He spread his sheets. "Ninety days to expiry. They need a survey filed before then showing beneficial use, or the land reverts and their claim dies. So the survey is real too. What's not real is the reason."

"Give me the reason."

"Water." He said it plainly, because dressing it up would be a kindness and Elodie had never once thanked him for kindness. "Not mining, not land. The source controls the whole river. A hundred miles of rights, forty thousand people's worth, and clauses in here that let the holder extinguish every other claim once they renew. Someone intends to own a river, Elodie. Not rent it. Own it. And the massacre twenty years ago wasn't a tragedy that happened near this paperwork." He turned the folio to the date line. "It was the paperwork's first milestone."

The lamp between them hissed. Out in the dark, a waystation dog barked twice at nothing and thought better of it.

Elodie picked up the folio. She read the way she did everything—fast, level, giving nothing away—and Ovid watched her reach clause seventeen and saw the only tell she had, which was stillness. Elodie in motion was unreadable. Elodie perfectly still was a woman standing very carefully on thin ice.

"We haven't signed anything," she said.

"No."

"And Vane hasn't named her price."

"Not yet."

"Then we know nothing we can be accused of knowing." She closed the folio and set it down, and her voice was light in the way Ovid had learned to distrust more than anger. "Get some sleep. I want those figures copied clean before we move on."

"Elodie." He kept his voice low. "I've blown up bridges for less money than this job pays, and I've never once needed to know who was crossing them. But this—" he gathered his sheets, squared their edges, a demolitions man's tic, everything true must be neat— "this isn't a bridge. This is the ground under forty thousand feet. Somebody's going to stand on it either way. The only question we get to answer is whose side of the charge we're standing on when it goes."

For a moment she looked at him with something almost like the old warmth, the crew-of-no-names warmth, the warmth that had kept them all alive through years when being alive had been a full-time occupation.

"You always did read fine print," she said.

"Somebody has to. The rock certainly won't warn us."

She took the folio and went upstairs, and Ovid stayed at the table copying his figures clean, because she'd asked, and because the act of writing them out made them harder to un-know. Ninety days. Forty thousand people. One river, patient as paper, wearing away at everything soft.

He was on the second sheet when Isandro drifted over, ostensibly to refill the lamp, actually to read upside down, which the boy did with the shameless fluency of the truly curious.

"What's 'extinguish' mean?" Isandro asked. "In the paper sense."

Ovid considered lying, found he didn't have the energy, and considered the truth, found it weighed about the same.

"It means," he said, trimming the wick, "that somebody wrote down a plan to make a lot of things stop existing, and they were careful enough to spell it politely." He met the boy's eyes. "Why do you ask?"

"Because," Isandro said, taking the lamp and turning toward the stairs, "the captain's been up there an hour, and I can hear her pacing, and she only paces like that when she's already decided something and hates it."

Ovid looked at his neat columns of figures, at the ninety days marching through them like a fuse burning backward toward its charge, and thought: *yes*. That was exactly the trouble with paper. Sooner or later, it caught.
