## 7. Hiring On

The contract arrived the way bad news always did in the Escarpment: folded small, carried by someone who wouldn't meet your eye, and smelling faintly of another person's perfume.

Isandro watched it happen from the top of the water wagon, where he was supposed to be checking the canvas ties and was actually watching Bex count barrels for the third time, which was how Bex prayed. The courier was a lean woman with dust to her knees and a company pin on her collar that she kept touching like a worry-stone. She handed Elodie the envelope at the edge of camp, said six words Isandro couldn't hear, and left at a walk that wanted very badly to be a run.

Elodie stood there holding it for a long moment. Then she came back into camp, sat down at the folding table Bex used for ledgers, and unrolled the contract with the flat expression she wore when she was deciding something and didn't want anyone guessing which way.

Isandro climbed down off the wagon without pretending otherwise. Nobody stopped him. That was one of the strange graces of the crew: nobody ever told him he was too young to watch, they just let him stand at the edge of things until he understood them or didn't.

"You're reading it," Bex said. It wasn't a question, but it had the shape of one, the way all of Bex's sentences did when she disapproved in advance.

"I'm reading it," Elodie agreed.

Ovid drifted over from the cook fire with his hands still black from whatever he'd been detonating or nearly detonating that morning. Tamsin appeared out of the medic tent with the particular silence she used instead of opinions. Ferro didn't appear at all, which meant he was already somewhere high up with a view of everyone's faces, doing whatever Ferro did, which was everything.

Isandro squeezed in between Ovid and the wagon wheel and read upside down.

He'd learned his letters from Tamsin, on flour sacks and waystation manifests, so he read slowly, but he read everything. He'd learned early that in this crew, reading was survival. A misread water mark could kill you faster than a snake.

The contract was long. Company contracts were always long; Ovid said length was how lawyers buried the bodies. But Isandro got the bones of it fast enough. *The party known as Yarrow's Rangers agrees to escort and safeguard a survey expedition ascending the River Anselm to the headwaters of the Concession tract.* Ninety days. Water rights language. Clauses about silence — those were everywhere, thick as thornbush, whole paragraphs about what the crew would never say, never write, never testify to, in exchange for sums that made Isandro's eyes stick.

He knew the number was enormous because Bex made a sound when she reached it. Bex never made sounds. Bex communicated approval by saying nothing louder than usual.

"The Concession," Tamsin said quietly.

Elodie didn't look up. "It says Anselm headwaters."

"It says Concession," Tamsin said again, and there was something under her voice that Isandro had only heard once before, the night a drifter at a waystation had joked about dead miners and Tamsin had broken his nose with a ladle and then apologized to the ladle.

* * *

They argued about it for two days. Isandro heard all of it, because the crew argued the way families argue, in pieces, over chores, never all at once, as if the argument were a load too heavy for any single conversation to carry.

Bex was for it. Bex laid it out at the ledger table with her finger on the numbers: the crew's water stores were low, their route-knowledge trade had dried up since the caravans started hiring government scouts, and winter on the high flats killed the unprepared. "We take the job, we eat for two years. We refuse it, we eat for one, and we spend that year hungry and proud."

"Proud keeps," Ovid said. He was cleaning blasting caps and not looking at anyone, which was how Ovid participated in conversations. "Hungry doesn't."

Ferro voted with a shrug that meant *I'll go wherever we go and I'll be fine either way*, which was Ferro's position on weather, politics, and death.

Tamsin was against it with a flatness that frightened Isandro more than shouting would have. "That ground is sworn off. Not by law. By us." She looked around the table. "By the people who built this crew. There's a reason none of us will say why, and I'm not going to be the one who says it, but I'll say this much: some places you leave alone out of respect, and some places you leave alone because you're afraid of what you'd find, and I have never once been sure which kind the Concession is."

"And the vote?" Elodie asked.

Tamsin held her gaze. "You know my vote. You've known it for twenty years, same as I've known yours."

Which meant, Isandro realized, sitting very still by the cook fire, that Elodie's vote was already cast. The arguing was a ceremony. The decision had been made the moment she'd unrolled the paper.

* * *

On the third morning, Elodie signed.

Isandro made sure he was there. He told himself it was because someone should witness it, because that was a ranger's duty — you witnessed things, you remembered them, you carried the true version even when everyone else agreed to carry a comfortable lie. That was the code. He'd had it taught to him across a hundred miles of salt flat by the very woman now dipping a pen.

*Take nothing you can't carry,* she'd told him, that first day, when he was a starving boy caught stealing water with both hands. *Leave no name behind. And never, ever work for a man who counts his money while you're still thirsty.*

She'd laughed when she said it. She'd ruffled his hair with a hand gritty from salt, and he'd thought, *this is the truest thing anyone has ever said to me.*

Now she sat at the ledger table with the company courier standing respectful and patient at her shoulder, and she read every line — he'd give her that, she read every line — and when she reached the clause near the end, she set the pen down and said, "Before I sign. Amendment."

The courier's smile tightened by a degree. "The terms are—"

"The terms are what I make them," Elodie said pleasantly. "Your employers want us because nobody else can get a survey team up the Anselm alive. That gives me leverage, and I intend to spend all of it." She pulled a fresh sheet toward her and wrote in her quick slanted hand while she talked. "One: the crew goes armed. All of us, sidearms and long guns, no discussion. Two: my people answer to me and no one else. Any surveyor, any company man, any titled gentleman who gives an order to one of my crew directly gets that order ignored and possibly repeated back to him in a tone he won't enjoy. Three: if I judge the route unsafe, we stop. Not negotiate. Stop. Those three clauses go in, or this pen stays dry."

Isandro's chest did something complicated. Part of it swelled — that was his captain, that was the woman who'd taken a thief off the salt and made him a tracker, bending a company to her will with three sentences. And part of it felt like watching someone he loved walk confidently into a wall.

Because here was the thing Isandro couldn't stop seeing, the splinter working its way under his skin: she was negotiating beautifully. She was winning. And the whole time, the prize she was winning was permission to do the one thing the code said you never did.

*Never work for men who count money while you're still thirsty.*

The company had counted its money for twenty years over the graves of the Concession. Everyone knew it, the way everyone knew the sky was up. Miners had gone in and hadn't come out, and the paperwork had come out clean, and the land had been closed like a mouth, and the crew's own founders — Tamsin's face went shut whenever it came near — had helped close it.

And now Elodie was signing on to walk armed men through that country for wages.

"Sign there," the courier said, "and initial the amendment."

Elodie signed. Her hand didn't shake. Isandro watched closely for it, wanting it to shake, needing some crack in the composure to prove that the woman underneath still believed the things she'd taught him. It didn't shake. It moved smooth and certain across the page, three signatures and a date, and then she blotted it and rolled it and handed it over like she was returning a borrowed tool.

"Water and provisions within the week," she said. "And tell your employers: my crew eats first, always. Before the surveyors, before the porters, before me. If that's a problem, refund yourselves now."

"Not a problem," the courier said, and left with the contract, and the camp exhaled.

* * *

That evening Isandro didn't come to the cook fire.

He sat on the wagon tail instead, in the dark, sharpening a knife he didn't need sharpened, listening to the others laugh at something Ovid had said. The laughter sounded different tonight. It sounded like laughter coming from inside a house whose foundation had shifted.

Footsteps. He knew them before he saw her — Elodie walked heel-first, quiet but not sneaky, the walk of someone who'd decided long ago that sneaking past your own family was a waste of energy.

She sat down beside him on the tailgate. For a while neither of them said anything. Out past the picket lines, the first stars were coming up over the Escarpment rim, hard and white.

"You're angry," she said.

"I'm not angry." He tested the knife edge against his thumbnail. "I'm confused. There's a difference."

"Tell me the difference."

"Angry is when somebody does wrong." He found it hard to say and said it anyway, because she'd taught him that too — *say the hard thing while it's small, before it grows teeth.* "Confused is when you can't figure out if they did."

Elodie nodded slowly, like he'd handed her something heavy and she was testing its weight. "All right. Confused. Walk me through it."

"You taught me the code." He kept his eyes on the knife. "Take nothing you can't carry. Leave no name behind. Never work for men who count money while you're thirsty. You taught me that last one yourself, the first day, on the salt. You laughed when you said it. And now—" He gestured at the camp, at the ledger table where Bex was already entering figures, at the whole warm lit shape of the life he loved. "Now we're working for exactly those men. For the ones who—" He stopped. He almost said *the ones who killed the miners*, but Tamsin's silence hung over those words like a roof he wasn't allowed under yet, so he finished differently. "For the ones who count money over that place. Over the Concession. We're being paid to help them hold it."

Elodie was quiet for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice was level, and that was somehow worse than if it had been sharp. "Do you know why I took you off that salt flat instead of turning you out?"

"Because you were merciful."

"Because you were stealing *water*." She turned to look at him, and the starlight caught the lines around her eyes. "A horse thief steals a horse and sells it and buys water. You skipped the middle step. You went straight for the thing that keeps people alive. I looked at you and thought, there's a boy who understands what matters. Everything else — the code, the rules, the pretty little proverbs — everything else is scaffolding around that one truth. Water is life. People are worth more than property. Say it back."

"Water is life. People are worth more than property." He'd said it a hundred times. Tonight it tasted like reciting.

"Now." She leaned forward, elbows on knees. "There's a company that owns the source of every river in a hundred miles. Owned it for twenty years on paper that smells wrong — don't ask me how I know, I'm not ready to tell you, and when I am, you'll wish I weren't. In ninety days, that paper expires. After that, the land goes back to the crown, and every town downstream stops drinking at that company's pleasure." She let that sit. "Who do you think is better for the water, Isandro? The men who fenced the spring? Or ninety days of our boots on that ground, looking at everything, remembering everything?"

Isandro stared at her. "That's not what the contract says. The contract says survey. The contract says *document*."

"Yes," Elodie said. "It does."

Something in the way she said it — light, almost careless, a door pulled gently shut — snagged on him like a barb. She knew something. She knew something about what the survey was really for, and she'd signed anyway, and she was dressing it up for him in the language of the code, water is life, people over property, as if words could launder a job.

"That's not an answer," he said.

"No," she agreed, standing. "It isn't. It's the answer I have tonight. Ask me again when we're on the river." She put her hand briefly on his head, the old gesture, the salt-flat gesture, and it made his throat hurt worse than any argument would have. "Get some sleep, apprentice. Tomorrow we hire on porters, and porters are worse than horses — they talk."

She walked back toward the fire, toward the laughter, toward the family she'd just spent like coin.

Isandro stayed on the tailgate a while longer, knife across his knees, watching the stars. He tried to rebuild the feeling he'd had for four years — the solid, warm certainty that Elodie Yarrow was the straightest thing in a crooked country, that her code was load-bearing, that if he learned it perfectly he'd never have to be ashamed of himself.

The feeling wouldn't come back right. It came back bent, the way a wheel comes back after a bad crossing — usable, rolling, but pulling always a little to one side.

*The hero's code bends,* he thought, *the moment money appears.* And then, because he was honest even when honesty hurt, he added the rest: *or the moment something bigger than the code walks into camp wearing the code's clothes.*

Two days later, the survey team arrived at the rendezvous bend — twelve porters, a mountain of crates, a nervous young engineer who introduced himself twice, and a hard-eyed company man named Dreele who shook Elodie's hand while counting the crew's weapons with his eyes.

Ferro slid down off the rocks beside Isandro and murmured, without moving his lips, "Count along with me, boy. And tell me when you get to the part that doesn't add up."

Isandro counted. He got there quickly.
