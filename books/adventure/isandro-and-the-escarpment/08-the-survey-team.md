## 8. The Survey Team

Ferro counted things. It was not a habit so much as a spine—something that held him upright when the world went soft and dishonest. He counted the steps from the water barrel to the cook fire (forty-one), the times Bex had said *we're fine* in the last week (nine, each one less convincing than the last), and the number of days since Elodie signed Vane's contract (four, and he still hadn't decided whether to be angry or just tired).

So when the survey team came up the river road at midmorning, dusty and strung out like beads off a broken necklace, Ferro did what he always did.

He ignored their gear entirely and counted their weapons.

* * *

The meeting point was a flat below the last farmstead on the Anselm, where the water ran brown-green and the willows grew thick enough to hide a camp from anyone who wasn't already standing in it. Elodie had chosen it for exactly that reason. Ferro approved. He did not approve of much else about the arrangement, but he kept that behind his teeth, where he kept most things.

"Twelve porters," Bex had said, reading the contract aloud the night before. "Two engineers. Company's called Meridian Water and Land, which is a name like a handshake from a man wearing gloves."

"And we're escorting them," Ovid had said, "into a canyon system I could seal with four charges and a bad attitude."

"We're escorting them," Elodie had said, in the voice that ended conversations.

Now Ferro stood at the edge of the willows and watched the column come, and his spine did its quiet arithmetic.

Porters first: twelve, as promised, though two of them looked barely older than Isandro and moved like men who'd been hired yesterday and fed once. Their loads were wrong. Ferro noted that immediately. Survey teams carried instruments—tripods, theodolites, brass things that caught light and made porters curse. These packs were heavy at the bottom and wrapped in oilcloth, and not one man carried anything that clinked.

Then the engineers. The first was easy to read because he was trying to hide nothing: a young man, maybe twenty-two, with ink stains on three fingers and the expression of someone who had rehearsed looking competent in a mirror and hadn't liked the result. His satchel actually did hold instruments; Ferro could see the tripod leg poking out. This one, then, was real. A junior engineer who believed he was doing engineering.

The second was the problem.

He rode—nobody else rode—and he sat his horse the way Ferro imagined a knife sits in a drawer: patiently, pointed at something. Forty-ish, gray at the temples, clean-shaven with the kind of care that costs money in the middle of nowhere. His clothes were good but deliberately plain, the way rich men dress when they want to be underestimated by everyone except the people paid to notice them. He wore no visible weapon, which in Ferro's experience meant you should count again.

Ferro counted again.

There. Under the dust coat's left side, the line of a shoulder holster. A boot knife on the right calf, worn enough at the strap to be habit rather than fashion. And the horse itself—a company brand over an older brand, burned twice, which meant the animal had been requisitioned, not bought, which meant this man had authority to requisition.

"Twelve porters, two engineers," Elodie murmured beside him. She'd come up without a sound, as she always did, and Ferro had long ago stopped being startled by it. "You're staring."

"I'm counting."

"What's the count?"

"Weapons." Ferro let a moment pass. "Nine among the porters—knives mostly, two short rifles they're carrying like they've never been hungry enough to love them. One pistol on the young engineer, probably never fired. And our rider has two hidden, minimum, plus whatever's in those saddlebags."

Elodie was quiet. "That's a lot of iron for measuring a river."

"That's my point." Ferro turned to look at her. She had the contract-look on her face—the one where her jaw set slightly left of center and her eyes went somewhere forty yards past whatever she was pretending to examine. "Elodie. Twelve porters and thirteen weapons. For a survey. You don't need a gun to write down how wide a river is."

"No," she agreed. "You need guns for other things."

She walked out to meet them before he could say the rest of it, which was: *then why are we here.* But Ferro suspected she'd heard it anyway. She always heard it anyway.

* * *

The introductions happened in the shade of the biggest willow, with Bex laying out water and the flat hard biscuits she called *travelers' bread* and everyone else called *reasons to travel faster.*

"Dreele," the rider said, swinging down. He offered his hand to Elodie first, then to each of them in turn, and his grip was calibrated—firm with the men, brief with Tamsin, warmer with Elodie, as if he'd sorted them all within four seconds and filed them accordingly. "Meridian Water and Land. I'll be directing the survey's schedule. Pell here handles the instruments."

"Pell Ashgrove," the young one said, too fast, and then seemed embarrassed by his own speed. He had a handshake like an apology. Up close he was even younger than he'd looked—sunburned nose, collar too clean, eyes that kept flicking to the crew the way a man checks the exits of a room he's just realized he doesn't control. "I've—I've read the geological reports on the upper Anselm. All of them. There aren't many. That's part of why the company wants a proper—" He stopped himself, visibly deciding he'd said something wrong. "A proper survey."

"A proper survey," Dreele repeated smoothly, and smiled, and the smile had the specific warmth of a lamp turned low to save oil.

Ferro drifted during the talk, the way he always did—unhurried, unremarkable, a lean gray man checking the remounts. Nobody watched a man checking horses. He worked down the porter line, murmuring a compliment here, a question there, and got back more than he gave, because porters always talked. Porters were the weather vanes of any expedition. They knew who paid late, who drank early, whose packs were heaviest and why.

The oldest porter, a leathery woman called Mother Sef who claimed—implausibly—to have carried freight on every river west of the Escarpment, told him the truth in eleven words while tightening a cinch.

"They loaded us at night. We don't know what's in 'em."

"Heavy?" Ferro asked.

"Heavy like paper," she said. "Heavy like boxes of paper." She spat. "I've hauled ore, I've hauled salt, I've hauled a piano once, gods help me. Paper's the worst. Paper's heavy on purpose."

Paper. Ferro filed it. In his experience, expeditions that hauled boxes of paper into canyon country were either churches or governments, and neither of those sent men with shoulder holsters.

He circled back toward the willow in time to catch the tail of the scheduling argument.

"—ninety days is generous," Dreele was saying. "We'll move fast. The permits lapse at the end of the season, and after that the crown reverts the whole concession, and nobody wants to do this work twice."

"Ninety days is generous if nothing goes wrong," Elodie said. "Something always goes wrong."

"Which is why I engaged professionals." Another smile, this one aimed at the whole crew like a thrown net. "Rangers. Off-map people. My employers speak very highly of discretion."

Bex, pouring water, said pleasantly, "Discretion's our best crop. Grows wild around here."

Dreele laughed—a real-sounding laugh, which Ferro distrusted more than a fake one—and clapped Pell on the shoulder. "You see? Worth every coin. Now. I'd like to make camp early today and start upstream tomorrow at first light. Time is the one expense my employers won't forgive."

*Time*, Ferro thought. Not distance, not data, not discovery. Time. As if the deadline were the destination.

* * *

They made camp where the willows thinned, and Ferro took first watch partly because it was his turn and mostly because he wanted to watch the client camp without being watched watching it.

What he saw, through the blue hour and into dark:

Pell spent the evening with his instruments laid out on a blanket, cleaning them, adjusting them, whispering to the theodolite like it was a nervous horse. Once, Ferro saw him glance over at the ranger fires with an expression Ferro recognized because he'd worn it himself, years ago, before he'd learned to erase it: the look of a man standing next to something he suspected was wrong and hoping someone older would say it first.

Dreele spent the evening walking the perimeter. Casually. Twice. He paused at the porters' loads—touched one oilcloth bundle, checked a knot—and then stood a long time facing upstream, into the black mouth of the canyon country, hands clasped behind his back like a man admiring a view he owned.

And the porters slept in a tight huddle, closer than tired men sleep, the way men sleep when they're afraid of each other.

Ferro found Isandro beside him at some point—he always knew when the boy was near now, some sixth sense worn smooth by two years of sharing watches—and they sat together in the dark without talking, which was their best language.

"You counted them too," Ferro said finally. "Don't lie. You've got the face on."

"The face?"

"The one where your eyes keep moving and your mouth pretends it isn't."

Isandro huffed, almost a laugh. "Thirteen weapons. Twelve porters. Two engineers, but only one of them looks like an engineer." He paused. "And the loads are wrong. I helped Mother Sef shift one when it slipped. Whatever's inside is packed in layers. Who layers paper?"

"Men hiding something," Ferro said, "or men planning something. Sometimes both." He glanced sideways at the boy—at the sharp profile, the restless hands, the hunger to understand that Elodie kept feeding and Ferro privately worried would get him killed. "Rule of counting, Isandro. When the numbers don't match the story, believe the numbers. Stories are cheap. Numbers cost somebody money."

"Whose money, though? That's the part I can't count."

"Yet," Ferro said.

Below them, in the client camp, a lantern moved. Dreele, awake past midnight, standing at the edge of his own firelight, looking upstream again. Looking toward the Concession line the way a man looks at a door he intends to open whether or not it's locked.

Ferro realized he was gripping his rifle stock without having decided to.

"One more thing," Isandro whispered. "The young one. Pell. He asked me today if the crew was—this is his word—'reputable.' Like he wanted me to say no. Like he wanted permission."

"Permission for what?"

"I don't know." Isandro's voice dropped further. "But he looked scared, Ferro. Not canyon-scared. Other-scared."

Ferro nodded slowly and watched the lantern across the camp gutter and die. Above them the stars came out in their cold thousands, indifferent, keeping their own ledger.

The job smelled wrong. Not permit-wrong, not pay-wrong—those were ordinary smells, and the crew had lived on ordinary wrongness for years. This was a deeper smell, old and chemical, like the air before lightning: twelve porters hauling layered paper, thirteen weapons for a survey, a company man who loved a deadline more than a map, and a boy-engineer asking permission to disbelieve his own employers.

Tomorrow they'd walk into the canyon country side by side, and every one of Ferro's counts said they were not walking toward the same place.

He settled deeper into the dark and began, out of habit, to count the hours until dawn.

He didn't like that number either.
