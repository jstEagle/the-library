## 9. First Water

The river announced itself a full day before we saw it.

Ferro heard it first—of course Ferro did, the man could hear a beetle changing its mind at forty paces—and by midmorning the rest of us caught it too: a low hush threading through the heat, like the desert murmuring behind its hand. By noon there were green things at the edges of the wash. By evening, when we crested the last stony rise and looked down into the first true canyon of the Anselm, Isandro made a sound I hadn't heard from him since the salt flats.

"Oh," he said. Just that. Just *oh*, small and undone.

I let him have it. A body only gets one first river, and a body that doesn't feel it properly is poorer for life.

Below us the canyon ran westward in long red curves, walls rising sheer on either side, and down the middle of it all went the water—fast, loud, braided white where it broke over stone, deep green where it pooled and thought its slow thoughts. Cottonwoods leaned out over the banks like gossips. The air coming up off it was ten degrees cooler than the air above, and it smelled the way water smells when it has traveled a long way to meet you: mineral, cold, faintly sweet.

"First Water," I said, because the crew had named every camp on this stretch years ago and some names you keep even when nobody's left who remembers why. "We make camp above the high-water line. Not near it. *Above* it."

"How high is high?" Isandro asked.

"Higher than you think," said Tamsin, shouldering past with her medic's roll and the expression she wore whenever someone asked a question that had corpses in its history. "Higher than feels reasonable. Higher than your pride wants."

"Everything's higher than my pride wants lately," Isandro said, and Tamsin snorted, and I decided not to ask what he meant by that, because I suspected I was in it somewhere.

* * *

The trouble with rivers, I told him that night while the crew rigged camp and Dreele's porters grumbled through their own further downstream, is that they lie.

Not maliciously. Rivers don't have malice any more than they have mercy. But they lie the way beautiful things lie—by making you believe the surface is the whole story. You look at the Anselm running calm and green between its banks and you think: *there's a sensible river, a well-behaved river, a river that knows its place.* And then two hundred miles upstream a cloud drops half a season's rain onto ground too hard to drink it, and all that water goes looking for the lowest path home, and the lowest path home is where you pitched your tent.

"So how do you know?" Isandro sat cross-legged on a flat rock above the bank, watching the current with the total suspicious attention he gave everything now. Three weeks of survey work had changed him. He'd filled out some, stopped flinching at Dreele's voice, learned to spit accurately off a moving mule—a skill I neither taught nor endorsed. But the watchfulness was new, and it cut both ways. He watched me closer than he watched the river.

"You listen," I said. "You look. The river tells you what it's doing if you speak its language. Green foam, for instance."

I pointed downstream to where the current bent around a boulder. In the eddy behind it floated a thin scum of bubbles, pale as spit, green-tinged where the algae came loose from the stones upstream.

"Foam's just broken water," Isandro said. "Bex says it means nothing."

"Bex keeps ledgers. Ledgers mean nothing." I crouched, dipped two fingers in the eddy, and held them up so he could see the bubbles clinging, slow to pop. "Foam from white water pops fast. It's young—it was born back there around that bend and it'll be dead in a minute. But foam that sits and *lingers*, foam that's gone green—" I rubbed my fingers together. "That foam came a long way. It was churned hard, miles upstream, through narrow stone. It picked up algae off rocks that only grow in fast, violent water. Green foam means somewhere above us the river is being squeezed through a throat."

"And squeezed rivers do what?"

"What does anything do when you squeeze it?"

He thought about it. I watched him think about it, which was better than watching most people think about anything. "It comes through fast," he said finally. "And angry."

"And loud. You'll hear it before you see it—sounds like a freight train, if you've ever heard one, or like the whole canyon clearing its throat. When you hear that, you don't pack. You don't warn anyone politely. You run uphill and you scream the word *agua* until everyone with legs is running too." I stood, knees cracking their usual complaint. "Flash floods kill more people in canyon country than thirst, snakes, and bad decisions combined. And bad decisions are *very* competitive."

Isandro grinned. Then, because he was Isandro: "Have you ever been caught in one?"

I fed the fire a stick instead of answering, which was an answer, and he was smart enough to read it, and kind enough not to push. That kindness was the thing about him I trusted least, if I'm honest. Mercy in a boy that age usually means he's already forgiven you for something, and I hadn't yet worked out what.

* * *

The desert had opinions about our expedition. I want that on the record, because Dreele kept a journal too, all measurements and elevations, and nowhere in it did he write down what the country actually thought of us.

It thought we were too many. Twelve porters, two engineers, six rangers, thirty-one animals—the canyon took one look at that arithmetic and started charging interest. Water holes we'd counted on from old knowledge came up thin or fouled. The cottonwood shade we'd planned to lunch under had burned in some lightning summer and left us exposed on naked sand. On the fourth day a wind came up the gorge that wasn't in any season, hot and gritty, and it stole two canvas flysheets and Pell's hat, and Pell mourned that hat like a relative.

"The desert's testing us," Isandro said, chasing his hatless engineer friend's papers across camp.

"The desert isn't testing anybody," I said. "The desert doesn't know we're here. That's the whole point of it."

But privately—I will admit this to no living soul except this page—I thought the country was testing *me*. Because trust was fraying in camp like old rope, and I could feel every strand.

Dreele counted our rifles twice a day and pretended he was admiring them. Ovid counted Dreele's men's rifles once and reported the ratio to me with a face like a closed door. Vane's shadow, or something shaped like Vane's shadow, had appeared at the edge of Ferro's glass two nights back—a dust plume moving parallel to us on the rim, keeping pace, never closing. And Elodie Yarrow, who had built her whole life on knowing exactly what she was doing, had signed a contract she didn't fully understand for reasons she couldn't say aloud, and the not-saying sat in my chest like a swallowed stone.

Isandro felt it too. Kids always feel weather before adults admit there are clouds.

"You'd tell me," he said to me on the fifth evening, out at the water point while I showed him how to fill skins without stirring silt, "if we were doing something wrong. Wouldn't you?"

I kept filling the skin. "Define wrong."

"If the job stopped being what you said it was."

The river chuckled over its stones. Somewhere upstream, I noticed without letting my face notice, the foam in the eddies had gone distinctly green.

"Isandro. Lesson three of reading water." I handed him the full skin. "Sometimes the river rises because of rain you can't see, falling on country you've never walked, and there's nothing you did to cause it and nothing you can do to stop it. Your only choices are where you stand when it arrives."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only honest one I've got." I looked at him then, straight on, and gave him the truth in the smallest coin I could pay it in. "If the day comes when I know we're standing in a flood path, I will move you myself. That I promise. The rest—" I shrugged. "The rest is weather."

He took that the way he took everything now: carefully, turning it over, filing it somewhere in that quick ledger of his. Then he ruined the moment beautifully, which was another thing about him I relied on more than I said.

"Tamsin says you snore like a rockslide."

"Tamsin," I said, "is a liar and a gossip, and also correct. Go check the picket lines."

* * *

On the sixth day the river tried to kill us, politely, as a warning.

We'd made good distance through a narrows section—walls close enough that Ferro claimed he could shake hands with himself across the gap, and the light came down in dusty gold shafts like the inside of a cathedral built by someone with more ambition than patience. Beautiful country. Deadly country. I knew it, and I pushed through it anyway, because Dreele was pushing and because the alternative was losing a day and giving the company man more reasons to distrust us, and I chose speed over instinct, and I have replayed that choice enough times since to wear grooves in it.

It was Isandro who heard it.

We were strung along the bank, porters ahead, animals mid-column, when he stopped dead in the trail and turned his head upstream with his whole body, the way a deer does. "Elodie." Not Captain. Not ma'am. Just my name, cracked down the middle. "What's that sound?"

And there it was. Under the river's ordinary chatter: a deeper note, a rolling, grinding hush, far off but coming on like a debt collector. My stomach dropped through the canyon floor.

"*Agua!*" I screamed it before my mind finished the thought, the word tearing out of me raw. "*Agua! Agua! HIGH GROUND!*"

The canyon came apart into motion. Porters dropping loads and scrambling. Ovid hauling two of the younger ones bodily by their collars. Tamsin herding Pell, who had frozen—engineers freeze, it's in their training—up a talus slope. Bex cutting a mule loose rather than waste seconds on the tangle of its lead. And Isandro, my apprentice, my boy-of-the-flats, running the line and screaming the word himself, screaming it in two languages, shoving a porter named Cusco toward the rocks with both hands, doing exactly what I'd taught him, exactly, the lesson landing in his bones four days after I put it in his ears—

The wall of water came around the bend like the canyon exhaling.

I won't dress it up. It was brown and it was tall and it sounded like the end of argument. It took the lower trail where we'd stood ninety seconds earlier and erased it. It took two pack mules that couldn't be freed in time—I can still hear that, and I expect I always will. It took the cook fire, the spare ropes, Dreele's dignity, and a good hundred yards of bank, and then, having made its point, it settled back into its channel and resumed looking sensible and green and well-behaved, as though nothing whatsoever had happened.

Silence, after. Canyon silence, huge and ringing.

We counted heads on the talus slope. All rangers. All engineers. All twelve porters, minus Cusco's eyebrows, which would take weeks to grow back and which he touched reverently for the rest of the day. We'd lost gear, animals, and roughly all of my margin for error.

Dreele stalked up, dry except where it mattered, eyes wet with fury. "You knew those narrows flash. Any fool knows it. Why were we in them?"

"Because your schedule said to be in them," I said, level, while inside me something smaller and truer said *he's right, he's right, you knew the foam was green yesterday and you pushed anyway.* "Because you set the pace, Mr. Dreele, and I let you. That's on both of us. It won't happen twice."

He glared. Behind him, Pell was quietly sick over a rock. And Isandro came and stood next to me—not touching, never touching, the crew didn't touch much—but standing where I could feel him, and he said nothing at all, which from Isandro was a paragraph.

* * *

That night we camped high, on a bench of red sandstone well above any water the river could plausibly invent, and the mood in camp was quiet the way a bruise is quiet.

I found Isandro sitting at the edge of the bench, legs dangling over the drop, watching the river run below—running calm again, shameless, moonlit silver over its rearranged stones. I sat down beside him. Below us, in an eddy, foam gathered and lingered, green-tinged, patient.

"You saved Cusco," I said.

"He saved himself mostly. I just yelled."

"Yelling the right word at the right time is the entire job. Everything else is paperwork." I turned a stone over in my fingers, weighing something heavier than the stone. "I taught you the lesson four days ago and you used it tonight. Most people take years. Some take their whole lives and die stupid."

"You used it too," he said. "You just knew it too late."

There it was. Out of the boy's mouth, plain as a cairn. I looked at him and he looked at the river, giving me the same mercy he always gave me, the kind that sees everything and forgives early, and I understood suddenly that he wasn't asking me to be perfect. He was asking me to be honest. There was a difference, and the difference was the width of a canyon, and I had spent twenty years learning to walk along its rim.

"No," I said. "I knew in time. I just hoped I was wrong." I tossed the stone out into the dark, toward the water, and heard it click somewhere below. "Hoping you're wrong is the most expensive habit in canyon country. Remember that. It'll matter more than the foam ever will."

He nodded slowly. Then he frowned, the way he did when his quick ledger found an entry that didn't balance.

"Elodie—the foam today. Before the flood. It was green since yesterday morning. I saw it at the water point. You saw it too." His voice was careful, curious, not accusing—he didn't yet own the equipment for accusing, thank God. "So if green foam means the river's already being squeezed upstream... what was squeezing it? There wasn't a cloud within a hundred miles. Ferro checked."

I opened my mouth to give him the standard answer—seep storms, distant rain in the high country, the thousand ordinary explanations—and found, to my considerable irritation, that I didn't fully believe any of them. The flood had come down a canyon that had no storm above it. The river had risen angry out of a clear sky, as if something upstream had released it. As if a gate had opened. As if somebody, somewhere above us in the narrowing stone, had moved a lot of water very suddenly, for reasons of their own.

"I don't know," I said.

It was the second honest thing I'd given him that night, and judging by his face, the more expensive one.

He looked upstream, into the dark where the canyon bent away toward country none of our maps admitted existed, toward the Concession line and everything beyond it. The river ran on below us, carrying its green foam and its secrets toward the sea, patient as a ledger.

"Then maybe," Isandro said quietly, "we should go find out."
