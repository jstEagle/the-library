## 10. Old Tracks

The river had opinions about mornings, and this morning it was sulking.

Isandro crouched at the water's edge where the Anselm bent wide and slow around a shelf of red stone, and he watched the current carry a dead branch downstream with the patient, grudging pace of something that would rather be going backward. Three days into the canyon country and he'd learned to read the river the way Elodie read people: by what it refused to say. Green foam meant flood upstream. A sudden coolness in the air meant the water had just come off snow. And a river that ran too quiet for too long, Elodie said, was either resting or lying.

He dipped two fingers in, tasted. Silt and iron and something faintly sweet, like the water had passed through an orchard nobody had planted.

"You're supposed to be checking the footing on the north bank," said Ferro, materializing beside him the way Ferro did—not walking so much as arriving, as if he'd always been there and Isandro had only just noticed.

"I am checking the footing."

"On the north bank."

"This is the north bank."

Ferro looked at the river, then at Isandro, then back at the river, with the expression of a man doing long division and not liking the remainder. "The north bank is over there." He pointed across the water. "You're on the south bank. The south bank is fine. I checked it yesterday while you were asleep."

"Then why'd you send me?"

"To see if you'd argue." Ferro scratched under his hat brim. "You argued. Good. Means you're paying attention." He turned to go, then stopped, because Ferro never left a place without first deciding whether he wanted to leave it. "Watch the ground today. Not the river. The ground."

"Why?"

"Because the ground remembers things the river wants everybody to forget."

And he was gone, upstream along the bank, moving with that low-shouldered economy that made Isandro feel, as always, like a colt trying to walk like a mule.

* * *

The survey party moved at the pace of its slowest porter, which was a boy named Cusi who carried the theodolite case and complained about it in three languages, all of them fluently. Dreele rode at the front of the column like a nail driven into the day's forehead, and Pell scurried between the porters taking notes nobody asked for, and the crew of no names drifted along the flanks doing the actual work of keeping twelve strangers alive in country that killed careless people as a hobby.

Isandro walked sweep, at the back, which was where apprentices walked and where he pretended he didn't mind. It gave him time to think, and thinking was dangerous out here, because thinking led to questions, and questions were the one cargo the crew had never let him carry.

He thought about the ledger word anyway. He couldn't help it. The gunman at the Narrows had said *she wants the ledger*, and since then Elodie had gone quiet in a way that wasn't her quiet—her usual silence was full, like a held breath before a joke—but this new one was empty, a room with the furniture taken out. He'd seen her look at Tamsin twice yesterday, and both times Tamsin had looked away first, and neither of them had said a word, and the nothing they said had been louder than anything Ovid's demolitions could manage.

So when his boot struck metal, he stopped.

Not a clank—metal doesn't clank under sand, it *thuds*, a dull private sound, like knocking on a door in an empty house. He stood still. The column moved on ahead of him, Dreele's voice carrying thin and irritated: *"Mark the bearing, mark the bearing, we are not here to admire geology—"*

Isandro knelt.

He brushed the sand away with the side of his hand, gently, the way Tamsin had taught him to clear a wound—no hurry, no digging, let the thing underneath decide how much of itself to show. First came a curve of rusted iron, pitted orange-brown, wider than his palm. Then more of it. It ran in a straight line into the bank and out again on the other side, parallel to another line just like it, half a stride apart.

Two rails.

He knew rails. Every kid raised anywhere near a haulage town knew rails—the ore carts in the foothill camps, the tramways that crawled up canyon walls like iron centipedes. But there was no mine here. There was nothing here. That was the whole point of this stretch of the Anselm: the maps showed blank space, the permits showed blank space, and blank space was the crew's favorite kind of country because blank space didn't ask questions.

Except the ground remembered.

He followed the rail line. It emerged from the sand, dove under, surfaced again twenty yards on, always running the same direction—upstream, deeper into the canyon, toward the Concession line they weren't supposed to reach and were being paid to pretend they weren't heading for. Here and there the ties survived beneath the rail, black wood gone hard as horn, and once he found a spike, and turned it over in his fingers, and felt the weight of it like a small accusation.

Someone had built a road here. A real road, with real iron, hauled in over distances that must have cost a fortune. You didn't spend a fortune on blank space.

"Isandro."

Tamsin's voice, behind him. He hadn't heard her come up—nobody ever did; she moved like mercy, quietly and only when needed. She crouched beside him, and for a moment she just looked at the rail, and her face did something he had never seen it do.

Nothing. Absolutely nothing. Tamsin's face was usually a weather map—every feeling front clearly marked. Now it was flat gray sky.

"Where are we?" he asked.

"The Anselm."

"Whose road is this?"

She picked up the spike from his palm. Turned it over once. Set it down exactly where it had lain, aligned the way it had lain, which told him more than any answer would have. "It's old."

"How old?"

"Old enough that it doesn't matter."

"It matters if someone built it and everyone pretends they didn't."

Tamsin looked at him then, and her eyes were kind and closed at the same time, a door with light behind it and the latch thrown. "Isandro. Some questions are like splinters. You can dig for them and make it worse, or you can let the body close around them. This one, you let close."

"But—"

"Elodie will tell you what you need to know," she said, standing, brushing sand from her knees, "when you need to know it. Until then, your job is water, footing, and the moods of rivers. Leave the past to people who've already paid for it."

She walked on. She didn't tell him to drop it. She didn't tell him to forget the rail. Both of those instructions would have been lies, and Tamsin didn't lie to him, which was somehow worse than if she had.

Isandro stayed kneeling a moment longer. Then he took the spike after all—he didn't know why, except that the ground had offered it and leaving it felt like agreeing to something—and put it in his pocket, and caught up with the column.

* * *

They made camp that night in a hollow above the river where the wind couldn't find them, and the crew cooked apart from the survey party, as they always did, two fires like two countries. Bex doled out stew with the exactitude of a woman who weighed everything, including grief. Ovid was cleaning the grit out of his fuse spools and humming something tuneless. Ferro was nowhere, which meant Ferro was everywhere.

Isandro sat down next to Tamsin with his bowl and worked up to it the way you work up to jumping cold water.

"Tamsin. What happened at the Concession?"

Ovid's humming stopped. Bex's ladle paused mid-pour, one extra second, then resumed. Nobody looked up. That was the thing about the crew—they could have an entire conversation without speaking, and this one went: *don't, not now, not here,* and also, unmistakably, *he asked.*

Tamsin ate her stew. When she finally spoke, her voice was level and warm and completely unyielding, a blanket laid over a fire. "Twenty years ago, some people died in a bad way, in a place we don't go. Men who helped make it look small are ashamed, and men who made it look legal are rich, and none of it is yours to carry."

"Whose men?"

"Isandro."

"One name. Just give me one name. If it's nothing, one name costs nothing."

Bex set down the ladle. "Boy," she said—not unkindly, Bex was never unkind, she was simply built like arithmetic and couldn't help the shape of her sentences—"in this crew you get paid in water and silence. You've been drawing full wages for four years. Don't spend your silence all in one place."

Silence settled over the fire like ash. Ovid went back to his fuses. And Isandro sat with his cooling bowl and understood, suddenly and completely, that he had just done the one thing the code forbade: he'd asked where someone came from. Not with words—with a rail and a spike and a question shaped like a name.

But here was what the crew hadn't counted on, what maybe they'd never had to count on before: he wasn't asking about *them*. He was asking about the dead. And the dead, as far as Isandro could tell, had never signed anybody's contract.

* * *

He found Elodie at the edge of camp, sitting on a rock above the water with her boots off, which she only did when she was thinking hard enough to forget her feet existed. He sat down at a respectful distance. She didn't tell him to go away, which from Elodie was practically an invitation.

For a while they watched the river. It ran black under the stars, and somewhere far upstream it was doing whatever it was doing, and it would tell them about it later or it wouldn't.

"I found rail today," Isandro said. "Old cart rail, under the sand. Running upstream."

Elodie nodded slowly, as though he'd reported the weather. "Concession road. Hauled ore out, hauled men in. Twenty years ago."

"So it's true. The massacre."

"It's true that people died." Her voice was careful, each word set down like a foot on loose scree. "It's true that the company called it an accident, and the government believed them, or was paid to. It's true that this crew doesn't go there, and now you know one reason why."

"What's the other reason?"

Elodie was quiet long enough that he thought she wouldn't answer. Then she said, "Ask me again when we're through this job. Ask me anything, and I'll answer. That's a promise, Isandro, and I keep my promises even the ugly ones. But tonight I need you to carry the question instead of the answer. Can you do that?"

He thought about it honestly, because she deserved honesty. "A question's heavier than an answer."

"Yes," Elodie said. "That's how you know it's worth something." She reached over and squeezed the back of his neck, brief and rough, the way she did. "Now go sleep. Tomorrow we cross the shingle flats, and the river gets meaner from here."

He went. But at the mouth of his bedroll he stopped, and took the spike out of his pocket, and looked at it in the starlight—a stub of rusted iron, worthless, worthless except that it had come out of the ground everyone agreed was empty.

He wrapped it in a rag and tucked it deep in his pack, next to the things he actually owned, which were few.

The ground remembered. Well, so did he.

And tomorrow, he decided, watching the dark line of the canyon walls against the stars, tomorrow he was going to start remembering right along with it—because somewhere upstream, under all that beautiful lying blankness, there was a road that led to the truth, and for the first time in his life, Isandro intended to be the one holding the map.

He didn't yet know that the map was already drawn—that it slept in Dreele's saddlebag, inked with symbols for cairns and graves and a burned homestead, waiting for Ovid's quick fingers and a lamp and one very bad night.

He only knew the spike was heavy, and questions were heavier, and he was keeping both.
