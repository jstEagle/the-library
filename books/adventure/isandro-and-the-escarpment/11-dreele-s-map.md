## 11. Dreele's Map

Ovid had three rules about other people's belongings. The first was that everything could be opened if you were patient enough. The second was that you should never open anything you weren't prepared to understand. The third — learned at considerable cost to a younger version of himself who still had all his original eyebrows — was that some things are locked for a reason, and the reason is usually flammable.

He was thinking about rule three as he knelt in the dark beside Dreele's expedition trunk, working a wax seal loose with a sliver of bone and more tenderness than he'd ever shown a living creature.

"Tell me again why we're doing this," Ferro whispered from the tent flap, where he was doing his best impression of a man who belonged there: arms crossed, weight on one hip, eyes moving in slow sweeps across the camp. Ferro's impressions were always excellent and always wasted, because nobody in the company was watching him. That was rather the point of Ferro.

"We're not doing anything," Ovid murmured. "I am conducting an inventory of safety hazards. There is a difference, legally speaking."

"There is no legal anything out here."

"All the more reason my inventory be thorough." The seal gave way with a sound like a small sigh. "Besides. You counted their weapons. I count their paper. Between us we'll know exactly what kind of trouble we've been hired into, and knowing is half of surviving. The other half is me being very good with explosives, which I already was before we started."

Ferro grunted, which from Ferro was a full paragraph of agreement.

The trunk smelled of lamp oil and cedar and something sharper underneath — ink, fresh ink, the kind that came from a proper press and not a field pen. Ovid lifted out folded canvas groundsheets, a tin of survey instruments wrapped in wool, a leather document case stamped with a company sigil he didn't recognize: a circle bisected by a line, like a sun cut in half. Underneath the case, wrapped in oilcloth, lay maps.

Three of them. He took only one — the middle one, because a man who takes the middle map leaves a stack that still looks like a stack — and pressed it flat against his chest as he backed out of the tent.

* * *

Back at the crew's fire, forty paces downwind and around a bend of rock so the light wouldn't carry, Ovid spread the map across his knees and let Tamsin hold the lantern low.

It was a beautiful map. That was the first wrong thing about it.

Ovid had spent twenty years reading terrain the way other men read letters, and he knew what a surveyor's chart was supposed to look like: contour lines like fingerprints, elevation figures marching up the margins, geology hatched in careful codes, water marked in blue so faint it looked apologetic. This map had all of that, done by a competent hand — Pell's hand, he'd have wagered, because the lettering had the nervous precision of a man who checked his work twice and then apologized to it.

But over Pell's clean cartography, someone else had made additions. A second hand, heavier, in red-brown ink that had gone on wet and dried unevenly, like old blood soaked into cloth.

There were marks along the river route. Not geological marks. Ovid knew every conventional symbol a surveyor used, and these weren't any of them.

Small stacked triangles — four, five, six of them, clustered at intervals upstream. He turned the map sideways. Upside down. They didn't resolve into anything technical.

"Cairns," Tamsin said quietly.

Ovid looked up. She wasn't looking at the map. She was looking at the middle distance, at something twenty years away.

"Stone cairns," she said. "That's how they mark graves out here when there's no wood for crosses and no time for digging deep. Stacked stones. I've seen them on the high passes. Travelers' cairns, sometimes. But travelers' cairns don't come in clusters of six."

Bex leaned in from the other side of the fire, and her shadow fell across the paper. Bex did not lean in for things. Bex was a woman who believed that personal space was a resource to be managed like any other, and she spent hers carefully. If she was leaning, the map had earned it.

"Let me see the margin," she said.

Ovid tilted the lantern. In the lower corner, in that same heavy second hand, someone had written a column of numbers. Not coordinates — Bex would know coordinates the way she knew the backs of her own ledgers, and she shook her head slowly. These were counts. Little tally groups, five-barred gates of them, next to each cluster of triangles.

Six. Four. Eleven. Nine.

"Those aren't elevations," Bex said. Her voice had gone flat, which was how Bex sounded when she was frightened and had decided nobody would be permitted to notice. "Those are bodies. Someone counted."

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the dark, one of the porters coughed in his sleep, and everyone's shoulders came down half an inch when it turned out to be only that.

Ovid kept turning the map. Two miles further upstream, the second hand had drawn a small square with a jagged roofline and filled it in solid black. Beside it, in cramped script: *H — burned, nothing recoverable.* And past that, near where the river forked toward the high country, a single symbol that stopped Ovid's breath in his chest.

A rectangle. Long and narrow. With small crosses inside it, too many to count at lantern-light resolution, drawn so densely they'd merged into a hatch pattern.

"That's a grave symbol," Tamsin said. "Mass grave. That's how it's marked on casualty charts. Military ones." She said it the way you'd report weather. Tamsin always reported terrible things like weather; it was her way of keeping her hands steady while she worked. "I've seen it on field reports from the border wars. Nobody uses it for geology. There is no geological formation on earth shaped like a trench full of people."

Nobody said anything for a while. Isandro was asleep ten feet back, curled in his blanket with his boots on, the way he always slept, as if the desert might ask him to leave at any moment and he intended to be ready. Elodie was up the slope somewhere, taking first watch. Ovid found himself glad, in a distant and guilty way, that neither of them was looking at the map just now. Some knowledge went down easier in small doses.

"So," Ferro said at last, settling cross-legged at the edge of the light. He'd come back from his watch of nothing and now watched the map instead, which was considerably more alarming. "Our employer's man has a survey map with graves on it. Marked, counted, catalogued. Twenty years before our permits were even drafted." He tapped the blackened square. "And a burned homestead noted as *nothing recoverable.* Who writes *nothing recoverable* unless they're checking?"

"An arsonist keeps receipts," Ovid said. It came out before he could stop it, and it came out true, and true things have a way of filling a silence completely.

Tamsin looked at him. "Say that plainer."

"I build charges," Ovid said. "Demolition is my trade, and demolition is mostly paperwork wearing a loud coat. Before you bring a structure down, you survey it. You document what's there. You note what can be salvaged and what must go. *Nothing recoverable* is a phrase from a destruction order, not a survey. Somebody wrote that after the burning, not before it. Somebody walked through the ash and made a list of what was no longer there."

"And the cairns?" Bex asked. "The counts?"

"Somebody counted the dead." Ovid ran his thumb along the tally marks, careful not to smudge them. He felt suddenly that he owed the little inked numbers gentleness, which was absurd, they were ink, but there it was. "You count what you intend to account for. Or—" and here his voice did something he didn't authorize, dropping quieter, "—you count what you intend to make sure no one else ever finds."

Ferro nodded slowly. "Dreele's got us walking a route that visits every place the dead are buried. Cairn by cairn. Homestead by homestead. All the way up to—" he touched the dense-hatched rectangle, the mass grave symbol, and pulled his finger back quickly, as if the ink were warm, "—that."

"The survey isn't a survey," Tamsin said.

"It's a search," Ovid agreed. "Or it's an erasure. And I genuinely cannot tell you which frightens me more, because both of them mean the same thing: somebody wants to know exactly where the bodies are before the permits lapse. Either to find them or to make certain no one ever does."

* * *

They sat with it. That was the thing about the crew that outsiders never understood, the thing that made them seem slow when they were actually being careful: they sat with bad news the way you sit with a wounded animal, letting it calm down before anyone tried to move it.

It was Bex who finally spoke, and she spoke practically, because Bex's griefs always arrived dressed as arithmetic.

"Ninety days on those permits. We're eleven days in. Whatever Dreele means to do at those sites, he means to do it inside the window, and then the land reverts and whatever happened out here becomes crown property and crown problem." She paused. "Or it becomes nothing at all, if the evidence goes into the river first."

"You think they'd destroy the sites?" Ferro asked.

"I think," Bex said, "that I have read a great many contracts in my life, and the companies that write them do not pay for surveys of places they intend to leave untouched. You survey what you're going to change. Everything else is scenery."

Ovid looked down at the map again, at the neat nervous contours of Pell's honest work underneath the heavy red-brown hand of whoever had annotated it. Two maps in one. A lie laid over the truth, or the truth laid over a lie — he honestly couldn't decide which direction it ran, and that indecision sat in his stomach like a swallowed stone.

There was one more thing. He almost didn't say it. Then he thought of Isandro asleep with his boots on, and said it anyway.

"There's a watermark." He held the map's corner up to the lantern, angling it so the light passed through the paper. Faint, ghostly, pressed into the sheet at the mill: the same sigil from Dreele's document case. The circle cut in half. "This stock comes from the same printer as the concession permits Ovid examined— that I examined," he corrected himself, because referring to yourself in the third person was a habit he'd caught from spending too long alone with dynamite. "Same mill. Same run, maybe. Whoever drew these grave marks used company paper. This isn't one man's morbid hobby. It's policy."

"A company that buries people," Tamsin said softly, "and then surveys the burying."

"And files it," Bex added.

"And prints it on nice paper," Ferro finished. "With a watermark. So everyone downstream knows whose work it is." He shook his head. "That's not hiding. That's ownership."

The lantern flame bent in a gust off the canyon, righted itself. Up on the slope, Elodie's silhouette crossed the skyline, patient as a heron, and moved on. Ovid watched her go and felt the map under his hands grow heavier by the minute, though paper weighs nothing. Paper never weighs anything. It's what's written on it that breaks your back.

"We give this to Elodie," Tamsin said. It wasn't a question, but Ovid answered it anyway.

"At first light. She's got first watch and a head full of Vane, and a woman should never have to read a mass grave chart by herself at midnight." He began folding the map along its original creases, matching them precisely, because a copied map that returned wrinkled was a copied map that announced itself. "Besides, I want to put it back in the trunk exactly as I found it. Rule of the trade: leave no trace behind. We taught Isandro that. Seems only fair I practice it."

"You're stealing information and returning the theft," Ferro observed. "That's very tidy."

"Tidiness is all civilization is, Ferro. The rest is shouting."

But his hands weren't as steady as his mouth. Because underneath the horror of it — the counted cairns, the burned homestead filed as *nothing recoverable*, the trench-symbol with its hundred small crosses — underneath all of that was a smaller, colder thought that he hadn't yet said aloud, and that he suspected he'd be carrying upriver like a stone in his boot:

If the company had surveyed its own massacre twenty years ago, in its own ink, on its own paper —

Then somewhere in Dreele's document case, or in whatever office had sent Dreele, there might be a record of who had done the counting.

And Ovid had been in this crew long enough, had helped bury enough of its secrets, to know that the founding members had carried paperwork out of the Concession once before. Paperwork that made it look legal.

He smoothed the last crease flat and wondered, with a quiet horror that had nothing to do with corpses and everything to do with family, whose handwriting he'd recognize if he saw it.

The lantern guttered. Downriver, in the company camp, Dreele's tent stood dark and square against the stars, and Ovid thought: *he checks that trunk every morning at dawn.*

Which meant they had until dawn to decide what Elodie Yarrow did with a map that proved the job they'd taken was a search for the dead —

—and that morning, when Ovid slipped the map home beneath the oilcloth and resealed the wax with a heated knife-blade and a prayer, he heard footsteps outside the tent flap that stopped, considered, and moved on.

Someone else, it seemed, had been counting too.
