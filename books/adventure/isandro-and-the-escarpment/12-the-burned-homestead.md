## 12. The Burned Homestead

The ruin announced itself a half-mile before we reached it, the way ruins do—by smell. Char and old ash, but underneath that something sweeter and sadder, like a house that had been trying to become a garden when it died. Ferro called a halt at the top of the bench and pointed with his chin, which was how he said most things.

"Homestead," he said. "Burned."

"I can see that," Dreele said, riding up beside him with his notebook already out. "Mark it. Grid reference forty-one."

Ferro looked at me. I looked at my boots. This was becoming a habit between us, this silent conversation about Dreele, and I wasn't sure either of us liked where it was going.

We came down off the bench in single file, and the homestead opened up around us like a wound that had learned to be scenery. The walls still stood on two sides, black stone gone silver at the edges where twenty years of wind had been sanding the grief off it. A chimney stood alone in what had been the middle of everything, tall and stubborn, wearing a crown of yellow wildflowers that some seed had decided to plant up there where nobody could stop it.

I'd seen burned places before. Out on the flats you found the occasional line shack that had lost an argument with a stove, and once a whole waystation that Ovid swore he hadn't blown up, which from Ovid was practically a legal affidavit. But those were accidents. This was different. This fire had been thorough. It had gone through the place like a debt collector, taking everything of value and leaving only what couldn't be carried away or argued with.

"There was a family here," Tamsin said quietly. She'd dismounted and was standing very still, the way she did before she worked on someone. "A big one. Look—the corral's doubled. Two gardens." She nodded at two rectangles of richer soil, gone to scrub now, side by side behind the house. "Two women kept gardens. Or a woman and a mother-in-law," she added, and Bex snorted despite herself.

Dreele walked the site with his notebook, making his little marks, saying nothing. Pell trailed after him carrying the survey instruments, and every few steps Pell would look at something—a door hinge fused to nothing, a child's boot sole half-sunk in the ash—and then look away fast, like a man who kept touching a hot pan to check if it was still hot.

Elodie set us to work the way she always did, which was to say without appearing to give orders at all. "Camp in the lee of the north wall. Water the stock from the barrels if any held rain. Isandro—walk the perimeter with me."

Walking the perimeter with Elodie meant walking slowly while she touched things. She crouched at the foundation stones and rubbed her thumb across them. She picked up a scrap of window glass gone cloudy as sea ice and held it up to the light. She didn't say anything for a long time, and I've learned that when Elodie goes quiet, the quiet is doing work.

"You knew there was a homestead here," I said finally.

"I knew there were people here." She set the glass down exactly where she'd found it. "The Concession wasn't empty when they drew the lines around it. Maps make land look unpeopled. That's half of what maps are *for*."

"And the other half?"

"Making the people look far away." She stood, brushed off her knees, and gave me a look that was almost her normal look, except for a hairline crack running through it. "Stay close to Pell today. He's about to come apart at the seams, and seams tell you more than faces do."

"Pell? He's scared of his own shadow."

"Good," Elodie said. "Scared men talk. Confident men issue reports."

* * *

Camp went up in the shadow of the standing wall, and supper was beans again, because Bex believed with a religious fervor that beans built character, and because our stores had entered the phase of the journey where everything tasted like the journey. Ovid did his nightly trick of making the fire burn green by feeding it a pinch of copper filings, purely so he could watch the porters' faces. Ferro sat apart sharpening a knife that was already sharp, which was how Ferro prayed.

Pell didn't eat. That was the first thing I noticed. He sat with his tin plate balanced on his knees, full and cooling, watching the burned garden rectangles go purple in the dusk. I'd been told to stay close to him, and staying close turned out to be easy, because he was sitting right next to me and radiating misery like a stove.

"The flowers on the chimney," I said, because it seemed like the kind of thing you could say to a man who wasn't eating. "Somebody should've planted those on purpose. They got up there all on their own."

Pell blinked at me. Then, unexpectedly, he smiled—a small, cracked thing, but real. "My grandmother would have loved that. She said weeds were just flowers that refused to fill out paperwork." He looked down at his plate. "She'd have hated this job."

"Yeah?" I kept my voice light, the way you keep your hand light on a spooked horse. "Why's that?"

He was quiet long enough that I thought he wouldn't answer. Around us the camp sounds carried on—Bex tallying stores under her breath, a porter laughing at something, the horses shifting—and inside that little bubble of quiet around Pell, something was bending.

"Can I ask you something?" he said. "And you won't repeat it?"

"I'm not great at repeating things," I said. "Bex says I have a memory like a sieve and a face like an open book, which she seems to think cancel each other out."

That got the smile back for half a second. Then he leaned toward me, and his voice dropped to almost nothing, and the last light was off the wall behind him so I couldn't see his face well, only hear how careful each word was, like a man crossing ice.

"Why do you think the company sent twelve porters and two engineers and a hundredweight of instruments," he said, "to survey a route they already surveyed four years ago?"

I didn't say anything. In my experience, silence pulls harder than questions.

"Because it isn't a survey." His hands had started moving, wringing each other. "There's no route to find. The maps are done. The instruments are—" he laughed, a horrible small sound, "—the instruments are theater. We're carrying a hundred pounds of theater up a river. You know what our actual orders are? The real ones, the ones Dreele keeps in the oilskin packet instead of the ledger everyone gets shown?"

"No," I said honestly.

"*Document nothing.*" He said it flat, like reading off a form. "That's the phrase. Verbatim. Every site of interest—we document nothing. No measurements recorded, no features named, no structures noted. And where there's anything left standing—" his eyes flicked, involuntary, to the black walls above us, to the chimney with its defiant flowers, "—anything that might be documented later, by someone else, after the permits lapse... we make sure there's nothing left to see."

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the dark a nightjar called its three-note question and got no answer.

"Erase it," Pell whispered. "That's the job. We're not surveyors. We're the broom that comes through before the guests arrive. There's a schedule, Isandro. Sites flagged on Dreele's private map. This homestead is third on the list. There's a cairn field further up that's—" He stopped himself, jaw working. "There's a reason the permits expire in ninety days. After that, the land reverts to the crown, and anyone can walk in here with honest eyes. So before that happens, everything honest eyes might find has to be *gone*. Ash raked. Stones scattered. Rail pulled up and melted. They've been doing it for months with contract crews. We're the finish work."

I sat with that. I want to tell you I understood it immediately, all at once, the whole shape of it—but the truth is it went into me slowly, the way cold water goes into boots, seeping through seams you didn't know you had.

My crew had a code. Elodie taught it to me my first week, sitting on a salt flat with a stolen water skin between us: *take nothing you can't carry, leave no name behind.* I'd thought, all this time, that the second part was about us. About staying invisible, staying safe, staying free. It had never once occurred to me that other people might be doing the erasing too—that somewhere there were men with ledgers and oilskin packets whose entire profession was leaving *no name behind* for the dead. That erasure could be a product. Something you could sell.

"We signed on to draw lines on paper," Pell said. "Water levels. Grade slopes. I'm a junior engineer, Isandro. I wanted to build a bridge someday. An actual bridge, with my name on the plaque." He stared at his untouched beans. "Instead I'm going to help scatter the stones off graves so a company can own a river clean."

"How long have you known?"

"Since the hiring hall." He finally looked at me straight, and his eyes were wet and furious in the firelight. "I told myself it was legal. I told myself permits are permits, and companies do what companies do, and I have a mother with bad lungs who needs the wage. I've been telling myself things since the hiring hall." He wiped his face with the heel of his hand, rough, angry at himself. "Then today I saw the boot. The little boot. And I'm done telling myself things."

Around us, the camp was settling. Tamsin moved among the porters with her willow-bark tea. Dreele's tent glowed faintly, lamplight through canvas, and I caught myself staring at it—at the oilskin packet that was supposedly sleeping inside—with a feeling I didn't have a word for yet. It wasn't fear exactly. Fear is about what might happen to you. This was about what had already happened, years ago, to people who'd planted two gardens.

"What happens if you refuse?" I asked.

Pell huffed. "What happens to anyone who refuses? You get replaced by someone who doesn't. And the work gets done anyway, only now it's done by someone worse." He shook his head. "That's the trap. That's always the trap. They don't need good men. They need *present* men."

"So why tell me?"

He was quiet for a moment. The green firelight made his face look underwater.

"Because you're the only person on this expedition who looks at the ground like it matters," he said. "The rest of them look at it like it's in the way. Even your captain—she reads it, but she reads it like a road. You look at a boot sole in the ash like it's a person." He shrugged, miserable. "I needed to say it out loud to somebody, or I was going to start doing what Dreele says without even hating it anymore. That's how it gets you. Not all at once. You just... stop hating it."

I looked over at Elodie. She was sitting at the fire's edge, turning her cup slowly, watching the flames with an expression I'd never quite been able to read and suddenly understood completely: she already knew. Of course she knew. She'd probably known before we ever signed the contract—that's why she'd insisted on the clause, armed and answerable to no surveyor. She'd hired onto a job she suspected was a crime, walking in with her eyes open, and she hadn't told me. Hadn't told any of us.

Or maybe she didn't know. Maybe she only feared it. I genuinely couldn't tell, and that was its own small vertigo—not knowing whether the person you trusted most was keeping a secret from you or just carrying one alone.

"Isandro." Pell's voice again, urgent, low. "The cairn field upstream. When we reach it—you'll know it when you see it—it's on the list too. Top of the list. Whatever your crew decides to do about that..." He swallowed. "Decide fast. Dreele's been pushing the pace all week. He wants the finish work done before the storm season closes the river. Before anyone else can come look."

Across the fire, Dreele emerged from his tent, checked the sky with a professional squint, and made a note in his book by lamplight. Then he looked over at our corner—just a glance, casual, a man checking on his instruments—and Pell went smooth and blank as poured concrete, and I did my best impression of a bored apprentice picking at his beans.

Dreele smiled at us. It was a perfectly pleasant smile. It was the worst thing I'd seen all day, and I'd spent the day looking at a burned house.

"Early start tomorrow," he called out to the camp, cheerful as a schoolmaster. "Site forty-two is only six miles up. Let's make good time, gentlemen. The river won't wait, and neither will we."

Nobody answered. The fire crackled. On the chimney above us, the wildflowers held their little yellow heads up in the dark, blooming their hearts out on a grave nobody was supposed to remember, and I lay back on my bedroll and stared at them until sleep took me, thinking: *six miles.*

Six miles to whatever site forty-two was. Six miles to find out what my crew was made of—and what I was.

I didn't know it yet, but the sky over the high country had already begun to stack its clouds, and the river, far upstream, had begun to gather its answer.
