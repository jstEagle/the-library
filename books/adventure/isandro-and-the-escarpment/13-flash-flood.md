## 13. Flash Flood

The morning the river killed two men, it began by lying to them.

Elodie knew that now. She had known it for years, in the way you know a thing without weighing it—green foam on the water, sweet and harmless-looking, like soap from a washerwoman's tub. She'd pointed it out to Isandro only three days before, crouched at the bank with her fingers in the current, and he'd repeated it back to her like scripture: *Green foam means rain upstream. Green foam means get high.*

She had seen the foam at dawn. She had noted it, filed it, and then she had looked at the sky over the canyon rim—blue as a fresh-painted door—and decided they had time.

That was the mistake. Not the foam itself; the foam was honest. The mistake was believing the canyon shared her schedule.

* * *

They broke camp late because Dreele insisted on re-counting the survey instruments, twice, in the manner of a man who trusted nothing that breathed and several things that didn't. Pell hovered around him apologizing to everyone on his behalf, which was becoming its own full-time position.

"An hour," Bex muttered, cinching a pack strap hard enough to make the mule flinch. "He cost us an hour arguing about a theodolite."

"Theodolites are expensive," Ovid offered.

"So are mules, and I've met both, and only one of them has ever apologized."

Elodie let them grumble. Grumbling was ballast; it kept a crew steady in rough water. She was doing her own arithmetic instead—the kind she did without letting her face move. Twelve porters, four crew, two clients, fourteen mules. The canyon narrowing ahead into the slot section, walls close enough that Ferro said you could shake hands across it if you were friendly and long-armed. Water running higher than yesterday by the width of two fingers.

Two fingers wasn't much. Two fingers was usually nothing.

"Captain." Ferro dropped back beside her, walking soft the way he always did, as though the ground might report him. "Foam's thickening."

"I saw it."

"And?"

"And the sky's clear."

Ferro looked up at the blue, then back at her, and said nothing, which from Ferro was an entire argument delivered in full.

"Storms don't come out of blue skies," Elodie said.

"No," Ferro agreed. "They come out of other skies. Ones we can't see from down here." He touched two fingers to his hat brim and drifted forward again, leaving the sentence sitting between them like a stone neither wanted to pick up.

She should have picked it up. She knew that too, now.

* * *

The slot canyon took them just past midday. It was beautiful, in the way the Escarpment was always beautiful right before it tested you—walls of red sandstone rising sheer on either side, striped with twenty centuries of flood lines laid down like the rings of a tree, so that the rock itself kept a record of every wall of water that had ever come through here and erased everything standing in its path. Isandro walked with his head tipped back, reading those bands.

"How high?" he asked.

"Higher than you want to think about."

"But how high?"

"Fifty feet. Sixty, in the bad ones." She nodded at a tangle of dead brush wedged in a crack thirty feet up, pale and sun-bleached. "See that? That grew on the bank once."

Isandro went quiet, which was his way of filing things. He'd been quieter all week anyway, ever since the burned homestead, ever since Pell's confession. He carried things now that Elodie couldn't take back off him, and she felt each one of them like a splinter working deeper.

Ahead, the porters strung out along the gravel bar in their usual loose order, Jemmo singing something low and tuneless about a girl in Corrow who had wronged him in ways he refused to specify, and Hadduk telling him to shut up, and both of them laughing. The mules plodded. The water ran green-flecked and quick.

It was Tamsin who heard it first.

Later, everyone would claim they'd heard it first. But Elodie saw Tamsin stop, mid-stride, and turn her head toward the upstream bend with the particular stillness of a woman who had spent twenty years listening to bodies tell her the truth they wouldn't say aloud.

"What is that?" Pell asked. "Wind?"

"That's not wind," Tamsin said.

It came around the bend as sound before it came as water—a deep, grinding roar, like the canyon clearing its throat, and above it a smell, wet clay and torn vegetation, the smell of a country being dismantled somewhere behind them and shipped downstream.

Elodie's body understood before her mind finished the arithmetic. "HIGH GROUND!" she screamed. "EVERYONE OUT OF THE CUT—MOVE!"

* * *

What happened next happened in pieces, and afterward each member of the party would keep their own piece forever, the way survivors do, trading fragments like people salvaging cargo from a wreck.

Ovid's piece: he grabbed the nearest porter—old Sef, half his weight in biscuit and gristle—and threw him bodily up a ledge route no wider than a goat's opinion, shouting the whole time, not words so much as propulsion.

Bex's piece: she cut the mules loose rather than waste seconds fighting them uphill, slapping rumps and screaming, and watched six of them bolt downstream along the bar, which was exactly the wrong direction, and hated herself for the arithmetic even as she did it.

Ferro's piece: he got Pell up the wall by the collar and the belt, the junior engineer climbing like a sack of wet flour, and held him there against the stone while the world below turned brown.

Tamsin's piece: she counted. That was Tamsin's gift and her curse; she counted heads the way other people counted money, compulsively, and she stood on her ledge with her lips moving—*nine, ten, eleven*—and then stopped moving them.

Elodie's piece was the worst one, because hers included the part where she saw it happen and could not reach it.

Jemmo and Hadduk had been at the rear of the line, as they always were, because Jemmo sang better when nobody official could hear him. They were forty yards back when the water came around the bend—not a wave, not a wall in the picture-book sense, but a moving hill of brown destruction, twelve feet tall and gaining, carrying whole trees in its teeth like a dog carrying sticks.

They ran. Elodie watched them run, and she watched the water run, and she did the terrible arithmetic that her body had already finished: the water was faster.

"HOLD ON TO SOMETHING!" she bellowed, though there was nothing to hold, nothing within reach, the ledges on that side too far and too high, and Jemmo heard her anyway—he turned his head, and for one absurd half-second he grinned at her, still the singer, still the boy from Corrow with the girl who'd wronged him—and then the water took him the way a door takes a candle flame.

Hadduk made it further. Hadduk caught a root, a real one, a tamarisk root thick as a wrist growing from a seam in the stone, and he hung on while the flood hit him like a collapsing building, and Elodie screamed at him to hold, hold, hold, and he held—for five seconds, ten, his face above the brown roar wearing an expression she would carry to her grave—and then the root tore loose from the wetting sandstone and the river folded him under and did not give him back.

Then it was over, or the loud part of it was, and the canyon was full of a churning brown sea where a gravel bar had been, and the silence after the roar was worse than the roar, because the roar had at least been honest.

* * *

They climbed down when the water dropped enough, which took hours, the flood receding in shudders and gouts, leaving the canyon scoured and rearranged, a stranger's landscape wearing familiar clothes. The mules—all but one—were gone. Half the gear was gone. The survey instruments Dreele had counted twice were gone, and Dreele stood on the bank cataloguing the loss aloud in terms of currency, his voice thin and strange, a man discovering that his ledger and the world disagreed.

Elodie barely heard him. She was walking the banks.

They found Jemmo two miles downstream, wedged in the root mass of a fallen cottonwood, and Tamsin knelt beside him and did what she could, which was nothing, which was everything there was to do, and then she closed his eyes and sat back on her heels and was quiet in the way that frightened Elodie more than shouting would have.

They never found Hadduk. The river kept him.

By the time they made camp on high ground above the scour line, the light was going copper and the crew moved like sleepwalkers. Nobody built the fire properly until Isandro did it, small and careful, and nobody thanked him, and he didn't need thanking; he was learning that some work is its own shelter.

Dreele approached Elodie at the fire's edge with his hat in his hands, and for one moment she thought—hoped—that whatever came out of him would be human.

"We've lost two days' progress," he said. "And the instruments. The company will need to be apprised of the—"

"Two men died, Dreele."

"Yes. Unfortunate. The schedule nonetheless—"

"Get away from me." Her voice came out flat and quiet, and somehow that was worse than a shout, because Dreele actually stepped back, recalibrating, filing her away under a new heading. Pell pulled him off gently, murmuring, shooting Elodie a look of naked apology that she couldn't afford to receive just then.

* * *

It was Isandro who found her afterward, sitting apart on a flat rock above camp, watching the river run on below, fat and brown and unbothered, already forgetting.

"You should eat," he said.

"In a minute."

He didn't leave. He sat down next to her, cross-legged, the way he did, and for a while they watched the water together.

"I keep going through it," Elodie said finally. She hadn't meant to say it aloud. It came out anyway, the way water comes through a cracked dam. "The signs. Foam at dawn. Water up two fingers. Ferro told me storms come from skies we can't see. I had all of it, Isandro. I had every piece, and I put us in that cut at midday anyway, because the sky was blue and Dreele wanted his hour and I—" Her hands were shaking. She looked at them with something like disgust. "I misjudged by an hour. One hour. Jemmo would be complaining about his boots right now. Hadduk would be asleep and snoring and Bex would be threatening to stuff a sock in it."

Isandro was quiet for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice was careful, the voice of a boy handling something fragile on someone else's behalf.

"Tamsin says the river doesn't take payment for mistakes. It takes payment for being near it. Everyone who runs this country pays sometimes. You taught me that."

"I taught you that," Elodie said bitterly, "and then I forgot my own lesson inside a week of taking company money."

"It's not the same thing."

"It's exactly the same thing. That's what frightens me." She rubbed her face. Above them the first stars were coming out, indifferent and clean. "You know what the worst part is? Every one of them saw it. Bex, Ferro, Tamsin—they saw me hesitate this morning. Twenty years I've been the one who reads the desert right. And today they watched me read it wrong, and two men paid for my reading, and tomorrow morning none of them will look at me quite the same way again. I can feel it already. Like a draft under a door."

"They'll follow you anyway," Isandro said. "That's not the same as looking at you the same way."

Elodie huffed something that was almost a laugh. "When did you get wise?"

"About the same time you got human, I think. Somewhere up this river." He paused. "Jemmo's song. About the girl in Corrow. Somebody should finish it. At the burial. I don't know the words, but somebody does."

She looked at him—at this boy she'd fished starving out of the salt flats, who stole water and learned fast and carried other people's griefs like they were his own pack—and felt something crack and re-set in her chest, not healed, but braced.

"Yeah," she said. "Somebody does."

Down in camp, the firelight showed Bex standing at the edge of the glow, looking up at the rock where Elodie sat, and Elodie realized with a lurch that Bex had been listening—or enough of it—and that the look on the quartermaster's face wasn't doubt.

It was fear. Not of the river. Of the captain doubting herself, which in Bex's private arithmetic was the one catastrophe the books couldn't absorb.

And beyond Bex, at the fire's far side, Ferro had risen silently from his bedroll and was standing motionless, facing downstream, head tilted, listening to the night the way he listened to everything.

"Captain," he called softly. "You'll want to hear this."

Elodie came down the rock. Ferro's eyes were fixed on the dark below the camp, where the river ran, and somewhere beneath the water-sound—faint, rhythmic, wrong—came the unmistakable clink of metal and the murmur of voices.

Someone was following them down the canyon. And they weren't being quiet about it anymore.
