## 14. Burial Without Names

Tamsin had buried more people than she could count, which was precisely the problem.

She knelt in the gravel above the high-water line and let her hands remember the work while her mind went somewhere flat and gray and useful. Dig first. Grieve later, if later ever came. The river had given back two of Dreele's porters at dawn—laid them out on a sandbar like an apology it didn't mean—and the other four were still downstream somewhere, wrapped around boulders or gone all the way to the delta, and there was nothing medicine or mercy could do about that arithmetic.

Two graves. Two boys, really. The older one couldn't have been past twenty-two.

"His name was Sef," said one of the surviving porters, a wiry man called Adem who hadn't stopped shivering since yesterday, though the morning was warm enough. "Sef Oduya. He was from Corrow. He used to sing when he carried, bad songs, off-key on purpose to make the others laugh." He swallowed. "The young one—we called him Little Ferret because he could squeeze through anything. I never knew his true name. He signed on with a thumbprint."

Tamsin looked up at that. "A thumbprint."

"He couldn't write. The company clerk wouldn't read it out for him, just pointed where to press." Adem's jaw worked. "So there's a paper in some office with his mark on it and no name beside it."

*There's a whole canyon full of people like that*, Tamsin thought, and then made herself stop thinking it, because the last thing she needed today was to start digging up twenty-year-old ground along with this fresh gravel.

"Thank you," she said instead. "That's more than most get."

"It's not enough."

"No," she agreed quietly. "It isn't."

* * *

Ranger custom was simple, and like everything simple, it was old for a reason. You buried the dead above the flood line, facing the water they came from or the country they were bound for—you chose based on what you knew of them, and if you knew nothing, you faced them toward home, because everyone had one even if they'd left it badly. You stacked the stones yourself, one by one, so the work cost you something. And you put no marker, cut no name, because a marked grave was a map, and a map was a debt someone would come to collect.

Elodie had taught her that rule eleven years ago, standing over a grave much like this one, and Tamsin had repeated it to apprentices since without ever once asking herself the obvious question until Isandro asked it at supper two winters back, mouth full of beans, in the way of boys:

"But how do you know whose grave it is when you come back?"

And Elodie had said, "You don't," and Bex had said, "That's the point," and Ovid had said something clever about anonymity being load-bearing architecture, and the conversation had moved on the way conversations did in their crew, smoothly, away from edges.

Tamsin stacked stones now and thought: *we are very good at moving away from edges.*

"You're doing the facing-toward-home thing," said Ferro, appearing at her shoulder the way Ferro did, soundless as dust. He crouched, picked up a stone, weighed it, set it on the pile at exactly the place her hands would have chosen next. "For the thumbprint boy."

"For both of them. Adem says Sef came from Corrow, but Corrow's downstream, and you don't send a man back the way the river took him. You send him home the long way, overland, the way he'd have walked if he'd lived."

Ferro considered this. "That's generous."

"That's geography," Tamsin said. "Generosity is when it costs you something. This is just knowing which way west is."

"Mm." Ferro placed another stone. He was quiet a while, which from Ferro meant either nothing or everything; his silences were like his footsteps, identical whether they carried weight or not. Then: "Elodie hasn't slept."

"I know."

"She's down at the waterline. Been there since before light. Just standing, looking upstream."

"I know, Ferro."

"She misjudged the storm by an hour."

"*I know.*" It came out sharper than she intended, and she softened it the only way she knew how, which was with work: she handed him another stone. "And when she comes up here—and she will come up here, because Elodie Yarrow has never once in my acquaintance avoided a bill that had her name on it, metaphorical or otherwise—I don't want anyone on this crew handing her a rope and calling it help. She'll punish herself fine on her own. Our job is to make sure she doesn't do it alone."

Ferro took the stone. "That's a strange kind of medicine."

"It's the only kind that works on captains."

* * *

By midmorning the whole camp had drifted to the graves, the way people do, pretending they had other reasons to be walking in that direction. Bex arrived carrying the good tea—the hoarded stuff, the brick-red Assali leaf they traded for at three days' travel and drank maybe twice a year—and nobody commented on it, because commenting would have made it a smaller thing than it was. Ovid came with his hat off, which Tamsin had seen perhaps four times in nine years, each occasion involving either explosives or grief, and sometimes both.

Isandro came last, and he came wrong—too fast, too eager, straight up to the mounds with his jaw set like a boy about to fix something.

"Shouldn't there be markers?" he said. "Even small ones. Even just—" He gestured at the stones. "How will anyone know?"

"Know what?" Tamsin asked gently.

"That they're here. That they *were* here. That they had names." His voice cracked on the last word and he pushed through it, furious at himself for cracking. "Sef Oduya sang off-key on purpose. Little Ferret could squeeze through anything. That's—that's a whole person, and we're going to cover it with anonymous rocks and walk away, and that's exactly what the company does, isn't it? That's exactly—"

"Isandro." She said his name the way she'd say *hold still* before setting a bone, firm and close. "Sit down."

He sat, glaring at the gravel.

"Do you know why we leave no markers?" she asked.

"Because maps are debts. Because marked graves bring collectors." He recited it like scripture, because it was; they'd all learned it as scripture.

"And do you believe it?"

He opened his mouth. Closed it. Looked at the two mounds, at Adem standing behind them with his hat crushed in both hands, at the river running brown and swollen and unrepentant below.

"I don't know," he said finally, and it cost him visibly. "I used to believe everything you all told me. Lately I keep finding cracks."

Tamsin sat down beside him in the dirt, which her knees protested and she ignored. Around them the crew waited, and the porters waited, and even Dreele's remaining men hung back at the edge of things with the uncomfortable look of men attending a service in a language they didn't speak.

"Here's the truth, then, since you're collecting cracks," she said. "The custom started honest. Bad men hunted rangers; marked graves got robbed, or worse, got *found*, and found graves led to found camps. So we went nameless, and it kept us alive, and then—here's the crack—it stopped being a tool and became a comfort. It's comfortable, being no one. No one can hold you to anything. No one can come looking." She turned a stone over in her hands. "I've been no one for nineteen years. I'm good at it. I have excellent references."

Ovid snorted softly. Bex almost smiled.

"But," Tamsin went on, "there's a difference between choosing to be nameless and having your name taken. We choose it. Every morning, we choose it, and every morning we could stop. These two didn't choose. Sef Oduya didn't choose to be a mound of rocks. Little Ferret didn't choose to be a thumbprint on a clerk's ledger." She looked at Isandro. "So you're right. It's not enough. It's what we have today, but it's not enough, and the fact that it's not enough is going to matter. I can feel it the way I feel weather coming. Can't tell you from which direction yet."

"What do we do about it?" Isandro asked.

"We remember them," Tamsin said. "Out loud. That's the only marker that can't be collected." She stood, knees be damned, and raised her voice to carry to the whole gathering. "Adem. You said he sang. Sing one of his songs. Off-key on purpose."

Adem stared at her. "I don't—"

"You do. Badly. Perfectly."

And Adem, trembling, wet-eyed, began one of the hauling songs of the Corrow docks—a crude, rollicking thing about a fishmonger's wife and a bishop, absolutely unfit for a funeral, and therefore exactly right. His voice cracked and wobbled and went spectacularly off-key, and by the second verse two other porters had joined in, and by the third even Ovid was humming along with the solemn concentration of a man defusing something, and Little Ferret's grave got its own song though nobody knew his name, and somehow that was worse and better at once.

When it ended, Tamsin laid the last stone herself.

"Sef Oduya," she said clearly. "And the boy called Little Ferret, whose name the world owes and doesn't know it owes. Walk home the long way. Go well."

The crew murmured the closing words—*go well, go dry, go found*—and it struck Tamsin, not for the first time, that the old ranger blessing was a lie they told each other. *Go found.* Nobody went found. They went lost, all of them, permanently, professionally.

But standing there in the sun with the song still hanging in the air, she thought: *maybe that's the crack too. Maybe we've been blessing each other with the one thing we refuse to give ourselves.*

* * *

Bex found her afterward at the medical tarp, restocking bandages that didn't need restocking, which was Tamsin's version of pacing.

"Tea was a good call," Tamsin said without looking up.

"The tea was strategic. Grief dehydrates." Bex settled onto a crate with the groan of a woman whose body had opinions about crates. For a while she just watched Tamsin fold gauze into squares of identical perfection. "You told the boy the truth."

"Part of it."

"All the part that matters right now."

"Maybe." Tamsin smoothed a fold that was already smooth. "Bex. When we voted on taking this job, I voted yes. Did you ever wonder why?"

"You said the money was real and the route was ours anyway."

"I said that." She set down the gauze. "What I didn't say is that I've spent nineteen years telling myself the Concession thing was finished. Carried and done. Paperwork goes where paperwork goes; we were couriers, not judges. And I believed it right up until I watched those two boys' faces when Adem said *thumbprint*. And I realized—" Her hands had stopped. She looked at them like they belonged to someone else. "I realized I've been practicing a smaller version of the big crime for two decades. Every nameless grave I dug, I told myself it was protection. But some part of me knew it was also erasure. Same act. Smaller scale. Kinder intentions." She laughed, short and joyless. "Intentions. The company had intentions too, I imagine. Efficient ones."

Bex was quiet for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had none of its usual ledger-keeper's dryness. "If you're working up to saying you want to turn us around, don't bother. I've been doing sums since the flood."

"Sums."

"Ninety-day permits. A survey crew that counts weapons better than instruments. A route map I haven't seen but Ferro has, and Ferro's face when he saw it." Bex ticked invisible entries on her fingers. "And now a company that hires porters who sign with thumbprints, sends them up a killer canyon during storm season, and loses two of them without sending a man to stand at the graveside. Dreele sent a clerk to note the time of death. That's all. Time of death, for the insurance schedule." She let her hand fall. "Tamsin, I've kept books for a lot of employers. I know what a balance sheet looks like when it's hiding something."

"So what are you saying?"

"I'm saying the vote was three-two and the two of us who said yes did it for different reasons, and mine just changed." Bex stood, joints announcing each stage of the process. "Whatever's really going on up this river—when we find it, I want it written down. Recorded. In a book that can't burn." She paused at the edge of the tarp. "Names matter, Tam. I erased mine because I wanted to. Nobody should ever have theirs erased for them. That's the whole difference between us and them, and I'd like to keep holding onto it."

She crunched away across the gravel, leaving Tamsin alone with her perfectly folded gauze and a feeling she couldn't quite name—something like dread, but purposeful. Dread with a spine in it.

* * *

Elodie came up from the waterline at dusk.

Tamsin saw her coming from a distance and made a decision she'd been circling all day: she intercepted her, alone, before the captain could reach the graves and stand there all night flagellating herself in the dark like some penitent saint of the salt flats.

"They're buried," Tamsin said simply. "Facing home. Adem sang. It was terrible and perfect."

Elodie nodded. Her face was composed, which from Elodie meant nothing; the woman could compose a face in a rockslide. But her eyes were red-rimmed and her voice, when it came, was half a size too small. "An hour, Tamsin. I read the sky wrong by an hour. I've read that canyon my whole life and I read it wrong by—"

"You read it wrong by an hour because the storm was wrong by an hour. Storms off the high plateau have been arriving early all season; ask any caravan master south of the Scarp. You made the best call available with the information the sky gave you." Tamsin held her gaze. "I was there. I read the same sky. I said nothing. If we're holding funerals for guilt, I'll be standing at the front of the line."

"That's not—"

"It's exactly so. And here's the other thing, and then I'll let you go be miserable in private like a professional." She stepped closer. "Those boys died in a company operation, in a canyon the company has no business in, on permits that smell like a dead fish left in the sun. I have spent nineteen years burying the past politely, Elodie. Today I ran out of politeness. Whatever this job actually is—whatever Dreele's hunting, whatever Vane wants, whatever's rotting under the Concession—I think we were hired to be the shovel, and I think it's time we decided to be the witness instead."

Elodie looked at her for a long moment. Something moved behind the composed face—not agreement, not yet, but the ground shifting toward it, the way ice shifts before it decides to become water.

"You sound like Isandro," she said at last.

"He sounds like the truth. It's contagious."

A ghost of a smile crossed Elodie's mouth and died there. "Get some sleep, Tamsin."

"You first."

"I will."

"Liar," Tamsin said fondly, and let her go.

She was folding the tarp when Ferro materialized beside her for the second time that day, and this time his silence had weight in it; she could hear the weight, the way you hear rain approaching across hardpan.

"Say it," she said.

"Second party on our trail," Ferro said quietly. "Five riders, maybe six. Light pack animals, no survey gear. They crossed our back-trail at the narrows sometime yesterday—they didn't try to hide it, which worries me more than if they had." He paused. "They're moving like people who already know where we're going."

Tamsin looked up at the darkening sky, at the two new mounds of stone above the flood line, at the river hurrying itself downstream as if late for something.

"Well," she said. "It seems the collectors found a map after all."
