## 15. Vane Follows

Ferro came back into camp the way he always did—sideways, quiet as a rumor, carrying a look that meant trouble had been out walking and he'd made its acquaintance.

Elodie was mending a pack strap when he crouched beside her, ostensibly to inspect her stitching.

"Your seam's crooked," he said.

"My seam is fine."

"Your seam is a scandal." He turned the strap over in his long fingers, and under the cover of fussing with it, his voice dropped to almost nothing. "Smoke. Two miles back, east rim. Cook fire, banked low. Somebody who doesn't want to be seen cooking."

"How many?"

"Hard to say. The smoke moved twice while I watched. Two camps or one camp that likes to relocate." He handed the strap back. "And this morning I found boot prints at the second water stop. Fresh ones. Someone's been drinking our water an hour behind us for two days."

Elodie kept her eyes on the strap. Across the fire, Isandro was showing Pell how to set a snare that caught nothing but taught you patience, and Dreele stood apart with his arms folded, watching the river like it owed him money. Which, in a sense, it might.

"Anyone else know?" she asked.

"Just you."

"Keep it that way. And Ferro—count them properly tomorrow. I want numbers, not weather reports."

He nodded once and drifted off toward the horse lines, whistling three notes of nothing, a man with no opinions about anything.

Elodie sat with the crooked seam and let the cold walk up her spine one vertebra at a time.

* * *

She didn't sleep that night. She told the crew she was taking first watch, which was true, and that she liked watching the moon come over the rim, which was a lie so comfortable it practically qualified as furniture.

The canyon at night was a cathedral with the roof torn off. Stars in such quantity they seemed less like lights than like an audience. The River Anselm muttered to itself below, working through whatever rivers work through—sediment, mostly, and grudges.

Two days behind them. Drinking their water. Banking cook fires like people who'd been taught by someone careful.

There were only so someones in the world.

Sable Vane.

The name arrived in Elodie's head the way bad news always did—not as a thought but as a weight, settling onto the chest like a cat that knew it wasn't welcome and intended to stay anyway.

They hadn't crossed paths in eleven years. Eleven years, four months, and change, not that Elodie was counting; counting was the sort of thing Sable would do, and Elodie prided herself on being above it, right up until she caught herself doing arithmetic in the dark like a lovesick teenager, except what she was sick over was older and meaner than love.

Here was the thing about Sable Vane: she was the best tracker Elodie had ever known, including herself, and she said so only slightly more often than necessary. Here was the other thing: Sable had walked away from the Concession business twenty years ago with money in her pocket and a smile on her face, and Elodie had walked away from *her*, and neither of them had ever once said why out loud, because saying it out loud would have required words, and words were the one supply neither of them had ever learned to ration properly.

And now here was Sable's signature all over this job—the too-generous fee, the survey team with too many guns and too few instruments, the route chosen not because it surveyed well but because it passed every place where something could be buried.

Elodie had told herself the contract was just a contract. Money was money, and the crew needed water credits the way fish needed the wet part of water.

But if there was a second party shadowing them—

Then the crew wasn't an escort. The crew was a leash. Or worse: witnesses. And witnesses, in Sable's world, got managed.

Elodie looked down at her hands in the starlight. They weren't shaking. She checked twice, the way Bex checked a ledger, and they weren't shaking, and somehow that was worse, because it meant her body had already accepted the news and moved on to planning, leaving the rest of her to catch up.

* * *

Morning brought the crew together around the fire with the practiced economy of people who'd eaten breakfast in worse places.

"We're being followed," Elodie said.

No gasps. That was the thing about rangers; surprise was a luxury item, and none of them carried it. Ovid paused in the middle of buttering flatbread, considered, and continued buttering. Tamsin finished wrapping a splint on one of the porters' ankles before looking up. Bex simply opened the ledger to a fresh page, because Bex's response to any crisis was documentation.

"How many?" Ferro asked.

"Unknown. You're going to find out today."

"And you're certain it's hostile?" Dreele asked, in the tone of a man suggesting the weather might clear. "Survey work attracts scavengers. Claim-jumpers. It's probably nothing."

"Probably," Elodie agreed pleasantly. "In my experience, 'probably' is what people say right before the interesting part happens."

Dreele's jaw did a small, expensive thing. "My employers expect this survey completed on schedule."

"Your employers," Elodie said, "expect a great many things. Today I'm concerned with what follows us."

Isandro had gone very still by the fire, snare wire forgotten in his hands. He was sixteen and wore every thought on his face like weather, and right now the weather was: *someone is hunting us and everyone is pretending this is normal.* Elodie caught his eye and gave him the smallest nod—*later*—and he swallowed his questions whole, which cost him visibly, like swallowing a live fish.

"Pell," Elodie said. "Your instruments. How heavy is the theodolite?"

Pell blinked. He was the junior engineer, a young man built entirely out of elbows and anxiety, and being addressed directly appeared to short-circuit him briefly. "Heavy? Um. Sixteen pounds. Why—"

"Because if we have to move fast, it stays. Everything stays except water, ammunition, and the people. Understood?"

"That's—" Pell glanced at Dreele, then back, and something in his face suggested he'd done this math before and gotten an answer he didn't like. "Understood."

"Bex, refigure our water. If we push hard for two days, what do we carry?"

Bex licked her thumb, turned pages. "We carry thirst," she said. "Manageable thirst. Two days' hard march puts us at the Narrows, and the Narrows has springs."

"The Narrows also has walls close enough to spit across," Ovid said mildly. "Speaking as our resident expert on things that go bang, narrow places are where clever ambushers go to feel tall."

"Noted. Which is why we don't camp in them. We pass through at midday, full light, spread out." Elodie looked around the circle. "Questions?"

"I have one," Tamsin said quietly. She was cleaning her kit with the unhurried thoroughness of a woman who believed dirt was a moral failing. "If it's who I think it might be—do we run, or do we turn and face it?"

The fire popped. Somewhere downstream a bird made an argument nobody won.

"We're escorting a survey," Elodie said. "We finish the job. Whatever's behind us can keep following until it gets bored or makes a mistake." She stood, brushing sand from her knees. "Either way, it runs out of water before we do. Probably."

"'Probably,'" murmured Ovid, "is doing a lot of work in that sentence."

* * *

They broke camp and moved, and the canyon closed around them like a hand deciding whether to hold or squeeze.

Elodie rode point with Isandro beside her, because the boy had earned the position and because she wanted him where she could see him. He lasted nearly an hour before the questions boiled over.

"It's her, isn't it. The one who sent the letter. The one you burned."

Elodie watched the rim. "What makes you say that?"

"You burned a sealed offer without reading it. Bex still talks about it. And now someone's tracking us like they learned it from us." He shrugged. "I do sums."

"You do sums," Elodie agreed. "All right. Yes. I think it's Sable Vane."

"Your rival."

"My—" Elodie huffed. "Where do you get these words?"

"Ferro. He tells stories when he thinks I'm asleep. He says you two used to be the best pair of trackers on the whole Escarpment, and then one day you weren't speaking, and he says—" Isandro hesitated, choosing, which was unlike him. "He says you left a piece of yourself wherever you two parted ways, and neither of you went back for it."

"Ferro," Elodie said, "talks too much for a man whose entire profession is silence."

"So it's true?"

Elodie was quiet long enough that the river filled the space. "It's true that Sable Vane is the finest tracker I've ever seen, and if she's behind us, then she knows exactly where we are, what we're eating, and probably what we're dreaming. It's true that she doesn't do anything without three reasons, and it's true that none of those reasons will be the reason she gives." She glanced sideways. "And it's true that if she wanted us dead, we'd already be dead. Sable doesn't waste. So whatever she wants—" 

"—she wants it alive," Isandro finished.

"She wants it *managed*. There's a difference, and the difference matters."

"What's the difference?"

Elodie found a stone in the trail and nudged it loose with her boot, watched it roll down toward the water. "Alive means she needs something from you. Managed means she needs something from you *and* has decided what you're allowed to know while giving it." She met his eyes. "Remember that word, Isandro. Managed. It's the politest word in the world for what happens to people who've stopped being useful."

He was quiet after that, but she could feel him turning it over, the way he turned everything over—rocks, snares, other people's silences. Good. A boy who asked questions grew into a man who noticed answers.

* * *

They made the Narrows by midday, exactly as planned, which was the day's single comfort. The walls rose sheer and rust-colored, close enough that riders could touch both sides with outstretched arms, and the river ran quick and green between them, chattering like it knew secrets.

Halfway through, Ferro materialized alongside Elodie's horse without seeming to have traveled there.

"Three," he said. "Maybe four. I got close enough to smell their cook fire. Coffee and cured meat. Company rations."

"Company."

"Same stock Dreele's porters carry. Same brand of tin, even." He paused. "And one more thing. Boot prints again, freshest set. Small feet. Narrow heel."

"Sable," Elodie said.

"Sable," Ferro agreed. "Walking point on her own scout line, which she only does when she doesn't trust anyone else to read ground. Which means she expects company worth reading." He scratched his jaw. "Or she's showing off. She always did like an audience."

"Eleven years," Elodie said, "and you remember her habits better than I do."

"I remember everybody's habits. It's the job." Ferro's eyes flicked ahead, to where the Narrows opened onto a wide gravel bar bathed in afternoon light. "You want my honest read?"

"I want your honest read."

"They're not trying to catch us. If they were, they'd have hit us in the dark weeks ago, or picked the slot canyons. They're pacing us. Herding. Keeping us on the route." He tipped his head back at the towering walls. "Somebody wants us exactly where we are, going exactly where we're going, and getting nervous about it right on schedule."

Elodie felt the wire tighten another notch. Because Ferro was right, and she'd known it since the smoke on the rim, and knowing it changed the shape of everything. This wasn't pursuit. Pursuit you could outrun, outfox, outlast. This was *escort*—the ugly kind, the kind with guns, where the escorted party never learns it's being escorted.

Vane had hired them to walk a road she'd already mapped, past graves she needed forgotten, on a clock that expired in seventy-some days. And when the job was done—or when the crew had seen one thing too many—the managing would begin.

Unless Elodie began it first.

"There's a side drainage," she said slowly, "half a mile past the gravel bar. Deer Canyon. Steep, ugly, no survey value whatsoever."

Ferro's mouth twitched. "No survey value whatsoever," he repeated, delighted. "So naturally we'll be camping there tonight."

"Naturally."

"And Dreele?"

"Dreele will object. Dreele will be overruled. Contract says route decisions are ours." She smiled, and it wasn't a warm smile, and Ferro had known her long enough to recognize it as the kind she wore when the plan had teeth. "Let's find out what our shadow does when we step off her map."

* * *

Deer Canyon was everything the survey maps ignored: a crack in the earth choked with tamarisk and shadow, climbing away from the river in switchbacks too steep for the pack animals to love. They made camp on a bench halfway up, back to stone, sight lines down both approaches. Ovid spent a cheerful hour placing little surprises along the lower trail, humming, patting the earth flat over each one like a gardener tucking in seeds.

"If anyone asks," he said, dusting his hands, "these are purely defensive arrangements. Horticultural, even."

"Nobody's asking, Ovid."

"They never do. That's the tragedy of my art."

Dusk came down. Supper was beans and the last of the good bread, and Elodie watched her crew settle into watch rotations with the smooth interlocking ease of gears cut for each other, and felt the old fierce tenderness rise in her chest—this ragged, nameless family, held together by habit and trust and the shared agreement never to ask each other anything important.

And somewhere out in the dark, small-footed and patient, the person who knew her better than anyone alive was watching the same stars and counting the same watches, and had been for days.

Tamsin sat down beside her at the fire, offered tea, and waited until Elodie had drunk half of it before speaking.

"You knew," Tamsin said. Not accusing. Establishing.

"I suspected."

"For how long?"

"Since the contract. Since the route. Since the guns." Elodie turned the cup in her hands. "I told myself I was being paranoid. Paranoid is a word people use to make caution sound like sickness."

"And now?"

"Now paranoia sounds like the sensible option." She looked up at the black slash of the canyon rim. "Tamsin. If it is her—and it's her—if she's here to manage us, then this job was never a job. We were hired to be led. To see what she needed seen and nothing else, and then to be handled." Her voice stayed level, which took work. "I signed us onto it. I read the permits, I counted the guns, I set the terms, and I still walked us straight down her road like sheep with excellent boots."

Tamsin considered this, then refilled Elodie's cup unasked. "For what it's worth," she said, "sheep with excellent boots are still harder to handle than regular sheep. Especially these particular sheep." She nodded across the fire, where Isandro was teaching Pell to play a card game whose rules Isandro invented anew each round, always in his own favor, always with total conviction. "That boy's going to ask you the real question soon. About her. About you. About all of it."

"I know."

"And when he does?"

Elodie watched the fire. Down in the dark, far off, something shifted—a pebble rolling, or a boot placed carefully, or nothing at all.

"When he does," she said, "I suppose I'll finally have to decide which lie I can live with."

The wire hummed, tight as a bowstring, all the way down the canyon and out into the waiting dark.

Above the rim, unseen, a cook fire banked itself to nothing, and a woman with small feet and narrow heels settled in to wait for morning—and for the moment Elodie Yarrow stepped, one way or another, back onto her map.
