## 16. Ambush at the Narrows

Ovid had a theory about mornings, which was that they were ambushes you agreed to in advance. Every dawn was the world sneaking up on you, and the only question was whether it came politely or with bullets. He'd spent twenty years arranging his life so that he would always know which kind of morning he was getting. He slept light, slept armed, and slept—this was the part people found funny—beside his boots.

So when he woke in the grey hour before first light at the camp below the Narrows, and something felt wrong about the silence, he did not waste time wondering whether he was being paranoid. Paranoia was just pattern recognition with bad manners.

The river was wrong. The Anselm ran loud through the Narrows, a constant gravel-voiced argument between water and stone, and that noise covered sound the way snow covers tracks. But birds used it. Every morning since they'd entered canyon country, the rock wrens had started up before the sky went from iron to ash, stitching their little songs over the top of the water. This morning the wrens were quiet.

Birds don't take days off. Birds only go quiet for one reason.

Ovid rolled out of his bedroll without moving his shoulders, the way you do when you don't want to be seen moving, and put his hand on the satchel under his head. Inside were six charges of his own manufacture, wrapped in waxed canvas, each one a small opinion about walls and doors. He'd promised Elodie he wouldn't use them on people. He'd also promised her, years ago, that he'd stop calling her "captain" when she wasn't listening, and both promises had about the same life expectancy.

He looked across the dead coals of the fire to Ferro's position. Ferro was already awake. Of course Ferro was already awake; Ferro slept like a knife sleeps in a drawer. Their eyes met in the dark, and Ferro held up two fingers, then turned his hand flat and moved it low along the ground.

Two men. Low. Coming down the talus slope on the north side.

Then Ferro's fingers changed, quick as a card sharp: four more, spread wide, downstream side.

Six. And where there were six visible, there were usually four invisible, because nobody who planned an attack sent everyone to the pretty side of the camp.

Ovid began doing arithmetic. It was what he was for. Bex kept the ledgers; Ovid kept the sums that couldn't be written down. Twelve porters asleep in a row by the mules. Pell in his tent, snoring like a man who had never once been shot at, bless him. Dreele downstream with his own tent and his own guards, which meant Dreele's camp would be hit first or not at all. Tamsin nearest the horses. Isandro—

Isandro had taken last watch and should have been sitting up by the fire. Ovid's stomach dropped through the canyon floor when he saw the boy's bedroll empty and the watch stone cold.

Then he heard it: a soft whistle from upstream, three notes, the signal Isandro had invented himself during the second week of the expedition because, as he'd put it, "the old signals are boring." The boy had gone to piss and noticed something, and instead of panicking he'd whistled.

Good lad. Terrible signals. Good lad.

* * *

What happened next happened fast, and Ovid would spend the rest of his life replaying it in pieces, because that was how memory worked after gunpowder got involved—it broke the morning into shards and made you sort them forever.

First shard: the flare. Someone upstream fired a signal charge—not one of his, a bought one, red and ugly—and the whole canyon lit up like the inside of a furnace door. In that light Ovid saw them coming down the north slope, four men in a staggered line, rifles slung for climbing, moving like professionals.

Second shard: Elodie's voice, calm as a woman reading a shopping list, cutting through the red glare: "Porters in the rocks! Tamsin, cut the mule lines!"

Third shard: the mules. God, the mules. They'd been picketed too close to the open sand, and when the shooting started they went mad together, all eight of them, a single screaming animal with sixteen legs. They tore loose, took two picket pins out of the ground like teeth, and bolted straight downriver into the dark with most of the expedition's food, rope, and dignity attached to their backs.

Fourth shard: Ovid himself, moving. He didn't remember deciding to move. His body had been in this business longer than his conscience had, and it went where it went. He came up behind the big boulder at the camp's north edge—the one he'd privately named Mrs. Pemberton years ago, on the theory that every camp needed a stout aunt standing guard—and he put his rifle over her shoulder and started working.

He was not a good shot. He'd say so himself, freely, to anyone. What he was, was economical. He didn't try to kill anyone; he tried to make the ground where people wanted to stand become ground they no longer wanted to stand on. A bullet into the ledge above the first climber dropped a fan of rock onto him. A second into the dead juniper beside the third man's path convinced that gentleman, quite reasonably, that the juniper was haunted. Professionals are professionals, but nobody signs on to be shot by shrubbery.

It bought seconds. Seconds were the currency of the whole affair, and Ovid spent them like a drunk lord.

Because here was the ugly truth of the thing, the part that would sit in his chest for weeks afterward: they won, and winning looked almost exactly like losing.

* * *

The fight lasted, by Ovid's later count, somewhere between ninety seconds and the rest of his life.

Vane's men were good. That was the insult of it. They came down the slope in pairs, laid down disciplined fire, and drove toward the porters' row with the clear, cold intention of hurting people until the crew gave up whatever they wanted. This was not robbery. Robbery happens at night to strangers. This was a message written in lead, and the message was: *you are not safe anywhere between here and the source.*

Ovid heard Pell screaming—one long unbroken note, like a kettle—and heard Dreele's voice too, further off, shouting orders to his own guards, and noted with the detached interest he reserved for human nature that Dreele's guards seemed mostly interested in guarding Dreele.

Tamsin got the porters moving. He saw her in the flare-light, small and furious, hauling men up by their collars and shoving them into the slot of rocks above the beach, and she was saying something to each one as she pushed—later he learned it was "breathe, breathe, run"—because Tamsin could not touch a frightened person without doctoring them, even mid-battle, even when the medicine required was only permission to keep breathing.

Ferro worked the downstream flank alone, silent, and Ovid knew he was there only because Vane's men on that side kept flinching at nothing and shifting cover for no visible reason. Ferro fought the way fog fights. You lost to him before you knew there was a war.

And Elodie—Elodie stood up. Full height, in the open, in the flare-light, and shouted "Enough!" in the voice she used maybe once a year, the voice that had ended bar fights in towns that no longer appeared on maps. And for one strange heartbeat, the shooting actually faltered, because some voices are load-bearing and hers was one of them.

That was when the porter died.

His name was Sef. Ovid knew it because Bex knew everyone's name and made everyone else learn them too—"we don't hire hands, we hire people, write it on your heart if your head won't hold it." Sef was nineteen, from the terraced farms two hundred miles back downriver, and he had a laugh like a cart going over a bridge. When the shooting started he froze, the way green men do, and he was still frozen, standing up in the open, when a rifle spoke from the slope.

One bullet. No drama. He sat down slowly, like a man deciding to rest, and did not get up.

Tamsin reached him before the echo died. Ovid saw her hands press down, saw her mouth move—counting, she was counting, she counted everything, pulse and breath and seconds—and then saw her hands stop, and go flat, and stay flat. Even at forty yards, even in the dark, Ovid knew what flat hands meant.

Something went off in him then. Not the powder—he was past the powder, the powder was craft. Something older. He stood up from behind Mrs. Pemberton, entirely exposed, entirely furious, and fired three shots into the slope with a precision that surprised him, and the fourth man on the line decided, with excellent judgment, that the expedition was over, and went back up the rocks at a speed that would have impressed a lizard.

After that it was over the way storms are over—the rain stops but the air stays wrong for hours.

* * *

They counted themselves in the grey light like misers counting coins.

Twelve porters: eleven alive, one dead. Eight mules: three returned on their own at first light, wandering back with the sheepish air of animals who know they've behaved badly, and five gone downriver with half the food and all of the spare rope. Pell: alive, pale, sitting on a rock with his head between his knees, apologizing to no one in particular. Dreele: untouched, immaculate, emerging from his tent with his coat already buttoned, wearing the expression of a man reviewing a disappointing harvest.

"Your contract," Dreele said to Elodie, "specifies protection."

"Our contract," Elodie said, without turning around, "specifies that we answer to no surveyor. Read your own paper, Mr. Dreele. I wrote the clause myself."

Ovid, checking the chamber of his rifle, allowed himself a small private smile. There she was.

But the smile didn't last, because Sef was lying under a blanket on the sand, and Tamsin was kneeling beside him with her hands still flat on the wool, and Bex was standing over them both with the ledger open against her forearm, writing Sef's name in it. Not the accounts ledger. The other one, the little black book she kept in her shirt, the one nobody asked about. Names in, names kept. That was Bex's whole religion, and she practiced it now in silence while the river argued on, indifferent, the way rivers are.

"We bury him proper," Bex said finally, and her voice was steady except at the very bottom of it, where it wasn't. "Not ranger-style. Proper. With his name on something."

"Tamsin," Elodie said quietly, "when you're ready."

"I'm ready," Tamsin said, and did not move for another minute, and nobody hurried her.

* * *

It was Ferro who found the gunman.

The man had gotten separated from his retreat in the dark, holed up in a wash fifty yards downstream, and might have escaped clean except that he made the classic professional's error: he assumed the fight was over because the shooting stopped. Ferro simply walked to the wash and stood at the lip of it until sunrise, and when the man finally peeked out, there was Ferro, crouched comfortably, holding a cup of coffee.

"Morning," Ferro said. "There's a second cup if you come out slow."

The man came out slow. He was thirtyish, weathered, with the flat eyes of hired work, and he let Ferro bind his wrists without any fuss at all, which told Ovid more than a confession would have. This was a man who understood how the week was likely to go, and had already priced his own survival into it.

They brought him to Elodie at the fire. She was sitting on a stone with Sef's blanket-wrapped body nearby and the whole ruined morning arranged around her, and Ovid watched her look at the gunman for a long moment before she said anything at all.

"You have a name?" she asked.

The man said nothing.

"A pay rate, then. Everyone has one. Six bits a day? A dollar? Enough to sit in a wash while other men die?"

Nothing. But his jaw tightened, and Ovid, watching, thought: *there. She found the seam.*

Elodie crouched down to his level, forearms on her knees, and her voice went gentle, which from Elodie was more frightening than shouting. "Here's what I know," she said. "Sable Vane doesn't waste money. If she sent eight of you against twelve porters and four rangers, she wanted something done that couldn't be undone. Scare us off? She could've done that with a letter. Kill us? You'd have brought more men. So it's neither. It's *search*. You were meant to push us around, see what we protected, see where we ran." She tilted her head. "And I'm guessing you were told to take someone alive. Or something."

The gunman's eyes flicked—just once, just a hair's width—toward the pack line, toward the strongbox strapped under the survey gear.

"There it is," Elodie said softly.

She waited. The river filled the silence, gravel-voiced, patient. Somewhere above, one lone rock wren finally remembered itself and started singing again, tentative, like a musician testing whether the war was really over.

"She wants the ledger," the gunman said.

Just that. Four words, flat as a dropped stone. And then he shut his mouth the way a man shuts a door on a room he never intends to enter again, and nothing Elodie, Bex, or Ovid could bring to bear—threats, offers, coffee, silence—opened it one inch wider.

But four words were enough. Ovid watched them land on his crew one at a time, like stones into still water. Bex's hand went to the little black book in her shirt. Tamsin looked up from Sef's body. Ferro's face did nothing at all, which for Ferro was a shout.

Because there was only one ledger worth sending guns for. Only one ledger that mattered anywhere on this river, buried or hidden or waiting in the high country ahead of them, in the place they were all pretending they weren't traveling toward.

Elodie stood. She looked downriver, the way the mules had gone, then upriver, toward the Narrows, toward the source, toward the Concession line that grew closer with every mile like a debt coming due.

"Rig the camp," she said. Her voice was level, but Ovid had known her twenty years, and underneath the levelness he heard something he had heard only twice before: the sound of a decision being forged, white-hot, in real time. "We burn what we can't carry and we move within the hour."

"And him?" Ferro asked, nodding at the gunman.

Elodie considered the man for a moment. "Feed him," she said. "Then turn him loose downriver with water and his boots. Tell him the next crew he works for might not be as polite."

She turned away, and Ovid caught her muttering, half to herself, half to the river, in a tone that had no politeness left in it at all:

"The ledger. So it's real, and she knows where it is, and she's afraid of what's written in it." A pause. "Good. Let's find out what she knows that we don't."

Ovid went to fetch his satchel of charges, and behind him the wren sang on, and the river carried the morning's dead names down toward the sea.
