## 17. The Ledger

The gunman woke up angry.

That was the first thing Elodie noted as she crouched at the edge of the lean-to they'd built for him behind the cook fire's windbreak. Angry, and alive, and tied at the wrists to a stake Ovid had planted with the same cheerful efficiency he brought to everything, as if human restraint were just another kind of demolition work.

"Water," the man rasped.

Elodie looked at him a long moment. He was maybe thirty, sunburned to the color of old brick, with a broken nose that had been broken more than once and set badly every time. His boots were good. That was what she noticed second—good boots, oiled leather, the kind you couldn't buy on a gunman's wages. Somebody was paying this man in more than coin.

She dipped the tin cup into the water jar and held it to his mouth herself, tilting it carefully, letting him drink until he turned his head away.

"There," she said. "Now we're civilized."

"We're nothing." He spat into the dirt. "You're dead people. All of you. You just don't know it yet."

"Mm." Elodie settled onto her heels, forearms resting on her knees, the way she'd sat across a hundred campfires from men in exactly his position—some on his side of the rope, some on hers. "See, that's the interesting part. Dead people don't usually get shot *at*. Wasted effort. But your friends last night wasted a great deal of ammunition on us, which suggests somebody thinks we're worth killing, which suggests we know something worth killing for." She smiled at him, the smile she used on caravan masters who were trying to lowball Bex. "I don't like not knowing things. It makes me peevish. Ask anyone."

"I'm not telling you anything."

"You already told Ferro your name."

"That was—" He stopped.

"Ferro has that effect on people," Elodie said. "Very disarming. In his case literally, but let's not dwell." She let the silence stretch. The morning was doing its slow work around them—the smell of Tamsin's porridge, the clink of Bex reorganizing the salvage from the ruined packs, somewhere out by the horse lines Isandro's laugh, bright and unguarded, cut off quickly when he remembered. Two porters dead yesterday. One porter and six animals lost in the night. The camp had the particular hush of a place where everyone was counting what was left.

The gunman drank his water like a man who'd been taught never to refuse it. Desert-born, then, or close enough. That narrowed things.

"Here's how I see it," Elodie went on, conversationally. "Vane hired us as an escort. Then she followed us with guns. Now, I've known Sable Vane since before you could grow that beard, and one thing I can promise you about her: she does not spend money twice. If she paid for an escort *and* a firing line, it's because the escort was always meant to be managed. Which means whatever she's after isn't the survey. The survey is theater. So." She tilted her head. "What's the play?"

The man said nothing, but his eyes flicked—just once, just barely—upstream.

Elodie kept her face pleasant and her heart very still. Upstream. Upstream was the Concession. Upstream was the source, the grave country, the place none of them spoke of around the fire except Tamsin, once a year, alone, and even then only in the dark where no one would admit to hearing.

"You're a long way from home to be guarding theater," she said.

"I don't know what you're talking about."

"No. You know exactly what I'm talking about. That's the problem." She stood, brushed off her knees, and made a show of stretching, glancing toward the cook fire where Tamsin was pretending very hard not to listen while stirring a pot that needed no stirring. "Well. I'll leave you to think about it. We'll be moving upstream within the hour, so you'll have plenty of time to consider whether you'd rather talk to me or to the river."

* * *

Bex caught her by the elbow behind the supply tarp.

"You're bluffing," Bex said quietly. "We're not leaving him."

"I haven't decided yet."

"Elodie." Bex's voice dropped further. Bex was the only person alive who could say her name like a scolding schoolmistress and survive it. "He's a hired gun who tried to kill us in our bedrolls. Tamsin patched two of our porters all night and buried one of them at dawn. Ferro wants to hand him to the river towns and let the law sort it. And you're sitting out there sharing water with him like he's a stray dog you're thinking of keeping."

"He knows something."

"They all know something. That's why Vane hires them."

"This is different." Elodie rubbed the bridge of her nose. Under the brim of her hat, out of Bex's sightline, her hand wasn't quite steady, and she hated that. Hated that Bex had seen it three weeks ago over a burned letter and hadn't forgotten, and wouldn't. "He looked upstream, Bex. When I mentioned the survey being theater. Not downstream, not back the way we came. Upstream. Toward the Concession."

Bex was quiet for a moment. Around them the tarp snapped in the morning wind, and beyond it the camp sounds continued—Ovid whistling something tuneless while he checked the raft lashings, the porters murmuring in their own tongue, Pell's nervous footsteps going back and forth, back and forth, a man pacing a hole in his own conscience.

"You think she's after the mines," Bex said. "After twenty years. After the permits are nearly spent."

"I think Sable Vane doesn't ride three weeks into cursed country for geology."

"And you think a gunman with good boots is going to tell you what she's actually after because you gave him a cup of water."

"No," Elodie said. "I think he'll tell me because he's starting to do the same arithmetic we all do out here. Vane spends men like water. He watched her throw twelve of them at a defended camp at dawn for what we took from them. He's lying on the ground doing sums, and sums like that only come out one way." She paused. "Also I gave him water because he's a human being and thirsty. Both things can be true. Don't make that face."

"I'm not making a face."

"You're making the face."

* * *

It was Isandro who broke him, though nobody planned it that way.

The boy came by mid-morning with the prisoner's ration—porridge, flatbread, a strip of dried meat—and Elodie, watching from ten paces off under the pretense of checking the tack, saw him crouch down outside arm's reach and set the plate down with the exaggerated care of someone handling something venomous.

"You're the one who shot at Kessin's brother," Isandro said. Not accusing. Just stating it, the way a boy states weather.

The gunman looked at him. "Kid, run along."

"He was nineteen. He carried the water for the survey crew. He used to sing when he thought nobody was listening, and he sang badly, and now he's under a pile of rocks with no marker because of you and your friends." Isandro's voice didn't rise. That was the strange part. It stayed level and young and terribly clear. "Tamsin says we don't mark graves because names aren't ours to give. But I keep thinking about how he won't get one. Ever. Because of a morning you were part of."

The gunman's jaw worked. He looked at the food. He looked at the boy—this skinny, sun-dark apprentice with his too-big hat and his steady eyes—and something in his face came loose, some knot that fear hadn't touched.

"I'm not sorry," he said, but it came out wrong, came out like a question.

"Maybe not yet," Isandro said, and stood, and walked away, leaving the plate.

Elodie exhaled slowly through her nose. Twenty years of reading men across card tables and campfires, and the boy undid in ninety seconds what she'd been working at all morning—not with cunning, just with the plain unbearable weight of being young and honest in front of a man who had stopped being either.

She waited until the gunman had eaten. Then she came back and sat down across from him, and this time she didn't smile.

"Talk to me," she said. "Not for me. For you. Because when this is over—and it will be over, soon, one way or another—you're going to want to have been the man who talked."

A long silence. Wind in the canyon willows. Somewhere a hawk complaining about the morning.

"She wants a ledger," the gunman said finally. His voice was flat, emptied out. "Paymaster's ledger. From the Concession. From before."

The word went through Elodie like cold water through cloth.

"The massacre," the man went on, staring at the ground between his boots. "There was a paymaster. Company man. Kept the books himself—wages, bribes, the gunmen's bonuses, the magistrate's cut. Everything written down, because he was the kind of man who wrote everything down. When it went bad, when the government started asking questions, he ran. Took the ledger with him. Hid it. Everybody says upstream, in the canyon country, in a cache only he knew." He laughed, short and ugly. "Then he died, and took the location with him. Or everybody hoped he did."

"And Vane?"

"Vane's signature's in it." He raised his eyes to hers, and there was something almost like pity in them, which was the worst thing yet. "So is half the territory's gentry, if the stories are true. Names, amounts, dates. Who paid for the guns. Who signed the permits that made it legal. Who got rich off two hundred dead miners." He shrugged against the stake. "Company's burning the canyon clean before the concessions lapse. Vane needs the ledger found first—or destroyed first. Depends which story you believe. Me, I believe both. She wants to find it so nobody else ever can."

Elodie sat very still.

Two hundred names. Bribes. Signatures. A list of everyone whose hands had been on the rope that day, written down by the one man careful enough to keep receipts. Hidden upstream, in the country her crew had sworn off, in the country she had crossed once, twenty years ago, carrying papers she'd been told were boundary work—

*Papers,* said a small cold voice in her memory. *You carried papers, Elodie. Whose hands were yours on?*

"Rumor says," the gunman added, quieter now, watching her with those pitying eyes, "that among the signatories is a name even the rangers would know. A ranger's name. Somebody who ran the paperwork through clean, back when. Somebody invisible." He tilted his head. "Funny thing about invisible people. Paper remembers them anyway."

The wind moved through the willows. Out past the cook fire, Isandro was helping Bex tally the remaining stores, and he glanced over—just a glance, quick, the way he always checked on her without meaning to—and Elodie felt the whole shape of the next days open up in front of her like a slot canyon in flood: narrow, deep, and no way back.

"Untie him after noon," she heard herself say to Ferro, who had appeared at the edge of the lean-to with his rifle slung and his expression unreadable. "Feed him. Keep him walking with the column. If he runs, let him—we're faster than he is and hungrier."

Ferro nodded once and didn't ask. That was the crew's oldest mercy: nobody asked.

But as she turned toward the river, toward the upstream trail, toward the country she had spent twenty years not looking at, Elodie Yarrow understood—with a clarity that felt like standing at the lip of a drop—that somewhere ahead of them, in a cache above the water, a dead paymaster had written down a name.

And she was almost certain she knew whose it was.

Because she could still remember the weight of the documents in her satchel. And she had never, not once in twenty years, let herself remember what they said.
