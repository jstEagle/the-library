## 18. What Elodie Signed

Elodie had never told anyone the whole truth, and she had gotten very good at not telling it. Twenty years of practice. She could hold a secret the way Bex held a ledger—balanced to the last drop, nothing spilling.

But secrets have weight, and weight tells on you eventually. Hers told on her now, in the small hours before dawn at the upstream camp, when she sat alone by the banked fire with the paymaster's ledger open on her knees and her own name looking back at her from page forty-one.

*E. Yarrow, courier. Signature received in good order.*

She had signed it *E. Yarrow* because she'd been twenty-two years old and stupid about names, before she learned that a name was a door and every door could be kicked in. She'd signed it in ink that was probably worth more than she made in a month. She'd signed it smiling.

The ledger lay heavy as a sleeping animal. Around her the camp breathed—Ferro's soft snore from the rocks, Tamsin's bedroll empty because Tamsin was sitting up with Pell, who still woke screaming some nights. Isandro slept curled like a comma near the fire's edge, one hand under his cheek, and Elodie looked at him for a long moment and then made herself stop looking, because what she had to do next required her to look inward instead, and that was worse country.

She closed the ledger. She built the memory a fire of its own, and sat down inside it.

* * *

Twenty years ago there had been a girl called Elodie Yarrow, and she had been, by the standards of the Escarpment Rangers, a promising idiot.

She was twenty-two, third-year, all elbows and certainty, with a ranger's sash she'd sewn the badge onto herself slightly crooked and never fixed because fixing it would mean admitting the mistake. Her captain was a broad, patient woman named Odile Marchetti, whom everyone called Captain Odo behind her back and never to her face, and whom Elodie worshipped with the pure, undiscriminating devotion of a dog for a person who feeds it.

"You'll run a courier packet," Captain Odo told her one gray morning at the waystation, handing over a leather tube sealed with green wax. "Up the Anselm, through the Concession line, to the survey office at the source fork. Boundary reclassification paperwork. Routine."

"Through the Concession?" Elodie had heard of the Concession the way you hear of weather in a far country. Land upriver, company-held, closed to rangers. "I thought we didn't go in there."

"We don't." Odo's face did something complicated and then settled into tiredness. "This once we do, because the paperwork has to be carried by someone sworn, and I'm short on someones. You'll go in on the company road, you'll hand the tube to a man named Corvess at the source fork, you'll take his receipt, and you'll come home. You speak to no one. You look at nothing. Three days, in and out."

"Boundary reclassification," Elodie repeated, tasting the words. They tasted like nothing, which is the trick of them. "That's just lines on a map."

"That's just lines on a map," Odo agreed, and something in how she said it—a hair too fast, a hair too flat—should have told the girl everything. But the girl was busy being flattered. Chosen. Trusted with the sealed tube like a knight with a relic.

Here is what Elodie Yarrow understood at twenty-two: that being trusted felt better than being safe. Here is what she took another twenty years to understand: that trust can be a harness, and someone else holds the lead.

* * *

The company road into the Concession was wider than any road had a right to be out there, graded and graveled, with mileposts painted in company green. Elodie rode it feeling like a trespasser in church. The canyon walls rose around her in red shelves, and the river ran beside the road, quick and loud, and she remembered thinking—it came back to her now with the force of a slap—that she remembered thinking how beautiful it was, and how strange that no song she knew mentioned this river. The Escarpment had songs for everything. Salt flats, sandstorms, even the joke of a place called Dry Gulch where the gulch wasn't dry. But the Anselm above the falls? Nothing. As if the whole upper river had been cut out of the world's memory with scissors.

There were miners' camps along the road. That was the thing she had spent twenty years not remembering, and now, with the ledger open in front of her, she let herself remember it fully, item by item, the way Tamsin catalogued wounds.

A camp at the second milepost: tin roofs, laundry strung between carts, children—children—chasing a dog between the sluice boxes. A woman mending a net who waved at Elodie, just waved, friendly, because a rider in a ranger's sash meant the law, and the law meant safety, or so people believed who hadn't yet learned better.

A cook shack at the fourth milepost where they fed her stew without asking anything, because she was sworn and sealed and obviously official, and an old man with white dust in his eyebrows told her, laughing, "First ranger come through since the road went in. You tell your captain the water's sweet up here. Sweetest in the territory." And Elodie had smiled and said she'd pass it along, and thought no more about it than you think about a bird crossing your path.

At the sixth milepost there was a school tent, and a teacher rang a handbell as she rode past, and the children lined up outside the tent and watched her go, and one of them—a small boy with his shirt on backwards—gave her a solemn salute, and she saluted back, and that was the last time in twenty years that Elodie Yarrow had let herself think about any of them at all.

Because here is what happened at the source fork.

* * *

Corvess was waiting where the map said he'd be, a neat man in a neat coat with a clerk's hands and eyes like coins. He took the tube, weighed it in his palm, and said, "Reclassification," in a voice that savored the word.

"Boundary lines," Elodie said, proud of her errand. "Routine."

"Routine," Corvess agreed, and gave her the receipt, and she signed it—*E. Yarrow*, crooked as her badge—and rode away down that wide green-posted road past the school tent and the cook shack and the laundry lines, and she was home in three days exactly, and Captain Odo asked no questions and she offered no answers, and that should have been the second warning, because silence for its own sake is peace, but silence with a price attached is purchase.

Six weeks later the news came downriver, the way bad news travels—fast, and changing shape as it goes. Trouble at the Concession. Violence at the Concession. By the time it reached the waystation it had settled into the version everyone agreed on: miner unrest, a dispute gone wrong, regrettable, closed, sealed by magistrate's order. No names released. No inquiry. The company expressed sorrow and doubled its water claims, and the government, which owed the company more than it liked to say aloud, accepted the sorrow.

And Elodie, twenty-two years old and newly careful, put the two facts together in the dark of her bunk—the sealed tube, the dead camp—and then, with the discipline that would later make her famous, took them apart again and buried each piece separately. Because the alternative was to walk into Captain Odo's office and say *what did I carry*, and she had seen Odo's face when she returned, and she knew, the way you know a storm is coming by the ache in old scars, that Odo already knew. Knew and had sent her anyway. Knew and had chosen a third-year nobody precisely because a third-year nobody asks no questions and leaves no questions behind.

Her invisibility started there. Not as philosophy. As hiding.

* * *

The fire had burned down to red eyes. Elodie became aware of the present again slowly, the way you surface from deep water: Ferro's snore, the river's mutter, the cold coming off the stone. The ledger sat on her knees. Page forty-one. *E. Yarrow.*

She had spent twenty years telling herself a merciful story: that she'd carried paper, that paper was only paper, that whatever Corvess had done with it was beyond her knowing and therefore beyond her blame. It was a comfortable story. It had kept her warm for two decades, the way a coat keeps you warm long after you've stopped noticing you're wearing it.

But she had read the ledger now. She knew what the document had been. Not boundary lines. A reclassification of persons—the legal machinery that turned two hundred living miners, with their laundry lines and their school bell and their backwards shirts, into *unrecorded occupants*, squatters on company land, people who could be removed without the inconvenience of having ever officially existed. Her signature was on the receipt that proved delivery. Her feet were on the road those children had saluted. She had been the seal on the envelope, and the envelope had been a coffin lid.

And underneath the horror, quieter and somehow worse, was the arithmetic she couldn't escape: they had chosen her *because* she was nobody. Because a girl with a crooked badge and no family name worth stealing could carry death through a canyon and leave no trace worth following. Every skill she had built since—every trick of going unseen, every erased trail, the whole creed of *take nothing you can't carry, leave no name behind*—had grown from that first seed. She had thought she invented her code. She had been given it. Fitted for it, like a harness, by people who needed a courier who would never ask what she carried.

Isandro stirred by the fire and murmured something in his sleep—she caught the word *upstream*—and Elodie watched him with an expression she would have hated anyone to see. Because she had taken him in with the same mercy she prided herself on, and now she wondered, coldly, thoroughly, whether mercy was just another word for what the company had done to her: finding someone young enough to shape, and shaping them toward your own need. He was hers to protect. She had admitted it at the falls. But protection implied she knew which direction danger came from, and the terrible joke of her life was that the danger had always been carrying her own signature.

Tamsin appeared at the edge of the firelight, silent as her profession, and stood there a moment reading Elodie's face the way she read a wound—not asking what happened, assessing how deep.

"You haven't slept," Tamsin said.

"No."

"The boy found rail iron today. Old Concession track, half under the sand." Tamsin crouched, added a stick to the fire. Sparks climbed. "He asked me about the massacre. I gave him nothing. He'll ask you next. He always saves the hard questions for you."

"I know."

"He's going to keep digging, Elodie. Boys like him are shovels with legs." A pause, and then, gentler than Tamsin ever was before noon: "Whatever's down there—you want to be the one who shows him, not the one he finds."

Elodie almost laughed. Almost. "When did you get wise?"

"About the same time you got stubborn. We've both had practice." Tamsin rose, paused. "Dreele's pushing to move at first light. Double pace. Bex is already saying no, and Bex saying no is a geological event. You might want to be awake for it."

She melted back into the dark. Elodie stayed.

Downstream, Dreele's camp showed three fires, tidy as a garrison. Somewhere south, Sable Vane moved with hired guns and an appetite for a ledger. Somewhere ahead, the cairn field waited with its hundreds of stones, each one a person the paper in Elodie's hands had unmade. And somewhere behind her, twenty years down a road she'd ridden smiling, a small boy with his shirt on backwards was still saluting a rider who never came back.

Elodie Yarrow closed the ledger, and for the first time in twenty years she said her own full name out loud, quietly, to the fire, like testing whether a broken bone would bear weight.

"Elodie Yarrow," she said. "Courier. Complicit."

The fire took the words and gave back heat, judgment-free, which was more than she deserved and exactly what she needed. She stood, rolled her bedroll with hands that had finally stopped shaking, and turned toward the sound of raised voices at Bex's fire—because dawn was coming, and Dreele's clock with it, and the crew was about to discover that their captain had decided, after twenty years of carrying other people's paper, to start writing her own.
