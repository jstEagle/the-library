## 19. Mutiny Weather

Bex Quarle had kept the crew's books for eleven years, and in that time she had learned one truth above all others: every disaster announces itself first as an arithmetic problem.

The water was announcing it now.

She crouched at the edge of the river shallows with her ledger open on her knee, doing sums she already knew the answer to, because that was how you made sure. Twelve porters remaining after the flood and the ambush—no, eleven, since young Sefi had gone over to Dreele's fire two nights back with his cousin's promise of double wages. Eleven porters, four crew, one nervous engineer, one company man, and forty-one gallons of drinking water in skins and barrels, minus what they'd drunk this morning, minus evaporation, minus the two skins Ovid had punctured testing a charge he swore up and down was a controlled test.

Thirty-one gallons. Thirty-two mouths if you counted the mules, which Bex did, because mules were honest about their needs in a way people weren't.

At the pace Dreele wanted—the pace he'd been shouting about since dawn—they would reach the fork of the Anselm in three days. At that pace, in this heat, with this grade, they would need fifty gallons.

The gap between thirty-one and fifty was where people died.

Bex closed the ledger, which was her way of closing an argument before it started, and looked up at the sky. It was the color of old tin. The kind of sky that either broke into rain or sat on you like a lid for six days straight, and there was no telling which until it happened. Mutiny weather, Ferro called it. The kind of air that made sensible people say unsensible things.

Speaking of.

"Quarle!" Dreele's voice carried down the shingle like a thrown rock. "I asked you a question."

"You did," Bex said pleasantly, standing up and brushing sand off her knees. She was a short woman built like a flour sack, with a face that had been described by more than one caravan master as *unhelpfully calm*, and she had found over the years that pleasantness was the sharpest tool in her kit. People expected quartermasters to be dour. Pleasantness unsettled them. "And I was consulting my figures so I could give you an answer worth having."

Dreele came down the bank with his boots crunching, his collar buttoned despite the heat, because Dreele believed that a man who sweated through discipline was better than a man who stayed comfortable without it. He had a rolled survey chart under one arm like a truncheon.

"Well?"

"We can make the fork in five days," Bex said. "Comfortably. Six if the weather turns."

"I said three."

"You said three," Bex agreed. "The river says five. One of us has been listening to it longer."

* * *

The trouble, Bex reflected later—and she did reflect on it, sitting on her pack roll with the ledger open again because reflection and bookkeeping were, for her, the same activity—was that Dreele wasn't stupid. That was the whole problem. A stupid man could be managed; you just pointed him at the obvious thing and let him walk into it. But Dreele was clever in the narrow way of men who had spent their lives being paid by results, and results, in his experience, came from pressure. You leaned on the thing until it moved. If the thing didn't move, you leaned harder. Nobody had ever explained to him that porters were not crates.

He'd been leaning since first light. Faster intervals between rests. Shorter halts. And now, at midday, in the worst heat, with the tin sky pressing down like a hand on the back of the neck, he'd announced they would push through the afternoon rest entirely and make the bend below the rapids by dark.

Bex had done the arithmetic on that too. It came out to heatstroke, and she had buried enough people to know she wasn't interested in the sequel.

So here they stood, on the white shingle above the shallows, the whole expedition drawn up in a loose crescent like an audience that hadn't been told whether it was watching a play or an execution. The porters stood behind Bex—not because anyone had arranged them that way, but because feet drift toward whoever seems likeliest to feed them. Pell hovered at the edge of things with his hat in his hands, turning it like a man wringing out laundry. Elodie stood slightly apart, arms folded, saying nothing, which Bex knew from long experience meant she had decided this was Bex's fight and was prepared to enjoy it.

"Five days is unacceptable," Dreele said. "The permits expire in—" he checked a pocket watch he consulted the way other men consulted scripture, "—thirty-eight days. Every day lost upstream is a day the government spends deciding our work doesn't matter."

"Dead porters also don't matter," Bex said. "To the government, I mean. They matter considerably to me, mostly on account of them being people."

"They are contracted labor."

"They are contracted *people*, Mr. Dreele, and the contract says water at every halt, shade at noon, and no forced march above the second gorge. It's in the schedule of conditions. Article nine." Bex tapped the ledger. "You wrote your name under mine."

Something flickered across Dreele's face—the look of a man discovering that the paperwork he'd waved through in a border-town saloon had teeth in it after all. Bex treasured that look. She collected them, really. Somewhere in her mental ledger there was a column for *expressions of surprise from men who thought they'd read the fine print*, and it was the fullest column she had.

"Article nine," Dreele said slowly, "governs ordinary travel. These are extraordinary circumstances."

"Extraordinary circumstances," Bex repeated. "That's a lovely phrase. Do you know what we call extraordinary circumstances in the Escarpment?"

"I'm sure you'll tell me."

"Tuesday," Bex said. "It's always something out here. Flash floods, rockslides, bandits, rival survey parties shadowing us with rifles—which, incidentally, we've had, and I don't recall you adjusting the schedule for that either. If we stopped for every extraordinary circumstance, we'd never move, and if we ignore them all, we die. The middle path is the one with the numbers in it, and the numbers say five days."

For a moment she thought she'd won it. Dreele looked at the porters, at the tin sky, at the ledger, and Bex watched him do his own arithmetic—the company's clock against the risk of losing his labor force entirely. Clever men could be brought around, given a lever.

Then he made his mistake.

"Perhaps," he said, "the porters would prefer to hear the terms themselves. Rather than having them filtered through—" a small gesture that took in Bex, her ledger, and everything she represented, "—staff."

The crescent went very still.

Bex felt it happen, the way you feel a change in wind before the trees show it. Dreele thought he was appealing over her head to simple men who wanted wages. What he had actually done was tell eleven exhausted people, in front of witnesses, that their quartermaster's care for them was a fiction maintained by their betters.

Sefi had left for money. These ten had not.

Old Hask, the porter with the bad knee who'd been carrying loads up and down the Escarpment since before Isandro was born, spat carefully into the shallows and said, "We heard the terms. Five days."

"Three days means bonus pay," Dreele said. "Double rations of salt meat at journey's end."

"With respect, sir," said Hask, "salt meat is heavy going down and worthless coming up, and dead men can't eat either one."

A couple of the younger porters snorted, then tried to pretend they hadn't. Bex kept her face pleasant and her heart warm. Old Hask, God love him. She'd have to put an extra fig in his ration pack and never mention it.

"The decision," Dreele said, and now his voice had gone flat and cold, the voice of a man retreating into the fortress of his own authority, "is not theirs. It is not yours. This survey proceeds on my schedule. We move through the afternoon."

"No," Bex said.

It was a small word and she said it quietly, and somehow that made it land harder than any shout would have. Even the river seemed to hush between its riffles.

"Quarle," Dreele began.

"No," Bex said again, and closed the ledger. "Not the porters, not the mules, not the crew. Not me. You can march yourself through the afternoon heat if you like, Mr. Dreele, and I'll even mark your route in my book so somebody can collect what's left of you. But this party moves when the numbers say it moves, and the numbers say five days, and article nine says I'm right, and if you want to argue with article nine you're welcome to take it up with the magistrate at Corrow when we get there. I'll write you a letter of introduction. I'm very good at letters."

* * *

What happened next, Bex would remember for the rest of her life, and she would remember it not as a battle but as a settling, the way silt settles in still water after a flood.

Pell stepped forward.

The junior engineer had been hovering at the crescent's edge the whole time, wringing his hat, and Bex had privately filed him under *harmless*, along with his trembly hands and his habit of apologizing to the mules. Now he cleared his throat—a small sound, but in that silence it cracked like a whip—and said, "The quartermaster is correct."

Dreele turned on him with genuine astonishment. "Pell."

"The evaporation rates alone—" Pell's voice wobbled and then, remarkably, steadied itself, the way a boy's voice does when he finds something true to stand on. "I've been measuring. Twice daily, since First Water. Mr. Dreele, the loss figures at this temperature exceed anything in the regional surveys. If we force-march in this heat we will lose porters, and if we lose porters we lose carrying capacity, and if we lose carrying capacity the survey fails regardless of the calendar. The mathematics—" he swallowed, "—the mathematics are not on your side. I'm sorry. I should have said so days ago."

There was a pause in which Bex revised her opinion of Pell upward by several full categories, from *harmless* to *braver than he looks, which is the best kind of brave*.

Then Hask shifted his load off his shoulder, set it down on the shingle with a deliberate thump, and sat down beside it in the thin shade of the cutbank. One by one, without a word passing between them, the other porters did the same. Ten men arranging themselves on the ground with the placid immovability of stones. It was not a strike. It was not even a protest. It was simply ten people declining, collectively and calmly, to die on schedule.

"You'll be reported," Dreele said. His voice had climbed half an octave. "All of you. Breach of contract. There are penalties—"

"There's water in those barrels," Hask said comfortably, "and shade under that bank, and five days to the fork. Those are the only penalties I see today."

Bex caught Elodie's eye across the shingle. The captain gave her the smallest nod—one degree of the chin, no more—but Bex had been reading Elodie Yarrow's chin for eleven years and knew exactly what it said: *Well held.* Coming from Elodie, that was a parade with brass bands.

Ovid, meanwhile, had produced a strip of dried fish from somewhere and was eating it with the serene expression of a man at the theater. Tamsin was already moving among the seated porters with her water gourd, checking tongues and pulses, murmuring her medic's nonsense at them—*drink slow, breathe through your nose, you're built like an ox, Hask, oxen don't complain*—which was Tamsin's way of loving people, and everyone knew it, and everyone let her get away with it.

Only Ferro seemed uneasy. Bex noticed her scout up on the rocks, not watching the standoff at all but scanning downstream, back the way they'd come, with her spyglass out. When Bex caught her glance, Ferro touched two fingers to her eyes and then pointed south. *Watching. Something there.*

Later, Bex thought. There was always a later, and it was usually worse.

* * *

Dreele stormed upriver to his own tent and did not come out for two hours, during which time the tin sky finally chose rain—a brief, furious, sideways cloudburst that filled every skin and barrel to the brim and sent everyone scrambling to pitch canvas while laughing like children. The desert giveth, Bex wrote in the margin of her ledger, mostly to annoy herself. Forty-one gallons became sixty-three. The arithmetic problem dissolved overnight, which was the desert's idea of a joke: it had waited until after the mutiny to solve the crisis, so that everyone would know exactly what the crisis had really been about.

By evening, the split had become official without anyone signing anything.

Dreele announced at supper—he emerged from his tent crisp and buttoned as ever, as though the afternoon had been a minor administrative hiccup—that he would proceed ahead with a light party at speed, and that any porter wishing to earn the bonus was welcome to join him. He looked at the porters as he said it.

Hask looked back at him and chewed.

In the end, nobody moved. But the camps separated anyway, by mutual unspoken agreement, the way a bad marriage separates into two ends of the same table. Dreele's fire burned a hundred yards downstream, alone except for the mule-wrangler who drew wages too good to refuse. The crew's fire burned bright and close, and Pell sat at it—sat at *it*, the crew's fire, not the company's—with his hat finally off and a bowl of Bex's barley stew in his hands, looking astonished to be there.

"You understand," Elodie said to him mildly, across the flames, "that eating at this fire makes you ours. That's how it works. There's no appeal process."

"I—" Pell looked into his bowl as though it might contain advice. "Mr. Dreele ordered me to falsify the evaporation logs. Last week. To bring the figures down." He said it quickly, like a man diving off a ledge. "I copied them honestly instead. Both sets. I've kept both sets."

The fire popped. Downstream, Dreele's lone fire crackled its smaller, lonelier crackle.

Bex reached into her ration stores, found the last of the dried apricots she'd been hoarding against some future catastrophe, and dumped a generous handful into Pell's bowl. Some catastrophes, she figured, you could get ahead of.

"Well then," she said pleasantly. "Welcome to the numbers side."

Elodie was quiet a long moment, watching the dark water slide past. When she spoke, her voice had that low evenness Bex had learned to distrust more than any shout.

"Bex. In the morning, we take the upstream fork."

"That was always the plan, Captain."

"Yes," Elodie said. "But tomorrow we take it knowing Dreele wants us slow, Vane wants us dead, and the river—" she glanced up at the clearing sky, where stars were coming out hard and cold over the canyon rim, "—the river hasn't told us yet which one it's working for."

Bex opened her ledger to a fresh page, wrote the date, and beneath it, in her neat round hand: *Sixty-three gallons. Two camps. One fork.*

Under that, after a moment's thought, she added: *Weather breaking.*

She only hoped she meant the sky.
