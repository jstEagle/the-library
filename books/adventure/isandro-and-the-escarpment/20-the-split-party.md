## 20. The Split Party

The river forked the way rivers do in canyon country: without apology and without a map that agreed on where.

Isandro stood on the gravel bar between the two channels while the expedition came apart behind him, and he thought—not for the first time—that water was smarter than people. It didn't argue about which way to go. It just went, both ways at once, and met itself again somewhere downstream.

The argument had started at dawn and ended by midmorning, which Bex later said was fast for an argument involving Dreele. Dreele wanted speed. The permits were dying day by day, and every hour spent resting porters was an hour the company's clock ate. Bex had planted herself between Dreele and the porters' water barrels like a woman defending a doorway in a burning house, arms crossed, voice flat as a salt pan.

"Two of them can't keep fluids down," she'd said. "You move them today, you bury them tomorrow, and I don't dig for free."

"They signed on for the route," Dreele had said. "They knew it was hard."

"They signed on for wages," Bex had said. "Nobody signs up to die of thirst so a company man can make his quota."

And then Pell—nervous, ink-stained Pell, who had once apologized to Isandro for bumping into him in his own camp—had set down his instrument case beside Bex's barrels and said, quietly, "I'll stay with the rangers," and three of the porters had put their loads down too, and that was that. Expeditions split the way rivers split. Without apology.

Now Isandro counted heads, because counting heads was what Elodie had taught him first, before tracking, before reading weather, before anything. *Count what you're responsible for, boy. Everything else is decoration.*

Their party: Elodie, Bex, Ovid, Tamsin, Ferro. Himself. Pell. Porters Sef, little Maro, and old Hask, who was sixty if he was a day and had opinions about everything, most of them delivered sideways at the fire. Nine souls, eleven mules, and one very confused dog that had attached itself to the column outside the border town and refused all offers of reassignment.

Downstream, Dreele kept eight porters, twelve hired guards, and the smell of a man who had lost something and intended to take it out of the landscape.

* * *

"You're staring," Ferro said, materializing at Isandro's shoulder the way Ferro did, soundless as a shadow with a rifle.

"I'm counting."

"Mm." Ferro chewed on a stalk of something. "And?"

"And we're nine against twenty, and they have more guns, but we have the high channel, and Elodie says the high channel is worth ten men."

"Elodie says a lot of things." Ferro spat the stalk out. "She also said this job would be simple escort work, and look how that's going."

Isandro almost smiled. Almost. Then he looked downstream again, to where the boy-porters were breaking camp under Dreele's overseer, moving too fast, the way people move when someone is watching them with a whip in his voice instead of a hand. Maro was fourteen. Isandro was sixteen. The difference felt enormous from up here and probably looked like nothing at all from down there.

"Go talk to them," Ferro said.

"Talk to who?"

"Don't do that. You've been doing that all morning—the thing where you look at those boys like you're carrying them and then pretend your hands are empty." Ferro shrugged. "I did the same thing my first year. It doesn't fool anybody. Go."

Isandro went.

Maro was coiling a rope badly, the kind of bad that gets people killed on scree, and Isandro stopped himself twice from reaching over and fixing it, because that wasn't the job anymore, apparently. The job was standing next to him until he noticed.

"That's a granny knot," Isandro said finally.

"It holds."

"It holds until it doesn't, and then it holds nothing." He knelt, took the rope, showed him. Over, under, through. "Like this. My captain made me redo it forty times in a row until I could do it asleep. I hated her for a week."

"Which one's your captain? The tall one?"

"The tall one."

"She looks like she bites."

"She does. But only when it helps." Isandro handed the rope back. "Where are they taking you?"

Maro glanced downstream, then at his own boots. "Faster," he said. "That's all anyone will tell us. Faster, faster. Old Hask says the last crew that went upstream with Dreele's company came back short two men and nobody asked questions." He paused. "Sef says you lot are different. That you buried the two who drowned proper, with stones, even though they weren't yours."

"They were ours," Isandro said, before he thought about it. "Once they walked with us, they were ours."

Maro looked at him then, really looked, the way a boy looks when he's deciding whether to trust somebody with his life or just his luggage. Whatever he saw, he nodded once, slow, and went back to his rope, and tied it right this time.

Isandro walked back up the gravel bar feeling like he'd been handed something heavy without being told its weight.

* * *

Elodie had claimed a flat shelf of rock above the fork and was doing the thing she did when the world got complicated: drawing lines in the dirt with a stick. Isandro had learned to read these maps the way other boys read letters home. Lines meant routes. Circles meant water. X's meant trouble, and there were a great many X's.

"We take the north channel," she said without looking up. "It's slower but it has springs. Dreele takes the south because it's faster and it has none, and he'll drive his porters dry rather than admit it. In six days we'll be above him and rested. In seven, if I'm wrong, we'll be dead, so let's hope I'm not."

"You're never wrong," Isandro said.

"I'm wrong constantly. I'm just wrong in private." She glanced up at him then, and her eyes did the thing they sometimes did—measured him, weighed him, found him heavier than yesterday. "Ferro says you talked to the porters."

"Some of them."

"Pell tells me three of them want to come with us instead. Permanently. Not just as far as the springs." She laid the stick down. "That's not a small thing, Isandro. Men who leave a company man mid-contract get remembered. There are places in this territory where that memory follows you like flies."

"They're not leaving because of the contract. They're leaving because of the graves." He heard his own voice come out harder than he meant. "We buried two of theirs under stones with no names, and Dreele wrote them off as cargo losses before Tamsin finished the cairn. Maro watched that happen. They all did."

Elodie was quiet a long moment. Down at the fork, the south channel ran brown and quick, hurrying toward whatever Dreele was hurrying toward.

"When did you start speaking for the crew?" she asked.

Isandro opened his mouth to apologize—he'd been apologizing for existing since the salt flats, it was practically his native language—and then closed it again, because something in her tone wasn't a rebuke. It was a question. A real one.

"I don't know," he said honestly. "Somewhere between the flood and the ambush. Somewhere around the time I realized nobody else was saying it."

Elodie nodded slowly, and picked the stick back up, and drew a new circle on the map. "Then listen. Speaking for people is a job, same as any other, and it has rules. Rule one: only speak what you know is true. Rule two: when you don't know, say so louder than when you do. Rule three—" she tapped the circle, which sat at the head of the north channel, where the canyon walls pinched together like a held breath— "never lead people somewhere you wouldn't go alone."

"What's the circle?"

"A spring. If it's still running." She stood, brushed off her knees. "Get Pell. Tell him we move within the hour, and tell him if he brings that ridiculous brass theodolite, Ovid gets to carry it, and Ovid will complain the entire way."

"He already complains the entire way."

"Yes, but currently it's about the dog."

* * *

They broke camp in the white heat of noon, which Tamsin called foolish and Elodie called tactical, and which Isandro privately understood to mean *so Dreele can see exactly how few of us there are and exactly how unbothered we look.* The nine of them and the eleven mules and the dog took the north channel single file, climbing away from the fork, and Isandro made himself not look back.

He looked back anyway. Once.

Dreele's camp was a scatter of canvas and armed men on the far bank, and among the porters loading up, one small figure raised a hand—not waving, just raised, the way you raise a hand to show you're still there, still watching, still keeping count on your side of the river too.

Isandro raised his own hand. Then he climbed.

* * *

The north channel narrowed through the afternoon until the walls pressed close enough that the mules' packs scraped stone on both sides, and the light came down in ribbons, and the air smelled of wet rock and something green, which in canyon country was the smell of a promise.

Ovid walked beside Isandro for a while, which Ovid rarely did; Ovid generally preferred the company of explosives, which didn't ask questions.

"You handled the boy well," Ovid said.

"Maro?"

"The knot. And the question after it. *Where are they taking you.* That's the only question that matters to a porter, and most people never think to ask it." Ovid adjusted the strap of his satchel, which clinked in a way Isandro tried very hard not to think about. "When I was your age, I asked everyone the wrong questions. Loud ones. With powder."

"What happened?"

"I got very good at running." He smiled, thin and genuine. "The point is, Elodie's been watching you since the flash flood. Watching how you pulled porters out of the water. How you sat with Tamsin at the burial. How you didn't sleep, but didn't complain either. She won't say it—she'd sooner eat her own hat—but you've stopped being an apprentice in her ledger. You're staff now. Staff gets blamed when things go wrong."

"That sounds terrible."

"It's the best thing there is," Ovid said. "Blame means you matter. Ask the dog."

The dog, trotting ahead, looked back at the sound of its name and wagged its entire rear half.

* * *

They made the spring by late light, and it was running—cold, clear, pooling in a basin of pink stone before spilling over into the channel. Bex tasted it, pronounced it honest, and began filling skins with the contentment of a woman whose religion was inventory. Tamsin checked the dehydrated porters, fed them sips and salt, and declared that nobody would die tonight, which from Tamsin was practically a hymn.

Supper was beans and flatbread and Hask's opinions, which tonight ran to the subject of mules.

"That gray one," Hask said, pointing with his spoon, "is possessed. I've known possessed mules. This one's worse. Possessed mules at least fear God."

"The gray one carried double load for two days after the flood," Isandro said.

"Then she's possessed *and* a martyr, which is the worst combination there is."

Laughter went around the fire, real laughter, the first in days, and Isandro sat in the middle of it and felt the strange weight settle onto his shoulders properly for the first time—not dropped on him, but taken up. Maro and Sef weren't here yet, but they would be. He'd seen it in the raised hand. And when they came, they'd come looking to him, the boy-porter who'd become a ranger's apprentice who'd become—whatever this was. Somebody who spoke. Somebody who counted heads.

He found Elodie at the edge of the firelight, standing watch on the rocks, and sat beside her without asking, which a month ago would have earned him a look and now earned him nothing at all, which was its own kind of promotion.

"Dreele will send someone after us," he said. "Not guns. A bargain. He came alone to offer triple pay once already."

"You remember that."

"I remember everything lately. It's exhausting."

Elodie huffed something that was almost a laugh. "Yes. He'll try. Probably tomorrow, probably with honey instead of vinegar, because the vinegar failed." She turned her head and studied him. "If he comes to you instead of me—if he tries the porters, or the boy-engineer—what do you do?"

Isandro thought about it seriously, because she'd given him rule one: only speak what you know is true.

"I listen," he said. "All the way to the end. Because if I interrupt, I learn nothing, and if I learn nothing, I can't warn anyone. And then I say no. But politely." He paused. "Bex says politeness confuses company men. They think it's a trick."

"Bex," said Elodie, "is occasionally wise, which she'd deny under oath."

Below them, the spring spilled its cold thread into the darkening canyon, and the north channel carried it away toward the fork, toward the south channel, toward the sea, gathering everything as it went.

Isandro slept that night with his boots on and the rope Maro had tied—badly, then well—coiled at the head of his bedroll, and dreamed of water splitting two ways and meeting again downstream.

He woke before dawn to Ferro's hand on his shoulder and Ferro's voice low and flat as slate:

"Up. We've got tracks. Fresh ones, coming up the north channel behind us—one man, walking soft, and Isandro—" Ferro's eyes in the grey light were grim as a closed door. "He's not following our trail. He's following *yours.*"
