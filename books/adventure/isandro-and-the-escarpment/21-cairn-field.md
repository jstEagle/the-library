## 21. Cairn Field

Tamsin had stitched up gunshots, snakebites, a man who'd been struck by lightning twice and lived to complain about it both times, but nothing in twenty years of ranger medicine had prepared her for the sight of the valley floor at first light.

Stones. Hundreds of them. Thousands.

They rose out of the river grass like teeth in an old jaw—cairns, each one the height of a kneeling man, each one stacked by hands that had been in a hurry or in grief or both. They followed the curve of the water for nearly half a mile, thickest near the bend where the current ran slow, thinning upstream as though whoever had done the burying had simply run out of strength, or out of bodies, or out of time.

"Stop," she said, and her voice came out wrong, so she said it again. "Stop. Everyone stop."

The porters halted in a ragged line behind her. Isandro came up beside her, and she felt him go still in that particular way of his—the way he went still when he was memorizing something.

"What is it?" he asked.

"You know what it is." She didn't mean to be sharp with him. She was sharp anyway. "You've known since the rail."

He had, of course. He'd found the mining cart rail three weeks back, half-swallowed by sand, and asked his question about the massacre to her face and gotten silence in return, and he hadn't asked again. That was the boy's gift: he could wait like the desert waited. She'd told herself the silence was mercy. Standing here now, looking at what the silence had been hiding, she understood it had been cowardice wearing mercy's coat.

Elodie came forward past the line of porters, Ferro at her shoulder with his rifle slung and his eyes already working the ridgelines out of habit. Elodie stopped when she saw it. Tamsin watched her captain's face do something complicated and then do nothing at all, which was worse.

"Well," Elodie said quietly. "There it is."

"There it is," Tamsin agreed.

* * *

They made camp well back from the field, on a shelf of ground above the river, because it seemed wrong to pitch tents among graves no matter how exhausted everyone was. Bex got the cookfire going with her usual economy—three sticks, one match, no wasted motion—and Ovid wandered off alone along the shelf's edge, which was what Ovid did when his feelings got too large for his body. He'd come back later and say something dry and sideways and true, and nobody would acknowledge it, and that would be how they all survived the evening.

Tamsin did not sit by the fire. She took the stolen map from inside her shirt where she'd been carrying it against her skin since the Narrows, since Ovid had pressed it into her hands with a look that said *you're the one who should hold this*, and she walked down alone into the cairn field with Pell's borrowed survey notebook and a stub of pencil.

The map was Dreele's route map, lifted from his saddlebag weeks ago, and it had seemed like quiet horror then—marked sites that weren't geological, little symbols in a company clerk's neat hand. A triangle for a burned homestead. A crosshatch for a mass grave. At the time, in the lamplight of their camp, the symbols had been abstract. Evidence. Something to argue about.

Up close, with the morning wind moving through the grass and the stones standing patient in their hundreds, abstraction died fast.

She began at the downstream end and worked upstream, counting.

The first cairn had been built well. River stones, flat ones, fitted tight. Someone had cared about this one. The second was sloppier. By the tenth they were rough heaps. By the fiftieth they were barely more than piles, stones dropped rather than placed, and Tamsin found herself doing the arithmetic she hated herself for doing: *how many diggers, how many dead, how many hours of daylight.* Twenty years ago. Late summer, if the old stories were right, though there were almost no old stories, which was rather the entire point.

Two hundred and six.

She counted twice because the first time she assumed she'd miscounted, double-tapped a cairn somewhere, counted a shadow as stone. Two hundred and six. The map's crosshatch symbol sat in the margin of this exact bend of the river with a notation beside it in Dreele's clerk-hand: *est. 190–210, verify.*

Verify. As in confirm. As in check the count before the paperwork goes through and the land changes hands and someone brings in a demolition crew to turn two hundred and six graves into gravel for a dam footing.

Tamsin sat down on the grass between cairns forty-one and forty-two and let herself shake for exactly one minute, because she was a professional and professionals budgeted their grief. Then she wiped her face on her sleeve and went back to work.

Because here was the thing she'd realized somewhere around cairn sixty, the thing that had curdled her solemnity into something harder and more useful: *this is why we were hired.*

Not to survey. Not to escort. To let these stay anonymous. Dreele's employers hadn't sent them upriver despite the graves—they'd sent them *because* of the graves. A survey team walking the canyon gave the company's map legitimacy. Every measurement Pell took, every benchmark set, would become part of a record that showed productive, empty land awaiting development. Empty. The word doing all the work, the way it always did. Two hundred and six people, erased in advance, and she and hers were supposed to be the eraser.

Well. She'd been carrying other people's secrets for twenty years. She knew exactly how heavy an eraser got.

* * *

Isandro found her at midday. She heard him coming—she always heard him coming; the boy moved like a herd of goats having an argument—and she didn't look up from her notes.

"How many?" he asked. No preamble. He'd earned the right to skip preambles about a week after she'd taken him on, though he rarely used it.

"Two hundred and six."

He was quiet for a while. He crouched down beside the nearest cairn and, very carefully, touched the topmost stone with two fingers, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal.

"Somebody built these," he said.

"Somebody did. At the start, anyway." She tapped her pencil against the notebook. "The early ones are careful work. Fitted stones. Some of them have a larger rock set on top, like a marker. By the end—" She gestured upstream. "By the end they're just piles. Whoever was doing the burying was running out of everything. Strength. Time. Maybe hope."

"Do you think they were survivors? Of the town?"

"I think they were whoever was left," Tamsin said. "That's usually who does the burying."

Isandro nodded slowly. Then he said, in the too-careful voice he used when he was about to ask something he'd been carrying a long time: "Tamsin. You were there. Weren't you. Not at the massacre—you'd have told me that much—but after. When the crew was founded."

The wind moved through the grass. Somewhere upstream, a bird made an idiotic cheerful noise, entirely indifferent to history.

"Yes," she said. Because he'd waited years to ask, and because the cairns had voted, in their silent stony majority, that the era of not-answering was over. "Ovid and I and Elodie and Bex—we carried the paperwork. Not the guns. The papers. Reclassification documents, boundary surveys, a death register with causes listed as 'fever' and 'cave-in.' We thought we were clerks. We told ourselves we were clerks for a long time." She looked at him. "Then we read what we'd been carrying, and we ran, and we swore off names because names were what had gotten those people killed. Men with names in ledgers decided two hundred and six human beings were a line item. So we became people with no names, and we told ourselves that was virtue."

"And was it?"

"It was anesthesia," Tamsin said. "It kept us from hurting. It did nothing at all for them." She nodded at the field. "Look at them, Isandro. Twenty years and nobody's even pulled the weeds. Nobody's spoken a single one of their names, because most of them don't *have* names anymore—not written down anywhere the law would recognize. The paymaster's ledger might change that. If it exists. If it's intact. If we live long enough to use it."

"That's a lot of ifs."

"We're rangers. Ifs are our native habitat."

That got the ghost of a smile out of him, which had been the intent. Then his smile faded and he looked down the length of the field, and Tamsin watched him arrive at the same conclusion she had, watch it land on him like weather.

"They sent us here to make this disappear," he said.

"They did."

"So everything we've done since the fork—every day we've spent cataloguing instead of measuring—we've been working against our own contract."

"Every hour," Tamsin agreed. "And Dreele knows it. Which means the question isn't whether he acts. It's when, and how many guns he has left."

* * *

She spent the afternoon finishing the catalogue, and the crew drifted down in ones and twos as the light went long and gold, and something happened among the cairns that none of them planned and none of them spoke about afterward.

Bex came down first, ostensibly to bring Tamsin water, and stood for a while with her arms crossed, reading the field the way she read a ledger—row by row, total at the bottom. Then, without a word, she picked up a loose stone from the riverbank and set it on top of the nearest cairn, the one whose stack had slumped. A small repair. One stone.

Ferro came next and pretended he was checking sightlines, which fooled nobody, and added two stones to a collapsed heap near the water.

Ovid arrived last, carrying nothing, saying less. He walked the rows slowly with his hands in his pockets. Then he stopped at a small cairn near the upstream end—one of the careful early ones, with the marker stone on top, the kind that might belong to a child or a small-statured adult, and Tamsin saw his shoulders change—and he stood there for a long time. When he turned around his face was its usual dry mask, but his voice wasn't quite level when he said:

"Whoever stacked this one was showing off. Fitted stones, proper courses. Mason's training, maybe. Somebody's father taught somebody a trade, and somebody used it to lay a stranger to rest decently." He cleared his throat. "I'm just saying. People are better than their paperwork."

Nobody answered that, because there wasn't anything to add, and because Ovid's feelings were like his charges: best approached slowly and never directly.

Elodie came down at dusk. She walked the whole length of the field alone, and Tamsin, packing up her notes, watched her captain stop at intervals and stand with her head bowed, and understood that Elodie was doing her own accounting—the private, merciless kind, the kind where the ledger being balanced was her own complicity. Whatever document young Elodie Yarrow had carried through this canyon twenty years ago, its fingerprints were on every stone in this valley.

When Elodie reached the downstream end she bent, picked up a stone, and placed it. Then another. Then she straightened and called up the slope, her voice carrying clean across the water:

"Tamsin. How many?"

"Two hundred and six."

"Two hundred and six," Elodie repeated, loud, as if the number needed to be said aloud to be real—as if speech itself were the first act of putting things back. "Hear that? You're counted. You're *counted.*"

The wind took her voice away up the canyon. Nothing answered. But Tamsin felt something shift in her chest anyway, some load redistributing, and she suspected she wasn't the only one.

* * *

They gathered at the fire after dark, and for once nobody suggested cards or songs or Ovid's theory that the moon was a government installation. Bex poured out the tea ration without anyone asking, which meant the occasion was formal.

"We need to talk about what happens next," Elodie said. "Properly. All of us."

"Dreele's camp is four miles downstream," Ferro reported. "Cookfires lit early. More guards posted than yesterday. And they've got the porters digging at the homestead site—I could hear shovels."

"Burning the past ahead of schedule," Ovid murmured. "Efficient."

"Pell?"

"Still with us. He came up at noon, brought the theodolite 'for calibration.'" Isandro's mouth twitched. "He also brought a list. Names. His grandmother's family worked the Concession mines before the massacre—uncles, cousins. He wrote down everyone he could remember. Said if we ever find a ledger, we should look for them."

Tamsin felt her throat tighten. The nervous junior engineer, the one they'd all privately filed under *liability*, had been keeping his own counter-survey the whole time. Names against names. Memory against paperwork.

"He's braver than any of us gave him credit for," she said.

"He's terrified," Ferro said. "Brave and terrified aren't opposites. I checked."

A few tired huffs of laughter around the fire. Even Elodie smiled briefly. Then the smile went away and she leaned forward, elbows on knees, firelight carving her face into planes and shadows.

"Here's where we stand. In seventy-three days the permits lapse and the Concession reverts to the crown. Until then, Dreele's employers intend to erase every trace that anyone ever lived or died here, and we are contractually obligated to help them do it. We are not going to help them do it." She let that sit. "Which makes us, technically, in breach. Which means from this moment, everything we do upriver, we do as outlaws. Again. Only this time—" she glanced around the fire, at Bex and Ovid and Tamsin and Ferro, at Isandro sitting very straight, at the dark shape of the grave field beyond, "—this time I'd like to think we're breaking faith with the right people."

"The ledger," Bex said. "If it exists, it's upstream. The map's last symbols point above the source spring. Paymaster's cache, if Ovid's reading is right."

"My reading is right," Ovid said. "My readings are always right. It's my personality that's questionable."

"Then we go up," Elodie said. "Tomorrow. Catalogue finished, evidence secured, witnesses willing. We find that cave, we find that ledger, and we get it downstream to a magistrate before Dreele or Vane or anybody's employers can stop us." She paused. "Questions?"

One question, as it happened, and it came from the youngest person at the fire, and it was the only question that mattered.

"If the ledger has names," Isandro said slowly, "and some of those names signed the orders—" he didn't look at Elodie, but everyone else did, briefly, and away again, quick as a flinch, "—what happens to the people whose names are in it? All of them?"

Silence. The fire popped. Down in the dark, the river kept talking to itself, the way rivers do, patient as a witness.

"We'll deal with that when we can read it," Elodie said at last, evenly, and Tamsin heard the door closing gently behind the words. "Get some sleep. First watch is Ferro's."

The crew broke apart to their bedrolls. But Tamsin stayed up a while longer, sitting at the edge of the firelight with the map on her knees, and it was there, in the grey hour before her own watch, that Ferro's whistle came soft and urgent from the ridge.

She climbed up to him. He handed her the glass without a word and pointed downstream.

Four miles off, at the edge of Dreele's camp, a single rider was saddling up in the dark. Small, quick, riding light. The rider turned once at the edge of the firelight—a woman's silhouette, hat brim catching starlight—and then put her horse down the river trail at a pace that meant business.

Tamsin lowered the glass. Her mouth had gone dry.

"Ferro," she said. "Go wake the captain. Sable Vane just left Dreele's camp alone, and she's headed straight for us."
