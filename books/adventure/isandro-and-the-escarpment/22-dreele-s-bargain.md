## 22. Dreele's Bargain

He came up the river alone, which was the first interesting thing about him.

Ferro spotted him an hour before he arrived—a single figure walking the gravel bars with his hands visible and empty, moving at the unhurried pace of a man who wanted very much to be seen as harmless. Ferro relayed it back to camp in the clipped hand-signals they used when porters might be watching: *one man, alone, no pack, walking like a wedding guest.*

"Wedding guest," Bex repeated, squinting downriver through the spyglass. "That's generous. He walks like a tax collector arriving early."

Elodie took the glass. Dreele indeed—dust-caked boots, sweat-dark collar, but the jacket still buttoned to the throat because some men would rather cook than look rumpled. No escort. Which meant either he trusted them, or he'd left his guns somewhere close enough to matter, or—and this was the option Elodie liked least—he genuinely didn't think he needed any.

"He's coming to buy us," she said.

"With what? We've got his pay already."

"No." She handed the glass back. "We've got a *down payment* of his pay. There's a difference, and men like him keep the difference in a locked box."

She made her preparations the way she made all her preparations: slowly, and with tea. Tamsin watched her measure out the leaves and said nothing, which from Tamsin was a paragraph. Ovid checked the perimeter twice and then came and sat on the far side of the fire, whittling something that had started life as a spoon and was showing signs of becoming a small aggressive bird.

"You want us in earshot?" Ovid asked.

"I want you in earshot," Elodie confirmed. "Bex behind me counting his pauses. Ovid where he can see the riverbank. If anyone else comes up that gravel, I want to know before he does."

"And if he offers you something insulting?"

"Then I'll be insulted quietly and we'll all learn something."

Isandro was there too, of course, because Isandro was always there now, hovering at the edge of things with that expression he thought was neutral and wasn't—it was a boy doing arithmetic with his face. Elodie caught his eye and gave him the smallest shake of the head. *Not this one.* He frowned but went to sit with Pell, who was teaching him to sketch contour lines badly, which was at least keeping both their hands busy.

Dreele arrived at midmorning, when the light was flat and honest, and stopped at the edge of camp exactly as far away as politeness required and no farther.

"Captain Yarrow."

"Mr. Dreele. You walked."

"I did. It seemed wise."

"It usually is, when you're outnumbered nine to one and your host has a demolitions expert." She gestured at the fire ring. "Sit. Tea?"

"I don't drink tea."

"More for me." She poured hers anyway, unhurried, letting him stand a moment longer than comfort allowed. It was an old trick and he knew it was an old trick, and knowing didn't help him, which was the beauty of old tricks.

He sat. Up close he looked worse than he had at the hiring—the desert had gotten into the seams of him, sand in the creases of his neck, sun scald across his knuckles. But his eyes were the same: hard little ledgers, always totaling.

"You've made good time upstream," he said.

"We've made careful time. Good time got two of your porters killed."

Something flickered behind the ledger-eyes—not grief, she noted, but recalculation. "The flood was an act of God."

"The flood was an act of weather. The deaths were an act of schedule. You pushed us into that slot canyon a day after I told you the foam was green."

"I pushed us according to the survey timeline, which you contracted to meet."

"I contracted to meet it *if the country allowed.* Read your own paper sometime, Mr. Dreele. Clause eleven. My handwriting, my insistence, and the only reason you're drinking—" she lifted her cup "—well. Not drinking. Sitting. With all your teeth."

Bex, behind her, made a sound that in a less disciplined woman would have been a laugh.

Dreele let the silence sit, then leaned forward, elbows on knees, and dropped the charm entirely. That was the thing about company men—they could shed manners like a snake sheds skin, and underneath was always the same animal.

"Triple pay," he said. "For everyone. Back-pay doubled, plus a completion bonus, paid the day you turn the party around. And amnesty."

"Amnesty." Elodie sipped her tea. "How generous. Amnesty from what?"

"From whatever comes next." He said it plainly, no threat in the tone, which somehow made it worse. "The permits lapse in under three weeks. When they do, this land reverts to the crown, and my employers intend to have certain... documentation matters settled before that happens. Your crew has seen things. Maps. Sites. A grave field, Captain—I know you found the cairns, because Pell writes letters home and one of my porters reads over shoulders. What you've seen can be managed two ways. One way costs me money. The other way costs me something else."

"Something else being?"

"Being managed by people who aren't me." He spread his hands. "I'm offering you the cheap version. Triple pay, amnesty, and you walk downstream rich and ignorant, and nobody ever thinks about you again. That's not a threat, Yarrow. That's arithmetic. I've done the sums on your crew—you're worth more bought than buried, so I'm buying. Take the deal while I'm still the reasonable one."

* * *

Elodie refilled her cup. This was the part she'd been waiting for, and she intended to enjoy it the way she enjoyed all bitter medicines: slowly, and with sugar.

"Let me tell you what I heard," she said. "You heard it too, everyone—correct me if I'm wrong." She ticked points against her fingers. "One: triple pay, which tells me my silence is worth at least triple, which tells me what's upstream is worth considerably more than that. Two: amnesty, which tells me there's something to be amnestied *from*, which means your employers already consider us criminals for having eyes. Three: 'nobody ever thinks about you again,' which is the single most expensive promise in the world, because the only way to guarantee it is to make sure there's nobody left to think."

"That's a creative reading."

"I'm a creative woman. It's why I'm alive." She set down the cup. "Here's my counter-offer, Mr. Dreele, and I'll make it once. Full pay as contracted—we finish the survey, all of it, every site on your map, including the ones that aren't geological. Your porters go home safe. Pell goes home safe. And when we're done, you take your documents and your deadlines and you explain to whoever holds your leash why the quiet version failed."

Dreele stared at her. Then, remarkably, he smiled—a thin, genuine thing, almost admiring. "You're going to ask for more money. Everyone asks for more money eventually. Pride is just price discovery with better posture."

"Is that what they teach at the company school?"

"They teach that everybody sells. The only question is the currency." He stood, brushing off his trousers with brisk little strokes, a man reassembling himself. "Triple stands until Friday. After that my employers revise their arithmetic, and I won't be able to protect you from the revision. Think about what your crew eats when the pay stops, Captain. Loyalty is lovely, but it doesn't grind flour."

He turned to go, then paused. "The boy. Isandro. He's young to be carrying other people's sins."

Elodie went very still. "He carries his own. That's quite enough for anyone."

"Mm." Dreele walked back down the gravel bar, hands still visible, still empty, still somehow the most threatening thing on the river.

* * *

They waited until he was well out of sight before anyone spoke, and even then it was Bex, low: "Friday."

"He's bluffing about the deadline," Ovid said. "Probably."

"He's not bluffing about the arithmetic." Elodie stood and stretched, working the stiffness out of her shoulders. "He told us everything we needed, wrapped in an insult. They're on a clock tighter than the permits. Men don't offer triple pay unless Friday is real."

"So we run for the source," Ferro said from the rocks above, where she'd been listening to every word, because of course she had.

"We do nothing so obvious." Elodie held up a hand and counted again, slower this time. "We break camp tomorrow morning, yes—but we break it *loudly*. Porters grumbling, gear repacked wrong, a very audible argument between myself and Bex about whether the bonus is worth it. Let the desert hear us turning around."

Tamsin raised an eyebrow. "And then?"

"Then we move at night, light, fast, and leave Dreele's spies a cold camp and a false trail heading downstream. Ovid—" she glanced at the whittled bird "—how do you feel about making our mule tracks look like eight people leaving and four people returning?"

Ovid examined the bird. "I feel like it's the best use of my talents since the bridge."

"Pell stays with us. He doesn't get a vote; he gets a choice, and I suspect I know which way he'll jump. The porters who want to go home go home with full pay and my thanks, and Ferro picks the route so they pass nowhere near anything important." She looked around the fire ring, at each of them in turn. "Dreele thinks greed moves us. Fine. Greed is the one lie he'll believe without checking, because it's the only motive he has himself. So we let him believe it right up until the moment it stops being useful."

"And after?" Isandro asked quietly, from the edge of the light. He hadn't gone back to Pell's lesson after all. "After the source. After the ledger, if it exists. What does greed buy then?"

Elodie met his eyes. Somewhere along this river the boy had stopped asking questions like an apprentice and started asking them like a ranger, and she hadn't decided yet whether to be proud or afraid.

"After," she said, "we stop needing to be invisible. One way or another."

Bex snorted softly. "You say that like it's simple."

"No," Elodie said, and picked up her tea, which had gone cold, and drank it anyway, because waste was a sin out here. "I say it like it's inevitable. There's a difference, and men like Dreele keep the difference in a locked box—one we're going to open."

Above the river, far downstream, a dust plume rose where no dust plume should be—too big for one man, too fast for porters.

Ferro saw it first. She didn't say anything. She just looked at Elodie, and pointed, and began, very quietly, to count.
