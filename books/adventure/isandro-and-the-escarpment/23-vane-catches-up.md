## 23. Vane Catches Up

The cairn field at dusk looked like a congregation that had been waiting twenty years for someone to say amen.

Elodie stood at its edge while Tamsin moved among the stones with her ledger copy and a stub of pencil, cataloguing. Isandro was helping Pell set the cook fire downwind of the graves, which showed the boy had learned something after all—grief deserved smoke in another direction. The porters had gone quiet in the way people go quiet around dead things they've only just understood.

Ferro whistled twice from the ridge. Low notes. *One rider.*

"One," Elodie said to Bex, who was already at her shoulder with the field glasses.

"Alone." Bex handed them over. "No rifle slung. Hands visible."

"Vane doesn't need a rifle slung. She needs everyone else's."

They watched the rider come down the long slope without hurry, horse picking its way through the stones as if it too knew what ground this was. Sable Vane wore no company colors. She wore dust and good leather and the particular stillness of a woman who had decided something on the ride over and did not intend to renegotiate it.

"She'll want to talk," Bex said.

"She always wants to talk. Talking is how she loads the gun."

"What do you want us to do?"

Elodie lowered the glasses. Downstream, out of sight beyond the bend, Vane's hired guns were somewhere—Ferro had counted six fires two nights back, then four, then nothing, which was worse than any number. And their own camp held three porters too slow to run, Pell, and a boy who thought he was a ranger.

"Nobody moves," Elodie said. "Whatever you hear. Nobody moves unless I say the word *salt*."

Bex nodded once. "And if she says something that makes you want to shoot her?"

"Then I'll want it very quietly."

* * *

Vane stopped ten paces out and dismounted, which was either courtesy or theater, and with Sable it had never once been possible to tell the difference. She was grayer at the temples than Elodie remembered. That was the only soft thing about her.

"You buried two porters last week," Vane said. No greeting. "I heard about the flood. I'm sorry for it."

"No, you're not."

"I'm sorry it happened on your watch instead of mine. There's a difference, and it matters to me, even if it shouldn't."

Elodie said nothing. Around them the light went the color of old brass. Somewhere behind the nearest cairns, she knew, Ferro had a crossbow trained on a spot of empty air, waiting for the second party that had to be there—the one Vane would never travel without.

"They're not here," Vane said, reading her. "My guns. Not yet."

"*Yet.*"

"I sent them wide this morning. Upstream, past your pickets. They'll be in your camp by now or very near it." Vane's voice didn't rise at all. "Your porters, your engineer, your boy. All of them standing in a circle of my rifles while you and I have this conversation like civilized women."

The cold that went through Elodie wasn't fear. It was older than fear. It was the specific chill of recognizing a move because you'd once been the kind of person who made it.

"You're holding children hostage to talk to me about a ledger."

"I'm holding adults accountable for a choice you all made when you signed Vane's contract. You read the terms. Everyone reads the terms. Nobody ever believes the terms are real until the terms arrive." Vane took off one glove, finger by finger. "I told Dreele you'd figure out what the survey was for by the cairns. He didn't believe me. He thinks everyone can be managed with money. Money manages most people. It just has never once managed you, and that is the entire problem between us, Elodie, and it has been the entire problem for twenty years."

"Say it, then. Whatever it is. Say it out here where the dead can hear you, since apparently we're doing honesty."

Vane smiled, and it was the worst smile Elodie had ever seen on her—because it was genuine.

* * *

"We ran from the same burning building," Vane said. "That's the thing nobody knows. That's the thing I have carried alone so long I've started talking to horses about it."

She walked a few steps along the first row of cairns, reading the stones the way Tamsin read them—not counting, just acknowledging.

"The night of the massacre, I was nineteen. A clerk. Paymaster Hobb's clerk, if you want the whole ugly genealogy. I kept his books, I sharpened his pens, and I told myself every week that I was just a girl who wrote numbers, that numbers were clean, that whatever the numbers were *for* happened in rooms I wasn't in." She turned. "You were a courier. Nineteen, twenty. You carried a sealed document through the Concession and you didn't ask what it was, because asking questions gets couriers searched, and searched couriers get replaced."

"How do you know what I was?"

"Because Hobb briefed us together. One evening, in the office at Corrow, before either of us understood anything. He sat us down with tea and told us the reclassification papers had to move fast, that the survey boundary was shifting, that speed mattered more than scrutiny. And he looked at me and said, *the girl checks the arithmetic*, and he looked at you and said, *the ranger asks nothing*. Two instruments. He introduced us and I remember thinking—" Vane stopped. For one unguarded moment something moved through her face that wasn't performance at all. "I remember thinking I'd like to know you. Under other circumstances. In some other life where neither of us worked for a man who paid for graves."

Elodie's mouth was dry. "I never met you before the border towns. Before the saloons and the rival contracts."

"Because three weeks later there were two hundred dead men up this river, and Hobb burned the office, and I took the ledger copies I could carry and I ran south, and you took your clean hands and your clean name and you built a crew of people who don't exist." Vane spread her arms, encompassing the grave field, the river, the dark coming down. "We both ran, Elodie. Don't you dare sit there on your high horse of scruples and pretend we didn't. The difference—the *only* difference—is what we did with the running."

"Go on."

"You ran into invisibility. Off every map. Take nothing you can't carry, leave no name behind—I've heard your code quoted in three territories, usually by people who learned it secondhand and think it's poetry. It isn't poetry. It's a bunker. You buried yourself so deep that no one could ever hand you a paper again, and you called it virtue." Vane's voice stayed level, but something under it had begun to burn. "I ran into the opposite direction. Into the companies. Into the ledgers. I spent twenty years making myself indispensable to men like Dreele so that someday, *someday*, I'd be standing close enough to the fire to control what it burned. Every contract I brokered, every concession I greased, every dirty job I took—it was all one long walk back toward this river. Toward Hobb's ledger. Because my signature is in it. Page nine. Payment received, services rendered, witness: S. Vane. Nineteen years old, and I signed for my tea money while men died upstream."

"And you want it destroyed."

"I want page nine destroyed. I want—" She laughed, short and bitter. "You know what I wanted, originally? I wanted to bring it to a magistrate myself. Ten years ago I tried. I found a magistrate in Halvern who seemed honest, and I got within a day of handing him copies, and then his own clerks sold me to the company, and I watched two of Hobb's old associates hang a man in a stable yard for possessing less than I was carrying. So I learned. The system isn't broken, Elodie. The system is the weapon. You don't hand a weapon to the people it's pointed at."

"So you became the people it's pointed at."

"I became *survivable*." For the first time Vane raised her voice, and the sound of it scattered a bird off the cairns. "There is a difference between profiting from running and surviving it, and you have never once in twenty years let me have that difference. You look at me across contract tables like I'm the massacre's daughter and you're its orphan, and it is not true. It was never true. You carried the paper. I counted the bodies. We are the same age and the same guilt and the same cowardice, and the only thing you have that I don't is a found family that loves you, and the only thing I have that you don't is the truth about what we both did—and I am so tired, Elodie. I am so tired of being the mirror you won't look into."

The silence afterward was enormous. The river spoke somewhere below the field, patient, indifferent, carrying everything.

* * *

Elodie found that her hands had gone steady, which surprised her. She'd expected shaking. What she felt instead was stranger—a terrible, clarifying pity, the kind you feel looking at your own face in water.

"You're right," she said.

Vane blinked. Whatever script she'd rehearsed on the ride down, that line wasn't in it.

"About all of it. About the bunker. About the code being armor. I carried that document through the Concession and I never asked, and when I finally understood what I'd carried, I didn't go to a magistrate. I went underground. I collected five damaged people and taught them to disappear, and I told myself it was mercy, and maybe some of it was. But the root of it was that I couldn't stand to be seen by anyone who might ask me the question you just asked." Elodie stepped closer, one pace, gravel under her boot. "So yes. Mirror. Both of us. But hear the rest of it, Sable, because this part matters more than your page nine."

"Say it."

"There's a version of tonight where you get what you came for. You walk me to my camp at gunpoint, you take the map work, you find Hobb's cache before the permits lapse, you burn the ledger, and you spend the rest of your life free and rich and hollow, checking every road behind you, because the company that owns you now will own you forever—they don't release assets, Sable, you know that better than anyone. And there's another version. Harder. Where the ledger goes to Corrow, all of it, every page including yours, and you stand up in front of Magistrate Halloway and testify, and you trade your name for the truth, and maybe you hang, and maybe you exile, and maybe—maybe—you sleep for the first time in twenty years."

"That's not an offer. That's a eulogy."

"It's the only door out of this canyon that doesn't lead back into it." Elodie held her gaze. "You said I have a family and you have the truth. Come take the family. It's the only thing I've ever owned that was worth anything, and I promise you it works better than a ledger."

For a long moment Vane said nothing. Her jaw worked. Her eyes—Elodie saw it happen—went wet and furious about going wet.

"You sanctimonious—"

A whistle from downstream. Three notes, sharp. Ferro's signal, and not the one for *all clear*.

Vane's head snapped toward the sound, and her whole body changed—the confessor gone, the operator back, cold and fast. "That's early," she said. "They weren't supposed to move yet."

"Your guns?"

"My guns are supposed to be *waiting*." She was already turning for her horse. "Dreele. It has to be Dreele—he doesn't trust me, he never trusted me, and if he's moved them up without my order—"

The second whistle came, and this time it was Bex's whistle, from the direction of camp, and it was the alarm note, and underneath it, thin across the darkening river, came the sound Elodie had been dreading since the word *yet*: shouting, and a shot, and a boy's voice calling her name.

"SALT!" Elodie shouted it herself, already running, Vane swearing behind her, the cairns streaming past like a congregation finally rising from its knees. "Sable—your guns or Dreele's, whoever's down there—if you want that family, now's your chance to earn it!"

Vane swung into the saddle. "This changes nothing between us," she called, wheeling the horse.

"It changes everything," Elodie said, and ran toward the guns.
