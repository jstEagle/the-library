## 24. Hostages

Isandro had learned to count things the way Ferro counted them: not for comfort, but for truth. So he counted now, lying flat behind a fallen slab of sandstone at the edge of the cairn field while morning came up gray and cold over four hundred little stone heaps.

Six of Vane's hired guns on the rim above the graves. Two more flanking the porters, who sat in a huddle by the dead fire with their hands laced on top of their heads like men praying badly. Sable Vane herself stood in the open, alone, which was its own kind of arithmetic. Nobody stood in the open in front of Elodie Yarrow unless they were very sure of something.

He counted the crew next. Elodie and Tamsin behind the slab with him. Bex and Ovid back at the fork camp with Pell and the three porters who'd sided with them, a half mile downstream, out of earshot and out of reach. Ferro somewhere in the rocks, presumably, because Ferro was always somewhere in the rocks, though Isandro had stopped being able to find him years ago and had made peace with it.

"Fourteen barrels," Tamsin murmured. She wasn't looking at the guns; she was looking at the porters' water skins, stacked where Vane's men had made them drop them. "They took the water first. That's not a raid, that's a negotiation."

"It's a siege," Elodie said. Her voice was flat as the salt flats. "Sieges are just negotiations that haven't found their number yet."

Isandro looked at her hands. They weren't shaking anymore — they'd shaken once, over a sealed letter from Sable Vane, months ago at a waystation, and the whole crew had seen it and never spoken of it — but they were very still, the way a person is still when they're holding themselves together with both palms.

"Captain," he said. "What does she want?"

Elodie didn't answer right away. Below, one of Vane's men said something to the porters and laughed, and old Samet — sixty if he was a day, with knees like weathered rope — flinched, and something in Isandro's chest went hard and small.

"She wants the paymaster's cave," Elodie said finally. "The gunman we took at the Narrows told us enough before Ovid turned him loose with no boots and a long walk home. There's a ledger upriver. Names, payments, signatures. Everything the Concession cost and everyone it paid." She paused. "And she's in it."

"You know that for certain?"

"I knew it before the gunman opened his mouth." Elodie's jaw did something complicated. "Twenty years ago I carried paper through this canyon. Somebody else signed for what that paper bought. I've spent twenty years wondering whose name was under mine."

* * *

The parley happened at midmorning, in the open, because Vane insisted and because Elodie refused to give her the satisfaction of refusing.

They met halfway between the slab and the grave field, each walking alone, each stopping ten paces apart. Isandro watched from cover with Tamsin's hand heavy on his shoulder, and he heard every word, because sound carried strangely in the canyon and because Vane, he suspected, wanted to be heard.

"Elodie Yarrow," Vane called. She was smaller than Isandro expected, quick-moving, with silver shot through her dark hair and a smile that never reached anything below her cheekbones. "Still hiding behind other people's stones."

"Still hiring other people's guns," Elodie answered. "At least my habit is cheaper."

Vane laughed, and it was almost a real laugh, and that frightened Isandro more than the rifles had. "You always were funny. Here's the joke, then. My signature is in that ledger. Along with yours, if rumor serves, and half the honest people in this territory who needed feeding that winter. The company wants it burned before the permits lapse. I want it burned because I'd rather be forgotten than remembered wrong. You—" she spread her hands, "—you want it read aloud, because you've spent two decades playing saint to salve your conscience. We can't all have what we want. But I can make sure you don't get yours."

"And the porters?"

"Collateral. They walk when the ledger burns. Not before."

"They're children and old men, Sable."

"They're witnesses," Vane said pleasantly. "Everything is witnesses. That's the whole trouble with this canyon." She turned, unhurried, and walked back to her line, and Isandro understood suddenly why Elodie's hands had shaken over a letter: some people you could fight, and some people simply knew you.

* * *

The crew argued in whispers behind the slab for an hour, and the argument went nowhere, because there was nowhere for it to go.

"We can't storm six rifles to save eight hostages," Bex would say, if Bex were here. "We can't trade the ledger; it's the only thing standing between those miners and being erased twice. And we can't sit here, because Dreele's demolition crew is coming downriver and Vane knows exactly how long our water lasts."

Ovid, if he were here, would already be sketching angles. Tamsin would be counting bandages. Ferro would be gone, doing whatever Ferro did, arriving later with an answer nobody could explain.

But none of them were here. It was Elodie, Tamsin, and him, and eight porters in a gun line, and a ledger hidden in a cave that only the map's last symbols could find — symbols Ovid had copied onto a scrap of paper currently sitting in Elodie's breast pocket, which meant the most dangerous document in the canyon was pressed against the heart of the one person Vane couldn't afford to let walk away.

"I'll go," Isandro said.

Tamsin's hand closed on his shoulder again, harder this time. "No."

"They won't shoot me." He heard his own voice come out steadier than he felt, which was a trick he'd learned from watching Elodie lie to Dreele about wanting triple pay. "I'm the youngest thing in this canyon. I'm worth nothing dead. That makes me the safest person here, and the only one who can carry a message without either side deciding it's a trick."

"That's not—" Tamsin started.

"He's right," Elodie said quietly, and Isandro felt the words land strange, like praise and sentence at once. She turned and looked at him, really looked, the way she had across the salt flats the day they met, when he'd been a starving boy stealing water and she'd been a tired woman deciding whether mercy was affordable. "You don't have to be. Being right doesn't volunteer you."

"Somebody has to walk between them," Isandro said. "And I'm the only one both sides will let arrive alive." He tried a grin, because humor was Bex's armor and he'd borrowed it before. "Besides, I'm owed a favor. I saved the ledger from drowning. The river and I have an understanding."

"The river," Tamsin said, "has no understanding with anyone. I've pulled better swimmers than you out of calmer water."

"Tamsin." Elodie didn't raise her voice. She never raised her voice; she lowered everything else around it instead. "Give him the terms."

* * *

The terms were simple, which Isandro distrusted immediately. Simple terms were how deserts killed you: no shade, no warning, just distance.

He would walk into Vane's line carrying nothing but words. He would tell her the crew had found the cave — true, or nearly; Ovid and the map had narrowed it to three candidates above the falls — and that Elodie offered a trade: the location, in exchange for the porters released unharmed, downstream, with their water. Then Vane and Elodie would go to the cave together, and whatever was in it, they'd settle it there, between the two people whose names were actually in the book.

It was a lie in the way all good plans were lies — mostly true, arranged dishonestly. Elodie had no intention of letting Vane burn anything. What she intended, Isandro suspected, lived behind her eyes and would be revealed to him approximately never.

"If she takes you hostage," Tamsin said, knotting a fresh bandage around his forearm where a rock scrape from yesterday had stiffened, "don't be brave. Be boring. Boring people get fed."

"Noted."

"If she offers you water, drink slow. If she asks about your family—"

"I don't have one."

Tamsin's hands paused on the knot. For a moment her face did the thing it did over the porter graves, the thing that had hardened into resolve. "You have eleven," she said. "Some of us are just bad at announcing it. Go be boring."

Elodie walked him to the edge of the cover. Up close, in the gray light, she looked older than she ever let the crew see, lines around her eyes like dry washes.

"Isandro." She used his name, which she rarely did; names were currency and she spent carefully. "Twenty years ago I walked into a place like this thinking I was carrying paperwork. Everyone tells themselves a story about what they're carrying. When Vane talks to you, listen for hers. People reveal themselves in the story they think they're telling."

"And what am I carrying?"

She almost smiled. "A message. That's all. Keep it that way." Then, quieter, so quiet the canyon nearly ate it: "Come back. That's not an order. It's the only thing I've asked anyone for in twenty years."

* * *

Walking into a gun line, Isandro discovered, felt less like courage and more like walking into cold water: the first three steps were impossible and after that your body simply betrayed you by continuing.

The porters watched him come. Old Samet's eyes were wet with fury rather than fear, which was somehow worse. One of the younger boys, Wic, fourteen and all elbows, gave him the smallest nod, man-to-man, and Isandro's throat tightened so hard he nearly coughed.

"Hold there." A gunman stepped out, rifle lazy across his forearm, professional in the way of men who'd done this in worse places. "Empty hands?"

"Empty pockets too. You're welcome to check. Fair warning, there's a pebble in the left one I'm fond of."

Something twitched at the corner of the gunman's mouth. "Vane says bring him in."

Sable Vane waited by the dead fire, and up close she smelled of coffee and gun oil, and she studied Isandro with the frank appraisal of someone pricing a horse.

"So you're the apprentice." She said it like a diagnosis. "Yarrow collects strays. Sent the stray because she can't risk the others. That's not trust, boy, that's accounting."

"Probably," Isandro agreed. "She taught me the code the first week. Take nothing you can't carry. Leave no name behind. I've noticed she breaks the second rule whenever it matters."

Vane's eyebrows rose a fraction. "Does she."

"She named the drowned porters at the burial. All of them. She'd only known them nine days." He kept his voice level, bored even, boring, per Tamsin. "You knew these miners twenty years ago and you want their graves to stay anonymous. I'm just saying, only one of you sounds like the story she thinks she's telling."

For a moment something moved behind Vane's face — fast, buried, real — and Isandro thought: *there*. Elodie had been right. Vane wasn't here for the ledger out of greed. Greed didn't look like that. Guilt did, wearing a gun belt.

"Terms," Vane said crisply, recovering.

Isandro delivered them word for word. Location for porters. Cave for the two of them. He watched her weigh it, watched her glance at the sky — measuring light, measuring Dreele's timetable, measuring Elodie — and he saw the exact moment she decided, because her shoulders dropped half an inch, the way a gambler's do when the bluff gets called and the pot turns out to be worth it anyway.

"Porters walk," she said. "Downstream, unarmed, with water. Your captain comes to the cave. Alone except for the boy." Her eyes flicked to Isandro, sharp as a burr. "Because I don't trust her, and she doesn't trust me, and you, apprentice, are the only collateral either of us will accept."

"That's not what I was sent to offer."

"No," Vane agreed pleasantly. "It's what I'm accepting. Tell her the trade changed. Tell her—" and here she smiled, and it reached all the way down this time, and it was terrible, "—tell her I know about the third candidate cave. The map shows three. Only one has the strongbox. And I know which, because I helped bury it."

The ground tilted gently under Isandro's feet. Three candidates. Ovid had narrowed it to three. And Vane —

"You helped hide it?"

"I helped the paymaster carry it up that trail," Vane said, "when I still believed names should survive. Then I grew practical." She waved a hand at the gunmen. "Walk him back. Sundown, at the fork. Bring the map and bring the girl, and the old man and the children live to be boring somewhere else."

* * *

He walked back across the cairn field with the sun climbing and his heart going like a drum in a parade, and the strangest part was that he wasn't afraid anymore, or rather the fear had transmuted into something with a direction. Behind him, the porters were being given their skins back, grumbling, alive. Ahead of him, Elodie stood up out of the rocks, and Tamsin beside her, and Isandro realized he was smiling and couldn't stop.

"Well?" Elodie said.

"She took the trade. Porters for the cave. But there's a change." He stopped in front of her, breathing hard from nothing, from everything. "She says she knows which cave. She helped the paymaster hide it. And she wants you at the fork by sundown — you, me, and the map. She's keeping me as surety."

Silence. Tamsin swore softly, fluently, in three dialects.

Elodie looked at him for a long moment. Then she reached into her breast pocket, drew out Ovid's scrap of map, and held it up between two fingers.

"Three candidates," she said. "And she claims to know the right one."

"Yes."

"Then we have a problem, apprentice." Elodie's voice was calm, but Isandro had spent seven years learning to read her, and underneath the calm was something bright and moving fast, like green foam on the river — the sign, she'd taught him, that a flood was coming from upstream. "Because Ovid narrowed it to three. But last night, going through the paymaster's letters again, I found a fourth mark. One the survey map never had."

She folded the scrap away.

"She thinks she knows where the ledger is. Which means, at sundown, one of us is going to be very surprised."
