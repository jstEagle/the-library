## 25. The Paymaster's Cave

Ovid had spent thirty years teaching stone how to let go of things, and in all that time he had never once met a wall that wanted to keep a secret this badly.

They'd been climbing since before dawn—Isandro ahead on the goat track, Ovid behind doing his best impression of a man whose knees still belonged to him. The map's last symbols were scratched into a slab of sandstone at the top of a shelf above the river bend: three lines like a bird's foot, and beneath them a circle with a dot. Ovid had stared at that circle for a long while, chewing the end of his mustache, before it came to him.

"That's not a survey mark," he said. "That's a drill point."

"A what?"

"Where you put the charge, boy." He tapped the circle. "Somebody meant to blow this shelf closed and then thought better of it—or somebody else did the thinking for them. Either way, if there's a hole meant to be filled, there's something worth filling it over." He grinned despite himself. "I do love a wall with an opinion."

Isandro looked at him the way the boy always did when Ovid talked about explosives—which was to say, with the particular wariness of someone who has watched a friend enjoy his work too much. "You're not going to blow anything open. Tamsin said if you bring down half the canyon she's going to bury you under the part that falls."

"Tamsin says a great many things. It's why we keep her." Ovid patted the slab. "No charges today. Today we knock politely."

* * *

The cave mouth was exactly where a careful man would want it: behind a fall of rock that looked natural until you got close enough to see the chisel marks, hidden from the river by a lip of stone, dry even in flood season because the shelf tilted inward. Whoever built this hiding place had known water the way Elodie knew water—and had known men, too, which was rarer.

Ovid knelt at the rubble seal and ran his fingers along the joints. His hands were scarred maps of every mistake he'd ever made, and they read stone the way other people read letters.

"Set with lime mortar," he murmured. "Not rushed. Somebody mixed this properly, wetted the stone first. This isn't a bandit's cache, Isandro. This is a man who expected to come back."

"Do you think he did?"

Ovid considered the question honestly, which was his habit and his curse. "Men who seal things up expecting to return usually come back as bones," he said. "But the ones who don't come back—they leave the door shut so the thing stays safe. There's a kind of hope in that." He worked a flat iron bar from his pack—the gentlest tool he owned, though he'd never say so aloud—and began levering stones loose with the patience of a man defusing his own temper.

It took two hours. Isandro hauled rock; Ovid directed, grumbled, and occasionally did nothing at all with tremendous authority. By midmorning there was a gap a slim boy could squeeze through, breathing dust and cold air that smelled of nothing—not bat, not rot, not death. Just stone keeping its own counsel.

"You first," Ovid said. "If it collapses, I'll dig you out. If I go in first and it collapses, who digs out me? Nobody. I'm irreplaceable."

"You're replaceable," Isandro said, already wriggling through. "Bex has a list."

"She would," Ovid said fondly, and followed.

* * *

Inside, the dark was total until Isandro lit the lamp, and then it wasn't dark at all—it was a room.

That was the word Ovid's mind kept reaching for. Not a cave. A room. The floor was leveled and swept, still bearing the ghost of broom strokes in the dust. Along one wall stood a row of crates stenciled with a company mark Ovid knew from twenty years of blowing up company property: the Anselm Consolidated Water & Mining Company, its eagle clutching a faucet instead of a scepter, which Ovid had always found funnier than anyone else in the crew did.

And against the far wall, squat and black under the lamplight, sat a strongbox the size of a coffin for a very small corpse.

"Well," Ovid said softly. "Hello, gorgeous."

The lock was a three-lever affair, good quality, and Ovid could have opened it in four minutes with picks or in one second with a charge, and he stood there weighing the two options like a man choosing between tea and arson. In the end he picked it—slowly, reverently, narrating each tumbler to Isandro in a whisper, because some pleasures should be shared—and when the lid came up on oiled hinges that still swung true after twenty years, the lamp light fell on paper.

Paper. Stacks of it. Wrapped in oilcloth, tied with cord, dry as the day they were sealed.

The ledger sat on top: fat, leather-bound, corners squared, a paymaster's pride. Beneath it, bundles of letters. Beneath those, a smaller box, and inside the smaller box—a watch, stopped; a wedding ring wrapped in a scrap of cloth; and a folded page in a careful hand.

Ovid did not touch any of it for a long moment. He was a man who handled destruction daily and documents rarely, and something about the neatness of it all pressed on his chest. This was a dead man's tidiness. This was a man arranging his evidence the way you'd arrange your affairs before a journey you didn't expect to survive.

"Read the folded one," Isandro said quietly. "The ring is on top of it. I think—I think he meant it to be read first."

Ovid unfolded it with both hands, the way you'd handle a wounded bird.

*To whoever finds this,* it began. *If you are a company man, close the box and leave, and I will consider us even. If you are anyone else—read everything. I was paymaster for Anselm Consolidated eleven years. Every wage, every bribe, every signature passed through my hands. What happened at the Concession was not a riot and not a raid. It was a purchase. Two hundred names bought and paid for, and mine is the hand that wrote the prices.*

*I could not stop it. I counted it. That is worse, and I know it is worse.*

*When the permits lapse, this land reverts to the crown, and the company means to burn the canyon clean before that day. They will look for this box. They will not find it, because I have been clever, and being clever is the only courage I have left. Take it to a magistrate who can still be shamed. There are fewer than you'd think. Start with the sons.*

*My name is Aldous Merrin. Write it down somewhere. I kept everyone else's name. It seems fair someone should keep mine.*

*—A.M.*

The cave was silent except for the river, far below, going about its business.

Ovid found that his eyes were wet, which embarrassed him greatly, and so he blamed the dust. "Twenty years," he managed. "This man sat up here—or down in some office—with all of this, and sealed it away, and died, most likely, waiting for somebody honest to walk past a bird's-foot symbol on a rock." He laughed, a short, cracked sound. "And who walks past? A demolition man and a boy. The desert has a sense of humor, Isandro. It's cruel, but it's funny."

Isandro was looking at the strongbox with an expression Ovid recognized from mirrors: the face of someone holding more than their arms can carry. "Two hundred names," the boy said. "Tamsin buried two porters without markers because names matter, and she said—" He stopped. Started again. "She said if names matter, someone owes the Concession dead theirs."

"Well," Ovid said, "someone kept the bill."

* * *

They worked the rest of the day, and Ovid insisted on doing it properly, because a find like this deserved craft. They photographed nothing—no camera among them—but Ovid made Isandro copy the map symbols and the cave's position into the boy's field book twice, in case one copy got wet, lost, or stolen, and Ovid had learned at the Narrows that all three were live possibilities on this river.

He inventoried the crates without opening them all: blasting supplies, mostly, and canned goods gone gray with age, and—one crate that made him sit down abruptly on a stone and laugh until Isandro checked him for heatstroke—a full case of detonators, packed in wax, preserved better than anything Ovid had owned in his life.

"He was a paymaster," Isandro said. "Why does he have detonators?"

"Because he was a paymaster who planned to seal this cave himself, and sealing means drilling and drilling means charges, and then he thought better of leaving a blast pattern for company eyes." Ovid wiped his eyes. "Or he took them so the company couldn't use them on the town. Either way, the man thought in my language. I feel like I've met him." He paused. "I'd have liked him. We'd have argued constantly."

By late afternoon they had the ledger and the letter bundles wrapped in oilcloth and strapped into a padded frame Ovid built from crate slats—because he was not going to carry two hundred dead men's names down a goat track loose in a sack like turnips, and no one would ever convince him otherwise. The ring and the watch and Merrin's letter went into Isandro's breast pocket, against the skin, where the boy said he'd feel them.

At the cave mouth, Ovid paused. He looked back at the swept floor, the broom ghosts, the tidy black box now empty of its heart.

"We should reseal it," he said. "Partially. If Dreele's people find it empty but sealed, they'll waste days wondering what we took and whether we got it all." He smiled the smile his crew called his masonry smile, which had preceded many loud events. "And I do so love giving a wall its opinion back."

He set no charge—he was a professional, whatever Tamsin said—but he rebuilt the seal with lime mortar mixed fast and sloppy, deliberately wrong, a forgery of haste that would fool a foreman and insult an engineer. Then he pressed his palm flat against the stone, the way he did after every job, the little superstition he'd deny under oath.

"Rest easy, Mister Merrin," he told the wall. "Your paperwork's in better hands than yours were."

"What hands?" Isandro asked. "Whose?"

Ovid looked at the boy—at the apprentice who had walked into a gun line at the fork of the river, who dove into floodwater for loose pages, who was standing in a dead man's secret room asking questions nobody in this crew had been allowed to ask for twenty years.

"Ours," Ovid said. "Apparently ours. Come on. Elodie will want to burn the whole escarpment down by supplication, and Tamsin will want to start reading names tonight, and Bex will want to inventory everything, and Ferro will already have counted Vane's guns twice. We're the only ones who get to carry this slow." He settled the padded frame across his shoulders and felt the weight of it settle into his bones, heavier than any charge he'd ever packed, and lighter too, somehow, in the way that only true things are heavy.

They climbed down as the sun went copper behind the western rim, and below them the camp fires were coming alight one by one along the bank—small, defiant, visible.

Ovid stopped halfway down the track.

"Ferro keeps low fires," he said slowly. "Always. Low and screened. That's her rule since the Narrows."

Isandro saw it a heartbeat later. Four fires, high and bright, and figures moving between them that moved wrong—too many, too casual, spaced like men who wanted to be seen.

The ledger pressed warm against Ovid's spine, and the river roared on below, indifferent, carrying everything toward Corrow.

"Boy," Ovid said quietly, easing the frame off his shoulders and into the shadow of a boulder, "how do you feel about arriving home the long way?"
