## 26. Reading the Dead

They read the ledger by lamplight because Ovid refused to let anyone light the big fire.

"Light draws bullets," he said, arranging three lamps on a flat stone like an altar to some small, cautious god. "Lamplight says *we're humble*. Lamplight says *we're nobody*. Lamplight is a lie we tell with wax."

"You've thought about this a lot," Bex said.

"I think about everything a lot. It's why I'm still alive and so pleasant."

The cave mouth faced downstream, toward Dreele's camp at the fork, and Ferro had spent the last hour doing whatever Ferro did in the dark—Elodie had stopped trying to picture it years ago. Somewhere out there he was either watching their enemies or becoming one of the rocks. Either way, they'd know if someone came.

So they gathered around the strongbox the way people gather around a sickbed.

The paymaster's ledger was a fat thing bound in cracked black leather, swollen from years of damp before it was ever sealed away, its pages gone soft as cloth. The letters stacked beside it were tied with ribbon that had once been red. Isandro sat closest, because he'd carried it up from the cache and no one had the heart to make him give it up. Tamsin sat opposite, sleeves rolled, her medic's hands flat on the stone as if she were about to take the book's pulse.

"Two hundred and six names," she said, opening it. "I counted while you were all pretending not to watch me count."

"We weren't pretending," Pell said.

"You were all pretending," Tamsin said kindly. "It's fine. It's what people do near hard things. They arrange supplies."

And indeed Bex had arranged supplies—water skins, a strip of dried apricot for each person, laid out with the precision she brought to everything. Elodie took hers without comment. Apricots at a graveside reading. It was either absurd or exactly right, and she'd learned long ago that Bex's rituals usually turned out to be exactly right.

Tamsin began to read.

She didn't perform it. That was the thing about Tamsin—she had no theater in her at all. She read the way she set bones: firmly, without flinching, giving each name its full weight and moving on.

"Harrow Beckman. Paid forty crowns. Signature witnessed, R. Voss."

A pause. The river spoke below them, patient and enormous.

"Denna Ilves. Paid forty crowns. R. Voss."

"Who's R. Voss?" Isandro asked quietly.

"The witness," Ovid said. "Same initials on every page so far. A man who watched two hundred murders get paid for and wrote his name neatly under each one." He shook his head. "Neat handwriting. That's what gets me. You'd think a man's hand would shake."

"Maybe it did," Tamsin said, "and he just practiced."

She read on. Forty crowns. Fifty for the overseers. Sixty for whoever had fired the homestead. The payments ran through spring and into summer, page after page, and the crew sat in the lamplight listening to the arithmetic of a massacre, and the gentle humor that usually lived among them like a camp cat had crept off somewhere to be alone.

Elodie listened for her own guilt and found, instead, something stranger: the dead becoming people. Harrow Beckman. Denna Ilves. A man named Sorrel who got only twenty crowns—"partial payment, work incomplete"—and Elodie felt her throat close, because *work incomplete* meant he had died mid-task, mid-something ordinary, hauling or digging or mending, and someone had docked him for it.

"They priced them," Pell whispered. He was the junior engineer, the nervous one, and his hands were shaking around his apricot strip. "Like freight."

"Companies price everything," Dreele wasn't there to say, but Elodie heard it anyway in the memory of his voice. "That's what companies are."

* * *

An hour in, Bex took over the reading because Tamsin's voice had begun to fray at the edges, and Bex's voice never frayed—it was quartermaster's iron, built for reciting inventory in windstorms. She read names the way she read manifests, and somehow that made it worse and better at once: worse because it sounded like cargo, better because nothing in Bex's delivery could be dismissed as sentiment. This was stock. This was on the books. This had been *recorded*, by men who believed no one would ever check.

Isandro had gone very still. Elodie watched him from across the lamps—the boy she'd caught stealing water on the salt flats, all elbows and hunger once, now sitting straight-backed with the patience of a much older man. He hadn't spoken since the reading began. His birth was tangled somewhere in these pages—Tamsin had told her, and Elodie suspected Tamsin had told him—and every name read aloud might be his mother's, his father's, someone's. He was listening the way you listen outside a door when your whole life is being discussed.

When Bex reached a name—*Mara Ishikawa, paid thirty-five crowns*—the boy's breath went out of him slowly, controlled, the way Ferro had taught him to control it. Elodie looked away to give him privacy, which was its own small mercy, and found Ovid looking at her instead.

"Well?" he said softly. "You going to make us wait for the magistrate?"

"The magistrate comes last," Tamsin said. "Paymasters are tidy. Summary page at the end."

Bex reached the end. The final pages weren't names but totals—sums disbursed, sums withheld, a column labeled *discretionary*—and then a certification block, formal as a wedding record:

*Received and audited. The Concession lands are hereby cleared of unlawful settlement effective this date. No further claims shall be recognized.*

Below it, a signature.

Bex read it aloud, and her iron voice finally bent, just slightly, on the second word.

"Magistrate Aldous Halloway."

Nobody said anything for a moment. The lamps fluttered. Downstream, the river kept up its indifferent conversation with the rocks.

"Aldous Halloway," Pell repeated. "The old magistrate. The one whose son—"

"Whose son holds the seat now," Elodie said. "Magistrate Edmund Halloway. The same Edmund Halloway who's been renewing the company's permits every ninety days for twenty years, and who is due to renew them again inside the week."

"So the man signing our destruction," Ovid said, "is tidying up his father's signature."

"That's one way to put it."

"It's the only way to put it. I checked."

* * *

They should have stopped there. Elodie knew that even then—knew it the way you know a storm line on the horizon and keep walking anyway. But Tamsin turned back to the front of the ledger, to the earliest entries, the ones from before the massacre proper, when the payments had been smaller and meaner: bribes to boundary riders, fees to surveyors, retainers to escorts hired to move paperwork through the Concession.

Escort work. Paperwork. Elodie's stomach dropped a full second before her eyes found the entry.

*Boundary reclassification documents conveyed by licensed ranger. Fee: twelve crowns. Discretionary supplement appended per arrangement.*

And beneath it, in a clerk's careful imitation of a ranger's hand—a signature she knew better than her own real one.

*E. Yarrow.*

The cave went quiet in a way that had texture to it, weight, like water pressure. Elodie heard her own breathing. She heard Isandro shift. She heard, distantly, the sound of everything she had built over twenty years settling on its foundations and finding one of them hollow.

"There it is," she said. Her voice came out level. She was almost proud of that.

"Elodie." Tamsin's voice, gentle. "We knew."

"You suspected."

"I *knew*. I stitched the shoulder wound you got carrying those papers, remember? You told me it was a smuggling run." Tamsin's mouth twitched, not quite a smile. "You're a terrible liar. You always have been. You lie like a man reading from a document he doesn't believe."

"I was twenty-three," Elodie said. "They told me it was boundary paperwork. Reclassification. There was a fee and a seal and I was hungry, and the code said take nothing you can't carry, and papers weigh nothing." She laughed, and it came out wrong, dry as salt flats. "Papers weigh nothing. That's the joke, isn't it. Twenty years running from ledgers, off every map, out of every book—and I'm *in* the book. I've been in the book the whole time. Twelve crowns. I sold my invisibility for twelve crowns and didn't even know it."

"Hey." Bex, quietly. "Look at me."

Elodie looked. Bex held up the ledger, open to the signature, and tapped the forged line with one blunt finger.

"This isn't your signature. Look at it. The loop on the Y is wrong—you cross your Y like you're stabbing it. This clerk drew a polite little loop. Any magistrate worth his ink sees forgery in ten seconds." She closed the book on her finger, keeping the place. "You didn't sign it. Someone signed *at* you. That's different. That matters."

"It enabled the same thing."

"It did," Bex agreed, merciless and kind at once, which was Bex's particular gift. "And now it unmakes it. That's the trade. That's always been the trade, captain. You taught us that."

Elodie sat with it. Around her the lamplight made small warm islands on the stone, and the dead lay in their hundreds along the river behind them, and somewhere upstream the water rose out of the ground inside a fence with a company's name on it, and none of it—not one stone of it—had ever belonged to the men who'd paid for it in crowns and signatures.

Twelve crowns. She thought of Isandro on the salt flats, stealing water because water was life and life shouldn't be locked. She'd taken him in rather than turn him out. Weary mercy, Ferro called it, teasing. And here was the other side of the coin she'd never looked at: the reason she'd been weary in the first place. The reason the code existed. Every rule the crew lived by—leave no name, take nothing you can't carry, stay off the maps—was a scar tissue grown over this exact wound. They hadn't built a life of secrecy. They'd built a hiding place from one bad day, and called the hiding place a family.

Well. It *was* a family. That was the thing. Hiding places could be homes too, if you fed the people in them.

"All right," Elodie said. She stood, and her knees complained, and she let them. "Here's what we have. Two hundred and six names, priced and paid. A certification signed by Aldous Halloway clearing land that was never cleared—those cairn fields are still full of his 'unlawful settlers.' Letters tying the payments to the water rights. And my forged signature, which proves the company used rangers as unwitting couriers, which means the rot goes wider than one paymaster." She looked around the lamplight at their faces—Bex steady, Ovid sharp, Tamsin tired and unbroken, Pell terrified and present anyway, Isandro watching her with an expression she couldn't read yet and would spend the rest of the book, she suspected, learning to read. "This isn't grief anymore. Grief keeps. Evidence moves. Tomorrow we start south for Corrow."

"Corrow's eight days downriver," Ovid said. "Dreele's camp is between us and the rafts."

"I know."

"And Vane is somewhere behind us with guns."

"I know that too."

"And the permits expire—"

"*Inside the week,*" Elodie finished. "Which means the company has until then to burn this canyon clean, and Magistrate Halloway has until then to decide whether he's his father's son or his father's correction. We're going to give him the chance to be the second thing. After that it's out of our hands and into the river's."

Tamsin closed the ledger and wrapped it in oilcloth, and her hands were steady again, surgeon-steady. "One more thing," she said. "Before we sleep. The names. We read them tonight in the dark for ourselves. In Corrow, they get read in daylight, in a courtroom, into the record." She tied the ribbon. "But tomorrow morning, before we break camp—I want to walk back to the cairn field and say ten of them out loud. Just ten. Harrow Beckman. Denna Ilves. Mara Ishikawa." Her eyes flicked to Isandro, and softened. "The river's been carrying them unnamed for twenty years. It can start learning them now."

Nobody argued. Nobody ever argued with Tamsin about the dead.

They banked the lamps. Ferro materialized at the cave mouth long enough to report all quiet, then dematerialized again, and Bex handed out the last of the apricots because apparently the ritual hadn't finished with them.

Elodie took the first watch, sitting where she could see both the river and the boy asleep against the strongbox, his arm thrown over the oilcloth bundle like it was a saddle he intended to keep. Below, the water moved in the dark, going about its ancient business, asking no one's permission, honoring no permit, remembering everything.

Twelve crowns, she thought. And then, correcting herself with the ledger's own cold arithmetic: two hundred and six lives, at forty crowns apiece, less deductions.

Tomorrow the bill came due—and for once, she intended to be the one holding it out.
