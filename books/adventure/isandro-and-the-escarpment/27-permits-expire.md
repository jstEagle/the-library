## 27. Permits Expire

Dreele had always believed that time was the only honest employee a man could hire. It never asked for wages, never slept, never developed opinions about the quality of its boots. And like every honest employee, it eventually turned on you.

He sat in the downstream camp at the fork of the River Anselm, beneath an awning of oiled canvas that did nothing against the heat and everything against his dignity, and watched the sun crawl toward the notch in the western rim where it would set exactly on schedule, as it had for four billion years without once requiring a permit.

Ninety days.

He turned the company chronometer over in his palm—a heavy brass thing, engraved with the Concession's seal, a gift from the board when he'd taken the survey contract. The second hand ticked with the smugness of a machine that knew something he didn't. Or rather, that knew precisely what he didn't want to know: that somewhere upstream, past the fork, past the narrows, past the grave field where two hundred men lay under stones like a field of bad teeth, a woman named Elodie Yarrow was holding a ledger that could unmake everything the board had built.

*Everything,* Dreele corrected himself. Not everything. The board would survive. Boards always survived; they were designed to, the way cockroaches were designed to survive and with roughly the same moral profile. What would not survive was Dreele's career, Dreele's pension, and quite possibly Dreele himself, because the board had a long institutional memory for failure and a short one for loyalty.

The pigeon arrived at dusk.

It came out of the south, wings catching the last copper light, and dropped onto the coop with the weary authority of a creature that had flown forty miles through canyon wind and expected to be thanked. Ferro's people had shot their messenger pigeons weeks ago—Dreele had heard the story from a porter who'd defected back downstream, a nervous boy who talked too much and ate too little—but the company's birds flew a different line, high along the rimrock, beyond the reach of anyone who might think to look up.

Dreele took the message tube, dismissed the bird-keeper with a nod, and broke the seal alone in his tent.

The paper inside was thin, official, and smelled faintly of the ink they used in the Corrow land office—the good ink, the kind that cost more per ounce than the porters made per week. He read it twice.

*By order of the Crown Land Commission, the Anselm Concession expires in seven days' time. All claims revert to the Crown upon lapse. The Board directs you to ensure that no evidence of prior operations remains accessible after reversion. You are authorized to expedite.*

*Expedite.* Dreele almost laughed. There was a word that had been sanded smooth by lawyers until it could mean anything. Expedite the shipment. Expedite the payment. Expedite the burning of a canyon so thoroughly that two hundred dead men would stop existing even as ghosts.

Seven days. Then the land belonged to the Crown, and any man with a map and a mule could walk into it legally—which meant that before that happened, there could be no maps worth walking with, no ledgers worth reading, no witnesses worth cross-examining.

He thought of Yarrow's face when she'd come upstream to meet him at the fork. Cool as well water. She'd listened to his offer—triple pay, amnesty, forget what you've seen—with the polite attention of a woman pricing fish, and then she'd said, "Mr. Dreele, I've been offered bribes by better liars than you," and walked back upriver without turning around. He'd admired it, in the way a man admires a knife pointed at him. Clean work. No wasted motion.

She wasn't going to forget anything. Which meant the ledger was real, which meant the paymaster's cache had been found, which meant that in seven days either the ledger burned or Dreele did—professionally speaking.

He stepped out of the tent. The camp was settling into its evening rhythms: cook fires, the low complaint of mules, porters mending gear by firelight. Twelve had started the expedition. Between the flood, the ambush, and the defection at the fork, he had five left, plus Pell—who had gone upstream with Yarrow's people, the little traitor, taking three porters and Dreele's entire reserve of goodwill with her—and nine hired guns, because Dreele had learned early that in the Escarpment you paid for muscle the way you paid for water: constantly, and more than you'd budgeted.

"Mr. Dreele." His foreman, a slab-faced man called Hark, materialized beside him the way he always did, soundlessly, smelling of gun oil and chewing tobacco. "Bird come in?"

"It did."

"And?"

"And we're on a clock." Dreele handed him the message. Hark read it with the slow, moving-lip concentration of a man for whom literacy was a professional skill acquired late and maintained grudgingly.

"Seven days," Hark said. "That's tight."

"That's the point. They want it done while the permits still technically cover us. After reversion, anything we do in the canyon is trespass. Before reversion—" Dreele shrugged. "Before reversion, it's property management."

Hark chewed on that, and on his tobacco. "And the rangers?"

"The rangers have a ledger."

"Then we take the ledger."

"Yes. Eventually. But we do it in the right order, Hark, because order is the difference between a plan and a massacre." He said it flatly, and watched Hark decide whether to be reassured or disappointed. Dreele genuinely didn't know which answer the man preferred, and that frightened him more than the deadline did.

Here was the thing Dreele had never told anyone, not the board, not Hark, not the mirror: he had signed on for a survey. A *survey*. Lines on paper, elevations, flow rates, a report bound in blue cloth with his name embossed on the spine. He was a company man, yes—he'd been a company man since he was nineteen and had discovered that companies fed you more reliably than principles did—but there were kinds of dirty he'd told himself he wasn't. Burning a homestead, he could file under *site remediation*. Paying off a magistrate, *regulatory engagement*. But marching nine guns up a river to burn a graveyard and shoot whoever stood near it—that was a different column in a different book, and Dreele had spent thirty years keeping his books clean enough to sleep behind.

He was going to do it anyway. That was the joke, if you wanted one. He'd already decided, the moment he read the word *expedite*, that he would do it, because the alternative was a pensionless future in a border town where men like Sable Vane drank, and Dreele had seen how men like Sable Vane aged.

But he would do it in the right order.

* * *

The right order, as Dreele laid it out to his men that night around the map table, went like this:

"Tomorrow we move upstream. Not fast—fast gets men killed in slot canyons, and I've lost enough of you to water this season." He let that sit. Two porters drowned in the flash flood, and even the hired guns had gone quiet for a day afterward, the way hard men do when the desert reminds them it was here first. "We move deliberate. Day one, we reach the narrows. Day two, the grave field. The rangers will be dug in above it—they know we're coming, they've known since Vane's people got bloodied at the narrows."

One of the gunmen, a lean Kettering-city type with a scar through his eyebrow, raised a hand. "Why not just wait them out? Permits lapse, they've got no legal standing, crown troops come in—"

"Crown troops come in to *survey*, son, same as we were supposed to. Nobody sends soldiers for paperwork." Dreele tapped the map. "We go in first because first is the only advantage left. After reversion, every hour is a coin toss. Before it, the canyon is ours on paper, and paper has been very good to us so far."

He didn't say the rest: that the board's letter contained a second page, which he had burned, which had instructed—in the passive voice that boards used for things they wanted done but not attributed—that *personnel associated with documentary evidence should also be considered part of the site*. Considered. Part of the site. Somewhere in a marble office eleven hundred miles away, a man with good cufflinks had written a sentence that meant *kill the rangers*, and had probably felt a small glow of professional accomplishment at the phrasing.

Dreele looked around the fire at his nine guns. Hark. The Kettering man. Seven others whose names he made a point of knowing—not from sentiment, but because a commander who couldn't name his men couldn't predict which of them would bolt, and bolting was contagious. Sixty miles upstream, Yarrow had maybe six crew, a handful of defecting porters, a boy apprentice, and a nervous junior engineer. Numbers favored him. Position favored them. Time—

Time favored nobody. Time was just the antagonist now, patient and impartial, ticking away in its brass case, and all any of them could do was try to spend it better than the other side.

"One more thing," Dreele said. "When this is done—if it's done—every man here gets double the agreed rate and passage out of the territory on the company's coin. And every man here keeps his mouth shut for the rest of his natural life. Those are the terms. Anyone unhappy with them can walk downstream tonight and I'll pay him half and wish him luck."

Nobody walked. He hadn't expected anyone to. Half pay in the Escarpment was a slow death, and slow deaths were the one commodity the desert produced in surplus.

He rolled up the map, dismissed them, and sat alone by the coals with his chronometer open in his hand, watching the second hand eat the night.

* * *

He wrote two letters before sleeping. This was habit, not conscience—or possibly conscience wearing the costume of habit, which was the only way Dreele's conscience had ever been permitted outdoors.

The first letter was to the board, confirming receipt of instructions and outlining the operational timeline. Dry, competent, deniable. He sealed it for the morning bird.

The second letter he wrote to a woman named Marta Dreele, née Ostrand, who lived in Corrow and believed her husband managed mineral surveys for a respectable firm. He had written her a letter from every posting for nineteen years. They were never long. *The river is high. The men are well. I saw a heron today, grey as smoke.* Small true things, arranged to imply a life that contained nothing worse than distance.

Tonight he sat with the pen hovering longer than usual.

*Marta,* he finally wrote. *If a man named Halloway publishes anything in the next month bearing my name, know that I signed papers twenty years ago believing they were boundary lines, and that I am sorry, and that the heron was real. Everything else was real too. That's the trouble.*

He read it back. It was the most honest sentence he'd produced in three decades, and it was useless, and he loved it, and he held it over the candle.

He didn't burn it. He folded it into the envelope instead, and addressed it, and set it with the morning post, because some cowardly, stubborn corner of him wanted it to exist somewhere outside his own skull—even if Marta read it as a strange mood brought on by river air, even if it changed nothing, even if it was just a stone dropped down a very deep well in the childish hope of hearing it land.

Then he checked his pistol, though it didn't need checking, and slept badly, which was also becoming habitual.

* * *

They broke camp at first light, and the Escarpment, which had opinions about everything, gave them a morning of perfect, mocking beauty. The canyon walls glowed rose and gold. The river ran green and quiet between the stones. Somewhere upstream a bird sang three notes and stopped, as if it had said all it intended to say on the subject.

Dreele rode at the head of the column and did not enjoy the view. Beauty, in his experience, was what the desert put up like bunting before it billed you.

At midmorning they passed the mouth of a side canyon where, three weeks ago, a burned homestead had stood—or rather, where Dreele's own orders had sent men to make sure a burned homestead stayed burned. He'd stood in that ruin himself, kicking a child's tin cup out of the ash with his boot, telling himself *remediation, remediation,* the way a man tells himself a rosary he no longer believes in. The cup had been enameled blue. He still saw it sometimes, in the moment between waking and remembering where he was.

"Mr. Dreele." Hark, riding up alongside, jerking his chin at the sky. "Another bird."

It came down onto the trail ahead of them, bold as a tax collector. Dreele dismounted and took the tube, expecting another directive from the board.

It wasn't from the board.

The handwriting was small, precise, and unfamiliar. The message was eight words long:

*WE HAVE THE LEDGER. COME AND SEE.*

No signature. No location beyond what the bird's leg band told him, which was a coop station at the old waystation below the fork—Yarrow's ground, or close enough to it.

Dreele stood in the middle of the trail with the paper in his hand and felt, distinctly, the sensation of a trap being baited—and felt, underneath it, something worse: relief. Because a trap meant negotiation, and negotiation meant delay, and delay meant that somewhere in the arithmetic of seven days there might yet be a remainder.

"She wants to talk," Hark said, reading his face.

"She wants me to walk into a canyon with my hat in my hand." Dreele remounted. "Which is why," he added, with the grim lightness of a man stepping off a ledge on purpose, "we're going to do exactly that. All of us. Well armed, well spread, and smiling."

Hark frowned. "And if it's a trick?"

"Hark, everything from here to the source is a trick. The river tricks the sky, the sky tricks the rock, the rock tricks the surveyor." He nudged his horse forward. "The question was never whether it's a trick. The question is whose."

Behind them, the pigeon lifted off the trail and flew south, carrying nothing, free of charge, the only honest courier in the whole godforsaken canyon—and ahead, sixty miles upriver, the clock ticked on, six days and counting, patient as stone, hungry as water.
