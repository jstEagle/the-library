## 28. Betrayal at Camp

Ferro had a rule about sleeping in camp: he never did it all the way. Twenty years of scouting had taught him that the body could rest while the ears stayed on duty, and so he slept the way a heron fished—still on the surface, everything working underneath. Which was why, when the ledger left the camp that night, he heard it.

Not the theft itself. The theft was quiet. What he heard was the absence of a sound—the small, wet click of the oilcloth bundle being lifted from under Tamsin's cot, and then no footsteps where footsteps should have been. A person carrying two hundred names' worth of paper walks differently than a person walking empty. Ferro's ears knew the difference the way his hands knew the difference between river stone and river stone pretending to be river stone.

He opened his eyes to moonlight and an empty bedroll belonging to Sef, one of the porters who'd sided with them at the fork. The bedroll was still warm. That was the part that sat wrong in Ferro's chest, later. Not the running. The warmth.

He was up without standing up first—that trick Ovid had taught him, rolling out of the blankets and onto his feet in one motion like water deciding to be a waterfall—and by the time he'd crossed the camp, Sef was already a shape against the paler shape of the trail downstream, moving fast for a man carrying what he was carrying.

Ferro didn't shout. Shouting woke Elodie, and Elodie would want to come, and Elodie hadn't slept properly since the grave field, and somebody in this crew had to keep their captain from running herself into the ground over a thief. Besides, this was Ferro's job. He found people. It was the only thing he'd ever been good at, back before he'd been nobody, and it turned out you could be nobody and still be good at something.

He counted to thirty—long enough for Sef's nerve to carry him past the sentry line of boulders, short enough that the trail wouldn't cool—and then he went after him.

* * *

The river at night was a different animal than the river by day. By day it was work: current, depth, the moods Elodie taught Isandro to read. At night it was just sound, a long low conversation the water was having with itself, and every rock you stepped on wanted to add its opinion. Ferro moved along the high bank where the ground was gravel and gave nothing away, keeping the sound of the river between himself and Sef the way you'd keep a wall between yourself and a dog you didn't trust.

Sef was twenty-two, maybe. He'd come to them at the split with three other porters, Pell beside him, and when Bex had asked why, Sef had said, "Because Dreele counts us like sacks." And Bex had nodded once, which from Bex was a parade. In the weeks since, Sef had learned to laugh at Ovid's jokes even when they weren't funny—which showed judgment—and he'd sat with Isandro two nights running while the boy talked about the cairn field, about the hundreds of stones and the hundreds of people under them, and Sef had listened the way you listen when someone is handing you something heavy and you want them to know you can hold it.

That was the thing Ferro kept tripping over as he ran. You couldn't fake listening like that. He'd watched enough liars in his time to know they listened with their mouths.

So either Sef had been lying from the start, or something had changed him. And Ferro, who trusted almost nothing except his own feet and Tamsin's diagnosis, found he was hoping—actually hoping—for the second one. Because if Sef had been bought recently, then it meant the family was real and one member of it had been stolen. If Sef had always been false, then the family itself was a thing Ferro had invented, and he didn't know how to run home to a thing he'd invented.

The trail bent south around a shelf of red rock, and Ferro gained. Sef was young and scared and carrying weight, and fear makes people fast in bursts and slow in the gaps between bursts. Ferro ran the way water runs—no bursts, no gaps. He closed the distance to forty yards. Then thirty.

Then Sef reached the old ford below the narrows and did something clever, which Ferro noted even through the anger, because cleverness deserved noting no matter whose it was. Instead of crossing, Sef turned upstream along the shallows, walking in the water itself, breaking his trail. A company man's trick. Dreele's training, or Dreele's coin, teaching a porter how to disappear.

Except Ferro had grown up disappearing before Dreele ever learned to count, and there were exactly two ways out of this stretch of canyon: the ford, and a goat track four miles on that Sef didn't know about because Ferro had never told anyone about it, not even Elodie, because some things you keep the way you keep a knife—close, and for emergencies.

So Ferro stopped chasing and started cutting.

* * *

The goat track was less a track than an argument the mountain had lost. It climbed through scrub and scree along a ledge barely wide enough for a man with sense, and Ferro took it at a jog because he'd taken it at a jog maybe two hundred times and the mountain had given up arguing with him specifically. Below and to his right, the river muttered. Somewhere down there, Sef was wading north thinking himself invisible, and Ferro permitted himself one small private smile, the kind he never let out in daylight where it might frighten someone.

It was on the ledge, halfway along, that he let himself think about what came after the catching. Because Ferro wasn't a fighter—not the way Ovid was, or Elodie, who fought like weather. He was a finder. Once he found Sef, he'd have to hold him, and holding a frightened man with a knife is a conversation with bad grammar.

And beyond that: what did the crew do with him? He pictured it—them dragging Sef back to camp, and Tamsin looking at him with those healer's eyes that saw straight through to whatever was broken, and Bex doing arithmetic on his face, and Isandro—

Isandro. That was the worst of it. The boy had spent the whole expedition learning that people could be trusted in pieces, that a crew was a thing you built out of strangers and called family until it held. Sef had eaten at their fire. Sef had helped Ovid haul the raft timbers. Sef had asked Isandro, only three days ago, very seriously, whether the ranger code really said *take nothing you can't carry*, and Isandro had recited it proud as a psalm, and now Sef was out here in the dark carrying the one thing in the world too heavy for anyone to carry alone.

Two hundred names. Ferro thought about the cairn field, the way Tamsin had walked it row by row with the stolen map, cataloging, saying each recovered name under her breath like she was introducing the dead to each other. All of that, wrapped in oilcloth, bouncing against a traitor's spine.

No. Ferro corrected himself, boots scraping on stone. Not a traitor yet. A runner. Traitor was a verdict, and verdicts belonged to magistrates and captains, not to scouts in the dark. His job was simpler than justice. His job was the finding.

The ledge ended above the narrows pool, a black lens of water where the river slowed to think. Ferro crouched at the lip of the drop and looked down and there—there—a pale smear of movement in the shallows, upstream side of the pool. Sef, out of the water now, wringing his trousers by feel, checking over his shoulder at a darkness that contained nothing, because Ferro had made sure of that hours ago.

Sef started downstream again, along the bank, toward the falls. Toward the falls. Ferro felt his stomach drop ahead of him like a stone down a well. The falls were loud, white, lethal at night—but they were also the fastest way down to Dreele's camp, and a man with a deadline and no respect for rivers would take them. Dreele would have promised him a boat below. Dreele promised everyone things; that was practically his trade.

Ferro went down the cliff face the way he'd gone down it since he was nine years old, which is to say quickly, quietly, and with a running commentary of curses delivered entirely inside his own head.

* * *

He caught Sef at the top of the falls, where the river gathered itself and committed.

Later, when Ovid demanded the full account with gestures, Ferro would tell it plainly: he simply walked out of the rocks and stood on the trail, and Sef froze like a rabbit that has done the arithmetic on rabbits versus men and gotten a number it doesn't like. But the truth had more parts. The truth was that Ferro had planned three approaches and discarded two, and the third only worked because Sef slipped on wet stone at exactly the wrong moment, and the truth was that when Ferro stepped out, his heart was going hard enough that he half expected Sef to hear it over the water.

"Evening," Ferro said.

Sef's hand went to his belt. Ferro shook his head slowly, the way you'd shake your head at a child reaching for the stove.

"You don't want that," Ferro said. "Tamsin sews wounds, but she charges interest. And I'd hate for you to meet the falls the fast way."

"The falls are the way down." Sef's voice cracked in the middle, young, terrified, furious. "There's a boat. There's men."

"There's men," Ferro agreed. "Men who count you like sacks. You said that yourself. Best speech I heard all season." He took one step closer, gravel whispering under his boot. "So here's my question, and I'll only ask it once, because I'm not an interrogator, I'm a scout, and scouts ask better questions. What did they give you?"

Sef's jaw worked. "Nothing yet."

"Ah," said Ferro, and something in his chest unclenched and re-clenched around a different ache. "Then you're not a traitor. You're a debtor. That's worse and better both."

"They have my sister." The words came out of Sef like water through a cracked dam, all at once, years of pressure behind them. "Corrow. She works the company laundry. Dreele's man showed me a letter—her name in it, her lodging house, the door she goes through every morning. They said if the ledger reaches Corrow before the permits lapse—" He stopped. Swallowed. "They didn't say what. They didn't have to say what."

The river roared its long white roar below them, patient as everything else in the desert.

Ferro stood very still. This was the trouble with finding people: sometimes you found too much. He had come out here prepared to hate a bought man, and instead he'd found a brother, which was infinitely harder to tackle and tie.

"Give me the ledger," Ferro said.

"And my sister?" Sef's eyes were wet and blazing. "You'll give me my sister? You'll ride to Corrow tonight and stand at her door? No. Nobody gives anybody anything. That's the whole lesson of my life." He shifted his grip on the bundle, and Ferro saw him measuring the distance to the water, and Ferro's blood went cold, because a desperate man with a bundle and a waterfall does terrible mathematics.

"Sef." Ferro kept his voice level, scout-calm, the voice he used on spooked horses. "Listen to the river a moment. Just listen."

Sef didn't move. But he listened. Everyone listens, eventually, when the alternative is a decision.

"That water goes to Corrow," Ferro said. "Every drop of it. Same water that fills their laundry boilers, same water their magistrate drinks, same water that's going to carry every name in that bundle to the sea eventually, because that's what rivers do—they deliver. You want to help your sister? That bundle at the courthouse helps her. That bundle in Dreele's fire helps nobody, least of all her, because men who burn ledgers don't release hostages, they make more of them." He paused. "I know the man who wrote that letter exists. I've met his kind. They show you a door and promise to guard it, and the day you're no use, they show another man the same door."

Sef's shoulders dropped an inch. An inch is a lot, at the top of a waterfall in the dark.

"I can't promise you your sister," Ferro said, because lying to a frightened man seemed both cruel and inefficient. "But I can promise you this: we go to Corrow anyway. All of us. Ledger, magistrate, the whole ugly business. And Halloway's got crown authority now the permits are dead, and crown authority can put a guard on a laundry door faster than Dreele can write a second letter. Come back to camp, stand in front of Elodie, and tell her what you told me. Every word." He tilted his head. "She's terrifying, but she's fair. It's her worst quality."

Something happened to Sef's face then—something collapsing and rebuilding at once, like a scaffold coming down off a finished building. "She'll hang me."

"She won't. She'll do worse. She'll forgive you, and then you'll have to live up to it." Ferro held out his hand. "Ledger."

For a long moment the only sound was the falls, and the wind worrying the scrub, and two men breathing.

Then Sef said, "If anything happens to her—"

"Then it happens to all of us too," Ferro said. "That's the arrangement. That's the whole point of a crew. We carry what can't be carried alone." He wiggled his fingers. "Bundle. Before I get sentimental. I hate that."

Sef laughed—one broken, astonished note of it—and held out the ledger.

And that was when the lantern light came around the bend downstream.

Three lights, moving quick and disciplined, spaced the way hired men space themselves when they're hunting. Ferro's stomach performed a maneuver Ovid would have admired. The boat below the falls wasn't waiting for Sef's arrival. Its escort had come up to collect.

"Change of plan," Ferro murmured, already turning, hand closing on Sef's collar and the ledger both. "Run first. Apologize later."

Behind them, a voice carried over the water, flat and company-precise: "There! Take the bundle alive—the boy can drown!"

They ran, upstream along the dark bank, three lanterns blooming behind them like bad flowers, and Ferro grinned into the night because terror and relief feel identical in the legs, and ahead—two miles of river, one exhausted traitor-brother, one irreplaceable bundle of the dead's names, and a camp full of people who were going to have opinions.

He hoped Elodie was awake. He hoped, for all their sakes, that she'd had the coffee on.
