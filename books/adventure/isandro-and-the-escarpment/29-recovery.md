## 29. Recovery

Elodie had the coffee on.

She'd had the coffee on since midnight, which was not a decision so much as a confession—she had known, in the way she knew weather and knew lies and knew the particular quality of silence that meant something was about to go wrong, that the night was not finished with them. So when Ferro came up out of the dark at a dead run with Sef's collar in one fist and terror written all over his usually unreadable face, she was already standing, already handing Tamsin her coat, already saying, "Talk," before anyone had to decide how to begin.

"Ledger's safe," Ferro gasped. "Mostly. Mostly safe. The falls—"

"The falls," Elodie repeated, because the word *mostly* was doing terrible work in that sentence, and because Isandro was already moving, grabbing a rope coil from the gear line, and she wanted to hear the rest of it before the boy did anything heroic.

She didn't get the rest of it. She got two more sentences—"He went in after it, the pages scattered, he dove off the rocks"—and then the camp was in motion around her like an anthill kicked open, lanterns swinging, Ovid swearing with impressive creativity, Bex bellowing for dry blankets, and Elodie running.

The river ran loud this time of year, swollen with the week's storms, and the falls were half a mile downstream where the canyon pinched and dropped the water through a throat of black rock. She ran it in the dark on ground she knew by feel, Ferro and Sef pounding along behind her, and the whole way she was doing arithmetic she hated: cold water, spring current, a boy who'd grown up in salt country and had never once, as far as she knew, swum anything deeper than a stock tank.

*He can drown,* the company man had said, according to Ferro. Said it like a scheduling note.

"Where?" she shouted, and Ferro pointed, and there—caught in the wash of Tamsin's lantern, who had somehow gotten ahead of everyone because Tamsin always got ahead of everyone when it mattered—was the pool below the falls, churning white and furious, and no boy in it.

No boy. No bundle. Just the water going over the drop in a long silver sheet and pooling and spinning and sliding away south, indifferent as arithmetic.

"He went under there," Ferro said, pointing at the base of the falls, at the place where the falling water folded back on itself and held whatever it caught. "Twenty seconds ago. Maybe thirty."

Thirty seconds. Elodie had pulled drowning men out of rivers before; she knew what thirty seconds meant and what ninety meant, and she stood at the edge of the pool with a rope in her hands and made the coldest calculation of her life, which was that if she went in after him, they would likely pull out two bodies instead of one, and that knowing this changed nothing whatsoever about what she was going to do.

Tamsin's hand closed on her arm. "I'm lighter than you," Tamsin said. "And I've done this in flood water. Tie me."

It was the sensible argument. It was correct in every particular. Elodie heard herself say, "Both of us, then," and was already knotting the rope around her own waist when the water gave him up.

He came up in the boil below the falls like something the river had decided, at the last moment, not to keep—one arm flailing, one arm locked around a sodden leather bundle pressed to his chest, his head tipping back and under and back again. He got half a breath. He got his eyes open, and even across the churning dark, even at that distance, Elodie saw him find her lantern light and fix on it the way a man fixes on the only star there is.

"Hold!" she screamed, and it wasn't a word, it wasn't instruction, it was just everything she had, thrown across the water.

Then she went in.

The cold hit her like a door. Spring snowmelt, storm-fed, the kind of cold that stops a heart's opinion before the drowning gets a chance to. She swam anyway—badly, heavily, the rope dragging behind her, Tamsin paying it out from the bank—and the current took her sideways almost at once, and she understood in one bright flash why sensible people did not do this, and kept swimming.

Isandro went under again. Came up. Went under. Each time the bundle came up with him, gripped in both arms now, and some small distant part of Elodie's mind—the part that had trained him, the part that was proud of him past all sense—registered that he had not let go of it, that the boy who had once stolen water because he was dying of thirst would not let go of two hundred names even to save his own breath.

*That's my fault,* she thought, absurdly, furiously, stroking through water that fought her like a living thing. *I taught him to hold on to things.*

Her fingers found cloth. His sleeve. Then his wrist, slick and shockingly thin, and then his collar, and she hauled him against her and he was still fighting—not her, the water, the instinct to breathe, his legs churning uselessly—and she got her arm across his chest from behind, the way you're supposed to, the way she'd taught a dozen green rangers and never once believed she'd need it herself.

"I have you," she said into his ear. "I have you. Let the river have your legs. I have the rest of you."

Whether he heard or simply ran out of strength, she never learned. But he stopped fighting her, and the rope came taut, and above them Tamsin and Bex and Ovid and Ferro—all four of them, all four of them hauling like the world depended on it, which as far as Elodie was concerned it did—began to win, inch by inch, against the Anselm.

They came out of the water on the gravel bar like things born rather than rescued, and Elodie knelt in the shallows holding a boy who was not breathing.

* * *

Later, she would remember it in pieces, the way bad nights are remembered: Tamsin's hands, sure and fast, rolling him; the ledger bundle set down gently on the stones by Ferro, who then stood over it dripping like a sentinel; Ovid turning away and pressing both fists to his mouth; Bex saying, quietly, steadily, "Come on, lad, come on, you stubborn scrap of nothing, come on," in the tone of a woman arguing with God and expecting to win.

And Elodie kneeling opposite Tamsin with her hands hovering useless, because there is a moment in every rescue when the rescuer becomes a spectator, and it is the worst moment there is.

"Tamsin," she said. Her voice came out wrong. "Tamsin—"

"Don't talk." Tamsin tilted his head, pressed, counted. Pressed again. Water came. More water. Then—a sound, a wet ragged cough that was the finest sound Elodie had ever heard in twenty years of deserts—and Isandro turned onto his side and vomited half the Anselm onto the gravel and began, between convulsive breaths, to cry.

He was sixteen years old and he cried the way boys cry when they've been trying not to for a very long time—silently, furiously, hating every second of it—and Elodie gathered him up against her chest without deciding to, one hand cradling the back of his head, and felt him shake, and shook a little herself, and let the whole camp see both.

"It's all right," she said, which was a lie, because nothing was all right, the ledger was soaked, the company was hunting them, and the boy had nearly died for names that weren't even his. "You're all right. You're here. Breathe when it comes, don't chase it."

"You d-dropped—" he managed, furious at his own stammering, "you dropped everything, you were going to leave the ledger, you were going to let it go down—"

"Yes," Elodie said.

"You always say—" A cough wracked him. "Take nothing you can't carry. Leave no name behind. You always say—"

"And I meant to let it drown," Elodie said, "and I went in after you instead. Do you understand what I'm telling you? I went in after *you.*"

He looked up at her then, blue-lipped, hair plastered to his forehead, eyes enormous in the lantern light, and she watched him try to make sense of it and fail, and she realized—with a clarity that arrived like the cold water had, all at once and everywhere—that he genuinely hadn't known. That in six years he had never once been certain, not really, whether he was crew or cargo. Whether he was hers or merely assigned to her.

Well. That ended tonight.

"Listen to me." She took his face in both hands, and her voice cracked down the middle and she let it. "Six years ago I found a starving boy stealing water on the salt flats, and I could have turned him out, and I didn't, and every day since then I have told myself it was mercy, and it was never mercy. It was mine. You were mine from the first morning you ate my breakfast and complained about it. You are mine, Isandro—mine to teach, mine to scold, mine to lose sleep over, and mine to protect, and I am telling you aloud, in front of witnesses, so that you can never again wonder where you stand. Do you hear me?"

Behind her, she heard Bex blow her nose with tremendous dignity. She heard Ovid mutter, "About bloody time," and Ferro say nothing at all, which from Ferro was a standing ovation.

Isandro's face did something complicated. "Even though I—" he started, and she knew he meant the diving, the recklessness, the ledger clutched to his chest in the boiling water, and she cut him off.

"*Especially* then. Though we will be having a conversation about the difference between brave and stupid, and it will be long, and you will hate it."

"That's fair," he whispered.

"Good." She pulled him back against her shoulder and held on, and the river went on talking its endless talk beside them, and nobody moved for a while, because nobody needed to.

* * *

They carried him up to the fire wrapped in every dry blanket the camp owned, looking, as Bex observed, "like a parcel the desert finally decided to deliver." Tamsin checked him over twice—ribs, lungs, the deep shiver that meant the cold had gotten further in than skin—and pronounced him bruised, half-drowned, and structurally sound. "He'll be sore for a week and insufferable for two."

"He skipped the sore part," Isandro said from inside the blankets, teeth still chattering. "I'm already insufferable."

"See?" said Tamsin.

The ledger was another matter. They laid it open by the fire, page by page, and the news was better than it had any right to be: the paymaster had bound his book in oiled leather, and the core bundle—the roster itself, two hundred names in a clerk's careful hand, payments and dates and signatures—had survived the river nearly intact. The outer pages, the loose correspondence, had fared worse. Several sheets had torn free in the current and gone, and Ferro described, with unusual gentleness, watching them scatter white on the black water like a flock of startled birds.

"How many lost?" Elodie asked.

Ovid, who had been counting, held up three fingers. "Three letters. Fragments. The names are all in the bound section—every name, I checked twice. What we lost is paper trail. Context." He hesitated. "One of them might have been the magistrate's letter. Can't be sure."

"Then we say nothing about what we lost," Elodie said. "We present what we have, and what we have is enough. Two hundred names don't drown."

"No," Bex agreed, turning a dried page toward the firelight with the reverence of a woman handling scripture. "They float. Apparently."

Sef sat apart from the circle, wrists bound in front of him with soft cord, watching the ledger dry with an expression Elodie couldn't fully read—shame, mostly, but underneath it something that looked like relief, the look of a man setting down a weight he'd forgotten he was carrying. Dreele had bought him with money and threats in equal measure, it emerged in pieces over the next hour: money for his family downstream, threats for himself. He'd taken the ledger at dusk and run for the fork, meaning to trade it back for his people's safety.

"They have my sister," he said simply, when Elodie crouched in front of him. "Dreele's men took her off the caravan road two weeks ago. I'm sorry. I'd do it again. I'm sorry."

Elodie studied him for a long moment. Six years ago she'd have turned him out into the dark. Three years ago she'd have turned him over to Vane's justice, if Vane could be said to have any. Tonight she looked at a frightened man who'd betrayed a found family to save his actual one, and found she couldn't summon a single clean feeling about it.

"Ferro caught you," she said. "Ferro decides."

Everyone looked at Ferro, who had been uncharacteristically quiet since the falls, sitting with his elbows on his knees and the river-light moving on his face. He looked at Sef for a while.

"You ran well," he said at last. "For a porter." And then, standing: "He stays tied until Corrow. After that, he carries double loads and eats last, and when we get to the magistrate, he testifies. All of it. His sister too—we get her back. That's the price."

Sef bowed his head. "Yes."

"That's the whole price," Ferro added, and something in his tone closed the matter the way a door closes.

* * *

The camp settled toward dawn the way water settles after a stone. Ovid took first watch with more weapons than usual. Pell, woken by the commotion, hovered at the edge of the firelight until Isandro waved him over, and the two of them sat shoulder to shoulder drying the ledger pages by turns, the junior engineer handling each sheet as if it might bite, which in a sense it might—it was, after all, the most dangerous paper in a hundred miles.

Elodie should have slept. Tamsin told her to sleep. Bex told her to sleep with the specific authority of a woman who controlled the coffee. Instead she sat on the high bank above the camp, wrapped in her own damp coat, and watched the river.

It looked innocent now. It always did, afterward. That was the trick of it—rivers and companies and men like Dreele, all of them smooth on the surface, all of them keeping their wreckage private. Somewhere downstream, the demolition team was loading its charges. Somewhere upstream, Sable Vane was regrouping with hired guns and a grudge twenty years old. Somewhere in a strongbox of a mind, Dreele was recalculating, and men like Dreele always recalculated toward cruelty.

But here, on this gravel bar, a boy had refused to let go of two hundred names, and a crew had hauled him out of a flooded river by main strength, and a traitor was sleeping bound by his own request because the alternative was a darkness he trusted less.

"Captain." Tamsin lowered herself onto the bank beside her, unhurried, offering a tin cup. Coffee, black enough to trot a horse across. "You went into flood water after him. You know that's against your own rules."

"My rules are for other people."

"Mm." Tamsin drank. "Funny thing about rules. The ones we break for love tend to be the only ones worth having."

Elodie snorted softly and drank her coffee, and for a while neither of them said anything, which was the oldest and best conversation they had.

Down at the fire, Isandro had finally gone under—truly asleep, dead weight, Ferro adjusting the blanket over him with the exaggerated casualness of a man who would deny it under oath. The ledger lay drying on flat stones in a careful fan, ink ghosting pale where the river had gotten to it, names surviving.

Two hundred names. Elodie found she could almost hear them, the way you hear a river through a canyon wall—not the words themselves, but the fact of them, running.

"We move at first light," she said. "Downstream. Straight for Corrow, no more detours, no more cleverness. Vane will try to intercept—I'd bet the ledger on it. And when she does..."

She didn't finish. She didn't have to. Tamsin followed her gaze to the sleeping boy, to the drying names, to the east, where the sky had begun, very faintly, to consider the idea of gray.

"When she does," Tamsin said quietly, "we'll be ready."

But Elodie was looking at the water, and thinking about what it carried, and wondering—not for the first time that night—what Sable Vane did with her sleepless hours, and whether somewhere out there in the dark, the rival who knew her oldest secret was already turning toward them through the night, drawn downstream by the same current that had tried, and failed, to take the ledger for good.
