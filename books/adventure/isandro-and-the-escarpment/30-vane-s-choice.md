## 30. Vane's Choice

Sable Vane had been rained on before. She had been shot at before, betrayed before, and once—though she told no one this—married for eleven days in a border town whose name she'd paid a clerk to forget. But she had never been all three at once, and she was beginning to suspect the combination would be fatal to something in her that had survived everything else.

The rain came down the gorge in gray sheets, turning the sandstone walls weeping orange, pooling in the wheel ruts of the old Concession road until the whole canyon floor looked like a river remembering what it used to be. Above her, on the rim, torchlight moved in disciplined intervals. Company guns. Sixteen of them by her count, maybe twenty, fanning out along both rims with the unhurried competence of men who had done this kind of work for pay and expected to be paid again.

Below her, fifty yards across the flooded flat, the ranger camp huddled under tarps: Yarrow's crew, the porters, the boy, and the ledger—the paymaster's ledger, two hundred names in faded ink, sitting in an oilskin wrap under Tamsin's elbow like a sleeping animal everyone was pretending not to watch.

And between them, Sable Vane, holding a lantern in one hand and a pistol she had no intention of drawing in the other, waiting for Elodie Yarrow to come stand in the rain with her.

*You built this,* she thought, watching the water sheet off the lantern's tin roof. *Twenty years of building it. Every careful step.*

She had told herself, riding up from the border towns with hired guns and forged papers, that this was simply business concluded. That the ledger was a loose thread and she was a woman who owned scissors. She had even believed it, in the way she believed most convenient things, right up until the moment her own men had put their rifles on the porters and she had seen the boy Isandro walk into the gun line with his hands open and his chin up, and something in her chest had turned over like a cart on a bad grade.

The boy was still alive. That was the only entry in her favor, and she knew it wouldn't balance.

Boots splashed behind her. She didn't turn. She'd know that stride in her grave—in whatever grave anyone bothered to dig for a woman with no name worth carving.

"Vane." Elodie stopped three paces off, rain hammering her hat brim, and Vane turned and saw that the captain of no names had come unarmed. Not even a knife. That was either trust or contempt, and after twenty years Vane still couldn't always tell which.

"Yarrow." Her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "You look terrible."

"You look rich."

"Only on the outside." Vane gestured upward with the lantern, at the torches crawling the rim. "Your audience arrived early. I was promised until dawn."

"Dreele doesn't keep promises. You knew that when you signed with him."

"I've never signed anything with Dreele. I hire people who sign things with Dreele. It's called insulation." She heard how it sounded even as she said it—the old Sable, the clever one, the one who always stood one remove back from the fire—and watched Elodie's face do nothing at all, which was worse than any expression.

"The porters," Elodie said. "They're released. Now. Before we talk about anything else."

"They're released." Vane said it quickly, because it was true—she'd countermanded her own guns an hour ago, when the rain started and the rim positions lit up and she understood that Dreele's men hadn't come to support her but to tidy her. "They're sitting under your tarps drinking Bex's terrible tea. My men are disarmed where they can see them. All except me." She lifted the pistol slightly and then, deliberately, turned it and held it out grip-first through the rain.

Elodie didn't take it.

"Keep it," the captain said. "You'll want it tonight, and I'd rather you had it pointed the right way."

That undid something. Vane felt it go—a small structural failure somewhere behind her sternum, load-bearing, twenty years old. She lowered the gun slowly and found her hand wasn't quite steady, and hated that Elodie saw it, and hated more that Elodie pretended not to.

"So," Vane said. "Converging guns above us, a flood below us, and between us the only document in the Escarpment worth killing for. Shall we discuss terms? I'm told you're good at terms."

"I'm going to offer you something," Elodie said, "and you're going to want to shoot me for offering it, and I'd ask you to wait until I finish."

"Sable Vane," Vane said, spreading her free hand, "waiting for the end of a sentence. The river towns will speak of it for a generation."

"Your name is in that ledger."

The rain filled the silence. Somewhere upstream, thunder rolled along the canyon like a barrel being dragged upstairs.

"I know what's in the ledger," Vane said carefully.

"You know there are payments. You don't know what else. Tamsin read it by lamplight last night—all of it, every page. Two hundred names of the dead. The paymaster's disbursements. And the signatures." Elodie stepped closer, close enough that the lantern light caught the rain running down her face, so that it might have been tears or might have been weather. "It isn't just a list of bribes, Sable. It's a confession with witnesses. Payments authorized, payments received, payments *witnessed*. Three columns. Your signature is in the third column. So is mine."

Vane had known. Of course she had known—that was why she'd ridden north with guns instead of lawyers. But hearing it said aloud, in Elodie's flat ranger cadence, made it real in a way her own midnight arithmetic never had. "Forged," she said. "Mine was forged. I never—"

"Mine too." Elodie said it without flinching, and Vane realized with a strange lurch that this was the confession—not hers, Elodie's, offered first, freely, standing in the rain with no weapon and no crew within earshot. "Twenty years ago I carried a reclassification paper through the Concession because a magistrate's clerk told me it was boundary work. I couldn't read the seals. I could read well enough now. What I carried let them call murdered miners trespassers. My signature witnessed it." She paused. "I didn't know. But the dead don't care what I knew. They care that my name is on the paper that buried them."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because here's my offer." Elodie's voice dropped, not softer—lower, the way water goes lower before it goes over the falls. "Halloway. The magistrate's son, in Corrow. He has the ledger, he has Pell, he'll have your testimony if you give it. And Halloway is an honest man, which means he's a dangerous one, because honest men follow documents wherever they lead—including to forged signatures in the third column. Including yours. Including mine."

"And?" Vane's mouth had gone dry despite the deluge. "You're offering me a trial?"

"I'm offering you a deal. Testimony—full, sworn, on the record, against the company and against whoever survives of the signing magistrates. In exchange, Halloway strikes the forged signatures. Yours and mine. Not hidden—*struck*, with his own seal, entered into the record as forgeries, so that no one can ever hang a massacre on a name that never consented to be there." Elodie's jaw tightened. "The dead get their names read aloud at dawn. We get ours taken back. That's the trade. Take it or burn."

For a long moment Sable Vane could not speak, and the reason was not the rain.

Because here was the thing, the thing she had carried up the river with her guns and her grudge and her beautiful insulated distance: she did not actually want the ledger destroyed. She had told herself she did. She had told her hired guns she did. But what she wanted—what she had wanted for twenty years, alone, in fine rooms, counting money that never once bought her an hour's sleep—was to have never signed it. To reach back into that room in the paymaster's office where a younger, hungrier Sable Vane had witnessed a disbursement she hadn't understood yet, and unmake her own hand.

And Elodie Yarrow, who had every reason to let her hang, was standing in a flood offering her exactly that.

"That's not mercy," Vane said hoarsely. "You don't do mercy. You do ledgers. What's the cost?"

"The cost is you say it out loud. On the record. In Corrow, in front of the town, in front of the families if any come. You tell them what the company ordered and what the paymaster paid and what you saw. You spend your name buying theirs back." Elodie almost smiled; it was a terrible, tired thing. "You spent twenty years making sure no one could find you, Sable. I'm asking you to stand in a courthouse and be found."

Above them, a torch went out on the rim. Then another. Vane's eyes went up on instinct—twenty years of reading ridgelines—and her stomach dropped. "They're repositioning."

"They're advancing. Down both rims, and a skiff party on the water. Dreele's orders came by pigeon an hour ago—we caught the bird. Burn the canyon clean before the permits lapse. Starting with anyone holding evidence." Elodie glanced at the camp, at the oilskin bundle, and Vane saw her do the same cold arithmetic Ovid did, the arithmetic of minutes. "So I need your answer now. Not at dawn. Now."

"And if I say no? If I keep my guns and my insulation and ride out of this gorge the way I've ridden out of everything?"

"Then in a week you'll be a woman whose name appears in a burned ledger that no longer exists, tied to a massacre nobody can prove happened, employed by men who are currently moving to erase you along with us." Elodie shrugged, and it was the gentlest cruelty Vane had ever received. "You've seen what they do to loose threads."

The rain hammered the lantern. Downriver, faint through the storm, came the knock of oars on gunwales—the skiff party, closing.

Sable Vane thought about the eleven-day marriage. She thought about the clerk she'd paid, and the border town, and how every erasure she had ever purchased had felt, for exactly one night, like freedom—and every morning after like a smaller room. She thought about a boy walking into a gun line with open hands, and understood suddenly that he had learned that from Elodie, and that Elodie had learned it from no one, and that courage like that was the one commodity all her money had never bought.

She thought about her name. Her real one, the one from before, the one she'd buried so deep she sometimes had to stop and dig for it. *Marguerite.* A grandmother's name. A name for a woman who planted things.

"If I testify," she said, "Dreele's men will hunt me the rest of my life."

"Yes."

"If I burn the ledger, I live comfortable and hollow, and two hundred people stay dirt."

"Yes."

"You're enjoying this."

"A little," Elodie admitted, and for the first time in twenty years Sable Vane laughed in front of her—a short, cracked, astonished sound, swallowed by the rain.

Then Vane did the hardest thing she had ever done, harder than the marriage, harder than the signing, harder than every escape: she reached into her coat, took out the sealed orders she'd been carrying since the border—her insurance, her insulation, Dreele's own handwriting promising her the source rights—and held them out to the rain.

"Testimony," she said. "Full. Sworn. Loud enough to embarrass a governor. And Yarrow—" her voice caught, and she let it, because a woman choosing redemption ought to sound like it cost something, "—when it's done, when Halloway strikes those signatures, I want to hear my name spoken once. Out loud. By someone who isn't selling me or hunting me. Just once. Then I'll take my exile like a lady."

Elodie took the orders from her hand, and her grip stayed closed around Vane's fingers a moment longer than the transaction required. "Marguerite," she said quietly.

Vane's breath went out of her like she'd been shot after all.

"How—" 

"I've known for nineteen years. I've known since the day you left." Elodie's face, in the lantern light, was finally doing something: not softness exactly, but its rough country cousin. "You were never as invisible as you thought. None of us were. That was always the joke, wasn't it? We erased ourselves for people who'd already written us down."

A flare went up on the rim—white, then red. Company signal. Advancing.

"They're coming," Vane said, and the old competence rose in her like floodwater, welcome, familiar, useful at last. "Skiff party reaches your camp in ten minutes. Rim teams in fifteen. You can't fight twenty with—" she counted the camp with a glance— "eleven, and half of those are porters."

"No," Elodie agreed. "But we don't have to fight them. We have to *beat* them downriver." Her eyes went to the flooded gorge, the brown torrent tearing through the narrows, and Vane followed her gaze and felt her stomach drop a second time, because she understood. Rafts. The whole mad company of them, ledger and porters and all, racing the demolition team down forty miles of flooded gorge to a courthouse.

"That river will kill half of you."

"It killed two of ours already. I won't feed it more by standing still." Elodie was already turning, already raising two fingers toward the camp—the signal Vane remembered from another lifetime, another canyon, *move now, grieve later.* "Ovid rafted worse water at seventeen. Probably. He claims. Can you handle a sweep oar?"

Sable Vane looked at the river that had carved the whole Escarpment out of stubborn stone, at the torches converging on the rims, at the woman who had just handed her back her own soul with a signature struck through it.

"I once ran the Sorrel Falls at spring melt," she said, "for a bet, drunk, in a boat I'd stolen that afternoon."

"Good enough. Get your men under cover before the rim teams see them—if they die tonight, it's murder, and we need Dreele's hands clean-looking when we hang him with paperwork." A ghost of the old warmth, quick and gone. "Welcome to the crew, Marguerite. Try not to steal anything."

Vane turned for the trees where her disarmed men waited, and stopped, and looked back through the rain.

"Yarrow. For what it's worth—I didn't come up this river to destroy you. I told myself I did. But I think I came to find out if you'd become what I became." She smiled, and it hurt her face, and she let it stay anyway. "You didn't. Someone had to."

Then the flare-light caught the water, the skiffs rounded the bend below, and there was no more time for anything—not grief, not grace, not twenty years of unsaid things—only the river, rising, and the race downstream about to begin.

* * *

From the rim, high above the drowned camp, the company spotter watched two figures shake hands in the lantern light, and signaled down the line: *Vane has turned. All parties. Take them all.*
