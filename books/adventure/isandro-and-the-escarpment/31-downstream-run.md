## 31. Downstream Run

Ovid had built things that went boom for most of his adult life, and he had a professional's opinion about the demolition charges Dreele's men left stacked on the ledge above the falls: sloppy work.

"Look at the spacing," he muttered, crouched behind a boulder with Ferro's spyglass pressed to his eye. "No pattern. No redundancy. They just piled them like firewood and walked away."

"You're critiquing the aesthetics of the explosives that are meant to destroy our evidence," Tamsin said.

"I'm critiquing their *craft*, Tamsin. It's insulting. That's enough black powder to drop half the canyon wall into the river, and they've wired it like a drunk wiring a chandelier." He handed back the spyglass. "Also, it means we have maybe two days before someone comes back to finish the job, which is why we're doing this the stupid way."

"The stupid way being?"

Ovid pointed downriver, where the Anselm—swollen fat and brown with the week's rains, chewing through the gorge like it had a personal grudge—disappeared around a bend toward Corrow. Three days by trail. One day by water, if the water didn't kill you.

"Rafts," he said.

Tamsin looked at the river for a long moment. "I'd like the record to show I objected."

"Noted," said Elodie, coming up the bank with Isandro and Pell in tow. She was gray-faced and damp from hauling Isandro out of the current two nights ago, and she moved like a woman running on arithmetic instead of sleep. "Record it in your head, because nothing we write down survives this expedition. Ovid. Can you build us something that floats?"

Ovid spread his hands. "Captain, I can build you something that *mostly* floats. The distinction is going to matter."

* * *

He built four rafts out of cottonwood trunks, pack-saddle leather, rope, and the last of Bex's precious pitch, working through the night while the porters cut wood and Pell held torches and asked questions in the tone of a man who had recently learned that his employers were arsonists and was recalibrating his entire personality accordingly.

"Why lashed and not nailed?" Pell asked.

"Nails crack in rapids. Rope flexes." Ovid cinched a crossbeam and tested it with his whole weight. "Everything flexible survives. Everything rigid breaks. It's the first rule of demolitions and, as far as I can tell, the first rule of people too."

Pell considered that. "Is that why your crew has lasted twenty years?"

"It's why anything lasts." He glanced over at where Elodie stood at the water's edge talking low and fast with Vane—their new, deeply improbable ally, arms crossed, rain still dripping off her hat brim—and lowered his voice. "Mostly."

The ledger rode in a waterproof oilskin bundle strapped to the center of the lead raft, wrapped twice, tied with a quick-release knot Ovid had invented at three in the morning and named after no one because naming things after yourself was how you got remembered, and being remembered was against his religion.

By first light they launched: twenty-two souls on four rafts, Vane's remaining guns split between rear guard duty and white-knuckle grip work, and the Anselm taking them all in its brown fist like it had been waiting all season for exactly this.

* * *

The first hour was almost pleasant, which Ovid recognized immediately as the river lying to them.

The gorge narrowed. The banks rose. The water, which had been moving fast, began moving *fast*, and the sound changed from rushing to roaring, and Ferro—who had scouted this stretch years ago and claimed to remember every bend—stood at the bow of the lead raft shouting hand signals downstream.

"Rapids!" Ferro called back. "Half a mile! Stay off the left side, there's a strainer!"

"A what?" yelled one of Vane's hired guns, a big man called Bratch who had joined the redemption cause with the enthusiasm of someone who hadn't thought it through.

"Fallen tree in the current!" Ovid shouted. "It eats rafts! Paddle right!"

They paddled right. Mostly. The third raft, carrying three porters and Bex—who had appointed herself quartermaster of terror and was distributing instructions the way she distributed flour, precisely and without sentiment—drifted left when the current grabbed it, and Ovid watched, heart in his throat, as the black branches of the strainer reached out over the water like a drowned man's fingers.

"Bex!" he roared.

"I see it!" She did not sound afraid. She sounded *furious*, which was worse. "Porters! Lean! LEAN!"

The raft leaned. The current argued. For three full seconds—which Ovid would later describe to anyone who asked as the longest three seconds of a life that included several actual explosions—the raft hung sideways against the pull, and then the porters threw themselves starboard in a tangle of limbs and the bow swung clear, missing the strainer by an arm's length, and Bex's voice carried over the roar:

"SUPPLIES ACCOUNTED FOR!"

"She nearly died," Tamsin said, beside him, "and her summary is about inventory."

"That's how you know she's fine."

Then the real rapids took them, and there was no more conversation.

Ovid had blown bridges, tunnels, and once, regrettably, a chapel, and none of it had prepared him for the sheer indifferent violence of a flooded gorge. The river didn't fight you; that was the terrible thing. It simply picked up the raft and *decided*, and all your paddling was a polite suggestion it acknowledged or ignored according to its mood. They went through standing waves taller than a man. They spun. They shipped water. Somewhere behind him a porter lost his grip and slid halfway off the raft and Isandro—of course Isandro, the boy had a magnet in him for exactly the wrong moment—caught the man's wrist and hauled him back with a strength that seemed borrowed from somewhere else.

And then, over the roar, a sound Ovid knew better than his own heartbeat.

Gunfire.

* * *

"Muzzle flash, high left!" Ferro screamed, pointing up at the canyon rim. "Dreele's riders! They're pacing us along the bluff trail!"

Of course they were. Ovid ducked flat as a bullet slapped the water a yard short of the raft. Dreele had received his orders to expedite—he'd seen the pigeon message, everyone had—and a company man with a deadline doesn't wait for the canyon to do his work. He'd ride the rim trail, which paralleled the river here, and pick them apart at his leisure.

"Return fire?" Bratch shouted, dragging his rifle around.

"With what aim?" Ovid yelled back. "We're on a bucking log! Save your powder!"

But Elodie was already moving. She crawled to the stern, pulled the raft's spare sweep oar free, and jammed it upright in the lashings—a mast, of sorts, bare and obvious.

"What—" Pell started.

"Flag," Elodie said grimly. "Civilians under fire. Company men hate witnesses to their shooting. It's the one law they still pretend to obey." She tore her own shirt-tail loose and knotted it to the oar top, a rag of white snapping in the spray. "It won't stop them. But it'll slow them. Men hesitate when they think a magistrate might ask questions later, and Halloway is going to be asking *all* the questions."

It worked, a little. The firing became sporadic, careful, aimed at the rafts' rigging rather than the people—one shot parted a lashing on the second raft and Ovid heard the wood groan and swore with genuine feeling.

"They're not trying to sink us," he realized aloud. "They're trying to *cripple* us. Slow us down so the demolition team gets to Corrow first and the story dies before we arrive."

"Then we don't slow down," Elodie said.

Ahead, the gorge bent hard right, and Ferro—who had gone very quiet for the last minute, which from Ferro meant either great calm or great alarm—turned and shouted the thing Ovid would carry to his grave:

"HOLE IN THE ROCK! The river goes THROUGH the hill! There's a tunnel! Half a mile of tunnel!"

"There's no tunnel on any map," Pell cried.

"The maps are company maps!" Ferro grinned, wild and soaked. "Company maps don't admit the river has opinions! Paddle for the middle channel—it's the only way past the bluff trail, and Dreele's riders can't follow us underground!"

Ovid looked at the hole. It was less a tunnel than a wound—a raw black arch in the canyon wall where the river bored straight through a shoulder of stone, water pouring in and (presumably) out the other side, the space above the current maybe six feet of screaming dark.

"We'll be inside a gun barrel," Tamsin said.

"Yes," Ovid agreed cheerfully, "but a gun barrel nobody's shooting down. Ferro! Lead us in!"

* * *

The tunnel ate them.

Darkness, total, except for the pale ghost-light ahead marking the exit and the phosphorescent foam churned up along the walls. The roar doubled, trapped and amplified, a sound you felt in your teeth and ribs. Ovid crouched low over the ledger bundle with his body wrapped around it like a mother hen made of scar tissue and bad decisions, and in the black he heard—not saw, heard—Vane's voice somewhere behind him, steady as a metronome, counting strokes for the porters so they wouldn't panic. *Pull. Pull. Pull.* The woman had led ambushes on them nine days ago. Now she was a drumbeat keeping children alive in the dark. People were flexible. Everything flexible survived.

Something scraped overhead—the ceiling, close enough to touch, close enough to kill—and then the light swelled, and they shot out the far end into open sky and blinding morning, and the bluff trail was empty. Dreele's riders, balked, were already falling behind, tiny figures on the rim shaking their rifles at the sky.

The crew cheered. Actually cheered—porters and gunmen alike, hoarse and soaked and laughing, and Ovid found himself laughing too, the unhinged laugh of a man who has just survived his own profession turned inside out.

Bex's raft pulled alongside. She looked him over, checked the ledger bundle with two fingers, and said, "Supplies accounted for," but her eyes were wet, and Ovid pretended not to notice, because that was the courtesy they paid each other.

* * *

They made camp that night on a gravel bar below the tunnel, exhausted, half-drowned, victorious. The river had gentled. Somewhere downstream, thirty miles maybe, Corrow waited with its courthouse and its honest magistrate and its reckoning.

Elodie sat down next to Ovid's fire without asking, which was her way.

"You wired the rafts' spare pitch cans as flares," she said. "I saw you doing it. Why?"

"Because tomorrow we hit the last stretch of open water, and Dreele will have telegraphed ahead. There'll be men at the Corrow landing." Ovid fed the fire a stick. "When we come around the final bend, I want them looking at burning light on the water instead of aiming at us. Two seconds of confusion. Two seconds is a lot, at the end of a race."

Elodie watched the flames. "You've changed, Ovid. You used to build things to tear things down. Now you build things to get people home."

He shrugged, uncomfortable. "Same skill. Different target." Then, because the fire was warm and the hour was honest: "You know what happens when we land. Your signature's in that ledger. The moment Halloway reads it, you're a name in a public record for the first time in twenty years, and names get found."

"I know."

"And you're going to stand there anyway."

"I'm going to stand there anyway."

Ovid nodded slowly and looked downriver, where the dark water ran smooth and certain toward Corrow, carrying everything they had left—two hundred dead names, one living confession, and a boy asleep on a raft who had started as a horse thief and ended as the bravest of them.

"Well then," he said. "Best make sure we arrive loud."

Downstream, faint on the night wind, came the smell of woodsmoke from the direction of Corrow—and beneath it, unmistakable to a demolitions man's nose, the sharp chemical bite of fresh blasting powder.

Dreele's demolition team wasn't waiting at the landing.

They were already in town.
