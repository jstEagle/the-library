## 32. The Source

The river had been lying to them for six days.

That was how Elodie thought of it, anyway—though she'd never say so aloud where Isandro could hear and adopt the habit. Rivers didn't lie. Rivers simply told you what they were made of, if you knew how to listen, and for six days the Anselm had been telling them something impossible: that it was bigger than it should be.

"You're doing the face," Bex said, falling into step beside her on the morning of the seventh day.

"What face?"

"The one where you've done arithmetic you don't like and you're waiting for the numbers to apologize."

Elodie snorted. "The river's too full. We're a day's walk below the source, per Dreele's map, and it's running like it's already past the spring snowmelt. Either the map's wrong or—" She stopped herself.

"Or there's more than one spring," Bex finished. "And somebody's been counting on nobody ever checking."

They walked on in silence for a while, boots crunching on the pale gravel the canyon floor wore like a shawl. Behind them, the expedition strung out in its familiar order: Ovid and Tamsin arguing about whether the last night's camp had been haunted or merely drafty; Ferro ranging ahead and back like a dog who couldn't decide which flock was his; Pell with his instruments wrapped in oilcloth, clutching them the way other men clutched prayer books; and the porters, the ones who'd stayed, moving with the loose-shouldered ease of people who had decided, somewhere in the last hundred miles, that they trusted these strange nameless rangers more than they trusted their own paymasters.

Isandro brought up the rear, because Elodie had put him there and because he'd earned it. Rear guard was a position of trust. It said: I believe you'll notice what matters. He'd taken to it the way he took to everything—with a hunger that sometimes frightened her, and a seriousness that made the rest of them lighter by contrast.

He caught up to her at midmorning, when the canyon walls began to lean inward and the light went green and underwater-colored.

"The water's warm," he said.

"It's what?"

"Warm. Not hot-warm. Just—" he crouched, dipped two fingers in the current, held them out to her. "Feel."

She felt. He was right. A degree, maybe less, but a ranger learns to read water the way a mother reads a forehead, and this was a fever. Somewhere upstream, the Anselm was being fed from something other than cold stone.

"Ferro," she called, and the scout materialized out of the shade the way he always did, as if he'd been standing there all along waiting to be noticed. "Anything ahead I should know about?"

Ferro considered. Ferro always considered; asking him a question was like dropping a coin down a well and waiting for the splash. "Fence," he said finally.

"A fence."

"Wire and posts. Painted posts. Runs wall to wall across the canyon." He paused, savoring it. "There's a gate. And a sign."

"What does the sign say?"

Another pause. Ferro did not read well and had never once admitted it. "It says something official," he offered.

* * *

The fence was exactly as ugly as advertised. New wire on new posts, galvanized and smug, strung across a canyon that had stood unfenced since before anyone alive had been born to object. The gate was iron, painted company green, and locked with a padlock the size of a fist. And the sign, bolted to the gate at eye level, said:

**PROPERTY OF THE ANSELM CONSOLIDATED WATER & LAND CONCESSION. TRESPASSERS WILL BE PROSECUTED UNDER STATUTE 44. BY ORDER OF THE MAGISTRATE OF THE SOUTHERN DISTRICT.**

Beneath it, smaller, as if the sign-painter had run low on conviction: **NO ENTRY.**

"Well," said Ovid, spitting thoughtfully into the current. "I've been threatened by better."

"They fenced a river," Tamsin said. She wasn't laughing. None of them quite were, though Ovid's mouth kept trying. "You can't fence a river. Water doesn't recognize statute forty-four."

"Somebody bet a great deal of money that it would," Elodie said quietly.

She put her hand on the gate. The iron was cool even in the canyon heat, and under her palm she could feel, faintly, the vibration of the water moving through the works beyond—the whole hidden machinery of the source, humming like a struck tuning fork. Twenty years she had kept away from this place. Twenty years of routing around the Concession the way you route around a house where someone died badly, giving it a wide berth on every map, striking it from every ledger, teaching the crew the code without ever explaining why the code had a hole in it shaped exactly like this canyon.

And here was the forbidden thing. A fence. A lock. A sign with a bureaucrat's name on it.

She almost laughed. It came out of her as something between a laugh and a wound, and Bex looked over sharply, but Elodie waved her off.

"Ovid," she said. "The lock."

"I could pick it," Ovid said, already producing tools from somewhere in his vest with the serenity of a man asked to pass the salt. "Or—" he patted the gate frame, eyeballed the hinge pins, and smiled the smile that had gotten him banned from three mining towns and one monastery, "—we could simply remove the gate from its conversation with the fence. Less evidence of picking. More evidence of, oh, catastrophic structural disagreement."

"Do it."

The pins came out with a groan and a shriek that sent echoes tumbling up the canyon, and four of the porters swung the gate aside like men carrying a coffin—which, Elodie thought, watching them do it, was not far wrong. They laid it in the shallows, sign skyward, and the river immediately began working at the paint.

Then they walked through, and the canyon opened, and Elodie Yarrow stood inside the Concession for the first time in twenty years.

* * *

She had expected to feel something enormous. That was the thing about forbidden places—you carried them so long they grew heavy enough to hold up a sky. The Concession had been the weight behind every rule she'd made, every name she'd burned, every careful invisible year. She had built a life in the shape of its absence.

What she found was a meadow.

The canyon walls fell back on both sides, and the river spread out into a dozen bright braids wandering through grass gone gold at the tips, willows leaning over the banks, red-winged birds going up in indignant clouds. And at the head of the valley, pouring out of a seam in the cliff face itself, the spring: a broad dark mouth of stone maybe thirty feet across, water coming out of it in a steady glassy rush, cold now—she could feel the temperature change before she reached it—so clear that the bottom showed pale and unbroken, like the floor of a room no one had ever walked across.

It was beautiful. It was completely ordinary. It was just water, coming out of the ground, the way water had come out of the ground since before there were statutes or magistrates or companies with painted signs, and it would go on coming out long after every one of them was dust and their permits were kindling.

Isandro crouched at the edge of the pool below the spring mouth and cupped a handful and drank, and then looked up at her with an expression she couldn't parse until he spoke.

"That's it?" he said. "This is the place?"

"This is the place."

"It's—" He turned a slow circle, taking in the meadow, the birds, the braided silver channels. "It's nice."

Tamsin laughed—a short, startled bark—and then pressed her hand over her mouth, and then laughed again, properly, until she had to sit down on a rock. "Twenty years," she managed. "Twenty years of ghosts, and it's *nice.*"

"Don't," Elodie said, and her voice came out flatter than she intended, and everyone stopped.

Because that was the trap, wasn't it. The relief of it. You walked into the forbidden place expecting horror and found a meadow, and some traitorous part of you wanted to conclude that nothing had happened here, that the weight had been self-inflicted, that the dead were a story rangers told each other to explain why they lived like moles. The massacre hadn't happened in this meadow. It had happened downstream, in the workings, in the shaft towns—but it had happened *for* this. For this water. Men had died so that a fence could be built around a spring, and the fence was the only monument anyone had bothered to raise.

She knelt at the pool and let the cold close over her hands, and let herself remember—not the paperwork, not this time, but the faces. She'd only seen three of the miners' faces, twenty years ago, and only in the photographs clipped to the reclassification file she'd carried without reading. A man with a beard going gray at the chin. A woman in a canvas coat, arms crossed, glaring at the camera as if it owed her money. A boy about Isandro's age, trying not to smile.

She had carried their faces through the Concession in a leather tube and delivered them to a man who fed them into a stove, and she had taken her pay in silence, and she had spent twenty years calling the silence a code.

"Captain." Ferro's voice, low, at her shoulder. "There's a building past the willows. Stone. Old."

Old. Not new. That mattered.

* * *

The building had been a pump house once, or a claim office—it was hard to say, because someone had tried to burn it and failed, and the fire had taken the roof and the door and whatever the walls had said about themselves. What remained was stone, blackened at the lintels, standing open to the sky like a ribcage.

Inside, the grass grew up through the cracked floor. And against the far wall, half-toppled, someone had stacked stones.

Not rubble. Stones. Rounded ones, chosen ones, carried from the riverbank and placed with care, dozens of them, in rows. Cairns. Small ones—waist-high, some of them smaller still, some hardly more than a handful of rocks balanced on one another, the way a child builds them, the way a grieving person builds them when there is nothing else left to build with.

Tamsin went in first. She always went in first to the places where bodies had been; it was the medic in her, Elodie supposed, or something older than training. She moved down the row of cairns slowly, touching none of them, and when she reached the end she stood very still for a long moment.

"There are children's sizes," she said, when she turned around. Her voice was steady. Her eyes weren't. "Somebody buried people here. Somebody who stayed after, when everyone else ran, and did it right. No names." She swallowed. "No names, but done *right.*"

Pell had come in behind them, and the junior engineer was white as the spring foam. "The survey brief," he said. His voice cracked and he pushed on. "The brief said—site seven, structural assessment, recommend demolition. This is site seven. They want to knock this down. They want to knock this down and grade the ground and call it flood mitigation, and the permit expires in—" he fumbled for his watch, missed his pocket twice, "—days. Days, Captain Yarrow. I did the schedule myself. I thought—I told myself it was just engineering. You tell yourself things."

"Yes," Elodie said. "You do."

Isandro had stopped at the smallest cairn, the child-sized one, and he was crouched beside it, and he wasn't crying, which somehow was worse. He was looking at it the way he looked at tracks—reading it, cataloguing it, filing it somewhere behind his eyes where Elodie suspected he kept everything, every cruelty and kindness anyone had ever shown him, in perfect order.

"Whoever built these," he said, "knew the custom."

"It's an old custom," Tamsin said gently. "Stones instead of names, when names are dangerous."

"No." Isandro shook his head. "I mean—stones instead of names *until* the names are safe again. That's what you taught me. First winter. You said a cairn isn't a grave marker, it's a promise marker. You bury them plain and you come back with the names when it's safe to speak them." He looked up at her. "Nobody came back."

The meadow was very quiet. Even the birds had settled.

"No," Elodie said. "Nobody came back."

"Then we came back," Isandro said, simply, as if it were the most obvious thing in the world, as if twenty years and a fence and a statute and a company army were small obstacles compared to the plain fact of a promise outstanding. "We're here now."

Elodie looked at him—at the boy she'd pulled out of the salt flats stealing water, who had grown into this person who walked toward gun lines and dove into floods and now stood in the middle of the worst secret she owned and treated it like a debt come due—and she felt the thing she'd been refusing to feel since the ledger, since the ambush, since Vane's name in the paymaster's book alongside her own forged signature. Not dread. Not guilt, exactly, though both were in attendance.

Purpose. Clean and cold as the spring water.

"Bex," she said. "How much rope have we got?"

"Enough. Why?"

"Because we're going to do this properly." She turned to face them all—crew, porters, Pell, the lot of them, standing among the cairns in the forbidden meadow with the source roaring softly behind them like applause. "Every stone gets counted. Every cairn gets measured and drawn and written down, location and size and number, in Tamsin's hand and mine and Isandro's, three copies. Pell—" the engineer straightened, "—your instruments are going to earn their keep. Survey it. Properly. Coordinates, elevations, the works. If they want to demolish site seven, then site seven is going to exist on paper in a magistrate's office before they can strike a match."

"And the spring?" Ovid asked, jerking a thumb at the roaring dark mouth of it. "The actual prize? The reason for all the dying?"

Elodie walked to the edge of the pool. The water was so clear it seemed not to be there at all, just the pale stone of the bottom holding up the sky's reflection. She crouched and touched it one more time.

"The spring doesn't need us," she said. "Springs don't need anybody. That's rather the point. But everything downstream—every town, every field, every well from here to Corrow—drinks from this hole in the rock. Whoever holds the paper on it holds a hundred miles hostage." She stood. "So we take the paper question to the one man whose job it is to answer it. Halloway. The magistrate's son, with the magistrate's seal, in the magistrate's own court."

"Dreele won't let us walk out of this valley with that plan," Ferro said. Flat. Factual. Ferro.

"No," Elodie agreed. "He won't. Which is why we leave tonight, light and fast, down the old cart road Isandro found, and why Ovid is going to spend the afternoon having a long private conversation with that lovely new fence."

Ovid's smile returned, slow and radiant. "Catastrophic structural disagreement?"

"On a delay," Elodie said. "Long enough for us to be over the ridge. Short enough that when Dreele's men come up here to burn the cairns, they find the gate back on its hinges and welded shut by its own wreckage, and a spring that has opinions about trespassers."

Tamsin was already unpacking her kit, laying out ink and paper on a flat stone with the tenderness other people reserved for infants. Isandro knelt at the first cairn with a measuring cord, and Pell hovered at his elbow with a transit he clearly couldn't believe he was aiming at anything but ore.

Elodie stood a moment longer at the water's edge, alone with the sound of it.

"You waited," she said quietly, to the meadow, to the stones, to the bearded man and the woman in the canvas coat and the boy who hadn't quite smiled. "I'm sorry it was me. I'm sorry it took twenty years. But we're here now, and we brought names."

Behind her, Isandro called out the first measurement, his voice bright as a bird in the canyon air, and the work began.
