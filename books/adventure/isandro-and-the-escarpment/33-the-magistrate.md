## 33. The Magistrate

Magistrate Aldous Halloway had held his office in Corrow for eleven years, and in that time he had learned three things about the river towns: that disputes over water were never really about water, that a man who said "as your magistrate" too often was usually lying, and that the worst days of his career would begin with someone knocking on his door before breakfast.

The knock came at dawn, which was at least honest of it.

He was already awake, of course. He was always awake by then, sitting in the small room behind his chambers where he kept his father's things — the portrait, the ledgers, the ceremonial seal — and not looking at them, which was a discipline he had practiced so long it had become almost a form of prayer.

"Aldous," his clerk, Mrs. Tam, called through the door. She had been his clerk longer than he'd been magistrate and still refused to call him anything but Aldous, on the grounds that she'd known him when he had spots. "There's people at the gate. A lot of them. Wet ones."

"Wet?"

"Soaked to the bone, the lot of them. And they're asking for you by name." A pause, the particular pause of Mrs. Tam deciding how much to say. "They've got a boy with them who looks half-drowned and a woman who looks like she's been awake since the last flood season. And they say—" she lowered her voice as though the gate could hear her, "—they say they've come about the Concession."

Aldous set down his tea. His hand was steady, which surprised him.

"Open the gate," he said.

* * *

He knew, watching them file into his courtroom, that he should have been afraid. He had spent eleven years cultivating a careful, deliberate ignorance of what went on upriver. He had signed the renewal papers his father's old associates slid across his desk every ninety days, and he had told himself each time that a magistrate's job was order, not archaeology, and that digging into the Concession would only get good people hurt.

He had told himself this so many times that the words had worn smooth as river stones, and like river stones they no longer held any grip.

Now here were perhaps thirty people dripping on his floor: rangers with sun-cracked faces, porters with the hollow-eyed look of men who had seen a canyon eat their friends, a young engineer clutching a satchel like it contained his own heart, and at the front, a tall woman with grey coming into her dark hair who looked at him the way one looks at a locked door one has been carrying keys for.

"You're Halloway," the woman said. Not a question.

"I am Magistrate Halloway," he said, because the distinction mattered to him even if it wouldn't to her. "And you are?"

"Elodie Yarrow." She let the name sit there between them, and something in her face suggested that saying it aloud cost her something, or freed her of something; he couldn't tell which. "We've come to give you evidence, Magistrate. All of it. And we'd like it read into the record."

Mrs. Tam made a small sound from the corner, the sound of a practical woman doing arithmetic on how much ink this was going to require.

"The record," Aldous repeated carefully, "of what?"

Elodie Yarrow looked at him for a long moment. He had the uncomfortable sense of being weighed, measured, and found to be an unknown quantity — which, in his experience, was how most people saw magistrates anyway.

"Of two hundred murders," she said. "Signed off by your father."

* * *

The courtroom filled faster than Mrs. Tam could manage the doors. Word ran through Corrow the way word always did — down the laundry lines, along the docks, through the taverns before they'd properly opened — and by mid-morning there were more people outside than inside, standing in the street in the kind of silence Aldous had only heard twice before, both times at funerals.

Inside, the evidence came out piece by piece, and each piece landed on his bench like a stone dropped into still water.

The ledger first. It was water-stained and swollen, its leather cover scarred, and when Elodie opened it on his bench he saw columns of names — names, amounts, dates — running back twenty years. Payments to miners. Payments *for* miners, in the way one pays for timber. Signatures of men whose descendants still sat on councils in three river towns.

Then the maps, marked in a surveyor's hand with symbols that were not geological. Cairns. A burned homestead. A grave field of hundreds of stones, catalogued by a quiet medic named Tamsin who spoke in the flat voice of someone who had decided that if she allowed herself inflection she would not stop screaming.

Then the letters, pulled from a paymaster's strongbox above the source itself, proving that the massacre had been ordered — ordered, in writing, with a company seal — to clear the water rights before the original permits lapsed.

And then the witnesses. Porters who described the ambush at the Narrows. A demolitions expert named Ovid who explained, with the cheerful precision of a man describing a recipe, exactly what charges the company had left behind and what they'd been meant for. A scout named Ferro who said perhaps forty words total and managed to make them count. A quartermaster named Bex who produced, of all things, a ledger of her own — water debts, wages owed to dead men's families — and laid it beside the company's ledger like a mirror held up to a crime.

And Pell. The junior engineer stood at the witness rail with his hands shaking so badly that Elodie Yarrow quietly moved to stand beside him, close enough that he could steady himself against her sleeve without anyone having to say so.

"They hired us to document nothing," Pell said. His voice cracked and he pushed on. "To erase the sites before the permits expired. I thought — I told myself it was just survey work. But Dreele had orders. There were demolition charges. They were going to burn the canyon clean after the reversion." He swallowed. "I'm telling you this because I helped carry their instruments. That makes me part of it. I'd rather be part of the fixing."

Aldous wrote it all down. His hand did not shake either, and he was grateful for that, because he suspected that if it started it would not stop.

It was near noon when Elodie Yarrow turned to the ledger's final pages and said, evenly, "There's a signature in here you should see, Magistrate. It's the one that made the whole thing legal. Or legal-shaped."

She turned the book toward him.

His father's name looked back at him. Josiah Halloway, Magistrate of the River District, in the firm, handsome hand that Aldous had imitated as a boy, practicing on scraps of paper until he could forge it perfectly — a game, back then. A child's trick.

Above the signature, in the same hand, a single line of certification: *Reclassification of lands confirmed. No irregularities noted.*

No irregularities noted. Two hundred people, and his father had written *no irregularities.*

"I didn't know," Aldous heard himself say, and was immediately ashamed of it, because it was the sentence every man in that ledger must have said, and because it wasn't entirely true. He had known the shape of the thing for years. He had simply kept the shape at arm's length and called the distance prudence.

"We know," Elodie said, and to his surprise there was no cruelty in it. "That's rather the point. Nobody knew. That was the product being sold. Erasure." She tapped the ledger. "This is the un-erasing. What happens next is up to you."

* * *

He adjourned until evening, which everyone understood to mean *until I can think*, and he walked out past the crowd — nobody stopped him, which somehow made it worse — and up the hill to his father's house, which he had inherited and kept and rarely entered.

The portrait hung in the front room still. Josiah Halloway, painted at fifty, with the confident jaw of a man who had never once doubted his own signature. As a boy, Aldous had wanted nothing so much as to grow into that jaw.

"He knew," said a voice behind him.

Mrs. Tam stood in the doorway. She had followed him, which she never did, which meant she considered this more important than propriety.

"Don't," Aldous said.

"He knew," she repeated, implacable. "Your mother's cousin worked the Concession supply road, Aldous. Your father signed the reclassification six weeks after the massacre, not before. Six weeks. He had time to ask questions. He chose the signature instead." She came further into the room, arms folded. "I've watched you spend thirty years trying to be the opposite of that man, and mostly succeeding. So don't you dare stand in this room feeling sorry for him. Feel sorry for the two hundred. Then go do your job."

Aldous looked at the portrait a while longer.

"You know what the worst part is?" he said finally. "I used to practice his signature. As a game."

"Well," said Mrs. Tam, "now you can practice voiding it. Better use of the talent."

Despite everything — despite the drowned porters and the grave field and the woman downstairs who had carried a massacre in a satchel up a river — he laughed. It came out ragged, but it was a laugh.

* * *

By evening the courtroom was full past capacity, windows open to the street so those outside could hear, lanterns lit against the dusk. Aldous took his seat and found his hands were steady again, and this time he understood why. For eleven years he had been a caretaker of other people's paperwork. Tonight he was, for the first time, actually a magistrate.

He had the ledger brought forward. He had Tamsin the medic bring forward her catalogue of cairns. He had Pell repeat his testimony for the record, and the porters give their names — their actual names, spoken aloud, some of them hesitating over syllables they hadn't said in years — and he entered each one into the record in his own careful hand.

Then he rose.

"In the matter of the Anselm Concession," he said, and his voice carried out the open windows into the street, "this court finds the original reclassification fraudulent, the renewal permits void, and the land reverted to the crown and to the public trust as of this hour." He paused. "Furthermore, this court orders the full reading of the names of the dead into the permanent record, beginning tonight, and continuing until all are spoken."

From the street came a sound he had never heard in Corrow before — not cheering, not yet, but a great collective intake of breath, hundreds of people inhaling at once, as if the whole town had been holding its breath for twenty years and only now remembered how to let it out.

Elodie Yarrow watched him from the back bench with an expression he would remember for the rest of his life: not triumph, not relief, but something quieter. The look of a person setting down a weight and not quite trusting the ground to hold it.

Later, when the reading was done — the first forty names, at least; the rest would take days, and Mrs. Tam had already requisitioned more ink with the grim satisfaction of a woman preparing for siege — she approached the bench while the others filed out.

"You understand what you've done," she said. It wasn't gratitude. It was a warning wearing gratitude's coat.

"I've started something I can't stop," Aldous said. "Yes. I'd worked that out around the time I voided my father's signature."

"Dreele got away upstream with a handful of men. And the company has lawyers, magistrates-of-their-own, and worse than lawyers." She glanced toward the door, where the rangers waited — Bex counting heads, Ovid humming something tuneless, the boy Isandro standing straighter than he had that morning, as if a name spoken in a courtroom had added a spine to him. "You'll want to think about guards, Magistrate."

"I've thought about it," Aldous said. "I've also thought about the fact that the company's own charter requires disputes to be heard in the nearest crown court. Which is this one. They can't touch this record without touching the law itself, and the law is the one thing they've spent twenty years pretending to respect." He smiled, thin and tired. "So we'll hold the courthouse. With whatever the town can spare."

"That's not many men."

"No," he agreed. "But I'm told the crew that brought me this ledger once won a fight at the Narrows against worse odds, and won it ugly."

From the doorway, Ovid called out cheerfully, "We prefer 'creatively,' Magistrate!"

It was near midnight when Mrs. Tam came down from the records room, moving fast in a way she almost never moved, and put a slip of paper on his bench.

Rider from the ford, it said. Company men on the south road. Armed. Twelve at least, maybe more. Making for Corrow. They'll be here by morning.

Aldous read it twice. Then he folded it, squared its edges neatly on the bench, and looked up at the empty courtroom — at the lanterns, the ledger, the list of names waiting for dawn.

"Mrs. Tam," he said, "wake the town."
