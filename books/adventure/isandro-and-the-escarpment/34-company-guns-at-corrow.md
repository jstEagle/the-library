## 34. Company Guns at Corrow

The trouble with a town, Isandro decided, was that it had too many doors.

He'd spent the evening counting them. The courthouse in Corrow was a squat stone building with a bell tower, and it had eleven ways in if you counted the coal chute, which Ovid did, immediately, with the delight of a man handed a present. Eleven doors meant eleven things to watch, and they had—Isandro counted again on his fingers—nine defenders who could be trusted not to run, plus Magistrate Halloway, who kept insisting he could shoot, and Pell, who had never once in his life shot anything except a survey instrument.

"Twelve," Bex said, appearing beside him with a lantern hooded so low it was more suggestion than light. "You're forgetting yourself."

"I'm not much of a door."

"You're the best door we've got. You're young, you're quick, and you fit through the coal chute." She pressed something into his hand: a short club, river-polished, heavier than it looked. "Tamsin says don't aim for heads. Tamsin says a lot of things."

Down the street, Corrow's other doors were going dark one by one. That was how you knew word had spread—not by any announcement, but by the lamps. A town that had lived twenty years next to the Concession's shadow knew what it meant when company guns came upriver, and it knew to put out its lights and pretend to be nothing. Isandro understood pretending to be nothing. He'd been nothing for most of his life, and it had kept him fed.

But tonight the thing worth keeping wasn't himself. It was a ledger full of dead men's names, sitting in an iron strongbox two rooms away from where he stood, and tomorrow morning—if there was a morning—Magistrate Halloway meant to read every name into the record where no permit, no company, and no amount of burning could reach them.

Dreele's men would come tonight. Ferro had counted them coming: fourteen, maybe sixteen, moving along the levee road with the unhurried confidence of people who'd been paid to be confident.

"They'll want the fire to look like an accident," Elodie had said at the last council, her voice flat as the salt flats. "So they'll wait for full dark, and they'll bring lamp oil, and they'll be polite about it right up until they aren't." She'd looked around the lamplit room—at Bex, Ovid, Tamsin, Ferro, at Halloway in his magistrate's sash that looked like it had been borrowed from a larger man—and then at Isandro, last, longest. "Nobody here has to stay. This isn't crew business anymore. It's town business."

"We're the town now," Isandro had said, before he could think better of it, and the porters behind him—six of them, the ones who'd walked away from Dreele's clock and hadn't stopped walking until they reached Corrow—had made a sound that wasn't quite a cheer and wasn't quite a laugh, but held both.

Elodie had looked at him for a long moment. Then she'd said, "Coal chute," and that was that.

* * *

Full dark came down like a lid.

Isandro lay in the coal chute with his cheek against cold stone, listening to the building breathe. Buildings breathed differently than canyons—he'd learned that in three days of helping Ovid rig the place. Canyons breathed slow and enormous; this courthouse breathed in small creaks, timber settling, the tick of the stove cooling, Halloway upstairs clearing his throat every few minutes like a man rehearsing arguments against an empty bench.

Beside him, wedged into the chute's mouth with his knees folded under him like a folding rule, Ovid hummed very quietly. It took Isandro a while to recognize the tune—one of the work songs the porters sang on the portages, the one about the mule who outlived everyone who'd ever beaten him.

"That's not soothing," Isandro whispered.

"It's not supposed to be soothing. It's supposed to be accurate." Ovid patted the wall beside him, where he'd packed black powder into three seams behind loose facing stones. "When I tap twice, you roll left and keep rolling until you smell dust. Don't stop to admire my work. Everyone admires my work. It's a flaw in their character."

Outside, a dog started barking two streets over, thought better of it, and stopped. That was worse than the barking. Dogs in Corrow had clearly been briefed.

Then came the sound Isandro knew from the Narrows, from the ambush at dawn, from every bad memory the Escarpment had given him: boots. Not marching—company men didn't march—but the soft coordinated shuffle of many people trying to be quiet at once, which is its own kind of noise, unmistakable once you've heard it, like rain that thinks it's sneaking.

They came up the main street first, four shadows with a lantern shuttered to a coin of light, and knocked politely on the courthouse's front door. Actually knocked. Isandro heard the knocker rap three times, courteous as a tax collector.

"Magistrate Halloway," a voice called, pitched to carry. "There's been a fire at the assay office. We need access to your records room. Urgently."

"There has been no fire," Halloway called back through the door, "at the assay office or anywhere else, and my records room is closed until dawn, at which point it will be open to anyone with lawful business." A pause. "I would know. I'm the magistrate."

The politeness lasted another ninety seconds. Isandro gave it exactly that long before the front door shuddered under a battering ram that had been, until recently, a fence post.

"Now?" he whispered.

Ovid held up one finger. Waited. The ram hit again, and this time Isandro heard the old lock's iron give a groan of surrender, and heard boots start to pile up in the entryway like water finding a drain—

Ovid tapped twice.

The world went sideways in noise and orange light. The facing stones blew outward into the entry hall, and with them went the fence post, the front door, the polite voice, and a good deal of the polite voice's confidence. Isandro rolled left as instructed, smelled dust, kept rolling, and came up in the records room doorway with his ears ringing like the bell tower had fallen on him personally.

Through the smoke: shapes stumbling back off the porch, and one shape down inside, groaning, alive enough. Tamsin was already moving toward the man with her kit, because that was Tamsin, because she'd bury the dead and bandage the ones who tried to kill them and call it all the same night's work.

"Coal chute!" someone shouted outside—a new voice, angry, organizing. "They've got a man in the coal chute!"

"No they haven't," Ovid said comfortably, from the far end of the chute, where he had already relocated himself and a second surprise. "They've got two surprises in the coal chute. There's a difference."

* * *

What Isandro remembered afterward, in the years when he told this story—which he told often, being the only member of the crew who liked telling stories—was not the powder or the shouting. It was smaller things.

It was Bex on the stairs with the ledger's strongbox strapped to her back like a mother carrying a child through a flood, directing traffic in a whisper that somehow reached every corner: "Pell, away from the window, you're worth more unshot. Porters, wedge that cart against the west door. Ferro—" and Ferro was already gone, out some window Isandro never saw him use, to be everywhere along the roofline at once, dropping slate tiles on the heads of men who thought they were flanking.

It was the porter named Sefu, who had once carried Dreele's theodolite forty miles without complaint, standing in the west doorway swinging a cart axle with a formality that would have been funny in daylight, calling out, "Excuse me. Excuse me. Excuse me," with each swing, as though the company men had merely bumped into him in a market crowd.

And it was the moment—the one that changed the shape of him, quietly, the way water changes stone—when a company man came through the broken front door low and fast, younger than the rest, scared, holding a pistol in a grip that said he'd held one before but never meant it, and Isandro stepped into his path instead of away from it.

Every instinct he owned, everything the Escarpment had taught him, said *run, break contact, live small*. He'd survived six years on those instincts. They were good instincts. He loved them like family.

He stepped in anyway.

Not to kill—to *stop*. He caught the man's wrist against the doorframe the way Elodie had once caught his own wrist on the salt flats, hard and precise, and turned him into the wall, and put the river-polished club across the backs of his knees, and the man sat down all at once with an expression of profound betrayal, as though furniture had conspired against him.

"Don't get up," Isandro said. His voice came out steady, which surprised him. "There's a medic. You'll be fine. Stay fine."

The man stared up at him—young face, dusty, a boy really, barely older than Isandro—and said, "He told us it was just papers. He said nobody'd even fight for papers."

"Then he lied to you about the papers," Isandro said, "and about us, and probably about the pay. That's three lies before breakfast. Go home when you can walk."

Later, Tamsin found him sitting on the stairs with his hands shaking, now that it was over, now that the shaking felt permitted. She sat down beside him without a word and let the shaking have its minute.

"First time?" she asked eventually.

"Fighting for something. Yes."

"How is it?"

Isandro considered. His ears still rang. Smoke hung in the rafters. Somewhere above them Ferro was repositioning a barrel with the tenderness of a gardener moving a plant.

"Worse," he said. "And better. Both at once. Like the flash flood."

"Mm." Tamsin checked the bandage on his forearm—he didn't remember getting cut, which she said was normal. "Surviving, you fight to keep what you have. Tonight you fought to give something back. The second one costs more." She tied off the bandage. "It's also the only kind that doesn't rot you. Now come on. They're regrouping at the assay office, which means Ovid gets to be creative, and somebody responsible should supervise him."

"Am I the somebody responsible?"

"Heavens, no. I just want company while I do it."

* * *

The second push came at the hour when even honest people's courage thins, past midnight, when the body believes the night will simply go on forever. Dreele's remaining men—ten now, maybe nine—came from two directions at once, and they'd learned, and they brought fire this time, actual torches, done with pretending.

They got one torch onto the courthouse roof.

Isandro was hauling water with a bucket line of porters and townsfolk—old Marta from the chandlery, who'd sworn for twenty years that she never took sides, hauling alongside Sefu and a boy of twelve whose mother would have opinions later—when he saw the flame catch in the shingles and felt his heart drop through the floor of him.

Ferro got there first. Of course Ferro got there first; Ferro moved through towns the way he moved through canyon country, by routes that shouldn't exist. But it was Ovid's voice out of the dark that saved the roofline, calm as a ledger-keeper's:

"Everyone off the north wall, please. Everyone. Thank you."

The charge he'd seeded in the chimney breast went off with less boom than the front door trap—more of a *whump*, a shrug of masonry—and the burning section of roof slid neatly off into the street, where the bucket line drowned it, and where it very nearly drowned a company man who'd been standing underneath it planning further arson. Sefu pulled the man out by his collar, set him on the ground, and said, "Excuse me," one final time, almost tenderly.

After that, it unraveled for them. That was the thing about hired courage: it was a candle, not a lamp. When the third man went down—Ferro's doing, silent, from somewhere impossible—and the fourth simply sat down in the street and put his hands up unprompted, saying loudly, "No. No, I read the permit, it expired Tuesday, I read it myself," the rest of the night's arithmetic solved itself.

By the time the sky over the escarpment went the color of ash and rose petals, the street in front of the Corrow courthouse was littered with disarmed men sitting in rows, guarded by a twelve-year-old with a bucket, and the courthouse itself stood smoking slightly but standing, and inside it, intact, wrapped in Bex's spare shirt because the strongbox had seemed too grand, was the ledger.

Two hundred names. Waiting for morning.

* * *

They gathered in the records room as the light came up, and it was less a victory gathering than a very tired family refusing to go to bed before the important thing was finished. Tamsin stitched a porter's scalp. Bex inventoried prisoners' confiscated weapons with visible satisfaction, stacking them like cordwood. Ovid slept sitting up, mid-sentence, and no one woke him, on the principle that artists are fragile.

Elodie stood at the window watching the street, and Isandro came to stand beside her. Below, the townsfolk were already sweeping glass, and Magistrate Halloway was walking the prisoner rows with his father's sash finally fitting him right, taking statements in a voice that shook only a little.

"You stepped in front of a gun last night," Elodie said. Not a question. Tamsin reported everything; Tamsin always had.

"The man was scared. Scared men shoot wild. Standing still was actually the safe option."

"Liar."

"Yes," Isandro admitted. "Completely."

She was quiet a moment. Down in the street, Halloway paused at the row of seated men, and one of them—the young one, Isandro's young one—said something, and pointed up at the courthouse window, and Isandro had the strange sensation of being described.

"When I found you," Elodie said, "on the flats, stealing water, I told myself I was too tired to turn you in. That was a lie too. I kept you because you looked at the water like it mattered more than the rules, and I'd forgotten anyone could." She turned from the window. Her eyes were tired all the way down, but steady. "Tonight you didn't fight like a ranger. Rangers fight to stay invisible. You fought like a man putting his name on something."

"Whose name?"

"That's the question, isn't it." Almost a smile. "Halloway reads the names at dawn. All two hundred. And after that—" she glanced at the strongbox, at the ledger sleeping inside it, at whatever page in it bore her own forged signature, and something crossed her face like weather—"after that, some of us will need new arrangements. But that's not yet. Right now there's just this."

From the street below came the sound of the bell tower—Halloway must have climbed it himself—and then his voice, carrying over the rooftops, calling the town to witness.

Bex shouldered awake. Tamsin put away her needles. Ferro appeared in the window frame from outside, upside down briefly, then right-side up inside, as if gravity were optional for him. Even Ovid opened one eye.

"Dawn," Bex said, and picked up the strongbox. "Court is in session."

Isandro followed his family down the stairs, out the smoke-scarred door, into the light—into the square filling with people, into the record about to be opened after twenty years of silence—knowing, with a certainty that sat in his chest warmer than fear had ever sat, that whatever happened next, the names would not die unread.

Halloway unrolled the first page, and began.
