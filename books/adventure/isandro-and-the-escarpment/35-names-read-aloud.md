## 35. Names Read Aloud

Tamsin had not slept.

She had tried—she had a medic's discipline about sleep the way other people had religion—but every time she closed her eyes she saw the ledger open on Magistrate Halloway's desk, lamplight pooling on two hundred names written in a paymaster's careful hand, and her own fingers itching to check pulses on people twenty years dead.

So instead she had spent the night doing what she always did when grief got ahead of her: she worked. She restocked the courthouse infirmary with what remained of her kit. She rebandaged Ferro's forearm where a company bullet had grazed him in the night action. She sat with Pell, who had taken a rifle butt across the shoulder defending the evidence room, and told him the bruise was spectacular and would look worse tomorrow, which was the kind of medical news a nervous junior engineer could actually enjoy.

And at some point past three, Isandro had come and sat beside her without a word, the way he'd learned to do, and she had let him hold the lamp while she worked, and neither of them had said anything about the fact that they were both thinking about names.

Now the sky over Corrow was going grey-rose at the edges, and the town square was filling up.

* * *

Tamsin stood on the courthouse steps with the crew and watched them come.

They came on foot, mostly, because Corrow was a river town and river towns did not waste horses on a walk of six streets. They came from the net-sheds and the pump houses and the terraced gardens above the flood line. They came from the taverns, some of them still buttoning their shirts, having been woken by children sent running through the lanes with the news: *the magistrate is going to read the names. All of them. At dawn.*

All of them. As if everyone in Corrow had known for twenty years that there were names, the way you know about a splinter under your own skin. As if the whole town had been carrying it.

An old woman came down the lane with a cane she didn't seem to need for walking so much as for emphasis, tapping the cobbles like she was counting them. A man in a dredger's oilskins brought his two daughters, one on each arm, and Tamsin noticed he'd made them wash—their hair was still wet, combed flat, dressed as if for a wedding or a funeral, which, she supposed, covered it.

By the time the sun cleared the escarpment and laid a long gold stripe down the middle of the square, there were four hundred people there. Maybe five. Tamsin had learned head-counting in triage situations and she made herself stop counting, because counting was how you turned a crowd into a number, and numbers were the whole disease, weren't they? Two hundred dead. One ledger. Ninety days. The Concession had been built out of numbers, and this morning was meant to be the antidote.

Magistrate Halloway came out onto the steps in his formal grey coat, and Tamsin, who had spent a week learning to read his face the way Elodie read water, saw that he had not slept either. He looked, in fact, like a man who had spent the night arguing with a ghost and lost, and had decided that losing was the honest outcome.

He carried the ledger in both hands.

* * *

"Before I begin," Halloway said, and his voice carried the way voices do in river towns, trained by open air and long distances, "I want to say something my father never said."

The square went quiet enough that Tamsin could hear the river behind the buildings, working at its endless patient job.

"My father signed the paper that made the massacre at the Concession legal. He signed it for money, and he signed it knowing what it was, and then he went home and ate dinner and raised a son and never once—not once in my childhood—said the word *Concession* aloud in our house." Halloway's hands were steady on the ledger. His voice wasn't, quite, and he let it not be. "I have spent my career believing that the law was a thing you kept clean by keeping it separate from everything else. That was a lie I was comfortable in. This morning I am taking it off."

Beside Tamsin, Bex exhaled slowly through her nose—the quartermaster's equivalent of weeping—and muttered, "Well. Somebody raise the boy right after all."

"He raised himself," Ovid said softly. "That's rather the point."

Halloway opened the ledger.

"I am voiding the Anselm Concession," he said. "Effective now, by my authority, such as it is, and may whatever court reviews it choke on its own paperwork before it undoes this." A ripple went through the crowd—not quite laughter, not quite a cheer, something rawer. "The land reverts to the crown, and the crown can answer for it, and the water—" he paused, and Tamsin saw him glance, just briefly, at Elodie, standing at the edge of the steps with her arms folded and her face doing absolutely nothing, which was how Tamsin knew she felt everything, "—the water belongs to whoever drinks it. That is the oldest law there is, and no permit ever superseded it, whatever the permit claimed."

Then he turned the page.

"The following persons," he read, "were killed at the Anselm Concession on the seventeenth day of the dry season, twenty years past. Their deaths were recorded as a collapse. It was not a collapse. Their names are as follows."

And he began to read.

* * *

Tamsin had prepared herself for this. She had thought she had.

She was a medic; she knew grief the way a farrier knows hooves—all its temperaments, all its kicks. She had sat with dying men in canyon camps and held their hands while they said the names of their children into the dark, and she had believed that made her ready for a morning of nothing but names.

She was not ready.

It was the ordinariness that undid her. She had expected the names to sound like history—grand, terrible, carved. They did not. They sounded like a work roster. *Aldous Merrick. Tomas Merrick. Wen Atterby. The brothers Fenn, Josiah and Cole.* Men who had shared surnames because they were brothers, fathers and sons because that's who hires on together at a mine, cousins, neighbors, a man listed only as *Old Sam, surname unknown, called Sam by all.*

Halloway read them slowly. Not for effect—Tamsin watched him and understood he was reading them slowly because each name deserved the full weight of being spoken, the way you set a stone carefully rather than dropping it.

And the crowd—

That was the part Tamsin kept returning to afterward, the part she would carry. Nobody in that square had known these men. Twenty years was long enough that the miners' own families had scattered or died or stopped coming to the river towns. These were strangers weeping for strangers. A dredger with rope-scarred hands cried openly at the name *Wen Atterby*, and Tamsin would have bet her whole kit he'd never heard the name before last night. The old woman with the cane repeated each name under her breath after Halloway said it, like an echo, like she was filing each one somewhere safe in case the magistrate's copy ever burned.

*Because that's what erasure does,* Tamsin thought, watching. *It doesn't kill the mourning. It just leaves the mourning with nowhere to go. Twenty years of grief with no address, and today somebody finally delivered the letters.*

Isandro was beside her, and she felt him trembling. She put her hand on the back of his neck, the way she had when he was fifteen and feverish, and he didn't pull away.

"They're just names," he whispered. It wasn't a challenge. It was a boy trying to understand why his eyes were burning.

"That's the whole point," Tamsin whispered back. "They're just names. That's all anyone gets. And someone decided even that was too much for them."

* * *

Somewhere around the ninetieth name, Halloway's voice began to roughen, and a woman near the front—a schoolteacher, Tamsin later learned—took up the next name when he faltered, and then others took turns, so that the reading became a relay, the whole town carrying it in shifts like men hauling a boat up a rapids. Halloway yielded the ledger's edge to them gratefully. Tamsin watched him stand there listening to his own redemption being read aloud by strangers and thought: *there's a man being born and buried at the same time.*

Elodie hadn't moved. Tamsin drifted along the steps until she stood next to her captain, close enough to speak under the names.

"You should eat something," she murmured. Medic's privilege: she could say anything to anyone if she framed it as a prescription.

"After."

"You said that yesterday."

"After," Elodie said again, but she unfolded her arms, which from Elodie was a confession. Her eyes were on the crowd, moving face to face. Cataloguing, Tamsin knew. Counting exits, counting weapons, twenty years of habit. But underneath the habit, something else. Tamsin had known her long enough to see it.

"There'll be a name in there you know," Tamsin said quietly. "Besides yours."

Elodie was silent through three more names. *Roderick Vail. Anselm Petit, the surveyor. A boy, name unrecorded, approximately fourteen years.*

"A boy," Elodie finally said, barely audible. "Fourteen. Same age I was when I carried the paper through. They put him in the ground with no name at all, and I got to keep mine by hiding it." She looked at the ledger, at the schoolteacher's finger moving down the columns. "I used to think leaving no name behind was a code. Clean hands. Turns out it was just the same crime, done gently."

"It wasn't," Tamsin said. "Elodie. Listen to me as your medic and not your friend, because as your friend I'd be crying too hard to say it. What you did wrong, you're paying for. What you did right is standing in that square. Five hundred people showed up at dawn because you and your crew of beautiful criminals refused to let a river burn quietly. Don't you dare file yourself under the same heading as Dreele."

Elodie almost smiled. Almost. On her face it was an earthquake.

"We'll see what the record says."

"The record," Tamsin said, nodding at the ledger, "is being rewritten as we speak. Write yourself in honestly. It's the only entry nobody can forge."

* * *

Near the end of the list, something happened that Tamsin would tell stories about for the rest of her life, though she would never manage to make it sound as simple as it had been.

The name was *Marta Voss, cook, the mess house.* And when it was spoken, the old woman with the cane stepped forward—all the way forward, out of the crowd, into the empty space before the steps—and said, in a voice cracked with age but perfectly clear:

"She made bread on Sundays. Real bread, with the flour they hauled up at ruinous expense, because she said men who eat like animals dig like animals. I was her sister. I'm the only one left who remembers her face, and I'm here to say it: she had a laugh like a cart going downhill, and she was afraid of thunder, and she deserved better than a stone pile in a canyon."

Nobody moved to stop her. Halloway inclined his head and waited. And when she had finished, another voice came—from the dredger in oilskins: "Josiah Fenn taught me knots. Before any of this. I was a boy on his boat." And then another, and another, small offerings, fragments of the dead surfacing like things released by a thaw, until the reading stopped being a list and became something none of them had a word for. A reconstruction. A town assembling two hundred people out of memory shards, most of the memories secondhand, all of them offered anyway.

Ferro, at the edge of the steps, leaned toward Ovid and said something too low for Tamsin to catch, but she caught Ovid's reply, because the demolitions man had tears running freely down his face and made no effort whatsoever to hide them.

"What's that?" Tamsin asked later.

"I said it sounded like a demolition in reverse," Ovid had answered. "All the rubble deciding, all at once, to be a building again."

* * *

It took until midmorning. When the last name was read—*and one hundred and forty-three more whose names the paymaster recorded, and whose entries follow*—Halloway closed the ledger and held it up so the crowd could see it, plain, unburned, real.

"This book stays in this courthouse," he said, "under guard, in public view, forever. Any person descended from these names, or claiming to be, has my protection and my help. The company men who fired on this building last night are in custody. Their employers will be named in my report, and my report will travel by every road and every bird I can buy." He paused. "Justice is slow and it is imperfect and I am one tired man in a grey coat. But the dead of the Anselm Concession are, as of this morning, no longer anonymous. Let that be the beginning and not the end."

The cheer, when it came, started low—somewhere in the net-sheds contingent, Tamsin thought—and rolled up through the square like the river rising, until it crashed against the escarpment and came back.

* * *

Afterward, the square emptied slowly, the way a wound stops bleeding—gradually, then all at once. People lingered. The old woman with the cane stood a long time at the bottom of the steps, and Tamsin saw Isandro go down and speak to her, hat in hand, and saw the woman take his hand in both of hers and hold it, and whatever she said made him stand very straight for the rest of the day.

The crew gathered on the courthouse side porch, out of the sun. Bex produced water and the last of the Corrow hardtack, which tasted like sweetened gravel and was, in Tamsin's professional opinion, the finest meal any of them had ever eaten. For a while nobody talked. They didn't need to. Twenty years of silence had been broken in a public square; a little private silence, the good kind, the kind between people who trust each other, was a luxury they'd earned.

It was Ferro who broke it, in his usual fashion—which was to say, sideways, while looking at nothing.

"Dreele's gone."

The porch went still.

"Custody cells don't hold company men overnight, not in a town half their payroll," Ferro went on. "Turnkey found the cell empty at first light. Window bar cut—clean work, not his own. And the turnkey himself doesn't remember the evening." He finally looked at them. "Somebody bought the door. Which means somewhere out there, Dreele isn't running. He's reporting. And a man like that, with orders to expedite and nothing left to lose—"

"He knows where the ledger sleeps," Ovid finished quietly.

Bex was already on her feet. Elodie hadn't moved, but Tamsin saw her hand had gone, without her seeming to notice, to the worn spot on her belt where a weapon lived.

"No," Elodie said.

Everyone looked at her.

"Not yet." She nodded toward the square, where the old woman was still standing, and the schoolteacher was copying names onto fresh paper, and Isandro was helping carry chairs back into the courthouse like the world's most solemn furniture apprentice. "We just gave two hundred people their names back. Today, that holds. Today, nobody in this town runs anywhere." She rose at last, unhurried, and Tamsin saw the weariness in her and the iron under it, load-bearing. "Tomorrow, we find out what a hunted man does when his company starts hunting him too. But tonight—" and here her gaze crossed each of theirs, Bex and Ovid and Tamsin and Ferro, and rested longest on the empty step where Isandro had been sitting, "—tonight we stay. Tonight we're people with names, in a town that knows them. Just for one night."

She walked back inside toward Halloway's office, and Tamsin watched her go, and thought: *one night. She's giving us one night.*

From somewhere down the river road, faint on the morning wind, came the sound of hoofbeats.
