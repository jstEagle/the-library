## 36. Costs

The morning after the reading, Corrow smelled of wet stone and woodsmoke, and the whole town seemed to be talking at once. Elodie stood in the doorway of the magistrate's house with a cup of chicory she had no intention of drinking, watching the square fill with people who had come to look at the courthouse as if it might change shape now that the names were loose inside it.

Two hundred names. Spoken once into the record, and already the river towns had taken them up like a song learned too fast—shopkeepers saying them under their breath, children chanting a few of the shorter ones for the pleasure of the sound. *Aldous Merrin. The Vey sisters. Old Tam Corta.* Names that had been stones for twenty years, suddenly mouths again.

"You should eat something," Tamsin said, appearing at her elbow with the particular gentleness she reserved for patients who were about to argue.

"I'm accounting," Elodie said.

"You're staring."

"Accounting takes many forms." She turned from the doorway. "Where's Vane?"

* * *

Sable Vane was in the cell behind the courthouse—not locked in, because Halloway had no stomach for locking her, but sitting on the cot anyway with her boots off and her hat on her knee, looking for all the world like a woman waiting out a rainstorm she'd been promised would pass.

"You could fight it," Elodie said from the doorway. "Halloway would give you a fair trial. Your testimony bought you that much. Half the charges would never stick."

Vane laughed, and it was the old laugh, the one that used to make campfires feel warmer than they were. "A fair trial. In Corrow. With two hundred freshly read names hanging over the proceedings and every jury pool in a hundred miles weeping into their beer." She shook her head. "I've sat through trials, Elodie. I've *sat* through them. I know how they end for women whose signatures are in ledgers. They end with the signature being the whole story."

"It was one page. One payment. You were young."

"I was paid," Vane said simply. "That's the difference between us, and it took me twenty years and a canyon full of graves to say it out loud. You carried paper you didn't understand. I signed mine with my eyes open and my hand steady." She set her hat on the cot beside her and looked at it for a long moment—a good hat, a costly hat, the kind of hat that announced a woman had arrived somewhere. "So here's what I'll do. I'll take the road. Exile, self-imposed, before your honest magistrate has to decide whether I'm a witness or a defendant. I'll go somewhere the Anselm doesn't reach and start over with whatever's in my pockets."

"And the company?"

"The company will find me eventually. Companies always do." She smiled, and there was nothing warm left in it, only the old competence. "But I'll be hard to catch, and expensive, and by then Dreele will have made a better story for them to chase. Speaking of which."

"Dreele."

"Gone before dawn. Took three horses and the junior surveyor's notes—the ones that would have implicated him personally—and rode south through the salt. But here's the beautiful part." Vane leaned forward, and for a moment she was the woman Elodie had once trusted with her life on a rope line above a flooded wash. "Dreele didn't just fail. He failed *expensively*, after his own employers ordered him to expedite, and he took their documents with him when he ran. The Concession Company does not forgive expense. Right now there are men riding south with quiet contracts and longer rifles, and they aren't working for justice. They're working for the balance sheet."

"So he's hunted."

"He's hunted. And I am merely *departed*. There's a difference, and the difference is time." Vane rose and put her boots back on, unhurried, a woman dressing for weather. "One last thing, captain. When you settle your accounts—and I can see you're already doing it, you have the face of a woman balancing books since dawn—leave a line for me. Not forgiveness. Just... note that I came back to the canyon. Note that I stood in the grave field and didn't look away. That's more than most of us managed."

Elodie considered her for a long moment. Twenty years of grievance, and underneath it, all along, the same tired truth: they had run from the same fire, in opposite directions, and both directions had cost.

"Noted," Elodie said. "In ink."

Vane nodded once, picked up her hat, and walked past her out into the bright morning without looking back, because looking back was a luxury neither of them had ever been able to afford.

* * *

Bex found her at midday at the waystation, where the crew's old ledger lay open on the counter—the real one, water-stained and dog-eared, the book that held every debt and credit the crew had ever run against each other. Bex kept it the way priests keep scripture: precisely, and with private doubt.

"You're doing it in the wrong book," Bex said.

"There's only one book."

"There's always been one book." Bex sat down across from her and turned the ledger around. "Look. Page one. 'Ovid owes the crew one bridge, quality unspecified.' He blew up a toll bridge in his first week because he thought it was haunted. We billed him. He's never paid."

"Bex—"

"Tamsin owes forty days' medic work she did for free during the fever year, which I entered as a debit to the crew, not to her, because she'd have argued. Ferro owes nothing and knows it and mentions it constantly. And Isandro—" Bex's voice caught, just slightly, on the name, and she covered it by turning pages too fast. "Isandro owes one apprenticeship, five years, value beyond reckoning, marked *paid in full* the day he dove into the falls after those pages."

Elodie looked at the ledger. Her eyes were doing something inconvenient.

"What are you asking me, Bex?"

"I'm asking what happens to the crew when the captain walks into exile." Bex said it flatly, the way she said everything that mattered. "Because I've done the arithmetic. You can't stay—your signature's in that ledger upstairs, the forged one, and Halloway is honest enough that he won't look away from it, and thank the dry earth for that even though it's killing us. So the crew needs a quartermaster who can read this book and a code that still works without you standing in front of it. And I need—" She stopped. Started again. "I need to know you're not walking out because you think we'd be better off. Because that's the kind of noble nonsense you pull, and I want it on record that I oppose it."

Elodie laughed despite herself, a rough sound. "On record. In the book?"

"In the book. 'Captain proposes self-sacrifice. Quartermaster opposes. Motion fails.'" Bex closed the ledger gently. "The crew stays together. We take contracts. We work the river towns—honestly, now, with our faces where people can see them, because Ferro says the hiding is over whether we like it or not. And every spring, we leave a marker at the cairn field, and every autumn, someone rides out to wherever you are and brings you the news and a decent meal, because exile shouldn't mean bad cooking. Those are my terms."

"That's extortion."

"That's *accounting*." Bex pushed the ledger toward her. "Sign it. Your real name. It's the first thing in here that's ever been true."

And so Elodie Yarrow—who had been nobody, and the captain, and *that woman from the flats* for twenty years—signed her own name into her crew's book, and her hand only shook a little.

* * *

She found Ovid on the roof of the assay office, because of course he was on a roof; the man treated elevated surfaces as a moral position. Below him, the demolition team's abandoned charges sat in a wagon under guard, and Ovid regarded them the way a shepherd regards wolves.

"You're going to ask me to disarm them properly," Elodie called up.

"I'm going to *insist*," Ovid called down. "Whoever wired these wanted them to fail safe, which is the only professional courtesy anyone in this entire affair has shown. Give me an hour and they'll be soup."

"An hour. Then come down. I'm settling accounts."

Ovid climbed down with the unhurried grace of a man who had survived his own profession for thirty years. Up close he smelled of black powder and soap, and his eyes were red-rimmed in a way he'd deny to his grave.

"You're leaving," he said.

"You knew I would."

"I knew. Knowing isn't the same as liking." He wiped his hands on a rag that was filthier than his hands. "I owe you a speech, Elodie. I've been composing it since the courthouse. It's about how you taught a man who only understood destruction that some things are worth building, and how the crew is the only structure I ever made that didn't blow up. It's very moving. I've been crying about it in private."

"Don't—"

"But I'm not giving it." He folded the rag away. "Because speeches are for endings, and this isn't one. You'll get letters. Terrible ones, full of technical detail. And when the river towns rebuild—and they will, they're already arguing about the mill sites—you'll come see the first bridge, and it will bear weight, and no one will remember why that matters except us." He stuck out a hand, then ruined the gesture entirely by pulling her into a hug that smelled of powder and lasted exactly as long as either of them could stand. "Take the east road," he muttered into her hair. "The west road has a washout. I'd know. I made it."

* * *

Ferro caught up with her at dusk on the town's edge, materializing out of the scrub the way he always did, silent until he chose not to be.

"Dreele crossed the salt line heading south," he said, falling into step beside her. "Company riders crossed an hour behind him. They'll have him before the border."

"You sound sorry for him."

"I sound *accurate* about him." Ferro shrugged. "He's a coward with a map, and cowards with maps die tired. But I counted his guards once, back at the beginning, and I didn't like the ratio, and I was right, and somehow that doesn't taste like anything." He walked a few paces in silence. "I counted something else. This crew. Four of us staying, one going. Bad ratio too."

"Ferro—"

"I'm not arguing. Arguing's for people who think outcomes are negotiable." He stopped at the edge of the last lamplight and looked at her with those flat, patient eyes. "Here's my account, since you're collecting. You found me half-dead in a ditch with a price on me, and you never asked what the price was for. Twenty years, you never asked. That's the biggest debt in any book, and it's unpayable, so I won't try. I'll just say this: the boy stays in Corrow. Halloway's training him. And somebody ought to tell him that being the first one to carry a name in public is harder than anything we did in the dark, and that he shouldn't do it alone." A pause. "That somebody shouldn't be me. I'm terrible at talking. But it should be somebody who loves him, and we both know the list is short."

Elodie put her hand on the back of his neck, briefly, the way you steady a friend on a high ledge. "Tell him I'll write."

"He won't believe you."

"Then tell him I never lie about paperwork."

Ferro almost smiled. Almost. Then he faded back into the scrub, and the night took him the way it always had, gently, like family.

* * *

Tamsin was the last, because Tamsin was always the last—she stayed until every other wound was dressed.

She found Elodie packing at the magistrate's house, or rather pretending to pack, moving the same three possessions from bag to table and back. Tamsin sat down on the bed and watched for a while.

"Your hands," she said finally. "Show me."

"They're fine."

"They've been shaking for two days. Show me, or I'll make it official."

Elodie held them out. Tamsin turned them over, pressed the fingertips, watched her eyes. Whatever she saw made her mouth go thin.

"Exhaustion and grief wearing each other's clothes," she pronounced. "It'll pass. Mostly." She reached into her kit and pressed a small tin into Elodie's palm. "Willow bark, desert sage, and the bitter one you hate. Steep it strong. And Elodie—" her voice softened into the register she used for truths, "—you saved two hundred names and lost one home, and everyone in this town thinks that's a fine trade, and you're allowed to think it's a terrible one. Both things are true. Grief doesn't check the ledger before it arrives."

"When did you get wise?"

"I was always wise. You were always too busy to notice." Tamsin stood, and hugged her quickly and hard, the field-medic hug, the one that checks for broken ribs while breaking your heart. "Autumn," she said. "Someone rides out with news and a decent meal. Bex's terms. I seconded them. It passed unanimously while you weren't voting."

* * *

Isandro was waiting at the east road at first light, which meant he hadn't slept, which meant Ferro had told him, which meant the whole crew had known before she did that she'd choose this road.

He'd grown, she thought. Somewhere between the salt flats and the courthouse he'd grown a spine of his own, and it showed in how he stood—not like an apprentice anymore, but like a ranger, feet planted, hat brim level, a young man seeing his teacher off instead of clinging to her skirt.

"You could come with me," she said, though she knew the answer.

"No." He said it kindly. "The river towns need rangers who don't hide. Somebody has to be the first, and apparently it's me, because I'm young and stupid and Halloway likes me." He handed her a bundle—bread, dried fruit, a flask. "Tamsin packed it. Don't tell her you cried about it later, she'll be insufferable."

"I haven't cried about anything."

"Yet." He grinned, and it was the grin from the salt flats, the starving boy stealing water, except now it belonged to a man with a name and a post and a future written in public ink. "Write to me. Real letters. Tell me where you are, even if it's nowhere. Especially if it's nowhere."

"Isandro." She took his shoulders. "You were the best thing I ever stole."

His face crumpled and reassembled itself, quick as weather. "You didn't steal me. I followed you. There's a difference, and Bex probably has it in the book."

"She does. I signed it yesterday. My real name."

"I know. I saw." He stepped back and squared his shoulders, and his voice went formal, ranger-formal, the old code spoken new: "Take nothing you can't carry. Leave no name behind—unless the name is owed." He touched his brim. "Safe roads, Elodie Yarrow."

Nobody had said her full name aloud to her in twenty years, and hearing it from him, in the gray first light, with the east road opening long and empty ahead—it nearly finished her. She let it nearly finish her, and then she didn't let it.

"Go learn something," she said, gruffly, and turned east.

* * *

The road took her past the turnoff to the cairn field, and she let it, because Bex's terms included markers and she intended to pay. She climbed the ridge in the early heat and stood among the hundreds of stones, each one a name now, each name a person, each person owed.

At the highest cairn she set a flat rock she'd carried from Corrow, and on it, scratched deep with her knife, she left the only currency she had left:

*ELLODIE YARROW WAS HERE. SHE CAME BACK.*

Not elegant. Not legal. But it was in ink of a kind, and it was true, and truth was the whole account now—everything else spent.

She stood a moment longer, listening to the wind move through the stones like a slow reading, and then she shouldered her bag and took the east road, alone, named, and—for the first time in twenty years—not hiding.

Behind her, far downstream, a pigeon beat its way north toward Corrow carrying a scrap of paper, and whatever it said would wait. Roads were for walking. News was for autumn.

She walked.
