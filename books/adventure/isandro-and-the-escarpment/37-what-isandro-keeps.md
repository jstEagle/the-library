## 37. What Isandro Keeps

The morning after the names were read, Corrow smelled like bread.

Isandro noticed it before anything else—before the hammering of the courthouse shutters being repaired, before the porters' laughter drifting up from the river landing, before the strange new quiet where gunfire had been. Bread. Someone in the town had decided that a day that began with two hundred names spoken into the public record deserved bread, and by dawn every oven on Anselm Street was going, and the smell had rolled down through the town like a second river.

He stood in the doorway of the room Halloway had given him—a real room, with a door that locked and a window that faced east—and he tried to remember if he'd ever woken up anywhere that smelled like bread.

Camps smelled like ash and leather and Tamsin's medicinal smoke. The salt flats smelled like nothing at all, which was its own kind of lesson. But bread meant people who expected tomorrow. Bread meant someone had planned past sunset.

"You're up early." Halloway's voice came from the stairwell, and then Halloway himself, still in yesterday's coat, carrying two cups of something hot. He handed one over without asking. "Magistrates don't sleep after days like that. Nobody tells you that part of the job."

"Are you a magistrate yet? Properly?"

Halloway considered this over his cup. "As of an hour ago, yes. The old council signed off before breakfast, which tells you they wanted it done while I was still too tired to make speeches." He looked out the window at the town, at the river beyond it running brown and swollen with spring melt. "Your captain left before first light. You know that."

"I know."

"She didn't say goodbye to you?"

Isandro wrapped both hands around the cup. "She said it last night. In her way." He'd gone looking for Elodie after the reading, found her on the courthouse steps checking the buckles of her pack in the dark, everything already squared away because of course it was. She hadn't made a speech either. She'd put one hand on the back of his neck, the way she had the first day on the salt flats when she'd caught him stealing water, and she'd said, "You were the best thing I ever took that wasn't mine to take," and then she'd told him to eat something, and she'd walked out of town before the lamps were lit.

That was Elodie's way. Goodbyes at night, so no one had to watch her go.

"There's paperwork," Halloway said, and there was something almost shy in how he said it. "If you're staying. Ranger-in-training isn't just a title I invented to keep you fed. It comes with a name in the register. A public one. Your own."

Isandro looked at him for a long moment.

"I've never had my own line in any book," he said. "Bex keeps the crew ledger. I'm in it as *the boy*."

"Not anymore." Halloway set down his cup and produced, from inside his coat, a slim book bound in green leather—the town register, the real one, the one that would survive fires and floods because Halloway had already survived one attempt on it and had opinions about storage now. He opened it to a fresh page and held out the pen. "Whenever you're ready. No rush. Names are like rivers—you want to choose the crossing carefully."

Isandro took the pen. His hand didn't shake, which surprised him. He wrote *Isandro Yarrow* in careful letters, and then stopped, pen hovering.

"Yarrow?" Halloway said gently. "Or something else? It doesn't have to be hers."

It didn't. That was the whole point of the page—that it could be anything. But Isandro thought about the woman who had taught him to read green foam on brown water, who had misjudged a storm by one hour and carried two drowned men in her chest ever since, who had spent twenty years being no one so that other people could stay safe, and who had finally said her own name out loud on the courthouse steps yesterday, *Elodie Yarrow*, clear as a bell across a silent square, and the whole town had heard it and nobody had flinched.

"I'll keep it," he said, and finished the line.

Halloway blotted the page, closed the book, and shook his hand formally, which felt absurd and wonderful at once. "Then welcome to the worst-paid profession on the river," he said. "We'll start your training Monday. Today—" he nodded at the window, at the bread smell, at the sound of hammers and laughter, "—today you have goodbyes to do. I saw Bex inventorying the courthouse cellar like it owed her money. You should get there before she reorganizes the entire town."

* * *

Bex was exactly where Halloway said she'd be, down in the courthouse cellar with the recovered evidence spread out on trestle tables: the ledger under glass now, the maps flattened and labeled, Dreele's route notes tagged in her small fierce handwriting. She'd commandeered a lamp, a stool, and a pot of tea, and she looked up when Isandro came down the stairs with the expression of a woman who had been expecting him since dawn.

"Took you long enough," she said. "I've got your inheritance ready."

"My what?"

She reached under the table and came up with a canvas roll, tied with cord, worn soft at the edges from years of riding in a quartermaster's pack. She pushed it across the table. "Open it."

Inside were instruments. A brass compass with a dented lid. A folding rule. A set of surveyor's pencils worn down to stubs. And a small notebook, half-filled, in Bex's cramped hand—water sources, distances, cache sites, the whole secret geography of the Escarpment that the crew had spent years mapping off every official record.

"Bex." His throat went tight. "This is your route book. This is—this is the whole—"

"It's outdated the moment I hand it over, which is why I'm handing it over." She tapped the notebook. "Half those springs have shifted. The cache at Redstone Wash is flooded—I checked on the way down. Your job is to walk it all again and write the corrections. That's what a ranger does. Keeps the true map alive, because nobody else will." She sipped her tea. "Also I've been meaning to be rid of it for years. Carrying the only honest map of the Escarpment in your head is a terrible way to live. Believe me."

"You could sell it," he said. "Caravans would pay—"

"Don't finish that sentence in my presence." But she was smiling, the rare full one that crinkled her whole face. Then the smile settled into something more serious, and she studied him across the lamplight. "You know I argued against taking you on. First week. Told Elodie a camp was no place for a starving boy, that you'd slow us down or get us killed or both."

"I know. Ferro told me."

"Ferro talks too much." She turned her cup slowly. "I was wrong. And I've been quartermaster long enough to know that being wrong costs you, and you pay the debt honestly or you don't pay at all. So here's my payment: whatever you need from me, ever—letters, supplies, a place to land when the river towns get too small—you send word and it arrives. That's not sentiment. That's accounting." She stood, brisk again, and straightened his collar with two efficient tugs like he was eight years old. "Eat something. You're too thin for a man with a salary."

* * *

Ovid found him at midday, down at the landing, where the crew's remaining animals were being sold off to farmers who kept asking, in delighted disbelief, whether the prices were real.

Ovid had a crate under one arm and the particular bounce in his step he got when he'd built something he wasn't supposed to build.

"So," Ovid said, setting the crate on a bollard. "Ranger-in-training. Very official. Very legal. Disgusting, really. I hardly recognize you."

"You're one to talk. You spent yesterday helping Halloway's men disarm the demolition charges and catalog them for evidence."

"And it was the proudest day of my life, thank you. There is nothing—*nothing*—more satisfying than taking apart another professional's work and finding it sloppy." He flipped open the crate. Inside, packed in straw, lay six small cylinders of paper-wrapped something-or-other, and Isandro took an instinctive step back.

"They're empty," Ovid said, wounded. "Mostly. Look—"

"What do you mean *mostly*—"

"Flour and saltpeter and red dye. Training charges. They pop loud, throw red dust, scare livestock, injure no one." He lifted one out and turned it in the light. "Every ranger should know what a charge feels like, how heavy, how it sits in the hand, how to spot one buried in a roadbed before it takes out a bridge. Because there will always be another Dreele, boy, and next time the person standing on the bridge might be you." He pressed it into Isandro's hands. "I'll teach you the difference between these and the real thing until you can tell them blindfolded by weight alone. That's the goodbye gift. Not the toys. The knowing."

Isandro turned the dummy charge over. It sat heavier than he'd expected, denser, and he understood suddenly that Ovid had just handed him the most dangerous lesson he owned, dressed up as a joke.

"Ovid. Why did you really stay through all of it? The massacre cover-up, the hiding, all of it. You could have walked anytime."

Ovid was quiet for a moment, watching the river.

"Because walking away doesn't unmake what your hands did," he said at last. "You have to stay near the mess until it's cleaned up. Otherwise you're just spreading it around." He clapped Isandro on the shoulder. "You're staying near the mess. Good lad. Now come on, I promised the ferryman I'd look at his engine, and you're going to hold things and learn something."

* * *

Tamsin said goodbye to him without saying it, which was the most Tamsin way possible.

He found her at the edge of town at dusk, at the small cemetery on the rise above the river, kneeling in the grass beside the two graves they'd dug in the canyon—the two porters whose bodies they'd been able to bring downriver. Halloway had given them proper ground here, and proper stones, and yesterday, after the reading, townsfolk had come up one by one and added their own markers: carved names, painted ones, a child's arrangement of river pebbles spelling out letters it took Isandro a minute to decipher.

Tamsin was tending the plants she'd started there—desert marigold, stubborn stuff that grew in cracked stone.

"You'll want to water these twice a week," she said, without turning around. "Morning, before the heat. Not evening; the nights are still cold enough to rot the roots."

Isandro crouched beside her. "You're leaving them with me."

"I'm leaving them with whoever stays. You stayed." She brushed soil from her hands and finally looked at him, and her eyes were tired in the deep way they always were, but steady. "Twenty years I patched people in secret, Isandro. Stitched them up behind rocks so no healer's record would show their faces. Do you know what I learned?" She touched one of the marigolds. "A wound hidden heals crooked. Every time. The body wants light on it. So do names. So do people." She stood, knees cracking, and looked out over the little cemetery, at the two hundred names now read aloud in the town below, at the stones going up on graves that had waited two decades for theirs. "You're the crooked-healed one, boy. We all bent you back into shape as best we could. Now stand in the light and grow straight."

"Tamsin—"

"Twice a week," she repeated firmly, because Tamsin could not bear a sentimental scene any more than she could bear an infected cut left open. "And if the fever comes through the river towns in autumn—and it will, it always does—you send for me. I'll come. Not for the town." She allowed herself one small smile. "For the marigolds."

* * *

Ferro was the hardest.

Not because Ferro was distant—Ferro had never been distant a day in his life—but because Ferro was the one who'd taught him everything about the country itself, and saying goodbye to Ferro felt like saying goodbye to the Escarpment.

He found him on the roof of the ferry house, because of course Ferro was on a roof, legs dangling over the eaves, watching the river with a strip of dried meat in his teeth. Isandro climbed up and sat beside him. Below, the water ran high and loud, carrying the last of the spring flood down toward the lowlands.

"So," Ferro said. "Staying."

"Staying."

"Huh." He chewed. "You know what I figured out, up on that ridge during the fight at the courthouse? Watching you move?" He nudged Isandro with an elbow. "I stopped worrying about you. Somewhere in the middle of it, I realized I'd stopped. You were covering the east stair and I caught myself thinking, *he's fine*, and went back to my own work. Do you know how long it's been since I thought *anyone's fine* in a fight? Years. I count everyone's exits. That's the job. And there you were, making your own exits." He shrugged, elaborately casual, which from Ferro meant the feeling underneath was enormous. "Apprenticeship's over. Don't let it go to your head."

"Ferro. Come stay. Halloway needs scouts. The river towns need—"

"Nope." Ferro said it kindly but absolutely. "Walls make me itch. Towns make me itch worse. You know this." He pulled a folded scrap of oilcloth from his jacket and tossed it into Isandro's lap. Inside was a whistle, carved from river cane, tuned—it turned out when Isandro tried it—to carry three distinct notes over a canyon wind. "Low note means *danger, come*. High note means *safe, all clear*. Middle note means *I'm hungry, bring food*, because priorities matter." He grinned. "Blow it at the escarpment on a still night and I'll hear it. I'll hear it from forty miles, I swear it. And if you ever blow the low note, I'll come so fast I'll beat the echo."

"That's not possible."

"Test me someday and find out."

They sat together on the roof until the stars came out, saying nothing, which was also a Ferro kind of goodbye, and maybe the best one.

* * *

On his last evening as a boy with no obligations, Isandro climbed to the top of the courthouse steps with the green register's memory in his head and looked out over Corrow.

Lamps were lighting along Anselm Street. The bakery was closing. Down at the landing, Pell was arguing cheerfully with three former porters about the correct way to build a dock, and winning, because Pell had discovered that being believed was better than being invisible and had thrown himself into it with an engineer's thoroughness. Somewhere upstream, past the cairn field, past the fenced spring that belonged to everyone now, the river ran on toward the sea, carrying snowmelt and silt and, if Tamsin was right, every name that had ever been given to it.

His name was in a book. Two books, actually—Halloway's register and Bex's ledger, where she'd crossed out *the boy* before leaving and written, in her cramped hand, *Isandro Yarrow, ranger*, with the date.

He thought about the code Elodie had taught him on the salt flats, the first law of his life: *take nothing you can't carry, leave no name behind.* He'd lived by it for years. It had kept him alive. It had kept all of them alive.

But standing here, in the light, in a town that knew his face and his name and his business, he understood that the code had been a splint, not a spine. You wore it while the bone healed. Elodie had known that—had known it for twenty years, maybe—and yesterday she'd taken the splint off and walked into exile upright, and it was the bravest thing he'd ever seen anyone do.

Monday, his training began. There would be ledgers to keep, honestly this time. Springs to find and map and share. Fever seasons and flood seasons and, someday, some other starving kid on some other flat, stealing water, and Isandro intended to be there, weary and merciful, to offer the same bargain he'd been offered: come with me, ask no questions, belong.

He went down the steps two at a time, whistling the middle note—*hungry, bring food*—and the bakery door swung open before he even reached it, because in Corrow now, somebody was always listening for him.

He was halfway through his second loaf when Ferro's whistle sounded from the dark edge of town—one long, clear note Isandro had never heard before, a fourth note that wasn't in the code at all.

High. Low. Then high again, urgent, repeating.

Isandro set down the bread, and ran.
