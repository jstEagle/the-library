## 38. Exile Road

Elodie Yarrow had left camps before. She had left them in the dark, in the rain, in a hurry, and once—she still winced about it—in the middle of someone else's argument about beans. Leaving was a skill like any other, and she had practiced it for twenty years until it sat in her hands as easily as a knife.

This leaving was different. This one she did at first light, on purpose, slowly, with her boots laced properly and her bedroll tied with the good knot Bex had taught her, the one that never slipped and always looked smug about it.

She had expected to slip out quietly. That was the old habit: take nothing you can't carry, leave no name behind. But the crew had other ideas.

They were all standing at the edge of camp when she shouldered her pack, arranged in a loose line that pretended to be casual and fooled no one. Bex was holding something wrapped in oilcloth. Ovid was leaning against a rock as though he'd been there all night, which he probably had. Tamsin had her medic's satchel slung and her arms crossed, which meant she was prepared to treat whatever emotional injuries might occur. Ferro simply stood, arms at his sides, watching the eastern ridge the way he watched everything—as if it might lie to him.

And Isandro. Isandro stood front and center, jaw set, eyes bright, holding a waterskin like it was a formal document.

"You're supposed to be asleep," Elodie said.

"It's first light," Isandro said. "Nobody respectable is asleep at first light."

"Exactly my point."

Bex stepped forward and pressed the oilcloth bundle into her hands. "Provisions for nine days. Ten if you ration like a saint, twelve if you ration like you."

"I ration like me."

"Then twelve." Bex's voice was steady, but her eyes were doing that thing they did over the ledger at night—counting something that couldn't be counted in columns. "There's dried apricots in the side pocket. Don't argue. You'll want them around day six when you hate the world, and I refuse to have you hating the world under-provisioned."

Elodie weighed the bundle. It was heavier than nine days of food needed to be. She didn't open it. Some gifts you don't inspect; inspecting them says you think the giver made a mistake.

"Thank you," she said.

"Don't thank me," Bex said briskly. "Thank the ledger. There's an entry. *One captain, retired, fully stocked.* Balanced."

Ovid pushed off his rock and came over rolling something between his fingers—a small charge, no bigger than a walnut, wrapped in waxed paper. He held it out on his palm like a man offering candy.

"Before you say anything," he said, "this is not a weapon. This is a *tool*. For moving stubborn boulders off trails. Trails you might encounter. On roads. Where boulders are notorious."

"Ovid."

"There are boulders everywhere, Elodie. The world is mostly boulders with some weather on top."

She took it, because refusing Ovid a farewell gift was like refusing the sunrise—it would happen anyway and make you look foolish. "If I blow up anything other than a boulder, I'll tell everyone you taught me badly."

"You couldn't. I teach beautifully." His grin faded into something softer. He squeezed her shoulder once, hard, the way he did with fuse wire when he wanted to be sure it would hold. "Twenty years, Captain. Best demolition work I ever did was helping build something nobody could tear down."

"That's not demolition."

"No," he agreed. "It's better."

Tamsin embraced her without warning, which was very Tamsin—diagnosis first, consent second. She smelled of yarrow and lamp smoke. "Your left knee," she said into Elodie's ear. "It's going to give you trouble on downhills. Wrap it before noon every day, not after it hurts. And eat the apricots. Potassium."

"You've planned my whole exile."

"Somebody had to. You certainly hadn't." Tamsin pulled back and looked at her with those clear clinical eyes that saw through bandages and bluster alike. "You know what I'd prescribe, if you asked."

"I'm not asking."

"No," Tamsin said. "But I'll say it anyway, because I'm the medic and you can't stop me. Come back. Not to hide. To visit. There's a difference, and you of all people finally know what it is."

Ferro was last of the adults. He didn't hug—he wasn't built for it, physically or spiritually—but he took her hand in both of his, callus against callus, and held it a moment like he was reading sign.

"Roads east are watched," he said. "Roads south are washed out past the falls. North there's nothing but rock and honest weather." A pause. "I'd go north."

"Ferro. Are you giving me directions?"

"I'm telling you where the land tells the truth." He released her hand. "You spent twenty years being nowhere. Be somewhere now. Even if it's only rock."

Which left Isandro, who had been vibrating slightly this entire time, the way a kettle vibrates just before it commits to whistling.

Elodie crouched a little—not much; her knees were, per Tamsin, a matter of ongoing negotiation—and looked at him. Sixteen now, or nearly. Tall in a way that suggested he hadn't finished deciding how tall. Sunburned, capable, carrying more weight behind his eyes than any boy should, but wearing it upright.

"So," she said. "Ranger-in-training."

"Ranger-in-training," he confirmed. "Magistrate Halloway says I can start with the water routes. Somebody has to teach the river towns to read green foam."

"Green foam means flash flood upstream."

"Green foam means flash flood upstream," he repeated solemnly, and then ruined it by grinning. "I'm going to say that to people for the rest of my life. Every time. In your voice."

"My voice isn't funny."

"Your voice is *exactly* funny. That's why we kept you."

The laugh got out of her before she could catch it, and once it was out, it brought friends. She laughed until her ribs complained, standing in the gray-gold light with her strange nameless family arranged around her, and thought: *this* is what I was protecting all those years. Not the secrecy. This.

When she could speak again, she said, "You were a horse thief."

"I was a *water* thief."

"You were starving in a salt flat stealing from my canteen, and I should have turned you over to the caravan magistrates and saved myself a decade of worry."

Isandro considered this. "But then who would've dug the latrines?"

"The latrines," Elodie said, "were the single greatest argument in favor of mercy I have ever encountered."

He hugged her then, quick and fierce, face buried against her shoulder the way he hadn't done since he was small enough that she could carry him over bad ground. She let herself have three seconds of it. Four. Five, if anyone asked, but no one would ask, because everyone in this crew understood accounting.

"Write to me," she said into his hair. "Care of the waystation at Dry Fork. Bex will figure out how it reaches me. Bex can figure out anything short of honesty."

"I heard that," Bex said, without turning around.

"You were meant to."

Isandro stepped back. His eyes were wet and he was pretending they weren't, which was also something he'd learned from her, and she resolved to feel guilty about it later, on the road, with time to spare.

"What name will you use?" he asked. "Out there. People will ask."

Elodie straightened. She looked east, where the light was coming up along the escarpment rim, gilding the top of the world while the canyon below her still held the night.

"Elodie," she said. "Just Elodie. Yarrow too, if they press. It's mine. I paid for it."

* * *

She walked north, because Ferro was right—the north roads told the truth—and because north lay the high ground above the river country, and because she wanted, one last time, to pass the place where the dead waited under their stones.

The morning was cool and enormous. The Escarpment in early light was at its most honest: every fold of rock visible, every shadow accounted for, nothing hidden that the sun couldn't find within the hour. She had spent two decades learning to move through country like a rumor, and now she walked upright on the skyline like a fool, or a free woman, and found the two conditions surprisingly similar.

Around midmorning she stopped at a seep she remembered, drank, refilled, and ate a strip of dried meat while a lizard regarded her from a stone with what she chose to interpret as approval.

"Well," she told the lizard. "I did it. I broke the code."

The lizard said nothing. Lizards were famously discreet; it was why she'd always liked them.

*Take nothing you can't carry,* the code went. *Leave no name behind.* She had lived it so long it had felt like virtue. It had taken a burned homestead, a field of cairns, two hundred names read aloud into the dawn air at Corrow, and a boy nearly drowning in white water to show her the difference between a code and a hiding place.

A code protects the weak. A hiding place protects you.

She'd been hiding so well she'd forgotten there was anything on the other side of the wall.

By afternoon of the second day she reached the rim trail that overlooked the cairn field. She knew it before she saw it—the land changed there, went quiet in a way that wasn't emptiness. She crested the rise and looked down at hundreds of stone mounds scattered across the benchland like a frozen flock, each one raised by Tamsin's hands and the hands of porters and townsfolk who had come upriver after the magistrate's ruling, adding stones, whispering names they'd only just learned.

Some of the cairns had markers now. Small ones. Scratched tin, carved wood, a few proper stones cut by the masons in Corrow who had refused payment. *Names read aloud*, Halloway had said, *and names stay read.* Two hundred men and boys who had been ledger entries and ash were becoming, stone by patient stone, people again.

Elodie stood on the rim for a long time.

She had passed this place twice before in her life. The first time, twenty years ago, with a document tube strapped to her back and a lie folded inside it, she had ridden past at speed, eyes forward, because looking would have cost her something and she'd been young and poor and unwilling to pay. The second time, weeks ago, coming upstream with Vane's guns behind them and the truth ahead, she had looked, and it had nearly broken her, and that breaking had turned out to be the door.

Now she walked down among the cairns.

The wind moved through the grass between the mounds with a sound like low conversation. She went from one to another, reading the new markers, some careful, some crooked, one written by a child's hand with the letters drifting downhill. *Tomas Rell. Miner. Age 19.* Someone had added: *liked dogs.*

"He liked dogs," Elodie said aloud, to no one, to him. "Good. That's worth knowing. That's worth more than everything the company ever wrote about you."

Her throat tightened. She let it. That was the whole discipline now, she was discovering—not the old discipline of silence, but the harder new one of feeling things in daylight and surviving it anyway.

She walked to the center of the field, where the largest cairn stood, the one Tamsin had built first, before any of them knew how many there would be. It was waist-high and wide and solid, and it had no marker yet, because Tamsin had refused to guess at names she couldn't verify. *We don't invent the dead,* she'd said. *We wait for them to arrive.*

From her pack, Elodie took a stone.

She had carried it four days. She'd picked it up on the bank of the Anselm, near the source, near the spring that fed everything—the fenced, claimed, fought-over spring that was, when you knelt beside it, just water coming up out of the dark because that was what water did. The stone was river-smoothed, gray with a vein of white running through it like a signature.

She knelt—both knees, consequences be damned—and set the stone on top of the great cairn, fitting it into a gap where it sat as though it had been waiting.

"For the unnamed," she said. Her voice came out rough. She cleared it and started again, louder, because the dead deserved volume and she was done whispering. "For the unnamed. I carried papers that erased you. I didn't know, and it doesn't matter, because not knowing was my job and I did it well. I can't give you back the years. Nobody can. But your names are in the record now, in ink, in a courthouse with a young magistrate who reads them every morning so he doesn't forget whose son he is. And I—" 

She stopped. Breathed.

"And I am Elodie Yarrow," she said. "That's my name. My real one. I stole nothing to get it and I hid it for twenty years, and I'm sorry, and I'm saying it here where it can do some good. Witness me."

The wind moved through the grass. Somewhere downslope, a dog barked—there were dogs in the river towns now, real ones, and she chose to believe Tomas Rell approved.

She stayed until the light went long and gold, and then she climbed back to the rim, and at the top she did not look back. Looking back was for people checking whether they'd been followed. No one was following her. For the first time in her adult life, no one was following her, and the freedom of it was so large it was almost vertigo, and she stood on the skyline and laughed at the sheer ridiculous size of the sky.

* * *

The north road was everything Ferro promised: rock, honest weather, and not another soul for six days.

She fell into the old rhythms gladly. Rise before the light. Walk while it was cool. Read the country—scat, tracks, the angle of dry washes, the mood of clouds. Camp high, sleep cold, wake sharp. Her body, which had spent years pretending to be older than it was, unclenched by degrees, and by the fourth day her stride had lengthened and her left knee had accepted the terms of employment.

She talked to herself. She was mildly embarrassed about this until she remembered she'd spent twenty years talking to no one, and at least now she was doing it openly.

On the fifth day she crossed a high plateau where the wind had polished the stones into shapes like sleeping animals, and she ate her first apricot.

Bex had been right, curse her. Day five was exactly when the road turned long and the horizon turned smug and a person needed something sweet enough to constitute an argument for continuing. Elodie sat on a wind-carved ledge, ate the apricot slowly, and let herself miss them—really miss them, specifically, one at a time. Bex's ledgers and her terrible jokes about balancing. Ovid humming while he worked, always slightly out of tune, always convinced otherwise. Tamsin's hands, sure as tide. Ferro's silences, which said more than most people's speeches. And Isandro—Isandro learning to be a ranger in daylight, with a name and a badge and a magistrate's protection, walking the water routes and telling strangers about green foam in her voice.

She missed them the way you miss a limb, not the way you miss a place. Places you can leave. Family you carry.

That was the discovery of the exile road, the one she hadn't expected: solitude without bitterness was possible. She had assumed the two came bundled—that alone meant abandoned, that invisible meant worthless. But walking north with a full pack, a real name, and a clean conscience, she found the quiet was just quiet. Roomy, even. She could hear herself think, and what she thought, mostly, was: *Well. That worked out better than expected.*

Not painlessly. Nothing worth the ink had been painless. There were nights she woke reaching for a pistol that wasn't there and remembered instead that the danger was over, truly over, and lay listening to the enormous harmless dark. There was the morning she passed a company survey stake, rusted and leaning, its permit number weathered to illegibility, and spat on it with tremendous dignity. There was the evening she caught herself calculating sightlines and escape routes out of pure habit and had to laugh at herself—an old ranger, alone on a safe road, fortifying her camp against absolutely nothing.

"Honestly," she told the campfire. "You'd think the war followed you."

The fire crackled. The stars came out in their unhurried thousands. She slept fine.

On the eighth day the land began to change. The rock softened into soil, the soil into scrub, the scrub into the first straggling farms of the northern valleys—fence posts, a windmill, goats with the entitled manner of goats everywhere. Smoke rose from chimneys. A child saw her on the road and stared, and Elodie, who for twenty years would have melted sideways into the terrain, lifted a hand and waved.

The child waved back with both arms, the way children do, as if waving were a whole-body conviction.

At midday she reached a crossroads with a leaning signpost. One arm pointed toward the river towns, far south now, days behind her. Another pointed north and west toward country she'd never seen, marked with a name she didn't know: *Marrow Vale, 40 miles.*

She stood at the crossroads longer than the decision required. The decision itself was easy—north and west, new country, somewhere to be useful that had never heard of the Concession or the Anselm or the woman who'd carried the papers. What held her was simpler: this was the last fork where she was still *leaving*. Past it, she'd be *arriving*. It seemed worth marking the difference.

She dug in her pack and found, tucked in beside the apricots, a folded square of paper she hadn't put there. Bex's handwriting, small and exact:

*Entry, final. One captain, discharged with honor. Assets: one pack, one knife, one conscience (recently repaired), one family (non-transferable, non-expiring). Liabilities: none outstanding. Balance: owed forward. Pay it to strangers.*

Elodie read it three times. Then she folded it away carefully, next to the walnut-sized boulder-moving tool she was absolutely going to use on a boulder someday, out of principle.

"Sentimental old bookkeeper," she said fondly, to the south, to all of them.

Then she turned north and west, toward Marrow Vale and whatever needed doing there, and she walked with her name riding easy on her shoulders like a coat that finally fit.

Behind her, far to the south, a bird rose off the rim of the Escarpment carrying something small and pale in its beak—a carrier pigeon, homing toward Dry Fork waystation, a letter sealed with a thumbprint in candle wax, written in a young ranger's careful hand: *Dear Elodie. Spring flood came early. Green foam everywhere. You'd have loved it. Write back soon. —Isandro.*

She didn't know it yet. But the road was long, and letters, like rivers, always find their way to the sea.
