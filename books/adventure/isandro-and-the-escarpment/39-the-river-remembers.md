## 39. The River Remembers

The old camp had gone soft in their absence. That was Bex's first thought, standing at the lip of the wash with her ledger under her arm and the spring wind pushing grit against her boots. The tarp over the cook shelter had sagged into a hammock shape. Sand had crept up over the fire ring like a tide coming in slow motion. A lizard regarded her from the top of the water barrel with the flat entitlement of a squatter.

"Rent's due," she told it.

It did not move. She respected that.

Bex had kept books for this crew for eleven years, and in all that time she had never once written a line she'd be ashamed to read aloud in a courtroom—which was fortunate, because lately it seemed half the Escarpment wanted them in one. The Corrow trial had emptied three of her inkwells and given her a permanent cramp in her writing hand that Tamsin swore was psychosomatic and Bex swore was justice taking its toll.

Now the ledgers were public record. Or most of them. She'd kept back exactly one page—the page with Isandro's first wages on it, four coppers and a promise—and she carried it the way other people carried letters from home.

She heard Ovid before she saw him, which was the point of Ovid. He came down the wash with his hands empty and his pockets audibly not, whistling something tuneless.

"You're late," Bex said.

"I'm early. Everyone else is later." He dropped onto a crate, patted his pockets out of habit, then visibly decided against whatever lived in them. "Ferro's a mile out. Tamsin's two behind him, walking, because she says horses are gossip."

"Horses *are* gossip."

"They're also faster than grief," Ovid said, "which is why I ride them anyway."

They worked in silence after that, the good kind, the kind two people who'd spent a decade together could fill without effort. Bex righted the tarp. Ovid rebuilt the fire ring higher, because he always built things higher than they needed to be, and if you asked him why he'd say *structural honesty* and refuse to elaborate.

By the time Ferro trotted in, leading a second horse loaded with supplies, the kettle was on. Ferro dismounted the way he did everything—without any wasted motion and without any visible pleasure—and scanned the camp twice before he let his shoulders come down an inch.

"All quiet?" Bex asked.

"Quiet as a courthouse," Ferro said, which since the trial meant something entirely different than it used to.

* * *

Tamsin arrived at dusk, on foot as promised, and the camp felt complete in a way none of them said out loud. Four of five. They cooked without discussing the empty bedroll. They ate without discussing it. Then Ovid, who could dismantle a bridge but not sit comfortably through an evening, finally said:

"Somebody's going to say it, so it may as well be me. It's strange without him."

"It's strange without *her* too," Ferro said.

"Yes, but we expected Elodie. Elodie leaving was—" He gestured vaguely at the whole sky. "That was the ending. Endings make sense. Isandro staying doesn't fit my diagrams."

"Nothing about that boy fits your diagrams," Bex said fondly. "He's the variable you didn't account for. Best thing that ever happened to your arithmetic."

Ovid considered this with the seriousness he gave everything. "I did once try to account for him. The equation collapsed."

"That's called love, you great demolitionist," Tamsin said, and threw a pebble at him.

Later, when the fire had settled and Ferro had taken first watch out of pure habit, Tamsin sat with her back against the water barrel and let herself think about names. She'd done a lot of that lately. In Corrow there was a wall now—limestone, newly cut, still pale as bone—with two hundred names chiseled into it in Halloway's careful hand. She had read every one of them. She had touched several. Some of the miners had been young. One had been fourteen, which had taken her somewhere dark for a day and a half until Ferro had silently put a cup of tea in her hands and stood nearby being a large immovable fact.

The dead had their names back. That was the miracle, and miracles were supposed to feel like trumpets, but mostly it felt like paperwork, which Tamsin suspected was what all real miracles felt like from the inside.

What stayed with her wasn't the wall. It was the morning after the reading, when a woman she'd never met—a river-town widow, gray-haired, hands like rope—took Tamsin's arm outside the courthouse and said, "My father's name was third from the bottom. Nobody's said it in twenty years. I'd forgotten how it sounded." And then the woman had cried, and Tamsin had cried, and neither of them had known each other's names yet, and somehow that hadn't mattered at all.

Names mattered going forward too, she thought. That was the part nobody warned you about. You spend twenty years learning to leave no name behind, and then one day the code changes, and you have to learn the opposite thing at your age, and your mouth keeps trying to introduce you as *nobody*.

"Tamsin." Ferro's voice, low, from the edge of the firelight. "Rider. Single. Coming up the wash."

Everyone moved at once, smooth and wordless, the old choreography—but then Ferro held up a hand. "It's fine. It's a boy on a mule."

"A boy," Bex repeated.

"On a mule. Carrying a satchel marked with the Corrow magistrate's seal." A pause. "And wearing a ranger's badge."

Silence around the fire. Then Ovid started grinning so hard it looked painful.

"Oh," he said. "Oh, this is going to be good."

* * *

Isandro rode into the light of the fire looking taller than any of them remembered, which was absurd, because it had been four months. But he sat the mule differently now. He wore the badge on his chest instead of in his pocket, where apprentices keep things they're not sure they've earned. His hair was shorter. There was a scar on his forearm he hadn't had before, and when Bex looked at it, he said, "Flash flood. March. I'll tell you the whole story, it's a good one, I come out of it holding a baby goat."

"You're a ranger," Ferro said. Not a question. An audit.

"Deputy ranger. Halloway says full badge in the autumn, if I stop losing forms." He swung down and embraced them one at a time—Bex first, hard enough to knock her ledger askew; Tamsin longest; Ovid, who lifted him clean off the ground; Ferro last, with one arm and a nod that contained more affection than most men manage in a lifetime.

Then he stepped back and looked around the camp, at the sagging tarp and the squatting lizard and the four faces lit by firelight, and his eyes went bright, and he pretended they didn't, and everyone politely pretended along with him.

"I brought mail," he said, voice only slightly unsteady. "Official correspondence. Also jam."

"Jam first," said Bex, because she was wise.

They ate jam on flatbread while Isandro talked—about Corrow rebuilding, about the river towns organizing their own water councils, about Pell, who had taken a post teaching engineering and was famously terrible at discipline and beloved for it. About the widow whose father was third from the bottom, who had appointed herself keeper of the memorial wall and chased off two vandals with a broom. About the spring floods, which he described with his hands, rising out of his chair, nearly upsetting the kettle: the river coming down the gorge brown and roaring, carrying whole trees, and the new warning system—foam watchers on the high banks, signal mirrors chained to posts—working for the first time, getting a shepherd family to high ground with minutes to spare.

"Nobody drowned," he said, and stopped, and looked at the fire. "Nobody drowned," he said again, quieter, like a man setting down something heavy. "First flood season in the canyon's history, far as anyone knows, where nobody drowned."

Tamsin reached over and squeezed his wrist. Neither of them said anything else, because nothing needed saying. Two porters under stones in the canyon country. Two hundred names on a wall. The arithmetic of water, finally being paid down.

"There's one more letter," Isandro said, digging in the satchel. "Not official. It came to the Corrow office addressed to 'the crew of no names, wherever they aren't.' Postmarked from—" he checked— "honestly, the postmark's smudged. Somewhere west. It's from Elodie."

The fire popped. Nobody moved for a moment.

"She wrote to us?" Ovid said. "She knows where we—"

"She doesn't. Halloway's office forwards things. There's a system. It's very boring and very clever and I'm not allowed to describe it." He handed the letter across the fire to Bex, as quartermaster, keeper of records, reader of difficult news. "She wrote one copy. For all of us."

Bex turned the envelope over. Cheap paper, road-worn, smelling faintly of woodsmoke and some flower that didn't grow anywhere near here. Her hands—steady these days, steady as stone, the shaking long since paid off—opened it carefully.

"Read it aloud," Tamsin said. "Please."

Bex cleared her throat.

"*Dear impossible people,*" she read. "*I am writing this from a town whose name I'll omit, partly for habit and partly because the name is ridiculous and you'd never let me hear the end of it.*"

A ripple of laughter around the fire. Even Ferro's mouth did the thing it did instead of smiling.

"*The road west is long and mostly flat, which gives a person time to think, and I want you to know I've been thinking about you all, and about the code, and about whether we ever really believed it or just found it useful. I've decided it doesn't matter. What matters is that for eleven years you were the only map I trusted, and maps, it turns out, can survive being redrawn.*

"*Tell Bex the accounts are settled—I left the last of the Vane testimony money with the widows' fund in Corrow, earmarked for the porters' families. Tell Ovid to stop building things higher than they need to be.*"

"He'll never do it," Isandro said.

"*Never,*" Ovid agreed solemnly.

"*Tell Tamsin that I passed a graveyard outside a town called Merrow, and someone had marked every stone with names, even the ones worn blank, even the ones they had to guess. I stood there a long time. I thought of her.*

"*Tell Ferro I saw tracks on the road that spooked me for a full minute before I realized they were made by a man walking backward for a bet, and that I laughed alone in the desert like a lunatic, and that I miss being spooked alongside him.*

"*And tell Isandro—*" Bex paused. Around the fire, the boy in question had gone very still. "*—tell Isandro that I walked past the cairn field on my way out. I left a marker stone, the way we never did. And I said the names. All two hundred. It took me most of a day. My voice went hoarse and a hawk watched me the entire time like I was insane, and maybe I was, a little. But the wind came up off the river afterward, and I choose to believe it was approval.*

"*You taught me that a person can be no one and still be everything to the people who know them. I intend to find out if a person can be someone and still be free. Early reports are promising. The jam out here is inferior. Send jam.*

"*Yours in exile and excellent health, E.Y.*

"*P.S. — If any of you ever find yourselves on a road west, look for a woman trading stories for supper. She'll have a scar on her left hand and an opinion about everything. She'll be glad to see you. She won't admit it.*"

Bex lowered the letter. For a moment nobody spoke, and the river—two miles off, invisible in the dark, but audible, always audible now that they knew how to listen—filled the silence with its low continuous work.

"Well," said Ovid at last, thick-voiced. "She's fine."

"She's better than fine," Tamsin said. "She's *free*."

"She's eating bad jam," said Bex, "which is the truest exile of all," and that broke the tension, and they laughed, and Isandro laughed hardest, wiping his eyes and blaming the smoke, and nobody corrected him.

* * *

They stayed up late, the way crews do when the work is done and the company is precious. Isandro produced the baby goat story, which was indeed a good one, involving a flooded culvert, a heroic swim, and a magistrate's daughter who had named the goat after Ovid, to Ovid's mixed feelings. Ferro actually smiled—briefly, in shadow, but witnesses existed. Bex got out the private page, the one with Isandro's first wages, and showed it to him, and he read his own name there—his real one, spoken and written and recorded—and touched the ink like it might still be wet.

Around midnight, Ferro took second watch and Isandro, refusing the offer of a bedroll in favor of sitting up with the adults like he was still one of them and also like he'd never left, ended up beside Tamsin at the fire's edge.

"Do you ever regret it?" he asked. "Any of it. The trial. The names. Everything changing."

Tamsin considered the question properly, because he deserved that. "Regret's the wrong tool," she said finally. "You don't use a hammer to check if a house is level. But I'll tell you what I feel. I feel lighter. Twenty years I carried those dead without their names, and it turns out I was carrying them wrong. Names are how you carry people. Anything else is just weight."

Isandro nodded slowly. Out past the firelight, the land ran dark to the river, and the river ran on toward Corrow and the towns and the sea, doing its patient ancient work, remembering everything it passed, keeping every name it was given.

"One more thing," he said, and dug into the satchel one last time. "Halloway sent this. Officially it's a summons for testimony in the appeals case. Unofficially—" he handed Bex a second envelope, heavier, sealed with the magistrate's crest— "unofficially, he said, and I quote, 'tell those four stubborn ghosts that the river towns could use rangers who know how to stay invisible when it matters and visible when it counts. The badge is theirs if they want it. No pressure. Well. Some pressure.'"

Bex held the envelope and looked around the fire at her crew—at Ovid already sketching bridge designs in the dirt, at Tamsin with the dawn's first gray light catching her face, at Ferro watching the horizon the way he always would, and at Isandro, no longer theirs exactly, but theirs forever, wearing a badge and waiting on their answer.

"The vote splits three-two," she said, testing the old joke.

"It won't," Tamsin said.

It didn't.

Far to the west, on a road with a ridiculous name, a woman with a scar on her left hand stopped at a crossroads inn, traded a story for supper, and felt—for no reason she could name, or rather, for every reason she could—a sudden warmth, like a letter arriving, like a river turning, like a name being said kindly somewhere behind her. She raised her cup to the east, to the people who knew her, and drank.

The river carried it all downstream: the names, the letter, the laughter of a crew around a dying fire. And the river, being a river, kept everything.
