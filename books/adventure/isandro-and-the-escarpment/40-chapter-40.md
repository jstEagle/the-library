## 40. Chapter 40

### Bex

The ledger had been gone from Corrow for a month, locked in the magistrate's vault where it belonged, and Bex still caught herself reaching for the weight of it at the bottom of her pack like a phantom tooth.

She was rebuilding the waystation instead. That was the thing nobody warned you about after a war—however small, however brief—and she supposed this one counted as both: there'd been guns at a courthouse, and a magistrate's signature, and two hundred names read aloud into the morning air while strangers wept into their sleeves. Afterward, everyone went home. Except home, for the crew of no names, had always been a camp that moved.

So Bex made it stop moving.

The waystation sat at the old fork of the River Anselm, close enough to Corrow to hear its church bell on windless days, far enough that Isandro could ride out on his half-grown bay horse and pretend he wasn't just visiting. She'd bought it with the last of Vane's amnesty money—which felt right, somehow, dirty coin washed clean by use—and she'd spent three weeks re-thatching the roof and arguing with Ovid about whether the well needed a second pulley.

"It needs a second pulley," Ovid said now, hanging upside down off the wellhead with his boots hooked over the crossbeam, which was how he did most of his thinking. "One rope for drawing water, one rope for lowering things you don't want to draw up. Buckets. Letters. The occasional magistrate."

"Magistrates have legs."

"Yours doesn't, when he wants something."

Bex let that stand, because it was true. Magistrate Halloway rode out twice a week now, young and earnest and still flinching slightly at his own father's name, and every visit he left a little more of himself behind—a stack of land claims to arbitrate, a question about water rights he already knew the answer to, once, memorably, a pie. Tamsin had pronounced the pie edible, which from Tamsin was a eulogy.

The river towns were learning to keep books again. Real ones. Bex ran the waystation's ledger herself, in her own cramped hand, every transaction dated and signed, because she had decided—if a woman who'd spent twenty years erasing herself could be said to decide anything—that the difference between them and the company men came down to exactly this: they wrote things down, and they signed them, and they stood behind what they'd written.

She was entering the day's trade—two sacks of meal from the Aldous farm, a coil of rope, a promise of mutton next week that she'd learned to record as *mutton (aspirational)*—when Ferro came up the track at a lope, dusty to the eyebrows, leading a string of three horses that had clearly been ridden hard.

"Caravan's coming up from the salt side," Ferro said. "Big one. And Bex—" He stopped, and Ferro never stopped mid-sentence unless the sentence had teeth. "There's a rider ahead of them. Alone. Riding like the devil's collecting."

"Company?"

"No." Ferro shook his head slowly. "Riding like somebody going somewhere she means to arrive."

*She.* Bex set down her pen.

### Tamsin

Tamsin was in the herb garden she'd bullied out of the gravel behind the station—twelve stubborn rows of feverfew, yarrow, and a mint plant that had opinions—when the rider came through the gate.

Elodie Yarrow looked older. That was the first thing, and Tamsin catalogued it the way she catalogued everything, clinically and without mercy: older, thinner, sunburnt along the cheekbones in a pattern that said long roads and poor hats. Her coat was new but broken in fast, the way coats broke in when a person slept in them. She swung down from the horse and stood a moment just looking at the place—the repaired roof, the garden, the smoke from the cookfire—as if checking it against some private inventory.

"You're late," Tamsin said.

"I'm exactly on time," Elodie said. "I'm always exactly on time. It's everyone else who's early."

"You missed the spring floods. Isandro nearly drowned again, but this time recreationally, which we've all agreed is progress."

"He didn't write me about that."

"He writes you about everything else. Fourteen pages last month. I know because I carried it and I read the outside of it, which is legal." Tamsin wiped her hands on her apron and studied her old captain with the frankness of twenty years' friendship. "You look terrible."

"I look free." Elodie smiled, and it was the same smile, crooked and unarmored, the one that had started all of this. "It's an adjustment."

They walked together toward the cookfire, and Tamsin noted—said nothing, but noted—that Elodie walked past the cairn they'd built at the station's edge without stopping, then stopped anyway, ten paces on, and turned back. A small pile of stones, one for each of them, one for the porters, one blank stone at the end that nobody had explained and everybody understood.

Elodie added a stone of her own. Picked it up from the track, dusted it, set it carefully.

"For the road?" Tamsin asked.

"For the ones still on it." Elodie looked at the blank stone a moment longer. "And maybe, eventually, for coming back. We'll see which it turns out to be."

### Ovid

Ovid heard the news from the well, where he was installing the second pulley against explicit orders, and arrived at the cookfire covered in rope fiber and vindication.

He found the whole strange family assembled: Bex pretending to be busy with the ledger, Ferro leaning on the fence post doing his statue impression, Tamsin ladling out stew with the air of a woman administering medicine whether it was wanted or not. And Elodie in the middle of it, hat pushed back, eating Tamsin's stew with genuine enthusiasm, which Ovid took as proof either of her courage or her long absence.

"So," Ovid said, dropping onto the bench beside her. "Exile treating you well?"

"Terribly. Nobody out there knows my reputation. I have to build it from nothing. Do you know how exhausting it is to be anonymous on purpose?"

"Twenty years, Elodie. You did it for twenty years."

"Yes, but I was good at it." She pointed her spoon at him. "Now I'm famous in three river towns and a legend in a fourth, and every caravan master wants to shake my hand, and yesterday a child asked me if it's true I fought a gun battle on a raft in a flooded gorge, and I had to say yes, and then I had to say *why*, and the why took an hour, and the child fell asleep at the forty-minute mark, which I chose to take as a compliment."

"The story does sag in the middle," Ovid agreed. "The rapids are excellent. The courthouse scene drags."

"That's what I told Halloway. He's very sensitive about the courthouse scene."

Ferro spoke from the fence without moving anything but his mouth. "Vane?"

Elodie was quiet a moment. The fire popped. Somewhere downriver, the water talked to itself the way it always did, patient, keeping its own records.

"She writes," Elodie said finally. "Short letters. No return address, ever, which is very her. Last one said she'd found work running survey crews in the northern ranges—honest ones, she says, though she would say that. Said the mountains don't ask where you've been." She shrugged. "Said thank you. Took her four drafts, I think. You could see where she'd crossed things out."

"From Sable Vane," Bex said, "that's practically a sonnet."

"It's a start." Elodie looked around the fire—at each of them, one by one, the accounting she'd done a hundred times and would do a hundred more. "That's all any of this is. A start. Two hundred names in a public book. A river that belongs to whoever drinks it. A boy with a badge and a horse he can't fully control." She smiled. "A start is not nothing. I spent twenty years believing a start was too dangerous to risk. Turns out it's the only thing worth having."

Ovid raised his tin cup. "To starts, then. And to second pulleys, which history will remember as the true foundation of civilization."

Nobody toasted the pulley. Ovid drank alone, magnificently.

### Ferro

Ferro took the dawn watch, because Ferro always took the dawn watch; sleep and he had reached an arrangement decades ago and saw no reason to renegotiate.

He stood at the corral rail watching the light come up the Escarpment—the great stacked cliffs going gold, then rose, then the flat white of full morning—and thought about how many dawns he'd watched from hiding. From ridgelines, from culverts, from the backs of moving wagons. Always watching the light arrive somewhere he couldn't stay.

This morning the light arrived here, and he could stay, and the strangeness of it hadn't worn off yet.

Hoofbeats on the east track. One rider, young, riding badly in the specific confident way of someone whose horse has forgiven him eleven hundred times and is keeping count. Ferro didn't need to look. But he looked anyway, because that was the job, and because some mornings you check even the things you know, especially the things you know.

Isandro came up the track with the sun behind him and the river's mist burning off around his horse's legs, and he was carrying, of all things, a bundle of survey stakes over one shoulder like a man bringing firewood, and grinning like a man bringing the sunrise.

"You're up early," Ferro said.

"You're always up early. One of us should vary it." Isandro swung down, dumped the stakes against the rail. "Halloway's got me marking the new boundary lines. The real ones. Crown land, town land, common grazing. He says a map is a promise, so we'd better make promises we can keep." He glanced sideways at Ferro, suddenly younger, suddenly the boy from the salt flats again. "I wrote the boundary markers myself. My name's on the corner stake at the Concession line. Carved it deep."

Ferro considered this. Somewhere upstream, water moved over stones that had been graves, and was only water now.

"Deep's good," he said. "Water gets to everything eventually. Might as well leave it something worth finding."

They stood together while the camp woke—smoke from the cookfire, Tamsin's voice rising in argument with the mint, Bex's pen scratching, Ovid swearing cheerfully at the well. The sounds of a place that existed on maps now. The sounds of a place that could be found.

Isandro leaned on the rail beside him. "Ferro. Can I ask you something?"

"You can ask. Whether I answer depends on the question."

"When you found me on the flats—all those years ago, the thief who wasn't—did you know? What I'd turn into? All this?"

Ferro watched the light finish climbing the Escarpment. "No," he said. "Nobody knows that. Not about anyone. That's the whole point of people." He paused. "But Elodie took one look at you stealing water to stay alive and made a bet. Best odds she ever got."

Down the track, Elodie emerged from her tent, hat in hand, hair wild, squinting into the morning like a woman daring the day to be ordinary. Isandro straightened up. Bex looked over from the ledger. Even the mint seemed to settle.

"Right," Elodie called out, in the voice that had once held a crew together across a desert and a massacre and a river's whole temper. "Stakes won't carve themselves. Isandro—horses. Ferro—flank watch, old habits. Bex, close the book, the book will survive. Ovid—"

"I'm finishing the pulley."

"You're finishing the pulley," she agreed. "But you're finishing it *fast*."

They moved. All of them, into the morning, up the track, toward the line on the map where a boy's name waited carved deep in wood, and beyond it the green country and the gray country and the long road south where letters traveled between exiles and rivals and friends.

Behind them the waystation stood open, door unlatched, stew on the fire, a chair left out for whoever came next.

Ahead, the river ran on toward the sea, carrying everything—silt and sunlight, names and silence—and giving it all back, sooner or later, to the thirsty ground.

The End
