## 1. The Mood of the Sandbar

The river told you things before it showed you them. Ansel Eriksen had learned that at fourteen, standing on her father's shoulders while he pointed out the lie in the water — the smooth brown stretch that looked like deep channel and wasn't, the boil of current over a bar that hadn't been on any chart since the flood two years back. *Read it with your feet first,* he'd said. *Your eyes will try to cheat you.*

Twenty-two years later she stood alone on the roof of the *Magpie*'s pilothouse, barefoot on hot tar paper, reading the morning river like a letter from someone who owed her money.

The sandbar below Grayling Bend had moved again.

She could see it in the way the current kinked — a lazy S where yesterday there'd been a clean run down the middle. The bar was walking east, the way bars did in low water, shedding itself downstream grain by grain and building a new spine a hundred yards off. By next week the charts would be wrong. They were always wrong. The river redrew itself nightly, patient as a forger, and every pilot on the Vess Line still ran off surveys three seasons old because Vess Shipping didn't believe in paying for new ones.

Ansel believed in paying for them. She just couldn't afford to.

"Bar's shifted," she called down through the pilothouse hatch. "Starboard side's gone soft. Take us wide left, then cut back hard past the dead willow."

Tam Okafor's face appeared in the hatch, dark against the dim interior, one eyebrow already up. "There's no mark at the dead willow."

"There is now."

He looked at her a moment longer — Tam never took a reading on faith, which was why he was still here when everyone else had gone — then ducked below. A moment later the *Magpie*'s engine note changed, dropped half a tone, and the old boat leaned into her turn with the graceless dignity of a fat woman waltzing.

The *Magpie* was ninety feet of patched hull and stubbornness, built the same year Ansel was born, and like Ansel she was held together mostly by refusal. Her paddlewheel thumped the water in an uneven rhythm — *thump-thump, thump* — that any honest engineer would have condemned years ago. Her paint was the color of nothing in particular. Her name had been repainted so many times the letters sat in ridges above the surrounding wood, like scar tissue.

Ansel loved her the way you love a thing that has stayed when everything else left.

She came down off the roof and into the pilothouse, where the air smelled of hot brass, pipe smoke Tam pretended she didn't notice, and the faint green rot-smell of the river itself. Through the glass the water unrolled ahead of them, brown and muscular, carrying its usual freight of silt, cut cane, a drowned dog bloated and turning slowly, and — far upstream, catching the light — a long white sliver that might have been a crane taking flight or might have been a snag waiting to open their hull like a letter.

"Coffee," Ansel said.

"Pot's empty."

"You're the mate."

"I'm the *only* mate." Tam poured anyway, from the pot he'd secretly refilled, and handed her the chipped tin cup. That was the arrangement between them: he complained, she ordered, they both did the work of six people with the hands of two and a half. Down in the engine room, old Sefa was presumably keeping the *Magpie*'s heart beating, though Sefa communicated primarily through knocks on the pipes — one knock for all well, two for trouble, three for *come see this and bring the good wrench*. This morning there had been one knock, early, and silence since, which was as close to happiness as Sefa ever got.

"How's the run look?" Tam asked.

"Like money we don't have." Ansel drank. The coffee was terrible and scalding and exactly right. "Forty bales of cotton down to Meridian Landing, forty crates of salt pork coming back up. Vess takes sixty percent, fuel takes twenty, and if the wheel bearing holds till season's end we might clear enough to make a payment."

"Might."

"Might," she agreed.

They both looked at the river. It was easier than looking at each other.

The debt hung over the *Magpie* the way storm cloud hangs over flat water — not raining yet, but everyone aboard knew what it meant. Fourteen thousand crowns, owed to the Vess Shipping Line, secured against the boat herself, accruing interest at a rate Ansel suspected was illegal in at least two jurisdictions but provable in none. She'd signed the papers seven years ago, after the fire, after the winter the *Magpie* spent frozen in at Cutter's Slough with no cargo, no crew wages, and no options. Vess's man had come out across the ice in a fur coat worth more than the boat and smiled at her with teeth like piano keys.

*Everyone signs eventually, Captain. The river doesn't forgive debts. Why should I?*

She'd signed. She'd told herself it was for the boat. Some nights, in the honest dark, she admitted it was also because refusing would have meant admitting how completely she'd failed — the boat, the crew, the name her father had left her, all of it balanced on a knife-edge of credit extended by the woman she hated most on the river.

Maren Vess. Who had started as a rival, become a creditor, and ended as weather — something vast and impersonal that decided whether your crops lived.

* * *

They made Grayling Bend at midmorning, and the bend tried to kill them, the way bends did.

It came on fast: the channel narrowing between a collapsed bank on the left and the walking bar on the right, the current stacking up in the throat of it, and right where the water should have been deepest, a slick oily smoothness that Ansel's eyes read as safe and her feet — thirty years of instinct lodged somewhere below thought — read as a lie.

"Hard right," she said quietly. "Now."

Tam threw the wheel without argument. The *Magpie* heeled, her wake swinging wide, and where her bow *would* have passed — where the chart said eight feet of good water — the surface peeled back just slightly, just enough, revealing the brown shoulder of the bar sitting eighteen inches under the river's skin.

They slid past it close enough that Ansel could have spat on it. Then the channel opened, the current forgave them, and the bend fell away behind.

Tam exhaled through his nose. "You saw that?"

"Saw the river flinch."

"One day I'm going to write down how you do that."

"Don't bother. Can't teach it. Either the river talks to you or it doesn't." She said it lightly, but the words landed heavier than she meant them to, because there had been others the river talked to, once, and talking hadn't saved them.

The silence lasted four seconds, which was about standard.

"Captain—" Tam began.

"Bearings for Meridian are on the slate. Wake me at the fuel dock." She stepped out of the pilothouse before he could say whatever he was going to say, because she already knew, because he said some version of it every few weeks, and because she had no answer that wasn't either a lie or a wound.

What he wanted to ask was: *When do we stop running these thin-water runs? When do we tell the story about the Sweet Amity?*

What she would have answered, if she answered, was: *Never, because the story ends with me standing on a levee counting survivors, and I've counted them every night for nine years and it always comes out the same.*

* * *

Nine years ago there had been a different boat and a different life. The *Sweet Amity*, out of Harrow Bend, Captain Odile Ferrant commanding — a woman who stood five foot two and piloted flood-stage water the way other people walked downstairs. Ansel had been her second pilot, young and arrogant and certain the river loved her.

Odile used to say the river didn't love anybody. *It tolerates us,* she'd say, tapping the wheel with two fingers, *the way you tolerate a cat. Respect the claws.*

Then came the charter. High-paying, upper-reaches work, monsoon season, the kind of run sensible pilots refused. Vess Line brokered it; Vess Line guaranteed the cargo; Odile needed the money for her crew's winter wages, and the papers looked clean, and the river was high and generous that year.

The *Sweet Amity* went up with eleven souls and came back as scattered wreckage and three survivors, Ansel among them, pulled half-drowned from a drift pile forty miles below where anyone could explain. The official finding was pilot error. Odile Ferrant's error. Dead women made convenient scapegoats, and the Vess Line collected the insurance, and Ansel — who had stood on that levee and watched the wreckage go by and known, known in her bones, that Odile would never have taken that line of water — kept her mouth shut because she had no proof and a living to make and a debt that arrived soon after, like a vulture that had smelled the whole business from miles off.

Eleven names. She carried them the way other captains carried charts. Odile. Brannic. Little Wren, who was seventeen and played the fiddle badly and beautifully. She recited them sometimes at night, not as prayer exactly, but as ballast. Something to keep the boat of herself upright.

And every year the debt grew, and every year the runs got thinner and meaner, and every year Maren Vess's boats got newer and whiter on the water, and Ansel told herself the arithmetic was simple: keep the *Magpie* alive, pay it down, someday be free.

Someday. The most expensive word on the river.

* * *

Meridian Landing appeared in the early afternoon, a smear of tin roofs and drying nets on the east bank, and Ansel brought the *Magpie* in with the kind of approach that made the deckhands on the Vess freighter moored downstream stop and watch — a long sliding turn using the eddy behind the coal shed, killing way at exactly the right moment so the boat kissed the pilings without a sound.

Sefa surfaced from the engine room, wiped her hands on a rag that was blacker than her hands, and gave Ansel a single nod, which from Sefa was a parade.

They were warping the cotton off when the boy came running down the landing, white shirt, Vess Line buttons, a courier's satchel banging his hip.

"Captain Eriksen?" He was breathing hard, more from nerves than the run. "Message from the Line. From Ms. Vess personally."

Ansel took the envelope. Heavy paper. Wax seal. The Vess crest — a stylized wave over a keel — pressed deep enough to feel under her thumb.

Tam had drifted over, pretending to check a mooring line. Sefa had stopped pretending to work at all.

Ansel broke the seal and read it twice. It was short. Short messages from powerful people were never good news.

*Captain Eriksen — I'll call at your berth tomorrow, noon. I have a proposition, and a deadline. Wear something respectable. — M.V.*

"She's coming *here*?" Tam said. "Vess comes downriver maybe once a decade. Last time she—"

"I remember last time." Ansel folded the letter along its crease, precisely, and put it in her coat pocket, and found that her hand wasn't quite steady, and hated it.

Below them, the river went on sliding past the pilings, brown and patient, carrying its silt and its secrets down toward the sea. It flinched for nobody. It warned only those who listened.

Ansel listened, and heard nothing yet.

But somewhere upriver, in the high water and the coming monsoon, something was already moving toward her — and Maren Vess did not travel a hundred miles to offer propositions to people she intended to leave standing.
