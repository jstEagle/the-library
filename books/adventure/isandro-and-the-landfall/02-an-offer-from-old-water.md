## 2. An Offer from Old Water

The rain came in off the water like it had a grudge, and Ansel Eriksen stood on the Magpie's aft deck and let it soak her through.

She'd been doing that lately — standing in weather she could easily have avoided. Tam called it brooding. She called it inventory. You took stock of what you had left: one boat, three crew, a boiler held together with prayer and rivets, and a debt to the Vess Line so deep that some mornings she woke up already underwater.

Forty-one thousand marks. She'd stopped rounding it down in her head years ago, because rounding was a kindness and the debt had never been kind.

The dock at Graywater Landing was half-empty this time of year. Monsoon season emptied towns like fever did — everyone who could leave, left, and everyone who couldn't watched the river rise and made peace with their gods. The Magpie stayed because the Magpie always stayed. A boat that ran the shoulder season ate well; a boat that ran the monsoon ate last.

"Captain." Tam's voice behind her, low. "She's here."

Ansel didn't turn around. She'd felt it before Tam said it — the particular silence a dock takes on when money walks onto it. Porters stopping mid-haul. The harbormaster suddenly very interested in his ledger. And under all of it, the sound Ansel knew better than her own heartbeat: the flat, expensive purr of a Vess Line launch idling against the pilings.

"How many?" she asked.

"Just her. Two guards on the boat."

Vess never traveled heavy. She didn't need to. The whole river was her escort.

Ansel wiped her hands on her coat, which accomplished nothing, and went down the gangway into the rain.

* * *

Maren Vess stood at the end of the dock like she owned it, which by most legal definitions she did. Silver-streaked hair pinned back without mercy, gray traveling coat dry as bone despite the downpour — she'd have timed her walk from the launch between gusts, because Maren Vess did not get wet unless it profited her.

"Captain Eriksen." Her voice carried the way money carries. Not loud. Just certain no one would dare talk over it.

"Maren." Ansel stopped six feet out. Close enough to be civil. Far enough to smell the contract before it was offered.

"You look well."

"I look wet."

Something flickered at the corner of Vess's mouth — not quite a smile, but the memory of one, from a time when they'd been two young mates running cargo for other people's boats and racing each other to every landing on the lower reach. That was twenty years and one drowned crew ago. Neither of them mentioned it. They never did.

"I'll come to the point," Vess said. "I need a boat upstream. Your boat."

"No."

"I haven't named the price."

"Don't need to." Ansel turned toward the gangway. "Whatever it is, the answer's the same. I don't run the upper reaches. You know why I don't run the upper reaches."

"The Narrows Passage. In season." Vess said it the way a surgeon says the name of an old scar. "One passage. Up to Kestrel Bar and back. Triple your best year's take, and—" she paused, and let the pause do its work, "—the debt is forgiven. All of it. Forty-one thousand marks, struck clean. You'd own the Magpie outright by the first of the month."

The rain filled the silence. Somewhere behind Ansel, Tam had come down the gangway and gone very still.

Triple pay. Debt forgiven. It wasn't an offer; it was a key cut to fit the exact shape of her prison. Which meant Vess had spent real money learning the shape of it, and Vess never spent real money on anything she expected to lose.

"That route killed eleven people," Ansel said quietly.

"It killed nine," Vess said. "Two survived. One of them is standing in front of me being sentimental about a river."

Ansel's hand closed around the rail of the gangway until the old iron bit her palm. Nine names. She kept them the way other captains kept charts — worn smooth from handling. Captain Odile Brannoc first, because you always started with the captain. Then Hesk, and the Toma twins, and little Ferro who was seventeen and lied about his age to get aboard, and the rest, down to Wren, the cook, whose bread Ansel still couldn't eat without tasting grief.

The Sweet Amity had gone down in the Narrows during the spring flood, eight years back, running a Vess contract. Ansel had been her mate. She'd sworn on the salvage line — on Wren's floating coat, if she was honest, though she told people it was the line — that she'd never run that water again. Not for triple pay. Not for anything.

"No," she said again, and started up the gangway.

"Your debt's been sold, Ansel."

Ansel stopped.

She didn't turn around right away. She let the words settle the way silt settles, finding the low places in her chest. When she did turn, Vess hadn't moved. The rain beaded on her shoulders and slid off like the dock itself was sheltering her.

"Sold," Ansel said.

"To Harrow & Finch. Collection house, out of the capital." Vess produced a folded paper from inside her coat — dry, of course — and held it out. Ansel didn't take it, so Vess set it on a bollard between them, weighted with a coin. "They bought the paper three weeks ago. Called due in ten days. Full amount, plus penalties. They're not a patient firm. They take boats, Ansel. Boats, houses, hands, if the collateral runs short."

"They can't—"

"They can. They do. Ask anyone downstream of the capital." Vess's eyes were steady and almost kind, which was the worst part, because Ansel knew for a fact that Maren Vess had arranged the sale herself. Had probably picked Harrow & Finch out of a dozen collection houses the way you'd pick a hook out of a tackle box. But there was nothing on paper, nothing provable, just the timing sitting there like a loaded barge.

Ten days. Ten days to find forty-one thousand marks, or watch the marshals put chains on the Magpie's wheel and lead her crew off her deck.

"And if I run your passage," Ansel said slowly, "Harrow & Finch just... forgets?"

"Harrow & Finch will be paid in full the day you make Kestrel Bar. By me. Personally." Vess finally smiled, small and precise. "You'd be free, Ansel. Boat clear, name clear. Free to go anywhere the water goes — except, I suppose, back to owing me. Though I suspect you've grown attached to it."

Ansel looked past her, at the black rising river, at the rain stitching the surface into lace. Eight years she'd kept the Magpie alive on honest freight and thin margins, running the safe water, keeping her oath like a lamp lit in a window. And Vess had walked up a dock in the rain and blown it out with a piece of paper.

There were ways out. There were always ways out — sell the boat and vanish overland, beg the council for relief, run dark up some tributary and let the debt rot behind her. She'd thought about all of them at three in the morning, more times than she'd admit. But the Magpie was the last piece of Odile Brannoc's fleet still floating, and Ansel did not abandon ships. She'd learned that lesson at the bottom of the Narrows, holding a rope with nine dead men's weight on it.

"When?" she heard herself ask.

"Three days. I want you loaded and moving before the storm line settles in." Vess straightened her collar, business concluded. "Cargo's mine. Sealed. You don't open it, you don't count it, you don't ask. Standard terms."

"Sealed cargo on a flooded river through the Narrows." Ansel laughed, and it came out wrong, too sharp. "You're asking me to run blind water with a hold full of questions."

"I'm asking you to save your boat." Vess stepped close, close enough that the rain between them seemed to stop out of respect. Her voice dropped to something almost private. "I know what you lost up there. Better than you think I do. Run this one passage, and it stops costing you."

Then she turned and walked back to her launch, unhurried, dry even in the deluge somehow, and the guards cast off, and the expensive engine purred away upstream into the gray.

* * *

Tam found her an hour later in the pilothouse, going over charts she wasn't reading.

"Well?" he said.

"She wants the upper reaches. Monsoon run. Three days out."

Tam whistled low. He was sixty, built like a mooring post, and he'd been on the Sweet Amity's sister ship long enough ago that they didn't talk about it either. "And you told her—"

"I told her no."

"But."

Ansel set the chart down. Outside, the river had climbed another hand's width since morning, brown and muscular, carrying whole trees past the landing like it was clearing its throat.

"But the debt's been sold," she said. "Called due in ten days. It's her knife, Tam. She sold it to herself and she's calling it fate."

Tam leaned against the doorframe and was quiet for a while. Rain drummed the pilothouse roof like impatient fingers.

"So we run it," he said finally. "One trip. We load whatever sealed mystery she's selling, we thread the Narrows in flood season, and we come home owning our own hull for the first time in eight years." He looked at her. "Or we refuse, and in ten days I'm looking for work on a stranger's deck, and the Magpie's flying somebody else's colors. Those the choices, Captain?"

"That's the shape of them."

He nodded slowly, the way he nodded at bad water ahead — not happy, but done arguing with it. "Then I'll tell Rook and Sefa we sail in three days." He paused at the door. "For what it's worth — Odile would've told Vess to drown."

"Odile's dead, Tam. That's rather the point."

He grunted and went below. Ansel stayed in the pilothouse until the light failed, watching the river rise, and somewhere past midnight she unrolled the old charts of the upper reaches — the ones she'd sworn she'd burned, and had only hidden — and weighted their corners with cups, and did not sleep.

Three days later, at first light, a crate came up the gangway on six porters' shoulders. It was longer than a coffin, heavier than it should have been, and when it swung past her in the gray morning air, Ansel would have sworn she heard it knock.

Twice.

Like something inside, asking to be let out.
