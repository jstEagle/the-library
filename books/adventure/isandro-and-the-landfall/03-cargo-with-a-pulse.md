## 3. Cargo with a Pulse

The crate sat in the middle of the cargo deck like a coffin that hadn't made up its mind.

Ansel had noticed it at the dock, of course. She noticed everything on her deck — it was the one habit twenty years of river had never broken. But she'd been in a hurry that morning, Vess's contract burning a hole in her pocket and the debt collector's letter burning a hole somewhere deeper, so she'd signed for *one sealed crate, no manifest inspection required* without reading the clause twice. A mistake. The river punished every mistake eventually, and it always collected.

Now they were two hours upriver, past the last pilot station, the banks narrowing into the drowned willow country, and the crate was breathing.

She'd heard it first. A soft, rhythmic sound under the drum of the engines — too regular for water, too wet for wood. She'd told herself it was the bilge. Then Tam had heard it too, and Tam heard everything, and now the whole crew was standing in a half-circle around the thing with the wariness of people who'd learned the hard way that cargo on the upper river came in two kinds: heavy and trouble. Sometimes both.

"Captain." Tam's voice was flat. He was a broad, gray-whiskered man who'd been her first mate since before the disaster, and he had exactly one setting: steady. Right now the steadiness had a crack in it. "That crate is moving."

"I see it."

"Vess's crate. Sealed. No manifest. Moving." He let each word land like a stone dropped in still water. "You want to tell me what we're carrying?"

Ansel crouched by the crate. It was long — longer than a man needed, unless the man was tall — built of good pine, strapped with iron bands, and stenciled on one end in Vess Line blue: *MACHINERY PARTS — FRAGILE — DO NOT OPEN*. The stenciling was fresh. The crate was not. Somebody had painted a new label over an old one, and the old one was still legible at the edges if you knew where to look: a Vess Line routing code for the upper reaches. The same code Ansel had last seen on a manifest eleven years ago, the night before everything went to the bottom of the river.

Her mouth went dry.

"Crowbar," she said.

"Captain—"

"Crowbar, Tam."

He fetched it himself. Nobody else would touch the thing.

The first iron band gave with a shriek. The second one stuck, and Ansel put her shoulder into it, and the lid came up all at once, and what was inside was not machinery.

The man inside was maybe thirty, thin in the way of people who'd been eating badly for months, with ink-stained fingers and the hollow-eyed look of somebody who hadn't slept in a room with a door he trusted in a very long time. His clothes were a clerk's clothes, good wool gone gray with river damp. He was curled around a leather satchel the way a drowning man curls around driftwood, and when the light hit his face he made a sound — small, cracked, half a word, maybe *no* — and tried to press himself deeper into a space that had no deeper to go.

For a long moment nobody moved. The engines thudded. The river slid by, brown and swollen, carrying whole trees past the rail.

Then Tam said, very quietly, "Put him ashore."

* * *

The argument happened in the pilothouse, because arguments on the Magpie happened in the pilothouse — that was Ansel's rule, made after the third time a deckhand dispute had nearly gone over the rail. The crew crowded the stairwell. The stranger stood on the cargo deck below, dripping river water he'd scooped from the bilge bucket over his own head when Ansel told him he stank of crate, and looking up at the pilothouse windows like a man watching a verdict.

"Put him ashore," Tam said again. "Next landing. There's a fuel dock at Harrow Bend. We put him off, we radio nothing, we're clean."

"Clean," Ansel said.

"Captain, think. Vess sealed a man in a crate and put him on *your boat*. That's not cargo. That's a message, or a murder, and either way it's not ours. We run the contract, we deliver the crate — fine, it's machinery, we didn't know. But now we know. Now we're carrying a fugitive upriver for Vess, and when Vess decides she doesn't want him found—"

"I know what it is, Tam."

"Do you? Because eleven years ago you told me you'd never again take a sealed manifest you hadn't counted. And here we are." He wasn't angry. That was the worst part. Tam never got angry; he just laid things out in rows the way he laid out rope, neat and damning. "You took the job because of the debt. I understand the debt. But this man isn't in the contract."

Ansel looked out the pilothouse window. The river had gone the color of old pewter under the building clouds, and somewhere upstream the monsoon was gathering itself the way it did every year, and she could feel it in her bad knee the way she felt weather before the barometers did. The river was going to rise. It always rose. The question was what it would carry down to her when it did.

Below, the stranger hadn't moved. He was standing very still, hands at his sides, waiting to be put off at the next dock the way cargo was put off, and there was something in the set of his shoulders that snagged on something old in Ansel — a memory of herself, younger, standing on a Vess Line dock in the rain with everything she owned in one bag and a captain's license she was no longer sure she deserved, waiting to be told where she'd be sent next. That look. She knew that look from the inside.

"Who are you?" she called down.

The man flinched, then steadied. "Isandro," he called back. His voice was rough from disuse. "Isandro Vey. I was a records clerk. Vess Line, central office, eleven years."

"Eleven years." Tam said it like a curse.

"I ran," Isandro said. "I took something of hers and I ran, and she wants me back before anyone reads it. That's the whole of it. I don't care what you do with me. But if you put me ashore at Harrow Bend, there's a launch that's been following the river since dawn, and I'll be dead by nightfall." He said it without drama, the way a man reports the weather. "So. Do what you like."

The stairwell went quiet. Ansel could feel the crew behind her, feel their fear — not of the man, but of what he represented, which was the same thing she feared, which was the same thing the river had been teaching her for eleven years: that nothing on the upper water came free, and every kindness had a current under it.

"Captain," Tam said, low, just for her. "Whatever he took, it's not worth our necks. We're six souls and an old boat with a debt on her. You put him ashore, we finish the run, we get paid, we're clear."

Ansel looked at the man on her deck. He was shivering now, the adrenaline draining out of him, and he'd wrapped his arms around that leather satchel again, and he was watching her with an expression she recognized with a jolt: not hope. He wasn't hoping. He'd done his hoping somewhere else, in some other room, and it hadn't worked, and now he was just waiting to see which way the current took him.

She'd worn that expression for a year after the disaster. She knew exactly how heavy it was.

"The launch following the river," she said. "Vess's?"

"Vess's," Isandro said.

"Then if I put you ashore, I'm not putting you ashore. I'm handing you to her." Ansel turned from the window. "And I've spent eleven years telling myself I don't work for Vess. Turns out I've been working for her since the day I signed the debt. I'm not going to be her delivery boy too."

"Captain—" Tam started.

"Log it," she said. "One passenger, recovered from sealed cargo, destination upper reaches, reason: he'll be killed otherwise. Put it in the book in your own hand, Tam, so it's your signature next to the decision too. I'm not carrying this one alone."

That landed. She saw it land — saw the crew exchange glances, saw Tam's jaw work. She hadn't meant it as a kindness or a threat. It was just the truth, and the truth on the Magpie always went in the log.

Tam took the log book. Wrote. Handed it to her without a word. His signature under hers was stiff, and he didn't look at her when he passed it back, and she knew she'd just spent something she couldn't buy back — some portion of the crew's certainty in her. But she also knew, with the same deep river-sense that told her where the sandbars had shifted overnight, that putting the man ashore would have cost more.

* * *

They gave him the corner bunk in the forward hold, the one that leaked least, and a blanket that smelled of diesel, and a bowl of the stew Sefa made, which was terrible and hot and which he ate like a man who'd forgotten food could be either. The crew kept their distance. Ryn, the youngest, watched him from the doorway with open suspicion. Old Pell didn't watch him at all, which from Pell was a kind of courtesy.

Ansel found him an hour later, sitting up in the bunk with the satchel in his lap, watching the water through the loading door.

"Rules," she said. "You stay out of the engine room, out of the pilothouse unless I say, and off the roof. You eat what we eat. You pull your weight — Sefa will find you work, don't think she won't. And you don't open that satchel on my boat. Whatever's in it, it stays shut until we make the delivery. I'm carrying you, not whatever you're carrying. Understood?"

"Yes," he said. Then, after a moment: "Thank you."

"Don't thank me. I did it because the alternative made me sick, not because it made sense." She leaned against the doorframe. "One question. In the crate — you were curled around that bag like it was your own pulse. Whatever's in there, is anyone else going to die for it?"

Isandro looked down at the satchel. His ink-stained fingers moved on the leather, once, like a man touching a scar.

"No," he said. "Everyone it could kill is already dead. That's rather the point."

The way he said it — flat, final, with something buried under it that wasn't final at all — made the hair on Ansel's arms stand up. She thought about pressing. She was a captain; pressing was half her trade. But the clouds upstream had gone the color of a bruise, and her knee was singing, and she'd learned long ago that the river always told you what it was going to do, and so did people, if you waited.

"Sleep," she said. "We make Harrow Bend by dark and the upper water by morning. After that, you're on the river's schedule, not mine."

She was halfway up the stair when his voice caught her.

"Captain Eriksen." She turned. He was standing now, and there was something in his face she hadn't seen before — not fear, not gratitude. Purpose, banked like a coal. "Your name was on some of the manifests. The old ones. I read every one of them. I just want you to know that before you read them yourself."

She didn't answer. She climbed to the pilothouse, took the wheel from Ryn, and steered into the darkening water with those words turning over in her chest like stones in a current — and far upstream, low and continuous, the monsoon had begun to roll.
