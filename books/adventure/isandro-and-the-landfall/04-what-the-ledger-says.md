## 4. What the Ledger Says

I learned to read water the way I'd learned to read ledgers: column by column, looking for the number that didn't belong. The Magpie's deck was no different. Every board creaked in a pattern, every rope had its tension, every crew member had a rhythm — and every one of those rhythms had changed since they'd pulled me out of a crate that should have held machine parts.

Nobody had thrown me overboard yet. I counted that as progress.

The cook, a wide-shouldered woman named Odile who spoke mostly in grunts, had given me a bucket of bilgewater to bail and a corner of the galley to stay out of. I bailed. I stayed out. And all the while, the ledger rode against my ribs in an oilcloth wrap I'd sewn into the lining of my coat, heavier than any crate I'd ever hidden in.

Three days upriver. Two nights of listening to the crew argue about me in voices too low to catch, but not too low to catch the shape of the argument. Tam wanted me ashore at the next landing, no discussion. The boy, Pell, wanted to know what was in the ledger, because boys always want to know. Ansel Eriksen said nothing at all, and that silence was louder than both of them.

I had come up this river to reach the council at Greywater Landing. I had not planned on owing my life to a riverboat captain with a debt to the very woman I was running from. The universe, it turns out, has a sense of humor that would embarrass a tax collector.

On the third evening, the storm still two days south of us and not one of us knowing it, Ansel came into the galley while I was pretending to study a chart I couldn't make sense of.

"Eat," she said, setting a bowl in front of me. River fish, some kind of grain, a slick of fat on top. "You look like a drowned clerk."

"I was almost a drowned clerk. Close enough."

Something twitched at the corner of her mouth. Not a smile. The ghost of one, maybe, the way a sandbar is the ghost of an island. She sat across from me with her own bowl and ate in silence, and I understood that this was the closest thing to an invitation I was going to get.

"Captain," I said. "There's something you need to see."

"I need to see a clean bilge and my debt gone. That's what I need to see."

"You need to see this more." I pulled the oilcloth packet from my coat and set it on the table between us, and the sound it made — that soft, dense slap of paper and binding — went through the little galley like a shot.

She didn't touch it. "What is it?"

"Vess's own manifests. Eleven years of them. The originals, not the copies she files with the harbormasters."

"Originals." She said the word like it was something she'd bitten into and found a stone in. "Clerks don't get originals."

"I wasn't always a clerk." I unwrapped it anyway, because my hands had decided before my courage did. "I was a records man in her Vellmark office for nine years. I filed her cargo runs, her insurance claims, her loss reports. And captain — I want you to understand that I believed her. For nine years I believed her. The river takes boats. That's what everyone says. That's what I filed, year after year. Lost to flood. Lost to snag. Lost with all hands."

Ansel had gone very still. It was the stillness of a woman standing on a deck that has just begun, very slightly, to tilt.

"Open it," she said.

I opened it.

* * *

The ledger was a fat thing, bound in grey leather, its corners soft from nine years of my thumbs. I'd started copying it two years ago, the night I found the first discrepancy — a grain shipment out of Corrow's Bend that had been written as lost, then written again, three months later, as delivered, under a different boat's name. Numbers don't lie, but they do gossip, and once you hear them whispering you can't make them stop.

I walked her through it, page by page, and the galley lamp burned down and the river ran dark past the windows and I watched a woman's whole understanding of her life come apart in the space between one page and the next.

"The Sweet Amity," I said, when we got to the third year. "Outbound from Harrow's Deep, listed as lost to a snag with cargo of salt pork and tool steel. Insurance paid in full. Vess collected on the cargo and the hull."

Ansel's spoon had stopped halfway to her mouth. It stayed there.

"The Amity didn't hit a snag." I turned the page for her, because she didn't seem able to. "She was scuttled. Deliberately. Here's the repair bill from the yard at Corrow's Bend — hull patches, ordered before the Amity ever left Harrow's Deep. Here's the second manifest, the one where the same cargo is shipped again on the Kestrel's Wake six weeks later. You don't ship the same salt pork twice, Captain. Unless the first boat never delivered it because the first boat was at the bottom of the river with her sea cocks opened."

"Who was her master?" Ansel's voice came out flat and strange, a voice I hadn't heard her use before. "The Amity. Who ran her?"

I knew the name was coming. I'd known it since the night I decided to steal the ledger, and I'd rehearsed this moment in a hundred different ways, and none of them were good enough.

"Captain Maren Oduya," I said.

The spoon came down. Not set down — came down, clattering against the bowl, and Ansel stood up so fast her chair went over backward and cracked against the bulkhead, and for one long moment I thought she was going to put her hands around my throat, and I found that I would have deserved it.

"You knew," she said. "You sat there eating my food and you knew."

"I didn't know you. I didn't know what she was to you. Captain, I—"

"Get out."

"Captain—"

"Get out of my galley."

I got out. There was nowhere to get out *to* — a riverboat in flood season is a box with pretensions — but I got as far as the deck and stood in the drizzle with my heart slamming and the ledger clutched to my chest, and through the galley window I watched her right the chair, sit down again, and pull the ledger toward her with both hands.

She read for four hours.

* * *

I didn't sleep. I stood my watch at the rail and watched the light in the galley windows and listened to the river, which had begun, somewhere in the last day, to make a different sound — a deeper, more insistent sound, like a man muttering to himself before he does something regrettable.

Near midnight, the smell reached me. Burning. I was through the galley door before I'd decided to move, and found Ansel standing over the stove where a pan of fish had gone from dinner to charcoal to something past charcoal, a black smoking ruin, and she was just standing there, staring at it, the ledger open on the table behind her with her thumb holding a place.

"The fish," I said, stupidly.

"I know it's the fish." She scraped the pan into the firebox and the flames leapt and died and the smoke curled up the flue. "I've cooked that same fish four hundred times, clerk. I could do it asleep. I've done it asleep."

She said nothing else. She didn't cry. I want to put that down because it matters — she didn't cry, didn't shout, didn't do anything the stories would have her do. She just stood in her galley with the burnt pan in her hands like she'd forgotten what hands were for, and then she washed the pan, slowly, thoroughly, and set it back on its hook, and went out on deck, and stared at the river.

I followed her out at a distance. It felt like following a lit fuse.

* * *

What do you do with a truth that size? I'd asked myself that for two years, and the honest answer was: you carry it. You sew it into your coat and you sleep on top of it and you tell yourself that carrying it is the same as doing something about it, and some nights you almost believe that, and some nights you don't.

Maren Oduya. I'd never met her. I knew her only as a line in a column, a loss written in Vess's tidy hand, and then as a name I'd found in the crew manifests — six names under hers, six men and women who went into the water at Harrow's Deep bend and never came out, written off as river-taken, insured, replaced. One of the names had been familiar even to me: Tam's older sister. I hadn't known that when I boarded this boat. I'd learned it the second night, from Pell, who talked the way rivers run.

I understood then, standing in the drizzle watching Ansel's back, that I hadn't just brought a ledger upriver. I'd brought a bomb, and I'd lit the fuse the moment I opened the cover, and there was no version of the rest of this voyage where the thing didn't go off.

The question — the only question — was what she'd do when it did.

Toward dawn the rain thickened and the wind came around out of the south, and Ansel finally turned from the rail. Her face in the lamplight was the face of a woman who had been underwater for a long time and had just decided to come up.

"Clerk," she said. My name was Isandro Vey, but that night I was clerk, and I answered to it gladly. "The Amity's wreck. Your ledger says Harrow's Deep bend. Where exactly?"

I told her what the survey notes said — the old channel, the drowned timber claim, the slough that had silted shut after the flood two years back. She listened without blinking.

"And Vess's gunboats," she said. "How many does she keep on this stretch?"

"Two that I know of. The Marrow and the Cutter's Daughter. They patrol the bends above Corrow's Bend during monsoon season." I hesitated. "Captain, why—"

"Because," Ansel Eriksen said, turning back to the black water sliding past her rail, "if the river took the Amity, the river kept her. And I'm going to go and ask her what she remembers."

Behind us, south and low, the sky had begun to flicker — heat lightning, I told myself. Just weather.

But the river's muttering had grown louder, and I had spent nine years learning what numbers sound like just before they turn against you, and that sky, that water, that woman's voice — all of it sounded exactly like that.
