## 5. Gunboats at the Bend

The bend came at midmorning, the way bad news always does — when you're not ready for it.

Ansel felt it before she saw it. The river narrowed there, pinched between a bluff of red clay on the north bank and a drowned willow stand on the south, and in monsoon season the current ran through that throat like water through a funnel. Any boat coming up from Vess's yards had to slow for it. Any boat coming down had to hold its nerve.

She was holding hers when Tam's voice came down from the texas roof, flat as slate.

"Smoke. Two points off the bow. Low."

Low smoke meant a launch. High smoke meant a freighter with nothing to hide. Ansel set her coffee mug on the wheelhouse sill and didn't look at the hatch where Isandro had been splitting kindling ten minutes ago, because looking would tell the whole river everything.

"Deck crew to stations," she said. "Casual. Nobody runs."

The Magpie came around the bend doing six knots against a four-knot current, and there they were: two enforcement launches riding the slack water under the bluff, gunmetal gray, Vess Line crests stenciled on their cabins like brands on cattle. A swivel mount on the lead boat's foredeck, covered with a tarp that fooled nobody. They'd been waiting. They knew the schedule, or close enough to it, and they'd picked the one place on forty miles of river where a sternwheeler couldn't turn and couldn't run.

Ansel's hands were steady on the wheel. Her mouth wasn't dry at all, which surprised her. Fear, she'd learned, didn't always feel like fear. Sometimes it felt like clarity — the whole world sharpening down to wheel, throttle, and the next thirty seconds.

"Tam," she said quietly. "Get our passenger below. Bilge compartment, aft of the shaft alley. Now."

Tam came down the ladder slowly. She was sixty if she was a day, built like a bollard, with a face that had watched the river take better boats than this one. She looked at Ansel, then at the launches, then back at Ansel, and Ansel watched the arithmetic happen behind her eyes and come out wrong.

"You're hiding him," Tam said. Not a question.

"I'm hiding cargo. That's what captains do."

"That's not cargo and you know it." Tam kept her voice low, which somehow made it worse. "That's a man Vess is willing to put a gunboat on the river for. And you're going to lie to those guns with my people standing on your deck."

"Our deck," Ansel said. "Bilge. Go."

For three long seconds Tam didn't move. Then she went, boots heavy on the stair, and Ansel turned back to the wheel and began the performance of her life.

* * *

She took the Magpie into the bend slow. Slower than the current liked. The wheel went heavy, the stern started to walk, and she let it walk — let the boat wallow like something wounded, let the paddlewheel slap unevenly, and above all let the stack start to cough.

That last part was Sefa's work. Her engineer had gotten the signal — two short blasts on the brass bell, their old code for *act sick* — and now the Magpie's exhaust came ragged and black, hiccuping like a dying man. Sefa could make that engine do anything. Making it look broken was harder than making it run, and she did it beautifully.

The lead launch swung out into the channel and stood across it, and a voice came over a speaking trumpet, young and full of borrowed authority.

"Sternwheeler! Heave to. Vess Line inspection."

Ansel leaned on the rail. "Inspection of what? My engine's dying, if you want to inspect that. You're welcome to it. Bring tools."

The launch closed to fifty feet. Through the morning glare Ansel counted faces: six men, one woman, all in Vess gray, and the one holding the trumpet had the soft hands of a man who'd never worked a deck in his life. A clerk with a gunboat. Vess sent clerks first. Guns second.

"Captain Eriksen." He said her name like he'd rehearsed it. "We're searching for a fugitive. A records clerk named Isandro Vey. Stolen property, stolen documents. We have reason to believe he's aboard."

"Reason from who?"

"The Line."

"Then the Line can file a writ with the river magistrate like everybody else." She spat over the rail, theatrical, precise. "You've got no jurisdiction on open water and we both know it. But since you're here — come aboard. Inspect whatever you like. You'll be inspecting her in pieces if we don't get to a yard soon, but hey. Your funeral."

It was a gamble and she knew it. Hiding was suspicion; openness was boredom. She watched the clerk weigh it, watch him glance at the bilging water slopping greasy over the main deck, at the crew lounging against the rail with the studied indifference of people who'd been stopped so many times it no longer interested them. Rilla was actually mending a net. Old Fen was asleep, or performing sleep so well it amounted to the same thing.

The clerk came aboard with two of his people. Ansel walked them through the boat herself — lazily, complaining, narrating every rusted seam and patched hose like a woman with nothing on her mind but her engine bill. They looked in the hold. They looked in the crew quarters. They looked in the galley, where the dinner pot sat scrubbed and empty, and Isandro's kindling axe lay by the woodbox, and Ansel's heart did one cold turn before she remembered she'd split that wood herself yesterday.

They did not look in the bilge. Why would they? The bilge was where boats keep their filth — shaft grease, bilgewater, the smell of everything a boat swallows and can't digest. The clerk stood at the top of the engine room stairs, wrinkled his nose at the reek rising out of the dark, and said, "What's down there?"

"Water and regret," Ansel said. "Three feet of both. Mind your boots."

He didn't go down. She'd have bet the boat on it and did.

Twenty minutes after they'd boarded, they were back on their launch, and the trumpet voice called across the water: "If we find you're carrying him, Captain, the debt gets called today. All of it."

"It's already been called," Ansel called back. "That's why I'm running freight in flood season. Tell Vess I'll pay her in full when the river lets me."

The launches fell away downstream, and the Magpie groaned on upriver through the narrowing bend, coughing black smoke until the bluff swallowed them from view. Only then did Sefa kill the act and bring the engine back to its honest rhythm, and only then did Ansel allow herself to breathe.

* * *

Isandro climbed out of the bilge looking like something the river had rejected — soaked to the waist in gray water, shaking, grease-striped to the elbow. He opened his mouth to thank her and she held up a hand.

"Don't. Not yet." She nodded toward the aft deck, where the crew had gathered without being called, the way crews do when something has happened that concerns all of them. Tam stood at their center, arms crossed, and her face was the face of a woman doing sums she didn't like.

"Six guns on that launch," Tam said. "A swivel mount under a tarp. Next time they might not send a clerk. Next time they might just put a ball through our boiler and call it an accident, and the river won't even ask questions." She turned, slow, to face Ansel fully. "So I'm going to ask mine once, Captain, and I'd take it kindly if you answered it straight. What is he to you?"

"He's the truth about how Vess builds her empire," Ansel said. "Out of drowned men's names."

"And if telling that truth costs us this boat? Costs us—" Tam's voice caught, just barely, on the next word. "Costs us anybody?"

Ansel had an answer ready. She'd been building it since the bend, plank by plank: something about odds, about the launches being gone, about smooth water ahead. It was a good answer. It was probably even true.

But she looked at Tam — at the woman who'd pulled bodies out of the water beside her eleven years ago, who'd sworn off this exact route with her, who'd earned the right to ask — and she heard herself say instead:

"I don't know."

The silence that followed was worse than shouting. Rilla looked down at her net. Fen woke up, took the measure of things, and pretended to sleep again. Isandro stood dripping at the edge of the group like a man at a funeral for someone nobody would name.

"We're crew," Tam said finally, quietly. "Not martyrs. Remember the difference, Captain, before somebody has to teach it to you twice." She turned and went forward, and the deck seemed to tilt after her.

Ansel stood at the wheel and steered, and the river ran high and brown and silent, and somewhere behind her she could hear Isandro wringing out his shirt, and somewhere ahead the clouds had begun to pile up along the western hills, green-black and stacked like cordwood.

Sefa came up the stair, wiped her hands on a rag, and squinted at the sky.

"Barometer's falling fast, Captain," she said. "Faster than I've ever seen it fall."

Ansel looked at the storm building over the hills and thought: *of course.* Of course it wouldn't wait. Nothing on this river ever waited — not debts, not gunboats, not grief.

"Secure everything loose," she said. "And somebody wake Fen. We're going to need every hand we've got."

The first drops hit the deck before she finished saying it.
