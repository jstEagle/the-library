## 6. Monsoon Rising

The storm came early. That was the first thing Isandro understood about the river: it did not keep its appointments.

He was in the galley, pretending to be useful by scrubbing a pot that didn't need scrubbing, when the light through the porthole went the color of old pewter. Not gray — pewter, heavy and metallic, as if someone had poured molten coin across the sky. The wind arrived before the rain did, shoving the Magpie sideways against her mooring lines with a groan he felt through the deck plates and into his teeth.

"Get below," Tam said, passing him at speed. The first mate moved like a man who had done this a hundred times and hated every one of them. "And if you're going to be down there anyway, you can start on the bilge. Pump handle's aft of the engine."

"I don't know how—"

"You'll learn fast or you'll swim. Either way you'll be educated." He was gone before Isandro could answer.

The rain hit ten minutes later, and it did not fall so much as arrive — a solid wall of water moving sideways, hammering the deck hard enough to make conversation impossible above it. Isandro had grown up inland, among ledgers and filing rooms and the dry paper smell of the records house. He had read about monsoons. He had catalogued flood reports for six years, neat columns of figures: *river stage 14.2 meters, damages estimated at* — numbers that had never once meant anything to him until now, until water was coming up through the seams of a boat he'd been aboard for three days and he was holding a pump handle with both hands because if he let go, the bilge would win.

The bilge smelled of diesel and rot and something older, something that might have been the river itself seeping in. The water there was already shin-deep and rising. He found the pump — a brass-and-iron thing bolted to the frame, its handle worn smooth by years of other people's hands — and threw his weight against it.

Water gushed from the discharge pipe and out over the side. It felt like a victory. It lasted about four seconds, which was exactly long enough for him to learn the second lesson of the river: the bilge does not care about your victories.

* * *

Above him, muffled through the deck, Ansel Eriksen was fighting the river itself.

Isandro heard her voice through the engine hatch, half-shouts pitched to carry over the storm, and what she was shouting made no sense to him at all. *Hard to port — no, back off, back off — there's a bar under us I can feel it.* Feel it. In this chaos, with the charts in the wheelhouse curling into pulp and the river running brown and backwards in places, carrying whole trees past them like matchsticks — she was navigating by feel.

He learned later what she'd actually said, when Tam relayed it down the ladder in clipped translation: the charts were useless. Every sounding marked on them had been taken in low water. The monsoon had added nine feet to the river's depth and moved every sandbar it owned, and the channels they were following existed now only inside Ansel Eriksen's head, built out of twenty years of reading current lines and drift patterns and the particular way foam gathered above a submerged bar.

"She says the river's lying," Tam shouted down at one point, hauling on a line. "She says it always lies in flood, and you have to listen for what it's not saying."

Isandro pumped harder. He didn't understand what that meant. But he understood the tone — the absolute refusal to treat any of this as impossible — and he decided, in the simple way a drowning man decides things, to trust it.

* * *

By mid-afternoon the situation had stopped being dramatic and become arithmetic. Water in, water out. The Magpie was an old boat with old seams, and the storm found every one of them. The pump ran continuously. When Isandro's arms gave out — and they gave out twice, three times, four — someone else took the handle while he stood there gasping, and then he took it back.

That was the thing he hadn't expected. They took turns.

Nobody asked him to. Nobody thanked him. But when his grip slipped and the handle spun free and cracked him across the knuckles, drawing blood that immediately washed pink in the bilgewater, the deckhand called Sefa appeared at the bottom of the ladder without being summoned, took the handle, and jerked her chin at the ladder. *Go breathe.* And when he came back ten minutes later, she went back to her own station without comment, and the pump never stopped.

He had spent six years in an office where cooperation meant a memo copied to the right supervisor. This was different. This was a machine made of people, and he had just been fitted into it somewhere between the pump and the ladder, and no one had asked his credentials.

His hands blistered. His shoulders seized. Somewhere around the fifth hour he stopped thinking of himself as a fugitive hiding on a boat and started thinking of himself as the man on the pump, which was, he would reflect much later, possibly the entire point.

* * *

The near-sinking happened at dusk, though dusk and noon were indistinguishable under that sky.

They came around a bend — Isandro felt the turn more than saw it, the whole hull leaning — and the river simply changed its mind. A side channel that should have been slack water was running full force, funneled off the flooded plain, and it caught the Magpie's stern and swung her broadside toward a submerged wing dam, the kind of concrete-and-timber skeleton that normally showed above the surface and today showed nothing but a boil of white water where the current broke over it.

Ansel's voice came down through the deck, very calm, very flat, the calmest thing Isandro had ever heard: *"Full ahead. Full ahead NOW, and everyone hold onto something that loves you."*

The engine screamed. The deck canted. Isandro lost the pump handle and grabbed a stanchion as the Magpie lunged — and then, instead of the grinding catastrophic impact he had braced every bone for, there was a shudder, a scrape along the keel like a giant fingernail, and they were through, sliding off the dam's shoulder and back into the channel, the current releasing them like a hand opening.

Silence, except for the rain. Then Tam's voice, hoarse: "How the hell did you know that was there?"

From the wheelhouse, equally flat: "Because it wasn't there last spring, and rivers don't build things in a season. Something put it in our way, and I'd rather guess than ask."

Isandro sat down hard on an ammunition crate that turned out to hold spare gaskets, and laughed — a short, shocked, involuntary laugh that he immediately regretted when Sefa looked over at him. But she didn't look angry. She looked like she might be doing the same thing behind her face.

* * *

Near midnight, the storm eased just enough to make exhaustion audible. The rain thinned from a wall to a curtain. The pump still ran, but slower now; the bilge was losing ground for the first time all day.

Isandro climbed topside to dump a bucket of debris — torn chart fragments, a dead lantern, three inches of river silt — and found Tam at the rail, watching the black water slide past.

The first mate didn't look at him. "You bled on my deck plates," Tam said. "You'll clean that."

"Yes."

"And your pumping's sloppy. You waste half your stroke leaning instead of driving."

"I'll fix it."

Tam was quiet for a moment. The rain ticked against the wheelhouse roof. Then, without turning, without ceremony, without anything that could be mistaken for kindness in better lighting, he gave a single nod — a small downward dip of the chin, gone almost before it happened — and said:

"Bilge watch is yours till dawn, then. Wake me at four."

It was, Isandro understood, the closest thing to a welcome the Magpie issued. He took the bucket forward, cleaned the blood off the plates, and went back down to the pump.

* * *

At four in the morning, waking Tam as ordered, he paused at the wheelhouse door. Ansel was still there, both hands on the wheel, eyes on the water, running on something that wasn't coffee and wasn't sleep. Through the salt-streaked glass, Isandro watched her read the river in the dark — head tilting slightly at some change in the current's sound, hands correcting before the boat ever felt it.

Behind her, pinned to the bulkhead, hung the ruined remains of the charts, ink bleeding into grey ghosts. Useless. She hadn't looked at them once since the storm began.

He thought about the ledger strapped against his ribs, the neat falsified columns, the drowned men reduced to entries. He thought about how this woman had spent the whole day keeping a boat alive on instinct alone, on knowledge that lived nowhere but in her body — knowledge no ledger recorded, no clerk filed, no company owned.

*Vess thinks everything can be written down,* he thought. *She built an empire on paper. And this river doesn't even know paper exists.*

Then the thought arrived fully formed, cold as bilgewater, and he couldn't unthink it:

If Vess wanted Isandro badly enough to send gunboats after a clerk — and she did, he was certain of it now, certain the way you're certain about weather — then she knew roughly where the Magpie had to pass. There was one stretch ahead, the narrows at Crook's Bend, where no boat could leave the channel. One road, one river, one place where a launch with an engine and armed men wouldn't need charts or instinct or anything at all.

He woke Tam, said "four o'clock" as instructed, and stood a moment longer in the rain, listening to the river run dark and fast toward the bend.
