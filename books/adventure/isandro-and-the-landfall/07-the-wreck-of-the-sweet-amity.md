## 7. The Wreck of the Sweet Amity

The river took the charts out of my hands at dusk and never gave them back.

We'd been running blind for six hours, the Magpie wallowing through water that had swallowed every landmark I knew. The monsoon didn't fall so much as arrive — a gray wall that walked up the valley and erased it. Rain came sideways, hard enough to sting, and the current beneath us ran like something with an opinion. Twice I'd felt the hull shudder over a sandbar that should have been dry ground in any season but this.

"Slough on the left," Tam shouted from the wheelhouse door, rain streaming off his jaw. "Maybe. Could be a field."

I looked. A gap in the drowned treeline, black water sliding between trunks with a strange, glassy calm, like the storm hadn't found it yet. My hands knew before my head did. That stillness meant dead water — an old channel cut off from the main current, going nowhere.

"Nowhere is where we need to be," I said, and put her over.

The Magpie slid off the main channel into the slough, and the noise of the storm dropped by half, like someone closing a door. We anchored in four fathoms of brown quiet, trees standing around us in rows, their lower branches trailing in the flood. The crew moved without being told — lines doubled, pumps checked, Isandro already hauling the bail bucket with the grim rhythm of a man who'd learned the work fast because he had to.

Nobody said what we were all thinking: we were lost. Properly, honestly lost, in a flooded country where the river had become a hundred rivers and none of them were on the chart.

"We'll hold here till first light," I told them. "Hot food. Dry watches. Nobody's fixing anything in that."

Tam lingered while the others went below. He looked older in the lamplight, the gray in his beard suddenly visible. "Ansel. That slough mouth. You took it at speed without a sounding."

"I read the water."

"You read it like you used to. Before." He didn't finish the sentence. He didn't have to. Before the Sweet Amity. Before the wreck that ended my career as a pilot on the upper reaches and started my slow drowning downriver, one debt payment at a time.

"Get some sleep, Tam," I said.

He went. The lamp hissed. The rain kept up its patient work on the roof, and I stood at the wheelhouse window watching the dead water, and I did not sleep.

* * *

It was near midnight when I saw the shape.

At first I took it for a fallen tree — the slough was full of them, snags lying just under the surface, black limbs reaching up like drowned men's arms. But this shape was too regular. Too long. And there was a curve to it, a sheer line rising toward a broken sternpost, that my eyes recognized about three seconds before my stomach did.

I got the skiff down alone. I don't remember deciding to. My body simply went — lantern, boathook, line — the way it goes when you've been dreaming of a thing so long that waking life can't argue with the dream.

The water in the slough was shallow over the wreck, shin-deep over her main deck, and I waded it in my boots, each step sending rings across the surface that the lantern light turned to gold. The hull rose around me, green-black with weed, its planking soft as old cheese where my hand touched it. Letters had once been painted across the stern in gold leaf. Most of them had gone, eaten by ten years of water. But three remained, deep enough in the grain to survive:

**A M I T**

I stood in the flooded deck of the Sweet Amity and could not make my legs move.

Ten years. Ten years since Vess's office had sent me the letter — *lost with all hands in the spring rise, presumed snagged near Miller's Bend* — and paid out the insurance with a efficiency that should have told me everything. Ten years since I stood at the rail of another boat and swore I'd never run the upper river again, because the upper river had taken Captain Ondine Marsh and eleven good crew and left nothing to bury. Ten years of believing the river did it. Believing the river was just cruel the way rivers are cruel, indifferent, no malice in it, only current and chance.

And here she was. Not at Miller's Bend. Not snagged in the main channel where any honest wreck would lie. Here, forty miles off the marked route, tucked into a dead slough behind a screen of trees, hidden the way you hide a thing you don't want found.

Exactly where the ledger said she'd be.

Isandro's voice came out of the dark behind me, low. "You saw it from the wheelhouse too."

I hadn't heard him come down. He stood hip-deep in the shallows beside the skiff, soaked, holding a second lantern, and he didn't step closer. He let me have the space, which was more courtesy than I deserved after a week of treating him like cargo.

"The ledger says they towed her here," he said quietly. "After. Says they scuttled her with the hatches dogged. Says—" He stopped.

"Say it." My voice came out flat and strange, someone else's voice.

"Says the crew was aboard when they did it."

The lantern flame bent in a breath of wind moving through the trees. Somewhere out in the flooded forest, a heron cried once, a raw sound, and went silent.

I'd read that line in the ledger two nights ago, by lamplight, with my dinner burning on the stove because I'd forgotten it existed. I'd read it and felt nothing, which was itself a kind of horror — grief so old and so large that it had gone numb, like a limb slept on. But numbness, it turns out, is not the same as absence. It was all still in there. It had just been waiting for proof.

My boot struck something on the deck. I looked down.

A hatch cover, dogged shut from the outside. The iron dogs were rusted open now — someone had done that later, or the flood had worked them loose — but the marks were clear, the bolt-throw scored into the wood. Dogged from outside. You dog a hatch from outside when you want it to stay shut. You dog a hatch from outside when there are people below and you do not intend to let them out.

I knelt in the flooded deck of the boat Ondine Marsh had loved better than her own life, and I touched those scores in the wood, and ten years came up out of me all at once.

I don't know how long I stayed like that. Isandro held the lantern and said nothing, and that was the right thing, the only thing. When I finally spoke, my voice was wrecked but steady, the way a house is steady after the earthquake has passed through it.

"She fought them." I wasn't sure how I knew. I knew. "Ondine fought them. She'd have known what a tow line felt like. She'd have known the moment they turned her off the channel." I looked up at him, and the lantern light made his face young and frightened and certain. "They couldn't take her boat with her willing. So they took both."

"Yes," Isandro said. "That's what the ledger shows. Vess's father built the company on it. Insurance fraud, then smuggling cover, then — when crews got curious, when clerks asked questions — this." He gestured at the drowned deck, the dogged hatches, the whole green silence of the slough. "This is what questions cost."

"And the manifests. The 'lost cargo' runs."

"Cover. Every loss was insured. Every insurance payout went into the fleet. Every crew that drowned was written off as river risk." He hesitated. "Your name isn't in it, Ansel. I checked. You were never part of it. They used your grief. The letter, the payout — it was all designed to close the question before anyone asked it."

"They used me," I said. "They've been using me for ten years. Every payment I made on that debt, every run I swore off and then unswore — I've been rowing their boat the whole time and calling it mine."

The rain had softened while we talked, settling into a long steady whisper on the water. Out in the main channel, the flood roared on, indifferent as ever. The river hadn't killed them. I'd spent ten years hating a river for a murder men committed in an office, with paper.

I stood up. My knees cracked. Something had shifted in me, some load-bearing wall coming down and something else going up in its place, and I felt it in my balance, in the way the wet air sat on my shoulders. Lighter. Terribly lighter.

"Say it aloud," Isandro said softly. "Whatever it is. Say it so it's real."

I looked at the letters on the stern. AMIT. Half a name, like half a life.

"The Sweet Amity was murdered," I said. "Ondine Marsh was murdered. My crew was murdered. By Vess. For money." Each word landed like a hammer on cold iron, ringing. "And I've spent ten years paying tribute to the people who did it."

The words hung in the dead air of the slough, and nothing took them back, and I found I didn't want them back.

* * *

Tam was waiting at the gangway when we came aboard, arms crossed, having clearly watched the whole thing from the wheelhouse. Behind him, the off-watch crew had gathered in the doorway of the deckhouse — Rell, the twins, old Sefa with her gray braid dripping. They'd all seen the wreck. They'd all grown up on stories of the Amity; half of them had kin who'd crewed her.

"Ansel," Tam said carefully. "What is that boat doing here?"

I climbed the last step and stood among them, soaked to the skin, wrung out, and clearer than I had been in ten years.

"That boat is why we're all paying Vess's freight," I said. "That boat is the debt. All of ours."

Sefa's hand went to her mouth. Rell swore, low and vicious. The twins looked at each other.

"The ledger proves it," I went on. "Manifests, payouts, names. Eleven names, and then more before them, and more after. Isandro carried it out of Vess's own records room. It's real. I've seen the wreck with my own eyes and it matches page for page."

"So what do we do?" Rell asked. "Finish the run? Take his pay?" He jerked his chin at Isandro. "Turn him over and wash our hands?"

I thought about the deadline. Four days now. The debt called due, the Magpie forfeit, everything I'd ground myself down to keep. I thought about how badly I wanted to say yes — say yes, finish the job, save the boat, keep the small safe misery I knew how to live in.

Then I looked back at the slough, at the black water standing over the deck where good people had drowned in the dark, dogged in by other men's hands.

"No," I said. "We turn about at first light. We run the ledger downriver to the council at Harrow Bend before Vess can bury it the way she buried them." I met Tam's eyes. "I know what it costs. I know what we're walking away from. But I am done paying her."

Tam held my gaze for a long moment, and I watched him weigh it — the pay, the debt, the gunboats somewhere behind us in the rain, the crew, the odds. He was my first mate and my oldest friend and he had earned the right to tell me I was wrong.

Instead he said, "Vess will come after us. Gunboats, maybe worse. You know that."

"I'm counting on it," I said. "Because a chase runs downstream, and downstream is where the council sits. Let her come."

Below us, the flood current tugged at the anchor chain, impatient, wanting to carry us somewhere. Above us the rain thinned, and through a tear in the clouds the first stars in three days showed themselves, cold and far and indifferent, and for the first time in ten years I looked up at them without asking anything of the river at all.

"First light," I said again. "Get what rest you can."

And in the dead water behind us, the Sweet Amity lay quiet in her hiding place at last — not hidden anymore, but witnessed — while the Magpie swung slowly on her chain, pointed downriver, toward home.
