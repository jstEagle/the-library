## 8. Ten Days, One River

Tam Okafor had been counting days the way other men counted money. Six left. Six days until Vess's debt came due, six days until the Magpie could be seized at any landing she touched, and four days — if the river behaved, which it hadn't since the storm broke over them like a dropped ceiling — until they reached the upper trading post where the crate was meant to be delivered.

Except the crate had a pulse now. The crate had a name and a clerk's soft hands and a ledger that Tam hadn't been able to stop thinking about for three days.

He found Ansel at the wheel after midnight, running the boat by lantern-light through rain that came down in ropes. She stood with her hip against the spokes, one hand resting on the brass like it was a sleeping animal she didn't want to startle. The river was high and brown and moving faster than any chart accounted for, and she read it anyway — the way the current flattened over a drowned fence line, the way driftwood bunched on the upstream side of something that wasn't there anymore.

"You should sleep," he said.

"So should you."

"I did. Two hours." He climbed to the wheelhouse and shut the door against the drumming rain. "I've been doing sums."

"Tam."

"Hear me out. Triple pay. Debt forgiven. We deliver the man and his book to the post, we take Vess's money, and we walk away clean. That's the deal. That's the deal you signed."

Ansel didn't look at him. "I know what I signed."

"Do you?" He kept his voice level, the way you talked a skittish deckhand back from the rail. "Because from where I stand, you've got a fugitive in my engine room learning the pump handles, and a ledger under your bunk that says our old captain's wreck wasn't an act of the river. And every hour we keep both of those things aboard, we're not delivering cargo. We're carrying evidence. There's a difference, and the difference is gunboats."

"They went back downstream after the bend," she said. "We lost them in the flood."

"We *dodged* them in the flood. That's not the same thing and you know it." He moved closer, close enough to see the rain-ghosts sliding down the glass behind her. "Ansel. Finish the run. Collect the pay. Forget the ledger. That's not cowardice. That's arithmetic. You've got four people on this boat who took this job because you told them it was a delivery."

She was quiet long enough that the only sound was the rain and the far-off groan of the paddlewheel laboring against the current. When she finally spoke, her voice was low and even, the voice she used when the river was lying to her and she knew it.

"I burned dinner the night Isandro showed me the ledger. Did Tam notice? Yes. Did the crew notice? Yes." She turned her head, finally, and looked at him. "You want me to forget it. I tried. I sat up all night trying. And then yesterday we passed the mouth of Miller's Reach, and I thought about the Sweet Amity going down somewhere upriver with nine people on it, and I thought: somebody wrote that down as freight. Somebody signed their names into a column marked *losses, acceptable*."

"That's Vess's sin. Not ours."

"It's paid for with our boat, Tam. With our hands. Every dollar we owe her, every run we've made for her, we've been working for the woman who drowned them." Her jaw tightened. "I can't collect her pay. Not now. Maybe not ever."

Tam exhaled slowly. He'd known, in some cold compartment of himself, that this was coming. He'd watched her change since the slough — watched her go quiet in a way that wasn't grief anymore but something harder, something with a direction to it. Grief just sits in you. This moved.

"So what are you proposing?"

"The council. At Harrow's Landing." She said it fast now, like a pilot calling out water ahead. "The river towns' council sits in nine days — no, eight. They've got marshals, they've got jurisdiction over the shipping lanes, and half of them have lost boats to Vess's line and never known why. Isandro's ledger names manifests, dates, insurance payouts. It's not a story, Tam, it's paper. Paper is the one thing Vess can't outrun."

"Eight days. Downstream, in flood season, with her gunboats between us and Harrow's Landing."

"Yes."

"And the crew?" He spread his hands. "You're going to ask four people to give up triple pay and walk into a fight with the woman who owns our mortgage. Some of them have families. Sefa's got a daughter in Corrin's Bend."

"I know." For the first time she looked tired — truly tired, the weariness underneath all the competence. "That's why I'm telling you first. You're my mate. If you say put it to a vote, I'll put it to a vote. If you say turn the boat around and finish the run yourself, I'll... " She stopped. Started again. "No. I won't. But I'll put you ashore with your share and no hard feelings, and I'll mean it."

"That's generous," he said dryly. "You'll maroon me with my own conscience."

"With your pay, too. Which is more than Vess would do."

He laughed despite himself, a short bitter sound, and then leaned on the wheel housing beside her and stared out at the black water. The truth was he liked Isandro. The truth was the ledger had gotten into him too, the way river water gets into a bilge — slow, seeping, everywhere before you notice. The truth was he'd done his own sums three different ways tonight and every way ended with Vess holding the deed to the Magpie whether or not they delivered the clerk, because a woman who drowned crews for margin doesn't forgive debts, she *moves* them.

But the other truth was Sefa's daughter, and Rook's bad knee, and young Pell who'd signed on for one season and a story to tell.

"Put it to the vote," he said at last. "All hands, honest question, nobody pressured. And whatever they say — you honor it. Even if it splits us."

"Even if it splits us," she agreed.

* * *

They called the crew to the saloon at dawn, rain still hammering the deck above, the lamp swinging on its gimbals. Ansel laid it out plain: the ledger, the deadline, the choice. Finish the run and take Vess's money, or turn south for Harrow's Landing and hand the evidence to the council, knowing Vess's gunboats patrolled the channel and knowing the pay would die with the contract.

Isandro stood at the back with his ledger hugged to his chest like a child, saying nothing, letting them look at him. Tam watched each face the way he'd watch water ahead of a sandbar.

Sefa asked about his daughter. Ansel said honestly: turning back meant longer exposure, more risk, and no pay either way unless the council granted salvage claims, which might take months. Sefa nodded slowly and said nothing, which from Sefa was a whole speech.

Rook asked the real question: "If we finish the run, does Vess actually let us go? Or do we just become the next column in the book?"

Nobody answered that. Nobody could.

Then they voted, hands raised in lamplight. Sefa for finishing the run. Rook for the council. Pell, pale, torn, looking at Sefa — for finishing the run. And Tam, last, feeling Ansel's eyes on him without her turning her head.

He raised his hand for the council. Because of the sums. Because a woman who writes *losses, acceptable* doesn't release debts, she collects them, and the only arithmetic that saved the Magpie was the kind that put Vess's own paper in front of marshals.

Three for going on. Two for turning. And the boat itself couldn't be split down the middle.

They tied up at Corrin's Bend that afternoon, a mudbank landing with a roofed shed and a ferry rope sagging between poles. Sefa shook Ansel's hand at the gangway, long and hard, and said, "You were good to sail under, Captain. Whatever happens." Pell just mumbled and wouldn't meet anyone's eyes, and Tam pressed their final shares into their fists and told them the truth: "This isn't betrayal. It's just two rivers."

He stood on the deck and watched them walk up the muddy slope toward town, two figures getting smaller in the rain, and felt the cost of it settle into the hull like ballast. Three left. A captain, a mate, and a clerk with a book full of drowned men.

Ansel came to stand beside him at the rail. Below, Isandro was already coiling lines, unasked, learning the work.

"Three of us," Tam said. "Against her fleet."

"Three of us," Ansel said, "with the one thing she can't afford to let reach Harrow's Landing. Which means she knows exactly where we are and what we're worth." She squinted downstream, where the flooded channel narrowed toward the confluence, gray and churning under the rain. "And Tam — look there. Tell me what you see."

He followed her gaze. Far off, where the river bent toward the meeting of the waters, a shape was nosing out from behind a woodyard — low, dark, running without lights.

A gunboat. Coming up fast.
