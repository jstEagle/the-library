## 9. Landfall

The Magpie came around the last bend with her port wheel shuddering and her heart already broken, and Ansel knew before she saw them that Vess would be waiting.

She'd known since the slough. Since the Sweet Amity's hull had risen out of the black water like a confession. A woman didn't learn a thing like that and stay quiet, and Vess Alaric had never once in twenty years underestimated Ansel Eriksen. That was the trouble with old rivals. They read you like charts.

"There." Tam's voice from the pilothouse door, flat as slate.

Two gunboats sat athwart the confluence where the Sorrel poured itself into the Vane, their searchlights cutting white lanes through the rain. Not patrol boats — enforcement launches, the kind Vess kept for men who asked questions about manifests. Deck guns under tarps they weren't bothering to hide. Behind them, far downstream, more lights: the rest of the fleet coming up to watch the channel close.

Ansel stood at the wheel with the rain hammering the glass and did the arithmetic she'd been doing since midnight. The Magpie was crippled — her stern gland leaking worse by the hour, her paddles pulling uneven, three feet of water in her bilge that the pumps were losing to. She could not outrun anything. She could not fight anything. She carried six souls and one ledger that weighed less than a hymnal and meant more than every boat Vess had ever floated.

"Options," Tam said. It wasn't a question. It was an order dressed as one.

"Surrender Isandro, they let us drift home, debt dies, we keep our licenses." Ansel turned the wheel a spoke. "Or."

"Or."

"Or I put my boat on the bar."

Tam looked at her. Rain ran off his jaw. Twenty years he'd stood beside her, and she watched him arrive at it before she said it — watched him understand what *my boat* cost, what the Magpie was, what it meant to drive her aground on purpose in flood season with her owner standing at the wheel.

"You'll lose her," he said. "They'll scrap her for the bell."

"I know."

"They'll take everything. You'll walk away with wet boots and a ledger nobody may even read."

"I know that too."

He was quiet a long moment. The searchlights swept toward them, touched the pilothouse glass, slid away. Down in the engine room she could hear Joss feeding the furnace like it was a funeral pyre, because Joss always knew.

"And the other half of me," Tam said slowly, "wants to know why you're asking permission when you stopped needing it somewhere back at the bend."

"I'm not asking." Ansel set her hand flat on the wheel, on the wood worn smooth by her own two palms across ten thousand miles of river. "I'm telling you so you can be ready to jump."

* * *

The gunboats came up on either quarter like wolves flanking a limping elk. A voice through a speaking trumpet, distorted by rain: *Magpie, heave to. Cut your engine. This is Vess Line enforcement.*

Ansel cut nothing. She gave the Magpie full steam instead, and felt the old girl answer the way she always had — stubborn, groaning, loyal past all sense — and drove her straight down the throat of the confluence channel, the only deep water left between Vess's fleet and the upper river towns.

Isandro appeared beside her, soaked, clutching the ledger inside his coat. His face was gray. "They'll board us."

"No," Ansel said. "They'll watch us do something stupid, and they won't believe it until it's done. Nobody scuttles their own boat. Vess has spent twenty years betting that everyone can be bought or drowned. She's never once priced in somebody giving it up free."

"What are you doing?"

"Reading the river."

Because that was the whole of it, and always had been. Every captain on this water knew the confluence bar — the sandbar that shifted every flood, that no chart alive had mapped correctly since spring. Vess's pilots were running last month's soundings. Her hired guns knew gunboats, not water. But Ansel had been reading this river since she was nine years old, reading it the way other people read faces, and right now, in this light, at this stage of the flood, she could see the bar exactly where it lay — a long pale seam of troubled water just off the north point, shallow enough to ground a steamer, deep enough behind it to trap anything that tried to follow without local eyes.

She put the Magpie's bow on that seam and opened the throttle all the way.

"Everyone aft!" she shouted down the tube. "Joss — bank the fires and get topside! Tam — clear the crew off the bow!"

The grounding, when it came, was almost gentle. A long shudder, a grinding kiss of sand against hull, and then the Magpie swung broadside across the channel's throat and settled, listing hard to starboard, her hull laid across the deep channel like a body across a doorway.

Ansel stood at the wheel a moment longer than she needed to. Then she smashed the sea valves open with the fire axe — both of them, and the inspection ports besides — and felt the river come up into her ship like it had been waiting years for the invitation.

* * *

They went over the side into waist-deep water, six of them, the ledger wrapped in oilcloth and strapped to Isandro's chest. The gunboats milled beyond the bar, horns sounding, pilots arguing — Ansel could see the lights swinging, could hear the lead lines ringing off the sand, could picture the captains reading their useless charts and refusing to cross a bar they couldn't fathom with a grounded wreck lying across the only safe lane. Vess's whole fleet, stranded behind a dead steamship and a sandbar only one living soul could have found blind.

One living soul who was now wading through flooded bottomland with five companions and a book of drowned men's names.

The overland run was three miles of hell. Floodwater to the chest in the low places, cane brakes tearing at their arms, lightning showing them the way in stroboscopic flashes. Tam carried Joss when the current took him near the old fence line. Isandro fell twice and came up both times with the ledger held above the water, and the second time Ansel hauled him up by the collar and found him laughing — a cracked, disbelieving laugh — and realized she was laughing too, there in the flood, with her boat dying behind her.

"She's going down," Isandro shouted over the rain. "Your ship—"

"I know what she's doing." Ansel pulled him forward. "She's holding the door."

They came up out of the water onto the levee road below Harrow's Landing as the sky went the color of pewter, and ahead of them, on the rise, the council hall showed its lit windows through the rain. Marshals' lanterns at the door. The river towns' council, in emergency session over the flood, all seven towns represented, all seven sets of ears in one building for the first time in a year.

Vess had spent twenty years making sure the truth stayed scattered — scattered towns, scattered witnesses, scattered wrecks. Tonight the flood had gathered everything in one room.

Ansel put her shoulder to the hall doors and walked into the light, dripping, bleeding at one eyebrow, her first mate and her engineer and four ragged crewmen behind her, and a clerk with a ledger pressed to his ribs like a second heartbeat.

"My name is Ansel Eriksen," she said, to the seven tables, to the marshals, to the sudden hush. "Late master of the steamboat Magpie, which is at this moment blocking the Vess fleet's escape channel at the confluence, and sinking as we speak. And this man is going to read you the names of every crew Vess Alaric ever drowned for profit. Starting with the Sweet Amity."

She put the ledger in the chairman's hands.

Behind her, through the doors she'd left open, she could hear the rain — and beneath it, faint and getting fainter, the deep-water whistle of a gunboat turning helplessly in circles behind a sandbar, behind a scuttled boat, behind a captain who owed Vess Alaric nothing at all anymore.

The chairman opened the ledger. Read one line. Looked up.

"Marshals," he said quietly, "launch a boat. Tonight."
