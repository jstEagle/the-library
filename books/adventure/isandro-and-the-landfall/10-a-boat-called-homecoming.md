## 10. A Boat Called Homecoming

The marshals came for Vess on a Tuesday, in the gray hour after dawn, when the river towns were still shaking off their fog.

I watched it from the council hall steps, a cup of terrible coffee going cold in my hands. Ansel stood beside me, arms crossed, saying nothing. Below us in the square, four marshals in oilskins walked Odile Vess out of her own flagship's cabin like they were escorting a difficult passenger to the gangway. She didn't struggle. That was the thing about Vess — even ruined, even cuffed, she moved like a woman who still owned the dock under her feet.

She stopped at the bottom of the steps and looked up. Found Ansel first, then me. Her eyes did that ledger-thing they always did — weighing, totaling, filing us away as line items.

"Ten days," she said to Ansel. "You had ten days left on that debt. You could have paid it clean."

"I could have," Ansel agreed. Her voice was flat as slack water. "Then I'd have owed you something worse."

Vess looked at her a long moment. Whatever she found there, she didn't argue with it. The marshals walked her on toward the magistrate's barge, and the crowd of river folk parted around her the way water parts around a hull — without hatred, mostly, just the cold geometry of things no longer wanted in the channel.

The charges took three days to read aloud. Falsified manifests. Insurance fraud dressed up as maritime disaster. Nine boats scuttled over eleven years, forty-one souls drowned and written off as cargo loss, their names struck from the rolls so their families couldn't even claim the bodies' wages. I sat through every session with the ledger open on my knees, answering questions until my voice went hoarse. My old desk at the Vess Line records office had been a machine for making dead men disappear. Now I made them reappear, one by one, name by name, while their widows and sons wept in the gallery.

Ansel attended only once. She sat in the back row through the reading of the *Sweet Amity*'s crew list — Captain Maren Oduya, first. When the marshal read Maren's name, Ansel stood up, said "That's true," loud enough for the whole hall, and walked out. It wasn't testimony. It was something older than testimony. I understood it was the only kind of witness she'd been waiting years to give.

* * *

The debt died with the contracts. That's what the council's clerk told us, stamping the dissolution order — the Vess Line's paper obligations voided along with its charter, its assets seized, its ledgers (the honest ones, the ones nobody knew existed) turned over to the towns. Ansel Eriksen woke up on a Thursday owing nothing to anyone for the first time in six years.

She celebrated by fixing the bilge pump on a boat that wasn't hers anymore.

The *Magpie* sat grounded across the channel mouth where Ansel had put her, half-sunk in the shallows, her back broken by the grounding and the flood both. Salvage crews picked at her for weeks. Ansel went down twice to look at her and came back both times without saying much. The third time she didn't go at all. Tam went instead, and came back with the ship's bell wrapped in canvas, and set it on the table in front of Ansel without a word.

Ansel held it a long time. Then she laughed — a short, cracked sound, the first real laugh I'd heard out of her since the storm.

"She'd have hated this," Ansel said, turning the bell over. "Dying as an obstruction. Blocking traffic." She set it down gently, like laying a child in a crib. "Best work she ever did."

* * *

It was Tam's idea, the pooled pay. Of course it was. Tam had a genius for making generosity sound like arithmetic.

"Triple rate," Tam said, counting on thick fingers around the table at the Saltgrass Tavern. "Vess contracted triple for the run. Council honored the contract when they seized the accounts. We got paid for the worst trip of our lives, and most of us are still breathing, which means we're ahead." A pause. "There's a steamer laid up at Corben's yard. Old *Kestrel*. Rotted rails, tired engine, but sound bones. Corben wants two hundred and forty."

Silence around the table. Six of us remained of the crew that had walked away at the landing — half had taken their pay and gone home to wives, mothers, dry land, sensible lives. Nobody blamed them. But the ones who stayed looked at each other the way people do when they already know the answer and are checking to see if everyone else knows it too.

Ansel stared into her glass. "I'm done running boats," she said.

Nobody argued. Everybody waited. There's a difference, and river people know it.

"The debt's paid," she went on, quieter. "The reason I stayed on the water is gone. I could walk away now. Get a house. Sleep through the night." She turned the glass a quarter turn. "Maren used to say a captain is just a person the river trusts. And I keep thinking — the river trusted me to bring Isandro's ledger down. Maybe it's not done trusting me."

Tam grinned like a knife coming out of a sheath. "Two hundred and forty, Captain."

"I didn't say—"

"You named a price with your face. Two hundred and forty. We'll cover it by Friday."

* * *

I want to record, for whoever reads this someday, exactly how the offer came. Because it mattered, and because I want to remember that I was given a choice.

A council marshal named Brannoc found me at Corben's yard, where I was sanding rust off the *Kestrel*'s railings and pretending I knew anything about boats. He was a decent man, Brannoc — he'd wept openly during the reading of the *Amity* list.

"The council voted," he said. "Full pardon for the theft of the vessel, the false manifest, all of it. Self-preservation, aiding prosecution, the whole language." He handed me a folded paper. "And there's more. They want a records office for the river towns. Independent. Someone who knows how the Vess Line cooked its books, so it never happens again. A desk. A salary. A title."

I took the paper. I didn't open it.

For eight years I had wanted exactly this: legitimacy, safety, a door that locked, a life where no one fired at the boat I slept on. I had crawled inside a crate to get away from Vess, and here was the council handing me everything I'd fled toward, stamped and signed.

I thought about the desk. I thought about dry socks, and windows, and sleeping without listening for engines in the dark.

Then I looked down the yard at the *Kestrel*, where Ansel stood at her wheel — a wheel not yet attached to anything, bolted to nothing, just held up against the sky while Tam shouted measurements at her — and I thought about what a desk would cost. Not money. The other cost. The slow one. The one where you stop hearing the river every morning, and one day you realize you've become a man who files other people's rivers instead of living his own.

I gave Brannoc the pardon paper back, folded smaller than he'd given it to me.

"Keep the desk," I said. "But I'll consult. Whenever the towns need the old Vess records read, I'll come ashore and read them. Free. Just—" I nodded at the *Kestrel*. "Don't make me stay."

Brannoc studied me the way marshals study things, looking for the lie. He didn't find one.

"The river's full of fools," he said finally, and shook my hand. "Good luck to you."

* * *

We launched on the last clear day of the flood season, with the water still high and brown and generous, carrying boats like it forgave them everything.

The *Kestrel* wasn't pretty. Her rails were new wood patched into old, her paint was three colors fighting for dominance, and her engine had a knock Tam swore was "character." But when Ansel took her out of Corben's yard and swung her nose into the current, she answered the helm the way good boats do — a half-beat late, then true.

The whole town came down to watch, which embarrassed everyone except Tam, who waved like a departing admiral.

We ran her upriver ten miles and back, nothing aboard but the crew and the ship's bell from the *Magpie*, mounted fresh at the wheelhouse door. At the bend where the channel narrowed — the same bend where the gunboats had cornered us, though the water had forgotten it — Ansel slowed the engine and let us drift.

"That's the tradition, isn't it?" she said. "Naming a boat. Somebody's supposed to say words."

"Usually champagne," offered Pell, our engineer. "Usually before launch."

"We're not usually people." Ansel looked around at us — Tam, Pell, young Sefi at the deck line, me leaning on the rail. Her eyes stopped on me last and stayed. "This boat needs a name that tells the truth about her. She's rotted and patched and held together by people who shouldn't have survived last season. And she's going home every night, wherever she ties up, because home isn't a place any of us has. It's this. Whatever this is."

She put her hand flat on the wheel.

"*Homecoming*," she said. "That's her name."

Nobody spoke for a second. Then Tam cleared his throat violently, claimed something in his eye, and went to check a rope that needed no checking. Sefi whooped. Pell rang the *Magpie*'s bell once, a bright clean note that rolled across the flooded fields and came back to us off the far bluffs like the river itself repeating it.

*Homecoming.* I wrote it that night in the logbook, in my careful clerk's hand, the first entry of a new ledger: *Launched, blessed, named. Crew of five. Debt: none. Course: upriver.*

Because that was the hook of it, the thing Ansel said when I asked her, at the rail in the dark, where we'd run first.

"Upriver," she said. "All the way. Past the upper reaches, past where the charts give out." She smiled, and it was the old weary competence and something new underneath it, something unchained. "There are towns up there that never heard the names read out. Towns Vess drowned quiet. Somebody should carry them the truth."

"And after that?"

"After that," Ansel said, "the river decides."

The *Homecoming* leaned into the current, and the current, as if it had been asked, carried us up.
