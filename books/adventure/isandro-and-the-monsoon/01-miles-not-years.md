## 1. Miles, Not Years

The rope was lying to her.

Hazel Brightwater crouched over the coiled line in the dust behind Kestrel's Outfitters and ran her thumb along its sheath for the third time, feeling for the soft spot she knew was there the way you know a bruise is there before the color shows. Sixty meters of 9.2-millimeter half-rope, bought two seasons ago in a border town whose name she'd already let go of, and it had maybe forty hard pitches left in it before the core started whispering through the sheath like a bad secret.

She'd counted. She always counted.

"Third one from the left," she said, not looking up. "You put it back on the rack."

Dev froze halfway to the drying line with a harness slung over his shoulder like a dead snake. Twenty-three years old, all elbows and enthusiasm, still moved through the world like he expected someone to hand him a prize at the end of it. "It passed the pinch test."

"The pinch test is what you do when you don't have time to do it right." Hazel took the rope from him, uncoiled it across her forearms in smooth arm-spans — sixty meters, twenty-six spans, her body had become a measuring instrument whether she liked it or not — and found the soft spot by feel alone, three meters from the marker tape. She pressed her thumbnail into it. The sheath dimpled and stayed dimpled.

"Core shot," she said. "Retired."

"That's a whole rope, Hazel."

"That's a whole life, if you're stupid about it." She said it without heat, the way she said most things, and set the rope aside in the pile that would be cut into prusiks and haul loops and bootlaces, because even a dead rope earned its keep around here. Nothing wasted. Nothing wasted, nothing noticed, nobody asking questions about where the small crew with the worn gear came from or where they were going next.

Dev watched her a moment longer, then went back to the drying line. Good kid. Asked fewer questions than he wanted to, which was the first skill she'd actually taught him, though she'd never called it a lesson.

The yard behind Kestrel's was a rectangle of packed dirt and gravel the color of old bone, walled in by adobe on two sides and by stacked freight pallets on the third, and from the fourth side you could see the foothills — brown, then blue, then the white smear of high country that people in town pointed at when they wanted to sound like they knew something about mountains. Nobody in this town knew anything about mountains. That was why the crew wintered here. A place too dull to attract attention was worth more to Hazel Brightwater than a place with good climbing, and she'd learned that lesson the expensive way, in two countries, on two separate occasions, both times involving her name spoken aloud by someone with a stamp and a ledger.

Fifteen years. She did the arithmetic the way she always did, automatically, the way other people checked a watch: fifteen years since the inquiry, fifteen years since the blacklist, fifteen years since she'd stopped measuring her life in years at all.

Years were what people who stayed put used. Years were anniversaries and leases and the slow accumulation of furniture. Hazel measured in miles. Forty-one kilometers from the last checkpoint to this one. Three hundred and twelve kilometers of approach march last season with a client who cried at altitude and tipped anyway. Eleven thousand kilometers, give or take, since the night she'd walked out of the hearing room in Lhatsa with her guide's license revoked and her father's name still clean as new snow, and she had never once, in all those kilometers, looked back long enough to wonder what a year felt like.

Miles were honest. Miles only asked what your legs could do. Years asked everything else.

* * *

By midmorning the audit had spread across the yard like a surgical procedure, instruments laid out on tarps in rows: racks, slings, carabiners sorted into keep-pile and retire-pile, the stove stripped to its burner head while Okafor hunched over it with a wire brush and the particular silence he wore when he was happy. Priya had claimed the shaded corner with the medical kit open to its full wingspan, checking expiration dates on blister packs and sutures with the grim focus of a woman who intended to outlive everyone's mistakes. Tomas sat against the wall with the maps, because Tomas always sat with the maps, redrawing route notes in his small precise hand as if the act of copying them made them truer.

Four of them plus her. Five total. Small enough to feed off one stove, small enough to split two tents, small enough that when a border official asked how many were in the party, Hazel could say *four porters and me* and make it boring before the man finished looking up.

Small, legal, invisible. Fifteen years of it.

"You're retiring the yellow rope too?" Dev again, hovering over the tarp. He couldn't help himself. The boy audited her audits.

"I'm retiring anything I wouldn't hang my own weight from."

"You hung your weight from worse in the Karakoram. Okafor told me. You backed off an anchor made of two knifeblades and a prayer."

Hazel didn't look up from the biner she was flexing. "Okafor tells stories."

"It's true," Okafor said mildly, from inside the stove. He was a broad, deliberate man who spoke the way he tightened bolts — never more torque than necessary. "I also told him about the marmot that ate your expedition food in '19, so his picture of you is balanced."

Dev grinned. Hazel let him grin. There was a version of herself that existed in these stories — competent, lucky, faintly legendary — and she kept it fed on purpose, because a crew needed to believe their leader had survived worse than whatever was coming, and because the truth underneath the stories was not something she served at breakfast.

The truth was that she had been twenty-six years old and standing in a hearing room while men with laminated badges read aloud the findings of an inquiry into the Brightwater Expedition of 2009, and the findings had landed on her like weather — diffuse, unavoidable, technically nobody's fault — and she had said almost nothing in her own defense. She remembered that with perfect clarity every time she inventoried a rope. Not the verdict. The silence. Her own silence, six minutes of it, while they took her license and her routes and her name off the registers, while somewhere across town her father was being consoled for the loss of his partners instead of questioned about his maps.

She'd told herself then that she was protecting him. Grief-stricken, she'd told herself; the man lost six friends on that mountain, and you want him answering procedural questions? Later, in the years that had no names, she'd told herself other things. She'd gotten very good at telling herself things. It was, she suspected, the family talent.

"Hazel." Priya, from the shade, holding up a foil packet. "Your amoxicillin is older than Dev."

"Nothing's older than Dev," Dev said.

"Rotate it out at the next resupply," Hazel said. "And check the epinephrine dates too. If we're carrying medicine, it works, or we say we're not carrying it." That was another rule. She had a lot of rules. Rules were load-bearing; you didn't notice them until one failed and the whole structure came down on you.

Priya nodded and wrote it down. Priya wrote everything down, in a waterproof notebook she'd had so long the cover had gone soft as cloth. Hazel had never read it. Some part of her understood that Priya's notebook contained the true history of the crew — doses given, fevers broken, the night in the pass when Dev's cough had sounded like fluid and none of them had slept — and that Hazel appeared in it not as a legend but as a woman who checked ropes three times and flinched at certain words. She preferred Priya's version, probably. She'd never find out.

Tomas cleared his throat. That was how Tomas began every sentence that mattered; the rest were just weather.

"We should talk about the season," he said. "Before the money conversation gets ahead of us again."

"There's no money conversation," Hazel said. "There's a list of clients who haven't answered, and a list of routes that are legal, and where they overlap is our season."

"They overlap thin," Tomas said. "You know they do. Two treks and a film crew that wants drone access in a park where drones are banned. We can't eat a season like that."

"We've eaten thinner."

"We have." Tomas folded his hands over the map, patient as bedrock. He was the oldest of them, gray coming into his beard like frost forming, and he had been with her nine years — long enough that Hazel sometimes forgot he'd had a whole life before her, a wife, a father-in-law who'd been some kind of surveyor, a past he kept folded up as neatly as his maps. Everyone in the crew had a past folded away. That was the admission requirement, practically. "But eaten thin isn't the same as paid down. And there's the matter of—" He stopped. Recalibrated. Hazel watched him do it and wondered, not for the first time, what word he'd swallowed. "—the matter of the contract fees coming due in the spring."

The matter of Dev's indenture, he meant. Nobody said it plainly. Dev's family had signed him to a labor broker at nineteen to cover a hospital debt, and the broker had sold the contract twice since, and now it sat with a financing house in the lowlands that charged interest the way glaciers calved — slowly, massively, without malice. Hazel had been buying the contract down, year by year, out of her own margin, and the crew knew it, and Dev pretended not to know it, and the pretending was its own kind of tenderness.

"Spring is a long way off," Hazel said.

"Spring is four months off," Tomas said, "and we are eleven hundred short."

The number sat in the dust between them. Hazel picked up another carabiner and flexed it and found it true and set it in the keep-pile, and thought about eleven hundred, and thought about miles, and said nothing, because saying nothing was the other family talent.

* * *

They broke for food at noon — flatbread and lentils and the last of Okafor's chili oil, hoarded like state secrets — and ate sitting on the pallet wall with their boots in the dust, five people watching the same empty street. Kestrel's town had one street. It ran from the truck depot to the market shed and back, and in the afternoons the wind came down it carrying grit and diesel smell and, today, the sound of a vehicle engine working hard in low gear.

That was unusual. Freight came on schedule or not at all, and this wasn't schedule.

The vehicle resolved out of the heat shimmer as a dusty SUV with government plates, and Hazel felt the old cold move through her chest, quick and professional, the feeling of a piton seating into bad rock. Border patrol, or revenue, or some ministry's idea of a surprise inspection. In fifteen years she had developed a whole taxonomy of official vehicles by silhouette and dust pattern, the way other guides read clouds.

"Papers," she said quietly, and the crew moved without being told. That was the drill, rehearsed until it was boring: permits into the top folder, contracts into the middle, everything else into the lockbox under the stove. Dev's employment record went face-down. Priya's kit stayed open but innocuous. Okafor wiped his hands and became a mechanic who happened to be resting, which required no acting at all.

The SUV stopped at the mouth of the yard. One officer got out — young, sweating, bored — and Hazel catalogued him in two seconds: junior, alone, probably delivering something rather than hunting it. She stepped forward with the permit folder already extended, smile calibrated to the exact warmth that invited no follow-up questions.

"Good afternoon. Brightwater Trekking Services, seasonal outfitting, five staff, all local hires." All local hires was a lie of aggregation rather than fact, and she delivered it the way she delivered all her best lies: smoothly, and hating it.

The officer barely glanced at the folder. "Not here for you." He was squinting past her, up the street. "Looking for someone. Foreign woman, traveling alone. Tall. Gray jacket. Arrived on yesterday's truck?"

"No one like that registered with us."

He nodded, unsurprised, and got back in the SUV, and the engine noise faded up the street toward the market shed, and the crew exhaled in five-part harmony.

"A foreign woman," Dev repeated, when the dust settled. "Alone? Who travels alone to a place like this?"

"Surveyors do," Tomas said. "Journalists. People with more money than sense."

"People with money," Okafor said, "period. Nobody comes here for the cuisine."

Hazel said nothing. She was watching the street, where the heat shimmer had closed back over the road like water over a stone, and she was thinking about the officer's description — tall, gray jacket, alone — and about how in fifteen years of keeping her crew small and legal and invisible, the thing that had finally arrived in her town was not an inspection or a patrol or a warrant, but a single traveler asking after no one, arriving on no schedule, with money enough to come wherever she pleased.

She turned back to the gear. There were eleven ropes left to check, and counting was what she did instead of remembering, and the afternoon was long.

But that night, folding the retired ropes into their bundle, she caught herself doing the other arithmetic — the one she never allowed — and came up with the same answer it always gave. Fifteen years. No letter. No word. No sign that the world beyond the miles remembered her name at all.

Somewhere up the valley, the wind changed direction, and the temperature dropped, and Hazel stood in the dark of the yard with a dead rope in her hands and the sudden, unreasonable certainty that the quiet was over.

Two days later, Sable Voss walked into Kestrel's hall wearing a gray jacket, and every plan Hazel had built out of smallness and miles came apart in under ten minutes.
