## 2. The Crew That Stays Small

The gear audit took three hours because Okafor insisted on doing it properly, and by "properly" he meant the way a man does things when he has nothing left to hide behind.

Hazel sat on an overturned crate in the back of the outfitter's warehouse and watched him work through the rope inventory, coil by coil, holding each one up to the light from the high dusty windows like a jeweler checking for flaws. He ran every meter through his gloved hands, feeling for soft spots, core shots, that faint glassy stiffness that meant a rope had been frozen one too many times. He'd rejected two already this morning. Both were fine, honestly. Both would have held. But Okafor didn't deal in *would have held*. He dealt in certainty, or as close to it as a man could get with nylon and steel.

"Third rope's got maybe forty percent life," he said without looking up. "I'd retire it."

"Retire it."

"You're not going to argue?"

"Would it change anything?"

"No."

"Then retire it." She turned back to the manifest on her knee — a clipboard with a pencil stub tied to it with frayed cord, because clipboards with nice pens got noticed, and nice anything got noticed — and ticked off the line item. Ropes: seven serviceable, one retired. Harnesses: five, all buckles sound. Stoves: two white gas, one spare pump. Radios: two handhelds, one satellite unit that Okafor had rebuilt twice from parts of three dead ones, which was its own kind of crime in four jurisdictions she could name.

Fifteen years of audits. Fifteen years of counting what they owned down to the last carabiner, because the difference between a crew that survived and a crew that vanished was usually just arithmetic done early enough.

She measured her life in miles now. That was the thing nobody warned you about exile — not the borders, not the names you couldn't use, but the way time stopped meaning anything. Years were for people with addresses. Hazel had trailheads. She thought in kilometers of approach, in vertical meters gained, in the distance between the last place she'd been recognized and the next place she might be. Fifteen years came to something like eleven thousand miles of walking away, if you added it up, and she had added it up once, drunk, in a hostel in Kyrgyzstan, and never done it again.

The warehouse smelled of canvas and kerosene and old sweat baked into pack straps. Through the wall she could hear the outfitter's main hall, the morning trade: porters arguing rates, some trekking outfit's guide bellowing about departure times, the tinny radio behind the counter playing something with too much drum in it. Normal sounds. Town sounds. Sounds that meant nothing to her except that she'd been in this town nine days already, which was eight days too long, and the itch between her shoulder blades said so every morning when she woke.

Small crews stayed invisible. That was the whole doctrine, the entire religion, and she was its only prophet. Four clients maximum, no fixed base, no advertising, no name on the door. Work came by word of mouth along routes she chose herself, thin threads of reputation passed hand to hand among people who needed a guide who asked no questions and left no paperwork. They moved before dawn. They paid cash. They never stayed anywhere longer than the job required.

It wasn't freedom. She wanted to be clear about that, at least with herself, in the privacy of her own skull where no one could hear the honesty. Freedom was a word for people whose passports worked. What she had was a discipline, and the discipline had kept four people alive and fed for fifteen years, and most days that was enough.

Most days.

"Hazel." Priya's voice from the loft above the storeroom, where the medic had claimed territory the first day and defended it against all comers. "Your rookie is trying to reorganize my kit."

"I'm *inventorying*—" Dev's voice, younger, defensive, carrying badly across the rafters.

"It's alphabetized by injury class. There is a system. The system has existed since before you were born."

"He means well," Hazel called up.

"He means to touch things." A pause, then Priya appeared at the rail, a small woman with iron-gray hair cut short and reading glasses pushed up into it, holding a blister pack of antibiotics like evidence. "Tell your rookie that in a field hospital, order is the difference between thirty seconds and three minutes, and three minutes is how long it takes someone to bleed out from a femoral bleed, and I have watched it happen, and I will bury him in the moraine if he touches shelf two again."

"She's joking," Hazel told Dev, who had come down the ladder looking like a scolded dog, twenty-three years old and all elbows and earnestness, a kid she'd picked up eighteen months ago in a border town because he could carry more than his weight and looked at her like she was a map of somewhere better.

"She's not joking," Dev said.

"I'm not joking," Priya confirmed, and went back up.

That was the crew. That was the whole of it, and Hazel knew every seam of them the way she knew her own ropes.

Tomas came in through the loading door as she finished the manifest, shaking rain off a shell jacket that had been waterproof sometime during the previous administration. He was fifty-one, built like a question mark these days, knees that forecast weather better than any barometer, and the finest navigator she had ever worked with, which was saying something, because she'd worked with the best and the best had mostly been frauds. Tomas read terrain the way other men read faces — instantly, totally, and with a running commentary of suspicion. Give him a contour map and a cloud ceiling and he'd find a route through it that didn't exist until he walked it.

He also hadn't slept, which she could tell from across the room, though he'd have denied it. Tomas carried his reasons for hiding the way he carried his maps: folded small, close to the chest, never displayed. She knew pieces. Everyone in the family business knows pieces. His wife had died — that much was public, that much he'd said once, drunk, on a glacier in the Karakoram, the only time in fourteen years she'd heard his voice crack. The rest — the brother-in-law, the money, whatever it was that made him flinch at official letterhead — stayed folded.

"Border patrol's in town," he said quietly, coming to stand beside her crate so the words wouldn't travel. "Two jeeps. Green uniforms, the new ones. They're asking about trekking outfits, checking registrations."

"How many outfits are registered in this town?"

"Eleven."

"And we're not one of them."

"We're not one of them." He rubbed his jaw. "They started at the east end. They'll reach the outfitter's hall by afternoon, if they're thorough."

"They're always thorough. It's the only part of the job they enjoy."

Okafor had stopped working. He stood in the middle of the rope pile with a coil still in his hands, listening the way he listened to engines — head tilted, sorting signal from noise. "How long to break camp?" he asked.

"Twenty minutes if we're calm," Hazel said. "Ten if we're not. We're going to be calm."

* * *

Calm lasted until half past two.

By then they'd struck the storeroom camp, settled accounts with the outfitter — cash, overpaid slightly, because a man who feels cheated remembers your face and a man who feels fairly treated forgets it — and redistributed the loads. Hazel had walked the route out of town twice in her head: north through the market quarter, past the shrine with the prayer flags, onto the goat track that climbed toward the terraces, where four people with packs could dissolve into landscape inside ten minutes. It was a good plan. It was, in fact, the same plan she'd used out of six towns in three countries, refined each time, and she trusted it the way she trusted knots she'd tied a thousand times.

What she hadn't planned on was Dev.

Not his fault. That was the thing about rookies — you could teach them knots and pacing and how to read a slope, but you couldn't teach them the instinct that keeps a man from stopping dead in the middle of a street when he sees something interesting. And Dev saw something interesting. Dev saw a patrol jeep parked outside the tea house on the market road, green paint steaming dry in the sun after the morning rain, and Dev — twenty-three, curious, constitutionally incapable of walking past a locked door or a strange vehicle without cataloguing it — slowed.

Just slowed. Half a step. A hitch in his stride that any trained eye reads like a shout.

Hazel read it from six meters back and did the only thing there was to do. "Dev," she said, loud and bored, "you're dragging. Water break." And she steered them all into the mouth of the alley beside the shrine, into shadow that smelled of butter lamps and wet stone, and put her hand flat on Dev's chest before his mouth could open.

"Don't look at it," she said. "You looked at it. That's done. Now we're four friends taking shelter from the rain, and in ninety seconds we walk out the far end of this alley at a stroll, and you will be telling me a story. Any story. About your sister. About a dog. Make it boring."

"There's a jeep—"

"I know there's a jeep. I've known about the jeep since breakfast. Boring story, Dev."

His throat worked. Rain dripped off the prayer flags above them, ticking on the stones. Down the alley's mouth, the market went on with its noise, oblivious, and somewhere in it two men in green uniforms were working their way through a list of registered outfits, none of which were hers, and that was the whole game right there — not hiding, exactly. Being unlistable. Being a gap in the paperwork.

"My sister," Dev said finally, "hated goats. We had goats. She said they looked at her like they knew things."

"That's terrible," Priya murmured.

"It's boring," Hazel said. "Perfect. Keep going."

He kept going. Something about the goats getting into the kitchen, about his mother's fury, about a stew pot with hoofprints in it, and it was genuinely boring and genuinely funny and by the end of it his shoulders had come down from around his ears, and they strolled out the far end of the alley into the gray drizzle like four friends with nowhere particular to be, and the jeep steamed on outside the tea house, and nobody in it looked at them twice.

They didn't speak again until the goat track, until the town was a smear of tin roofs below and the terraces closed around them, green and dripping.

Hazel let Dev catch up to her on the switchbacks. He was breathing hard under his load — he always overloaded himself, trying to prove the hire hadn't been charity — and she matched his pace rather than making him match hers, which was a lesson she'd learned late and paid for early.

"What you did back there," she said, "the slowing down. Don't do it again."

"I know. I'm sorry. I just thought—"

"You thought information was free. It's not. Every fact you gather costs a second of attention, and attention is visible, and visible gets remembered." She kept her voice level, instructive, not cruel. Cruelty taught nothing; she'd had enough cruelty used on her to know. "In towns, we're boring. In camps, we're boring. On the mountain, you can be as curious as you want, because the mountain doesn't file reports. Understood?"

"Understood." Then, because he was Dev, and earnestness leaked out of him like sweat: "Does it ever stop? Looking over your shoulder?"

Hazel looked out at the valley, at the rain moving across it in gray curtains, at the country that had been good to them for nine days and would forget them entirely within a week, which was the arrangement, which was the price.

"No," she said. "But you get faster at it. Eventually it stops being looking over your shoulder and starts being just... looking. You see everything. That's the trade."

"For what?"

"For staying free enough to keep walking." She clapped his shoulder once, brief, and moved up the line, because there was always something to check, and checking was easier than answering questions she didn't have clean answers to.

* * *

Camp that night was a hollow beneath a rock overhang above the treeline, the kind of site Tomas found the way water finds low ground. They had a fire small enough to cup in two hands, screened by the overhang, burning driftwood-dry juniper that gave off almost no smoke. Rice and lentils and the last of the dried meat. Priya dosed Dev's blisters while lecturing him about sock rotation. Okafor field-stripped the stove pump by firelight, laying the parts out on a cloth in an order only he understood, reassembling it blind, testing it, nodding to himself like a priest satisfied with the liturgy.

Tomas sat apart, as he often did, studying tomorrow's route by the light of a shielded headlamp, and Hazel watched her crew the way a shepherd watches a fold in a storm — counting, always counting. Four. All present. All accounted for.

People asked her sometimes — the rare clients who talked too much, the occasional official who pried — why so small. Why not grow. A guide with her record, her routes, her eye, could run twenty-person expeditions, could franchise, could put her name on a company and let the name do the work. And Hazel would give the practical answer, the true-but-incomplete answer: small teams move fast, small teams eat less, small teams leave less trace. All true. None of it the truth.

The truth was that a big operation needed a paper trail, and a paper trail needed a clean name, and hers had been dirty for fifteen years. The truth was that every person she added was another person who could be questioned, detained, flipped, or lost, and she had already buried enough of the people who followed her family's name into mountains. The truth, the one she kept folded smallest of all, was that these four were hers — not employees, not clients, hers — and she had learned the hard way what happened when you loved a number bigger than you could carry.

Priya had a medical license suspended in a country that had decided triaging riot casualties was a political act. She never talked about it. She talked about everything else — endlessly, hilariously, a running commentary on the moral failures of boot manufacturers and the sexual habits of yaks — but the license stayed folded away, and Hazel never unfolded it, because that was the covenant: you hide your reason, I hide mine, and neither of us asks what the other carries.

Okafor had been chief mechanic on a mining operation that collapsed, officially due to geological instability, unofficially due to corner-cutting he'd documented and reported and been ignored on. Eleven men underground when it went. He'd walked out, testified, watched the report get sealed, and understood with perfect clarity that his name was now worth more dead than alive to certain people. He'd been drifting through mountain towns fixing engines ever since, and Hazel had found him in a truck depot repairing a bus with a screwdriver and sheer contempt, and hired him on the spot, and he'd never once asked why her crew stayed small, because a man who'd seen a report sealed knew exactly why paperwork was a weapon.

And Dev — Dev was the strangest case, because Dev wasn't hiding from anything yet. He was nineteen months into a life he didn't fully understand, a kid from a farm family who'd washed out of the national mountaineering program for reasons he'd never explained and Hazel had never pressed, and he'd attached himself to her crew with the wholehearted devotion of a dog that had decided where home was. Maybe he was hiding from a future that had already been decided for him. Maybe he was hiding from nothing, and simply hadn't yet learned that everyone eventually had something to fold away. Either way, Hazel kept him anyway, and told herself it was because he pulled his weight, and knew that was maybe sixty percent of it.

The fire burned down. Rain started again on the overhang, a soft percussion, and the talk thinned out the way it does when people are warm and tired and safe, which was the rarest weather of all, and Hazel sat in it and let herself feel it for exactly as long as it lasted.

"We should move farther north tomorrow," Tomas said from his corner, not looking up from the map. "Off the main track. There's a route through the pass that isn't on any survey."

"Of course it isn't," Priya said. "Nothing you walk is on any survey. I sometimes think you make them up."

"I make them up," Tomas agreed placidly, "and then they turn out to be real. That's navigation."

Okafor snorted. Dev laughed, tired and easy, and lay back with his boots on his pack the way she'd taught him, and within minutes his breathing went long and even.

Hazel banked the fire. She took first watch, because she always took first watch, because sleep came hard to her and watch duty gave the insomnia a job. She sat with her back to the rock and watched the rain move through the black valley and counted her people by their breathing: Tomas, slow and steady; Priya, with the slight whistle of an old cold; Okafor, silent as furniture; Dev, young, deep, untroubled.

Four breaths. Her whole world, sized to fit through gaps in the world's paperwork.

She was thinking — idly, the way you think on watch, thoughts drifting like the mist — that they'd need work soon. That the account was thinner than she liked, that Dev's contract debt was coming due with the spring, that invisibility was cheap but not free. She was thinking about routes, seasons, possibilities, running the mental ledger she ran every night—

—and then Tomas, who had not been asleep after all, who had been sitting in the dark with something in his hands, said quietly:

"Hazel. There's something you should see."

She turned. He was holding an envelope. Even in the dark she caught the flash of it — heavy paper, expensive, the kind of paper that didn't come to places like this — and on its face, catching the last ember-light of the banked fire, a seal pressed in dark wax.

A crest she hadn't seen in fifteen years.

Her family's.
