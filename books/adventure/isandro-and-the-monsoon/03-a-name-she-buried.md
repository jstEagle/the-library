## 3. A Name She Buried

The letter came on a Tuesday, which meant nothing to anyone except Tomas Reyes, who had spent fifteen years learning that the worst news always arrived on ordinary days.

He was in the outfitter's back room when it happened, doing what he did every Tuesday: reconciling their accounts against a ledger he kept in his own shorthand, one that would look like gibberish to any auditor and like paranoia to anyone who knew him well enough to know that was exactly the point. The room smelled of rope wax and diesel from the generator outside. A fan turned in the window, pushing hot air around without cooling anything. Somewhere beyond the wall, Hazel was running Dev through knot drills, her voice carrying through the plaster in clipped, patient bursts — *no, again, the tension lives in your hips, not your hands* — and Tomas had long ago stopped hearing the words and started hearing them as weather.

The mail came at four. It always came at four, carried up from the valley by a boy on a motorcycle who charged them triple the postal rate because they were foreigners and because he could. Usually it was nothing: a parts catalog for Okafor, a medical journal reprint Priya had ordered six months ago and forgotten, bills addressed to names that only half existed. Tomas sorted it standing up, fast, the way you'd defuse something.

The envelope was heavy cream stock, the kind of paper that cost more per sheet than their entire food budget for a week. No return address. His name — *Tomas Reyes* — written in fountain pen, in a hand so controlled it looked printed.

And pressed into the wax seal at the flap: a mountain peak above two crossed ice axes, crowned by a small star.

Tomas stood very still.

He knew that crest. He had known it before he'd known Hazel, back when knowing it was an honor instead of a wound. He'd seen it stamped on expedition manifests, embroidered on parkas, painted on the side of a Twin Otter that lifted off from a glacier airstrip in 2009 carrying six people who never came down. He'd seen it on the inquiry documents afterward, reproduced in grainy photocopy, exhibit after exhibit after exhibit, until the sight of it made his jaw ache. The Brightwater crest. Elian Brightwater's crest. The mark of a legend, and of a lie, and of everything that had happened to everyone he loved in the space between those two words.

Fifteen years, and no one had sent him a letter bearing that seal. No one had dared. The people who remembered the scandal wrote about it in magazines; the people who lived it didn't write at all.

He turned the envelope over twice, looking for a sender. There wasn't one. Just the postmark — a city he didn't recognize, dated eleven days ago, which told him it had traveled slow routes through slow hands, or that someone had wanted it to.

Hazel was twenty feet away, separated by a single plaster wall, teaching a nineteen-year-old how to tie a butterfly coil.

Tomas put the letter in his jacket, inside pocket, against his ribs, and went out to make dinner.

* * *

He was good at hiding things. He'd had practice.

Not the way Voss hid things, or the way governments hid things — not with intent to use, not as ammunition. Tomas hid things the way you'd hide a knife from a child: not because the child couldn't be trusted, but because some objects changed the shape of the hand that held them. He'd hidden his own name for three years in the early days, traveling under his mother's surname, until Hazel found his passport by accident and looked at him with an expression he still couldn't fully read and said only, *Reyes. Huh.* And that had been the whole conversation, because Hazel understood better than anyone alive that a person was allowed to bury one version of themselves if the first version had been buried for them.

He'd hidden Priya's medical license suspension from the crew for eight months until she chose to tell them herself. He'd hidden Okafor's letters from his sister — unopened, returned twice — in a gear box under the floorboards, because Okafor had asked him to and then, later, had asked him to burn them, and Tomas had burned them, and neither of them ever spoke of it again. He'd hidden the fact that he'd been the one who answered the phone, fifteen years ago, the night the inquiry called Hazel's number, and that he'd listened to the man on the other end say the word *blacklisted* in a voice like reading a shipping manifest, and that he had sat down on the floor of a rented flat in another country and understood that the life they'd planned was over.

So yes. He could hide a letter.

That didn't mean it was easy.

At dinner — rice, lentils, the flatbread Dev had finally learned to cook without burning — Hazel talked about the route survey she wanted to run in the eastern hills, cheap work, legal work, the kind that kept them fed through the off-season. Dev asked questions with the hungry earnestness that Tomas privately found exhausting and publicly found endearing. Priya corrected his field-dressing technique across the table with two fingers and a raised eyebrow. Okafor said almost nothing, as usual, but fixed the pressure stove's priming line while eating, which was its own kind of sentence.

And Tomas laughed in the right places and passed the bread and thought, continuously, about the weight of an envelope resting forty feet away in his jacket, hanging on a peg by the door, close enough that Hazel would pass within arm's reach of it a dozen times before morning.

She wouldn't open his mail. That was the thing. In fifteen years she had never once opened his mail, or Priya's, or anyone's — a scruple so absolute it bordered on superstition, left over, maybe, from a childhood in which every piece of correspondence in the Brightwater house had been fair game for Elian's review. Privacy was the one religion Hazel practiced without irony. Which meant the letter was safe sitting there in plain sight, and also meant that hiding it was, in some technical sense, unnecessary.

He hid it anyway. Because it wasn't the envelope that was dangerous. It was the question Hazel would ask the moment she saw the seal — *who still uses that crest?* — and the answer, whatever it was, would be a door, and doors were the one thing Tomas Reyes had built his whole adult life around keeping shut.

* * *

Later, when the others had gone to their bunks and the generator was cut and the town had gone down to dogs and wind, Tomas took the letter out and held it under the last of the lantern light without opening it.

This was the discipline. You could hold a thing. You could turn it over. You could learn its weight, its temperature, the faint ridge of the wax under your thumb. Opening it was a separate act, and acts could wait.

He thought about the handwriting. Fountain pen, machine-even, the loops closed tight like someone who'd learned penmanship in an institution or an army or a very old family. Not Hazel's hand. Not Elian's — he'd have known Elian's anywhere, the confident slant of it, the way the capital B leaned forward like a man walking into weather. Someone else entirely. Someone who owned the right to press that seal into wax, which narrowed the world to a handful of living people, and every one of them was someone Tomas had spent fifteen years making sure Hazel never had to see again.

An uncle, maybe. There had been an uncle — Elian's younger brother, a diplomat, present at the inquiry for exactly one day, silent throughout, gone before the verdict. Or a cousin. Or the estate lawyers, though lawyers didn't use family crests on personal stationery; crests on legal paper were threats dressed as courtesy.

Or — and this was the thought that made his hands go cold on the cream stock — someone connected to Sable Voss.

He had no evidence for that. He wantedit noted, even in the privacy of his own skull, that he had no evidence for that. But fifteen years in the margins of other people's disasters teaches you a certain literacy in coincidence, and the timing of this letter — now, this season, this dusty nowhere of a town, weeks before the Kharun route opened — sat wrong in his chest the way a load sits wrong on a porter's back. Not painful yet. Just balanced badly. The kind of wrongness that becomes pain the moment you start moving.

Because everyone in the trade knew Voss was moving on something this year. Voss always moved on something. But the rumors this season had a specific gravity: salvage, pre-season, the Kharun, a window measured in days. And the Kharun was not just any glacier. The Kharun was where the Brightwater expedition had gone in. Where the six had died. Where Elian Brightwater's body was presumed to lie, still seated in the ice somewhere below the summit he'd lied about, holding whatever the cold had kept of him for sixteen years.

If Voss was going to the Kharun, Voss was going for the wreck. Everyone who mattered knew it. And now, eleven days' travel behind the rumor, a letter with the Brightwater crest had found its way to the one man alive whose entire function in Hazel's life was to stand between her and the year 2009.

Coincidence was possible. Tomas had simply stopped believing in it at altitude.

* * *

He didn't sleep. He lay in his bunk listening to the building settle and the wind work at the loose shutter on the north wall — a shutter Okafor had promised to fix in March, then April, then stopped promising — and he ran the arithmetic he always ran, the quiet ledger beneath the ledger.

Option one: open the letter tonight, read it alone, decide alone what Hazel needed to know. This was the option that felt most natural and was, he knew, the most dangerous, because it cast him in the role he'd sworn off years ago — the keeper, the filter, the man who decided what reality reached her. He'd done it once, early, with the first newspaper clipping that mentioned her father's name, and Hazel had found out two years later and the look on her face had taught him a lesson he could recite verbatim: *I don't need you to protect me from the truth, Tomas. I need you to help me carry it.* He had never filtered anything since. Fifteen years of handing her every blade, handle-first.

Option two: give it to her in the morning, unopened, and let the day do what it did.

Option three: burn it unread.

He turned option three over longer than he should have. It would be easy. The stove was still warm. Paper that size would take ninety seconds and leave nothing but a smell, and Hazel would never know a letter had come, and the day would arrive tomorrow wearing its usual face, and the season would proceed — the Kharun job, if it came, would come as money and nothing else, and they would go, and they would come back, and the past would stay wherever he kept it.

Except the past didn't stay where you kept it. That was the entire curriculum of the last fifteen years. The past was a glacier: it moved, slowly, under its own mass, and it surfaced things. Rope. Metal. Bodies. Letters. You could pretend the surface was stable right up until the day it calved, and then everything you'd buried came down at once, all together, in a noise you heard from miles away.

He got up in the dark, quietly, and crossed the cold floor to the peg by the door, and moved the letter from his jacket to the bottom of his map case, under the folded sheets of the eastern hills route, where no one would find it unless they were looking for maps and would find maps.

It wasn't a decision. He knew that. It was a postponement wearing a decision's clothes. But he was fifty-one years old and he had learned that most of survival was choosing which bad options to defer, and deferral, done skillfully, could look like wisdom for years at a time.

* * *

Morning came gray and hot, monsoon pressure stacking up over the southern ridgelines in towers that Priya studied over breakfast with the expression she reserved for patients she didn't like.

"Early this year," she said, nodding at the sky. "Feel that? The air's wrong. That's a pre-monsoon build. We'll get rain inside two weeks."

"Rain's fine," Hazel said, coiling rope with her hands moving on their own. "Rain's just weather."

"It's not rain I'd worry about." Priya sipped her tea. "It's what the rain does to the approaches. And to anyone planning high-altitude work in the next month."

Tomas watched Hazel's hands pause — a half-second, barely a stutter, the kind of hitch only three people alive would have caught — and resume. High-altitude work. The Kharun. Even the weather was circling the same coordinates now.

Dev came in wet from the wash line, cheerful, oblivious, asking whether today was knots or anchors, and the ordinary machinery of the morning took them all, and Tomas let it. He drank his tea. He checked the map case twice without appearing to check it. He answered a question about resupply weights with numbers pulled from a part of his brain that was elsewhere entirely.

And then, near noon, with the heat pressing down and the fan turning its useless circles, the door of the outfitter's hall opened, and the conversation in the room died the way a flame dies when a window slams.

Sable Voss stood in the doorway, dust on her boots, a travel case in one hand, scanning the room with the unhurried certainty of a woman who has already been told the answer to every question she's about to ask.

Tomas felt the map case against his hip like a second heartbeat.

Voss's eyes found Hazel. Of course they did. They always had. But for one full second — one second no one else in the room would ever know had happened — they flicked to Tomas instead. To Tomas, specifically, standing by the map table, and something moved behind them that was not surprise.

Recognition, maybe. Or confirmation.

Or the look of someone checking whether a letter had arrived.

"Hazel Brightwater," Voss said, and the name filled the hall like smoke. "Fifteen years. You're a hard woman to find."

"I'm not hard to find," Hazel said evenly. "I'm expensive to bother."

Voss smiled and came forward, and Tomas stood very still beside his maps, and underneath the folded sheets, sealed in wax, the past waited with the patience of ice.
