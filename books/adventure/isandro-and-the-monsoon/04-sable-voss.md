## 4. Sable Voss

The rain came sideways off the square, and Hazel Brightwater watched it through the outfitter's window while pretending to inventory carabiners she already knew by heart.

Forty-one gates, three with worn gate springs. Twelve lockers. A rope bag that had been patched twice and would need a third patch before season's end. She wrote the numbers in the ledger without looking down at the page. Fifteen years of audits had made her hands independent of her attention, which was a mercy, because her attention was on the square, where a woman in a charcoal shell jacket stood under the eaves of the customs house, checking a watch worth more than everything in Hazel's pack room.

Hazel knew the silhouette before she knew the face. You spend enough years running routes, you learn to read bodies the way sailors read weather. That particular stillness — weight forward, chin level, scanning without appearing to scan — belonged to exactly one person.

"No," Hazel said out loud, to nobody. The pack room was empty except for gear and the smell of hemp rope and old wax. "Absolutely not."

Sable Voss crossed the square anyway.

* * *

The outfitter's hall doubled as a tea house, a post office, and on Thursdays a place where men argued about yak prices. It was the largest room in Talung-Dzong, which was not saying much: low beams, a stove that smoked when the wind turned, tables scarred by thirty years of crampon points. Hazel took the corner table by habit — back to the wall, sightline to both doors. She'd chosen that table for so long it had a permanent ring from her thermos, like a grave marker.

Voss came in shaking rain off her shoulders and did not look around the room, because Sable Voss had never once needed to check whether she was the most dangerous thing in it.

"Hazel." She said the name like she was testing an ice screw — firm, evaluative, prepared for it to hold or shear.

"Sable."

"Mind if I sit?"

"It's a free country for people whose paperwork is in order."

Voss smiled. That was the thing about her smile; it never reached anything above the cheekbones. Fifteen years ago it hadn't either, on the Lhotse wall, when they'd shared a tent for nine days and Voss had stolen Hazel's weather window by faking altitude sickness, summited alone, and told the press afterward that Hazel had turned back weeping. The lie had cost Hazel two sponsors. The truth had cost her nothing, because nobody had ever paid for the truth.

"You look well," Voss said, sitting across from her uninvited. "Mountain air agrees with you."

"Cut the foreplay, Sable. You didn't walk four hundred kilometers of monsoon road to compliment my complexion."

"Fair." Voss set a slim document case on the table between them, black leather, no logo. Everything about her was expensive in a quiet way — the kind of money that didn't announce itself because it had never needed to. "Salvage job. Pre-season. Kharun Glacier."

Hazel kept her face still. Inside her chest, something old and cold shifted its weight, the way a serac shifts before it decides.

"The Kharun's closed," she said. "Restricted zone since '09. You know that better than anyone — your foundation lobbied for the closure."

"My foundation lobbied for the memorial, yes. The closure was bureaucratic momentum." Voss opened the case and slid a single sheet across the table. "Calving season opens the tongue for eleven days this year. Glaciology models say the eleventh of next month. After that, the route seals until autumn, and after this year's melt rates, possibly forever. I want a crew in and out inside the window."

"Salvage what?"

"Equipment. Historical materials. A client of mine has an interest in the 2009 expedition era."

There it was. The name Hazel had spent fifteen years not saying sat in the middle of the table like a dropped axe. She picked up the sheet instead. Triple rate. Not double — triple, written into the offer itself, as if Voss had anticipated the objection and priced it. Salvage fee, hazard multiplier, completion bonus. The figures were clean, professional, insulting in their precision.

"I don't do the Kharun," Hazel said. "I don't do restricted zones, I don't do pre-season icefall work, and I especially don't do jobs where the client says 'historical materials' instead of naming what they want."

"You used to do harder things for less money."

"I used to be stupid in ways I've since corrected."

Voss leaned back. Around them the hall went on with its Thursday noises — the yak argument had reached the shouting stage, someone was hammering a loose shutter outside, the stove ticked as it cooled. Two worlds occupying one room, and only one of them aware a negotiation was happening.

"Fifteen years," Voss said. "That's how long you've been running this little operation. Four people. Legal routes, legal borders, small contracts, small money. You've built yourself a reputation as the most reliable invisible woman in the Himalayan trade. Do you know what I admire about that?"

"Enlighten me."

"Discipline. Genuinely. Most people who get burned the way you got burned either quit or get reckless. You did neither. You got careful." Voss turned her teacup ninety degrees, aligning the handle with the table edge, and Hazel remembered that gesture too — the compulsive tidiness of a mind that treated the world as something to be squared away. "But careful has a cost, Hazel. And I'm looking at it."

"You're looking at a cup of tea."

"I'm looking at a woman whose youngest crew member is carrying a debt contract with the Karmapa Trading Concern. Nineteen months left on it. Am I close?"

The hall's noise seemed to recede, the way sound does before a rockfall.

"That information isn't public," Hazel said quietly.

"Neither am I. I have interests everywhere, you know that. Dev Bahadur Thapa, twenty-three years old, signed his indenture at nineteen to cover his brother's medical bills, assigned to your crew as part of his service term. He's good, by the way. Your rookie. Watches you like you hung the moon." Voss's voice stayed level, almost gentle, which was worse than cruelty. "Nineteen months is a long time in this business. People get hurt. Contracts get extended for medical care, for equipment losses, for all kinds of reasonable things. His brother's condition could worsen. Costs accumulate."

Hazel set her hands flat on the table so they wouldn't do anything else. "Are you threatening my crew?"

"I'm describing arithmetic. There's a difference, though I understand it can be hard to see from where you're sitting." Voss slid a second sheet from the case. "Here's the alternative. Triple rate clears his contract in full. Today, on signature. It also clears Tomas's land lien and puts eleven months' operating capital in your account. Nobody threatens anybody. A woman offers a fair price for honest work, and a proud woman refuses it because the mountain has a name she doesn't like."

"You've done your homework."

"I always do my homework. It's why I win."

"You didn't win on Lhotse."

Something moved behind Voss's eyes then — quick, real, quickly filed away. "No," she said. "I didn't win on Lhotse. Which is why I'm here buying the best guide money can reach instead of taking my own team." She let that sit. "Flattery, Hazel. Notice it. I'm being charming, and I hate every second of it, and I'm doing it anyway, because I need you specifically. Not your crew. Not your permits. You. There are maybe six guides alive who could put a team through the Kharun icefall in an eleven-day window, and five of them would sell me to the first journalist who offered dinner. You hate publicity more than you hate me. That makes you the safest pair of hands in Asia."

"And if I still say no?"

"Then I hire the sixth guide, and I pay him double what I offered you, and he'll take porters who don't know the route, and some of them won't come home, and none of that will appear on your conscience because you'll have made the safe choice, the careful choice, the choice you've been making for fifteen years." Voss stood, buttoning her shell jacket, unhurried. "I'll be at the Dzong guesthouse for three days. The offer holds until I leave."

She put a folded note under Hazel's thermos — the figure again, handwritten, in ink the color of glacier water.

"One more thing," Voss said from the door. "You should know the salvage target isn't random. My client wants something specific from the 2009 site. Something that's been in that ice a long time." Her hand rested on the door frame, and for half a second — half a second Hazel would think about later, much later, turning it over at 3 a.m. like a stone with a strange underside — Sable Voss looked tired. Actually tired, down past the cheekbones, somewhere structural. "We all lost people in '09, Hazel. Some of us just lost different things."

Then she was gone, into the rain, and the hall swallowed her absence the way it swallowed everything.

* * *

Hazel sat at the corner table until the tea went cold and the yak argument dissolved and the lamplighter came for the wicks. She turned the note over. Turned it back. The number didn't change, which was the problem. Numbers were honest that way; they waited.

She thought about Dev, who sang off-key when he thought no one could hear, who had shown her last week a photograph of his brother's new prosthetic and said *nineteen more months, didi, then I'm free*. She thought about Tomas's lien — fourteen hectares of terraced land in Solu held under paper he'd signed to bury his father-in-law, a man Hazel had never met but whose handwriting, Tomas had once said drunk on chang, was on documents Hazel would know if she saw them. She thought about fifteen years of miles. Miles were how she'd measured everything since the blacklist: distance from trouble, distance from the past, distance from a mountain whose name she could not say aloud without tasting copper.

And she thought about her father, which she did not allow herself to do often, and which she now did helplessly, completely, the way a cracked dam does its work.

Elian Brightwater. Hero of the Kharun. The man who'd put six climbers on a false line and come back alone — no. Not alone. He hadn't come back at all. That was the story everyone believed: the whole expedition lost, the great Elian Brightwater taken by the mountain along with his survey data, his summit claim, his legend. Martyrs all. The mountain keeps its dead, and the ice keeps its secrets, and Hazel had built a life in the narrow country between those two sentences.

If the wreck was exposed — if Voss's client pulled *something specific* out of that ice — everything buried in 2009 came up with it. The maps. The route. The questions Hazel had stopped asking in the inquiry tent when she was twenty-four years old, because the answers were standing right there in her father's confident handwriting and she had looked away. She had looked away once. She had built fifteen years on top of that looking-away, the way you build a camp on a snow bridge, knowing, knowing, and refusing to probe.

She folded the note into quarters and put it in her chest pocket, against her sternum, where it felt less like money and more like a diagnosis.

Outside, the rain had thinned to mist. The square was empty except for a dog sheltering under the customs-house eaves — the same eaves where Voss had stood, watching, waiting for Hazel to notice her. Even the approach had been a move. Even patience was a weapon in that woman's hands.

Hazel walked back to the pack room and stood among the ropes and the racks and the smell of wax, and she did the thing she hated most, which was nothing. She let the silence ask its question.

Triple rate. Eleven days. One mountain she'd sworn off, one rival she'd sworn at, and a debt holding a twenty-three-year-old kid the way ice holds a body — patiently, thoroughly, without malice.

Pride said no. Pride had been saying no for fifteen years, and pride had kept them small and legal and alive, and pride had also kept them poor enough that a stranger could walk in off the monsoon road and price them like livestock.

Obligation said nothing at all. Obligation just laid the ledger open on the bench — Dev's contract, Tomas's lien, the patched rope bag, the winter stores that would run thin by February — and waited for her to read it.

Hazel blew out the lamp.

Three days, Voss had said. But Hazel already knew she wouldn't use them. She'd known from the moment the woman checked her watch under the customs-house eaves, because Sable Voss had never once come to Hazel Brightwater for something she could get elsewhere, and that fact was the most frightening sentence in the whole conversation.

Whatever was in that ice, Voss needed Hazel to find it.

Which meant Hazel needed to find it first.

She stood in the dark of the pack room, rain ticking on the tin roof, and reached for the ledger to write tomorrow's date — and paused, pen above the page, hearing her own pulse.

First, though, there was a campfire she had to call, and a vote she had to lose.
