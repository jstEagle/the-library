## 5. Eleven Days

The fire was Okafor's doing, which meant it was built like everything he built: overengineered, slightly smug about it, and guaranteed to outlast whatever it was made for. He'd stacked the wet juniper in a lattice, fed it paraffin from a cut-down water bottle, and coaxed a flame out of air that had been raining for six hours. Now it hissed and spat under the tarp they'd strung between the truck's bed rail and a leaning fence post, throwing enough heat to make the circle of camp chairs feel almost like a room.

Priya counted heads without meaning to. It was a medic's habit — headcount, breathing, color, posture. Tomas sat closest to the flames, turning his hands palms-out, the way he did when he was thinking with his whole body. Dev perched on an upturned crate at the edge of the light, seventeen years old and vibrating with the effort of pretending he wasn't listening to every word. Okafor occupied two chairs' worth of space, boots up on a cooler, a mug of something that smelled like burnt sugar balanced on his stomach. And Hazel stood just outside the tarp's edge where the rain could reach her, arms crossed, watching the dark as if it owed her an explanation.

Nobody had spoken for four minutes. Priya knew because she'd been counting that too.

"Triple rate," Dev said finally, unable to hold it anymore. "That's — do you know what triple rate is? That's my contract paid off. That's all of it. Gone. In eleven days."

"It's not eleven days of work," Tomas said. "It's eleven days of *window*. Calving season opens the Kharun route for maybe eleven days before the icefall eats itself again. If we're slow, if we're unlucky, if the monsoon comes up the valley early — we're inside a glacier when it decides to close."

"So we're not slow."

Tomas looked at him across the fire. He wasn't unkind — Tomas was never unkind, that was the problem, he was kind the way a surgeon is kind, precisely and without flinching — but he didn't soften it either. "Dev. I've been navigating ice since before you were born. The Kharun doesn't care how fast you are. The Kharun cares whether it feels like killing you that day."

"And yet," Okafor said into his mug, "we're having this conversation instead of laughing her off the mountain. Which tells you everything about the state of our accounts."

Priya wrapped her hands around her own cup. Tea, mostly, with the dregs of a honey jar dissolved into it. She watched Hazel through the steam.

Hazel hadn't moved. Rain ran off the brim of her hood in a thin continuous thread. Fifteen years Priya had known her — eight of them on this crew — and she'd catalogued every version of Hazel Brightwater there was: Hazel in a whiteout, Hazel delivering bad news to a client's family, Hazel at 3 a.m. re-roping a terrified teenager on a fixed line. But this version, standing in the rain outside her own fire while her crew argued about money, was one Priya recognized from only one other time. The night three years ago when the bank letter came about Dev's contract, and Hazel had read it twice, folded it into quarters small enough to disappear, and said nothing for two days.

Guilt wore her quietly. That was the thing people got wrong about Hazel Brightwater. They thought the blacklisting had made her hard. It hadn't. It had made her *responsible* — pathologically, expensively responsible — as if she were paying down a debt that had never been itemized.

"Hazel," Priya said. "Come sit. You're making the rain look intentional."

Hazel came in. She took the last chair, the one with the broken armrest, and let the fire have her face for a moment. Up close Priya could see the day on her: the gear audit dust still in the creases of her knuckles, a shallow cut on her thumb she'd taped herself and told no one about.

"Talk," Hazel said.

"We've been talking for twenty minutes," Okafor said.

"I know. I heard it from the rain." A ghost of a smile. "Now talk where I can see your faces."

So they did, again, faster now, the arguments worn smooth from repetition. Triple rate. Eleven days. Pre-season salvage on the lower glacier — Voss's paperwork said recovered equipment from a commercial survey team, nothing sensitive, nothing high. Route access through the tongue before calving opened it wide, then out before the meltwater channels turned the descent into a lottery.

"The part I don't like," Tomas said, "is the pre-season part. Nobody salvages pre-season. The ice isn't done moving. Anything left on that glacier in March is either buried or crushed by April. What is she recovering that can't wait for the season?"

"Maybe it's time-sensitive," Dev offered.

"Everything's time-sensitive when someone's paying triple."

"The part *I* don't like," Okafor said, "is Sable Voss. Not the job. The woman. I've read about her expeditions. She runs crews like drill bits. People come back from Voss trips with money and a thousand-yard stare, both."

"She pays," Dev said. "Everyone says she pays."

"Everyone says she pays," Okafor agreed. "Nobody ever says what it costs."

Priya let them run. Her own objection was quieter and she'd been holding it against her teeth all evening: it wasn't the money or the window or the woman. It was the name. *Kharun.* She'd watched Hazel say no to jobs in the Karakoram, no to the Pamir, no twice to anything touching the old restricted zones — and each time, the refusals had been clean, instant, almost easy. But when Voss named the Kharun Glacier, Hazel had gone still in a way Priya had only seen once before, at a memorial service for climbers none of them had ever met, where Hazel had stood at the back and left before the names were read.

There was history in that glacier. And history, in Priya's professional experience, killed more people than weather did.

"Hazel," she said. "You've been quiet longer than usual, and your usual is already a lot. What aren't you saying?"

Hazel looked into the fire. For a moment Priya thought she wouldn't answer. Then, in the flattest voice she owned:

"The money clears Dev's contract. All of it. With margin." She paused. "And I've been on that glacier before."

The circle went still. Even the fire seemed to lean in.

"You never mentioned that," Tomas said carefully.

"There's nothing to mention. Fifteen years ago. Before." Before the blacklist. Before the name became a liability. Before, full stop; Hazel treated the word like a door she kept locked. "I know the approach. I know where the crevasse fields migrate. That's worth something on an eleven-day window, and you all know it, so don't pretend the math is complicated."

"The math isn't complicated," Priya said. "The math is fine. I'm asking about the rest of it."

"The rest of it stays on the glacier." Hazel's voice didn't rise. It never rose. It just got denser, like ice under load. "I'm telling you what I'm willing to carry. You decide what you're willing to carry. That's how this crew works."

"That's how this crew works," Okafor repeated, noncommittal, and reached into his jacket for the folded contract copy he'd been carrying all evening like a live grenade. He smoothed it on his knee. "Then let's talk about what we're carrying. Because I read this thing three times today, and there's a clause in section nine—"

"Okafor." Hazel held out her hand. "Give it here."

He gave it. She read by firelight, lips moving slightly, and Priya watched her arrive at the same paragraph Okafor had flagged, watched her jaw set, watched her read it a second time anyway as though the words might improve on acquaintance. *All materials, documents, data, and artifacts recovered during the course of the operation shall become the sole property of the contracting party.*

"Sole property of Voss," Okafor said. "Whatever we find up there, it's hers. We're not a salvage crew, we're her hands."

"It's standard salvage language," Hazel said.

"It's standard *when you're a salvage crew*," Tomas said. "We're a guiding outfit. Our insurance doesn't cover artifact recovery. Our permits certainly don't."

"Our permits," Hazel said, "are why we're having this conversation by tarp-light in a truck yard instead of in an office." She folded the contract along its existing creases. "Anything else?"

"Yes," Priya said. "One more thing, and then I'll vote and shut up." She set down her tea. "If we take this job, we take it knowing it smells wrong. Not dangerous-wrong — we can price danger, we do it every season. Wrong-wrong. A rival who hasn't spoken to you in fifteen years appears the week your debts peak, offering triple rate for a window nobody sane uses, on a glacier you won't talk about. Either Voss knows something we don't, or Voss wants something we haven't been shown. I'd like to know which before we're roped together above a crevasse field."

Silence. Rain on tarp. Fire settling.

"I asked her," Hazel said. "What the cargo was. She said survey equipment. Data cores, mostly. Weather instruments." A beat. "She looked me in the eye when she said it."

"And?" Priya asked.

"And Sable Voss has always been able to look people in the eye." Hazel turned the contract over in her hands once, twice. "Look. Here's the truth, since we're apparently doing truth tonight. I don't trust her. I trust the money. The money is real — I checked the escrow account myself, it cleared verification this afternoon. Triple rate, half on signature, half on completion. Half on signature clears Dev's contract *tonight*. Not after the job. Tonight."

Dev's head came up. "Tonight?"

"Tonight." Hazel looked at him, and something in her expression cracked open for just a second — the guilt showing through the competence like a seam of blue in white ice. "You've spent three years carrying a contract you signed at nineteen because you were hungry and I couldn't cover the difference. I've carried it too. Every season we turned down work because it might expose you, every border crossing where you rode in the false compartment — that's on me. This job ends it. Or it ends us. I genuinely don't know which."

"That's a hell of a sales pitch," Okafor murmured.

"It's the only honest pitch I have."

Priya studied her. Then the others. Tomas had gone back to warming his hands, but his eyes were distant, working some private calculation — Priya suspected it involved maps, and dates, and a letter-shaped weight she'd noticed him carrying in his jacket pocket for two days now, touching it like a talisman whenever he thought no one was looking. Something else was moving under this crew tonight, something older than the debt. But that was Tomas's to surface, and Priya was a medic, not a priest. She treated what presented.

"Vote, then," she said. "All in favor of taking Voss's contract, knowing it smells wrong, knowing the window is eleven days, knowing the clause gives her everything we find."

Okafor raised his hand first, slowly, theatrically. "Under protest. Recorded protest. I want it minuted that I said the clause was a trap."

"Minuted," Hazel said.

Tomas raised his hand without looking up from the fire. "Under protest also. Different protest. Mine's about the glacier."

Dev's hand shot up so fast he nearly left the crate. Then he caught himself, glanced around, and raised it again slower, trying to look like someone who weighed decisions. "In favor. Obviously. Sorry. In favor."

Four sets of eyes came to Priya.

She thought about the escrow account clearing tonight. About Dev riding in false compartments. About the shape of Hazel's silence around the word *Kharun*, and the letter in Tomas's pocket, and the way the rain sounded like static, like a radio searching for a signal in dead air.

Every instinct she had said walk away. Every number she'd run said they couldn't afford to. That was the trap, she understood suddenly — not the clause, not the window, not Voss. The trap was that the choice had been arranged so that refusing felt like abandoning each other. Whoever built this situation had understood her crew better than she'd like.

"In favor," she said. "Under protest. My protest is that we're all lying to ourselves about why, and I include myself in that."

"Carried," Hazel said quietly. "Five yes, five protests. That's the most honest vote we've ever taken."

She stood, tucked the contract inside her jacket, and stepped back out into the rain to make the call. Through the tarp's plastic window they watched her silhouette at the truck, phone screen lighting her face blue, and Priya found herself reading that face the way she read a patient's: pulse visible at the throat, too fast. Whatever Hazel felt about the voice on the other end of that line, it wasn't relief.

Dev drifted over to the fire, glowing. "Eleven days," he said. "Triple rate. On the Kharun." He said the name the way you'd say a place from a story, a place that happened to other people.

Tomas looked up sharply. "Where'd you hear that name?"

"It's on the contract. Sector designations. K-G-L, Kharun Glacier Lower—" Dev stopped. "Why?"

"No reason," Tomas said, but his hand had gone to his jacket pocket again, pressing flat against whatever lived there, and Priya saw it, and saw that Hazel, returning through the rain with the deal struck and her face closed like a door, saw it too.

Neither of them said anything. The fire cracked. Somewhere up-valley, too far to be thunder and too rhythmic to be anything else, the mountains made a long low sound like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

"Eleven days," Hazel said, sitting back down in the broken chair. "We leave tomorrow at dawn."

* * *

Priya didn't sleep. She never did well the night before a signing-off — her body treated commitment like a symptom. So she was awake, cataloguing the camp's small sounds, when Hazel emerged from her tent at two in the morning and walked out past the dead fire to stand where the valley opened toward the north.

Priya watched her from the gap in her tent flap. Hazel stood a long time, motionless, facing the black wall of the foothills behind which, somewhere, a glacier waited with its eleven-day mouth open. Then she took something from her pocket — small, metallic, catching the starlight — turned it over in her fingers once, and put it away.

An old carabiner. Worn smooth. Stamped with letters Priya couldn't read at this distance but somehow already knew.

Hazel went back to her tent. Priya lay in the dark and listened to the rain turn to sleet, ticking against the fly like a countdown, and told herself the same lie the whole crew had voted into office around the fire:

It's just money.

Up north, under the ice, something that was not money waited with the patience of frozen things.
