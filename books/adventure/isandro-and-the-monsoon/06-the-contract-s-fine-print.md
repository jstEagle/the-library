## 6. The Contract's Fine Print

Okafor read contracts the way other men read scripture: slowly, twice, and with a pencil in hand.

It was a habit from another life, one he didn't talk about. Before the mountains there had been a shipping office in Lagos, a desk with a good lamp on it, and four years of learning that the difference between a fair deal and a ruinous one lived in the subclauses — in the small grey print where honest people stopped reading because they trusted, and dishonest people counted on exactly that. He had signed things in that office he'd spent years paying for. Never again. Now he read everything. Leases, gear warranties, the liability waiver for the ferry across the Sengar gorge that charged extra for livestock but not, curiously, for goats under thirty kilos. Everything.

So when Voss's contract arrived at the outfitter's hall — delivered by a runner who wouldn't meet anyone's eyes and left before the ink was dry on the receipt — Okafor took it to the corner table by the window, where the light was best, and he read it.

All eleven pages.

The first three were standard. Scope of work: salvage and recovery operation, Kharun Glacier, pre-season window. Party of five guides plus client. Voss had written *client* in her own hand over the printed word *employer*, which Okafor noted and did not yet understand. The fourth page covered insurance, or rather the absence of it — Voss carried a policy that covered herself and her two porters and nothing else, a hole in the document you could drive a supply truck through. Page five was payment: triple rate, half up front, half on completion, wired to an account Hazel would name. That number was real. Okafor checked it against the debt schedules taped inside his own footlocker, the ones listing what Dev still owed and to whom, and the arithmetic worked. It worked with room to spare. That alone made him suspicious; money that clean usually had something buried under it.

Pages six through nine were boilerplate. Indemnification, force majeure, arbitration in a jurisdiction Okafor had never heard of and suspected did not have a courthouse. He initialed each page anyway, because that was the ritual, and rituals kept you sharp. You initial the boring pages fast and your eye goes soft, and then on page ten, when the language changes register — when it stops sounding like lawyers and starts sounding like intent — you're already skimming.

Page ten was where the language changed.

*Client shall retain sole and exclusive rights to all recovered materials, including but not limited to documents, instruments, survey data, cartographic records, personal effects, and wreckage of any vessel, aircraft, or expedition equipment encountered during the course of operations.*

Okafor set down his pencil.

He read it again. Then a third time, moving his lips slightly, the way he did when something was wrong and he hadn't yet found its shape. *Recovered materials.* On a salvage run, that meant cargo, scrap, anything of value pulled from the ice. But this wasn't a shipwreck. This was a glacier, and glaciers don't hold cargo. Glaciers hold whatever fell into them and stayed. Ropes. Tents. Instruments. Paper.

*Cartographic records.* *Survey data.*

He thought about the way Voss had stood in the outfitter's hall three days ago, hat dripping monsoon rain onto the floorboards, watching Hazel with those pale unblinking eyes while she named a number so large it was obviously designed to end the conversation. He thought about how she'd said *salvage* without ever saying salvage *of what*. He thought about the route she'd filed — legal, technically, threading the edge of the restricted zone like a needle through cloth — and about how a woman that careful about borders was never careless about clauses.

He turned to page eleven. Signature blocks. Hazel Brightwater, guide of record. Sable Voss, client. And beneath Voss's line, already signed — she'd signed before sending it, which meant the contract was not an offer. It was a fait accompli with a blank space where their consent went.

Okafor clicked his pencil closed and carried the contract across the room to where Hazel sat re-wrapping the tips of her ice axes with fresh tape, working by feel, not looking at her hands, looking at nothing.

"Boss."

"Hm."

"You need to see page ten."

She took the contract without wiping her hands, which left grey tape residue on the corner, and read the clause. Her face did what her face always did, which was almost nothing — a stillness that Okafor had learned over six years to read the way sailors read water. The stillness itself was the information.

"All recovered materials," she said quietly.

"That's the one."

"It means if we find anything up there — anything — it's hers." Hazel set the contract on her knee. "Not shared. Not reviewed. Hers outright."

"Documents too. Survey data. Maps." Okafor kept his voice level, because the crew was within earshot — Dev was oiling the stove two tables away, pretending very hard not to listen, which was Dev's way of listening with his whole body. "On a glacier salvage, boss, paper is the payload. Nobody pays triple rate for scrap metal. She's not hiring us to recover something. She's hiring us to find something, and whatever we find, she owns it before we touch it."

Hazel was quiet for a long moment. Outside, the monsoon came down on the town's corrugated roofs like applause from a crowd that wouldn't stop. Somewhere down the street a mule train was being loaded, bells clanking, men shouting numbers.

"We could strike the clause," Okafor said. "Redline it. Joint custody of anything recovered, review before removal, our names on any chain of custody. She wants this route badly enough to pay triple. She'll negotiate. People don't negotiate for things they can get elsewhere."

"She won't renegotiate," Hazel said.

"How do you know?"

"Because I know Sable Voss." Hazel picked up the contract again, and Okafor watched her thumb move along the edge of page ten, back and forth, once, twice, like she was feeling for a pulse in the paper. "And because of what's not in here. There's no description of the objective. No coordinates. No wreck manifest. A salvage contract that doesn't say what's being salvaged isn't a salvage contract. It's a permission slip."

"So we don't sign."

Hazel looked at him then, and for a second — just a second — she looked tired in a way that had nothing to do with the season. Fifteen years of tired, banked behind her eyes like a fire kept low so it wouldn't be seen from the road.

"The money clears Dev's contract," she said. "All of it. And the back taxes on the permit bonds, and Priya's sister's clinic fees, and your engine rebuild. With margin. With actual margin, Okafor. We haven't had margin since I've known you."

"Money isn't worth signing blind."

"I'm not signing blind. I'm signing with my eyes open." She said it flatly, and that flatness frightened him more than anger would have, because he recognized it. It was the voice of a person describing a thing they've already decided to do and are now only explaining. "Voss knows what she's hunting. Fine. Whatever it is, it's hers by law the moment we sign. But the mountain doesn't care about her contract. The mountain only cares whether we climb well. We go in, we do the work, we come out, and what Voss does with her papers is her business in her jurisdiction that doesn't exist."

"And if what she's hunting is ours?" Okafor heard his own voice drop. "If what's buried in that ice has our name on it?"

Hazel went very still.

It lasted maybe two seconds. To anyone else it would have looked like a woman considering a question. To Okafor it looked like a door slamming somewhere deep in the house, fast, before the draft could reach the rooms where people slept.

"No," she said. "Nothing up there is ours."

She signed.

Okafor watched her do it — watched her take the pen, watch her hand not shake, watch her write *Hazel Brightwater* in the small controlled script she used for documents, different from the loose scrawl of her route notes, as if even her handwriting understood the difference between a map and a cage. She initialed page ten last, deliberately, after all the others, which meant she'd done it with her eyes open, which meant it wasn't oversight. It was consent.

"There," she said, and slid the contract across the table to him. "Courier leaves at dawn. Get it sealed."

Okafor picked it up. The pages felt heavier than paper should feel, the way rope feels heavier after someone falls on it.

"Boss," he said. "One question. Not about the clause."

She waited.

"When we're up there, and we find whatever she paid us to find — and we will find it, because she chose us because we're the ones who find things — what do you want me to do?"

Hazel looked out the window at the rain, at the mule bells, at the grey street running down toward the valley and the border beyond it. When she answered, her voice was the guide voice, the one she used on clients at the start of a route, calm and certain and carrying no information whatsoever.

"You do your job," she said. "That's what I pay you for. That's what she's paying all of us for."

Okafor nodded and took the contract to his bunk to seal it in the waterproof tube, and if his hands moved a little slower than usual, no one commented, because no one was watching, because that was the whole architecture of the crew: everyone saw everything and said nothing, until the day the ice made saying nothing impossible.

That night he lay awake listening to the monsoon hammer the roof and turned the clause over in his mind like a stone with something fossilized inside it. *All recovered materials.* He'd flagged it. He'd done the one thing his old life had taught him to do. And she'd signed anyway, with her eyes open, and told him to do his job, and he had said yes, because the debts were real and the window was eleven days and loyalty, in a crew like theirs, sometimes meant swallowing the thing you knew.

But he was a mechanic, and mechanics know that a machine tells you what's wrong with it long before it fails. A sound. A vibration. A clause in a contract. Something in the drivetrain of this job was already worn past spec, and they were about to load it with everything they had.

Three days later, at the trailhead, as they shouldered packs in the first grey light, Okafor noticed Voss checking her watch against the sunrise and smiling at nothing — the easy smile of a woman whose paperwork had all gone exactly where she wanted it.

And he understood, finally, why she'd written *client* over *employer*.

An employer gives orders. A client buys results.

Whatever Voss had bought, they were already carrying it up the mountain for her.
