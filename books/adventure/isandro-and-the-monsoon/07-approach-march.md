## 7. Approach March

The rain started before dawn and did not stop, which Dev would learn was simply how the foothills said hello.

He'd been awake since four, listening to it hammer the fly of his tent, cataloguing sounds: rain on nylon, rain on leaves, rain filling the gullies below camp until they ran like gutters. At home in Pokhara, monsoon meant closed trails, shuttered teahouses, his mother's roof leaking into the same bucket she'd used for twenty years. Here it meant work. Here it meant eleven days, minus however many it took to walk in.

He'd counted. Everyone had. Nine days of approach if the trails held. Two days of margin that everyone pretended not to think about.

By five he had the stove lit and water heating, because that was the job nobody wanted and the rookie always got, and by the time Hazel crawled out of her tent he'd already brewed her tea exactly the way she took it — black, scalding, no sugar — a detail he'd extracted from Tomas on day two of the trek with the sly patience of a man mining for gold.

"You're up early," Hazel said.

"Couldn't sleep."

"Liar." She took the cup. "Nobody sleeps in monsoon. You just learn to lie in the dark and call it rest." She drank half of it standing, looking at the cloud ceiling where the peaks should have been, and Dev watched her the way he caught himself watching her more and more lately — openly, hungrily, the way he'd once watched the pilots at the airstrip near his village until one of them let him sit in the cockpit and he'd known, absolutely, what he wanted to do with his life.

Except this wasn't flying. This was better. This was Hazel Brightwater, who had guided the Kharun approaches when other guides wouldn't take the contracts, who read terrain the way Priya read symptoms, who had once — Tomas told him this around a fire in week one, and Dev had made him tell it twice — walked a client out of a whiteout on the Chorten La using nothing but slope angle and the sound of meltwater. Fifteen years of it. Fifteen years of miles.

Dev was nineteen years old and had never been farther from home than Kathmandu, and every step of this march felt like being handed something he hadn't earned yet.

* * *

They broke camp at six and climbed out of the river valley through forest so wet it seemed to be drinking. The trail was less a path than an argument between mud and stone, and the mud was winning. Okafor went first with the rope line, testing each root bridge with his boot before committing weight, calling back "solid" or "no" in a flat voice that Dev had learned to trust more than most people's promises. Then came Priya with the medical kit wrapped in three layers of plastic. Then Dev, carrying more than his share and glad of it. Then Tomas, navigating, and Hazel last, because that was where she could see everything.

That was another thing about her. Most guides put themselves at the front. Hazel put herself at the back, where the stragglers were, where the fear lived, and she managed the whole line like a hand on the tiller.

By mid-morning they'd gained the ridge above the valley and the forest opened into leech country. Dev learned about leeches. He learned about them thoroughly, screaming once, quietly, when he found one fat and satisfied behind his knee, and Priya laughed at him without malice and showed him the salt trick, and Okafor said, "First season?"

"Is it obvious?"

"Everything's obvious your first season. That's the good news. By your fifth you'll be pretending nothing's obvious, and that's when you'll actually start learning."

Dev thought about that all morning. He thought about how Hazel never pretended anything wasn't obvious. When the trail crossed a stream swollen past its stepping stones, she didn't bluster through; she stood at the bank and said, plainly, "This is worse than I expected. We rope up." And when Tomas argued that it would cost them an hour, she said, "It will cost us an hour," as if that settled it, and it did settle it, and they roped up, and the crossing took forty minutes instead of ten and nobody went into the current, and Dev understood that this — the unglamorous arithmetic of caution — was what fifteen years of survival looked like. Not heroics. Accounting.

At the midday halt, under a tarp strung between two rhododendron trunks, they ate cold rice and dried meat and Voss's porters passed them without speaking. That was the other thing Dev was still getting used to: Sable Voss traveled separately. She had her own tents, her own cook, her own two porters who answered to her alone, and she appeared at the halts like weather, checked her instruments, spoke to Hazel in short exchanges that sounded civil and weren't, and moved on ahead or fell behind according to some private schedule.

Today she stopped longer than usual. She stood at the edge of the tarp's shelter, rain streaming off her hood, and looked up-valley to where the clouds had torn open for perhaps ninety seconds — long enough to show a wall of rock and ice at the head of the watershed, impossibly high, impossibly far, white going blue going grey.

"The Kharun," Voss said. Not to anyone. To the mountain itself, almost.

And Hazel flinched.

It was small. Dev almost missed it, and would have missed it entirely if he hadn't been watching her, which he always was. It wasn't a jump or a gasp. It was a tightening — her jaw, her shoulders, the hand holding her tea — a single contraction like a muscle remembering an old injury. It lasted maybe a second. Then she said, "Eleven-day window starts when we hit the tongue. Not before. Save your sightseeing," and turned back to the stove, and the moment folded itself away as if it had never happened.

But Dev had seen it. And once seen, it couldn't be unseen.

* * *

The afternoon was a long grind up a spur through bamboo gone feral, and Dev used the hours to conduct a private investigation, because that was another thing about him: he noticed things. His mother said he'd been born noticing things, that as a toddler he'd once stared for an entire afternoon at a crack in their floor and then announced, correctly, that the house was settling unevenly and the east wall would fail within the year. It had. He'd been three.

So he noticed.

He noticed that whenever anyone mentioned the Kharun by name — Tomas reading distances off the map, a porter murmuring about the route, Voss saying it like a man saying a rival's name — Hazel's face did the thing. Not every time. Maybe one time in three. A flicker, a stillness, a half-second where she went somewhere else and came back. He noticed that she never said the mountain's name herself. In nine days of marching he could not remember hearing it leave her mouth once. She called it *the objective*, *the route*, *up there*, *the tongue*, as if naming it might summon it, or as if the name belonged to someone else.

He noticed, too, that on the second night, when a porter asked her directly — politely, the way locals ask, wanting to know if the guide knew the mountain's moods — whether she'd been on the Kharun before, the silence lasted three full seconds too long, and then Hazel said, "Once," and changed the subject with a question about the porter's home valley, and the porter, who was older than her and missed nothing, let her change it.

Dev lay in his tent that night doing what he did with everything now: filing it against the legend. Because that was the trouble with meeting your heroes at eye level. You started measuring them against the story, and the story was flawless, and the person kept producing these hairline fractures — a flinch, a silence, a name unspoken — and you had to decide what the fractures meant. Age? Fatigue? Or something buried deeper, something the story had been built over like a trail built over a crevasse?

He thought about asking Tomas. Tomas was the closest thing the crew had to an elder, and he talked freely about everything except, Dev had begun to notice, certain stretches of Hazel's past. Ask Tomas about the Chorten La and he'd give you forty minutes. Ask him about anything before fifteen years ago and he'd find a reason to check the radio.

Dev decided to wait. Nineteen-year-olds who ask questions get answers, but nineteen-year-olds who wait get better ones.

* * *

On the sixth day the trail left the trees for good and entered the world of stone and water, and everything got harder and more beautiful at the same rate. They climbed beside a river that had come down off the glacier itself, milky-grey with rock flour, cold enough to burn, and the gorge walls rose around them until the sky was a strip of churning grey overhead. Waterfalls came down the walls in ropes and sheets, some falling so far they shattered into mist before reaching bottom, and the trail clung to the rock in ledges barely wide enough for loaded men, slick as glass, with the river roaring below like something impatient.

Hazel worked the ledge sections herself. She stood at the bad corners in the rain, unroped, anchored to pitons she placed and removed, and handed each of them across the exposed moves one at a time, her voice level under the roar: "Weight on the rope, feet where I point, don't look down, look at me." And Dev, crossing, did look at her, and saw a woman utterly consumed by the task, every faculty aimed at the crossing like light through a lens, and thought: *this is what mastery is.* And then, thirty seconds later, watching her coil the rope with quick violent efficiency, thought: *and this is what fear looks like too.*

Because she was fast. Too fast. She moved through the dangerous sections like someone trying to get them over with, and there was a difference — Dev felt it in his bones, the difference a village boy knows between a man walking a cliff path and a man fleeing along one — between efficiency and flight.

That evening they camped on a shelf above the gorge, and the clouds lifted for an hour at sunset, and the Kharun showed itself entire: the great white shoulder of the peak, the icefall spilling from it like a frozen waterfall the size of a city, the glacier tongue reaching down-valley toward them, grey and ancient, cracked into a maze of seracs and crevasses that even from this distance looked like the surface of some ruined moon.

Everyone came out of their tents to look. Even the porters. Even Voss, who stood apart with her binoculars and studied the tongue with an intensity that Dev filed away with everything else.

"It's beautiful," Dev said, to Hazel, because he wanted to hear what she'd say.

She was quiet for a moment. "It's a machine," she said finally. "A very old machine that doesn't know we're here and wouldn't care if it did. Beautiful the way a flood is beautiful. From the right distance."

"And we're going onto it."

"We're going onto it." She said it evenly, but Dev heard the seam in the sentence, the place where the words were joined to something unsaid. Then she looked at him — really looked, the full attention, which she spent rarely and which landed on him like sunlight — and said, "You did well today. The ledges. You kept your head."

Coming from her, it was a decoration. He carried it to his tent like one.

* * *

Day eight brought them to the last village before the ice, a scatter of stone houses on a moraine terrace where the people grew barley in soil that had been a glacier once and would be again. They bought eggs and dried cheese and information. The information was free and grim.

An old woman selling fuel bricks looked at their rope coils and their plastic barrels and asked where they were bound. When Tomas said the glacier, she spat, carefully, to the side, a gesture of respect rather than contempt, and said the mountain was talking already. Talking all night. Her grandfather had herded on the moraines before the war, she said, and he'd taught her that a glacier speaks before it breaks, the way a tree creaks before it falls, and this year it had started talking early.

"When does calving season open?" Tomas asked, meaning the window, the eleven days, the arithmetic.

The old woman looked at him the way you look at someone who has asked the wrong question well. "You're asking the wrong question," she said. "Don't ask when it opens. Ask when it decides to close. The mountain opens the door when it wants and closes it when it wants. Your papers say eleven days. The ice has never read your papers."

Everyone laughed, because it was funny, and everyone stopped laughing quickly, because it was true.

Dev glanced at Hazel. She hadn't laughed at all. She'd gone still again — the flinch, or its cousin — and was looking up-valley at the tongue, visible now beyond the village roofs, a grey tongue of ice lying in its bed of rubble like something sleeping that might not be sleeping.

That night, unable to sleep, Dev heard voices at the fire and got up to fetch water and stopped when he heard his own name.

"—the boy notices things," Voss was saying. Her voice was low, conversational, the voice of someone discussing freight. "He watches her constantly. That's useful or it's dangerous, depending on the week."

"He's a good kid," Okafor said. "Leave him out of whatever this is."

"I'm not *in* anything, mechanic. I'm a client paying triple rate for a legal salvage. Everything else is your guide's history doing the talking." A pause. Rain on canvas. "But you might ask yourself why a woman who won't say the mountain's name agreed to climb it. People do strange things for money. Or for closure. Either way, my contract doesn't care which."

Dev stood in the dark with the water jug going cold in his hands, heart hammering, understanding two things at once. First: that Voss had seen him watching Hazel, had named it aloud, had weighed him like cargo. Second: that the fracture he'd found in the legend was real, and Voss had found it too, and was planning to use it.

He should have felt afraid. He mostly felt protective. He crept back to his tent and lay in the dark listening to the rain ease off for the first time in eight days, and somewhere up-valley, faint under the wind, he heard it — a sound like a giant clearing its throat, deep and grinding, far away and enormous.

The glacier, talking.

In the morning they struck camp in fog and climbed out of the village onto the terminal moraine, and the ground changed underfoot from earth to stone to ice-rubble, and the air changed with it, colder, thinner, smelling of nothing but rock and age. By noon the fog tore open and there it was directly ahead: the tongue, the gate, the blue walls of the icefall rising into cloud.

Hazel stood at the head of the line and looked at it for a long time. Dev watched her profile, waiting for the flinch.

It didn't come. Instead she said, quietly, to no one and everyone: "Camp tonight at the base. Route assessment tomorrow. Eleven days starts when I say it starts."

And Dev, filing it all away — the name she wouldn't speak, the voice Voss had given it, the sound the mountain was making in the night — understood that whatever Hazel Brightwater was walking toward, it wasn't salvage.

It was a reckoning.
