## 9. Base Camp at the Tongue

The glacier tongue announced itself before we saw it — a sound like a giant clearing its throat somewhere under the earth, low and wet and patient. Dev heard it first and asked if it was thunder. Priya said no. Okafor said nothing, which from Okafor was its own kind of answer.

I'd been on eleven glaciers in my life and every one of them had a voice. The Kharun's was the worst I'd ever heard: not the clean crack of alpine ice but something older, waterlogged, a monsoon-fed beast digesting itself. We came around the last shoulder of moraine at four in the afternoon and there it was, the tongue spilling out of the valley mouth like something poured rather than placed, gray-green with embedded rock, steaming faintly where the rain hit it.

"Base camp here," Hazel said, and pointed at a gravel bench maybe four hundred meters back from the ice margin. Far enough that a calving wave wouldn't reach us. Close enough that we could be at the gate in twenty minutes when the route opened.

Nobody argued. Nobody ever argued with Hazel about camp placement; it was one of the few subjects on which her authority went completely unchallenged, even by Voss, who had spent the entire approach march finding new ways to say *faster* without actually saying it.

Voss stood apart, looking up-valley, and I watched her the way I'd been watching her since the outfitter's hall — not because I distrusted her competence, which was real, but because I distrusted what she wanted. There is a difference between a client who pays for a route and a client who has already decided what she'll find at the end of it. Sable Voss walked like a woman reading a document she'd memorized years ago.

We pitched camp in the last hour of light. Four tents for us, two for Voss and her porters, kitchen fly strung between boulders, radio mast lashed to an old survey pole someone had planted in the gravel decades ago — the metal corroded green, the base ringed by stones arranged in a pattern too deliberate to be natural. Some other expedition's ghost furniture. The Kharun collected ghosts the way rivers collected silt.

By the time the stove was lit and the first pot of dal was on, the rain had settled into its evening rhythm, that steady drumming against the fly that you stopped hearing after a while and only felt, a pressure on the whole world. Dev ate like a boy who'd discovered food. Priya checked his hands for frostnip out of pure habit even though it wasn't cold enough yet, not really, not by the standards of what was coming. Okafor cleaned the stove jet twice, then a third time, which meant he was worried about something he hadn't said aloud.

And Hazel sat at the edge of the kitchen fly with her back to the glacier, drinking tea, watching the dark come down over the foothills behind us instead of the ice ahead of us.

I knew why. I'd known for six days, since the letter arrived, folded inside my gear duffel where the courier had left it in the outfitter town — addressed to me, because Tomas Reyes was a name that could receive mail without consequences, while Hazel Brightwater's name made officials sit up and check their files. The envelope had been heavy stock, cream-colored, and pressed into the wax seal was a crest I would have recognized anywhere: a mountain above three stars, the Brightwater mark, stamped into my life again after fifteen years of silence.

I had carried it through the checkpoint. Through the foothills. Through every night of the approach march with it sitting against my spine like a second knife. And every day I told myself I'd give it to her tomorrow, and every tomorrow I found a reason — the border crossing, the river ford, the storm building in the west — until the reasons ran out tonight, because tomorrow the route opened, and once we were on the ice there would be no more letters, no more chances, nothing but the mountain and whatever it was she'd come here to find or avoid.

The ice groaned. A long, tectonic complaint, rising through the ground and up through our boots and the folding stools and the bones of everyone sitting there. Dev flinched. Priya put her spoon down and picked it back up.

"It does that," Hazel said. "All night. You'll sleep through it by day three."

"You've been here before," Voss said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I've read everything written about this glacier." Hazel turned her cup slowly on her knee. "That's different."

"It isn't," Voss said, and smiled her thin smile, and got up to check her tent lines, and left the sentence hanging there like a hook in water.

* * *

Later — past ten, when the porters were asleep and Okafor had banked the stove and the only light was the blue flame of the lantern turned down to a whisper — I found Hazel outside the fly, standing in the rain without her hood up, looking at the tongue.

You couldn't see much of it at night. Just a paler darkness, a shape where shapes shouldn't be, and occasionally the glint of a serac catching some stray reflection. But she stared at it the way people stare at graves.

"Tomas," she said, without turning. She always knew my footsteps. Fifteen years of walking behind someone teaches you their gait the way it teaches you their silences. "If you're going to tell me the radio mast needs re-guying, it can wait until morning."

"It's not the mast."

She didn't turn around. The rain ticked against her shell jacket. Somewhere up-valley the ice shifted, a sound like a door being dragged open in another room of an enormous house.

"In the outfitter town," I said, "a courier brought mail. One piece. It was addressed to me, but it wasn't for me."

Now she turned. Slowly. In the spill of lantern light from the fly her face was half-lit, and I watched her understand, watched it move across her features like weather — first confusion, then arithmetic, then the thing underneath, the thing she'd been carrying longer than I'd been carrying the envelope.

"The crest," she said.

"Yes."

"And you waited six days to tell me."

"I waited six days to find the right moment, and then I ran out of moments, so now it's just this one." I took the envelope out of my jacket, inside a plastic bag, dry as the day it came. Held it out. "It's unopened. I didn't read it. I want you to know that. Whatever else happened, I never—"

"You hid it from me for six days."

"Yes."

The word sat between us. She looked at the envelope the way she'd look at a cornice she suspected was loaded — assessing, weighing, deciding whether to cross beneath it or turn back entirely.

"Who sent it?" Her voice was very level. Level the way ice is level right before it isn't.

"There's no return address. But the paper, the seal — Hazel, it's family stock. That's your father's stationer. I'd bet the contract debt on it."

"My father is dead."

"He's *missing*. There's a difference, and you taught me that difference yourself, fifteen years ago, when you wouldn't let anyone file the final paperwork." I pushed the envelope toward her again. "Someone with access to his things wrote to you. Someone who knew you'd be on this glacier eventually, or who wanted you here. Either way — you should read it before we go up. Not after. Before."

For a long time she didn't move. The rain fell. The glacier spoke its long slow language underneath everything, and I thought — not for the first time — that the Kharun sounded less like ice than like a stomach, something alive and enormous turning over in its sleep, and that everything we were about to do was happening inside it.

Then Hazel took the envelope.

I exhaled. I don't know what I expected — tears, anger, the story of 2009 finally dragged into the lantern light where the rest of us had been circling it for weeks. She held it in both hands, turned it over once, thumb brushing the wax seal, the mountain and the three stars, and her face did something I had never seen it do in fifteen years of partnership: it went completely blank. Not calm. Blank. The face of a woman standing in a doorway deciding whether to enter a burning building or let it burn.

Then she walked to the stove.

"Hazel—"

She lifted the lid, fed the envelope in, and set the seal on top of it, and we both watched the cream paper curl and blacken and go to ash among the coals. The wax bubbled, ran, and the mountain-and-stars softened into a shapeless red bead and then into nothing. It took maybe forty seconds. She kept the lid half-open the whole time, watching, making sure.

When it was done she closed the stove and stood there with her hand flat on the warm metal, and she still hadn't turned around, and I realized I was looking at armor. Not the absence of fear — the engineering of it. Fifteen years of it, load-bearing, riveted, and I had just handed her a letter that threatened the whole structure, and she had burned it the way you'd blow a bridge behind you.

"You didn't even read it," I said. My voice came out rougher than I intended.

"No."

"What if it mattered? What if it was—" I stopped, because I almost said *him*, and saying it aloud felt like handing her a second letter to burn. "What if it changed something?"

"Nothing changes." Now she turned, and her eyes in the lantern light were dry and absolutely steady, and that steadiness frightened me more than any breakdown would have. "He's dead, Tomas. He's been dead for fifteen years, whatever the paperwork says. Dead men don't write letters. Somebody found his old stationery and decided to play a game, and I'm not playing. Tomorrow we go up, we do the salvage, we clear Dev's contract, and we come home. That's the whole expedition. That's all it is."

"All it is," I repeated.

"That's right."

The glacier groaned again, longer this time, a descending moan that ended in a crack sharp enough that both of us looked up-valley instinctively, searching the dark for movement, for the white flash of a serac letting go. Nothing moved. Or nothing we could see.

"Fifteen years," I said quietly, "you've measured everything in miles. Distance from the blacklist. Distance from the inquiry. Distance from him. And now we're standing at the one place on earth where the miles run out, and you're telling me it's *all it is*."

Hazel looked at me for a long moment. Rain ran off the brim of her hood. When she spoke, her voice was almost gentle, which was how I knew I'd gone too far.

"Get some sleep, Tomas. Day one starts at first light, and the gate doesn't stay open for sentiment."

She went into her tent and zipped it, and the sound of the zipper was very small and very final, and I stood alone in the rain beside the stove that still held the ashes of the only letter her family had sent in fifteen years.

* * *

I didn't sleep either, not really. I lay in my bag listening to the glacier work through the night — the groans coming at irregular intervals now, sometimes minutes apart, sometimes stacked three and four together like a man muttering in escalating argument — and around two in the morning I gave up, dressed, and stepped out to check the mast lines.

That was when I saw Voss.

She was awake, standing at the edge of camp with a headlamp off, just a silhouette against the faint gray suggestion of the tongue, and she was holding something flat in both hands, angled to catch what little light the overcast sky offered. Paper. A sheet of paper, large, and she was studying it with the absolute absorption of someone reading scripture.

I should have called out. Camp protocol, bear-country habit, plain courtesy — any of them would have justified it. Instead I stood in the shadow of the supply tent and watched, because fifteen years of running had taught me that the things people study at two in the morning are the true cargo of any expedition, and I was tired of being the man who learned things six days late.

She turned the paper slightly. For half a second the sky-light caught its surface, and I saw lines. Contour lines. And marks — small, dense, clustered in one region the way flies cluster on a wound.

Then she rolled it, tucked it inside her jacket, and went back to her tent without a sound, and the glacier groaned, and I stood in the dark doing arithmetic I didn't like: coordinates, restricted zone, a client who'd paid triple rate for a *salvage* run and studied her own map at two in the morning like a woman checking a promise.

Behind me, Hazel's tent was silent. Ahead of us, the gate would open at first light.

I went back to my bag and lay down and listened to the ice, and for the first time in fifteen years of following Hazel Brightwater into bad places, I was afraid of what we'd find more than I was afraid of the finding.
