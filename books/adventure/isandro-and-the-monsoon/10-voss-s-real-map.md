## 10. Voss's Real Map

Priya counted sutures the way other people counted sheep.

It was a habit from residency, back when she'd had a residency to lose — thirty of this size, twenty of that, gut packed in wax paper, needles sealed against the damp. A medic's inventory was a prayer you made with your hands: *let us not need these. Let us not need any of these.* She'd done it every night since base camp went up at the tongue of the Kharun, and every night the count came out the same, and every night she told herself the same lie — that a complete kit meant a safe trip.

The ice under the tent floor groaned like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

She zipped the med bag shut and lay back on her pad. Across the dome, Tomas was already asleep, breathing slow, one arm flung over his eyes. Dev had gone out an hour ago to check the anchor line above camp and hadn't come back; Hazel had gone after him without a word, which was its own kind of data. Okafor was in the gear tent with the radio, because Okafor was always in the gear tent with the radio, coaxing signal out of the sky like a man trying to sweet-talk a mule.

And Voss.

Voss's tent sat apart from theirs, upwind, the way she did everything — positioned, deliberate, a woman who treated even her sleeping arrangements as statements. Sable Voss did not snore. Priya would have bet money on it. Sable Voss probably slept the way she climbed: efficiently, silently, and slightly better than everyone else.

Priya closed her eyes.

She lasted four minutes.

* * *

There were things about this job that had been bothering her since the fire circle, and she was a doctor, so she made lists.

One: Voss had named a price before naming a route. Triple rate, offered flat, no haggling, to a crew she'd spent fifteen years calling second-rate. People didn't overpay for things they wanted done cheaply. They overpaid for things they wanted done quietly.

Two: the salvage manifest. Priya had read it twice by lantern light while the others argued about the vote. It listed equipment caches, a weather station, "miscellaneous alloy wreckage." Miscellaneous. Nobody paid triple rate for miscellaneous.

Three: Voss watched Hazel. Not the way rivals watch each other — not the scoreboard glance, the *how's your pace, Brightwater*. She watched Hazel the way Priya watched a patient whose labs had come back wrong but not yet diagnostic. Waiting for something specific. Waiting for a number.

Four — and this was the one she kept circling back to — Voss never asked about the route ahead. Every client Priya had ever carried asked about the route ahead. *How bad is the icefall? Where do we sleep tomorrow? What happens if the window closes early?* Voss asked about the route behind. About what was under them. About how deep the ice went, and whether the moraine moved in summer, and once — casually, over tea, as if she were asking about the weather — how far down Priya thought the 2009 debris field might have been carried before the glacier swallowed it.

Priya had answered honestly, because there was no reason not to: two, maybe three kilometers down-glacier, folded into who knew which layer. And she had watched something move across Voss's face that wasn't curiosity.

It was confirmation.

So when Priya unzipped her tent at half past eleven, telling herself she was just going to check on the stove fuel, and found herself instead moving upwind through the blue dark toward the tent that sat apart — well. She was a doctor. She believed in following symptoms to their source.

Voss's tent glowed faintly from within. The lamp inside was turned low, but not off, and the shadow it threw against the nylon was sharp-edged and still. Working late. Of course she was.

Priya crouched beside the vestibule, meaning only to listen, and heard nothing — no voice, no radio, no rustle. Just the glacier underneath everything, that long tectonic sigh, and beneath it a sound so small she almost missed it: paper. The dry whisper of a page turning.

Nobody brought paper on a salvage run. Paper was dead weight. Everything lived on screens now, on Okafor's battered tablets and laminated route cards, because paper soaked and froze and tore. Paper was for people who needed to hold something in their hands.

She should have gone back to her tent. She knew that even then. There was a version of this night where she stood up, walked away, and let whatever Voss was doing stay Voss's business until it detonated on its own schedule. That was the smart version. That was the version that kept a crew small and legal and invisible.

But Priya had lost her license keeping her mouth shut about a surgeon's mistake, and she had learned — expensively, publicly, permanently — that the thing you don't say is the thing that comes back for you.

She waited until the shadow shifted away from the door seam, then lifted the vestibule flap.

* * *

Sable Voss sat cross-legged over a map, and she did not look up.

That was the first strange thing. An intruder lifting your tent flap at midnight should have produced a reaction — a hand to a knife, a curse, at minimum a startled breath. Instead Voss simply said, "Shut it behind you. You're letting the cold in," in the tone of a woman who had expected company, though perhaps not this company, at perhaps not exactly this hour.

Priya shut it behind her.

The map was old. That registered immediately, professionally, the way she noticed a wound's age before its severity. It wasn't a print — it was hand-drawn, ink on drafting film, the linework fine and confident, annotated in a cramped vertical script that reminded Priya of nothing so much as engineering notes. Contour lines dense as wood grain. A glacier rendered in loving, obsessive detail, its crevasse fields stippled in blue, its serac zones hatched in red.

And coordinates. Dozens of them, plotted in pencil over the ink, each one circled, each one numbered, a sequence running up the glacier's spine like beads on a string.

"Restricted zone," Priya said.

"Yes."

"The whole upper mountain's been closed since 2009. You know that. Everyone knows that. The permits we're working under don't cover—" She stopped, because Voss had finally looked up, and the look stopped the sentence in her throat.

It wasn't guilt. Priya had seen guilt often enough to know its weather — the flicker, the recalibration, the small sweat of being caught. This was none of those. This was patience. Voss looked at her the way she'd look at a piece of gear that had performed adequately: noted, filed, not especially interesting.

"You're the observant one," Voss said. "Tomas navigates, Okafor fixes, the boy climbs, and Brightwater leads. You're the one who watches. I've been trying to decide if that makes you dangerous or useful."

"And?"

"I haven't decided." She turned back to the map. "You can stand there or you can sit down. Either way, you're going to look at this, because you've already seen too much of it to unsee."

Priya didn't sit. She leaned over the map instead, and the lamp threw her shadow across it, and she made herself read it properly the way she'd read a chart — top to bottom, assuming nothing, trusting the numbers.

The drafting film was yellowed at the edges. The ink had faded unevenly, darker where a hand had rested while writing. In the lower corner, a title block: *KHARUN GLACIER — APPROACH AND UPPER ROUTE — SURVEY 2008–09.* Below that, a signature.

*B. Meridian.*

No. Not quite. Priya bent closer. The signature had been written twice, or rather, written once and corrected — the first attempt scraped away with a blade, the second written over the scar. *B. Meridian,* it read now, clean and official. But under the scrape, if you knew to look, the ghost of different letters. A longer word. A name that started with *B* and kept going.

Brightwater.

Her mouth went dry. "This is the original survey."

"It's a survey," Voss said mildly.

"This is *the* survey. The one they used in '09. The one the inquiry—"

"The inquiry saw copies," Voss said. "Certified copies, stamped, filed, very tidy. The originals were supposed to have been lost with the expedition." Her fingertip came down on one of the penciled circles, high on the glacier where the contours crowded together into a wall of blue hatch marks. "They weren't lost. They were cached. Standard practice — you cache your master documents below the technical section in case you need to retreat to them. Six people died following this line, Doctor, and the man who drew it cached his own handwriting forty meters from where they turned onto it."

The tent felt smaller. The glacier's groan rose through the floor and up through Priya's knees, and for a moment she couldn't tell whether the shaking was the ice or her.

"How do you have this?"

"Does it matter?"

"It matters enormously. If this is real, it's evidence. It's the single most important document in the ugliest mountaineering scandal of the century, and you're using it as a *map*, at midnight, in a restricted zone, on my crew's trip—"

"Your crew," Voss repeated. Something moved at the corner of her mouth — not quite a smile. "Yes. I suppose it is."

She reached past Priya and turned the film over. On the reverse, in fresher ink — recent ink, Priya realized, ink that hadn't faded at all — someone had written a list. Coordinates again, but fewer, refined, each one annotated in that same cramped vertical hand:

*Cache site — probable depth 2.1m, melt-lag adjusted.*
*Tent platform — bearing confirmed.*
*M. — last position per radio log, 0417.*

M. Priya stared at it. M. Meridian. The expedition's last radio transmission had come from the deputy leader, Marisol — no. Priya dragged the name up from fifteen years of news coverage she'd absorbed the way everyone had, unwillingly, in fragments. *Meridian.* The expedition had renamed itself for the survey firm. Before that, in the older articles, the ones from the search period, it had another name. The family name.

The name on the tent at base camp. The name Hazel flinched away from whenever the mountain said it aloud.

"You're not salvaging equipment," Priya said quietly.

"No."

"You're looking for bodies."

Voss set down the pencil she'd been holding. For a moment — just a moment — the composure shifted, the way ice shifts before it moves, and Priya saw something underneath that was older and rawer than ambition. Then it sealed over, smooth as the film on the map.

"I'm looking for the truth," Voss said. "The bodies are where the truth lives. That's not sentiment; it's logistics. Elian Brightwater falsified his route data. He moved the fixed lines on paper to a line nobody had climbed, so he could claim the first ascent of the north pillar, and he sent six people up a face he'd never surveyed, based on elevations he invented at a desk in Kathmandu. The inquiry proved negligence. It never proved forgery, because the proof was in the cache, and the cache was in the ice, and everyone decided the ice could keep it." She tapped the map, once, hard. "I'm not everyone."

"Why now? Why this way? Why hire *her*?" The question came out louder than Priya intended, and she saw it land — saw it land somewhere Voss hadn't armored. "You've had fifteen years and apparently the original survey. You could have taken anyone. You took the blacklisted daughter of the man who—"

"Because she'll get me there." Flat. Final. But then Voss exhaled, and something in her voice dropped half a register, and for three seconds she sounded less like a rival and more like a woman who had been carrying a weight so long she'd forgotten it wasn't normal. "Because when we find it, she needs to be standing next to it. Do you understand? Anyone else finds that cache, it's a story. *Hazel Brightwater* finds it — it's a verdict. The world will believe it coming out of her mouth, or it will destroy her trying not to. Either way, the lie dies in front of the only person alive with a right to grieve it."

The lamp hissed softly. Somewhere outside, Dev's laugh floated up from the anchor line — distant, young, oblivious — and Hazel's voice answering, low and steady, and Priya thought: *she's down there teaching him knots while this woman plans to dig up her father's corpse.*

"You're going to break her," Priya said.

"I'm going to *free* her," Voss said, and the conviction in it was the most frightening thing yet. "There's a difference. It just doesn't look like one from inside."

Priya straightened. Her hands had gone cold, and she tucked them into her armpits like a child, and hated herself for it, and did it anyway. "What happens to my crew? When you find what you're looking for. Are we in the verdict, or are we collateral?"

Voss considered her for a long moment. Outside, the wind picked up, worrying the tent fabric, and the shadows swung across the map like a searching hand.

"You're the one who watches," Voss said finally. "So watch. And when the time comes, ask yourself which you'd rather have — the version of this where Brightwater gets her name back clean, or the version where she gets it back at all. Because I promise you, Doctor, you won't get both."

Priya ducked out into the dark.

* * *

The cold hit her like water. She stood between the tents with her arms wrapped around herself, breathing, watching her breath, counting — ten counts in, ten counts out, the oldest trick she knew, the one she taught panicked climbers pinned on exposed ledges. *You are here. You are breathing. The mountain has not yet eaten you.*

Below, near the anchor line, Hazel's headlamp swung toward her. Even at eighty meters, even through the wind, Priya could feel the moment Hazel registered her — the stillness, the slight tilt of the head, the leader's instinct cataloguing a crew member standing alone in the dark at midnight looking, presumably, exactly like what she was: a woman who had just learned something she couldn't unlearn.

Hazel started toward her.

Priya thought about Voss's map. About the scraped-away signature. About *M. — last position per radio log, 0417.* About a father seated in the ice for fifteen years with his lies around him like tent walls, waiting for either his daughter or his accuser to arrive, and no way yet to know which one Priya would be helping dig him out for.

Hazel stopped three meters away and pushed back her hood. Wind pulled at her hair. Up close her face was tired in the old way, the permanent way, the way of someone who had been carrying a specific weight so long she'd mistaken it for her skeleton.

"You couldn't sleep either," Hazel said.

"No," Priya said.

Hazel waited. That was the thing about her — she never pried, never pressed, just left space the size of whatever you needed to put in it. Fifteen years of leading people who all had reasons to hide, and she'd built the leaving-of-space into something like grace.

Priya opened her mouth.

And closed it.

Because here was what she knew, standing in the wind with the truth sitting in her chest like a swallowed stone: whatever Hazel suspected, she was surviving on the suspicion. The not-knowing was load-bearing. Priya had seen it in patients a hundred times — the family member who wouldn't ask the oncologist the question, because as long as the question stayed unasked, both futures were still possible, and a person could live in both futures, briefly, the way a climber lives on both sides of a cornice.

If Priya spoke, the cornice would drop.

"Get some sleep," Hazel said at last, gently. "Big day tomorrow. We move to Camp One."

She turned back toward the anchor line, toward Dev, toward the small warm circle of lamplight and rope and trust, and Priya watched her go, and the stone turned over.

Tomorrow they would climb higher. Tomorrow they would climb toward the coordinates circled in pencil on a dead man's map, following a client who had hired them for a lie, to dig up a truth that would either save Hazel Brightwater or bury her — and Priya would have to decide, on the walk up, which side of that she was standing on.

Behind her, Voss's lamp went out.

In the sudden full darkness, the glacier groaned again — long, deep, almost articulate — and Priya stood alone in the cold and understood, with total certainty, that they were not the first people to hear it and answer.
