## 12. What the Ice Keeps

The icefall didn't care that it was Day Two.

Dev had learned that much in the first hour. The Kharun's lower gate was a kilometer of blue chaos — seracs the size of houses leaning against each other like drunks, crevasses splitting the route into a maze of ladders and fixed lines and prayer. Every sound was an argument: the tick of settling ice, the far-off boom of something giving up somewhere out of sight, meltwater running underneath everything like a pulse.

He climbed behind Priya, tied to Okafor, tied to Tomas, Hazel on point with Voss's two porters trailing. Five ropes, six people plus Voss, moving through a place that wanted them dead in maybe forty different ways.

"Short steps," Hazel called back, her voice flat and even, the voice she used for everything technical. "Test every rung. The ladders lie."

The ladders did lie. Dev found that out at the second crossing, when an aluminum rung rotated under his boot with a sound like a knuckle cracking and dropped him to his waist before his axe bit and Okafor hauled him back with one arm, barely looking up from the carabiner he was re-racking.

"You okay?" Okafor asked.

"Yeah." His heart said otherwise.

"You're not okay," Okafor said mildly. "Nobody's okay here. That's fine. Just keep moving."

That was the thing about Okafor. He never lied to you about how bad something was, which somehow made it less bad.

* * *

They'd been on the glacier for two days now, and Dev had started keeping a private count of things Hazel did that made no sense to him and perfect sense to everyone else.

She drank water on a schedule, whether she was thirsty or not. She checked Tomas's hands for frostnip during breaks without asking permission, just took them, turned them over, grunted. She never looked at the summit — Dev had caught himself staring up through gaps in the seracs at the white shoulder of the peak above, and then noticed that Hazel never once followed his gaze. She watched the ice under their feet and the sky over their heads and nothing else, as if the mountain itself were someone she'd stopped speaking to.

And she counted. Under her breath, constantly. Steps, minutes, meters of rope, knots per hour of wind. Dev had asked Priya about it the first night at base camp.

"Hazel counts because numbers don't leave," Priya had said, and then zipped her bag shut like the conversation was over.

Now, mid-morning of Day Two, they came to the long traverse.

It was the ugliest section yet: a shelf of ice cut diagonally across a wall, maybe forty centimeters wide where it was generous, carved by some previous season's traffic and half-melted since. Below it, a crevasse opened its mouth — not the narrow slots they'd been stepping over but a real one, wide enough that when Dev leaned out and looked down, the light went blue and then went out, and his stomach performed a slow revolution.

"Rope up tight on this," Hazel said. "No slack. If anyone goes, the rest of you drop and dig in. Nobody plays hero."

"I'm always the hero," Voss said from the rear, and her porters laughed, and Hazel didn't.

Dev went third. The traverse was worse than it looked, which he hadn't thought possible. The footing was candle-wax ice, polished by sun, and the wall on his left pressed close enough that his pack scraped it with every step. He kept his eyes down, on his boots, on the next placement, the way Hazel had drilled into him on approach marches: *the mountain is only ever one hold big.*

Which is why he saw it.

His boot slipped — not badly, a skid of two centimeters — and he swung his axe into the wall to steady himself, and the pick sank into old snow-ice softer than the rest, and a plate of it calved away under the blow and fell.

Not into the crevasse. Against it. It caught on a lip three meters down, teetered, and hung there, and Dev, flat against the wall with his heart slamming, looked down past his own boots to see what the falling ice had uncovered.

Fabric.

At first he thought it was rock — a band of darker material running along the crevasse wall, exposed where the surface had melted back. But rock didn't have a weave. Rock didn't fray. This was a rope, frozen into the wall at an angle, running down into the blue dark, and it was red once, he could tell, though the decades had bleached it to the color of dried blood and sun-rotted canvas. It emerged from the ice, crossed a gap of bare wall, and vanished again, stitched through the glacier like a vein.

And beside it, snagged where the rope kinked, a carabiner.

Even rusted — and it was rusted, orange-brown blooms spreading across the metal like mold — Dev knew the shape. Oval-gate, old style, the kind you saw in museum cases and in the gear closet at the outfitter's hall, hanging there as a joke nobody explained. The gate was jammed open. The metal was pitted almost through in places.

Stamped on the spine, small enough that he had to squint, the letters were still legible:

**BW**

Dev hung there on the traverse and forgot, entirely, how to breathe.

* * *

"Brightwater," he'd heard people say it his whole life, the way you say a weather event or a war. He'd grown up in the valley towns below the Kharun range, where every outfitter's wall had the same photograph: eleven climbers in matching red shells, squinting into a camera, and Elian Brightwater in the center with his arm around a man who died a week later. *The Last Great Expedition*, the caption said. 2009. Six dead, the summit unclaimed, the maps legendary.

And Hazel Brightwater — though nobody in the valley towns said *Hazel*. They said *her father's daughter*, or they said nothing at all, or they said things Dev had been too young to understand until he was old enough to understand them.

He'd signed onto her crew two years ago knowing exactly one fact about her past, the fact everyone knew: blacklisted from two countries, disgraced by association, gone. What he'd learned since was the person underneath the legend — the woman who counted steps, who checked hands for frostnip, who'd spent an entire evening teaching him prusik knots with a length of scrap rope while the rain hammered the tarps, patient as tide.

She had never once mentioned the expedition. Not the name, not the year, not the mountain they were standing on. When Voss had walked into the outfitter's hall with her triple-rate offer, Dev had watched Hazel's face do something he'd never seen it do — go completely empty, like a room with the lights switched off — and then come back on, and she'd named a price, and that was all.

Now he was hanging on a wall of ice above a piece of her family's gear, rusted into a crevasse on the exact route they were climbing, and he understood, suddenly and completely, that this was not a coincidence.

Glaciers moved. Glaciers swallowed things high on their flanks and carried them down and gave them up decades later, chewed and rearranged, in moraines and meltwater and walls of blue ice. Everyone knew that. It was why salvage crews existed. It was why Voss was paying triple rate.

But knowing it as a fact and seeing it were different animals. Seeing it was standing three meters above a dead man's rope with your axe buried in the ice that was keeping it.

"Dev." Priya's voice, low, from ahead. "Move. You're blocking the line."

He moved. His body did it on its own — axe, step, axe, step — while his mind stayed down in the blue dark with the frayed red rope. At the end of the traverse there was a stance, a bulge of ice wide enough for two, and he clipped in and stood there shaking in a way he couldn't blame on the cold.

Priya arrived thirty seconds later, clipped in beside him, breathing hard. She glanced at him, then did a double take.

"What's wrong?"

"Nothing."

"Dev."

"I slipped," he said. "On the traverse. I'm fine."

She studied him the way she studied wounds — quickly, thoroughly, deciding whether it needed stitching or just cleaning. Whatever she saw, she decided against stitches.

"We'll talk tonight," she said, and unclipped, and moved on.

* * *

The rest of the day was a blur of blue and white and the metronome of Hazel's counting. They gained four hundred meters through the icefall, fixed two more pitches of rope, and established Camp One on a shelf of compacted snow wedged between two seracs that had been leaning on each other, according to Tomas, since before Dev was born.

Dev pitched his tent with hands that worked fine and a head that wouldn't stop.

Because here was the thing he kept circling: the rope was old. Older than old. The fabric had that bleached, brittle look of things that had been through fifteen years of summers — and he knew what fifteen years of summers did to nylon, because Okafor had made him retire his own first rope at five years with a lecture about UV degradation that had lasted forty minutes. That rope in the wall wasn't fifteen years old. It was older. It might be *the* age.

2009. The Brightwater expedition. The last known position of eleven climbers, somewhere inside the restricted zone above the icefall — the zone they were now climbing into, the zone Voss's map was marked with, the map Priya had seen and shown Hazel and shown him, the coordinates that matched a disaster everyone in the valley towns could recite like scripture.

The rope was evidence. Of what, he couldn't say. A retreat line? A rescue attempt? Somebody's last anchor? The crevasse had it now either way, keeping it the way the glacier kept everything — without malice, without mercy, just keeping.

And he had said nothing.

He'd stood right there, three meters from it, with Hazel twenty meters behind him on the same traverse, close enough to call out to, and he had said *nothing*, and now Camp One was going up around him and the moment was closing like water over a stone.

Why? He turned it over while he cooked his ration, while the others talked route logistics in the mess tent, their voices muffled by nylon. Because it wasn't his to say? Because pointing at a rusted carabiner and saying *look* felt like opening a door in someone's house that they'd clearly nailed shut? Because he'd seen Hazel's face in the outfitter's hall, that switched-off emptiness, and some animal part of him had known — had *known* — that the BW stamp would switch it off again, and worse?

Or because of Voss. Because Voss was somewhere in this camp right now, and anything Dev surfaced, Voss would surface faster, and Dev had read enough of the contract — Okafor had read it to him twice, slowly — to understand that Voss owned *all recovered materials*. All of them. Whatever that meant. Whatever they turned out to be.

If the ice was full of Brightwater, and Voss had bought the rights to whatever the ice gave up, then every rusted artifact on this glacier was already hers. Legally. In writing.

Dev sat in his tent and listened to the icefall talk to itself in the dark — those deep, arrhythmic booms, like something enormous turning over in sleep — and tried to figure out what a good person would do.

Tell Hazel. Obviously. Tell Hazel, who led him across rotten ladders and never let him carry more than his share, who taught him knots in the rain. Tell Hazel, and let her decide what the rope meant and what to do about it.

Except.

Except that telling Hazel meant telling her that the mountain she wouldn't look at was full of her father's gear. That the disaster she'd built fifteen years of miles to outrun was embedded in the very route she was leading them up, stitched through the ice like a vein, and there was no climbing past it. There was only through.

And except that Dev had a debt. A contract debt, the ugly kind, the kind that had made him say yes to this job when every other member of the crew got to vote like it was a choice. Voss held his paper. Voss had made sure Hazel knew it. And if Dev started surfacing things from the ice — things Voss had paid to own — whose side did that put him on? Whose crew was he actually on, when the man holding his contract and the woman holding his trust pointed in different directions?

He hated himself a little for doing the arithmetic. Hazel counted steps; apparently counting was contagious.

* * *

Near midnight, he gave up on sleep and crawled out into the cold. The sky had cleared after sundown and the stars were brutal, close, the Milky Way a river of crushed glass from horizon to horizon. The seracs around camp caught the starlight and threw it back faintly blue, and the whole icefall lay quiet in a way that felt temporary, like a held breath.

A light was burning in the mess tent.

He ducked inside expecting Tomas, who slept badly at altitude, and found Hazel instead, alone, sitting cross-legged with a map board on her knees and a headlamp angled low. She looked up.

"Can't sleep?"

"No, ma'am."

"Don't ma'am me, it makes me feel old." She shifted, making room. "Sit. Have tea. Tomas's stash, don't tell him."

He sat. The tea was terrible and hot and he wrapped both hands around the cup and didn't drink. Hazel went back to the map, tracing tomorrow's line with a gloved finger, and Dev watched her and felt the secret sitting in his chest like a swallowed stone.

She looked tired, he realized. Not altitude-tired. Something else. There was a way she had of looking at the route ahead that reminded him of a person reading a letter they already know is bad news.

"The traverse today," she said, without looking up. "You slipped."

Heat climbed his neck. "I recovered."

"You recovered well. Good axe placement." She tapped the map. "But you froze at the stance after. Thirty seconds, maybe forty. Long enough that Priya noticed, and Priya doesn't notice anything she doesn't decide to notice." Now she looked up, and her eyes in the lamplight were steady and unhurried. "What happened?"

The truth rose in his throat like water in a crevasse, blue and rising, and he opened his mouth —

— and what came out was: "I looked down. Into the slot. I've never looked straight down into something like that before."

It was even true. It just wasn't the true thing.

Hazel held his gaze a moment longer, and Dev had the awful, vertiginous sense that she could see the other truths stacked behind the one he'd offered, the way you can see deeper layers of ice through a thin spot in a serac. Then she nodded and looked back at the map.

"It gets worse before it gets better," she said. "Day Four, we cross the upper crevasse field. Wide slots, hidden bridges. You'll want to look down again. Don't. The bottom of a crevasse has no information for you. Everything you need is at the top, under your feet." She rolled the map closed. "Get some sleep, Dev. Big day tomorrow."

He stood, mumbled thanks for the tea, and ducked out into the starlight.

Everything you need is at the top, under your feet.

He stood outside the tent for a long time, looking back down the route they'd climbed, toward the traverse, invisible now in the dark, toward the crevasse with its frayed red vein and its rusted letters. *BW.* Down there in the blue dark, keeping its secret the way glaciers keep everything.

Tomorrow they would climb higher, into the restricted zone, onto ground no one had touched in fifteen years. And the ice would give up more things. He understood that now the way you understand weather coming — not as a possibility but as a schedule. More rope. More metal. Maybe more than metal.

And when it did, he'd have to choose again, and this time he wasn't sure the choice would be his to make quietly. Priya had already marked him. Hazel had already marked him. And somewhere in the dark tents around him, Sable Voss was sleeping on top of a map with coordinates that matched a grave.

Behind him, faint through the nylon, he heard Hazel start counting under her breath — steps, seconds, something — the rhythm she used instead of sleeping.

Dev pulled his hood up against the cold and went to his tent, carrying the carabiner's three letters with him like a stone in his boot, and lay awake listening to the glacier breathe, waiting for morning, and dreading what the ice would give up next.
