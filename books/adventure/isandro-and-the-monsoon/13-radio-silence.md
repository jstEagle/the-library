## 13. Radio Silence

Okafor found the fault at 04:40, which was the kind of hour when a man's hands are honest even if his mind is still dreaming.

He'd set the satellite unit to wake him for the scheduled check-in — a formality, mostly, a ping back to Voss's logistics office in the valley so somebody down there knew they were alive and on schedule. The ritual of it mattered more than the content. Fifteen years of guiding had taught Okafor that crews didn't fall apart in the big moments; they fell apart in the small silences between them. A voice from the low country every twelve hours was a thread, and you kept hold of your threads.

The unit's display was dark.

Not sleeping — dark. The standby LED that should have pulsed amber was dead, and when he pressed the power stud he got nothing at all, not even the flicker of a dying battery. He checked the pack terminals first, because you always check the stupid thing before the sinister thing: connections clean, contacts bright, no corrosion, no frost in the housing. He'd sealed this radio himself in the valley with dielectric grease and a strip of neoprene, and he'd done it well, because radios were his religion and he did not do sloppy work for anyone.

He cracked the case under his headlamp, kneeling in the equipment tent with his breath fogging the plastic.

The main board looked fine until he tilted it into the light. Then he saw it: a hairline fracture running through the solder field beneath the power regulator, clean as a scribe line, straight through three joints. Not stress cracking — stress cracks wander, branch, look like what they are. This was one stroke. Deliberate. Precise. Someone had flexed the board against an edge until it broke exactly where it needed to break, and then they'd reseated everything so neatly that a casual inspection would have blamed the cold.

Okafor sat back on his heels and let the cold seep up through his knees while he thought about that.

* * *

By seven he'd rigged a workaround that wasn't one. He stripped the backup handheld — the little Iridium go-bag unit they carried for emergencies, rated for voice but barely, useless for data — and got it powered, but its antenna had been stowed in the bottom of a duffel and something had crushed the coax connector. He could probably solder a fix. Probably. In a warm tent with a full bench. Not here, not now, not with his fingers two-thirds numb and the soldering iron drawing more current than their little solar array wanted to give at dawn.

So: one dead primary, one wounded backup, and a repair window measured in hours of good light and spare patience.

He carried both units out of the tent and stood in the blue morning looking up the glacier toward Camp One, where the route threaded into the icefall like a stitch through flesh. The Kharun filled the whole southern sky, white going gold at the summits, and beneath all that beauty it groaned — a long tectonic complaint, the sound of something enormous shifting its weight in its sleep. Calving season hadn't opened yet. Eleven days, Voss had said. The route would hold for eleven days.

Okafor had been around mountains long enough to know that glaciers kept their own calendars.

"You look like a man doing arithmetic he doesn't like."

Priya came up beside him, steam rising off two tin mugs. She handed him one without asking. Tea, over-sweetened the way she made it for Dev because Dev liked it that way, and Okafor drank it anyway because refusing Priya's tea was not among his life choices.

"Radio's dead," he said.

"Dead how?"

"Murdered." He turned the primary unit so she could see the fracture. "Somebody put their thumb on this board and snapped it. Clean break. Then they closed it up like nothing happened."

Priya looked at the crack for a long moment. She was not a woman who startled, and she didn't startle now; she just went very still, the way she did before she gave bad news to a patient. "When?"

"Sometime since yesterday evening. I ran a check at nineteen hundred — it was fine. Woke up to this."

"So it happened inside camp. While we slept."

"Or while someone pretended to sleep."

She said nothing to that. Down-slope, near the cook tarps, Sable Voss stood talking with her two porters, gesturing at the icefall with the easy authority of a woman describing property she already owned. Even at this distance Okafor could see she was smiling.

"She smiles a lot," Priya observed.

"Too much," Okafor said. "Nobody pays triple rate to be happy."

* * *

He spent the middle of the day doing what he always did when something was wrong: work. There was always work. He rebuilt the crushed coax connector on the backup unit with parts cannibalized from a headlamp cable, wrapped the joint in self-fusing tape, and by early afternoon had a signal — weak, intermittent, dropping out every few minutes as the antenna swung in the wind, but a signal. Enough to send a burst message. Maybe enough for a voice call if the wind lay down.

Dev appeared at the mouth of the mechanic's shelter, ducking under the guyline, all elbows and earnestness. The kid had a genius for being underfoot in useful ways. "Need a hand?"

"Hold this. Don't breathe on it."

Dev held it. Didn't breathe on it. After a minute he said, quietly, "Tomas says the radio's down."

"Tomas says right."

"Is it bad?"

Okafor considered the question the way he considered all questions: mechanically. "A radio is a door," he said. "Right now we're standing in a room with no doors, four days' travel above the last checkpoint, on a glacier that's about to start shedding pieces of itself. You tell me if it's bad."

Dev swallowed. "Can you fix it?"

"I'm fixing it." He tightened the last wrap and held the unit up, watching the signal meter stutter between two bars and none. "But fixing it isn't the same as trusting it. Whoever killed the primary can kill this one too. And next time they might not be sloppy about the antenna."

"You think it was one of us?"

Okafor looked at him. The kid had Hazel's habit of asking the direct question without flinching from the answer, which was either courage or naivety, and at twenty-three it was usually both.

"I think there are eight people in this camp," Okafor said. "Four of mine, who I'd trust with my life and have. Two porters, who are professionals and getting paid whether we live or die. And Sable Voss." He packed the tools away, each piece in its loop, the ritual steadying him. "I think the math is simple even if the arithmetic isn't."

"But why? If she wanted us cut off—"

"Then cutting us off is exactly what she wanted." Okafor slung the repaired backup inside his jacket, against his body heat, where batteries lived longer and where he could feel it like a heartbeat. "You don't sabotage a crew's radio because you're worried about them. You do it because there's something coming you don't want them to report."

Dev was quiet for a moment. Above them the glacier spoke again, that long groan rolling down the valley like a word in a language older than people.

"Report to who?" Dev asked.

"That," said Okafor, "is the question worth staying awake for."

* * *

Evening brought the whole crew into the mess tent, and Okafor watched faces the way he watched gauges — looking for the needle that jumped.

Hazel sat at the head of the folding table with the map spread in front of her, marking tomorrow's line through the lower icefall in grease pencil. Tomas hovered at her shoulder, quiet as ever, though Okafor had noticed the navigator carrying a new weight these past days, some letter-shaped thing folded behind his eyes. Priya ladled out dal with the economy of a woman feeding people on a budget of calories and morale. Dev talked too much, which was how Dev processed fear, and nobody stopped him because everybody understood.

And Voss sat slightly apart, as she always did, eating her ration with neat, unhurried bites, listening to everything, offering nothing.

Hazel laid down her pencil. "Okafor. Tell them."

He told them. The fracture, the timing, the tidy reassembly. He kept it to facts, because facts were his trade, and he watched the facts land on each face in turn. Priya nodded slowly, already past surprise. Dev looked stricken, a boy learning that the world contained deliberate harm. Tomas went gray and said nothing at all, which Okafor filed away.

Voss set down her spoon.

"Well," she said. "That's unfortunate."

The words were right. The tone was wrong. There was no edge of anger in it, no frustration, none of the sharp practical irritation a expedition leader shows when her comms die four days deep. Just a mild, regretful smoothness, like a hostess apologizing for the weather.

"Unfortunate," Okafor repeated.

"These things happen at altitude. Cold does ugly things to electronics." She smiled — that smile again, calibrated, generous, reaching all the way to her eyes without moving anything behind them. "Your backup unit? Can you keep it alive?"

"I can keep it breathing. Whether it talks when we need it to—" He shrugged. "Ask me again when we need it to."

"Then we proceed carefully. Shorter rope teams, tighter spacing in the icefall, conservative turnaround times. We've done fine without a lifeline before, most of us." She glanced around the table, gathering agreement the way other people gathered firewood. "The schedule matters more than the comfort. Eleven days doesn't negotiate."

"The schedule," Hazel said quietly, "matters. But so does knowing who's holding the knife."

The tent went very still. Somewhere outside, a stake creaked in the wind.

Voss turned to her with an expression of such open, reasonable hurt that Okafor almost admired the craft of it. "Hazel. If you're suggesting I broke your radio—"

"I'm suggesting," Hazel said, "that you know more about this job than you've told us. I'm suggesting the salvage coordinates, the forged papers at the checkpoint, the way you watch the upper mountain like you're reading something written on it. And now I'm suggesting that whoever snapped that board knew exactly which unit to kill and exactly how to make it look like cold did it. That's not bad luck, Sable. That's competence. And in my experience, competence on a glacier belongs to somebody in this tent."

For a heartbeat — half a heartbeat — something moved behind Voss's smile. Not guilt. Recognition, maybe. The look of a chess player watching an opponent finally count the pieces.

"If I wanted you isolated," Voss said softly, "wouldn't I have done it before the checkpoint? Before your face was on a border officer's clipboard?" She rose, folded her bowl, and moved to the tent flap. At the opening she paused, backlit by the last violet light on the icefall, and looked back at them all with that easy, terrible warmth.

"Get some sleep. Day One starts at first light. And Okafor—" the smile widened a fraction, "—do try to keep that backup alive. I'd hate for anything to happen to us without the world knowing."

The flap fell shut behind her.

Nobody spoke for a moment. Outside, the glacier groaned, long and low, and this time it sounded less like a sleeping thing and more like something counting.

Okafor reached inside his jacket and touched the backup radio where it lay against his chest, warm from his body, fragile as an egg. Four days above the checkpoint. One wounded radio. Eight people, and one of them smiling too easily.

He started making a list in his head of everything else in camp that could be broken — stove, ropes, anchors, trust — and resolved to check all of it before dawn.

Because whatever Sable Voss was planning, the silence around them wasn't empty anymore.

It was closing.
