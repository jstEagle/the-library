## 14. The Falsified Survey

Tomas had learned to read moraines the way other men read tide charts. A glacier's rubble field was a ledger, every stone an entry: where the ice had been, how fast it moved, what it had swallowed and what it refused to give back. He'd spent twenty years teaching himself the language because his father-in-law had spoken it fluently, and after Anselm Reyes died on the Kharun in 2009, someone in the family had to keep speaking it.

He worked the eastern flank of the tongue while the others rigged the fixed lines below. Voss had ordered a sweep pattern — grid squares, flags, methodical as an audit — and Voss's orders had a way of sounding like suggestions until you realized they weren't. So Tomas walked his grid, eyes down, boots testing each block of gneiss before trusting it, and let the glacier talk.

It was saying too much. Three days into the route and the meltwater was already running loud under the ice, a sound like a crowd murmuring behind a wall. The monsoon was early. Everyone felt it. Nobody said it.

His square took him up-slope from camp, into a jumble of rock deposited by some ancient surge, boulders the size of trucks stacked at angles that defied patience. Good ground for nothing. Bad ground for everything. He was halfway through it, marking a flag on a horn of granite, when he saw the glint.

Metal, wedged deep in a crevice between two slabs. Not modern metal — not the anodized aluminum of their own gear, not Voss's titanium. This was duller, older, wearing a skin of oxidation like frost that wouldn't scrape off. He knelt. Worked his fingers into the gap. The thing was jammed tight, as if the mountain had gripped it deliberately and meant to keep it.

A tube. Sealed survey tube, brass end-caps, the kind they hadn't manufactured in fifteen years. His heart did something complicated — a stutter, then a long slow fall.

He knew this tube. Not this tube specifically. But he'd grown up around its siblings, rattling around Anselm's garage in their wooden crate, and he'd married into a household where survey data was treated with the reverence other families reserved for scripture. Anselm Reyes had believed a map was a promise. *You draw the line, you swear to it,* he used to say, tapping the table with one blunt finger. *Because somebody's daughter is going to walk that line in the dark.*

Tomas pried for ten minutes with a nut tool and a flat of tent pole until the tube came free with a crack of shifting scree. He sat back on his heels and turned it over.

The casing was scored, dented along one side where something heavy had struck it — a rockfall, maybe, or a body falling. There was no expedition tag. Those had been cut away, or rotted, or torn off by whoever last held it. But stamped into the brass near the top cap, small enough to miss unless you were looking, was a maker's mark and a serial number, and Tomas had spent one long winter of his marriage cataloguing exactly these serial numbers into a spreadsheet his wife had found morbid and Anselm had found hilarious.

Series 400 through 460. Reyes Survey Instruments, custom run. Issued to the Brightwater Kharun Expedition, spring 2009.

The tube was warm in his hands from the sun and cold in his bones anyway.

* * *

He didn't open it on the moraine. Some instinct older than caution told him not to — that whatever was inside belonged to shade, not light, that reading it here under the wide indifferent sky would be a kind of desecration. Or maybe he just wasn't ready. Both things were true, and he carried the tube back down to camp zipped inside his jacket, against his ribs, like contraband. Like a heartbeat that wasn't his.

Camp Two sat in a shallow col between two ribbed shoulders of ice, four tents and a cook shelter and Voss's larger dome pitched slightly apart, slightly above, the geography of command made physical. Priya was boiling water. Dev was coiling rope with the exaggerated care of a man being watched. Okafor had the radio housing open again, muttering at it. And Hazel stood at the edge of camp looking up-route toward the icefall they'd have to thread tomorrow, her silhouette sharp against the white, a woman carved out of the same material as the mountain and twice as weathered.

Tomas had known Hazel Brightwater for eleven years. He had followed her out of respect, then out of loyalty, then out of the particular gravity that forms around people who carry something enormous and refuse to set it down. He had never once seen her flinch at her father's name, because she arranged her life so that no one said it. That was the architecture of the crew: small, legal, invisible, and silent about 2009.

He touched the tube through his jacket. Thought about the letter with the crest that he'd shown her at base camp, the one she'd burned unread, flames eating the wax seal before the wind could take the ash. He had watched her burn it and understood that he was watching a woman hold a door shut with her whole body.

This tube was going to kick that door off its hinges.

"Tomas." Voss's voice, behind him. "Your grid complete?"

"Complete."

"Find anything?"

The question was casual. Voss's casualness always cost extra. She stood with her hands in her pockets, silver hair cropped short, eyes the color of the ice they both made their living on, and she looked at him the way she looked at everything — appraising, weighing, already calculating resale value.

"Rock," Tomas said. "Mostly rock."

"Mm." Her gaze lingered half a second past comfort. "The east flank sheds. Watch your spacing tomorrow."

She moved on. Tomas exhaled slowly and went to find Priya.

* * *

They read it in the medic tent, which was the only place in camp with a door, such as it was, and a flap that latched. Priya sat cross-legged on her sleeping pad, hands scrubbed, handling the documents with forceps from her kit like the surgeon she'd stopped being eight years ago. Dev stood at the flap, keeping watch and pretending not to listen. Okafor crouched by the stove, radiating the stillness he achieved only when something mattered.

The end cap gave a quarter-turn and a hiss of trapped air — air sealed since before Dev was old enough to remember, since before Hazel's world ended, since before six people walked up a mountain on a lie and didn't walk back down.

Inside: a map sheet, folded to the size of a fist. A data log, pencil on waterproof stock. And a cover sheet in the formal style of a certified survey, the kind Anselm had drilled into every apprentice, every line item accounted, every correction initialed.

Priya unfolded the map first. They spread it on the sleeping pads, weighted the corners with carabiners, and shone both headlamps down onto it.

It was the Kharun. The upper mountain, the west ridge, the route. Drawn in Anselm's hand — Tomas would have known it blindfolded, that precise architectural script, every contour labeled, every station numbered. Dated April 2009. And across the upper section, in a different hand, heavier, more confident, the hand that appeared on the title blocks of every published Brightwater map, ran a series of corrections. Elevations adjusted. Distances shortened. A key traverse relocated by nearly two hundred meters to a line that — Tomas checked it three times, his stomach dropping further with each check — did not exist. The terrain there was a hanging serac band. No surveyor alive would certify a route through it.

But the corrections weren't initialed by the surveyor. In Anselm's system, corrections were initialed by the surveyor. Always. *You draw the line, you swear to it.*

These initials were EB.

"He changed the data," Priya said quietly. It wasn't a question. She was reading the log now, lips moving slightly, the old clinical focus on her face. "Look at the stations. Stations nine through fourteen are logged at positions that don't match the plotted points. He surveyed the true line, and then the map shows a different line. Someone overwrote the plot."

"The summit line," Tomas said. His voice came out flat and strange, like it belonged to someone else. "The false line. The one the six of them—" He stopped. Started again. "The rescue teams searched the corrected coordinates for nine days. They found nothing, because there was nothing there to find. The bodies were never recovered because everyone was looking two hundred meters from where the route actually went."

The tent was very quiet. Outside, the glacier groaned — a long tectonic complaint, ice settling against ice — and beneath it the meltwater murmured its crowd-noise, louder than yesterday.

Okafor spoke without looking up from the stove. "Dates match?"

Tomas checked. Of course they matched. He'd known they would. "April 11th through April 19th, 2009. Final certification dated April 20th." The day before the ascent. The day before the storm. The day before six names became a memorial plaque in a valley town and one name became a legend, and one name — a nineteen-year-old girl waiting at base camp for a father who never radioed down — became a fugitive from her own inheritance.

Anselm Reyes had been on that expedition. Chief surveyor. Tomas had lost a father-in-law to the same mountain that took everything else, and for fifteen years the family had lived inside the official story: heroic line, freak storm, tragic loss, the maps vindicated by the inquiry because the inquiry had certified them using the surveyor's own instruments and the surveyor was dead and couldn't object.

Except he had objected. Here it was. Pencil on waterproof stock. The true line, logged honestly, day by day. And somewhere between the field book and the final plot, Elian Brightwater had redrawn the mountain to suit his ambition, and his chief surveyor had either not caught it in time or —

Tomas's throat closed. He thought of Anselm's blunt finger tapping the table. *Somebody's daughter.* Anselm had come home from expeditions and locked himself in the garage for days, re-checking plots, haunted by errors no one else could see. He would have caught it. He must have caught it. Which meant the sealed tube in Tomas's jacket — the one thing a surveyor was trained to do with disputed data, seal it, cache it, timestamp it, preserve it for arbitration — meant Anselm had known. Had hidden the truth in the ice like a message in a bottle, and died before he could testify.

Or died because of it. The thought arrived uninvited and stayed. The tube was dented. The tag was gone.

"Grief and fury," Priya said softly, watching his face. "That's what you look like right now. Both at once. I've seen it before."

"On who?"

"Mirrors." She folded the map with surgical care. "Tomas. You know what this is. You know what this does."

"It clears her." The words came out of him hot and fast. "Fifteen years. Two blacklists. Every border crossing in fear, every contract signed under a false shell company, every night she checks the perimeter twice — and the proof that none of it was hers has been sitting in the ice the whole time. Anselm kept it safe. Anselm kept it safe for *her*, whether he knew it or not—"

"And Voss knows it exists," Okafor said.

The sentence landed in the tent like dropped gear on rock.

"That's the job," Okafor continued, feeding a stick into the stove, unhurried. "Salvage run. Recovered materials clause. Radio that fails too conveniently. A grid search pattern aimed at a fifteen-year-old wreck site on a glacier Voss has no honest reason to care about. We're not salvage crew. We're a retrieval team, and we just retrieved."

Dev shifted at the flap. "Do we tell her? Tonight?"

Tomas looked at the map, at the heavy confident letters EB across the serac band, at the initials that should have been AR and weren't. He thought about Hazel burning the letter. About the door she held shut with her whole body. About what happens to a door when the thing behind it turns out to be, finally, after fifteen years, the truth — and the truth is worse and better than anything the door was keeping out.

"She'll want it at dawn," Tomas said. "Not tonight. Tonight she sleeps, if the ice lets her." He began re-rolling the log with hands that were steadier than he deserved. "And then we do this in the open, all of us, because she doesn't get to burn this one."

Outside, far up the glacier, something calved — a single deep report, like a door slamming in a house the size of a country. The sound rolled down the valley and faded, and in the silence after it, Priya met Tomas's eyes.

"Dawn, then," she said. "God help us all."
