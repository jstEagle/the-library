## 15. Confrontation at Camp Two

The storm had been building all afternoon, stacking itself against the western ridgeline like a debt collector who knew you were home. By the time they'd anchored the tents and dug in the cook shelter, the light had gone the color of old pewter, and the wind was carrying grit off the moraine in long, hissing sheets.

Hazel stood at the edge of camp with her back to the wind, watching Sable Voss.

Voss was at the far tent — her tent, the one she'd insisted on pitching apart from theirs, "for the equipment." She crouched over an aluminum case, feeding something into it with the careful, proprietary movements of a woman handling money. Or evidence. The satellite radio sat beside her on a rock, its antenna folded down, its power light dark. Okafor had been trying to raise base camp for six hours. Voss hadn't offered to help once.

Fifteen years of reading weather, of reading clients, of reading the small tells that preceded disaster — and Hazel had let herself be bought for triple rate because Dev's contract had a number attached to it. She'd signed a clause she didn't like because she wanted to believe this run was what it said it was: salvage, pre-season, eleven days, out before the ice woke up.

But the ice had been talking to her since the tongue. And so had Voss.

She crossed the camp. Her boots crunched on scree that sounded, in the rising wind, almost like applause.

"Voss."

Sable Voss looked up. She was fifty-one and built like a climbing harness — all buckles and tension, nothing decorative. Gray shot through her black hair at the temples now, and there were lines around her mouth that hadn't been there in Kathmandu fifteen years ago, when they'd stood on opposite sides of a hotel bar and hated each other with the clean, uncomplicated hatred of the young. The lines made her look tired. They did not make her look soft.

"Hazel Brightwater." Voss said the name the way she always did, letting it sit in her mouth like something she'd found under a rock. "Come to check my permits?"

"Okafor says the radio's dead."

"The radio's dead," Voss agreed. She closed the case. Latched it. Set her palm flat on top of it, casual as a poker player protecting her stack.

"You have a spare unit in your kit. I saw the inventory manifest. You listed it yourself."

"I did."

"And you haven't mentioned it."

"No," Voss said, "I haven't."

The wind gusted. Somewhere up-valley, deep inside the glacier, something boomed — a muffled, tectonic sound, like a door closing in a very large house. Both of them glanced toward the icefall without meaning to. When Hazel looked back, Voss was smiling slightly, and the smile was worse than any argument.

"Say it plainly," Hazel said. "Whatever this is. Say it here, where I can hear it, instead of letting me find it in your gear at twelve thousand feet."

"All right." Voss rose. She was taller than Hazel remembered, or maybe Hazel had shrunk — years of carrying loads will do that, compress you, teach you to make yourself smaller than the mountain. Voss brushed scree dust off her knees and looked at her with something that wasn't quite pity and wasn't quite respect. "You want it plainly. Fine. Do you know why I picked your crew? Out of every outfit between here and the coast — every legal one, anyway — why yours?"

"Because we're good."

"Because you're cheap and desperate and you don't ask questions," Voss said. "And because of your name. Because when I put *Brightwater* on a salvage manifest, every clerk in three provinces signs it without reading it, because your father is a saint and saints open doors." She tapped the case under her hand. "I needed doors opened, Hazel. You were the key. That's all you've ever been to me."

The words landed exactly where she'd aimed them. Hazel felt them go in, felt the old wound — the one she kept wrapped in miles and routine and the small daily competence of keeping four people fed and invisible — split along its oldest seam.

"And what," Hazel said, and her voice came out level, which cost her more than shouting would have, "is behind the doors?"

Voss studied her for a long moment. Then she unlatched the case.

Inside, packed in foam, lay a map. Not a printout, not a copy — an original survey sheet, brittle at the folds, water-stained along one edge. Hazel knew the paper stock. She knew the header format. She had grown up watching her father fill out that exact form at their kitchen table, his handwriting slanting uphill the way it always did when he was pleased with himself.

It was a Kharun Glacier survey. Dated 2009. Marked in red ink — not her father's red, someone else's, newer — with a single set of coordinates.

Coordinates inside the restricted zone.

Coordinates at the last known position of the Brightwater expedition.

"Where did you get this?" Hazel asked. Her voice sounded distant to her own ears, as if someone else were asking.

"Archives. Bribes. Fifteen years of both." Voss lifted the map out and held it between them, and the wind tried to take it and she let it strain against her grip, a small white flag that refused to fly. "Do you know what I've been doing since 2009, Hazel? While you were burying your name and measuring your life in safe little miles? I've been following your father's lie across two mountain ranges. Every sponsor he conned, every institute he charmed, every map he signed — I've pulled the thread. And do you know what I found at the end of it?"

"Tell me."

"He never summited." Voss said it quietly, and somehow that was worse than if she'd shouted it. "There was no first ascent. There was no new route. He turned back at the serac band — his own journal puts him below the icefall on summit day — and then he came down and drew a line on a map that goes where no human being went, and six people followed that line the next season because Elian Brightwater's maps were scripture." She rolled the map slowly, deliberately, her thumb pressing the red coordinates flat. "Six people, Hazel. I can name them. Can you?"

Hazel could. She'd been naming them for fifteen years, silently, the way other people say grace. Anselm Reyes. The Okonkwo brothers. Marta Ilves. Chen Wei. And Tomas's father-in-law, Petar, whose face went carefully blank whenever the expedition came up, which was how Hazel had known, years before anyone said anything, that the wound wasn't hers alone.

"I can name them," she said.

"Then name them out loud sometime," Voss said. "Because your father couldn't. Your father wrote beautiful books about a mountain he lied about, and the world gave him a glacier named after his route, and his daughter got blacklisted for asking one question too many at the inquiry — and everyone, *everyone*, has spent fifteen years treating that like a tragedy of circumstances." Her voice hardened. "It wasn't circumstances. It was arithmetic. Six lives traded for one legend. And the proof—" she patted the case, "—the proof is sitting in the ice up there, in his last camp, frozen at the exact coordinates he falsified. His journals. His instruments. His original field notes, with the real numbers in his own hand. I am going up there, Hazel, and I am going to dig your father's lie out of the ice, and I am going to carry it down and put it in front of every committee, every press office, every grieving family that ever lit a candle for the legend of Elian Brightwater."

"Why?" The word tore loose before Hazel could shape it. "Why does it matter to you? You weren't—"

She stopped. But Voss caught it, the way Voss caught everything, and her eyes went bright and terrible.

"Weren't I?" she said softly. "Say it. Finish the sentence. You've had fifteen years of practice not finishing sentences."

The wind screamed down the valley. Above them, high on the route they'd climbed two days ago, a serac leaned and groaned and held.

"You weren't on that expedition," Hazel said. "You weren't one of the six."

"No," Voss said. "My brother was."

The world went very quiet, the way it does sometimes around a loud thing, the way air goes still before lightning.

"Dario," Hazel said. The name arrived from somewhere deeper than memory. She'd seen it on the inquiry lists, read it aloud in a hearing room at nineteen years old while adults watched her to see if she'd cry. "Dario Voss. High-altitude porter."

"Rope leader," Voss corrected. "He carried your father's false line on his shoulders. Literally. He fixed ropes along a route that didn't exist, above a crevasse field your father had mapped as solid, because Elian Brightwater paid him well and signed his logbook kindly, and Dario believed the map. Everyone believed the map." She folded the survey sheet along its old creases, precise, unhurried. "They never even recovered his body. Do you know what that does to a family? No grave. No closure. Just a name on a list and a legend standing on top of it, taller every year, while the people who actually loved the dead are told to be grateful for the beautiful story."

"So this was never salvage."

"This was never salvage," Voss agreed. "This was a exhumation. And you—" she smiled again, and this time there was no warmth in it at all, only a kind of grim satisfaction, "—you led me right to the grave. The great Hazel Brightwater, the honest one, the one who got punished for the sin of being related to a liar. I needed your name and your crew and your guilt, and you sold me all three for enough money to buy a rookie's freedom. How does that sit in your stomach? Does it sit there like the truth? Because the truth weighs about the same."

Hazel's hands were shaking. She looked down at them, at the scarred knuckles, the rope-worn fingers, and willed them still, and they mostly obeyed, because her body had learned long ago to hold itself together in front of clients no matter what was happening behind her ribs.

"You could have told me," she said.

"Would you have come?"

No. That was the honest answer, and they both knew it. She would have refused, would have found a reason, would have protected the legend one more season, the way she'd been protecting it since she was nineteen — not because she believed it, but because believing it was the only thing left of him, and she had already lost everything else.

"That's what I thought," Voss said. "So I didn't tell you. I used you. And when we come down off this ice, I'm going to use what we find up there, and I'm going to use it thoroughly, and your father's name is going to come apart in public, in print, in every language that matters. And you can stand next to me or you can stand against me, but either way, Hazel Brightwater, the legend dies this month. On this glacier. In front of witnesses." She shouldered past, close enough that Hazel smelled rope wax and cold sweat and fifteen years of the same obsession. At the flap of her tent she paused, without turning. "Get some sleep. We move for Camp Three at first light. The coordinates are two hours beyond it."

* * *

Hazel didn't sleep.

She sat in her tent with the stove ticking down and the wind working at the guy-lines, and she took out the things she carried and laid them in her lap like a surgeon laying out instruments. A photo of her father, taken the summer before 2009, squinting into sun, one arm around her sixteen-year-old shoulders. A carabiner from her first lead climb, stamped with a B that had once meant *Brightwater* and now meant nothing, which was the point. The letter Tomas had finally shown her at base camp — the one bearing the family crest, which she had burned unread in the cook stove while Tomas watched her with grief in his face and said nothing, because he understood that some letters are addressed to whoever you used to be.

She had spent fifteen years telling herself a story: that her father was a great man undone by bad luck, that the inquiry was politics, that her blacklist was the price of loyalty. She had told it so long and so carefully that she'd built a life on top of it, the way you build a snow cave — layer by layer, each one sealing out a little more light, until the structure holds and you can live inside it and call the darkness shelter.

And underneath the story, all along, in the place she never went with a lamp — she had known.

She had known at nineteen, standing in that hearing room, when she'd asked to see her father's original field notes and the panel had gone quiet and her father's lawyer had touched her elbow and said, *Hazel, sweetheart, some questions don't help anyone.* She had known when she read the published route description and found the serac band described in terrain terms that didn't match the photographs. She had known, God help her, when she chose exile over testimony — when the committee offered her a chance to speak, formally, on the record, and she had looked at her mother's face and her father's empty chair and said, *I have nothing to add,* and walked out into fifteen years of small crews and forged papers and miles, miles, miles.

She hadn't stayed silent because she was loyal. She'd stayed silent because she was afraid. Afraid that if she pulled the thread, the whole sweater would come apart — the legend, the name, the man who taught her to read contour lines before she could read words — and she would be left standing in the wreckage holding nothing, not even the story that had kept her warm.

Voss had pulled the thread. Voss had spent fifteen years pulling it, with hate where Hazel had fear, and certainty where Hazel had doubt, and now they were both here, at twelve thousand feet, on top of the truth, and the truth was going to come out whether Hazel blessed it or not.

That was the part that broke something open in her chest, quietly, without drama: not that Voss was going to expose Elian Brightwater. That Voss was right to.

Hazel pressed the photo face-down against her knee. Outside, the storm arrived in earnest, and the tents snapped and strained, and somewhere beneath them the glacier shifted its enormous weight and groaned — a long, rising note, like something waking up, like something that had been patient for a very long time and was done waiting.

She thought of the six names. She said them once, out loud, into the dark of the tent, the way Voss had dared her to. Anselm Reyes. Joseph and Daniel Okonkwo. Marta Ilves. Chen Wei. Petar Lindqvist.

Then she said the seventh name, the one that hurt most, because it was hers to say: *Dario Voss.*

When the first gray light came, she crawled out into the storm's ragged edge and found Voss already awake, coiling rope with quick, efficient hands, and Hazel stood over her and said the only thing left to say:

"We go up together. But Voss — whatever we find up there, whatever's in that ice — it comes down in the open. Every page. Every number. To the families first, then the world. Not to you. Not to your version. If you're doing this for Dario, then Dario gets the truth, not a headline with your name on it."

Voss looked up at her, and for one unguarded second — half a heartbeat, no more — something moved behind her eyes that might have been relief, or might have been rage at being denied the ending she'd scripted for fifteen years.

"First light's wasted," Voss said, standing. "We move now."

Above them, through a tear in the clouds, the upper mountain showed itself for three seconds — white, immense, indifferent — and then the cloud sealed shut again, and the glacier groaned, and they started up.
