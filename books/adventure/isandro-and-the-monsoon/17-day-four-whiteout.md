## 17. Day Four: Whiteout

The storm arrived on the fourth morning like a debt collector.

Priya heard it before she saw it — a low compression in the air, the tents snapping taut against their guy lines, and then the snow came sideways, a white wall moving at forty kilometers an hour with nothing behind it to see. She had her head out of the cook tent for exactly nine seconds. That was enough. The world erased itself.

"Inside," she said to Dev, who was standing at the edge of the platform with his goggles up, watching the glacier disappear. "Now."

"It's early," he said. "Voss said—"

"Voss said a lot of things." She hauled him in by his pack strap and zipped the vestibule shut behind them. Her hands were already shaking, and not from cold. Monsoon in the Kharun was supposed to be a rumor this time of year, a gray threat on the horizon they'd be long gone from before it mattered. Instead it had come up the valley overnight like something hunting them.

By noon the barometer had fallen so far that Okafor's spare gauge bottomed out. By evening, no one was talking about the schedule anymore. You didn't climb in that. You didn't even stand up outside without a leash. Camp Two — four tents and a cook shelter lashed to a shelf of blue ice between two serac walls — became the entire universe.

* * *

Priya kept count of things. It was how she managed fear: convert it to data. Two days pinned. One stove running on their reserve fuel because the wind had shredded the windscreen on the backup and cracked its line. Forty-one hours since anyone had been more than three meters from a tent rope. Nine hundred milliliters of water per person per day, melted from snow they shoveled through the tent doors by the armful, because going outside to fetch it properly was a gamble none of them needed to take.

She also counted heartbeats, which was less scientific. Six of them, breathing around her in the dark of the cook shelter, and every one of them hers to keep alive if it came to that.

On the second night, the storm found a new register. Not the shriek of the first day but a deep, rhythmic percussion, the whole glacier flexing under the weight of new snow, and beneath that a sound Priya felt in her sternum rather than heard — ice settling, somewhere far below, the way a house settles, if the house were ten thousand years old and indifferent to its tenants.

Nobody slept. So they talked.

It started small. Okafor told a story about a generator he'd once rebuilt in a monsoon in Chittagong using a bicycle chain and prayer, and Dev laughed until he coughed, and Tomas told one about a client in Ladakh who had insisted on bringing a suitcases' worth of novels up a 6,000-meter peak and reading them aloud at camp each night. Even Voss contributed — a dry, clipped anecdote about a salvage job in Patagonia that ended with the punchline *and that is why I never sign anything I haven't read twice*, which got a laugh that died quickly, because everyone remembered who had written whose contract.

Hazel said almost nothing. She sat with her back against the gear wall, hood down, hands wrapped around a cup of tea she wasn't drinking, listening to the storm the way other people listened to music. Priya watched her and thought: *she's counting too.* Hazel Brightwater counted in miles, everyone knew that. Fifteen years of measuring her life in distance covered rather than time served. But tonight there were no miles available. Tonight there was only this room, this storm, these people, and whatever lived behind Hazel's eyes when the mountain made noise.

It was Dev who broke the silence, the way young men do — not with courage but with an inability to hold a thing any longer.

"I need to tell you all something."

The stove hissed. Wind pressed the shelter walls inward and let go.

"I should have said it on day two," Dev went on. His voice had that thin quality it got when he was scared and trying to sound steady. "In the icefall. When we were fixing the traverse below the serac. I was on anchor duty while you all moved across, and I was looking at the crevasse wall — the blue one, right where the route bends — and there's stuff frozen into it. Old stuff. Rope. A carabiner."

Priya felt the temperature of the room change before anyone spoke.

"Rusted through," Dev said. "But there was a stamp on the spine of it. Letters. *B-W.*" He looked at Hazel. He couldn't help looking at Hazel; Priya understood that completely. "Brightwater. It has to be. And I didn't say anything because—" He stopped, started again. "Because I thought maybe I imagined it. And then because I thought, what does it matter, it's just old gear, the icefall eats expeditions all the time. And then because Voss was standing right there and something about the way she looked at that wall made me keep my mouth shut."

"How did Voss look at it?" Hazel asked. Her voice was level. Too level. Priya had treated enough shock to recognize the tone.

"Like she recognized it," Dev said. "Like she was checking something off a list."

The wind hit the shelter hard enough to make the poles sing. Nobody moved.

"You're sure about the stamp," Tomas said quietly. It wasn't really a question.

"I'm sure it was a B and a W. I'm sure it was old — the rope was pre-modern, that flat weave nobody's used in twenty years. I'm sure I should have said something sooner and I'm sorry." Dev's jaw worked. "I keep thinking about my contract. About who owns my signature right now. And I keep thinking that's exactly why I almost didn't say anything, and I hate that."

Priya reached over and put her hand on his shoulder. It was the medic in her as much as anything — a pulse check disguised as comfort. His heart was hammering. He was twenty-three years old and he had just learned, in real time, that silence has a weight and it compounds daily.

"You said something now," she told him. "That's the part that counts."

"That's the part that costs," Okafor said, but gently, and he nodded at Dev like a man acknowledging good work.

Hazel hadn't spoken again. Priya watched her set down the untouched tea with great care, the way you put down something fragile when your hands aren't trustworthy. Then she looked at the shelter wall as if she could see through the fabric, through the snow, through the ice, down into the blue dark where a rusted carabiner hung in a wall of compressed time.

"My father's expedition went in through that icefall," Hazel said. "2009. Six people never came back off the mountain, and the seventh came back alone and ruined." A pause. "I've spent fifteen years telling myself the glacier took everything. Gear, records, bodies. Clean erasure." Another pause, longer. "Apparently it keeps receipts."

No one laughed. No one meant to. But something in the room loosened anyway, the way a knot loosens — not undone, just workable.

* * *

Confinement does something to people that open country never does. Priya had seen it in hospital wards during the flood years, in border detention rooms, in the two-week whiteout she'd once ridden out on a research station in Karakoram. Strip away motion, strip away task and horizon, and people start handing you pieces of themselves whether they mean to or not. The body panics at stillness and the mouth tries to compensate.

So it went, that second night, around the stove.

Okafor went first, though he did it sideways, talking to the stove instead of the room. "I had a wife," he said. "Back when I was still legal everywhere. She used to say I married engines and she was just the woman who fed the mechanic." A dry sound that might have been a laugh. "When the sanctions came, when my name went on the list over parts I never touched — she stayed two years. Then she stayed zero. I don't blame her. You can't build a life inside someone else's exile." He turned a wrench over in his fingers, some unconscious fidget. "This crew is the longest I've belonged anywhere since. That's why I read contracts the way I do. I'm not looking for traps. I'm looking for exits. Habit."

Tomas told them about the letter. Not the contents — Priya noticed he gave the contents a wide berth, the way you step around a hole in the ice — but the fact of it. The crest. The seal he'd recognized. "I hid it from her for six days," he said, nodding at Hazel. "Fifteen years I've navigated for this woman and I lied to her for six days over an envelope. Because I knew what it would do to her, and I thought I got to decide that." He rubbed his face. "I'm her friend. Friends don't get to curate your past for you. I'm still learning that."

"I burned it," Hazel said, to the wall. "Unread."

"We know," Priya said.

"Do we know why?" Hazel asked, and it wasn't rhetorical; there was a genuine question in it, aimed at the room or at herself. "Because I've been asking myself since base camp and I keep getting different answers. Because it would hurt. Because hope is worse than grief, operationally speaking. Because if I read it and it's kind, I'll have to grieve him properly, and I can't afford that mid-route. Pick one. They're all true."

Priya wanted to say something clinical and comforting. She was trained for it; she was good at it. Instead she heard herself say: "My brother drowned when I was nineteen. River, monsoon season, stupid dare. For eleven years I couldn't say his name out loud. Not because it hurt — because saying it made it *real*, and as long as I didn't say it, there was a version of the world where he was still swimming somewhere." She looked at her own hands. "I became a medic because I couldn't save him and I decided I'd spend my life being early instead of late. Every kit I packed, every protocol I memorized — it was all a letter to him I never sent." She shrugged. "You burn letters. Some of us become them."

The stove hissed. Somewhere above them, the storm drove snow against the serac walls and the glacier groaned like a ship in heavy weather.

Dev, of course, had the least to confess and confessed the most, because he was young and the storm had stripped his insulation down to the wiring. "I'm not here for the money," he said, and then winced. "No — that's a lie, I am here for the money, my contract, my family's contract, all of it. But I took this job before Voss ever showed up. I sought Hazel out. Do you know what it's like, growing up on the stories? *Brightwater's Line*, the Kharun traverse, the rescue on the Dola La where she carried a client down fourteen kilometers with a broken ankle? I built my whole idea of what a mountaineer is out of her. And then I got here and she flinches at the name of the mountain, and there's a letter she burns, and a rival who looks at her like they're keeping score in a game nobody explained to me, and I don't know anymore which parts of the story were true." He swallowed. "That scares me more than the icefall did."

"What do you do with a story that turns out to have cracks in it?" Tomas asked him, not unkindly.

"You decide what's load-bearing," Hazel said.

Everyone looked at her.

"A story's a structure," she went on, in the flat, precise voice she used for route briefings. "Some of it holds weight, some of it's decoration. My father's legend has cracks in it — I've known that since I was old enough to read his maps closely. But the part where he taught me to read terrain, the part where he taught me that the mountain doesn't care about your reputation, only your preparation — that part's load-bearing. I've climbed on it for thirty years." She finally drank the cold tea, grimaced, set it down. "The rest we'll see what the ice gives back."

Priya caught Voss watching Hazel across the stove, and the look on the woman's face was the most complicated thing Priya had seen in fifteen years of reading faces. Not triumph. Not malice. Something closer to recognition — the expression of a person watching another person stand at the edge of a drop they themselves had been standing at for years.

Then Voss caught Priya watching, and the look shuttered closed, and she said, "Barometer's still falling. Nobody sleeps past four-hour intervals. Rotating watch." Practical. Armored. Gone.

* * *

Near dawn — if it was dawn; the storm had abolished the distinction — Priya did her rounds. Tent to tent, checking faces for the gray pallor of altitude dehydration, checking fingers, checking the small economies of breathing that told her who was sleeping and who was lying still and pretending.

In the last tent, Hazel was awake. Of course she was.

"Pulse check," Priya said, and crouched inside, and pressed two fingers to Hazel's wrist without waiting for permission. Steady. Slow. Annoyingly healthy, as always.

"You handled Dev well," Hazel said. "When he confessed."

"He handled himself. I just kept him from spiraling."

"You do that for all of us. You think we don't notice." Hazel's eyes were on the tent ceiling, tracking something invisible. "Two days pinned. Storm's early. Route's going to be rotten when it clears — everything unloaded, everything unstable. We may have burned our margin."

Priya didn't argue. Arguing with Hazel's assessments was like arguing with a barometer. "Then we'll move fast when it breaks."

"Mm." A long silence. The wind rose and fell like something breathing. "Priya. If it comes to it — if the route closes and we're making choices — I need to know you'll tell me the truth even when it's the expensive truth. Tomas hid a letter. Okafor reads contracts like escape plans. Dev sat on a carabiner for two days. Everyone in this crew loves me enough to lie to me." Her voice didn't crack, but Priya heard the fracture underneath it, hairline-thin. "Don't be everyone."

Priya held her wrist a moment longer than the pulse required. "I'll tell you the expensive truth," she said. "But you have to promise to take it like medicine and not like a verdict."

"No promises," Hazel said, and for the first time in two days, she smiled — small, tired, real. "But thank you."

Outside, the storm went on erasing the world. Inside, Priya sat in the dark and counted: six heartbeats, one stove, two days down, however many left. And underneath the arithmetic, a colder number she didn't write down: the coordinates she'd glimpsed on Voss's map weeks ago, the ones inside the restricted zone, matching a last-known-position from 2009. A rusted carabiner stamped BW, frozen in the icefall wall, exactly where a wrecked expedition's debris field would drift as the glacier flowed downslope, year after year, carrying its dead toward the tongue.

The storm would break soon. Storms always break. And when it did, Voss would point them somewhere specific, and Priya already knew, with the certainty she usually reserved for diagnoses, that they were going to find it.

She checked Hazel's pulse one more time, purely as an excuse to stay a moment longer, and went out into the white to wake Okafor for his watch.
