## 20. The Journal's Arithmetic

Tomas did not sleep. He told himself it was the altitude, the thin air at Camp Two that made his heart stumble and his thoughts race like a man running downhill. But the journal sat in the bottom of his pack like a stone he'd swallowed, and every time he closed his eyes he saw the leather cover, water-stained and stiff with fifteen years of cold, and the crest stamped into it — the mountain and the compass rose — and beneath the crest, in faded ink, the initials E.B.

Elian Brightwater's field journal. The one the inquiry had never found. The one the world had spent a decade and a half insisting must have been lost on the mountain, burned for warmth, buried in a crevasse, anything but existing — because if it existed, then Elian Brightwater had known he was dying, and had chosen to write instead of shout.

Tomas had read three pages by headlamp while the others slept. He'd stopped there, hands shaking, because the arithmetic was already clear, and arithmetic like this did not get better with more numbers. It only got worse.

He crawled out of the tent at four in the morning, into the blue-black cold, and sat on the ice with the journal between his knees. Camp Two clung to a shelf of old snow above the crevasse field, and below them the glacier breathed — long, slow exhalations of settling ice, the sound of something enormous turning over in its sleep. Above, the seracs of the upper icefall stood against the stars like broken teeth.

He opened the journal again.

* * *

The early entries were ordinary, almost painfully so. Route notes. Weather observations. A sketch of a lark that had landed on a supply sled at base camp, drawn with surprising tenderness, annotated: *First living thing we've seen in six days. Okafor's grandfather would have said it was an omen.* Tomas smiled at that despite himself — the mechanic's family had a long history on these mountains, longer than anyone's, and apparently Elian had known it too.

Then the dates turned. June 2009. The expedition had been on the mountain nineteen days, and the entries began to shorten, the handwriting tightening like a fist.

*The northeast couloir is not going to give us the line. I've been honest with myself for nineteen days and the mountain has been honest back, and the answer is no. There is no route through the serac band that a sane person would sign their name to.*

Tomas read that sentence twice. Then a third time. Because the next entry, dated two days later, began:

*I've been thinking about what my father used to say. That a mapmaker's first duty is to the truth and his second is to the story. He said it as a warning. I've decided to hear it as permission.*

Tomas's breath fogged and hung in front of him, motionless in the dead air. Below him, the glacier groaned — a long tearing note, like a ship's hull giving way somewhere far off — and went quiet again.

He kept reading.

Elian had done it methodically. That was the part that made Tomas's stomach turn, the part that no eulogy or scandal sheet had ever imagined. It wasn't desperation. It wasn't a single bad decision made in the white glare of summit fever. It was drafting. Elian had sat in this same camp — maybe this exact shelf of snow; the coordinates in the margin matched within fifty meters — and he had redrawn the route. He had taken the failed line up the northeast couloir and folded it into a new one, a line through the lower serac band that his own notes proved was impassable, and he had written the false data in the same careful hand as the true data, and he had dated it forward, so that the record would show the route discovered after the attempt rather than before.

*If we go up the true line and fail, we come home with nothing,* he wrote. *If we report the false line and survive, we come home with the first ascent of Kharun's north face and a route other people can follow. The mountain will forgive me. Mountains always do. It's people who keep score.*

Six people had followed that line eleven days later. Not his team — Elian's team had turned back at the serac band, spooked by conditions, alive because Elian had hesitated at the last moment and called the retreat himself. The six were the second party. A commercial expedition, strong climbers, who had trusted the published survey the way climbers trust surveys, the way people trust the printed word, the way Tomas had once trusted a handshake and a family name.

They had entered the serac band on July 3rd, 2009. The icefall had released at midday. No bodies were recovered. No wreckage. For fifteen years the mountain had kept them, and the world had kept asking questions, and Hazel Brightwater had kept running from both.

Tomas turned the page and found the entry dated July 4th.

*They're saying six. Six names on the radio this morning and I knew four of them. I taught Riva Maren how to place pro on rock when she was nineteen. She sent me a card last spring thanking me for her career.*

*I keep trying to do the arithmetic. If I publish a correction, the route dies, the ascent dies, my name dies, and six families learn their people died following a lie. If I stay silent, they died following a mistake — mine, yes, but a mistake, which is a smaller thing than a lie. Smaller for them. Not smaller for me.*

*I am not writing this to forgive myself. I am writing it because someone will find this eventually — the ice gives everything back, my father said that too — and whoever finds it deserves to know that the man who drew the false line knew exactly what he had drawn.*

That was where Tomas had stopped, the first night. Now, in the dark, with his gloves off and his fingers numb, he read on.

The final week of entries deteriorated. The storm came in — the same storm the inquiry records described, the one that sealed the high camps and ended all hope of rescue. Elian's team descended as far as Camp Two and dug in. Two entries from those days, short, practical, about rations and shelter walls. And then a gap of two days, and then the last entry, and the handwriting changed — slower, larger, the letters of a man conserving strength, or deciding that strength no longer needed to be spent on speed.

It was addressed to Hazel.

Tomas stared at the salutation for a long time. *To my daughter.* Four words, and under them a horizontal rule, drawn carefully, the way you draw a line under an account that's about to be settled.

He didn't read past it.

Not because he couldn't — his headlamp was steady, his hands had gone numb enough to be steady too — but because it wasn't his to read first. He thought about that for a moment, sitting in the dark with a dead man's confession warming slowly against his knees, and admitted the truth to himself, which was that some part of him wanted to read it. Some part of him had wanted it since the letter arrived with the crest on its seal, since before that, maybe — some part of Tomas that had loved Hazel Brightwater quietly and hopelessly for years and had wondered, in the small hours, what it would be like to finally know her whole, to hold the missing piece everyone else had only heard rumors of.

If he read it first, she would never be able to unread the fact that he had. Whatever Elian had written to his daughter belonged to her alone until she chose otherwise. That was the only clean thing left in any of this, and Tomas was going to keep it clean.

He closed the journal. Tied it shut with the cord he'd used to secure it in his pack. And then he sat in the dark and did his own arithmetic, because someone on this crew had to, and Priya was asleep, and Hazel —

Hazel was standing twenty meters away, at the edge of the shelf, looking down into the crevasse field.

She'd come out without her parka. He could see the shape of her shoulders against the starlight, the way she held herself — perfectly still, the stillness she wore like armor, the stillness of a woman who had learned that moving fast got people killed and moving slow got you thinking, and thinking was its own kind of weather.

"Hazel," he said.

She didn't turn. "You've been out here an hour."

"You were awake."

"I'm always awake." A pause. The glacier sighed beneath them. "What's in the pack, Tomas?"

There it was. He should have known she'd seen it — she saw everything, that was the whole architecture of her, fifteen years of reading terrain and reading people until nothing slipped past. He stood, joints cracking in the cold, and walked over to her, and held the journal out between them like something that might detonate.

"I found it yesterday. In the moraine, wedged under the slab with the survey tube." He watched her face in the starlight — the flat control, and underneath it, barely, the tremor. "It's his. It's your father's field journal. From 2009."

For a long moment she didn't move. Didn't breathe, as far as Tomas could tell. Somewhere below them the ice shifted, a deep internal grinding, and neither of them looked down.

"The tube matched the dates," Tomas went on, because silence was worse. "The handwriting matches the survey. Hazel — the route data was altered. Deliberately. Before the second party went up. He wrote it all down. Why he did it, how he did it, and—" His voice caught. He steadied it. "And the last entry is addressed to you."

Still she didn't move. Then, slowly, she reached out and took the journal from his hands, and the weight of it seemed to travel through her arm and into her whole body, and she swayed slightly, and Tomas stepped closer without touching her, close enough to catch her if she fell, which was all he had ever really offered her and all she had ever let him give.

"He falsified it," she said. Not a question. Her voice was level in the way that ice is level right before it moves.

"Yes."

"And the six—"

"Followed his line. Yes."

She looked down at the journal in her hands. When she spoke again, her voice had dropped to almost nothing, and it was the voice of a girl, not a guide — the voice Tomas imagined she'd had at twenty-two, before the inquiries and the blacklists, before she'd built herself out of miles.

"Everyone told me he died a hero. Fifteen years. Every festival, every memorial plaque, every drunk old climber in every outfitter bar who bought me a drink because of my name." She laughed, and there was nothing in it. "I half-knew. Did you know that? I half-knew, and I ran anyway, because running was easier than knowing."

"I know," Tomas said.

"No, you don't." She turned and looked at him then, and her eyes were dry and terrible. "But you will. We all will. Because I'm going to read it, Tomas. All of it. Tonight, before Voss wakes up, before anyone else touches it." She tucked the journal inside her jacket, against her chest, the way she carried the things she couldn't afford to lose. "Whatever he wrote to me — whatever he thought he had the right to ask me — I need to know before Voss decides what the world gets to know."

Below them, far down in the dark, the glacier made a sound Tomas had never heard before — not a groan, not a settle, but a sharp crack, singular, like a bone breaking — and then another, closer, and the snow under their feet shivered.

Hazel's head came up. Fifteen years of instinct moved through her face, wiping everything else away.

"That's not settling," she said. "That's the calving season starting early." She was already moving toward the tents, waking the crew with low, clipped calls, and the journal rode against her heart, and Tomas followed her into the wind that had begun, very softly, to fall.
