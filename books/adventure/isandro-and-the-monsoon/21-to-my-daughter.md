## 21. To My Daughter

The storm had moved off sometime in the night without telling anyone, and when Hazel crawled out of the tent at Camp Two the sky was the color of a blade held up to light — thin, cold, clean. The kind of sky that made everything below it look small and temporary.

She hadn't slept. Nobody expected her to say so, but Priya looked at her over the stove anyway, reading the truth in her face the way she read pulses, and said nothing except to push a mug of tea into her hands. The tea was terrible. Okafor had stretched the leaves three days past decency. Hazel drank it like it was medicine, which it was.

The journal sat inside her jacket, against her sternum, where she'd carried it since the wreck of the Meridian. She could feel its weight even now, sitting on a crate beside the stove — not the physical weight, which was nothing, a few hundred grams of paper and leather gone stiff as bark, but the other weight, the one that didn't have units. Fifteen years of it. Her whole adult life pressed flat between covers that smelled of ice and old smoke.

Tomas emerged from his tent, saw her face, saw the journal, and understood immediately what day this was going to be.

"You read the rest," he said. Not a question.

"Three pages last night." She turned the mug in her hands. "There are more."

"How many?"

"I don't know. I stopped counting at the part where he—" She stopped. Started again. "I stopped counting."

Priya glanced between them, then stood, brushing frost off her knees. "Dev and I will check the anchors above the serac line," she said, too loudly, too casually. "Okafor's on the radio housing. We'll be an hour."

They were giving her the mountain the way you give someone a room. Hazel almost thanked her and couldn't make the word come out. Priya squeezed her shoulder once on the way past, hard enough to mean something, and then it was just Hazel and Tomas and the stove hissing and the glacier ticking underneath them like a clock with no hands.

"Want me here or gone?" Tomas asked.

Here. Gone. Both. He'd been her navigator for eleven years; he knew that some coordinates you had to fix alone and some you couldn't. She looked at him — gray in his beard now, lines around his eyes she'd never noticed until this trip, because she'd spent eleven years looking at maps instead of faces — and heard herself say, "Here. But don't talk."

"I'm excellent at not talking."

"You're famously bad at it."

"Famously," he agreed, and sat down on the crate across from her, and shut up.

Hazel took out the journal.

It was smaller than she remembered it being, though she'd never actually held it before yesterday. In her childhood it had lived on her father's desk like a relic, too important to touch, and relics grow in the imagination. This one fit in her two hands with room to spare. The cover was water-stained. The spine had cracked and been mended with what looked like a strip of tent fabric and wire — his field repair, done badly, done fast, done by a man who thought he had years left and had days.

She opened it to where she'd stopped.

* * *

The entries after the summit lie were different. She'd known they would be, and still it landed like a fall.

Before the falsification, Elian Brightwater wrote the way he talked: brisk, certain, a little vain, fond of his own jokes. *Fixed ropes to the col. Marta singing again, God help us all. Weather window looks like four days if the jetstream behaves.* A man building a legend one confident sentence at a time.

After — after he'd altered the route data, after he'd sent six people up a line he knew was wrong, after the whiteout took them and the mountain kept them — the sentences changed. They got shorter. Then longer, rambling, circling back on themselves like tracks in a whiteout. He wrote about the sound the wind made against the tents at night. He wrote about Marta's singing and how he would never hear anything again without hearing it. He wrote the same sentence three times over four days: *I checked the bearing twice. I checked it twice.*

He had. That was the horror of it, Hazel thought, sitting very still while the stove ticked. He'd checked it twice and changed it anyway, because the true line added nine hours and the false line made the first ascent possible, and somewhere in the arithmetic of ambition nine hours had seemed like a number rather than a cliff face in falling weather. Six people had climbed his beautiful lie in good faith, roped together, singing, trusting the map because it bore his name and his name had meant something.

Her name. Their name. Brightwater. It had meant something for fifteen more years because she'd kept it alive, hauling it through two blacklists and a hundred legal routes, polishing it, defending it, refusing — always refusing — to look directly at the thing she half-knew glinting under the ice of it.

*I checked it twice,* her father wrote, *and then I wrote down a third answer.*

Hazel set the journal on her knee and breathed until the shaking stopped. Tomas watched her do it and honored the contract: he said nothing.

She turned the page.

The final entry began without a date. Just a heading, in handwriting that had deteriorated from the crisp surveyor's script of the early pages into something narrow and careful, the writing of a man conserving strength:

**To my daughter.**

*If you're reading this, then someone found the Meridian, which means either the glacier gave us up or you came looking, and knowing you it's the second one. You always did come looking. When you were six you followed me up the quarry path in the dark because you wanted to see where I went, and when I caught you at the treeline you weren't sorry, you were furious that I'd walked faster than you.*

*So. Here is where I went. Here is what I did.*

*You've read the rest by now, or enough of it. I won't repeat it. I'll only say that every explanation I ever built — the storm, the error, the faulty barometer, the sabotage theory I let other men argue for me at the inquiry — every one of those was a rope I tied to keep myself from falling, and none of them would have held a climber, and I knew it, and I let them be tested anyway by people who believed me.*

*Including you. Especially you. You defended me when you shouldn't have. I watched you do it through other people's reports, year after year — the hearings, the blacklist, the articles. My brave stubborn girl, spending her life holding up a map I drew crooked. I am sorry in a way that language was not built for. I have written and burned forty attempts at this page. This is the forty-first. It will have to do.*

*Now listen, because I have one thing left to give you and it isn't comfort.*

*This journal is the proof. Every date, every bearing, the original survey notes with my corrections visible, the confession in my own hand. With this, the record gets corrected. The six get their names back — their real story, not the story of the crew that died following Brightwater's genius, but the story of six competent climbers murdered by a lie. The ascent record gets struck. And you —*

*And you get your name back too. That's the part I can't stand to write and can't leave out. They blacklisted you for my sins, Haze, and you wore it like weather, and I know — I know — that some part of you believes you deserved it, because I taught you that mountains collect debts and someone always pays. It wasn't you. It was never you. This book says so in ink.*

*But there's a cost, and you should hear it from me and not discover it yourself at some press table with cameras running.*

*Burn this journal, and the legend stands. The first ascent stands. The scholarships and the hut with our name on it and the young climbers who find their nerve because a Brightwater got there first — all of it stays true in the way that helps people. Your name gets cleared eventually, maybe, quietly, some clerk reviewing the file. And you carry what I carried. You spend the rest of your life knowing the truth and standing on top of it, and it eats exactly the way it ate me. I can tell you precisely how it eats, if you want details. I have fifteen years of detail.*

*Publish it, and the legend dies. Everything built on it shakes. People who loved me will hate you for it, and they'll be wrong, and being wrong will not stop them. You will trade a clean name for a true one, and I want you to understand, with whatever's left of my authority in your life, that the true one is worth more. But it will cost you things I can't list, because I chose the other road and never got to walk yours.*

*I'm not going to tell you what to do.*

*That's the sentence I burned forty drafts to arrive at. I'm not going to tell you what to do. You're thirty-one years old and you've led crews through ground that would fold me, and you don't need your dead father issuing instructions from inside a glacier. If I order you to publish, you'll spend your life wondering whose choice it really was. If I beg you to burn it, you'll spend your life hating me for asking. So I'm leaving it where I should have left everything: in your hands.*

*This is the only honest thing I can give you. Not absolution — I don't get to hand that out, and you wouldn't take it if I offered. Not forgiveness. Just the choice itself, unwrapped, unweighted, yours. Whatever you decide, decide it as yourself. Not as my daughter cleaning up after me. As the woman who walks faster than everyone else in the dark because she wants to see where the route goes.*

*Check your knots. Trust your own bearings. And when the mountain asks you who you are, answer with your own name.*

*— E.B.*

*P.S. The quarry path, the night you followed me — I wasn't angry. I've never told anyone that. I stood there in the trees watching you climb toward me with your fists balled up and I thought: there she is. There's the real thing. Everything else I ever did was a copy of that.*

* * *

Hazel read the postscript twice.

Then she closed the journal, set it down on the crate with exaggerated care, the way you rack a tool you might need suddenly, stood up, and walked twenty paces toward the edge of camp where the ice dropped away into blue shadow, and put her hands on her knees, and was sick.

There wasn't much in her stomach. It didn't matter. She stayed bent there a long time, breathing the thin bright air, feeling the glacier breathe under her — that vast slow tidal groan, the ice settling and shifting, indifferent, patient, keeping what it keeps until it decides otherwise.

Behind her, Tomas said nothing. He was excellent at not talking. He was also, she noticed distantly, holding her tea mug refilled and waiting, which was its own kind of speech.

When she straightened, her eyes were streaming from the cold and she let that stand in for the other thing.

"He knew," she said. Her voice came out scraped. "Fifteen years ago, frozen at eight thousand meters, dying alone — and he knew exactly what it would do to me. Both ways. He mapped both routes." She laughed, and it came out wrong. "Of course he did. He was a better cartographer than he was a man."

Tomas came and stood beside her, not touching, looking out at the icefall the way navigators look at terrain — reading it, respecting it, not pretending it's anything but what it is.

"So which route?" he asked quietly.

"That's the thing." She wiped her face with the back of her glove. "He says he's not weighting it. He says it's a gift. But it's not, Tomás. It's the heaviest thing he ever handed anyone. Burn it and I become him. Publish it and I burn down everything that ever sheltered me — the hut, the record, the kids who climb because of his name. Every person who ever said *Brightwater* like it meant something good." She shook her head slowly. "He gave me a choice between two avalanches and called it honesty."

"Maybe honesty is just naming the avalanches," Tomas said. "Somebody still has to pick a slope."

Hazel looked at him. "Whose side are you on?"

"Yours. Whichever slope. That's the job." He shrugged, gray-bearded, steady as a fixed line. "But since you asked — you already decided. Somewhere between the wreck and this morning. Maybe fifteen years ago. You're just doing the paperwork now."

She wanted to argue. She opened her mouth to argue and found there was nothing in it, because he was right, and they both knew he was right, and the knowledge sat in her chest next to the journal like a second organ.

Because here was the truth she'd been circling since the crevasse wall, since Dev went quiet and strange near that rusted carabiner stamped BW, since the moment she'd knelt in the wreck of the Meridian and touched her father's frozen hand: she had never once, in fifteen years, been able to make herself defend him *all the way*. Not at the inquiry, where she'd gone silent for three days. Not in the interviews, where she'd redirected. Not in her own head at three in the morning, where the question she refused to ask had waited patiently, year after year, like a bearing she wouldn't shoot.

She'd chosen exile over certainty. Because certainty would have required her to stop loving the legend, and she hadn't been strong enough, and her father had known that too, and had loved her for it, and had left her the door out anyway.

"The six first," she said aloud. "Marta. Okonkwo. The others. Their families have spent fifteen years thinking their people died following a great man's honest mistake." Her jaw tightened. "That's not a legend. That's a debt with interest."

Tomas nodded slowly. "Voss won't see it that way."

"No. Voss sees it as a trophy." Hazel turned back toward camp, toward the journal on the crate, toward the decision sitting in her hands like live rock. "Which means I don't have the luxury of taking a month in a valley somewhere deciding how to tell it. The moment we're off this ice, Voss controls the narrative unless I'm standing in front of it."

"And if Voss decides not to wait for the valley?"

Hazel stopped walking.

She thought about Sable Voss's face at Camp Two — the hunger barely leashed behind the competence, the way her eyes had tracked the journal since the wreck like a compass needle that had finally found north. She thought about the failed radio, the porters' uneasy glances, the exit icefall groaning a full week ahead of schedule. She thought about a woman who had lost a brother to Elian Brightwater's lie and had spent fifteen years sharpening herself into the instrument that would expose it — on her terms, with her name attached, whatever it cost.

"She won't wait," Hazel said. "Not if she thinks I might burn it."

"Then you'd better decide faster than you planned to."

Hazel crossed back to the crate and picked up the journal. Against her chest it went, inside the jacket, over the heart, where it had lived since the wreck and where it would stay until it reached daylight — one way or the other.

"I'm publishing it," she said. "All of it. His words, my voice, the six named first." She met Tomas's eyes. "Whatever it costs."

Tomas exhaled, long and slow, a man setting down something heavy. "Okay," he said. "Then we move tonight. First light at the latest. Because I've been watching Voss's porters, and two of them packed their loads an hour ago, and neither one has unpacked since."

The glacier groaned beneath them, long and low, like something clearing its throat.

Hazel looked toward Voss's tents and felt the mountain shift its weight, the way it did before it took something.
