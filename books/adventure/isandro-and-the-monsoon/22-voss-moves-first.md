## 22. Voss Moves First

I wake because the generator stops.

Not the wind — the wind has been screaming against the tent fabric for hours, a sound I've learned to sleep through the way you learn to sleep through your own heartbeat. It's the generator. The low diesel stutter that Okafor runs two hours every evening to charge our batteries has gone silent mid-cycle, and in its absence the camp feels like a held breath.

I lie in my bag and listen. Camp Two at four thousand nine hundred meters is never truly quiet. The glacier talks all night — pops and groans and that long tearing sigh it makes when a serac shifts somewhere above us, deep in its throat. But this is different. This is the silence of something *missing*.

Then I hear it: boots on packed snow, moving fast, away from camp. Not one set. Three.

I'm out of my bag before I've finished the thought, hands finding my headlamp in the dark by muscle memory, and by the time I get the zipper open Priya is already emerging from the medic tent across the way, her lamp beam cutting through the spindrift like a searchlight.

"Tell me you heard that," she says.

"Generator's dead. Someone's walking out."

"Tomas?"

"Getting him."

We don't run — running at altitude at night is how people die stupidly — but we move fast, hunched into the wind, and we find Tomas already at Voss's tent with his lamp inside it, lighting up an interior so empty it looks like a stage after the play has closed. Sleeping bag gone. Rope rack gone. The aluminum expedition cases she'd kept locked since base camp: gone. Even the small brass compass she used to fidget with during briefings, turning it over and over between her fingers while Hazel talked about route timing — gone.

"She took the porters," Tomas says. His voice is flat, which is how I know he's frightened. In the three weeks I've known him, Tomas has been everything except flat. "Dorje and Pasang. Their tents are stripped."

"The radio," Priya says.

We all look toward the comms tent at the same moment, and nobody moves for a second, because we already know.

Okafor beats us there. He's standing in the doorway holding the radio's mounting bracket in his bare hand, and even in the dark I can see the shape of his shoulders, which is the shape of a man trying very hard not to break something.

"Backup unit's still in the sled at base camp," he says. "Two days' travel down. If the route holds."

Nobody says what we're all thinking, which is that the route hasn't held since the storm broke yesterday morning and the glacier started shedding its skin.

* * *

Hazel takes the news the way she takes everything — standing very still, arms crossed, looking at the ground as if the answer might be written in the snow. We gather in the cook tent because it's the only place with a stove lit, and the five of us stand around it in a half-circle while the wind tries to peel the walls off the poles.

"Walk me through it," Hazel says. "Times."

Okafor has reconstructed the night from small evidences, the way he reconstructs anything mechanical. "Generator cut at approximately 0200. That wasn't failure — I checked the fuel line when I found it. Someone closed the valve. Clean shutdown." He holds up a gloved hand and counts. "Voss's tent was struck sometime after that. Dorje and Pasang pulled their kit around the same time — their tent pegs were pulled, not cut, so they left calm, no hurry. The radio was last. They went down-valley, toward the Meridian col. Tracks are filling in fast but they're fresh enough to follow."

"Why take the porters?" Priya asks. "She can't pay them."

"She doesn't need to pay them," Tomas says quietly. "Dorje's sister works for Voss's logistics company in Kathmandu. Has for two years. I found that out at base camp and didn't think it mattered." He looks up, and there's something raw in his face. "It matters now."

"So she planned this," I say. "Not tonight. Months ago."

Everyone looks at me. I'm the rookie; I've learned to be careful about speaking first. But Hazel nods, once, encouraging, and that single nod steadies me more than I want to admit.

"She's had a team on standby the whole time," I say. "A route out that doesn't need us. A reason for the porters to follow her that predates this trip. She didn't steal the journal on impulse. She stole it on schedule."

The stove hisses. Somewhere outside, the glacier makes a sound like a door closing in an enormous house.

"And the journal," Hazel says. Her voice is level. Too level. "And the survey tube."

"Yes," Tomas says.

Hazel closes her eyes. For a moment — just a moment — she looks younger than I've ever seen her, like a woman standing in the ruins of a decision she made fifteen years ago and is only now understanding the full cost of. Then she opens them and she's the guide again, and I almost prefer the other version, because at least that one was honest.

"Okay," she says. "Here's where we are. Voss has the journal, the survey tube, two porters who'll do what she says, and the only functioning radio within thirty kilometers. Without that radio we cannot call for evacuation, weather updates, or rescue if someone goes into a crevasse. Without the journal, my father stays a legend and six dead climbers stay a mystery, and Sable Voss gets to decide which story the world hears first." She lets that sit. "And without us, she has no witnesses. No one to contradict whatever version she broadcasts from the valley."

"She wouldn't hurt us," I say, and immediately hear how young it sounds.

"No," Hazel agrees. "She won't hurt us. She doesn't have to. She just has to be faster than us, and the route is closing." She turns to Okafor. "How long to strip camp?"

"Forty minutes if we leave the heavy tents."

"We leave the heavy tents." She's already moving, pulling her pack from under the bench. "Warm layers, two days of food, ropes, anchors. Everything else stays. We travel light and we travel now."

Priya steps into her path. "Hazel. The icefall."

"I know."

"Do you? Because I stood at the edge of it this afternoon and watched a serac the size of a cathedral lean eleven degrees further than it leaned yesterday. The exit route we came up through is dying in real time. Going down after her means racing a structure that is actively collapsing, at night, in wind, chasing a woman who has a twelve-hour head start and no reason to wait for safe conditions."

Hazel meets her eyes. "Yes," she says. "All of that."

"And we're going anyway."

"We're going anyway."

* * *

We move at 0340.

There's a strange clarity to breaking camp in the dark, a rhythm that takes over where thought leaves off. Okafor kills the stove and drains the fuel line. Priya strips the medical kit to essentials, leaving the bulk of it cached and marked for any party coming behind us, though we both know no one is coming behind us. Tomas folds the maps with the precision of a man folding a flag. I haul the sled out of the snow and gut it, keeping rope and hardware, abandoning everything that doesn't earn its weight in grams.

Hazel stands apart, at the edge of the light, looking down-valley toward the col where Voss went. I watch her from the corner of my eye while I coil rope, and what I see frightens me more than the theft did. It isn't rage. It isn't grief. It's recognition — the look of someone watching a pattern complete itself, a thing she'd feared so long that its arrival is almost a relief.

"Fifteen years ago," I say, before I can stop myself, "did she do something like this?"

Hazel doesn't turn. "Worse," she says. "But she was on my side of it then." A pause. "That's the part that should scare you. People don't become her. They just stop hiding it."

I want to ask what happened fifteen years ago. Everyone wants to ask; nobody does. There's a locked room in Hazel Brightwater's past and we've all agreed, without ever discussing it, to keep our hands off the handle. But standing here in the wind with her father's stolen journal somewhere down that black valley, I feel the lock straining.

Instead I say, "I'll take point on the descent until the icefall. You should save your eyes for the route."

She looks at me then, really looks, and something passes over her face that might be gratitude or might be pity — the rookie volunteering to walk first into the dark, thinking he's helping, not understanding yet that point position on a dying glacier is where guides put the people they can most afford to lose.

"Roped to me," she says. "Not ahead of me. Nobody walks unroped tonight."

* * *

The tracks are easy to follow, at first.

Three sets of boot prints and the shallow trench of a sled runner, descending the glacier's medial moraine toward the col, crisp-edged in the fresh spindrift. Voss isn't hiding her trail. That bothers me more than if she had. A hidden track means fear; an open one means confidence, or contempt, or a message: *come and see.*

By 0500 the sky has gone the color of dirty pewter and we can see the col ahead, a saddle of wind-scoured ice between two ridgelines, maybe ninety minutes out at our pace. The wind has dropped, which somehow makes everything worse — the silence lets you hear the glacier working, the deep structural groans of a body under stress, ice settling and shifting and failing in places we can't see.

Okafor calls a halt at the top of a rise and crouches over the tracks. "They stopped here." He points with his ice axe. "Stood around in a group. Then—" He traces the prints with a finger. "Dorje and Pasang continue down. Voss's prints go up."

"Up?" Priya says. "Up where?"

We follow the line of her tracks with our lamps. They climb the flank of the ridge to our right, switchbacking up a slope of blue ice toward a rocky spur perhaps two hundred meters above us, and end at a cairn.

"There's nothing up there," Tomas says. "I scoped that ridge on the approach. It's a dead-end spur."

"Then why would she climb it in the middle of the night?" I ask.

Hazel answers without taking her eyes off the spur. "To look back."

We stand in the wind and consider that. And then I understand, all at once, the way you understand a trap only after the jaw has closed — not on your leg, not yet, but close enough to feel the air move.

"She's watching the route," I say. "She climbed up there to watch us come down through the icefall. To time us."

"To make sure we're committed," Hazel corrects quietly. "Once we're on the lower icefall, there's no turning back to Camp Two. The upper route will be gone behind us. She wanted to see us enter the funnel before she walked out of it." She turns to face us, and the gray dawn light catches the lines of her face and makes her look carved rather than tired. "She's not running from us. She's herding us."

"Onto a collapsing route," Priya says, "with no radio."

"With no radio," Hazel agrees. "If we die in the icefall, it's an accident. Tragic. Unavoidable. The monsoon came early; the mountain did what mountains do. And the only people who could tell a different story are the ones she's watching walk into it."

The wind picks up again, moaning over the col, and for a long moment none of us speaks. I look at each of them — Okafor with his jaw set like a man reading bad news twice to be sure, Priya already checking the medical kit straps out of pure reflex, Tomas staring down the tracks with an expression I can't read, and Hazel, who has gone very calm, the way water goes calm right before it goes over the falls.

"We could go back," I say. "Wait it out at Camp Two. Signal from the ridge when the weather clears. Someone flies over eventually—"

"Eleven days," Hazel says. "That's the window. Today is day seven. The calving season started early, and every hour the lower icefall loses another piece of itself. Waiting doesn't make the route safer, Dev. It makes it nonexistent." She shoulders her pack. "Voss counted on us doing exactly what we're doing. So we do it — but on our terms, at our pace, roped as a team, and we come out the bottom of that icefall with the truth still ours to tell. She can carry the paper. Paper isn't the story. We are."

She starts down toward the col without waiting for agreement, because Hazel Brightwater has never once waited for agreement, and the strange thing — the thing I'll think about later, much later, hanging in the dark — is that we all follow. Instantly. Without a word. Four people tying themselves to a woman leading them into a funnel of falling ice after a thief, and following gladly, because the alternative is standing still in the wind while the world decides our story without us.

Tomas falls in beside me near the bottom of the rise. "Dev," he says, low, under the wind. "When we catch her — and we will catch her — stay close to Hazel. Whatever happens, whatever Voss says, whatever Hazel does." He glances forward at the straight line of Hazel's back. "Especially whatever Hazel does."

"What's that supposed to mean?"

He doesn't answer. Ahead of us, the col opens onto the upper icefall, and even from here, even in the half-light, I can see what the storm has done to it — towers leaning, bridges sagging, the whole glittering architecture of the route rearranged into something that no longer matches the map in anyone's head.

Hazel stops at the edge and studies it the way she studies everything, utterly still, utterly focused, and then she says, almost too quiet to hear:

"There she is."

Far below, tiny against the vast gray sweep of the glacier, three figures and a sled are just disappearing into the mouth of the icefall — and above them, on the rocky spur, a fourth figure stands motionless beside a cairn, watching us watch her.

Sable Voss raises one hand. Not a wave. Something closer to a salute.

Then she turns, and descends into the ice, and is gone.
