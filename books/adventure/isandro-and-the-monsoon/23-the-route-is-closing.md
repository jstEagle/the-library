## 23. The Route Is Closing

Priya felt it before she heard it — a tremor through the soles of her boots, faint as a heartbeat under the floor of the world. She was crouched at the edge of Camp Two, rinsing a suture tray with snow-melt, when the glacier spoke.

Not groaned. Spoke. A long, wet exhalation from somewhere down-valley, deep enough that the sound arrived through the ice before it arrived through the air. The suture water shivered in the basin. Somewhere above them, a serac the size of a cathedral shifted its weight and settled with a crack that rolled on for ten full seconds.

She set the tray down and stood very still, the way she'd learned to do in the earthquake zones of her residency years, listening with her whole body. Fifteen years of mountain medicine had taught her that disasters rarely announced themselves honestly. They murmured. They cleared their throats. They gave you exactly one chance to hear the difference between a glacier settling and a glacier leaving.

This was the second kind.

"Tomas." She didn't raise her voice. She didn't need to; he'd gone still too, halfway through coiling rope by the cook tent, his head tilted like a bird dog's. "Tell me you heard that."

"I heard it." He set the coil down without finishing it, which — in fifteen years of knowing Tomas — Priya could count on one hand. "That came from below us."

"Below," she agreed. "Down-route."

They looked at each other across the camp, and Priya watched him do the same arithmetic she'd just done: their exit lay through the lower icefall, the route they'd climbed four days ago, the seracs and snow bridges Hazel had threaded them through with that uncanny, surgical calm. If the sound had come from up-glacier, from the unclimbed reaches toward the summit wall, it would have been weather. Noise. Ambience.

From below, it was a door closing.

* * *

By the time the light came up — grey, bruised, the monsoon pressing down like a thumb — Hazel had already been to the edge of the ridge above camp twice with binoculars, and come back the second time moving faster than Priya had ever seen her move on flat ground.

"Gather everyone," Hazel said. Not *wake everyone*. Gather. Which meant she'd already woken them, or they were already awake, because nobody sleeps through a glacier changing its mind.

They crowded into the cook tent, five of them plus the two porters Voss hadn't taken, steam rising off six mugs nobody drank from. Dev sat closest to the stove, still hollow-eyed from the crevasse two days ago, the rope burns on his forearms freshly dressed. Okafor had his radio open on his knee out of pure habit, though they all knew it was dead weight since the night it failed. And Hazel stood at the mouth of the tent with the wind at her back, and Priya watched her decide how much truth to hand over.

Hazel always decided. It was one of the things Priya loved about her and one of the things that frightened her: the way she rationed fear like a medic rationing morphine, enough to keep you sharp, never enough to drop your pressure.

"The lower icefall is moving," Hazel said. No preamble. "The serac field we came up through has shifted. I counted three new collapse scars this morning where there was clean ice four days ago. The bridges we used are either gone or compromised, and the meltwater channels are running at volume they shouldn't hit for another week."

"Calving season," Tomas said quietly.

"Calving season. Early." Hazel let that sit. "The tongue is letting go ahead of schedule. The monsoon heat came in fast and the glacier is answering it. Everything between here and base camp is now a structure in active demolition."

Dev's voice came out smaller than usual. "How long do we have?"

Priya watched Hazel weigh the answer, and watched her choose honesty — which told Priya more than any number would have about what Hazel thought those numbers meant.

"If the rate holds? Three days. Maybe four if we're lucky and the cold snap the radio promised before it died actually arrives. But glaciers don't read forecasts." Hazel's eyes went around the tent, touching each of them. "The window we climbed up through is closing. We go down through it while there's still a down to go through, or we stay here and become part of the survey record."

Nobody laughed. Nobody moved. The stove ticked.

Okafor broke the silence the way he always did — practically, hands first. "Then we pack now and we climb now. What are we waiting for?"

"Light," Hazel said. "And Voss."

The name landed in the tent like a dropped tool.

"She took the journal and the tube," Dev said. "She took the radio. Why would we wait for—"

"Because she took two porters and headed *up*-route yesterday," Hazel said, "and this morning I found her tracks coming back down. She's behind us now, not ahead. Between us and the exit." She paused. "I want to know what she intends before we're all on the same rope together in a collapsing icefall. That's not sentiment. That's threat assessment."

Priya said nothing, but she caught Tomas's eye, and in his face she saw the thing they'd all been circling since the theft: Sable Voss had stolen the proof of Elian Brightwater's lie, and every hour she held it was an hour Hazel's choice — the choice she'd made at the wreck, jaw set, eyes wet, saying *the truth goes home with me* — hung unfinished in the air.

But that was a human problem. The glacier didn't care whose name got cleared. The glacier was simply, indifferently, ending the argument.

* * *

They spent the morning packing the way surgeons close: fast, ordered, no wasted motion. Priya stripped the med kit to essentials — trauma dressings, the good painkillers, splint material, antibiotics — and left behind anything that weighed more than it might save. She wrapped the frostbite ointment separately because Dev's fingers were still mottled from the crevasse and she'd be re-dressing them on the move. She made Dev carry his own kit and checked the fit of his harness twice without explaining why, and he let her, which was its own kind of progress. Three days ago he'd have bristled. Now he just lifted his arms like a kid being buckled into a coat.

"You're quiet," he said, as she cinched the last strap.

"I'm counting," she said.

"Counting what?"

"Everything." She nodded down-valley, where the cloud deck sat low and brown-grey over the tongue. "Steps. Bridges. Hours. People." She met his eyes. "It's what I do when the math starts to scare me. I make the math bigger than the fear."

"What's the count say?"

Priya considered lying to him, then remembered where lies had gotten everyone on this mountain — a father, a rival, a legend, six graves in the ice. "The count says we can make it if nothing else breaks and nobody hesitates. The count also says we've spent our margin. Whatever happens next, happens with no room left to be wrong."

Dev absorbed that. He was twenty-three and he'd signed an indenture contract to escape a debt and followed a blacklisted guide onto a dying glacier, and somewhere in the last week he'd stopped being cargo and started being crew. She saw it happen again right now, in real time: the swallow, the straightening.

"Okay," he said. "Then let's not be wrong."

* * *

At midday the mountain answered their plans.

It began as a sound like a freight train running on no rails, and Priya knew — everyone knew — before anyone said the word. They spilled out of the tents onto the ridge in time to see it, half a kilometer down-route: a section of the lower icefall's eastern flank, a serac face taller than a city block, leaning out of the glacier's side like a rotten tooth finally giving up its grip.

It did not fall so much as *leave*. The whole mass rotated, ponderous, almost gentle, shedding plates of blue ice that caught the flat monsoon light — and then it hit the glacier's body and the gentleness ended. The impact came up through their boots a full two seconds after the sight of it. The report arrived after that, rolling up the valley and bouncing off walls that had no interest in absorbing it. Powder snow fountained two hundred meters into the air and hung there, drifting, obscene in its slowness.

When the echo died, the silence underneath it was worse.

"That," Okafor said finally, "was not there yesterday."

"It was there yesterday," Hazel said. Her voice was level but Priya, standing close, could see the pulse jumping in her throat. "It was holding yesterday. Different thing." She had the binoculars up, sweeping the route below. Priya watched her face do something complicated around the eyes. "Our ascent line ran within two hundred meters of that face."

Nobody said what everyone was thinking: that they'd threaded that gap four days ago believing they had eleven days of season left, and the season had lied.

Tomas crouched and pulled out his notebook — the paper backup, the navigator's religion — and began sketching the icefall from memory, marking what they'd climbed and what the collapse had likely erased. Priya knelt beside him. "Can we still use the line?" she asked.

"Some of it. The upper third should be intact. The middle—" His pencil hovered. "The middle is a question mark. The lower section near the tongue is going to be a river by tomorrow. Meltwater channels are already opening. We may end up descending things we climbed, which is slower, or finding new line, which is worse."

"How much worse?"

He looked up at her, and behind the professional calm she saw the other thing he carried — the survey tube's loss, his father-in-law's handwriting in Voss's stolen possession, grief doing push-ups behind his eyes. "Worse enough that we should stop talking about it and start walking."

* * *

They struck Camp Two at 1300 hours, which was late, which was unavoidable, which Hazel logged aloud in that flat voice she used for facts that hurt. The plan was simple and terrible: descend to the shoulder above the icefall by nightfall, camp once, then commit to the full descent at first light with maximum daylight. One camp between them and base camp. One night on a glacier that had begun eating itself.

Priya took point position behind Hazel on the rope, with Dev behind her and Okafor anchoring the rear — the arrangement they'd fallen into without discussion, the medic near the front where casualties would happen first, the mechanic at the back where equipment would fail first. Tomas floated, reading the route. It was, Priya reflected as they moved, the most honest expression of who they were: not a team so much as a nervous system, each of them an organ doing the one job it existed for.

The glacier made conversation impossible and unnecessary. Every hundred meters, something shifted, cracked, sighed, or collapsed somewhere out of sight. The sounds had changed register overnight — deeper, wetter, less like groaning and more like chewing. Snow bridges that had held crisp and white on the ascent now sagged in their middles, stained grey with melt, leaking. Twice Hazel stopped the rope and probed a bridge with the axe until it rang solid, and both times the ring came back duller than it should have, and both times she found another line, wider, uglier, safer.

On the third crossing, Dev slipped.

It wasn't dramatic — a boot punching through a rotted lip of snow to the shin, a lurch, Priya's hand closing on his pack strap before his brain finished registering the fall. She hauled him back onto firm ice and held on a beat longer than physics required.

"Sorry," he breathed.

"Don't apologize to me. Apologize to the glacier by stepping where I step." She kept her grip until he nodded. Under her palm, through the fabric, his heart was going like a trapped bird. So, she noticed distantly, was hers. "Breathe. Four counts in, six out. You know the drill."

"You taught me the drill."

"Then it's working." She released him. "Move."

Behind them, Okafor's voice, dry as chalk: "For the record, I liked this glacier better when it was asleep."

"For the record," Hazel called back, not turning, "keep moving."

* * *

They reached the shoulder at dusk, a shelf of old compacted ice wedged against the valley wall where the glacier's lateral shear had piled up a rampart of rubble. It was the last defensible ground before the icefall proper, and everyone understood without saying it that tomorrow they'd trade this relative safety for the killing floor below.

Camp went up in twenty minutes — faster than they'd ever built it, hands moving on muscle memory and adrenaline. Priya dosed Dev's fingers, checked the porters' feet, forced calories into everyone including herself because hypoglycemic judgment kills more mountaineers than avalanches do. She worked by headlamp inside the cook tent while outside the glacier performed its nightly orchestra, and she catalogued the sounds the way she'd once catalogued fetal heart tones: normal, abnormal, *get the doctor*.

There were more of the third category tonight than she wanted.

Hazel ducked in at full dark, rimed with spindrift, and sat across from her without being asked. For a while neither spoke. The stove hissed. Down-valley, something enormous calved off the tongue with a boom that arrived muffled, like a door slamming in another wing of the house.

"Give it to me straight," Hazel said.

Priya looked up. "You already know."

"I want to hear you say it. Your read carries different weight than mine. Mine wants us to make it."

So Priya gave her the medical version, because it was the truest one she had. "Pulse rate of the glacier has doubled since this morning. Collapse events are coming every eight to twelve minutes instead of every twenty. The meltwater is doing what meltwater does — lubricating the failures, opening channels under the bridges we can't see. Tomorrow will be harder than today, and the day after will be worse than tomorrow, and the curve doesn't bend back in our favor." She held Hazel's gaze. "We have one shot at the icefall, and it's tomorrow morning, early, in the coldest hours. After that we're not climbing down a route anymore. We're outrunning one."

Hazel nodded slowly. "Casualty scenario. Worst case. Talk me through it."

"Someone goes into a crevasse on descent, in a destabilizing field, with no radio and no external rescue. We have the skills. We don't have the time. A hanging extraction that took forty minutes on Day Eight takes eighty when the anchors are suspect and the ice is failing around you." Priya let that land. "So the honest answer to your real question is: we can't afford another Dev. Not because of Dev. Because of the clock."

Something moved through Hazel's face — Priya watched it pass, named it privately, and said nothing. It was guilt. It was the look of a woman doing arithmetic with people instead of numbers, and finding the sum unbearable, and carrying it anyway because someone had to.

"We'll be careful," Hazel said.

"We'll be careful," Priya agreed, "and we'll be lucky, and we'll need both."

* * *

She stepped out of the tent at midnight for the ritual round — stove, ropes, boots, sky — and stopped.

The clouds had torn open along the valley's axis, a ragged seam of stars overhead, and in the starlight she could see down-route further than she wanted to. The tongue of the Kharun Glacier lay below them like a pale road, and the road was *moving*. Not visibly, not the way water moves — but she could see the fresh scar of the afternoon's collapse, and beyond it a shimmer where new fracture lines caught the light, spiderwebbing across the route they'd climbed eleven days ago in innocence and ambition.

Eleven days. The window Voss had bought them. It had been exactly honest and exactly a trap: long enough to get in, short enough that getting out was never guaranteed, and everyone had signed anyway because the money was real and the debts were realer.

A sound rose from the dark below — long, rising, almost musical, a note held by something with no lungs. Then a crack like the world's largest branch breaking, and a section of the icefall's spine, distant and vast, folded into itself. The powder cloud bloomed silver under the stars.

Priya stood in the cold and listened to the clock.

It wasn't ticking. Glaciers didn't tick. It was tolling — slow, massive, patient — and every stroke closed another meter of the road home.

Behind her, the tent flap rustled. Dev, sleepless, drawn by the noise, stood beside her and followed her gaze down to the shining, breaking dark.

"Priya," he said. "Is that the route?"

She found his hand in the dark and squeezed it once, because the truth was the only currency she had left worth spending.

"No," she said. "That's what's left of it. We leave at first light — and we race the ice the whole way down."

Below them, as if it had heard, the glacier answered with another stroke of the bell.
