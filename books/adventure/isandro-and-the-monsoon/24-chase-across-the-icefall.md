## 24. Chase Across the Icefall

I woke to the sound of Okafor swearing, which was how I knew it was bad. Okafor didn't swear at broken gear — he swore at betrayal.

"The radio's gone," he said. "The backup beacon too. And she took Ravi and Sena."

Voss. Gone in the night with the journal and the survey tube and my father's confession, across the icefall we'd spent four days learning like a language, and she'd taken the only two porters strong enough to move fast on a rope team.

I stood in the gray pre-dawn light outside my tent and did the arithmetic I'd been doing since I was nineteen years old, when a ranger first told me my father's expedition had gone silent on the Kharun. Distances. Loads. Light remaining. The route below us had maybe three days before calving season turned it into a shooting gallery of falling ice, and Voss had a six-hour head start and no reason to be careful.

"She'll kill herself doing this," Priya said, zipping her jacket to her chin. "Or the porters."

"She might kill all of us," Tomas said quietly. He was looking at me, not at the glacier. "If we go after her, we're choosing this. Nobody's making us."

Dev was already packing rope. Of course he was. The kid had been ready to chase her since he'd found the BW carabiner in the crevasse wall, since before that, maybe — since the day he'd signed his contract and learned what debt could make a person carry.

I looked down the route. The icefall dropped away from Camp Two in a staircase of seracs, blue-green towers the size of buildings, leaning against each other the way drunks lean in doorways. Between them ran the snow bridges we'd crossed coming up, each one now softened by two days of monsoon rain and melt. Somewhere below, the glacier's tongue was already groaning its morning complaints.

"We're going after her," I said. "Not to fight. To talk. She has my father's journal and I have questions that don't have answers yet, and I'm not letting her walk out of here with the only version of events anyone will ever hear."

Tomas held my gaze for a long moment. Then he shouldered his pack without another word, which from Tomas was a whole speech.

* * *

We moved fast and roped short. That's the first rule of chasing someone through an icefall: you can't actually chase them, because the ice doesn't care who's winning. You move as fast as the mountain allows, which is never fast enough, and you hate every second of it because every second is a choice between speed and survival.

By mid-morning we'd picked up their track — three sets of boot prints and a sledge trail where they'd dragged something heavy, probably the survey tube lashed to gear. The prints were fresh enough that the wind hadn't started erasing them. Six hours, maybe seven.

"Moving slow," Okafor said, crouching over the tracks. "Roped up, careful. She's not taking risks with the porters."

"She's taking risks with everything else," Priya said. She pointed east, where a serac the size of a chapel hung over the route at an angle that made my teeth ache. "That wasn't leaning like that yesterday."

It hadn't been. Two days of warm rain had gotten into the icefall's joints like water into a wall's mortar, and the whole structure was renegotiating its terms. We crossed beneath that serac at a dead run, unroped for thirty meters because roped would have meant all of us died together instead of one of us dying alone, and I hated myself for knowing that calculation so well.

We caught sight of them at noon.

Far below, tiny against the blue-white chaos, three figures moving along a shelf of ice between two serac walls. Voss in front, moving well — she always moved well, that was the insult of Sable Voss, that she was genuinely good at this — and behind her the porters, Ravi and Sena, roped together, hauling the sledge.

"There." Dev's voice cracked with the effort of keeping it level. "There they are."

"Don't shout," I said. "Sound travels weird in here. And don't run. Running is how people die in icefalls."

But I was already moving faster than I'd told him to.

* * *

The afternoon became a geometry problem with death as the wrong answer.

They were below us by perhaps four hundred meters of elevation, traversing a line that would take them to the lower icefall — the exit gates, the section we'd climbed on Day One, the Gate, where the route narrowed to a single corridor between two walls of compressed ice. If Voss reached the Gate ahead of us, she'd be through into the stable glacier below, and then it wouldn't matter how fast we descended; she'd have a day's lead and a radio and the story.

So we cut the line. Instead of following their track down, we traversed west, dropping through a maze of collapsed blocks that I remembered from the ascent — a route I'd rated marginal then and was betting our lives on now.

Tomas called the turns. He'd memorized the icefall on the way up, every anchor, every bridge, every place where the blue ice showed through thin snow. "Left at the block," he'd call, and I'd cut left, and behind me the crew would follow on the rope, and somewhere below us Voss's team would hear nothing but their own footsteps and think they were alone.

For two hours we descended like thieves, silent, fast, gaining on them through the simple fact that we weren't dragging a sledge.

Then the mountain spoke.

It wasn't a sound you hear so much as a sound you feel — a deep tectonic groan from the heart of the icefall, followed by a crack like the sky splitting. Every climber knows that sound. It's the sound of the mountain deciding something.

"Move!" I shouted, and we ran.

Behind us and above, a serac the size of a house let go. I didn't look back. Looking back costs time, and time was the only currency we had left. The impact came as a pressure wave and a white blast of powder snow that rolled over us like a wave breaking, and then silence, and then the fine rain of ice crystals ticking against my hood.

When the air cleared, we looked down.

The serac fall had taken out the shelf Voss's team had been traversing twenty minutes earlier. Where they'd walked there was now a scar of raw blue ice and a debris field of blocks the size of trucks. But the track continued below the debris — and so did they. Three dots, still moving, smaller now with distance.

"They felt it," Priya said. "They know we're here."

No. Not we. Me. Because ten minutes later, when we rounded a serac wall and found ourselves looking straight down at them across a gap of fifty meters, Sable Voss stopped, turned, and looked directly up at me.

Even at that distance I could see her face do something complicated. Not surprise — she must have heard the serac, done her own arithmetic, known we'd come. Something else. Recognition, maybe. Acknowledgment. Fifteen years of rivalry distilled into a single glance across a glacier.

Then she turned and kept moving, faster now.

* * *

We reached them at the top of the lower icefall, or near enough — near enough to speak, which in icefall terms is a kind of intimacy.

The route funneled toward the Gate: a corridor forty meters wide between two serac walls, floored with a series of snow bridges over a crevasse that ran the width of the passage. We'd crossed those bridges on Day One, four of them in sequence, testing each with our axes, moving one at a time. In four days of warmth and rain, they would have changed. Everything had changed.

"Sable!" My voice bounced off the ice walls. "We need to talk!"

She stopped. The porters stopped behind her, and I saw Ravi's face turn up toward us, and I saw the expression on it — exhausted, frightened, caught between two employers in a place where employers' quarrels got people killed.

"You want to talk?" Voss called back. Her voice carried perfectly in the cold air, crisp and mocking. "Talk while you walk, Hazel. This isn't a place for standing around."

She was right, and I hated that she was right.

"We both come out of here or nobody does," I shouted. "Give me the journal and we figure this out on the glacier below. You have my word."

"Your word." A laugh, sharp as falling ice. "Your word is worth exactly what your father's maps were worth, Brightwater. Six lives."

The words hit like a blow. She knew. Of course she knew — she'd read the journal, or enough of it. She knew what Elian Brightwater had done, and she was using it as a weapon, and the worst part, the part I'd carry home if I carried anything home, was that some cold chamber of my heart agreed with her.

"I didn't falsify anything," I said. "And neither did you. Come back and we settle this like professionals."

"Professionals." She turned away. "Keep up or don't."

They moved into the corridor. We followed.

* * *

Here is what I remember about the next hour, in fragments, because memory under adrenaline works that way — sharp slices with black space between:

Dev crossing the first bridge, testing it twice, waving us on. The bridge holding. The second bridge holding. The third bridge sagging visibly in the middle, a shallow dish of snow where four days ago there'd been a flat line, and me calling a halt and re-rigging the crossing with a hand line anchored to a screw set in the far lip, and Voss's team waiting on the far side, watching us work, because they couldn't go on without us and we couldn't go back the way we'd come.

That was the joke of it. The absurd, bitter joke. We were roped to each other now by geography if nothing else. The corridor was a dead end unless you crossed the bridges, and the bridges were failing, and there was exactly one way out for everyone.

Voss watched me rig the hand line with an expression I couldn't read. When we waved her team forward, she sent the porters first — Ravi, then Sena, each one clipped to the line, each one crossing the sagging bridge with the careful, mechanical steps of people who understood exactly how much snow was under their boots and how little of it was trustworthy.

Sena made it. Ravi made it. Then Voss crossed, light and quick, barely weighting the line.

Which left us.

I went last. That's not heroics; that's procedure — the most experienced climber crosses a suspect bridge last, because if it fails under the leader, everyone behind dies with her, and if it fails under the last person, only one person dies.

I stepped onto the bridge and the snow spoke to me. Not groaned — spoke, a soft compressive whisper, the sound of crystals rearranging themselves around my weight. Halfway across, the whisper became a crack, a bright sharp report right under my left boot, and I did what you do, which is distribute, freeze, listen.

The bridge held. I finished the crossing with my pulse hammering in my ears and my face burning with something that wasn't exertion.

Voss was watching me from five meters away. "Still teaching yourself lessons the hard way," she said.

"Still stealing other people's histories," I said.

Something flickered across her face — hurt, actual hurt, quickly buried. Then she turned and led on, and I told myself I'd imagined it, and knew I hadn't.

* * *

The fourth bridge was the one that broke.

Later, everyone would describe it differently. Dev said it went all at once. Priya said there was a warning, a settling, a softness underfoot that she'd clocked and dismissed. Okafor, who was nearest, said the truth, which was that it did neither — it simply stopped being there, the way a promise stops being there, between one step and the next.

What I know is this: Voss's team had just crossed it. All three of them, in sequence, roped, spread along the bridge's length. Ravi was stepping off the far side. Sena was in the middle. Voss was two steps behind her.

And I was fifteen meters back, watching, when the bridge exhaled.

There was no crack this time. No warning whisper. The snow simply sank — a smooth, almost gentle collapse, like a breath leaving — and the crevasse opened beneath it, and Sena dropped.

The rope caught her. That's the whole religion of mountaineering in one sentence: the rope catches you. Voss went down to the snow, braced, and the rope snapped taut, and Sena swung once against the far wall of the crevasse and hung there, screaming, over whatever depth of blue dark waited below.

We ran forward. There was no decision in it. Okafor and Dev reached the far lip and set anchors, and I rigged a haul system with hands that knew the work even while the rest of me was somewhere cold and old, watching another rope go taut over another void fifteen years ago.

"Hold it!" I shouted at Voss. "Don't move! Hold the load!"

"I'm holding the load!" she screamed back, and her voice had lost every ounce of its mockery. What was left underneath sounded young. Scared. Human.

We hauled. Sena rose out of the crevasse in a spray of snow, clawing at the lip, and Dev grabbed her harness and dragged her onto solid ice, and she lay there gasping like a landed fish, and for one moment — one stupid, shining moment — we were all just climbers, six people on a glacier who had saved a life together and were entitled to exactly one breath of shared relief.

Then Voss stood up, and I saw her look at the sledge, and at us, and do the arithmetic.

"No," I said, though she hadn't said anything.

"You have a radio," I said. "You have the journal. And now you've seen what this route does to bridges. Come with us. All of you. We walk out together and we settle this with lawyers instead of ice axes."

For a long moment she actually considered it. I watched her consider it. Fifteen years I'd known Sable Voss, and I had never once seen her hesitate, and there it was — hesitation, naked and brief as a bird's shadow.

Then she looked past me, down-glacier, and her expression changed.

I turned to follow her gaze. Below us, the lower icefall stretched toward the glacier tongue — and along its whole length, the seracs were moving. Not falling, not yet. Shifting. Leaning into new angles with the slow, grinding patience of a thing that had all the time in the world and intended to use it.

Calving season, opening early. The route closing from below while we stood arguing above it.

"Eleven days," Voss said softly. "That was the window. We're on day eight, Hazel. Do your own math."

She unclipped the sledge from the porters' rope, transferred it to her own harness, and started down alone — leaving Ravi and Sena standing on the ice with us, released, abandoned, free.

"Wait—" Dev started forward.

"Let her go," I said, and the words tasted like ash. "Not into that. Sable — the lower icefall is unstable. Take the west line, the one we climbed. It's slower but it holds. Do you hear me? The west line!"

She raised one hand — acknowledgment or dismissal, impossible to tell — and dropped into the corridor, moving fast, a small dark figure descending into a landscape that was rearranging itself around her with every minute.

And then the mountain spoke again, deeper than before, longer, and the bridge we'd all just crossed — the third one, the one I'd re-rigged — tilted, sagged, and folded into the crevasse behind us, cutting off the route back to Camp Two with a sound like a door slamming.

Silence. Wind. The tick of melting ice.

"Well," said Okafor, into the quiet. "Now it's a race."

I looked down the corridor at Voss's shrinking figure, at the shifting seracs, at the two abandoned porters shivering on the ice beside my crew, and I understood with total clarity that the mountain had just taken the choice out of all our hands. There was one way out. It was closing. And the woman ahead of us was moving too fast and too carelessly, because grief makes people careless, and I knew that better than anyone alive.

"Rope up," I said. "All six of us. We're going down."

Dev looked at me. "After her?"

"After her," I said. "Because the only thing worse than losing her to the ice is watching it happen and doing nothing."

And we dropped into the corridor, into the grinding, groaning dark of the closing icefall, running a race whose finish line was a doorway that was already swinging shut.
