## 25. One Porter Turns Back

Okafor found the man at the edge of a meltwater channel, half-buried in slush like something the glacier had tried to swallow and given up on.

He'd been scanning the terrain with binoculars every hour since dawn — not for Voss, not anymore. Voss was two seracs ahead and climbing fast, and chasing her across ground that moved underfoot was how crews became corpses. Hazel had made that call an hour ago without raising her voice: *We don't race her. We outlast her.* So Okafor had taken the glasses and swept the flanks instead, because that was his job now — reading the mountain's machinery while everyone else watched the woman running across it.

The glint came from a boot sole. Reflective strip, cheap trekking boot, the kind outfitters in the valley sold to porters who couldn't afford better.

"Contact," he said into the radio. "Two o'clock, four hundred meters, down in the channel. Not moving."

Priya reached him first, because Priya always reached people first. Okafor followed with the sled rope coiled over his shoulder, and behind him came Dev, breathing hard, and Hazel last, moving with that terrible economy she had, no wasted motion, no wasted words.

The porter was maybe twenty-five. Younger than Dev. His left leg was wrong below the knee — not broken, Okafor thought, running his hands down it with the practiced indifference of a mechanic diagnosing a machine, but badly sprained, ligaments stretched or torn, the ankle swollen to twice its size inside a boot they had to cut off him. His hands were frost-nipped white at the fingertips. His lips were cracked and gray.

And he was alone. No pack. No radio. No ice axe. Somewhere between Camp Two and here, Sable Voss had stripped him of everything that could slow her down and left him in a meltwater channel to become a statistic.

"He's hypothermic," Priya said. She already had the bivvy sack open, already had the heat packs cracking against her palm. "Moderate. Core temp's down but he's shivering, which is good. Shivering means the furnace still works."

"Name," Hazel said, crouching so her face was level with his. Her voice did the thing it did with frightened clients — dropped low, went slow, became the steadiest object in the landscape. "What's your name?"

The man's eyes tracked her like a trapped animal's. Then they focused, and something in them shifted — recognition, Okafor realized. The man knew who she was. Everyone on this glacier knew who she was.

"Dorje," he said. His English was accented, careful. "Dorje Tamang."

"Dorje. I'm Hazel Brightwater. These are my crew. We're going to get you warm and we're going to get you down. Can you tell me what happened?"

Dorje's eyes went past her shoulder, up the glacier, toward the route Voss had taken. He flinched as if the ice itself might be listening.

"She said—" He stopped. Swallowed. Started again. "She said if I could not keep pace, I should wait. She said the others would come back for me."

"When?" Okafor asked quietly.

Dorje looked at him. "Yesterday," he said. "She said this yesterday morning."

* * *

They got him into the bivvy sack and onto the sled, and Priya ran the rewarming protocol with the calm of someone who had done it in worse places, though Okafor doubted she'd done it anywhere colder. Okafor rigged the sled harness himself, checking each buckle twice, because a sled failure on a lateral moraine was just another way the mountain ate people.

While he worked, he studied Dorje the way he studied all machines brought to him broken — patiently, looking for the fault line, the place where the stress had entered.

Voss had hired six porters at the valley. Okafor had counted them at the checkpoint, standing in their cheap boots with their overloaded duffels, and he'd thought then what he thought now: *this is what exploitation looks like when it wears expedition logos.* Voss had paid them triple valley rate, which sounded generous until you understood she was paying them to carry her ambition up a glacier that was actively trying to die.

Now there were five. One had turned back at the icefall with altitude sickness — sensible, alive. And one lay in a sled with a dead leg and frost-nipped fingers because Sable Voss had measured his worth in vertical meters per hour and found him wanting.

"You knew," Okafor said to Dorje, low, as they began to move. "You knew she wouldn't come back."

Dorje was quiet for a long time. The sled runners hissed over the moraine gravel.

"In the valley," he said finally, "she told us the job. Salvage. Old camp, old equipment. She showed us photographs." His breath caught. "Photographs of tents. Of a crest on the tents." His eyes flicked to Hazel, walking ten meters ahead, and Okafor saw him understand, in real time, whose crest it had been. "I did not know then what it meant. The others did not either. We are porters. We carry. We do not ask."

"But you asked," Okafor said.

"I asked at Camp Two. I asked what we were really taking from the ice." Dorje's jaw tightened. "After that, I carried the heavy loads. Always the heavy loads. And yesterday, when my ankle went, she took my radio and my axe and said wait." He laughed, a small terrible sound. "She said wait. On a glacier. In monsoon season. She said wait."

* * *

They made Camp Three — a scraped hollow behind a serac wall — by late afternoon, and Hazel called a crew council that wasn't a council at all, because Hazel didn't do councils in crisis; she did briefings. But everyone understood what it was.

"We can't catch her," Hazel said. "Not before the route closes. She knows the descent line better than we do now — she's been studying it for fifteen years, same as me, different reasons. If we chase, we burn the hours we need to get out alive." She let that sit. "So we don't chase. We descend clean, we hit base camp, we hit the valley, and we tell the story first. That's the only ground where we beat her."

"And Dorje?" Priya asked.

"Dorje comes with us. Obviously."

"Obviously," Okafor echoed, and meant it, and noticed that nobody had even considered the alternative. Fifteen years ago, he'd have assumed, a crew in their position would have done the arithmetic: injured porter, closing route, limited carrying capacity. Fifteen years ago, Elian Brightwater had apparently done exactly that arithmetic on a mountain very much like this one, and six people had died of it.

Hazel had done different math. Hazel always did different math. It was the whole reason Okafor had signed onto her crew three years ago with a falsified work history and a past he kept in a locked box, same as Tomas and Priya and Dev, same as everyone who ended up here. Small crew. Legal enough. Invisible enough. Safe enough to be human in.

But there was a problem, and Okafor saw it before anyone said it, because seeing problems was his function the way fixing them was.

"Hazel," he said. "There's a flaw in 'we tell the story first.'"

She looked at him.

"We don't have a story. We have a journal we haven't finished reading, a survey tube we haven't opened, and a witness—" he nodded at Dorje, wrapped in the bivvy sack by the stove, watching them with dark careful eyes— "who knows what Voss has. Which is the same things. And Voss has a radio."

Silence around the stove. The glacier groaned somewhere below them, long and tectonic, like a ship changing its mind.

"She'll broadcast from the ice," Dev said. "If her radio works—"

"It works," Okafor said. "I checked the wreckage of ours myself. Our failure wasn't component failure. Someone pulled the power board. Clean job. Fast job." He'd known it for two days and said it aloud for the first time now, because saying it changed nothing about the cold and everything about the plan. "Voss sabotaged us before we ever entered the icefall. She wanted us deaf and dumb up here. Which means she planned to speak alone."

"So let her speak," Hazel said.

Everyone turned.

"She wants to sell a story," Hazel said. "Fine. Stories need buyers, and buyers need proof, and proof needs context, and context—" she tapped the journal, wrapped in waterproofing against her chest pack— "is mine. She has the document. I have the meaning. She can broadcast every word my father wrote, and without me, without the survey data authenticated, without the expedition records Tomas can cross-reference, it's a dead woman's diary read aloud on a satellite band. Sensational. Unverifiable. Worthless."

"Unless she has you," Tomas said quietly. "Dead or alive. A Brightwater confirming it makes it real."

"Yes," Hazel said. "That's why she left porters behind and kept moving. She's not running from us. She's running to a signal point. High ground, clear sky, the eastern shoulder where the rock breaks the storm cell." She'd clearly been turning it over for hours; Okafor could see the shape of the plan settling on her like snow load. "Which means we know where she's going. And knowing where she's going means we know where she'll be vulnerable."

* * *

It was Dorje, later, thawed and fed and wrapped in Okafor's spare insulation layer, who gave them the piece none of them had.

Okafor sat with him on watch, sharing tea, because that was what you did with rescued people — you sat with them until they stopped being cargo and started being a person again. Dorje held the cup in both hands like it was the first warm thing in his life.

"The radio," Dorje said suddenly. "You think she will call the valley. Sell the story, yes? To the networks, the publishers." He shook his head. "No. You misunderstand her."

Okafor set down his cup. "Tell me."

"At Camp Two, before my ankle, I heard her on the radio. She thinks we porters understand nothing, so she speaks freely." Dorje's English got more careful, more precise, as if accuracy were a form of courage. "She was not talking to networks. She was talking to one person. A name — Meridian House. She said, 'The package is intact. I want exclusivity. Full rights. My name on it.' She said—" he closed his eyes, retrieving it— "she said, 'Fifteen years I've carried this. It will be *my* truth or it will be no one's.'"

Okafor sat with that. Outside, the light was going blue and violet over the seracs, and the temperature was falling the way it fell up here, all at once, like a door closing.

"My brother," Dorje added, quieter. "Worked high-altitude salvage on Kharun, years back. Came home with money and silence. Never spoke of it." He looked at Okafor. "People like her think men like me are furniture. But furniture hears everything."

*Meridian House.* Okafor turned the name over. He didn't know it, but he knew the shape of it — the shape of every institution that bought truth wholesale and sold it retail, that paid for exclusivity the way Voss paid for porters: triple rate, full rights, your name on nothing.

And he understood, suddenly, completely, what Voss was doing. Not clearing Hazel Brightwater's name. Not honoring the dead of 2009. Building a monument to herself out of other people's bones. Fifteen years of obsession, and the ending she'd chosen wasn't justice — it was ownership. *My truth or no one's.*

Compassion had gotten them Dorje. Compassion — the expensive, inefficient, unstrategic choice to stop for a man in a meltwater channel — had bought them the one intelligence that mattered. Hazel's different math, paying dividends again.

He carried it to Hazel's tent, and she listened without interrupting, and when he finished she was quiet for a long moment, looking at the tent wall where the stove-light made shadows move like water.

"She's not selling the story," Hazel said finally. "She's selling *me*. Or my absence. A Brightwater scandal, exclusive, her byline, her version, her vindication." Her jaw worked. "And if I'm not there to contradict it, her version becomes the only version."

"That's the play," Okafor agreed. "So we make sure you're there."

"How? She has the high ground, the working radio, and a day's lead. We have an injured porter, a dying route, and three days of margin." Hazel almost smiled, and it wasn't a warm smile; it was the smile of someone doing arithmetic she hated. "Unless we don't need to catch her. Unless we just need to reach a signal first."

Okafor stared at her. "Our radio's dead."

"Our radio's *broken*," Okafor heard himself say, and then heard Hazel say, "Fix it."

He looked at the dead unit in his hands — the gutted power board, the clean sabotage job, the machine he'd already pronounced beyond field repair twice in his head. And he thought about Dorje in the bivvy sack, and about furniture that hears everything, and about the difference between a mechanic and a miracle worker, which was mostly just whether anyone had asked yet.

"I'll need the good solder," he said. "And six hours. And nobody asking me if it's done until it's done."

Hazel was already reaching for the spares kit. Outside, the wind picked up, and far to the east, toward the shoulder where the rock broke the storm cell, the last light caught on something small and distant and moving — a figure, or a trick of the ice, climbing toward the only high ground with a clear view of the sky.

Six hours, Okafor thought, and picked up his iron.

The mountain would decide how many of those they actually got.
