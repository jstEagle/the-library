## 28. Held on a Line

The fall lasted three seconds. I know because I counted them the way Priya taught me — one for the ground truth, two for the panic, three for the rope.

Then there was no falling anymore, just swinging, a slow pendulum in air that tasted like stone and old water, and my boots scraping nothing at all.

"Dev." Hazel's voice came down from above, thin and stretched by distance but steady as a rail. "Talk to me."

"I'm—" My voice cracked on the first word. I tried again. "I'm here. I'm okay. I think. The rope's holding."

"The rope's holding," she repeated, not to me but to herself, to Tomas, to Okafor somewhere up on the surface, to the mountain itself. A confirmation. A claim.

I looked down.

That was a mistake.

Below me the crevasse went on like a throat. Blue light filtered from somewhere far above — the slot had opened under me without warning, a hairline crack in the snow bridge that my weight had turned into an invitation — and it fell away into darkness that wasn't black so much as *deep*, a color I didn't have a name for. The walls were sculpted ice, scalloped by decades of meltwater, and they curved inward below me like the inside of a bell. Somewhere down there was a floor. Or wasn't. In glacier country you learned that "bottom" was a rumor.

My breath fogged out in front of me and hung there, refusing to disperse, because the air in a crevasse doesn't move. It just waits with you.

"Hazel," I said, and this time her name came out small. Not the way I'd ever said it before. For eight days I'd said it the way you say the name of someone whose poster hung over your bunk back in the training hostel in Namche — *Hazel Brightwater did the Kharun traverse solo in a whiteout, Hazel Brightwater pulled three climbers off the Serac Wall in '19* — a name spoken like a prayer or a headline. This was different. This was a name said by a man hanging off the end of her rope, sixty meters of nylon between him and the arithmetic of gravity.

"I've got you," she said. "Okafor's building the anchor. Tomas is on backup line. Nobody's going anywhere. Breathe."

I breathed. The fog bloomed and hung.

I'd been on ropes before. Two seasons of porter work, a winter course in Manang where an instructor with frost-nipped ears made us practice crevasse rescue in a parking lot until we hated him. But practice happens in daylight, on flat ground, with hot tea afterward. Practice has an ending. This had only a middle.

The rope hummed above me. I could feel it through my harness, a faint vibration like a plucked string — Hazel shifting her weight, feeding slack, talking to Okafor in clipped fragments that drifted down the shaft and arrived half-eaten: "*—equalize the—* no, the *other* screwgate—* Tomas, give me two more meters of—*" The machinery of her competence, running exactly the way it always ran, except now I was inside it instead of watching it.

Something shifted against my boot. I flinched, heart slamming, and then realized what it was: meltwater. A thread of it, impossibly thin, running down the wall beside me and dripping off some ledge below into a pool I couldn't see. *Drip.* A pause long enough to doubt it would come again. *Drip.* The glacier keeping time while I hung in its mouth.

"Dev." Hazel again. Closer now — she'd moved to the lip. Her head appeared against the white sky, haloed by spindrift, tiny and upside down. "Can you see the wall? Is there anything to stand on?"

I twisted, careful, the way you move when every motion costs you altitude. Ten feet below my boots, a ledge of old blue ice jutted from the wall, maybe wide enough for both feet if I trusted it. Above it, the wall was smooth as poured glass.

"There's a shelf. Maybe ten feet down."

"Good. I'm going to lower you to it. Slowly. You'll feel the rope pay out — don't fight it."

"You're sure the anchor—"

"*Dev.*" Just my name, once, with a weight behind it that stopped my mouth. Then, gentler: "I don't build anchors that fail. Lowering on my mark."

She lowered me. Of course she lowered me. Three minutes of exquisite, controlled slippage, the rope whispering through the carabiner above, my gloves burning on the line, until my boots touched the shelf and it held — solid, ancient, indifferent — and I almost sobbed with relief onto the ice.

"Status," Hazel said.

"Standing. Both feet. Legs are shaking but they work."

"They're supposed to shake. Shaking means your body still thinks it gets a vote." A ghost of humor in it, dry as chalk. "Rest there. We're rigging a haul system. Twenty minutes, maybe thirty."

"Thirty," I echoed. Thirty minutes standing on a shelf of ice over a bottomless blue dark, listening to water count seconds.

"Thirty," she confirmed. "And Dev — talk to me. Not about the crevasse. About anything else. Talking keeps the cold out of your head."

So I talked. I told her about the monsoon in my grandmother's village, how the rain on the tin roof sounded like applause. I told her about the first mountain I ever saw and thought was climbable, which turned out to be a hill. I told her about my contract — the debt contract, the one Voss's money was clearing, the indenture papers that had made me property with a pulse — and how I used to read the terms at night like scripture, looking for a loophole, finding none.

Hazel listened. She was good at that; I'd noticed it early, in week one, when Priya was teaching me field sutures and Hazel stood at the edge of the firelight saying nothing, absorbing everything. Now her voice came down the shaft in low responses, occasional questions, never pity. When I mentioned the contract her silence stretched a beat longer than usual.

"That ends when we're paid," she said. "You know that."

"I know."

"And after that, you stay because you want to. Not because a piece of paper says you have to."

I looked up the shaft toward her voice. The blue light fell in columns around me, and dust motes — actual dust, glacier flour, ground-up mountain — drifted through them like snow that had forgotten which way was down.

"Why does it matter to you?" I asked. "Whether I stay free? You could've kept any crew you wanted. Better crews. Legal ones."

The pause went on long enough that I checked the rope, thinking she'd moved away.

"Because I've seen what debt does to people," she said finally. "Fifteen years of watching good climbers sign themselves away, season by season, until they can't remember what climbing felt like before it was owed." Another pause. "Nobody hangs on my rope who isn't there by choice."

*Another Brightwater cost six people their lives,* I thought, and then immediately hated myself for it, because that was the story everyone told — the scandal, the falsified maps, the legend that ate its own daughter's career. But hanging there in the blue dark, hearing her voice come down the ice like something being handed to me across a gap, I understood I'd been reading the story wrong. Everyone had. They said Hazel ran from her father's name. They said the blacklist broke her. But she hadn't been running *from* anything. She'd been running *toward* a standard — a standard so high it looked like flight.

Elian Brightwater had put his ambition above other people's lives and called it exploration. His daughter had spent fifteen years putting other people's lives above everything — above her reputation, above her name, above the money that would have freed her years ago if she'd taken dirtier contracts — and called it nothing at all. Called it *a job*. Called it *keeping the crew small*.

She wasn't outrunning the legend. She was burying it, one safe descent at a time.

"Drip," said the glacier.

"Dev?" Hazel's voice sharpened. "You went quiet."

"Just thinking."

"Dangerous. Save it for the surface."

Above me, sounds of work: Okafor's low mutter, the scrape of the picket, Tomas calling measurements. The rope twitched. I wrapped it twice around my forearm, an old habit, feeling its pulse like a held hand.

"Hazel," I said. "When I slipped — the bridge, the crack — you were ten meters ahead. You were moving before I even screamed. How did you know?"

A short laugh, no warmth in it, all edge. "Because I always know. That's the job. You walk a snow bridge like it's already failing and you're just negotiating the terms." Then, quieter: "And because I heard the snow change. It sings differently right before it lets go. You learn to hear it or you learn to stop leading."

"Did your father teach you that?"

I regretted it before the words finished leaving my mouth. The wind at the lip picked up, pushing spindrift down the shaft in fine white veils that settled on my shoulders like ash.

"No," she said. Flat. Final. And then, unexpectedly, she gave me more: "He taught me maps. Beautiful maps. He taught me that a route drawn well is a promise kept." A breath. "Everything else I taught myself."

I stood on my shelf and did the math I should have done eight days ago, back in the outfitter's hall when Voss walked in smelling of money and old grudges. The salvage run. The coordinates inside the restricted zone. The way Hazel flinched at the mountain's name in the foothills, the letter Tomas carried like a live grenade, the survey tube Tomas had found wedged in the moraine with dates that matched 2009. I wasn't stupid. I'd just been star-struck, and star-struck men don't ask questions.

But hanging sixty meters down in the glacier's throat, with her voice as the only warm thing in the world, the questions assembled themselves whether I wanted them or not. Voss wasn't salvaging equipment. Voss was salvaging *proof*. Proof of what, I couldn't say yet — but it had something to do with Elian Brightwater, and everything to do with why Hazel had signed a contract with a clause she should have burned.

And she'd signed it anyway. For us. To clear our debts. She'd walked her father's ghost back up his killing mountain to buy four people their freedom, and she hadn't told a single one of us why.

"Rope's coming taut," Hazel called. "Haul system's rigged. On three, step up off the shelf and let the line take you. Don't kick the wall — you'll knock ice down on yourself. One. Two—"

"Wait." My voice bounced up the shaft. "Hazel. Whatever Voss is really after — whatever this mountain is to you — when we get out of here, you tell us. All of it. Deal?"

Silence. Long enough for three drips.

"Deal," she said. "Three."

I stepped off the shelf.

The rope took me the way she'd promised it would — smoothly, without argument, the haul system turning my hundred and seventy pounds into a problem solved by leverage and will. The blue light rose around me. The walls slid past, close enough to touch, carved with flow-marks like fingerprints, the glacier's own handwriting in a language older than any map. Higher. The lip of the crevasse brightened from deep teal to pale glass to white. Hands grabbed my harness — Okafor, grunting, his forearms like cable; Tomas hauling on the backup line, face red under his hood — and then I was out, sprawled on the surface, gasping at a sky so bright it hurt, the whole gray-white chaos of the icefall spread around us under a sky the color of wet slate.

Priya reached me first, hands moving — pupils, pulse, fingers, the fast triage inventory. "Color's good. Talk to me."

"I'm fine. I'm—" I started laughing and couldn't stop, the ugly adrenaline kind, my whole body shuddering with it. "I'm fine."

"You're hypothermic-borderline and you're fine." She was already peeling my shell open, checking layers, pressing heat packs into my gloves. "Drink. Small sips."

Hazel stood apart, coiling the rope with quick, exact movements, her back half-turned. But I caught her face when she glanced over — the tightness around her eyes, the thing underneath the composure that wasn't fear for herself, because Hazel Brightwater didn't spend fear on herself. It was the look of someone doing arithmetic she hated: *one fall, one rope, one rookie, and how many ways could it have gone wrong.*

"Tomas," she said quietly. "New route line. We swing east of the serac band — no more bridges than we have to cross. We cross them one at a time, belayed, no exceptions. Not even me."

"Not even you," Tomas repeated, and there was something in his voice — relief, maybe, or recognition. Like she'd just said something she'd never said before.

We moved within the hour. The storm was building in the west, a bruise spreading along the horizon, and the icefall groaned around us like a ship working loose from its moorings. I climbed at the back of the rope team, where rookies go, and every snow bridge we crossed I crossed knowing Hazel had tested it first with her own weight, alone, unroped, because that was the tax she charged herself for leading.

At the next rest stop, huddled behind a serac while the wind began to sing, she crouched next to me and pressed a heat pack into my hand without a word.

"For the record," I said, "you didn't let a Brightwater cost anyone their life today."

Her head came up sharply. For a second I thought she was going to deny it — but she'd said it herself, down in the dark, when she thought I was too far gone to remember. *I won't lose another person on this ice. Not another one. Not ever.* I'd heard it through the rope, muffled, more prayer than sentence, and I'd understood then what I understood now: she hadn't been talking about the fall. She'd been talking about 2009. About six names she carried like stones. About a father whose ambition had outweighed his conscience, and a daughter spending her whole life making sure the scales never tipped that way again.

"No," Hazel said at last. "Not today." She looked west, at the storm coming down the valley like a verdict. "But this mountain isn't finished asking. And neither, I think, is Sable Voss."

She stood, shouldered her pack, and pointed up-route — and that was when I saw it, far above us at the edge of the icefall, two figures moving fast and light, unroped, heading straight for the high camp where we'd cached the survey tube and everything else that mattered.

Voss. And one porter, breaking trail toward everything we'd risked our lives to carry this far.

Hazel watched them for three full seconds. Then she said, very quietly, in a voice I'd never heard her use — not leader, not guide, something older and harder:

"Pack faster."
