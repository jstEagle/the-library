## 29. The Trade

I caught up with Voss at the glacier's edge an hour before dawn, when the ice went from black to a color like old bone and the whole world held its breath between dark and light.

She didn't hear me coming. That was the first surprise. Sable Voss had spent thirty years in the mountains developing ears like a chamois, and here she was, standing at the lip of the terminal moraine with her back to me, watching the valley floor come up out of the mist, and she never turned around.

Maybe she heard me and didn't care. Maybe that was worse.

"You walk loud for someone who spent fifteen years learning to be invisible," she said.

"I learned to be invisible from people who wanted to find me."

That got half a laugh out of her. She didn't turn. The wind came down off the icefield behind us and pushed at us both, cold enough to feel personal. Below us the moraine spilled out in a long gray tongue toward the tree line, and beyond it, somewhere under the cloud deck, the valley where the porters' village sat, where the tea houses were just now lighting their stoves, where none of this had ever mattered to anyone.

Voss had the survey tube slung across her back with the journal strapped on top of it. My father's handwriting against her spine. I'd been tracking her since the middle of the night, reading her tracks by headlamp — she traveled fast but she traveled straight, no zigzag, no attempt at concealment, which told me something. She wasn't running. She was leaving. There's a difference, and the difference is that a person who's running looks over her shoulder.

Sable Voss hadn't looked over her shoulder once.

"Sable." I said it quietly, the way you'd speak to someone standing too close to a cornice. "We should talk."

"We're talking."

"Turn around first."

She did. And I saw what fifteen years of wanting does to a face when it finally gets what it wants. It doesn't look like triumph. It looks like exhaustion. Her eyes were rimmed red, whether from wind or something else I couldn't tell, and there was a rawness around her mouth like she'd been clenching her jaw all night. The two porters were gone — I'd found their tracks splitting off toward the lateral moraine hours ago, one set limping. She'd sent them ahead. Or they'd left her. With Voss I'd never be sure which story was true until she decided to tell it.

"You're alone," I said.

"I'm efficient."

"You abandoned your injured porter on the ice."

"He'll live. Men like that always live. It's the good ones who die." She shifted the tube on her back, and my father's journal rode with it, and something in my chest pulled tight as a rope coming taut. "You've come a long way to say nothing, Brightwater. Say what you came to say."

So I said it.

* * *

Here is what I'd worked out during six hours of walking through the dark, and I gave it to her plainly because anything less would have insulted us both:

"You want the truth published. So do I. You want your brother's name spoken without the asterisk of his own stupidity attached to it — because that's what the official record says, isn't it? That Meridian Osei died of navigational error. Pilot error. Human error. Fifteen years of people shrugging at his grave." I watched her flinch and kept going, because stopping would have been mercy and we weren't in a place for mercy yet. "The record's wrong. We both know it now. Elian falsified the route data. Six people followed a line that didn't exist. Your brother died believing he'd made a mistake, and that's the cruelest thing in this whole rotten story, and I am going to fix it."

Something moved behind her eyes. Not softening. Recalculation.

"But not your way," I said. "Not sold to the highest bidder. Not packaged as *Sable Voss Exposes Mountaineering's Greatest Lie*, exclusive interviews, book deal, speaking circuit. Not the truth as a product with your name stamped on the wrapping."

"And why not?" Her voice cracked upward, and there it was — fifteen years of it, all at once, the grief finally finding its register. "Because it's *my* brother? Because I bled fifteen years into this while you were hiding in border towns teaching tourists how to tie figure-eights? You don't get to decide how this comes out, Hazel. You had your chance in 2009. You stood in that inquiry room and you said nothing."

"I know."

"You knew. Even then, some part of you knew, and you chose the legend. You chose the name. You let six families grieve wrong so yours could stay golden." She stepped closer, boots grinding on the scree, close enough that I could see the frost in her eyebrows. "So don't stand there talking about how the truth should be handled. You lost the right to handle it before I ever started looking for it."

Everything she said was true. That was the worst part. I'd had six hours of walking to make peace with it, and I still felt each sentence land like a rockfall.

"That's why I'm here," I said. "Not to take it from you. To give it to you."

Her chin lifted slightly. Suspicion, yes, but underneath it, hunger. God help me, hope.

"The journal publishes. All of it — every entry, including the last one, including what he wrote about you. The survey tube goes to the authorities with full documentation. The ascent record gets struck, the maps get corrected, the inquiry gets reopened if any of those families still want answers. And the credit is joint. Brightwater and Voss. Equal billing. Your name next to mine on every page, in every archive, in every obituary correction they print. You spent fifteen years needing this exposed. Fine. It gets exposed. But it gets exposed *clean*."

"And the porters you abandoned?"

"They walk out with us. All of us. Nobody else goes into this ice."

Voss was quiet for a long time. The light was coming up properly now, and it showed me the glacier behind her — the whole broken white throat of it, seracs leaning like drunks, meltwater already ticking in the channels below. Calving season was early. Everyone on this mountain could feel it in their teeth.

"You think I'm doing this for credit," she said finally. "You think I climbed into this freezer for a byline."

"No. I think you're doing it for Meridian. I think you need the world to stop saying he screwed up, and you need to be the one holding the proof when it happens, because being the one matters when you've spent fifteen years being nobody." I took a breath. "But Sable — if you sell it, if you shop it around, if you turn it into a spectacle, then the story becomes about you. Every headline, every interview, every documentary voiceover. *Voss says. Voss claims. Voss, the disgraced guide.* And Meridian disappears again, buried under your name instead of his mistake. Is that what you want? Another fifteen years of him being a footnote?"

I watched the words go in. Watched them find whatever was soft in her and press.

"You've thought about this," she said, and her voice had gone flat and careful, the way voices go when the person using them is trying not to shatter.

"All night."

* * *

She sat down. That surprised me more than anything else — Sable Voss, sitting on a boulder at the edge of the world, forearms on her knees, staring at the valley like it owed her money. I sat too, three meters off, because distance mattered and we both knew it. Between us lay ten meters of scree and everything either of us had ever wanted.

"My brother called me the night before they went up," she said. "Satellite phone, four minutes, cost him a fortune. He was laughing. He said the route was beautiful, Hazel. He said Elian Brightwater had found a line so clean it was like it had been drawn on purpose, and that when he got down he was going to buy me dinner at the place in Kathmandu with the terrible jazz, and he was going to spend the whole meal telling me about it." She picked up a piece of scree, turned it over, set it down. "He believed it. That's the part nobody understands. He wasn't reckless. He trusted the map. He trusted the man who drew it. And the man who drew it—" Her hand closed around the stone. "Your father sat in a tent and changed numbers on a page, and my brother walked into the sky after them."

"I know."

"You don't know. You can't. You got to keep your father. I got to keep a phone bill and a reservation at a restaurant that closed years ago." She opened her hand. The stone dropped. "So yes. I wanted my name on it. I wanted the world to know that it was Sable Voss who wouldn't let it lie. Because if I'm not the one who fixed it, then I'm just the sister of the dead man, and I have been the sister of the dead man for fifteen years and I cannot be that for fifteen more."

The wind moved through the silence. Somewhere above us, deep in the icefall, something settled with a sound like a door closing in an empty house. Both of our heads turned toward it, the same instinct, the same training, and for one second we were just two guides listening to a mountain talk.

"It doesn't have to be either/or," I said. "Joint credit isn't erasure. It's both names. Yours first, if you want. Alphabetical order is a tyranny anyway."

That almost got a smile. Almost. "You'd do that. Put my name first."

"I'd put the dead first, actually. All six of them, named, on the first page. Then us."

She looked at me for a long moment, and I watched her run the arithmetic — the same arithmetic I'd been running all night, except hers had different variables. Trust. Leverage. The weight of the tube on her back versus the weight of the crew behind me. Because that was the other card I hadn't played yet, and I could see her counting it: she was alone, I was five, and the route home ran through territory my crew knew better than anyone alive.

"You'd fight me for it," she said. "If I said no. You'd chase me down this glacier and take it off my body."

"Yes."

"No hesitation?"

"None. But I'd rather not. I've buried enough people this week." I heard my own voice catch on the word *enough* and didn't bother smoothing it over. "Okafor repaired the radio last night, by the way. Before you ask. It works. If you took the working one, you took the decoy."

Something flickered across her face — respect, maybe, or the particular bitterness of a professional watching another professional play a hand well. "Of course you did."

"Sable. Listen to me. The glacier is done waiting for us. You've seen the meltwater. You've heard it. Whatever you carry off this mountain, you carry it down a route that's closing by the hour, and if you go alone and fast you might beat it, and you might not, and if you don't, Meridian's truth dies in the ice with you and stays buried for another fifty years until somebody else finds it and sells *their* version. Is that the outcome? After all of it?"

She didn't answer. She looked at the tube. She reached back and touched it, the way you'd touch a sleeping child, checking.

"The journal stays intact," she said. "No edits. Even the parts about you."

"Every word."

"The six are named. All of them. Full names, ages, what they were to the world."

"First page."

"And the inquiry reopens. Officially. Not a quiet correction in some archive — a real reopening, with the families notified, with testimony on the record. I want to sit in a room and say it out loud where it counts."

"You'll testify. I'll testify. Tomas has the original survey tube and he'll surrender it himself."

"Tomas." She almost laughed. "Your navigator. Whose father-in-law drew the false maps. This expedition is a family reunion of everyone the lie ruined." She shook her head slowly. "And me. The woman who hired you under false pretenses, sabotaged your radio, stole from your camp, and abandoned a porter. What exactly do I get out of walking into an inquiry room, Brightwater? Amnesty?"

"I can't promise amnesty. I won't lie to you — you've had enough of men lying to get what they want."

"No," she said softly. "You haven't. That's the strange thing about you, Hazel. Fifteen years in the margins and you still talk like someone who believes institutions work."

"I believe they can be forced to. There's a difference."

The sun cleared the ridge behind us then, and the whole glacier lit up at once, every serac throwing its shadow, and the light hit the melt channels and turned them to veins of mercury. Beautiful and catastrophic. We both stood, the way mountaineers do when the mountain changes its mind about the weather — some old reflex that says *up, pay attention, the day is starting whether you're ready or not.*

Voss unslung the tube. She held it in both hands, across her body, the way you hold something you're deciding whether to throw off a cliff.

"If I give you this," she said, "and you publish, and the record changes, and your name comes back — what happens to mine? Because I've done things these last eleven days, Hazel. Things that will be in the report. I'm not asking forgiveness. I'm asking what's true."

"What's true is that you found the truth. The rest is circumstance, and circumstance gets weighed by people who weren't here." I held out my hands. "Give me the journal. Keep the tube until base camp — you carry it yourself, I'm not taking it from you, that matters. We walk out together. We descend together. And when we reach the valley, we call the families together, yours and mine, and we tell them in person before any reporter hears a word. Meridian's family first. Always first."

For a moment — one long, suspended moment, the kind you get on a snow bridge when you feel it start to give — Sable Voss hesitated.

I saw the whole war of it cross her face. Fifteen years of singular purpose meeting its first real alternative. The hunger to be the one, fighting the need to be clean. Her fingers tightened on the tube. Loosened. Tightened again. And I understood, in that moment, that I was watching her decide not just about a metal cylinder but about who she was going to be for the rest of her life — the woman who sold the truth or the woman who carried it home — and that no words of mine could tip it. It was hers. It had always been hers.

"Joint credit," she said. "Equal. Not alphabetical. Side by side, same size type."

"Side by side."

"And dinner. When this is over. At whatever terrible restaurant still exists in Kathmandu. You and me, and we tell stories about him, and neither of us leaves until we've laughed at least once."

My throat closed. "Deal."

She held the tube out.

And that was when the mountain spoke — not the small settling groans we'd been hearing for days, not the tick of meltwater, but a sound from high in the icefall, deep and structural, the sound of something enormous changing its mind. Both of us froze. Above the serac field, a plume of snow lifted off the ridge crest and hung there, backlit, obscene in its beauty.

"Move," Voss said, and shoved the tube into my arms, and we ran.
