## 31. After the Roar

The silence came in layers.

First there was the roar itself — a sound Priya felt in her sternum before her ears caught it, a detonation that went on and on, the glacier unzipping its own throat. Then the echo, bouncing off the valley walls, doubling and redoubling until she couldn't tell where the mountain ended and the sound began. Then the dust — a white plume rising off the tongue of the Kharun like the mountain exhaling everything it had held for fifteen years.

And then, underneath all of it, the thing she would remember for the rest of her life: the quiet.

Not true quiet. The glacier never gave you that. But the groaning had stopped, the constant conversation of ice under tension gone suddenly absent, as if the mountain had said what it needed to say and was done talking.

Priya stood at the edge of Camp Two's platform with her medical kit half-unpacked at her feet and counted heartbeats. That was triage instinct, older than thought: when the world explodes, count. Count your own pulse first, because you're useless to anyone if yours is racing. Then count the people.

Hazel. Standing ten meters out, facing the calving zone, not moving. Her rope coiled over one shoulder, her ice axe planted, her whole body angled toward the place where Sable Voss had been standing ninety seconds ago.

Tomas. Behind her, on his knees at the platform edge, both hands flat on the snow. His lips were moving. Priya couldn't hear words over the settling hiss of the dust cloud, but she knew the shape of them. He'd been reading the survey tube's markings when the crack came. He'd been holding it when Voss reached for it.

Dev. Down by the cache, frozen mid-motion with a fuel canister in each hand, staring at the gap in the world.

Okafor. At the radio — the backup radio, the one they'd rebuilt from spare parts on day three — with his back to the calving zone, working the dials with steady, deliberate hands, as if the only way through this was to keep doing the next small correct thing.

Four. Plus herself. Five.

Voss was not among them. Neither were the two porters who'd gone with her, though one of those had already turned back days ago, carried on Okafor's shoulders down from the crevasse field with a shattered ankle and a story that had changed everything.

Priya picked up her kit and walked toward Hazel.

* * *

She'd been a combat medic before she was a mountain medic, and before that she'd been a girl in Chennai watching her grandmother die badly in a hospital that smelled of formalin and lilies, and every one of those lives had taught her the same lesson: grief is a luxury the living schedule for later. In the first minutes after catastrophe, your job is not to feel. Your job is to inventory.

So she inventoried.

Injuries: none visible. Dev had a healing scrape along his jaw from the crevasse rescue. Tomas's fingers were showing early frostnip at the tips; she'd need to rewrap them tonight. Hazel had a bruise blooming yellow-green along her ribs where a serac had clipped her on the chase — she hadn't reported it, of course, because Hazel Brightwater didn't report things, she simply absorbed damage the way the glacier absorbed rockfall, silently, structurally, until one day the whole face came down.

Supplies: eleven days of food for five, stretched thin. Fuel for six more days of melting water. Two tents intact, one shredded in the storm. One functioning radio.

Route: none behind them. That was the arithmetic the roar had rewritten. The exit icefall — the same route they'd climbed on Day One, the Gate, the serac corridor, the fixed lines they'd left in place like a ladder home — all of it was now buried under forty meters of fresh collapse. She could see the scar from here, a raw blue wound across the glacier's shoulder where the ice had folded in on itself.

Which meant the only direction left was forward. Down-glacier. Through terrain no one had surveyed, across the widening crevasse fields, past meltwater channels that were growing louder by the hour, on a glacier that had just demonstrated, publicly and catastrophically, exactly how much its promises were worth.

"Okafor." Her voice came out flat. Good. Flat was useful. "Status."

He didn't turn around. "Transmitting on emergency band. No confirmation yet." A pause. "Antenna took some vibration damage. I can get maybe three kilometers of range out of it."

"Base camp?"

"If they're still at base camp, and if their receiver's still alive, they heard us." He finally looked over his shoulder. His face was gray with dust and something else. "If they heard us, they'll have the lower route marked and hot tea waiting. If they didn't—" He stopped himself. Shook his head. "I'll keep trying."

That was Okafor's whole philosophy in four sentences: do the work, refuse the despair, state the contingency without finishing it.

Priya moved on. "Tomas."

He hadn't gotten up. She crouched beside him, put two fingers on his wrist — pulse fast but strong — and followed his gaze to the calving zone. The dust was settling now, sifting down over the wreckage of the icefall in a fine white veil, and beneath it, somewhere under a hundred thousand tons of new ice, was a woman who had spent fifteen years sharpening herself into a weapon against Hazel's family, and who had died reaching for a metal tube full of paper.

"She was still holding my sleeve," Tomas said. His voice sounded scraped. "When the crack started. I let go. I made the choice to let go, and I ran, and she reached for the tube instead of running." He turned his head, and his eyes were wet and furious and lost. "Did I kill her, Priya? By letting go? Or did she kill herself? Which one is it?"

Priya had answered questions like this in field hospitals, in triage tents, kneeling next to men who'd dragged buddies out of burning vehicles or failed to drag them out. She knew the honest answer and she knew the survivable answer, and once, years ago, she'd learned they weren't always the same.

"The ice killed her," she said. "You survived. Those are the only two facts we get to keep today."

"That's not—"

"It's the only version that lets us walk out of here," she said quietly. "Grief however you need to tonight. But right now I need you standing up, because Hazel's about to start making decisions and someone has to navigate them."

Something moved behind his eyes — the navigator waking up inside the grieving man, shouldering him aside. He wiped his face with the back of his glove, leaving a streak of dust and salt, and stood.

* * *

Hazel hadn't moved.

Priya reached her and stood at her shoulder, following her line of sight. The calving zone was still smoking with suspended ice particles, catching the afternoon light, almost beautiful the way forest fires are beautiful from far enough away. Somewhere in that white haze, the survey tube lay under new ice. Somewhere in that white haze, so did Sable Voss.

"We're not digging," Priya said. It wasn't a question and it wasn't permission. It was a medic telling a commander what the body budget allowed.

"No," Hazel said. "We're not."

"You understand I'm saying that out loud so everyone hears it. Because Dev is going to ask. And Tomas is going to ask again tomorrow. And someone has to say it first, clearly, so it doesn't sound like callousness. It sounds like medicine."

Hazel turned her head. There was dust in the lines around her eyes, and behind the dust her expression was doing something complicated — grief and relief and guilt all fighting for the same square centimeter of face. Fifteen years of hating this woman, and now the hatred had nowhere to live.

"I know," Hazel said. "Say it however you need to."

So Priya raised her voice, medic-calm, carrying across the camp the way she'd been trained to make it carry across a battlefield. "Listen up. Nobody goes into the calving zone. Not for the tube, not for her gear, not for anything. The ice is still moving in there — you can hear it settling. Anyone who crosses that line becomes a second patient, and I don't have the resources for a second patient. That's not cruelty. That's arithmetic."

Dev set down the fuel canisters. He opened his mouth. Closed it. Nodded once, hard, like a man swallowing something too big.

"And the route?" he asked. His voice cracked on *route*. "The way we came up—"

"Gone," Hazel said. Simply. The way she said everything that mattered. "The Gate collapsed. Even if we could climb it, the approach slopes above it are unloaded and unstable. We go forward. Down-glacier, along the eastern margin where the crevasses run longitudinal, then off the ice at the lateral moraine below the shoulder, if it exists, if the maps are right." A beat. "If the maps are right."

Nobody laughed. But something in the phrasing — the ghost of a joke, the acknowledgment that they were navigating a killer glacier on faith and faded ink — loosened a knot in the group's chest. Priya felt it happen. Small. Necessary.

"When do we move?" Tomas asked.

"First light."

"Why not now?" Dev again. Young, urgent, wanting to outrun the horror on his feet rather than sit inside it.

"Because it's four hours to dark, the light's going flat, and tired people die on glaciers," Hazel said. "We break camp at dawn, we move fast, and we don't stop until we're off the ice. Three days, maybe four, if the lower route holds." She looked at each of them in turn — the commander's census, counting her people the way Priya counted her patients. "Tonight we eat everything warm. Tomorrow we travel light."

* * *

They broke out the good food — the reserve stash, the chocolate and the dried fruit and the single precious brick of paneer Okafor had been hoarding like a dragon — and nobody objected, because everyone understood what it meant. Rations for a funeral. Calories for the road ahead. You fed the living; that was the oldest rule there was.

Priya moved through the camp doing her rounds, and it was mostly theater and she knew it — checking frostnip that wasn't worse, re-taping Dev's blisters, adjusting Tomas's finger wraps — but the theater mattered. Ritual was scaffolding. You built it around a broken thing so the broken thing could hold weight while it healed.

At Okafor's radio post, she paused. He was still transmitting in cycles: position, party size, intent to descend via eastern margin, request confirmation. Over and over into the static, patient as rain.

"Anything?"

"Nothing." He rubbed his eyes with a knuckle. "You know what gets me? I spent three days suspecting this radio of sabotage. Tore it apart twice looking for Voss's tampering. And now it's the most honest thing in camp. Just keeps sending. Keeps believing somebody's listening." He glanced up. "You think base camp heard the calving?"

"They heard it in the valley," Priya said. "They probably heard it in Kathmandu."

"Then why hasn't anyone—"

"Because radios fail, antennas vibrate loose, and people sleep." She squeezed his shoulder. "Keep sending. It's the right work."

It was the right work. That phrase had become their camp liturgy over eleven days — Hazel said it, Okafor lived it, and Priya had adopted it as armor. When the mind wants to spiral, give the hands a task. When the heart wants to break, hand it a splint and tell it to hold still.

* * *

Dusk came early under the storm's leftover clouds, purple and bruised. They ate warm. They melted snow and filled every bottle. Okafor stripped the camp of anything that burned and staged the loads by the door of each tent, weighed and balanced, so dawn would be motion instead of decision.

Priya sat last with Hazel at the platform edge, the two of them wrapped in the tent fly against the wind, watching the calving zone fade from white to gray to black.

"She wanted to sell it," Hazel said at last. "The journal. The truth. My father's confession, packaged and priced." Her voice was very even, which is how Priya knew the cost. "And I spent eight days hating her for it. And now she's in the ice with the survey data, and the only copy of the truth that matters is pressed against my ribs, and I don't—" Hazel stopped. Started again. "I keep waiting to feel something clean about it. Relief, maybe. Or grief. Something with edges."

"What do you feel?"

"Numbness," Hazel said. "And underneath it, this horrible practical voice saying: *good, now no one can stop you.*" She laughed, short and airless. "What kind of person thinks that standing over a grave?"

"The kind who's about to lead four people across a collapsing glacier," Priya said. "Numbness isn't a failure, Hazel. It's a resource. The feeling will come. It always comes. Usually at the worst possible moment — a week from now, a month, in the middle of a safe warm room when you least want it. Your job between now and then is just to stay functional and let me worry about the rest."

"You'll worry about my feelings."

"It's literally my job. Emotional vitals are vitals."

For the first time since the roar, Hazel smiled — small, crooked, real. "Get some sleep, Doctor."

"You first."

But neither of them moved for a long moment, sitting together in the dark, listening to the glacier breathe.

* * *

Priya woke at 0300 to a sound she'd been dreading.

Water.

Not the polite trickle of surface melt — a deep, muscular rushing underneath the ice, the sound of the glacier's plumbing opening for the season. She crawled out of the tent into a night gone strangely luminous, stars sharp above, and put her bare hand flat against the ice. Felt it. The pulse of meltwater moving beneath them, huge and accelerating, the glacier's blood running faster than it should in mid-season.

Okafor was already out of his tent, head tilted, listening. Their eyes met across the dark camp.

"How early?" she asked.

"Two weeks," he said. "Maybe three. The monsoon didn't come early, Priya. It came *on time*, and everything we planned was built for a season that doesn't exist anymore." He looked down-glacier, into the black, where the sound was coming from. "Those meltwater channels on the lower route? Tomorrow they won't be channels. They'll be rivers under bridges of snow that are getting thinner by the hour."

Priya did the arithmetic she'd been refusing to do all evening. Distance to the lateral moraine: a day and a half, best case, on a good route. Best case had just died in the calving along with everything else.

She woke Hazel.

Hazel listened for thirty seconds, hand on the ice, face unreadable in the starlight. Then she stood, and Priya watched the numbness recede and something harder take its place — not grief, not fear, just pure function, the commander assembling herself out of whatever pieces were available.

"Change of plan," Hazel said. "We don't wait for first light. We move now, by headlamp, while the snow bridges are still frozen solid. We cross the dangerous ground cold, and we rest at noon when the melt starts." She was already pulling on her harness. "Wake the others. Ten minutes."

Priya stood in the dark a moment longer, one hand on the ice, feeling the water rush beneath her palm like a countdown.

Then she went to wake the others, and the glacier, indifferent and enormous, carried them forward into the dark.
