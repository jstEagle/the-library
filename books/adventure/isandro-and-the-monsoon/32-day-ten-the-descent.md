## 32. Day Ten: The Descent

Okafor had a theory about machines and people, and it was this: they broke in the same places. Not the obvious places — not the joints, not the seams, not anywhere an engineer would have flagged on a diagram. They broke where they'd been asked to do something they were never built for, quietly, for years, until one morning the thing simply refused.

The stove at Camp Three had been asked to run at altitude it wasn't rated for, on fuel that was half water by now, by a crew that had no business still being alive. It sputtered at dawn like an old man arguing with the weather, and Okafor knelt over it with numb fingers and a length of safety wire, thinking: *me too.*

"Talk to me," Priya said behind him. She was packing the med kit with the economy of someone who'd stopped counting how many times she'd restocked it this trip. "Is it dying?"

"It's negotiating." He fed the wire through the jet housing, twisted, held his breath. The flame caught, steadied, went blue. "We're good for maybe two more boils. After that we're drinking snowmelt cold."

"Cold's fine."

"Cold's fine," he agreed, "until somebody falls in a melt channel and we need heat fast."

Priya didn't answer that. She didn't have to. They both knew the arithmetic of the descent now, the way you know a song stuck in your head: three days of route left, maybe less, and every hour the glacier spent another hour of itself becoming river.

He looked up from the stove and took inventory the way he always did, without meaning to, the mechanic's curse. Tomas at the tent door, folding the map along creases that were going soft from handling. Dev crouched outside, chipping ice off his crampons with the flat of his axe, methodical, jaw set — the boy had come back from the crevasse field two days ago changed, quieter, watching Hazel with something that wasn't hero worship anymore but hadn't decided what it was yet. And Hazel herself, standing apart at the edge of camp, facing down-glacier, doing what she did every morning now: touching her chest, once, where the journal rode against her ribs inside her jacket, checking it was there, the way Okafor checked a torque wrench before a critical bolt.

*She carries it like a live charge,* he thought. *And she is the only one of us who hasn't said out loud what she's decided to do with it.*

That was all right. He'd worked enough engines to know you didn't ask a machine for its decision while it was still under load.

"Hazel." Tomas's voice. "Route's changed overnight."

They gathered at the map even though the map was now mostly fiction. Tomas had redrawn their line in pencil twice since the calving, and the paper was gray with graphite where he'd erased and corrected and erased again. The meltwater channels that had been ankle-deep on the ascent were running brown and loud, braided across the glacier's lower flank like veins gone wrong. Two of the snow bridges they'd crossed coming up were marked with his small, neat X's.

"Gone?" Dev asked.

"Gone or lying about being gone. Same thing until we test them." Tomas tapped a point mid-route. "This is the bad one. The long bridge over the main channel. Coming up it held all five of us plus sled weight. Now—"

"Now it's had four days of monsoon," Priya finished.

"So we go one at a time," Hazel said. "Okafor, I want your eyes on every bridge before anyone's weight goes on it. You find one you don't trust, you tell me, and we build around it. Rope, pickets, whatever it takes. I don't care if it costs us six hours."

"You'll get your six hours back somewhere else," Okafor said. "Machines always pay you back if you maintain them. Routes are machines."

Hazel looked at him then — really looked, the tired gray look she'd been wearing since the ice took Voss — and something in it almost smiled. "Then keep us maintained."

* * *

The first channel came up on them within the hour, and it was nothing like it had been on the approach.

On the way in, nine days ago — a lifetime, another geological epoch — it had been a crack in the white, a place you stepped over. Now it was a trench eight feet wide, walled with blue ice, and at its bottom a current ran fast and opaque with rock flour, the sound of it filling the whole valley like a throat clearing. The snow bridge across it sagged in the middle, a smooth concave dip that told Okafor everything before he ever touched it.

"Nobody move past me," he said, and crawled out onto it.

This was the part of the work nobody romanticized. Belly down, axe laid flat beside him to spread his weight, moving by inches, reading the snow under his palms the way other men read faces. Good snow had a grain to it, a cohesion; you could feel it holding itself together, wanting to hold you too. Bad snow felt like a promise made by someone already leaving. This bridge had good snow at both ends and a lie in the middle — a softness, a hollowness, the faint wet give of a structure whose foundation had been eaten out from under it by the same water roaring beneath.

He backed off. "Middle third is rotten. There's a void under it — I can hear the water through the pack."

"Can we reinforce?" Dev asked.

"We can bypass." Okafor stood, brushed the snow from his jacket, and studied the channel's near bank. Twenty meters downstream, the trench narrowed where a slab of collapsed serac had fallen across it — a chunk of glacier the size of a truck cab, wedged between the walls, old enough to have refrozen into place. "That block. If it's seated, we cross on it. If it's not seated, it's a trapdoor."

"And if it's a trapdoor?"

"Then I've ruined everyone's morning."

He went down to it alone, because that was the job and because he was the heaviest of them and had earned the right to be the test load. The block was ice and gravel, fused, gritty under his boots. He stamped once, twice, walked its length, lay flat and put his ear to it. Underneath: water, but distant, muffled — the block was grounded, bridging to bedrock or packed debris. Solid.

"Cross one at a time. Soft steps. No poles planted mid-span."

They came across it single file, and Okafor stood on the far bank and watched each of them the way he watched a gauge needle — Tomas light and precise, Priya steady, Dev careful in the new way he had, and last Hazel, who paused in the middle of the block for half a second, looking down into the brown water, before finishing the crossing as if she hadn't stopped at all.

He didn't ask what she'd seen down there. He suspected none of them would have wanted the answer.

* * *

By early afternoon the descent had become a rhythm, and Okafor loved rhythms the way some men loved music.

Probe, test, cross. Anchor, fix line, descend. When a fixed rope they'd placed on the ascent turned out to be frozen into a wall that had melted and refrozen around it, he freed it with hot water from the thermos, poured slow along the line while Priya belayed his hips, the rope peeling loose centimeter by centimeter like a stitch drawn from flesh. When Dev's crampon strap tore at the buckle, Okafor laced it with the inner cord from his own bootlace and a zip tie, and said nothing when Dev tried to give the tie back later, just waved him off — *keep it, ties are cheap, feet aren't.* When Tomas's pencil finally wore down to a stub too short to hold, Okafor produced, from the repair kit he'd carried fifteen years across two blacklisted countries, a mechanical pencil with a broken clip, and handed it over without comment.

Tomas stared at it. "You've been carrying that the whole time?"

"I carry everything the whole time. That's the entire job."

It was true, and it was also not the whole truth, and Okafor knew the difference even if he never said it aloud. The whole truth was this: he fixed things for them because fixing things was the only language he'd ever been fluent in, and because somewhere around Day Four, pinned in the storm at Camp Two while confessions came out around the stove like splinters working free, he had understood that this crew — this small, illegal, invisible, impossible crew — was the finest machine he would ever be permitted to maintain. Five parts, worn, mismatched, each one carrying damage no manufacturer would warranty. And yet they ran. They ran because they checked on each other the way he checked the stove: every morning, without being asked, without making it a thing.

Competence was love. He'd learned that from his mother, who had kept a taxi running twenty years in a city that wanted it dead, and who had never once in her life said the word love to any of her children, but whose hands smelled of engine oil and whose children had never once been stranded.

So when the second long snow bridge — the one over the confluence channel, the worst crossing of the route — began to fail under Tomas at three in the afternoon, Okafor did not panic, because panicking was a maintenance error.

He heard it before he saw it: a sound like a heavy book closing, deep under the snow. Tomas froze mid-span, ten meters out, arms out, weight on both poles.

"Don't step," Okafor called. "Don't even breathe heavy. Listen."

Under the bridge, the water answered — louder here, the confluence of two channels, a real river now. The snow under Tomas's feet had settled two inches as they watched, the whole span easing downward like a breath let out.

"Tomas. Come back to me. Slow, heel-first, exactly retracing your steps."

Tomas turned, and it was the turning that did it — the pivot, the torque, the thing bridges never forgive. The snow cracked in a ring around his boots, a white flower opening, and dropped him to his waist.

He didn't scream, which Okafor would remember afterward. He said, very calmly, "Ah," and spread his arms across the surface, and the bridge held, quivering, his body the cork in the bottle.

Okafor was already moving. Rope off his shoulder, anchor — the nearest solid thing was a boulder crusted in ice at the bank, and he wrapped it twice, tied it with hands that were shaking only after the knot was dressed. Priya was beside him, on the rope, without either of them discussing it. Hazel arrived at a run and took the anchor too, three bodies on a line, and Dev fed slack and kept the rope clear of the sharp ice edge with his axe shaft, and nobody shouted anything, because shouting was for crews that hadn't done this fifty times in training and twice for real.

"Tomas. We're going to pull you out backward, flat, swimming. Kick when I say. One—" 

The bridge groaned. Somewhere below, a shelf of it calved into the water and the whole span lurched, dropping Tomas another few centimeters, and Okafor heard Priya say, low, "Now or never," and he said, "One, two, *pull*—"

Tomas kicked. The rope came tight. Three people falling backward onto the bank, heels dug in, and Tomas came up out of the hole like a fish landed over the side — arms first, chest, hips, boots trailing water — and the bridge behind him sighed, slumped, and folded into the channel with hardly a splash, swallowed whole, gone as if it had never existed.

They lay on the bank breathing. All five of them, in a row, staring up at a sky the color of wet slate.

"Well," Tomas said finally, water ticking off his jacket in the cold. "I believe that concludes the bridge portion of our program."

Priya laughed — a short, cracked sound, halfway to something else — and then Dev laughed, and then Okafor, flat and helpless, and even Hazel, one syllable, wrung out of her like the last of something. They lay there laughing at nothing, at the bridge, at themselves, at ten days of ice, and Okafor thought: *there it is. There's the machine running clean.*

* * *

They moved until the light failed, because stopping meant camping on a glacier that was rearranging itself hourly, and moving meant choosing their ground instead of having it chosen for them. At dusk they found a shelf of old ice above the lateral moraine — stable, dry, defensible — and Okafor built the camp while the others melted snow and rigged the tents, and by full dark the stove was running on its last boil and the five of them sat close around it, closer than warmth strictly required.

Dev spoke first, which was new. "My sister's going to ask why I smell like a river."

"Tell her the truth," Priya said. "You fell in a crevasse and got pulled out by a woman who doesn't lose people."

Dev glanced at Hazel. Hazel was looking into the flame, one hand resting flat over her chest, over the journal, and she said, without looking up: "I lost Voss."

"The ice lost Voss," Okafor said. "There's a difference, and you know it better than anyone."

Hazel was quiet a moment. Then she took her hand off her chest and looked at each of them in turn — Tomas dripping-dry and pale, Priya with her burned fingers, Dev with his borrowed lace and zip-tied crampon, Okafor with his wire and his kit and his fifteen years — and she said, "Tomorrow we hit the moraine by noon and the valley floor by dark. Eleven days. We make it in ten and a half, and we walk out of here owing the mountain nothing."

"And the journal?" Dev asked, soft.

Hazel's hand went back to her chest. Whatever moved behind her face, she kept it there, private, hers — but her voice, when it came, was level and sure in a way Okafor had only heard from engines running exactly right.

"Tomorrow," she said, "you'll all see."

Okafor banked the stove. Around them the glacier went on groaning and settling, shedding itself into the monsoon dark, indifferent and enormous. But inside the circle of their little camp, five people sat shoulder to shoulder, warm off each other's heat, a machine of flesh and trust that had taken ten days of the worst ice on earth and still ran true.

He slept the sleep of a well-maintained man, and dreamed, absurdly, of his mother's taxi, running clean through rain.

Dawn came gray and loud with meltwater. They struck camp in forty minutes, a record, and started down — and it was Dev, walking point, who stopped so suddenly that Okafor nearly walked into his pack.

Ahead, where the route bent around the last spur before the moraine, the glacier's surface was moving.

Not shifting. Moving — a slow, muscular slide of the entire lower tongue, ice and debris riding a film of meltwater toward the valley, and at its leading edge, directly across their exit line, a wall of the stuff was building as it went, piling up like a plow blade.

Tomas unrolled the map with hands that no longer shook, found their position, and looked up.

"That's the valley road," he said. "Everything below that wall is the way home."
