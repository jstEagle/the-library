## 34. The Last Bridge

The meltwater channel had been widening all morning. I'd been watching it since we dropped out of the serac field — a blue-grey seam in the glacier's skin that kept opening its mouth a little wider every time Okafor stopped to listen to it.

"It's not a river," he said, for the third time. "It's a throat."

Nobody laughed. Nobody had laughed in two days.

We came around the shoulder of the moraine at what my watch swore was eleven-forty but felt like some hour that didn't belong to any clock, and there it was: the last bridge. A snow arch spanning the channel where the glacier had cracked open like a struck bell, maybe thirty meters of compacted winter packed over summer void, sagging in the middle the way an old rope sags between anchors. Water sang underneath it. Not the polite trickle of high-altitude melt — this was a full-throated roar, muffled by the ice above it but present, insistent, like something breathing under a blanket.

Hazel stood at the edge with her poles planted and did not move for a long time.

I've watched Hazel Brightwater make maybe four hundred decisions on this trip. Route calls, anchor calls, weather calls, the call to leave Priya's spare stove behind when her pack frame cracked on Day Six. Most of them she made in seconds, the way she does everything, fast and clean, like the answer was already there and she was just picking it up off the ground. This one took longer. She stood at the lip of the channel with the wind pulling at her hood and studied the bridge the way Tomas studies a map — not looking at it so much as reading it.

"Sag's maybe a meter," she said finally. "Depth of the channel?"

"Can't see bottom," Okafor said. "Water noise says deep."

"Snow quality?"

Okafor knelt, pulled a glove, and pressed his bare palm flat against the surface. He held it there ten seconds. Fifteen. I watched his face do nothing at all, which from Okafor meant everything.

"Dense," he said. "Old. It's held all season." He put his glove back on. "It's also the only way down that isn't three days east through terrain none of us has surveyed, and we don't have three days."

"So it holds or it doesn't," Priya said.

"So it holds or it doesn't," Hazel agreed.

Tomas had gone quiet in the way he goes quiet — not silent, because Tomas is never fully silent, he hums when he's nervous, old Bengali folk tunes half-swallowed — but quieter, his voice folded down small. "Unroped?" he asked. It wasn't really a question. We'd all done the math already. Four people roped together on a bridge that might fail means four people falling into the same hole, tied to each other, which isn't rescue, it's just a tidier accident.

"Unroped," Hazel said. "One at a time. Everyone else back past the moraine crest, out of the runout." She turned and looked at us, all four of us, one by one, and I realized she was doing something I'd seen her do exactly once before — in the crevasse field, before Dev fell, before I fell, before she went over the edge after me on a line she'd anchored herself. She was asking permission. Not for the crossing. For being the one who decided.

"Your call, boss," Okafor said.

"My call," she said, and something in her shoulders settled, like a pack finding its fit.

* * *

She went first. Of course she went first. Fifteen years of leading people through places that wanted them dead and she still wouldn't send anyone anywhere she hadn't put her own weight first.

I watched from the crest with my heart going like a fist on a door. The bridge dipped under her as she moved out onto it, a slow visible settling, centimeters, and then it stopped settling and just held, and she kept moving — not fast, not slow, that deliberate flat-footed walk she uses on suspect snow, weight placed like money she can't afford to spend twice. Halfway across the sag was deepest and the whole arch groaned, a low structural sound I felt in my molars, and water roared underneath, and Hazel did not stop, did not hurry, just walked through the middle of the sound like it was weather.

Then she was on the far side, stamping a platform, driving a picket, rigging a belay line anyway — because even unroped, even trusting the bridge, she'd anchored a running belay from the far lip so that if the snow gave under one of us mid-span, there'd be something on the other end of the fall besides gravity.

"Tomas," she called back. "Come on."

He crossed. The bridge held. He crossed it the way Tomas does everything now, with his eyes closed for the worst three meters, humming, and when he reached the far side Hazel gripped his forearm and neither of them said anything and both of them said everything.

"Priya."

Priya crossed like she was crossing a street. She's the least dramatic person alive and possibly the bravest, if you define brave as doing the frightening thing without making it a story. Midway she paused — one beat, two — and I saw her look down through a thin spot at the water churning below, and then she looked up instead, at Hazel, and finished.

"Okafor."

Our mechanic, our engine, our human load-bearing wall, went across carrying forty kilos of gear because he refused to let anyone else haul the radios and the hardware, and the bridge complained about it, a long creaking protest, and Okafor talked to it the whole way. Low, steady, nonsense syllables, the tone you use on a spooked animal or a failing generator. The bridge held. Okafor stepped off the far lip and turned around and looked back at me across thirty meters of dying snow.

"Dev," Hazel called. "Your turn."

* * *

Here's the thing about the last nine days: I have learned what trust weighs.

I used to think trust was a feeling. Warm, abstract, the stuff of campfire confessions during whiteouts — and we'd had those, God knows, pinned at Camp Two while the monsoon tried to erase us, all of us saying things we'd never said sober in daylight. But standing at the near lip of that bridge, alone, unroped, with the whole crew watching from beyond the crest and the channel roaring underneath, I understood that trust isn't a feeling at all. It's a load calculation. It's the specific, physical certainty that another person's judgment is better than your fear, and that you will bet your body on it, here, now, with your boots, on snow you cannot read yourself.

I couldn't read this snow. I looked at the sagging span and saw only death with a lid on it. Every instinct I owned screamed the same thing: go around, go east, take the three days, take five, take anything but this.

But Hazel had walked it. And Hazel had left the belay line rigged. And Hazel was standing on the far side with the rope in her hands, taking in slack, watching me with an expression I'd spent the whole expedition trying to learn how to read and could only now, at the very end, translate.

It wasn't confidence. Confidence would have been easier. It was attention. Total, undivided, ferocious attention — the kind she'd given me hanging in the crevasse, the kind she gives the mountain, the kind, I understood suddenly, that nobody had given her father when it mattered, and that she had sworn on whatever altar guides swear on that she would never stop giving.

She wasn't telling me the bridge would hold. She was telling me she'd be there if it didn't.

"Dev." Her voice carried flat across the wind. "Small steps. Flat feet. Don't stop in the middle, but don't rush the middle either. You'll know the spot when you feel it. Trust the snow until it tells you otherwise."

"And if it tells me otherwise?"

"Then jump toward me," she said, "and I'll catch the rope."

So I stepped on.

The first meter was the worst, the transition from solid moraine gravel to snow that gave that soft hollow compression underfoot, like stepping onto a held breath. Then the next meter. Then the slope of the arch beginning, climbing gently toward the crown, and the roar of the water rising beneath me until it wasn't background anymore, it was the whole world, a freight train of meltwater running through a throat in the glacier directly below my boots, separated from me by — I looked once, I couldn't help it — by perhaps a meter and a half of snow that had fallen as flakes sometime before I was born.

Don't look down. Everyone says don't look down. But Hazel had never told me not to look down. She'd told me to trust the snow until it told me otherwise, and you can't hear what snow is telling you with your eyes shut. So I looked, once, deliberately, the way you check a knot, and I saw the water — green-black, violent, filled with grinding debris — and I saw the thin place ahead, a patch near the crown where the sun had gotten to work, the surface dimpled and rotten-looking, and I understood that this was the spot. The middle. The place she'd meant.

I slowed. Not stopping — she'd said don't stop — but slowing, shortening my steps, placing each foot like a signature. The snow under my right boot made a sound. Not a crack. Worse than a crack: a sigh, a soft structural exhalation, the sound of something deciding. My whole body lit up with adrenaline, every nerve screaming run, and I did the hardest thing I have ever done physically, harder than the crevasse, harder than the icefall on Day One: I did not run. I finished the step. Placed the left foot. Finished that one too.

And then I was past it, descending the far side of the arch, and the snow firmed, and the roar began to fade behind me, and Hazel's hand closed on my pack strap and hauled the last meter like I weighed nothing, and I was on solid ice with my legs shaking and my breath coming in whoops and Hazel steadying me with one hand on my shoulder.

"Breathe," she said.

"I am breathing."

"You're panting. Different thing." But she was almost smiling, and her hand stayed on my shoulder a second longer than balance required, and I knew — the way you know things at altitude, without evidence, completely — that she'd heard the snow sigh too. That she'd heard it when she crossed, and when Tomas crossed, and when Priya crossed, and she'd sent each of us anyway because she had read the snow more carefully than fear could and she knew the difference between a warning and a complaint.

That's what trust is. Not believing someone is right. Believing they're paying attention.

* * *

We didn't celebrate. There was no time and no surplus for it; the route behind us was closing audibly, deep reports rolling up the glacier like artillery, and the route ahead was three thousand vertical meters of unfamiliar descent. Hazel gave us ninety seconds — water, a bite, adjust layers — and then pointed down-valley and we moved.

But somewhere in that ninety seconds, sitting on my pack with my hands still trembling around a water bottle, I caught Okafor looking at me. He nodded once, small, the nod of a man acknowledging a job done, and I thought about how eight days ago I'd been the rookie, the kid whose contract was held by a debt, the reason Hazel had said yes to Voss in the first place. I'd hated that. Being the reason. Being the obligation.

Somewhere between the crevasse and the bridge, I'd stopped being the reason and started being the crew. I couldn't tell you the exact moment. Maybe there wasn't one. Maybe it was cumulative, measured the way Hazel measures everything — in miles, in meters, in placements of the foot — until one day the sum quietly changed.

"Hazel," I said.

She was coiling the belay line, already facing downhill. "Yeah?"

"The bridge. When it sighed under me. You heard it."

She tied off the coil. Looked at me. The wind pushed a strand of hair across her face and she didn't bother with it.

"I heard it," she said.

"You sent us across anyway."

"I did." No apology in it. No armor either. Just the plain fact, offered the way she'd offered everything on this glacier: honestly, take it or leave it. "Because I'd listened to it longer than you had, and I knew what it was saying. That's the job, Dev. Everybody downstream of my ears." She shouldered the rope. "You listened too. You heard it and you didn't run. That's the other half of the job."

She started down. One by one we fell in behind her — Okafor with his impossible pack, Priya light and quick, Tomas humming something that for the first time in days sounded less like nerves and more like a tune.

Below us the valley opened, brown and grey and brutally beautiful, the exit route stitched with meltwater channels that glittered like broken glass. Above and behind us the Kharun went on groaning, calving, erasing itself day by day, already forgetting we'd ever been on it. Somewhere up-ice, sealed in the blue dark, were Sable Voss and the survey tube and the wreck of the Meridian and everything else the glacier keeps.

Ahead of us was base camp, and the valley, and the press, and the journal against Hazel's chest, and whatever she had decided to do with the truth — because she'd decided, you could see it in her now, a stillness where the turmoil used to live.

I took one last look back at the bridge. Even in the minute since we'd crossed it, the sag had deepened. By tomorrow it would be gone. By spring there would be no trace that thirty meters of ancient snow had ever held six people's lives between its teeth and let them go.

Hazel was already moving. We followed.

Two hours later, rounding the final shoulder above base camp, we saw the tents — and saw, beside them, something that shouldn't have been there.

A figure, standing at the edge of camp, waiting for us.
