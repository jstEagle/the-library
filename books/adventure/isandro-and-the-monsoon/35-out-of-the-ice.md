## 35. Out of the Ice

The last hundred meters of the glacier tongue were running water.

Hazel heard it before she saw it — a sound like a river arguing with itself, like something enormous being taken apart joint by joint. She crested the final moraine ridge with Dev's rope coiled over her shoulder and Okafor's pack straps biting into her collarbone, and there it was: base camp, their tents still standing in the gray afternoon light, Tomas's stove pipe trailing a thin thread of smoke that looked, after ten days of ice and whiteout and death, like the most beautiful thing she had ever seen.

Behind her, the Kharun was finishing its sentence.

She didn't look back. Not yet. She had learned that on day one in the icefall — you don't look back while you're still inside the machine. You look back when the machine can't reach you anymore.

"Camp," she said, and her voice came out scraped raw, barely louder than the meltwater. "Camp is live."

Priya made a sound that wasn't quite a laugh and wasn't quite a sob. She had been making sounds like that for the last six hours, ever since they'd dropped below the serac zone and the mountain had stopped actively trying to kill them and started merely trying to exhaust them. Dev was walking on a sprained ankle he hadn't mentioned until this morning, favoring his left side with the stubborn rhythm of someone who had decided pain was negotiable. Okafor brought up the rear with the repaired radio harness slung across his chest like bandolier gear, and every twenty steps he patted it, the way you'd check on a sleeping child.

They came down off the moraine in single file, boots punching through the crusty snowmelt, and then the ground went soft and brown and real beneath them, and Hazel felt her knees nearly buckle at the sheer ordinary decency of dirt.

Tomas was out of his tent before she'd unclipped her pack. He took one look at the four of them — five minus two, because the arithmetic of this expedition would never come out even again — and did not ask where Voss was. He'd heard the calving from base camp yesterday. Everyone within forty kilometers had heard it. The sound had rolled down the valley like the sky clearing its throat, and Tomas had told Priya's replacement cook fire, told the empty air, told God if He was listening: *that's the route gone.*

"Hazel." Just her name. He crossed the distance and put his hand on the back of her neck, the way he'd done on every summit finish for fifteen years, and she let him hold her up for exactly three seconds before she stepped back and became the expedition leader again, because there were still things to count.

"Tents," she said. "Fuel. Radio."

"Radio's dead, we established that—"

"The satellite messenger. The backup. Did the storm take it too?"

Okafor was already kneeling at the comms tent, working the zipper with fingers that had been frost-nipped and re-warmed so many times they moved like they belonged to an older man. "Messenger's fine. Battery's at sixty percent. I can get us a position fix and a pickup request to the valley station by tonight."

"Do it. Priority medical for Dev's ankle, priority transport for—" She stopped. Started again. "For everything we're carrying out."

Nobody asked what she meant. They all knew what she meant. It was wrapped against her chest in waterproof layers, and it weighed less than two kilos, and it was the heaviest thing any of them had carried in fifteen years.

* * *

By the time the sun dropped behind the western ridgeline, base camp had the strange, humming order of a place that has just survived something and doesn't yet know how to behave. Priya had Dev's ankle wrapped and elevated and was force-feeding him soup with the gentle tyranny of a woman who had spent eleven days rationing calories and was now going to spend every last one of them on other people. Okafor sat cross-legged outside the comms tent, waiting for the messenger to acquire satellites, watching the little device blink with the patience of a man who had fixed enough broken things in his life to trust the process.

Hazel stood at the edge of camp, where the moraine began, and finally allowed herself to turn around.

The Kharun Glacier filled the head of the valley like a frozen river deciding, all at once, to remember it was water. Where the exit icefall had been — the gauntlet of seracs and collapsing bridges they had threaded through at dawn, one at a time, unroped across the last three spans because roped teams fall together and she had refused, absolutely refused, to let this mountain collect them as a set — there was now a field of shattered blue blocks taller than houses. Fresh debris fanned out across the tongue in a gray-white delta. Steam rose off the fresh fracture surfaces in the cold air, which was a thing ice should not do, a thing that looked like breath, like the glacier exhaling after holding itself for centuries.

Somewhere under that debris field was the survey tube Sable Voss had reached for as the world came down. Somewhere under it was Sable Voss.

Hazel had watched it happen from thirty meters away, close enough to see the orange of Voss's jacket, close enough to hear her shout — not a scream, something more controlled than a scream, almost an instruction, as if even at the end Sable was issuing orders to a mountain that had stopped taking them. Then the icefall section above the tongue had let go, a hundred thousand tons of it, and the sound had erased everything, and when the echo finally died there was no orange anywhere in the gray.

Eleven days. Exactly eleven days. Voss had bought the window, forged the papers, planned the approach to the hour — and the mountain had closed the door on schedule anyway, with her on the wrong side of it.

"We made it out by half a day," Tomas said quietly, coming to stand beside her. "Maybe less. If the calving had started twelve hours earlier—"

"It would have started while we were in the crevasse field." She said it flatly, because it was true and because saying terrible things flatly was how she kept them from becoming unbearable. "Dev would have gone into that slot with no one anchored above him. Or we'd have been pinned at Camp Two through another whiteout, and the exit would have sealed with us behind it. We didn't get lucky, Tomas. We got *hours* lucky. There's a difference and it doesn't matter."

He was quiet for a moment. Below them, the meltwater roared through channels that hadn't existed a week ago, carving the glacier's face as they watched.

"You have it," he said. Not a question. "The journal."

Her hand went to her chest without permission. "I have it."

"And the tube?"

"Gone. With her." Hazel watched the steam rise off the fresh ice. "You know what the worst part is? For about four seconds, when the icefall let go, I thought — I actually thought, *good.* The evidence is buried. The legend stays intact. Nobody ever has to know." She shook her head slowly. "Four seconds. That's how long it takes me to become my father."

"That's how long it takes anyone to become anyone," Tomas said. "It's what you do in the fifth second that counts."

In the fifth second, she remembered, she had been screaming Dev's name, checking his rope, counting her crew — five faces, five faces, all five — because that was the only arithmetic she trusted anymore. In the fifth second she had already known she was going to stand up in some valley office in front of officials and reporters and read Elian Brightwater's confession aloud, word for word, including the parts that named the dead, including the parts that ended her own name.

"I keep thinking about what she wanted," Hazel said. "Voss. Fifteen years she carried her brother's death around, and she wanted the truth out so badly she could taste it — but she wanted it *hers.* Her name on it. Her terms. Her revenge dressed up as justice." She exhaled. "And I stood there at the glacier's edge offering her a trade like we were negotiating a contract rate, and part of me knew even then that the mountain wasn't going to wait for us to settle it like professionals."

"The mountain never does." Tomas looked up at the darkening massif, at the peaks going violet and then black above the ice. "You know what I keep thinking? Eleven days. She promised us eleven days and triple rate, and she delivered both. Right up until the end, the contract was honored." His voice caught slightly. "That might be the saddest thing about her."

Hazel didn't answer. Down in camp, the messenger chirped — the acquisition tone, the good tone — and Okafor raised a fist in triumph, and Priya whooped from inside the mess tent, and Dev's answering cheer cracked halfway through because he was nineteen and exhausted and his ankle hurt and his hero had turned out to be human, which was somehow harder on him than the crevasse had been.

* * *

They ate together, all five of them crammed into the mess tent with the stove roaring, eating rehydrated everything with the ceremonial seriousness of a last supper in reverse — a first supper, the meal you eat when you've decided you're going to need more meals. Nobody talked about the mountain. That was the rule, unspoken and absolute: inside the tent, the Kharun did not exist. Outside it groaned and settled and sent occasional booms rolling down the valley like distant artillery, and everyone's eyes flicked toward the fabric wall and then away, and nobody said anything.

It was Dev who broke first, because Dev always broke first; it was one of the things Hazel had decided she liked about him.

"He said something to me. Before." Dev was staring into his soup. "Voss. Back at Camp Two, during the whiteout, when she was pretending to sleep. She said, 'Your boss is going to do the right thing, and it's going to cost her everything, and she'll do it anyway, and that's why she'll win.' " He looked up. "Was that — was she talking about the journal? Even then?"

"She'd read the coordinates on that map since before we signed the contract," Hazel said. "She knew what we'd find. She just didn't know what *I'd* find." She turned her spoon over in her hands. "Or maybe she did. Maybe that was the whole bet."

"What happens now?" Priya asked. "Practically. We walk out tomorrow, we hit the valley in three days, and then what? Officials? Press?"

"Officials," Hazel confirmed. "Press, probably. An inquiry, definitely — they'll want statements about Voss, about the porters, about the salvage permit she forged. And at some point someone official is going to ask me what we recovered." She met each of their eyes in turn. Tomas, who had hidden a letter from her out of love and shown it to her out of love and burned nothing even when she told him to. Priya, who had counted pills and pulses and kept them all alive on math and stubbornness. Okafor, who had flagged the fine print and stayed anyway, whose repairs had literally held their lives together across the last fifty kilometers. Dev, who had seen a rusted carabiner stamped BW in a crevasse wall and said nothing for four days because he understood, instinctively, that some discoveries belong to the person they're about.

"When they ask," Hazel said, "I'm going to tell them everything. All of it. What my father did, what the survey proves, what the journal says. I'm going to read it out loud if they make me sit down and do it formally." She paused. "I want you to understand what that means. My name gets dragged through it. The Brightwater name. Some people will say I betrayed the greatest mountaineer of his generation. Some people will say I did it for attention, or for money, or because Voss manipulated me. The blacklist review might go either way. There is no version of tomorrow where this comes out clean."

Okafor set down his spoon. "Boss," he said, "we followed you into a calving icefall on a salvage job that turned out to be a grave robbery run by a woman with a forged permit. Clean stopped being on the menu around day three." He looked around the tent. "Anyone disagree?"

Nobody disagreed. Dev raised his hand instead, which wasn't disagreement, just Dev.

"Then it's settled," Priya said, and refilled his bowl before he could object.

* * *

Later, when the others had turned in, Hazel sat alone at the mouth of her tent with the journal in her lap, not reading it. She had read it twice now — once in the wreck of the Meridian camp with her father's body sitting frozen three meters away, facing the summit he had lied about, and once more tonight by stove-light while the crew slept, because she needed to know every word she was about to make public. There would be no surprises in front of the officials. She owed the dead that much precision.

The last entry was still the hardest. *To my daughter.* Burn it or publish it, he'd written. Keep the legend or free yourself. The only honest thing I can give you is the choice.

He'd gotten it backward, she thought. The choice had never been hers to give or receive — it had been hers all along, sitting inside her for fifteen years like a splinter, every time she measured her life in miles instead of years, every time she flinched at the mountain's name, every time she chose small crews and legal routes and invisibility over the fight that might have cleared her. Elian hadn't given her the choice. He'd finally handed her the tool.

Outside, the glacier boomed — a deep, settling groan, the sound of a giant turning over in its sleep — and Hazel found she wasn't afraid of it anymore. It was just a mountain doing what mountains do. It had never been the thing that ruined her life. Men with pens had done that, and men with maps, and one man with her same gray eyes who had loved the truth less than he loved the summit.

Tomorrow they would break camp and walk down-valley, out of the ice and into the world, carrying everything.

She zipped the journal into its waterproof sleeve, tucked it against her chest where it had ridden for two days, and crawled into her bag. Sleep came faster than she expected — heavy, dreamless, the sleep of the used-up — and the last thing she registered was the meltwater roaring in the dark, patient and constant, the glacier keeping its oldest promise: that nothing stays frozen forever.

They broke camp at first light. By noon the tongue was behind them, and the valley opened green and enormous, and Hazel walked at the front the way she always did, counting her crew — five, all five — and measuring the distance home not in miles.

In days. Finally, in days.
