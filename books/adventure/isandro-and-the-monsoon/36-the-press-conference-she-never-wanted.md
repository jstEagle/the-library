## 36. The Press Conference She Never Wanted

Tomas counted the microphones the way he'd once counted anchors on a pitch: seven of them, clustered on a folding table draped in white cloth that some official had clearly borrowed from a wedding. Behind them, the valley's administrative hall — a squat concrete building with a faded mural of climbers ascending a mountain no one had ever climbed — and in front of it, maybe two hundred people. Reporters, yes, but also townspeople, porters' families, a delegation from the Mountaineering Federation wearing matching windbreakers like a funeral party dressed for the wrong occasion.

He stood at the edge of the platform with Priya and Okafor and Dev, close enough to catch Hazel if her knees went, far enough not to crowd her. She stood alone at the podium in her one clean shirt, hair still carrying glacier dust she hadn't bothered to wash out, hands flat on the lectern.

She had not wanted this. That was the understatement of the decade. She'd wanted to sign whatever form cleared the debt, hand over whatever testimony was required, and walk back into the hills where nobody asked questions. It had taken three days of argument — Priya doing most of it, in her medic's voice, the one that didn't leave room for negotiation — before Hazel agreed to stand up there.

But what she planned to do up there, none of them had known until an hour ago.

* * *

The night before, in the guesthouse room with its water-stained ceiling, Tomas had watched Hazel sit cross-legged on the bed with the journal open on her knees. Elian Brightwater's handwriting, small and precise even at the end, even starving, even dying of what the last pages made clear was a slow internal bleed from the fall that should have killed him outright. He'd lived eleven days after the others died. Eleven days alone at 6,400 meters, writing.

"You don't have to read all of it," Tomas had said. "You could give them the summary. The survey data corroborates. The route was falsified. Case closed."

Hazel had turned a page without seeming to see it. "You know what he wrote about Mateo Ferro? Page sixty-one. He wrote that Mateo argued with him at Camp One. That Mateo said the bearing was wrong, that the line didn't match anything he'd climbed on the approach. And my father wrote —" her voice caught, steadied — "he wrote, *'M. is right. I will deal with it after the summit.'*"

The lamp buzzed. Somewhere down the hall, Dev was laughing at something on his phone, the sound muffled and ordinary and unbearably young.

"After," Hazel said. "After the summit. Six people went up his false line because the man who drew it knew it was false and planned to deal with it after."

"So read them page sixty-one," Tomas said quietly. "That's enough."

She looked up at him then, and he saw the thing he'd been afraid to name for fifteen years — the resemblance. Not in the face, though it was there too, but in the set of the jaw when a decision had already been made and the rest of the world just hadn't been informed yet.

"They're going to want me to say I was wronged," she said. "That's the story they came for. The daughter, blacklisted, vindicated. They'll want me angry and they'll want me clean. And I can give them neither." She closed the journal. "I'm going to read all of it. Every page. Even the parts where he lies beautifully. Even the parts where he knows exactly what he's done. They can hear the whole arithmetic."

"It'll take hours."

"I know how long it takes. I've timed it." A ghost of a smile. "Four hours and twenty minutes, if I don't stop."

"And your name?"

"My name comes out of my father's mouth or it doesn't come out at all. I'm not going to trade his confession for my rehabilitation. That would just be another falsified map."

* * *

Now she stood at the podium and the federation chairman — a broad, sunburned man named Aldous Renk whose handshake Tomas remembered from better years — leaned toward his own microphone and began the machinery of introduction. Fifteen years ago, he said. A tragedy. A scandal. Questions that were never fully answered. And now, returned from the Kharun Glacier, evidence, and the daughter of Elian Brightwater herself.

Applause, scattered and hungry. Camera shutters like insects.

Hazel waited for it to die. Then she did not thank anyone. She did not smile. She adjusted the microphone down to her height — a small, practical gesture, so familiar it hurt, the same way she'd adjust a sling or a stove valve — and opened the journal.

"My name is Hazel Brightwater," she said. "Fifteen years ago I was blacklisted from guiding in this country and one other, following the inquiry into the 2009 expedition led by my father. Some of you have spent those fifteen years believing I was punished for something I did. Others have believed I was punished for something I didn't do. Both of you are wrong, and I'm going to take the next four hours explaining why, and then I'm going to read you my father's confession in full, in his own words, including the names of the six people who died because of it."

A ripple through the crowd. Renk's eyebrows climbing toward his hairline.

"If you came for a press conference," Hazel said, "you're in the wrong building. This is a reading."

* * *

She started at the beginning, which for her meant not the first page of the journal but the first lie — the survey data, the altered bearings, the route Elian Brightwater had drawn in his own hand and certified with his own seal, a line that existed nowhere on the mountain except on paper. She read the relevant passages aloud, her voice level, and when the technical language lost parts of the audience she stopped and translated, plainly, without condescension, the way she explained a fixed line to a nervous client.

"The bearing here reads 042," she said, tapping the page. "The actual line he climbed runs closer to 055. Seven degrees doesn't sound like much. On this face, in 2009 conditions, seven degrees put his team on a serac field that his own earlier notes describe as 'unacceptable risk, avoid in all circumstances.' He wrote that in March. He led six people into it in September."

Tomas watched the crowd the way he watched weather. The reporters' hunger curdling into something more serious as they understood this wasn't vindication theater. The federation delegates going rigid, because some of them had signed off on the original ascent record, and every word Hazel read was also an indictment of the machinery that had celebrated it. In the third row, a woman in a green shawl sat perfectly still, and Tomas realized — the family resemblance again, older, harder-worn — that it was Mateo Ferro's sister. She had come. Fifteen years, and she had come.

Hazel saw her too. Tomas watched it happen: the small hitch in her breathing, the half-second pause that a lesser reader would have covered with a cough. Hazel didn't cover it. She let the silence stand, and then she turned the page.

* * *

By the second hour, the light in the hall had changed and someone had brought water, which Hazel drank without comment, and the crowd had stopped being a crowd and become something more like a congregation. People had stopped checking their phones. A reporter near the front who'd arrived with the sharp-elbowed energy of someone hunting a headline now sat with her notebook closed on her lap, just listening.

Hazel read the middle sections fast, because they were the hardest — the summit push, the storm that wasn't forecast because the forecast had been for the true route, the moment at the serac field when the mountain began telling them what the map wouldn't. She read the entries of the men and women realizing, one by one, that the line under their feet matched nothing. She read Mateo Ferro's final radio transmission, transcribed in Elian's hand: *Bearing's wrong, Elian. Bearing's wrong.* And then she read what her father had written after: *I told him to trust the map.*

"He was below the summit by then," Hazel said. "He could see the top. And he sent them up anyway, because turning around meant admitting the map was his fiction, and the fiction was worth more to him than —" Her voice failed, briefly, actually failed, and she stood there in front of two hundred people with her mouth open and nothing coming out, and Tomas took one step forward before Priya's hand on his arm stopped him.

Hazel breathed. Found the page.

"Than the truth," she finished. "Than six lives. I'm not saying that for effect. I'm saying it because it's what he wrote. You'll hear it yourself in a moment. I'm not going to soften it for him anymore. I did that for fifteen years and it cost me everything I had."

* * *

In the fourth hour she reached the deaths, and here she slowed all the way down, because this was the part she'd promised and the part that mattered. Six names. She read each one with the date, the position on the mountain, and the cause as recorded in the journal — and then, where the journal allowed it, something human. Mateo Ferro, who had argued with the map and been overruled. Sanna Oduya, who had carried the rope for a teammate whose boots were failing. The Voss boy.

Tomas felt the hall change when she said it. *Dorian Voss, nineteen years old, junior member, recommended for the expedition by his sister, Sable Voss, on the strength of the route's certification.*

Sable wasn't there. Sable was under forty meters of ice on the Kharun Glacier, and the federation had declared her lost nine days ago, and there was no one in this hall to hear her brother's name read aloud by the daughter of the man who killed him — except everyone. Hazel read it steadily, and only Tomas, who had spent eleven days watching her hands, saw how hard she pressed her fingers flat against the journal's cover to keep them still.

When she finished the sixth name she closed the journal.

"That's the confession," she said. "There are seventy more pages. Medical detail, mostly. What he wrote while he was dying. I'm not going to perform those for you. They'll be available, complete and unedited, to the inquiry, to the families, and to any journalist who wants to verify a single word against the survey tube we recovered — which has been sealed and witnessed since base camp, and which four members of my crew will attest to under oath."

She looked out at them. Tomas had seen her look at crevasses the same way: measuring, unflinching, deciding.

"Now," she said, "the part you actually came for."

* * *

Renk tried to take the microphone back — Tomas saw him lean in, saw Hazel's hand come down flat on the table between them, not aggressive, just immovable, the gesture of a woman moving a client's pack off the rope path — and Renk, to his credit, subsided.

"Fifteen years ago," Hazel said, "an inquiry convened in this valley determined that the 2009 disaster resulted from navigational error under storm conditions. Unfortunate. Tragic. Nobody's fault, really. Weather. Mountains. My father was posthumously honored. His maps were entered into the national archive. His daughter — me — was blacklisted, not for the disaster, but for, and I quote the finding, 'failure to cooperate fully with the investigation,' which was the inquiry's polite way of punishing me for asking why nobody had checked the bearings."

She held up the journal.

"This book answers that question. My father falsified the survey. The inquiry either missed it or declined to find it. I don't know which, and I want to be honest with you: I don't care which. What I care about is that six families spent fifteen years being told their people died of bad luck, when they died of a lie. Sable Voss spent fifteen years trying to prove it, and she died in the ice getting the proof, and whether you loved her or feared her or both — and I did both — she was right. She was right about everything, and she never got to hear anyone say so, so I'm saying it now, in front of all of you, on the record."

Somewhere in the crowd, someone started crying. It was not the woman in the green shawl; she sat dry-eyed and straight-backed, and when Hazel's eyes found her, the woman gave a single nod, small and deliberate, like a carabiner clicking shut.

"As for my blacklist," Hazel said. "I'm not asking you to lift it. I'm telling you it no longer matters to me whether you do. I didn't come down off that glacier to get my name back. I came down to give these six people theirs. If this federation wants to restore my certification after what I've read today, it will have to do it knowing that I will spend the rest of my career telling clients, students, and anyone else who asks exactly what happened here, and exactly how institutions like this one let it happen. I am not a safe hire. I never was. That was always the real charge against me, and I plead guilty."

She stepped back from the podium.

The silence lasted three full seconds. Tomas counted them against his heartbeat, the way he counted seconds between lightning and thunder, estimating the distance of the strike.

Then the woman in the green shawl stood up, and the sound she made was not applause. It was quieter than that and heavier — a long exhale, fifteen years leaving one body — and it spread through the hall person by person until it became the other thing, the clapping, ragged at first and then not, and the reporters were on their feet and Renk was standing too, looking like a man who had just heard the foundation crack under a building he'd helped build.

Dev grabbed Tomas's arm. "Is she — should we go up there?"

"No," Tomas said. "Not yet. Watch."

Hazel stood at the podium with her eyes closed, just for a moment, just long enough. Then she opened the journal to the last written page — the one addressed to her, the one she'd shown no one, the one Tomas had read over her shoulder at midnight and would carry to his grave — and she did not read it aloud. She folded the corner of the page down, carefully, like marking a route she intended to return to, and set the journal on the table in front of the microphones where the cameras could see it, and she said:

"There's one more entry. It's private, and it stays private. But I'll tell you what it decided." She looked at the woman in the green shawl, and then at the hall, and her voice, when it came, was the voice she used at the start of a route, the one that makes frightened people believe the next step is survivable. "It decided that the truth belongs to the living. So does the mountain. Come spring, I'm drawing a new line up the Kharun — surveyed, published, checked twice — and I'm naming the route for the six. Any of you who want to climb an honest map, you know where to find my crew."

She stepped away from the podium, and the questions broke over her like the first wave of a storm — *Ms. Brightwater, Ms. Brightwater, the survey tube, the Voss woman, the federation's response* — and she walked through them toward the four of them at the edge of the platform, unhurried, deaf in the way she got when she'd decided a thing was finished.

Okafor cleared a path. Priya took her elbow. Dev was grinning like an idiot and crying at the same time.

And Tomas hung back one step, the way he always did, and watched the hall empty itself into the evening, and thought about letters with family crests, and burned paper, and how the past only ever stays buried until someone decides to dig — and how, standing there in the noise and the camera-light, Hazel Brightwater looked less like a woman who had just ended a fifteen-year exile than like a guide at the foot of a new route, checking her rack, waiting for the weather window.

Which, he realized, was exactly what she was. Because spring was coming, and somewhere in her pack — he'd seen it, folded into the map case — there was already a blank sheet of survey paper with a single bearing penciled at the top, and a name written beneath it in her father's old notation style, corrected, honest, hers.

The Kharun Route. First ascent pending.
