## 37. Fallout

The verdict comes down on a Tuesday, which feels wrong. Catastrophes should arrive with weather.

Priya reads it on her tablet in the outfitter's back room, where the smell of rope wax and kerosene has become the only smell she trusts. The Alpine Records Committee's statement is four paragraphs long and written in that particular bureaucratic dialect designed to say everything while committing to nothing. She reads it twice. Then a third time, because the second reading she was holding her breath.

*The 2009 first ascent of Kharun South Face, credited to Elian Brightwater's expedition, is hereby struck from the record. The survey data recovered from the glacier confirms material falsification of route documentation. Six climbers died following an inaccurate line.*

Six names follow. She knows them all now. Not from history — from Hazel, who recited them at the press conference like a rosary, like penance, like a woman paying out a debt fifteen years overdue with interest.

Priya sets the tablet face-down on the gear crate and listens to the building settle. Somewhere out front, Tomas is arguing with a supplier about carabiner prices. Okafor's radio plays low in the workshop. Dev is — where is Dev? Probably on the roof again, fixing shingles nobody asked him to fix. He does that when he can't sit still, and none of them have been able to sit still for eleven days.

Eleven days since they walked out of the ice. Eleven days since the mountain swallowed Sable Voss whole and gave them back nothing but each other.

* * *

The blacklist review notice arrives Thursday, hand-delivered by a courier who won't meet anyone's eyes. Official letterhead. Hazel Brightwater's guiding certification suspended pending review, effective immediately, all commercial permits frozen.

Hazel reads it standing in the doorway, rain coming down behind her in gray sheets, and her expression doesn't change at all. That's the thing about Hazel that took Priya two years to learn: the flatter her face, the deeper the water underneath.

"Well," Hazel says. "At least they're consistent."

"They're reviewing it," Priya says. "That's not nothing. The committee cited the falsified survey as mitigating context. Your name might actually come off."

"Might." Hazel folds the letter once, twice, precise as a map crease, and puts it in her jacket pocket like it weighs nothing. "Fifteen years of 'might,' Priya. I've got a whole wardrobe built out of 'might.'"

She goes out into the rain anyway. Priya watches through the window as she crosses the yard without hurrying, shoulders square, and climbs halfway up the ladder to where Dev is working before Dev even notices her. They talk for ten minutes. Dev laughs once — a short, startled sound, like he surprised himself. When Hazel comes back down she's carrying the hammer, having apparently taken over the job, and Dev follows her around the yard like a duckling reattached to its mother.

Priya turns the tablet over and reads the struck record one more time.

*Material falsification.* Four words doing the work of a landslide.

* * *

The internet finds them by Friday, because the internet always finds you eventually, and this time it had help: the press conference footage, bootlegged and subtitled within hours, Hazel's voice reading Elian Brightwater's confession in three languages she'll never know about.

The comments are what Priya expected and worse.

*Traitor. She sold her father's ghost for a clean record.*

*Fifteen years she knew. FIFTEEN. And she let the sport carry his legend while she guided tourists under a fake name.*

*You don't dig up your own dead to save yourself. Whatever happened to loyalty?*

*Good. Burn the myth. The six families deserve the truth.*

That last one has eleven thousand likes, and Priya catches herself staring at it longer than any of the cruel ones, because it's the one Hazel would want, and somehow that makes it harder to look at.

Okafor appears at her shoulder with two cups of tea, hands her one, and reads over her other shoulder without apology. He's a large, unhurried presence, and after three weeks of watching him repair radios in a whiteout, Priya has stopped noticing when he materializes.

"They're calling her a traitor," Priya says.

"They called her worse at the checkpoint." He sips his tea. "Man remembered her face after fifteen years. You know what that means?"

"That she's memorable."

"It means she was never hiding from the people who hated her. She was hiding from the people who loved the lie." He nods at the screen. "Those ones out there — half of them aren't angry she told the truth. They're angry the truth cost them something. Their hero. their poster. The story they bought tickets to."

Priya wraps her hands around the cup. "Doesn't make it cleaner."

"Nothing about this is clean." Okafor says it gently, which is how he says most true things. "Clean was never on offer. She picked the dirty right thing over the dirty easy thing. That's all anybody gets."

Down the hall, a door closes. Neither of them moves to check on Hazel. There's a crew grammar they've developed over years — when Hazel wants company she takes it; when she wants distance, giving her space is the only kindness that works.

* * *

Saturday, the Voss declaration comes through. Missing, presumed dead. Search suspended after forty-eight hours due to conditions. A photograph of Sable Voss from some gala five years ago, all sharp cheekbones and sharper smile, captioned *renowned expedition financier and mountaineer.*

Priya stares at the word *financier* and thinks about forged papers at a checkpoint, about a sabotaged radio, about two porters abandoned above the icefall. About a brother named Corin Voss, dead at twenty-six on a line Elian Brightwater drew that didn't exist.

*Renowned.* The obituaries are already softening her. By next month she'll be a tragic visionary. Priya has watched enough medicine and enough grief to know how memory launders people.

Dev finds her at the table that evening. He sits across from her, elbows on the wood, and for a while neither speaks. The rookie act is mostly gone now — three weeks on the ice burned it off him like fog off a windshield. What's left is someone quieter and older than the kid who signed on eight months ago.

"The porters' families got compensation," he says finally. "Voss's estate. Okafor made the calls."

"I saw."

"And the injured one — Ravi — he sent a message. Through Okafor." Dev turns his cup in a slow circle. "He said to tell Hazel he's sorry. That Voss promised them all a bonus if they kept moving, and he was the only one whose legs wouldn't do it, and he thought—" Dev stops. Starts again. "He thought we'd leave him too. Because that's what the mountain does. That's what everyone does."

"But we didn't."

"But we didn't." Dev looks up. "Is that going to be in any of the articles, you think? Any of the comments?"

Priya considers lying to him and decides he's earned better. "No."

He nods slowly, absorbing it, the way he absorbed the crevasse and the whiteout and the wreck and the body of a man seated facing a summit he'd lied about. Dev absorbs things the way ice absorbs rock — taking them in, carrying them, becoming slightly different stone.

"Hazel came up on the roof yesterday," he says. "Asked me if I regretted signing on. I told her no. She said, 'Ask me again in a year.'" His mouth twists. "I think that was her way of saying she doesn't know yet."

"She will," Priya says. "Or she won't. Either way she'll be honest about it. That's the whole point now, isn't it? That's what all of this cost."

* * *

Monday, the review board requests Hazel's presence. Tuesday, a journalist gets past the front desk by claiming to be a gear rep, and Priya finds him in the hallway asking Dev questions in a warm, reasonable voice — *how did it feel, discovering the wreck, were there tensions with Ms. Voss* — and Priya escorts him out with a medic's firm grip on his elbow and a smile that promises infection.

Wednesday, the first formal complaint arrives against Hazel's certification, filed jointly by three mountaineering clubs, citing "conduct damaging to the integrity of the sport's historical record." As if the record were a vase and Hazel the one who knocked it off the shelf, rather than the one who finally admitted it had been cracked for fifteen years.

Tomas reads the complaint aloud at dinner, deadpan, translating the bureaucratese as he goes. "It says here she 'acted unilaterally and without regard for institutional process.' Which means she didn't ask permission to be honest." He sets the pages down. "There are forty-one signatures."

"There'll be more," Okafor says.

"There'll be fewer, too," Tomas says. "You should see the letters coming in privately. Families of the six. Two of them thank her. One of them — the Okonkwos, their son was nineteen — they wrote nine pages. Nine pages, Priya. Half of it rage, half of it gratitude, and I honestly could not tell you which half was bigger."

Hazel eats through all of this with steady, mechanical attention, the way she ate through the descent, rationing herself. At the end she stacks her bowl on Tomas's and says only, "Nine pages deserves a reply. I'll write it tomorrow."

And that's it. That's the whole speech. Priya watches her carry the bowls to the wash basin and thinks: *fifteen years ago this woman chose exile over defending a father she half-suspected, and she's been paying for the cowardice ever since, and now she's paying for the courage too, and the bill never ends, it just changes currency.*

* * *

Thursday night, Priya can't sleep and finds Hazel in the gear room, sitting on the floor between racks of rope, a headlamp on low, the journal open on her knees.

Priya almost leaves. But Hazel looks up — she always knows — and says, "Sit. If you're going to lurk, sit."

Priya sits. The concrete is cold through her trousers. For a while Hazel just breathes, and Priya matches her, an old habit from altitude, from storms, from nights pinned at Camp Two listening to the glacier grind its teeth.

"The record's struck," Hazel says at last. "Did you see?"

"I saw."

"Fifteen years I wanted that. I used to rehearse it. Lying in tents, in hostels, in jail cells twice — I'd rehearse the day they struck the record and put my name back on the maps." She turns a page with a care that borders on reverence. "You know what it felt like when it actually happened?"

"Tell me."

"Like someone handed me a receipt for something stolen. Here's your proof, here's your refund, sorry about the fifteen years, sorry about your father, sorry about Voss, sorry about the six. Sign here." She closes the journal. "It doesn't give anything back, Priya. It just proves the theft happened."

"Some vindications are like that."

"All of them are like that. Nobody tells you." Hazel laughs, short and dry. "The clubs are filing complaints. Strangers call me a traitor. My father's name is mud in every alpine journal on three continents, and my mother—" She stops. Restarts. "My mother hasn't called. Did you know that? Eleven days. Not one call. I don't know if that means she always knew, or if knowing is worse than not knowing, or if she's just—"

She doesn't finish. Priya waits. Waiting is most of medicine.

"I keep thinking about Voss," Hazel says instead. "Fifteen years she needed this too. Same wound, same length of time. And she wanted the truth so badly she was willing to sell it, package it, brand it, make it hers — and the ice took her mid-reach." Her jaw tightens. "Some part of me understands her completely. That's the part that scares me."

"You're not her."

"No," Hazel agrees quietly. "I'm the one still here, holding the thing she wanted, deciding what it's worth." She looks at the journal, then at Priya. "The board offered me a deal today. Quiet reinstatement. Blacklist lifted, no public statement, the confession stays sealed as 'pending verification.' Everyone saves face. I get my permits back by spring."

Priya's stomach drops. "What did you tell them?"

"Nothing yet." Hazel's eyes in the low lamplight are very steady and very tired. "They gave me until Friday."

* * *

Friday morning, the whole crew ends up in the outfitter's front room without anyone organizing it. Rain on the windows. Tea going cold. Hazel stands by the counter with the board's letter in one hand and the journal in the other, and she doesn't perform the decision, doesn't dramatize it. She just tells them, the way she'd brief a route.

"Quiet reinstatement," she says. "Name cleared, permits restored, the confession stays in a drawer. Or I publish the full document myself, verified, unredacted — and the review happens in the open, with every club and every journalist entitled to their opinion, which they will have, loudly, for years."

"You already decided," Dev says.

"I already decided." A ghost of a smile. "But I wanted to say it out loud in front of the four people who carried me off that mountain, because whatever comes next lands on all of us. Business dries up. Clients get scared. The name Brightwater is going to be a lightning rod for a long time." She looks at each of them in turn. "Nobody's contract ties them here anymore. The debts are cleared. So I'm asking — not ordering, asking — who's staying for the storm?"

The silence lasts maybe four seconds.

Tomas sets down his tea. "I've been hiding behind your invisibility for fifteen years, Hazel. I'd like to try standing somewhere for a change."

Okafor shrugs, which from Okafor is a vow. "Radios need fixing somewhere. Might as be here."

Dev opens his mouth, and Priya watches Hazel hold up a hand — *not yet, rookie, you're young enough to leave* — and watch Dev ignore it completely. "I fell into a crevasse and you hung me on a rope and told me no Brightwater was going to cost another life. You don't get rid of me with bad publicity."

Then they all look at Priya, and she realizes she's last, and she takes her time, because the answer deserves it.

"When I joined this crew," she says, "it was because small and invisible meant safe. I'd run from enough. And then you walked into an icefall and a firestorm and a fifteen-year-old lie and chose the truth every single time, even when the truth was ugly, even when it was yours." She meets Hazel's eyes. "I'm not going back to safe. It's boring, and the company's better here."

Hazel exhales — a long, controlled release, the kind she does at the top of a pitch when the protection is finally placed — and sets the board's letter down on the counter, face-up, unanswered.

Then she picks up a pen, pulls a fresh sheet toward her, and begins to write. Priya reads upside down as the words take shape, blocky and unhurried:

*To the Alpine Records Review Board —*

*Thank you for your offer. I decline.*

*The full confession of Elian Brightwater will be published in complete, verified form within thirty days, together with the recovered survey data and the names of the six climbers who died following his false line. My reinstatement may proceed publicly or not at all.*

*I spent fifteen years learning that a cleared name built on a sealed truth is just a quieter lie. I'm done being quiet.*

*— H. Brightwater*

She signs it, folds it, and hands it to Tomas for the morning post. Outside, the rain keeps falling, and somewhere across town a printer is already running off tomorrow's headlines, and none of them — not one of the five people in that dusty room — knows yet that the letter will reach the board the same day a second envelope arrives, postmarked from a valley hospital, bearing handwriting Priya recognizes from a hundred charts.

Because the world isn't finished with them. The injured porter, Ravi, has woken up lucid — and he remembers something about the night Voss took the journal that changes everything they thought they knew about who else was on that glacier.

Tomas brings the envelope in at dusk, turns it over twice, and says, "Hazel. You're going to want to read this tonight."
