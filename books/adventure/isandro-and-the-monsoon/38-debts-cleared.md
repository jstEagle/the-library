## 38. Debts Cleared

The money arrived on a Tuesday.

I know because Tuesdays are when the valley's only functioning bank opens its shuttered window for three hours, and Priya had been standing in line since dawn with a paper slip and an expression I'd learned to read as *don't ask yet*. She came back to the guesthouse at half past ten carrying a receipt like it was a live grenade, set it on the table between the four of us, and said nothing for a full minute.

Then she said: "It cleared."

Nobody cheered. That was the strange part. Four people who had spent fifteen years — or in Dev's case, two years — counting every rupee against a debt they couldn't see the bottom of, sitting around a plastic table with a bank receipt that said the number was finally zero, and nobody made a sound. Tomas turned his cup a quarter-rotation. Dev looked at his hands. Hazel stood at the window with her back to us, watching the porters load gear onto a truck she wasn't going to be on.

I did the math out loud, because someone had to, and because numbers are the language I trust most. "Voss's fee, minus what we already drew for the approach, minus the porter wages we're still obligated on, minus the replacement radio." I tapped the receipt. "That leaves enough to clear Dev's contract in full, with the penalty clause voided by completion of service. It clears my workshop lien. It clears Tomas's advance from the Kathmandu syndicate — I checked the exchange rate twice."

"And yours?" Priya asked me.

"Mine too."

"And hers?" Dev said quietly, nodding at Hazel's back.

Hers was the one none of us had ever been able to price. Hazel didn't carry debt the way the rest of us did — not the paper kind, anyway. Her debts were the kind you pay in miles. But there had been legal fees after the inquiry, back before any of us knew her, fees that had followed her across two borders and into the fine print of every permit she'd ever forged or borrowed. Voss had known about those too. Voss had known about all of it. That was how Sable Voss worked: she never offered you money, she offered you the exact shape of your cage door.

"Hers is covered," I said. "Barely. If the salvage fee holds through the probate hold on Voss's estate, which it should, because the contract was signed before—" I stopped myself. Nobody needed the timeline recited. We'd all been present for the part where the glacier took her.

* * *

The thing about money that arrives after someone dies for it is that it doesn't feel like money. It feels like a receipt for something else entirely.

I spent the first afternoon doing what I always do when I can't sit still: I worked. The guesthouse had a lean-to out back that the owner let me use as a workshop, and I stripped the remains of our climbing rack down to the metal, sorting carabiners into piles — serviceable, retired, dead. The gate on the #3 ascender had cracked on Day Eight, during Dev's rescue, and I held it under the lamp for a long time thinking about what that piece of aluminum had actually done. It had held. Everything I build either holds or it doesn't, and there's no third category, no partial credit. I have built my whole life around that idea because it's simpler than the truth, which is that people are not equipment, and they fail in ways no inspection catches, and they also hold in ways no rating certifies.

Dev found me there near dusk. He didn't come in right away; he leaned in the doorway holding two cups of tea, and I've been around this kid long enough now to know that the tea is how he asks permission to talk.

"Okafor," he said. "The contract. When it clears tomorrow — does that mean I'm done?"

"That means your indenture is discharged. Paid in full, per clause nine." I set down the cracked ascender. "You're free to walk. Any direction. No notice required."

He looked at the tea like he'd forgotten he was holding it. "Free," he repeated, testing the word the way you test ice — weight forward, one step, listen.

"You don't have to decide tonight."

"I know." He handed me a cup and sat on the upturned crate I keep for exactly this purpose. For a while neither of us drank. Somewhere down the street a generator coughed and caught. "Can I ask you something? Why did you stay? Before. You could have left anytime, right? Your debt wasn't — you told me once it wasn't the same kind."

"It wasn't." My lien was a workshop, tools, a life I'd mortgaged to buy my way out of a country that had stopped issuing me visas. Paper debt. Clean arithmetic. "But Hazel doesn't hire people she can free, Dev. She hires people who need somewhere to stand. And then she stands next to them until they remember how."

"That's not an answer."

"No," I agreed. "It isn't. The answer is that freedom you can't afford is just a longer leash. I stayed because staying was the first choice I'd made in six years that nobody else had priced for me. After that it stopped being a question."

Dev nodded slowly, and I watched him do the same arithmetic I'd done, except his numbers were younger and less worn. Two years indentured to a trekking outfit that sold his contract to whoever paid, ending up with us because Hazel bought the paper out of pity or recognition — I've never asked which, and I suspect it was both. He'd been cargo. Now the cargo had a bill of lading marked PAID, and the question of what he was instead sat in the lean-to drinking tea with me.

"When it clears," he said finally, "I want to stay. Not because of the contract. Because of the route." He meant the new one — the map Hazel had started drawing, though none of us had seen more than the pencil skeleton of it. "Because somebody has to check your welds."

"Somebody does," I said. "Get out of my workshop."

But he was grinning when he went, and so, I'll admit, was I.

* * *

Tomas told me about his debt on the glacier, actually. Night of Day Three, stove hissing, both of us awake because the ice wouldn't let us sleep. He'd married into a surveying family — his father-in-law was the man whose handwriting we later found on that sealed tube in the moraine — and when the 2009 disaster broke, the family name went down with Elian Brightwater's. The syndicate that financed their instruments called the loans. All of them. Overnight. Tomas spent eleven years paying interest on a scandal he hadn't committed and couldn't disprove, because disproving it would have meant defending the man who'd caused it, and he couldn't make his hands do that.

"So when this clears," I'd said, "you're out too."

"When this clears," he'd said, staring at the tent roof, "I'm going to write to my father-in-law's widow and tell her the truth found its way out. And then I don't know. I genuinely don't know who I am without the debt. It's been the shape of everything."

I thought about that a lot over the following days, because it's the question underneath all of ours, isn't it. Strip away the obligation and what's left? A mechanic without a lien is just a man with tools. A navigator without a creditor is just a man with maps. The debt was never really the thing holding us together. But it had been the excuse, and excuses are load-bearing structures whether you admit it or not.

* * *

The discharge papers were signed Thursday morning in the back office of the bank, a room with a ceiling fan that turned with the optimism of a dying man. One by one. The clerk — a bored young man with excellent handwriting — stamped each page without reading it, and each stamp sounded like a small door closing.

Dev went first. He signed, the stamp fell, and he walked out of the office into the street and just stood there for a minute in the sun, blinking, like a man stepping out of a cave. Then he bought fruit from a cart — paid cash, counted it out himself, no one's permission sought — and brought it back and distributed it among us with the solemnity of a man performing a rite. We ate mango slices off a newspaper in the bank's doorway. It was, objectively, nothing. It was, subjectively, the best meal I've had in years.

Priya signed second. Her obligations were the quietest of ours — a medical board suspension she'd been working off through rural postings, a license that existed in a gray zone Hazel's paperwork had kept gray. The fee didn't technically clear that; what it cleared was the loan against her re-certification exam, the fees, the travel, the year of study she could now actually afford to take. "I could go back," she said afterward, on the steps. "Real posting. Real hospital. Name in a registry again."

"You could," I said.

She looked down the street toward the mountains, which were invisible behind haze but present anyway, the way they always are once they've been inside you. "I'm going to take the exam," she said. "And then I'm going to come back and tell Hazel I'm taking it, and watch her pretend she isn't relieved, and then I'm going to stay through the spring route at least. After that we renegotiate. Like adults. Like people who chose each other instead of being stuck with each other."

"That sounds terrible," I said.

"It sounds like a family with good boundaries," she said, and went inside to sign Tomas's witness line, because that's Priya — always three moves ahead on the paperwork.

Tomas signed last of the three. His hand shook slightly, and I watched him steady it against the desk, and when the stamp came down he exhaled like a man surfacing from a long dive. Eleven years. He stepped outside, took out his phone — the one working signal in town, one bar, temperamental as a mule — and dialed the widow in Kathmandu. I gave him privacy, but walls here are thin, and I heard the shape of it: the pause after he said *I have something to tell you*, the long silence, and then a sound I wasn't sure was laughter or weeping until it became both. When he came back in, his eyes were red and his voice was level. "She said she always knew. She said she'd been waiting fifteen years for someone to say it out loud."

"The truth's heavy," I said. "But it weighs less when it's shared."

"That's not one of yours. That's a fortune cookie."

"My workshop, my aphorisms."

* * *

Hazel signed alone.

Not that day — she waited until Friday, and she didn't tell any of us, and I only know because I was at the bank returning a deposit slip when she came out. She had the stamped paper folded into her jacket pocket, and she stood on the bank steps looking at the street the way Dev had, except Hazel doesn't blink at sunlight. She just stood there, and I watched her put her hand flat against her chest, over the pocket, over the journal she still carried everywhere — against her ribs, I knew, having watched her sleep sitting up on the descent with it strapped inside her shell.

Fifteen years of legal debt, gone with a stamp. Fifteen years of a different kind, transferred permanently into forty pages of her father's handwriting, which no court would ever accept as payment and no amount of money could retire.

"You could leave now," I said, coming down the steps. "All of it's settled. Every one of us is free. There's no structural reason for this crew to exist anymore."

"I know." She looked at me. In the mountain light her face is all weather and angles, but that morning it was just tired, and human, and younger than I usually let myself see. "You know what I keep thinking about? The route out. Day Ten. You fixed the stove burner at Camp Two with a spoon and a piece of inner tube, and Priya stitched Dev's hand by headlamp, and Tomas navigated us through meltwater channels he'd never seen, off memory and slope angle, and I just — walked point. That's all I did. Walked point on a team that was already carrying itself." She shook her head. "I spent fifteen years believing I led that crew. The glacier taught me I don't. I'm just the one who goes first."

"So stay the one who goes first."

"On what?"

I'd been waiting for that opening for two days. I pulled the folded paper from my own jacket — the sketch I'd been working on in the evenings, the reason my hands had smelled of pencil instead of solvent all week. It was a rig diagram. Fixed-line anchor system for the new route, the honest one, the one Hazel had started drawing in the guesthouse nights with the crew crowded around her shoulders. Except mine wasn't a route map. Mine was the hardware: anchors, pitches, the load calculations, the placement logic for a face nobody had surveyed properly in thirty years.

"You said the new route needs real surveying," I said. "Honest numbers. Which means it needs someone who builds things that hold. I'm free as of yesterday, Hazel. First time in six years. And the first thing I choose to do with it is this." I handed her the diagram. "Spring route. Full season. I'm in — not indentured, not contracted, not paid against a lien. Just in."

She read it the way she reads everything, fast and complete, and I saw the moment she understood it wasn't a gift but an application. Something moved through her face — relief, maybe, arriving disguised as irritation, the way it always does with her.

"You drew pitch placements for a face you've only seen in whiteout."

"I extrapolated. Flagged every assumption. Page two."

"There's a page two?"

"There's a page four."

She almost smiled. Almost. With Hazel that's a parade. She folded the diagram along my creases and put it in the pocket opposite the journal, and I noted that — one pocket for the past, one for the future, and her body the ledger between them.

"Ask the others," she said. "Properly. Not because I need crew. Because if we're doing this, it's the first thing any of us has done where walking away costs nothing. I want everyone to know what that feels like before they stay."

"They already know. Dev told me Tuesday. Priya told me on the bank steps. Tomas told the whole street via telephone."

Hazel looked at me for a long moment. "And you?"

"I just told you. Page four."

* * *

We voted that night on the guesthouse roof, under a sky rinsed clean by evening rain. It wasn't a formal vote — we don't really do formal — but Hazel laid out the spring route proposal, the real one, with dates and costs and the honest admission that the first season might pay nothing, and asked each of us, by name, in front of the others: in or out, no explanation owed.

Dev: in. Said it before she finished the sentence, then went red, then said, "Also I want lead-school certification, and Okafor's teaching me anchors, and that's non-negotiable."

Priya: in, conditional on her exam in March, which Hazel wrote into the plan on the spot, in pen.

Tomas: in, after a silence in which we all let him have whatever he was saying goodbye to. Then: "Someone has to draw the map honestly this time. Might as well be someone who knows what dishonest ones cost."

Me: in. Page four.

And Hazel — Hazel didn't vote. She just looked around the circle, four faces lit by one lantern and the last of the light, and nodded once, and said, "Then we're a crew. Not a contract. A crew." And Dev raised his tea like it was whiskey, and Priya clinked him, and Tomas laughed — actually laughed, the first time since the glacier — and I sat there with my hands around a tin cup thinking: this is what holds. Not the gear. This.

Later, when the others had gone down and the lantern was low, Hazel stayed up finishing the route sketch, and I stayed up pretending to check rope coils, and eventually she said, without looking up:

"Okafor. The money. Does it feel like enough?"

I considered lying, considered deflection, settled on the truth because she'd earned it on Day Ten with a spoon and a piece of inner tube. "No. It feels like a door closing. But doors close, Hazel. What matters is which way you were facing."

She was quiet a moment. Down in the valley, a truck ground through its gears on the switchbacks — the first supply trucks of the pre-season, heading up toward the range with fresh permits and fresh ambitions and no idea what the ice keeps.

"Then face this way," she said, and turned the sketchpad toward me, and I saw the new route for the first time in full: surveyed lines, flagged assumptions, honest elevations, and at the top, in her tight engineer's hand, a name I hadn't expected.

She'd named the route after the six.

And below the title, smaller, the date she intended to file the permit — and beside it, a second date, circled, that I didn't understand until she said:

"Because there's a hearing in the capital in three weeks. About my blacklist. And I've decided I'm going to attend it in person."
