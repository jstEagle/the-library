## 1. The Man Who Kept Moving

The light came up over the Vell valley the way it always did in October — slow, gray, reluctant — and Theo Zhou was already on the track when it did.

He had been walking the spur since before first light, lamp swinging from one hand, notebook open against his forearm, and he knew this line the way a doctor knows a patient who is dying: intimately, sadly, without illusion. The Kesselheim Spur. Eleven kilometers of rusted rail running east from a junction that no longer appeared on any map printed after the war, threading down through alder scrub toward the river flats where the state had once promised — in documents Theo had spent three years collecting from county archives, some of them stamped, some of them simply remembered by old men in bars — a station called Niederbruck.

Niederbruck did not exist. That was the official position. It had never existed, or if it had, it had been a surveyor's error, a clerical ghost, a name attached to nothing. The Vell River had been dammed at Hohenfels in the spring of a year Theo was too young to remember, and everything below the high-water line had become, administratively speaking, water.

But the rails were still here. That was the thing about rails. You could unprint a town; you could not so easily unpull eleven kilometers of steel driven into gravel bed by men with sledgehammers and pay stubs. The state could erase a place from its atlases, but the iron kept a different kind of record, and Theo had made a career — a quiet, poorly paid, deliberately invisible career — of reading it.

He crouched at kilometer marker seven and ran his thumb along the railhead. Rust, but rust with texture under it. Recent traffic? No. He'd have heard it, felt it through the ground in the night, the way he'd learned to feel trains through hotel floors in forty towns across three provinces. This was older movement — months, maybe a season. A maintenance vehicle, single axle, light load. Someone had come out here and come back, and no schedule anywhere accounted for it.

He wrote it down. *Km 7.2, north rail, polished band approx. 40mm, oxidation incomplete. Single-axle inspection car, direction of travel uncertain.* His handwriting was small and exact, an engineer's hand, the letters leaning slightly forward as if they too were in a hurry to get somewhere.

The habit of never sleeping twice in the same town had started as necessity and hardened into doctrine. Necessity first: a man whose job was mapping lines the government had ordered forgotten did not want a forwarding address. Doctrine later: somewhere around his ninth year of it, Theo had noticed that staying put made him sloppy. A second night in the same bed and he started leaving things out — the notebook on the nightstand instead of inside his coat, the good camera case unlocked, his real surname spoken to a landlady he'd begun to trust. Stillness made him legible. And a man who was legible, in his line of work, was a man who eventually received visitors he did not want.

So he moved. Fourteen towns last year. He could pack in four minutes; he timed himself sometimes, alone in rented rooms, like a man practicing scales. He knew which border crossings took bribes and which took stamps and which took neither because nobody manned them anymore. He knew how to read a timetable for what it concealed rather than what it offered — the gaps, the suspended services, the lines that appeared on the public schedule but hadn't run since the flood, kept listed purely so that their absence would look like neglect instead of intention.

That was the theory he'd built his life on, anyway: *neglect looks like accident; intention looks like neglect done carefully.*

He stood, brushed grit from his knee, and walked on toward the river end of the spur.

* * *

The morning fog held off the water longer than it held off the land, so that for twenty minutes Theo walked through a world divided in two: gray air above, and below him, when the ground dropped away at the old embankment, a flat white sheet where the reservoir lay. Somewhere under that sheet were the orchards of Niederbruck. Somewhere under it was the church they'd never officially built, the schoolhouse, the mill race, the graves — the graves had been moved, according to the relocation order, all four hundred and six of them, though Theo had once interviewed a gravedigger's daughter in a town two valleys over who said her father had been paid per grave moved and had quit counting at ninety because the money stopped and the orders changed.

He didn't think about that every day. He thought about it most days.

At the spur's terminus — a stub end, buffers wrapped in ivy, the gravel apron soft with moss — he set down his lamp and did what he always did at the end of a dead line: he looked at what the line had been trying to reach. In this case, water. Just water, and beyond it, through the thinning fog, the far shore where the hills stepped up in green terraces toward the Hohenfels crest.

And nailed to the buffer beam, square and white and obscene in all that decay, was a notice.

Theo went still. Not the trained stillness of a man avoiding attention — the other kind, the animal kind, the whole body listening.

Notices rotted out here. He'd found them before: timber permits thirty years stale, a hunting closure, once, memorably, a proclamation about a harvest festival in a town that was under nine meters of water. Those notices were part of the landscape's grammar, the way the ivy was, the way the rust was. They belonged to the past tense.

This notice was fresh. The paper was bright. The corners were sharp. The staple gleamed.

He crossed the apron slowly, the way you approach a dog you don't know, and read it twice.

*BY ORDER OF THE MINISTRY OF TRANSPORT AND TERRITORIAL RECORDS,* it began, in the heavy serif font the state used when it wanted to sound like history rather than bureaucracy. *Pursuant to Statute 44-C (Revision of Cartographic Materials), all maps, plans, surveys, photographs, and recorded observations pertaining to the Vell River corridor between Hohenfels Dam and the provincial boundary are hereby declared CLOSED MATERIALS. Possession of such materials after the thirtieth day of this month constitutes an offense under Section 9. Holders are required to surrender said materials to the nearest Records Office. No compensation will be provided.*

Below it, the seal. Below the seal, a signature Theo didn't recognize. Below the signature, in smaller type, the sentence that turned his mouth dry:

*Surrender includes materials of private origin, including inherited materials.*

Inherited materials.

His father's compass was in his inside pocket, against his ribs, where it had been every day for nineteen years. Brass-cased, needle clouded almost opaque, the maker's mark worn to a ghost on the reverse. It didn't point north — it had never pointed north, not reliably, not since the day a railway clerk had handed it back to Theo along with a box of his father's effects and a form to sign confirming receipt. Theo had been seventeen. He had signed the form. He had carried the broken compass ever since the way other men carried a saint's medal, and he had told himself for nineteen years that it was sentiment, that the needle's strange wandering meant nothing, that a dead man's tools were just tools.

The notice said otherwise. Or rather — the notice said nothing about compasses. But someone, somewhere in the Ministry of Transport and Territorial Records, had decided that the Vell corridor needed to be swept clean of paper, now, this month, with a statute dusted off and a new signature found. Sweeps happened for reasons. Reasons had authors. Authors had names, and files, and one of those files, Theo would have bet the compass itself, had his father's name in it somewhere, in a folder that had supposedly been closed when the folder's owner closed his eyes forever on a stretch of line outside Hohenfels.

He photographed the notice. He photographed it wide, then close, then each clause individually, working the little camera with hands that were steadier than he felt. Then he took out his knife, worked the staple loose, and folded the original into the oilskin sleeve where he kept the documents that mattered — the ones he'd have time to grab if he ever had four minutes' warning, which he had never yet needed and no longer believed he never would.

Then he stood for a while looking at the water.

* * *

He was three kilometers back up the spur, walking fast with the fog burning off around him, when he let himself admit what the unease in his chest actually was.

It wasn't fear of the sweep. He'd survived sweeps before; there had been a bad winter eight years ago when half his archive had gone into a stove in a boarding house in Marren and he'd watched twenty years of other people's memories curl and blacken, and he'd survived that too, thinner and angrier. Fear was a currency he was used to carrying.

It was the stillness. That was the thing he couldn't explain to anyone who hadn't lived it. Everyone assumed the danger in his life came from motion — from the trains, the borders, the moving from town to town like a man with a warrant behind him. But motion was safe. Motion was anonymous. A man on a platform was nobody; a man who'd slept three nights in the same room became somebody, became a shape, became a silhouette someone could describe to someone else. The danger came in the pauses. Every catastrophe of his adult life had arrived during a pause — the arrest of his mentor Ostrava had happened while Ostrava was convalescing, of all things, flat on his back in a sanatorium with his maps in a trunk under the bed. The trunk was found in an afternoon. Ostrava was found eleven years later, in a list, in a footnote, in a number.

Stillness was where they caught you. Stillness was where the past caught up, tapped you on the shoulder, and said: *I know where you live now.*

And this notice — this bright, stapled, freshly authored notice — was the past tapping. Not his past, maybe. Maybe it was bigger than him, a general housecleaning, a minister's whim, budget-season housekeeping. But *inherited materials*. Somebody had written that phrase into the text. Somebody had sat at a desk and decided that sons should be answerable for fathers, that the sweep should reach into pockets and attics and the inside linings of coats. That was not housekeeping. That was a hand reaching for something specific, and the Vell corridor was a long way to send a hand unless the hand wanted what was here.

What was here? Water. Dead rails. A drowned town that had never existed. And whatever it was that had made the state dam the river in the first place — not for the hydro power, Theo had long suspected, not primarily, because the power output figures had never justified the cost and he'd found the internal audit once, photocopied it, and understood why it had stayed internal — whatever it was that lay under the water or above it or upstream of it, in the gorge country where the maps went blank even in the oldest atlases, blank in a way that wasn't ignorance but decision.

He reached the junction at noon. The mainline here was closed too — suspended service, the timetable said, *pending review*, pending for six years now — but the rails were warm from the sun and he sat down between them with his back against a signal post and ate bread and hard cheese and let himself do the arithmetic he'd been avoiding all morning.

Option one: obey. Walk to the nearest Records Office, surrender his father's compass and his notebooks and the oilskin folder, accept whatever receipt they gave him, and become a man with nothing in his pockets. He imagined it with perfect clarity, the way he imagined all his exits: the clerk's bored face, the form, the door opening onto a street where he would be, for the first time since he was seventeen, genuinely empty-handed. He would be free, in the sense that a stripped screw is free.

Option two: run. Pack in four minutes, cross into the next province on the freight line he knew, and keep moving ahead of the sweep until the sweep lost interest or he died of it. This was the option his whole architecture of habits had been built for. Fourteen towns a year. Never the same bed twice. He could be untouchable by Thursday.

Option three, which he did not let himself name, was the one where he went upstream.

He finished the cheese. He brushed the crumbs from his coat with the deliberation of a ritual. And then he took out the compass and held it in his palm and did what he did perhaps twice a year, in moments of weakness he never planned: he asked it a question.

The needle swung. It always swung — that was its sickness, its wound, its inheritance. North, then off north, then firmly, stubbornly, impossibly off, settling with a fine trembling insistence toward the southeast. Toward the river. Toward the gorge country. Toward the blank space on the maps.

Toward home, said a voice in him that sounded younger than he was. Toward whatever Father wouldn't leave alone.

"Sentiment," Theo said aloud, to the empty junction, to the crows walking the rails. His voice came out flat and unconvinced.

He put the compass away, stood, and slung his bag. The sweep began in twenty-six days. The Records Office in the district capital opened at eight. He had a decision to make, and he had learned long ago that the only reliable way to make a decision was to make it in motion, walking, with the ground going by underneath — because a man in motion was hard to catch, and a man in motion could tell himself he was only passing through, only looking, only following a line to see where it went.

He walked west along the closed mainline, toward the district capital, toward the Records Office, telling himself with every step that he intended to surrender everything.

He was lying, and he knew it, and the knowing sat in him like a swallowed stone. Because the notice had used the word *inherited*, and whoever had written it had known — had known about the compass, or feared it, or needed it — and a man does not surrender the only piece of his father the state hasn't already taken. Not to an office. Not to a statute. Not to anyone.

By evening he had covered eighteen kilometers and slept in none of them. By the next dawn he was on a freight siding outside the capital, watching the Records Office lights come on across the yard, and watching, with the particular cold attention of a man who has spent his life noticing what official buildings try not to show, the black government motorcar that pulled up to its door before the clerks arrived.

A woman got out of the car. She stood a moment in the early light, consulting a paper, and even at that distance Theo saw her turn her head and look — directly, deliberately, unhurriedly — at the siding where he stood among the freight cars.

She couldn't have seen him. There was no possible way.

He slid back into the shadow of the cars anyway, heart going hard, and understood that the pause was over. Whatever came next, it would find him moving.
