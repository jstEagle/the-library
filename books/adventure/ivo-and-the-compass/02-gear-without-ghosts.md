## 2. Gear Without Ghosts

The camp announced itself before he saw it — a smell of woodsmoke gone cold and sour, the particular staleness of a fire that had died mid-conversation. Theo Zhou stopped at the edge of the alder thicket and stood very still, the way he'd learned to stand in forty-one towns that no longer existed on any map: first with his eyes, then with his ears, then with that older sense underneath both, the one that had kept him alive through collapsed culverts and one memorable night in a tunnel where the air itself seemed to be holding its breath.

Nothing moved. Nothing breathed. But someone had been here.

He came out of the trees with his hands visible, an old habit from surveying land where the owners hadn't been told the land was being surveyed. The camp sat in a natural clearing above the Vell River, maybe thirty meters back from the water, arranged with the tidy geometry of people who expected to return to everything they left. A canvas mess tent sagged at one corner where a guy-line had slipped its stake. Two sleeping tents stood zipped shut. A tripod leaned against a crate like a man waiting for a train that had already stopped running.

Theo counted the chairs. Four. Three of them pushed back from the folding table at angles that suggested haste; the fourth still squared to the table's edge, as if its occupant had meant to come back and finish something.

"Hello?" His voice came out flatter than he intended. He tried again. "Survey team?"

The river answered instead, low and brown-green with early snowmelt, sliding past below the bank with the patient indifference of water that had been carrying secrets longer than anyone had been alive to drown in them.

He circled the perimeter once, reading the ground the way other men read newspapers. Mud here, soft from last week's rain, and it told him things. It told him four sets of boots, two large, two medium, all moving *in* — toward the tents, toward the table, toward the center of the camp — and none moving out. No tracks led toward the river. None led up the game trail behind the clearing. The mud simply ended their story, the way a sentence ends when the speaker has been interrupted.

People didn't leave camps like this. People carried their gear out, kicked dirt over their fires, took down their tripods. People were careless in leaving, not careful. This camp had been abandoned the way a house is abandoned when the family believes they'll be back by Sunday.

Or the way a stage is struck after the audience has been escorted out.

Theo stood at the edge of it for a long moment, his breath making small ghosts in the cold morning air, and felt the familiar itch start between his shoulder blades — the one that said *walk away, Zhou, walk away, you've survived this long by never asking what's upstream.* He was very good at listening to that itch. It was practically a profession. He mapped closed lines for a government that closed them, and he had learned that the safest place in any erased landscape was exactly one step behind the erasure, close enough to see the shape of what had been removed but never so close that the removal noticed him back.

But the notice had changed something. Yesterday's notice, nailed to the freight office door in Halden with four bright staples: *ALL SURVEY DOCUMENTS, MAPS, AND INSTRUMENTS PERTAINING TO THE VELL RIVER CORRIDOR ARE HEREBY SUBJECT TO CONFISCATION UNDER EMERGENCY STATUTE 9-C. COMPLIANCE IS MANDATORY.*

Emergency Statute 9-C. He'd had to look it up in a reference book he kept for exactly this purpose, and the statute turned out to be older than he was, written for floods and bridge failures, dusted off now like a weapon from a closet. They wanted every map of the Vell corridor surrendered. Which meant something on the Vell corridor needed unmapping. And something unmapped, in Theo's long experience, was something that had happened.

He unslung his camera.

It was a Graflex, his father's before it was his, heavy as a brick and twice as honest. No electronics to fail, no battery to die in the cold. You cocked the shutter, you focused on the ground glass under the black cloth, and the world agreed to be whatever you saw. He'd photographed a hundred dead stations and a hundred flooded farms with this camera, and the photographs lived in oilcloth sleeves inside a false bottom of his instrument case, and if anyone ever found them, Theo Zhou would spend the rest of his short life explaining why a government surveyor documented the government's own forgetting.

He worked methodically, because method was how he kept the dread at arm's length. Wide shot of the camp from the north. Wide shot from the river side, showing the relationship of the clearing to the water. The mess tent interior: four tin plates on the table, three with food still on them — bread gone gray with mold, a smear of something that had once been stew, a boiled egg with its top neatly sliced off and never eaten. The fourth plate clean, wiped, stacked upside down on the others. Someone had finished their meal. Someone had washed their plate. While the others' food sat going bad.

Instruments next. That was the part that made his hands slow down.

A theodolite sat on its tripod, dust cover *on*, leveled, the vertical circle locked. Beside it, a stadia rod in its leather sleeve, strap buckled. A hand level in its case. A barometer — a real mercurial barometer, the kind you don't transport without crating — standing upright in a wooden box with straw packed around it, packed by someone with time and care. Every instrument cleaned. Every instrument cased. Every instrument ready to be carried somewhere.

Nobody carries instruments somewhere and then doesn't carry them.

He photographed the serial numbers. That was reflex, pure professional reflex — the Ministry stamped everything, and the numbers told you which office had signed for what, and Theo had spent years learning to read those numbers like tea leaves. The theodolite's plate read VR-114. The barometer, VR-109. The prefix wasn't one he knew. Not Northern District. Not Hydrographic. Not any of the eleven regional codes he could recite in his sleep. VR. Vell River. A designation that shouldn't exist, because officially there was nothing on the Vell River to survey. The Vell corridor had been decommissioned, de-listed, de-everythinged forty years ago. There were no active surveys on the Vell because there was no Vell. The maps said so. He had drawn some of the maps himself.

He photographed the tents from outside, then — after a full minute of argument with himself that he lost — unzipped the nearest one.

Sleeping bags rolled and strapped. Rucksacks packed, propped upright against the canvas walls. One of them had a name tape inside the flap: OSEI, P. Another read HALVORSEN. The third rucksack had no name, only a laundry mark, a faded blue cross-stitch that might have meant anything. In the second tent, the same story: beds made or bags rolled, kit stowed, a paperback novel face-down on a folded jacket with a pencil marking the page. *The Long Detour*, by some author Theo had never heard of, stopped eighty pages from the end.

Four people had lived here. Four people had planned to come back. Four people had walked into this clearing with their whole lives organized around the assumption of continuity, and then the continuity had ended so completely that nobody had even taken the book.

Theo crouched over the novel for a while, not touching it, photographing the open page, the pencil, the angle of abandonment. Eighty pages from the end. He thought about all the books he'd never finished in all the towns he'd never slept in twice, and felt something move in his chest that he refused to name.

* * *

The light was going long and amber by the time he worked his way to the far side of the camp, where a tarp had been strung between two alders to make a dry store. Crates beneath it, stenciled with the same VR prefix. Rations, mostly — cases of hardtack and tinned fish, a crate of paraffin, a medical kit with its white cross sun-bleached to pink. Standard expedition weight. Enough for six weeks in the field for four people.

He photographed the crates. He photographed the stencil. He knelt to check the seals on the ration cases — broken, all of them, opened and re-closed with the casual competence of people eating their own supplies — and that was when he saw the instrument case tucked at the back, half-shadowed under the tarp's drip line.

It was small. Brass-cornered, leather over wood, the leather cracked along the spine in a pattern Theo knew better than his own handwriting, because he had watched that crack spread across twenty years of his childhood. He knew the repair on the lower hinge too — solder, badly done, done by a man who understood bridges and not hinges. He knew the wear spot on the lid where a thumb had pushed it open ten thousand times.

His mouth went dry in a way that had nothing to do with the cold.

"No," he said, out loud, to the case, to the camp, to the river. "No, that's not —"

He lifted it out anyway. Of course he lifted it out. There had never been a version of this moment in which Theo Zhou saw that case and did not lift it out.

It was heavier than it should have been, the way it had always been heavier than it should have been, and when he pressed the worn thumb-spot and the lid swung open on its protesting hinge, the compass lay inside in its bed of faded green felt, and the world went narrow and ringing, like the moment after a blast before the sound arrives.

It was his father's compass. Not one like it. *It.* He knew the hairline scratch across the crystal from the time young Theo had dropped it on the station platform at Marrow Bend. He knew the maker's engraving on the inner lid — *E. VASSEUR & FILS, LYON* — in script worn nearly smooth. He knew the needle, which had never pointed true north in its life, which had always carried a stubborn eastern bias that his father had called "character" and never once had corrected, though any instrument man worth his salt would have corrected it in an afternoon.

And stamped into the brass baseplate, crisp and official and utterly impossible, was a serial number:

**VR-001.**

The first instrument of a survey series that did not exist, issued to a survey team that had vanished, and it was sitting in a store crate on the Vell River wearing his father's scratches.

Theo sat down in the mud. He didn't decide to; his legs simply resigned. The compass sat in his open hands and the needle swung in its lazy, drunken arc — east, east, always east, character — and settled pointing not north but upriver, deeper into the corridor, toward whatever the state wanted unmapped badly enough to resurrect a forty-year-old emergency statute.

His father had disappeared twenty-two years ago. Officially: reassigned, records sealed, file closed, the whole quiet machinery of erasure that Theo had grown up learning to recognize the way farm kids learn weather. His mother had waited three years and then stopped setting a place at the table, and had never spoken of it again, and had died still not speaking of it, and Theo had inherited the compass and the silence and the habit of never sleeping twice in the same town, and he had told himself for twenty-two years that the compass was just a compass, that the eastern bias was just a flaw, that his father had been reassigned to some northern district and lost his nerve about writing home, that men did that, that men did that all the time.

The needle pointed upriver. The needle had always pointed east-ish. The Vell ran northeast here.

*Character,* Theo thought, and heard his father's voice say it, warm and rueful, on a platform at Marrow Bend, twenty-two years and one drowned town ago.

He photographed the compass. Hands steady — he made them be steady, the way you make your hands be steady on a span that's swaying, by giving them a job and letting the job hold them. Serial number. Maker's mark. Needle at rest. The scratch on the crystal, raking light. Then, because the Graflex was already cocked and the ground glass already held the image and some part of him understood that this was the last photograph of a life that had ended sometime in the last hour without his noticing, he turned the camera on himself — no. He didn't. He lowered the camera and sat in the mud and looked at the thing in his hands until the light went from amber to iron.

The past hadn't followed him here. That was the thought that finally arrived, cold and complete, as the first stars came out over the gorge. He had spent twenty-two years assuming he was running ahead of something. But you can't run ahead of a thing that moves through files and stamps and serial numbers, a thing that issues instruments and then recalls the men who carry them. The past hadn't followed him. The past had been *sent*. Placed. Stamped VR-001 and left under a tarp on the exact river his father had been erased from, in a camp where four people had evaporated between one meal and the next, waiting — with the patience of all government property — for whoever came looking.

They had known he would come looking. Or they had known someone would, and Theo was what the river produced.

He got up. He packed the camera. He put the compass in his coat's inner pocket, against his chest, where he could feel its weight with every heartbeat, and he told himself he was confiscating evidence under Statute 9-C, complying with the law, doing his job, and he let himself believe it for almost the whole length of the clearing.

At the edge of the alder thicket he stopped and looked back once. Four chairs. Three dirty plates. A book eighty pages from its ending. And somewhere upstream, in the dark, the needle's patient insistence pulling northeast like a finger crooked in invitation — or summons.

Behind him, downstream, faint but unmistakable on the wind off the water, came the sound he had been dreading since noon: a handcar on the old closed line, clicking over the joints of rails that officially did not exist, coming up the valley at speed.

Someone from the Ministry had found his signature in the Halden freight office ledger. Someone was coming to collect the man who kept moving.

Theo buttoned his coat over the compass and stepped into the trees, and for the first time in twenty-two years, he did not choose a direction by habit.

He chose it by the needle.
