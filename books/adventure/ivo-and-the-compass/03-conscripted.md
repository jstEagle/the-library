## 3. Conscripted

The notice was still in Theo's pocket when the government came for him.

He had spent the morning doing what he always did after finding something he shouldn't have: nothing that looked like anything. He sat on the loading dock of a grain elevator that hadn't shipped grain since before he was born, drank coffee from a thermos gone metallic with age, and read the survey team's serial number off his father's compass until the digits wore grooves into his attention. VSR-114. He had photographed the abandoned camp at first light — instruments laid out on canvas with a care that looked less like departure and more like interruption — and then he had walked back down the spur line to the junction town of Halden's Crossing, which appeared on no map printed after 1979, and checked into the only rooming house still taking boarders.

He told himself he would sleep four hours and be gone by noon. He had not slept in Halden's Crossing before. The rule held: never sleep twice in the same town, because towns that stopped mattering to maps had a way of stopping mattering to the people in them, and a man who stayed became furniture, and furniture could be moved by anyone with a truck.

He was folding the compass into its leather case when the knock came — two raps, precise, spaced like a metronome.

Through the window he saw the car first: a state vehicle, dark green, unmarked except for the license plate that began with the prefix reserved for the Ministry of Interior Survey. Then the woman standing beside it, hands clasped behind her back, watching his window with the patience of someone who knew exactly which curtain would twitch.

Theo considered the back stairs. He had used a great many back stairs in his life and had developed an almost professional relationship with them. But the woman's position was not accidental; she stood where she could see both the stairwell door and the alley, and her coat was open in a way that was either careless or deliberate, and Theo had learned long ago that people who dressed deliberately careless were the most careful people alive.

He opened the door instead.

"You're Zhou," she said. Not a question. She was perhaps forty, with gray coming in at her temples like frost claiming a field edge by edge, and she wore her government-issue restraint the way some men wore holsters — visibly, so you'd know better than to test it. "Maren Kessler. Interior Survey."

"There's no such department," Theo said. "Interior Survey was dissolved in—"

"1981. Yes." Her mouth did something that was almost a smile but had too much arithmetic in it. "May I come in, Mr. Zhou, or would you prefer we conduct this on the landing, where your landlady can hear everything? I understand she's hard of hearing, but I'm told she reads lips beautifully."

He stepped aside. It was not, he told himself, surrender. It was reconnaissance.

* * *

She did not sit. She stood at the room's single window and looked out at the rail yard below — six tracks fanning into weeds, a water tower rusted to the color of dried blood — and Theo understood that she was letting him look at her while she pretended not to look at anything, which was its own kind of technique.

"You found the camp," she said.

"I walk old lines. I photograph things. That's not a crime yet."

"No. But possession of ministry property is." She turned. "The compass, Mr. Zhou. Serial VSR-114. Issued 1976 to the Vell River Survey. It was logged out to a team leader named Aldous Wren, and it has not been returned."

Theo kept his face still, which took work, because inside his chest something had begun to fall — that long vertical drop you feel when a floor you've been standing on turns out to be a trapdoor. *Aldous Wren.* He knew the name the way you know a scar's origin story: his father's partner, the other half of the only partnership Elias Zhou had ever tolerated, the man who'd taught him to read cut grades from the cab window. Wren had written exactly one letter to Theo after his father vanished, a letter that said *your father was the best surveyor I ever worked with and I am not permitted to say more,* and then Wren had also vanished, years later, into a survey assignment up the Vell that officially never happened.

"The compass belonged to my father," Theo said. "It came to me through his effects. Whatever stamp is on it, it's mine."

"His effects were seized in 1988."

"Then someone sold them back to me, or lost them, or didn't look hard enough. That's not my crime either."

Kessler crossed the small room in three steps and stopped close enough that he could see the fatigue under her competence — the bruised skin under her eyes, the way her weight favored her left leg as though she'd been riding handcars for days. Up close she smelled of coal smoke and river damp.

"Here's what happens now," she said. "Option one: I write a report stating that a freelance surveyor trespassed on a closed corridor, disturbed an active investigation site, and removed ministry equipment. You lose the compass. You lose your camera and its film. You lose your clearance to walk any closed line in this country, which means you lose your work, which means—" she glanced around the bare room, at the thermos, at the pack already half-stowed by the door, "—you lose the only life you appear to have. And you do all of this knowing nothing. That's option one. It's the option most people take, because most people are sensible."

"And option two?"

"Option two is that you come upstream with me."

The word *upstream* landed in the room like a dropped tool. Theo heard himself say, "Upstream of what?" and hated how fast the curiosity got out of him, how visible it must have been, because Kessler's expression shifted by a millimeter — the arithmetic becoming something closer to satisfaction.

"Four weeks ago," she said, "a five-person survey team entered the upper Vell corridor under authority I am not at liberty to describe. Their mandate was hydrographic assessment of decommissioned infrastructure. Standard closure work. Confirming that what we buried stays buried." She paused. "Three weeks ago they stopped transmitting. Two weeks ago a supply flight over the gorge reported their last camp intact and empty. Meals half-eaten. Instruments laid out. No tracks leaving in any direction."

"I saw the camp."

"I know you saw the camp. That's rather the point." She let that sit. "The team leader was Aldous Wren."

There it was, said plainly, and Theo made himself breathe through it. He walked to the window so she couldn't watch his face, and looked down at the dead rail yard, and thought about how a man spends thirty years learning to read erased landscapes and tells himself it's just a trade, just a living, and then one afternoon a stranger says a name and the whole trade reveals itself as a vigil.

"Wren worked with my father," Theo said.

"Yes."

"Before my father disappeared."

"Yes."

"And now Wren has disappeared the same way, and you want me to go find him, and you're leading with threats because you think that's what I'll respond to."

Kessler was quiet for a moment. When she spoke again, the bureaucratic lacquer had thinned enough to show something underneath — not warmth, exactly, but a kind of grim respect.

"I led with threats because threats are true," she said. "Everything I described will happen if you refuse. I don't bluff, Mr. Zhou; bluffing is for people whose institutions keep their promises. Mine doesn't, and I've stopped pretending otherwise even to myself." She moved to stand beside him at the window, both of them looking down at the weeds swallowing the sixth track. "But here is also true: Aldous Wren carried that compass for eleven years and never once asked to have it reissued, and when I asked why, an old clerk told me it was because the instrument wasn't standard issue. It was custom-built. By Elias Zhou. Your father built it, calibrated it, and gave it to his partner, and whatever it does, it does because your father wanted it done." She turned her head, and her eyes were level and tired and honest in a way that unsettled Theo more than the threats had. "You've been walking closed lines your whole adult life looking for what happened to him. Don't tell me you haven't. A man doesn't develop habits like yours out of love of scenery. So option two isn't my offer. Option two is the thing you've been walking toward since you were nineteen years old, and I am simply the first person rude enough to say it out loud."

The room was very quiet. Below, wind moved through the rail yard and made the weeds bow in sequence, west to east, like a crowd acknowledging something.

"If I refuse," Theo said slowly, "you take the compass."

"I take the compass, the film, and your freedom to do this work. Yes."

"And if I come?"

"Then you're a contracted specialist attached to a recovery operation. You get paid. You get access. You get answers — or as many as exist." She straightened. "And you get to be present when we find Wren, which given the state of that camp may mean you get to be present when we find out what's left to find."

Theo looked at the compass case in his hand. He thought about the camp at dawn: the transit set up and leveled, pointing not along the line but across the river, toward the far bank, toward the hills. He thought about meals abandoned mid-bite, which meant no time to run, which meant either panic or something that made running irrelevant. He thought about his father's last postcard, sent the summer before he vanished, a picture of a river gorge with a single line on the back: *The water keeps what we give it.*

"What aren't you telling me?" Theo asked.

"A great deal," Kessler said. "Some of it because I'm ordered to withhold it. Some of it because I don't yet know whom I can trust with it. Those categories overlap less than you'd hope." For the first time she sat, perching on the room's only chair, and rubbed her left knee absently. "I'll tell you one thing for free, because you'll learn it within a day anyway and I'd rather you hear it from me. My orders regarding this operation are not entirely what you'd expect. Recovery of personnel is listed third. Records recovery is listed first. And there's a second clause I'm not going to read to you, because reading it aloud would require us to have a different conversation than the one I'm hoping we're having."

"Which is?"

"The kind where you decide I'm a person and not just a function of the ministry." She said it flatly, without self-pity, and Theo found it was the most disarming thing she'd done. "I've read your file, Zhou. Every closed-line surveyor has one, but yours is thick. You've mapped four hundred kilometers of corridor the state pretends doesn't exist, and you've published none of it, and you've never once been caught until now — which means either you're very good or someone has been letting you work. I haven't determined which. I mention it because it means you already know how to move quietly through country that's watched, and I need that, and you need what I have, and neither of us has to like the arrangement for it to hold."

Theo turned the compass over in his hands. The brass was worn bright along the hinge where a thumb had worried it for decades — his father's thumb, then Wren's, then his own. He thought about refusing on principle. He was fairly sure he had principles; he simply couldn't locate the one that applied. The truth was simpler and uglier: Kessler had said the word *answers* and every rebellious cell in him had leaned toward it like a plant toward a window.

"One condition," he said.

"You're not in a position to negotiate."

"Everyone's in a position to negotiate. That's what conditions are for." He held up the compass. "This stays with me. Not logged, not bagged, not 'returned to inventory.' If it goes into an evidence locker, I walk, report or no report. Because whatever my father built into this thing, it's the closest thing to a message from him that I've ever held, and I'm not handing it to the same institution that erased him so it can erase him twice."

Something moved behind Kessler's eyes — recognition, maybe, or the memory of her own erasures. She studied him for a long moment, and then she nodded once, curtly, like a lock seating shut.

"It stays with you," she said. "For now. We leave within the hour — there's a handcar on the old mainline north of the yard, and I'd rather be moving before the regional office remembers I passed through without filing a travel authorization." She rose, wincing slightly at the knee, and paused at the door with her hand on the frame. "Zhou. One more thing, and then I'll let you pack. Wren's team included a junior engineer who kept a personal logbook. Unofficial. Against regulations. When we recovered the supply manifests, that logbook was missing — taken from the camp, or hidden, or carried off by whoever left those meals half-eaten. If you find it before I do, I'm asking you — not ordering, asking — to let me read it before you decide what to do with it."

"Why?"

"Because I've spent nine years filing reports that make the Vell corridor sound like paperwork," Maren Kessler said, "and three weeks ago five people walked into that paperwork and stopped existing, and I would very much like to know whether I've been describing the world accurately all this time, or just describing my orders." She opened the door onto the dim hallway. "One hour. North end of the yard. Bring warm clothes — the gorge holds winter longer than the maps admit. Most things hold on longer than the maps admit."

Her footsteps went down the stairs evenly, unhurried, and the street door closed behind her with a click that sounded, in the silence, like a page turning.

Theo stood a while with the compass open in his palm. The needle swung, settled, trembled — and he noticed, for the first time, that it wasn't settling quite where it should. Half a degree off north, maybe less. He'd have called it wear, any other day. He'd have called it a broken instrument.

Instead he closed the lid, packed his bag, and went down to meet the handcar, telling himself the pull in his chest was only resentment, and knowing it was not.
