## 4. Downstream of Everything

The handcar weighed less than Theo's grief, and that was the only reason it moved at all.

They'd found it in the weeds behind the last open station on the Vell line — a four-wheeled lever car with a cracked handle grip and a name stenciled on its frame that had been painted over twice. The paint beneath the paint was still legible if you knew how to look: *Vell Valley Regional*, then a government gray, then the newer, flatter gray that meant the state itself had claimed it before abandoning it. Three layers of ownership, none of them current. The car belonged to no one now, which meant it belonged to whoever was willing to pump its handles up a dead rail line into country the maps had stopped admitting existed.

Maren Kessler stood at the edge of the platform with her coat buttoned to the throat, watching him work the release lever with the expression of a woman cataloguing his habits for later use.

"You've done this before," she said.

"I've done everything before." Theo set the brake. "That's usually the problem."

She didn't laugh. He hadn't expected her to. In the two days since she'd appeared at the abandoned survey camp with her credentials and her threat shaped like an offer, he'd catalogued her in return: she slept sitting up, she ate without pleasure, and she looked at closed things — closed lines, closed buildings, closed files — the way other people looked at open wounds. Not with pity. With recognition.

"Rail's intact as far as the old Kiln Road crossing," he said. "After that, frost heave has probably lifted the joints. We push until we can't, then we walk."

"And you know this because?"

"Because I mapped this line." He said it flatly, without pride. "Eleven years ago. Before they closed it. I was the one who certified it safe for closure."

Something moved behind Maren's eyes — not surprise, something colder and more careful. "You surveyed a line in order to bury it."

"That's most of what surveying is, now." Theo gripped the handles. "The state doesn't tear anything down anymore. It just stops looking at it. My job is to write the report that makes not-looking official." He nodded upstream, toward the gray shoulder of hills where the Vell came down out of country no one named anymore. "Ready?"

"No," she said, and stepped onto the car. "Go."

* * *

The first hour was all birch and silence, the rails running straight through country so green it seemed embarrassed by it. Theo pumped the handles in a rhythm his body remembered from a decade of this work, and Maren matched him from the opposite side, and the handcar ran the dead line like a pulse through a vein that had been declared empty years ago.

But Theo couldn't stop reading. It wasn't discipline; it was compulsion. Every engineer who works closed lines develops it eventually — the inability to see landscape as scenery. Landscape was always testimony. Every cut and fill and culvert was a sentence someone had written about what this place used to be, and Theo had spent eleven years learning to read sentences the government wanted burned.

Two miles up, the testimony began.

It started with fence posts. They rose out of the ferns on both sides of the embankment in perfect rows, wire long since rusted to red thread between them, and Theo counted the posts because counting was how he kept himself steady. Forty posts to the field. Four fields. Somewhere under the alder scrub that had swallowed them were four farmhouses, and the fences were the only part of those farms anyone had bothered to remember.

"There was a village here," Maren said. Not a question.

"Harrow Bend." Theo eased the handles, letting the car coast. "Population three hundred and forty when the Vell line went through in '61. The river took the low fields every spring, and every spring they rebuilt, and that was the whole arrangement — the town and the river arguing, forever, and neither one winning." He watched a heron lift off from a flooded ditch, unhurried, as though the water below it had always been there. "Then the dam proposals started. Third one got built. Harrow Bend was inside the flood easement."

"You surveyed it," Maren said quietly.

"I walked every property line. I interviewed every landowner. I have — *had* — a file with three hundred and forty names in it, and the compensation each one accepted, and the date each signature dried." His hands kept moving on the handles, steady, mechanical. "Do you know what the average settlement was? Per household? Less than what the state spent on the ribbon for the dam's opening ceremony."

Maren said nothing. The car clicked over a joint. Somewhere off to the left, under forty years of willow, there was a church foundation, and Theo did not point at it, because pointing would mean saying its name, and some names he rationed like water.

"The file still exists?" Maren asked at last.

"Files don't exist. That's the trick of it. A thing gets closed, and then the closure gets closed, and after enough rounds there's nothing left to close." He glanced at her. "You know that better than I do. You work for the people who hold the eraser."

"I work for the people who file the paperwork," she said. "The eraser is above my pay grade."

It was the closest thing to honesty she'd offered, and Theo let it stand, because the alternative was stopping the car in the middle of a drowned county and having the argument he didn't have the breath for.

* * *

At midday they reached the Kiln Road crossing, and the rails ended — not broken, not washed out, simply *ended*, the steel running into a concrete apron poured across the right-of-way like a lid over a box. Beyond the apron, the ballast continued as a pale scar through the trees, walkable but not ridable.

Theo climbed down and crouched at the seam where rail met concrete, and what he saw made his chest go tight in a way he hadn't prepared for.

The apron was new. Not new-new — fifteen years, maybe twenty — but newer than the closure order, which meant it had been poured *after* the line was officially dead. Someone had come out here, to a crossing in the middle of nowhere, and spent public money sealing a road across a railroad that no longer existed on any map.

"Why seal a dead line?" he murmured.

Maren crouched beside him. She was quiet a long moment. "Because dead isn't the same as gone," she said finally. "A closed line can be reopened. Reopened lines bring back records. Records bring back—" She stopped herself.

"Names," Theo said. "Records bring back names."

He stood and shouldered his pack. They walked.

The scar of the old right-of-way led them up along the river's shoulder now, the Vell visible in flashes through the trees — brown-green, patient, carrying the whole drowned watershed on its back. And here the ledger of erasures opened wider with every mile. Theo read it aloud without meaning to, half to himself, half to Maren, because some readings needed a witness or they curdled into madness:

A bridge abutment standing alone in a pasture, its twin gone — the span relocated downstream in '78, the paperwork calling it a "realignment," the truth being that someone's grandfather had crossed that bridge to school every day and the state had decided the memory was worth less than the steel.

A orchard gone wild, apples small and bitter, planted in rows too regular to be accidental — a farm unlisted since before Theo was born, its owner's name surviving nowhere except in the roots of the trees he'd grafted.

A mile marker, cast iron, lying face-down in the grass. Theo turned it over with his boot. *VELL JUNCTION — 12*. There was no Vell Junction. There had been no Vell Junction in forty years. But the marker hadn't been removed, just knocked down, and there was a difference between erased and buried that Theo felt in his teeth.

"They didn't take the towns off the maps," Maren said, watching him turn the marker. "I checked, before we left. The maps just... end. Blank space north of the reservoir. Like the country was never drawn."

"That's worse," Theo said. "Taking a town off a map is an act. You have to decide to do it, and decisions leave fingerprints. Leaving blank space — that's just refusing to look. Anyone who asks is told there's nothing there. And how do you argue with nothing?"

"You can't," Maren said. "That's the design."

They walked on. The afternoon aged around them, light going amber through the birches, and Theo understood, with the particular ache of his profession, that he was walking through the largest blank space in the national archive — a county-sized hole where several thousand lives had been lived and then unwritten. His father had grown up two counties over. His father had worked these lines. His father had vanished into exactly this kind of blankness, and the compass in Theo's pack — the broken compass, the inheritance that wouldn't point anywhere useful — sat against his spine like a question he'd been carrying since he was nineteen.

Every closed line, he thought, is a memory someone paid to forget. The question that had started eating at him at the survey camp came back stronger now: *who paid, and who collected?*

* * *

Near dusk they came over a rise and stopped both at once, without agreeing to.

Below them, in a fold of the valley where a creek met the Vell, lay the bones of a station.

Not a ruin — ruins were honest. This was a station that had been *closed*: platforms swept, lamp posts standing, the building's roofline straight against the darkening sky. Paint peeled from its boards in long curls. The station sign was still mounted above the platform, and even at this distance Theo could read it, because he'd memorized the defunct gazetteer as a young man the way other boys memorized ballplayers' names.

*Alderford.*

Population, once: six hundred. Terminus of the upper Vell logging spur. Closed: the year Theo was seven.

"It's on our route," Maren said, in the tone of a woman confirming a fact rather than proposing a plan.

"It's on no route," Theo said. "That's the point of it."

But they went down anyway, because dusk was coming and the handcar couldn't pass the sealed apron and Alderford's platform was the first flat, dry ground in eight miles. Theo walked the platform edge while Maren checked the building, and his boots rang hollow on boards that had been maintained once and then simply left, and he thought: *someone swept this platform the last day. Someone believed, that morning, that trains would come tomorrow.*

Inside, Maren struck a light and found what stations always held when they died: a timetable framed behind glass, its dates curling; a ticket punch on a nail; a logbook open on the counter to a final entry in a stationmaster's careful hand. Theo leaned over it. The ink had faded to the color of weak tea.

*Last service, 14:40, mixed train to Vell Junction. Track condition fair. Station condition fair. Staff condition—*

The sentence stopped. The rest of the page was blank. As if the man writing it had looked up at something, or heard something, or simply run out of a way to describe what closing a place does to the person who has to close it.

"Staff condition," Theo read aloud. His voice came out strange. "He couldn't finish it."

Maren came to stand beside him. For a moment neither of them spoke, and the river sounded through the walls, low and constant, the way it must have sounded the night the last train pulled out and the lamps went cold.

"My grandmother came from a town like this," Maren said suddenly, as if the words had escaped custody. "Not on the Vell. East. It was closed when she was a girl. She never talked about it except once — she said the worst part wasn't leaving. It was that afterward, whenever she told anyone where she was from, they'd say *where?* And eventually she stopped saying it at all. She let them erase her twice." Maren's jaw tightened. "I filed closure orders for six years before I understood that's what I was doing. Un-saying people."

Theo looked at her. In the lamplight her face was unreadable and completely readable at once — the face of a woman standing on the far side of a line she'd just admitted she'd crossed.

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you're going to keep reading the landscape out loud whether I ask you to or not," she said. "And someone in my position should probably decide, soon, which side of the ledger she's standing on."

Before Theo could answer, the light caught something on the wall behind the counter — a notice, yellowed, pinned under glass like the timetable. Official letterhead. The same format, Theo realized with a slow cold drop in his stomach, as the fresh surrender notice he'd found nailed to the survey post two days ago. Orders for all maps of the Vell River to be handed in. Dated — he leaned closer — *forty-one years ago.*

They'd done this before. Whatever was coming, whatever the missing survey team had stumbled into, whatever his father's compass was trying and failing to point at — the state had swept this valley once already, a generation ago, and here was the receipt.

And beneath the old notice, half-hidden by the frame's shadow, someone had penciled a single line, small and hurried, in a hand that made Theo's breath stop in his throat. He knew that hand. He'd grown up watching it fill the margins of engineering notebooks. He'd know it upside down, underwater, dead.

*If you're reading this, the compass knows the way.*

Theo's fingers rose to the glass. Behind him, Maren went very still.

"That handwriting," she said carefully. "Whose is it?"

Theo didn't answer. Outside, far upstream, faint as a memory, something began to ring — a bell, deep and drowned-sounding, tolling up the darkening valley from a town that had never been built.
