## 5. The Last Station

The handcar died a mile short of the station, which Theo would later decide was the river's first courtesy to them.

It had been groaning for hours, its bearings grinding out a sound like teeth being pulled, and when it finally seized on a stretch of rail half-swallowed by moss, Maren didn't curse. She simply stepped off, shouldered her pack, and looked down the line as if she'd expected this all along.

"Walk's twenty minutes," she said. "There's a station on the old survey charts. Derelict. Nobody's filed a maintenance report on it since before either of us was born."

"Then why is it still standing?"

Maren didn't answer. She had a way of not answering that wasn't evasion so much as triage — deciding which of his questions deserved the dignity of a reply. Theo had catalogued the habit over two days of travel and found it less infuriating than he'd expected. It left room for his own answers, and his own answers were usually worse than whatever she was withholding.

They walked the closed line through country that made him ache in a way he couldn't name. The rails ran along the Vell here, close enough that he could hear it working under the willows — that low, patient sound water makes when it has all the time in the world. On their left, terraces climbed the valley wall where orchards had once been; now the trees grew wild and small-fruited, untended for decades, dropping their bitter harvest into the grass for no one. A fence post leaned at the angle of a man leaning against it after a long day's work, except there was no fence anymore, just the post, remembering.

Theo counted the erasures the way other men counted sheep. A culvert filled with silt. A lane grown shut with blackberry. The foundation of something — a house, a barn, a chapel — reduced to a rectangle of stones so settled into the earth it looked geological, like it had never been built at all but merely surfaced here, and the people who'd lived around it had been a weather event.

"You do that every time we stop," Maren said, without turning around.

"Do what?"

"Read the ground."

"It's what I'm for," Theo said. "The government doesn't pay me to have opinions. It pays me to know what used to be where."

"And what used to be here?"

He looked at the stone rectangle, the wild orchards, the lane gone to bramble. "People," he said. "A lot of them, for a long time. And then a decision got made upstream of their lives, and they were moved or bought or drowned, depending on how much notice they got." He started walking again. "This whole valley is a ledger. Every closed line is an entry. Somebody signed off on each one."

Maren glanced back at him then — a quick, assessing look, the kind she probably thought he didn't catch. He caught all of them. It was another thing he'd learned about her: she watched him the way he watched terrain, looking for what was buried.

* * *

The station appeared the way stations do when they've been forgotten — all at once, out of nothing, as if the land had been keeping it secret even from itself.

It sat on a low shelf above the river: a single-story building of grey timber, roof sagging at one hip but intact, windows hazed with grime except for one, which was clean. That clean window stopped Theo's feet. A derelict building doesn't maintain its own glass. Someone was washing that window, regularly, deliberately, in a valley officially empty of everyone.

A platform stretched along the track side, boards silvered and sprung at the edges. A nameboard stood at its end, and Theo read it twice because the first reading refused to make sense: **VELLBRÜCK HALT**. Not because the name was strange, but because Vellbrück Halt did not exist. He had memorized the closure schedules. He had, in fact, spent three years of his life compiling the master index of decommissioned lines for a ministry that kept losing its own records, and Vellbrück Halt appeared nowhere — not in the closure orders, not in the demolition manifests, not in the property rolls. Forty years of paper, and the station had been subtracted from all of it.

"They unlisted it," he said aloud.

"They unlist a lot of things," Maren said. But her voice had changed — tightened. She was looking at the clean window too.

Smoke rose from the chimney. Thin, domestic, unhurried.

Maren's hand moved toward her coat, and Theo said, quietly, "If you draw on whoever lives here, I'll know everything you told me about wanting to find that team was a lie."

Her hand stilled. "That's not your call."

"No," Theo agreed. "But it's my walk."

They went up the platform steps together, and before Maren could knock, the door opened.

* * *

The man who stood in the doorway was old in the way of trees — bent but rooted, with a face weathered down to its essential grain. He wore a railwayman's coat forty years out of fashion, mended at the elbows with thread that didn't match, and behind him the room glowed lamplit and warm and smelled of woodsmoke and stewed onions and, faintly, of machine oil.

His eyes went past both of them, down the empty track, the way a man checks weather. Then they came back and settled — settled hard — on Theo's chest, where the compass hung outside his jacket on its leather cord. Theo had taken to wearing it there since the camp, since the serial number, since the moment the past had begun following him instead of the other way around.

The old man's breath went out of him slowly, like air from a punctured tire.

"Well," he said. "You're carrying Halvard's work."

Theo's hand closed over the compass before he could think about it. "You know this instrument?"

"I know who made it." The old man stepped back and held the door wide. "Ivo Brandt. Caretaker of a station that isn't here, on a line that isn't either. Come in before the damp gets into your bones, and mind the third board, it lies."

Inside, the room was a museum of small stubbornnesses. A potbellied stove, black and gleaming. Shelves of tools arranged by a hand that loved order: files by size, wrenches hung from nails painted to match. A table set for one, with a second chair that was not dusty — that was used, occasionally, though not tonight. On the wall above the stove hung a framed timetable, yellowed to the color of weak tea, listing departures from Vellbrück Halt to places Theo knew only as entries in his own index: Marrenhof. Steig. Aldis Ferry. All of them gone. All of them running, once, four times daily, on time.

"Sit," Ivo said. "There's soup. It's mostly onion and mostly potato, but it's hot, and hot is most of what food is."

Maren hadn't sat. She stood with her back near the door, and Theo saw her doing the arithmetic she always did — exits, sightlines, whether the old man's hands were steady enough to matter. "We shouldn't impose," she said.

"You're not imposing. You're the first company I've had since spring, and the last company I'll have till thaw, likely." Ivo ladled soup without waiting for permission, as if refusal were a foreign language. "Besides, you'll want to ask me things, and I don't answer questions well standing up. Old knees. Old habits."

They ate. The soup was exactly as advertised, and better than anything Theo had eaten in a week, and he ate it slowly because eating slowly in a warm room felt like a skill he'd lost. Outside, the light failed over the river, and the stove ticked, and the timetable on the wall listed trains that would never run again to towns that no longer existed, and Theo found his eyes kept going back to it the way a tongue goes back to a broken tooth.

"You kept the timetable up," he said finally.

"Somebody has to remember the schedule," Ivo said. "Otherwise the dead are always late."

Maren set down her spoon. Theo heard it: the small, precise click of a woman recalculating. But Ivo only chuckled, low and unbothered, and refilled her bowl without asking.

* * *

Later, when the bowls were cleared and Ivo had poured them each a finger of plum brandy from a bottle with no label, Theo took the compass from around his neck and laid it on the table between them.

"I found it in a survey camp upstream," he said. "Abandoned. Instruments out, meals half-eaten, no tracks leaving. This was in the team leader's kit. It's stamped with their serial number." He turned it over. The brass case caught the lamplight. "It belonged to my father. Or — it came from him. I've carried it since I was nineteen and I never once asked where he got it, because he was gone before asking was possible."

Ivo didn't touch it. He looked at it the way some men look at a grave.

"Halvard Senn made that," he said. "In a workshop above the chandlery in Aldis Ferry, before Aldis Ferry had a reason to be careful about who visited. There were six of them. No — seven. Seven compasses, and every one different, because Halvard didn't believe in making two of anything. He used to say a compass that points where every other compass points isn't telling you anything." The old man smiled, and the smile was mostly grief. "Your father brought it here once. Showed me how it worked. Made me swear on the timetable — on that timetable, right there — that if anyone ever came through carrying it, I'd tell them the truth and give them a bed."

Theo's mouth had gone dry. "My father was here."

"Twice that I saw. More that I heard about." Ivo turned his brandy glass but didn't drink. "Last time was the autumn before he disappeared. He came down the line on foot, same as you, and he stood where you're standing, and he said, 'Ivo, they're going to drown it all, and they're going to say it was the river's idea.'" The old man's voice dropped, flattened, became quotation. "'And when it's done, there won't be a map left that says otherwise. So I'm making maps that aren't maps.'"

"What does that mean?" Maren asked. Her voice was careful, professional, and underneath the care Theo could hear something else — hunger, maybe, or fear.

"I asked him the same thing." Ivo shrugged. "He tapped that compass of his and said, 'It doesn't point north, Ivo. It never has. Point it at what matters and it remembers.' I thought it was a joke. Halvard's instruments, your father's talk — the whole valley was full of clever men saying clever things right up until the water came." He looked up, and his eyes were wet and level. "It wasn't a joke, was it."

Theo said nothing. His hand was on the compass, and under his palm he could feel — or believed he could feel — the faint tremor of the needle inside, restless, insistent, pointing somewhere that had nothing to do with north.

"The survey team," Maren said. "Four engineers, government charter. They passed through here. When?"

"Six weeks ago. Three days, maybe more." Ivo's face closed slightly, the way a door closes — not locked, but no longer open. "They were tired and scared and pretending to be neither. Their leader had a list of questions about the gorge. I answered the ones I liked."

"And the others?" Theo asked. "The ones you didn't like?"

"I told them what I tell you, and I'll only say it once, so listen." Ivo leaned forward, and the lamplight carved deep shadows into his face, and for a moment he looked less like an old caretaker than like something older — a keeper, a warden, a man appointed by nobody to guard something everybody had agreed to forget. "The river doesn't like being followed. Understand me. I don't mean it floods, I don't mean the banks are bad, though both are true. I mean it doesn't *like* it. Things go wrong for people who follow the Vell too far up. Instruments lie. Weather turns against you personally. Men who went up sane come back talking to their own reflections. Your father followed it. The survey team followed it. And every year the water takes a little more of the bank below this station, like it's digging — like it's *looking* for something."

"That's superstition," Maren said.

"Probably," Ivo agreed pleasantly. "Stay anyway. Both of you. Beds are behind that curtain, and the stove holds heat till morning, and I make bread at dawn if the mood takes me." He gathered the bowls, moving toward the basin with the unhurried economy of a man who had done ten thousand evenings alone and intended to do ten thousand more. At the basin he paused, back turned, water running over his hands.

"The leader of that team," he said. "Osei. Petra Osei. She asked me one question I didn't like answering, so I lied to her, and I've been sorry since. She asked if anyone else had come through carrying Halvard's work." He shut off the water. "I said no. You understand? I said no, and two days later they walked up into the gorge, and nobody's walked back down."

The room was very quiet. The stove ticked. On the wall, the timetable held its dead schedule, patient as scripture.

"So when you go up after them," Ivo said, turning around, drying his hands on a cloth gone soft with age, "you'll be the second person I've sent toward the water with a lie on his conscience. I'm too old to make it three." He nodded at the compass on the table, at Theo's hand resting over it. "Whatever that thing of yours is pointing at — and I saw it swinging while we talked, boy, I saw it plain — you might think about listening to it. Halvard's work doesn't point at directions. It points at debts."

Theo lifted the compass. In the lamplight the needle trembled, swung, and steadied — not north, not toward the door, not toward the river they'd followed all day.

Upstream. Into the gorge. Toward whatever the water was looking for.

Behind the curtain, Maren was already unlacing her boots, and Theo heard her pause — a small hitch in the rhythm, the sound of a woman writing tomorrow's report in her head and deleting a line of it before the ink dried.

He slept in a real bed for the first time in a week, under a roof that officially did not exist, in a town erased from every record he had ever indexed, and for the first night in longer than he could remember, he did not dream of moving.

He dreamed of water, rising patiently, looking for something.

And in the morning there was bread, as promised, and Ivo pressed a canvas bag into his hands at the door — bread, salt meat, a coil of good rope — and refused thanks with a wave, and stood on his impossible platform as they walked the dead rails toward the gorge, getting smaller behind them, a lit window in a valley full of erasures.

Theo did not look back until the line curved. When he did, the station was already out of sight, swallowed by the willows, as if the land had taken it back the moment they'd stopped watching.

"He gave us supplies and asked for nothing," Maren said beside him. Her voice was odd — stripped of its official flatness. "Nobody does that."

"He remembered the schedule," Theo said. "Somebody has to keep the dead on time."

Ahead, the valley narrowed. The river ran faster now, louder, and the compass at his chest pulled gently, insistently, like a hand on his sleeve — upstream, always upstream, toward the debt.
