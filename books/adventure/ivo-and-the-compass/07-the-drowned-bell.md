## 7. The Drowned Bell

The river dropped overnight.

Theo knew it before he opened his eyes — knew it by the sound, or rather by the absence of one. For six days the Vell had spoken to him in its sleep, a low continuous muttering against the pilings of the ruined mill where they'd made camp, and now that voice had thinned to a whisper, then to nothing at all. He lay in his bedroll listening to the silence the way another man might listen for footsteps, and when he finally rose and walked down through the willows to the water's edge, he understood.

The Vell was falling away from itself.

It drew back from its banks like an animal retreating into a den, leaving a margin of wet stone and dead silt a full arm's length wide, then two, then more as the morning went on. Maren stood downstream on a shelf of shale with her binoculars raised, watching the same thing he was, and when she lowered them she didn't say anything, which told him she'd been ordered not to explain it and had decided, for whatever reason, to let him see.

By noon the river had given up something it had kept for forty years.

* * *

They saw the bell tower first because it was the only thing tall enough to see itself.

It stood perhaps three hundred meters upstream, where the valley narrowed and the current had always run fastest, and at first Theo took it for a chimney — a squat stone column rising out of the shallows, furred with weed, crowned by a knot of driftwood. But chimneys did not have arched openings near their tops. Chimneys did not lean with the patient dignity of masonry meant to be looked at. And chimneys did not have bells.

This one still did. The bronze hung in its arch on a rotted yoke, green-black with age, tilted maybe ten degrees off true, and when the wind came down the valley it moved — just slightly, just enough — and though no clapper struck it, Theo felt the sound anyway, the way you feel a word someone wrote but never said aloud.

"That's not possible," Maren said. She said it flatly, without drama, the way she reported distances and water levels, and somehow that made it worse.

"The maps say there's nothing here," Theo said. "I've read them all. Every edition since the war. The Vell Valley is blank from the confluence to the headwaters. Not 'uninhabited.' Blank."

"Then what are we looking at?"

He didn't answer. He was already counting. The tower stood in water that, at flood stage, would run four meters deep over this bank — he could read the old high-water line in the stain on the stones, a dark tidemark across the arch like a bruise. Four meters of water, sustained for decades, and the mortar between the blocks was still holding. That wasn't luck. That was engineering. Somebody had built this tower to be drowned and to survive being drowned, and that thought arrived in Theo's mind whole and cold, the way certain thoughts do, and refused to leave.

A town that was built to be flooded. A town that was built knowing.

"We're going in," he said.

* * *

Maren argued, of course. She argued about currents, about hypothermia, about the standing order that survey personnel not enter flooded structures — she recited the regulation number from memory, which told Theo she'd been reading her orders more carefully than she pretended. But she didn't argue hard, and by late afternoon she was standing on the tower's exposed foundation ledge holding a rope tied around his chest under his arms, her face set in the particular expression of a woman doing arithmetic she doesn't like.

"If the water rises," she said, "you come up. Whatever you find, whatever you think it means. You come up."

"If the water rises, I'll be swimming anyway."

"Theo." She never used his name. It landed strangely, almost tenderly, and he understood it was the closest thing to a plea she would permit herself. "You come up."

The water at the tower's base was clear in the way mountain water is clear — a lie of clarity, thirty centimeters of perfect visibility over darkness. The low water had pulled back the river's curtain only partway; the rest of the town, if there was a town, lay below the new surface, visible now as shadows and suggestions. He could make out a street. An actual street, cobbled, running away from the tower into the green murk, lined with the dark shapes of doorways. Rooftops further out, collapsed inward like cupped hands. A town, drowned whole, preserved whole, sitting at the bottom of a river that every official map insisted had never touched anything worth naming.

His father's compass sat in his shirt pocket. He'd taken to carrying it against his skin. It didn't point north — he'd known that for years, had learned to correct for its errant wandering the way you learn to live with a limp — but here, over the drowned street, he noticed for the first time that the needle had gone still. Perfectly still. Steady as a struck nail, pointing straight down the cobbled way into the dark, as if it were looking at something.

Later, he would remember that. Later, it would matter enormously. In that moment he only registered it as one more wrongness in a day made of them, and then he put his face in the water and dove.

* * *

The cold hit him like a hand closing.

Below the first meter the warmth of the afternoon simply ended, and the light changed — gold to green to a blue so deep it was nearly black, lit from above in wavering shafts that bent and broke with the current far overhead. He swam down the outside of the tower, following the rope Maren paid out behind him, and the stones slid past beneath his palms, slick with forty years of river growth, and here and there he found marks cut into the blocks: surveyor's bench-marks, elevation stamps, the neat chiseled numerals of men who had measured this place before they buried it.

At the base of the tower, a doorway. Not a door — the door itself had long since gone, but the frame remained, oak gone black as iron, and above it, carved into the lintel where the water could not wear it away, a name:

**VELLMARK**

He hung in the black-green water reading it, his lungs beginning their slow complaint, and the name went through him like a current. Vellmark. Town at the source of the Vell. Population, per the census of sixty-one years ago: 1,140. He knew the number because he'd once tried to find the census record and found instead a gap — a page removed, a signature line signed by no one, an entire community deleted from the ledger of the country with the tidy efficiency of a line ruled through.

His father had grown up ninety kilometers from here. His father had worked these valleys his whole life. And in twenty years of listening to the man talk — over engine grease, over supper, over the last shared bottle the night before he disappeared — Theo could not recall the name Vellmark spoken once. Not once. You don't forget a drowned town next door. You don't fail to mention eleven hundred neighbors unless you've been asked not to.

His lungs burned. He kicked for the doorway.

* * *

Inside the tower the water was darker and utterly still, a column of held breath. Silt lay thick on everything, soft as ash, and when his boots touched the floor it rose around him in slow clouds that turned his torch-beam to milk. He'd brought a diver's lamp from the camp stores — Maren had produced it without comment, along with the rope, and he was beginning to assemble a private catalogue of the things Maren Kessler carried that a government courier had no earthly reason to carry.

The tower's ground floor was small, circular, and mostly empty. Flooded buildings gave up their contents to the current; anything loose had drifted or dissolved decades ago. What remained was what had been fixed — and fixed things, in a bell tower, meant records. He swept the lamp in a slow circle and found it: an iron cabinet bolted to the wall opposite the door, government pattern, the kind used in rail stations up and down the closed lines. Its door hung open. Its drawers had been emptied.

Except one.

The bottom drawer had jammed — rust had welded it shut sometime in the years after the water came, and whatever clerk or caretaker had been sent to strip the tower before the flooding had either missed it or given up on it. Theo got his knife under the lip of the drawer and worked it, hanging weightless in the cold, the lamp beam jittering with each pull, silt swirling, lungs tightening. The rust resisted, crumbled, resisted. On the third hard wrench the drawer shrieked open with a sound he felt in his teeth, and inside, wrapped in oilcloth that had done its work faithfully, was a book.

He knew immediately what it was. Surveyors know ledgers the way priests know scripture — the format, the binding, the pre-printed columns, the chain-of-custody stamp on the flyleaf. This was a field ledger, the master copy, the book in which every measurement, every boundary, every decision about a place gets written down by the man responsible for writing it down. The oilcloth had kept the pages dry. The ink had kept its blackness. And on the cover, embossed in faded gilt, the title:

**VELLMARK TOWNSHIP — SURVEY OF RECORD**

And beneath it, in the field marked *Surveyor of Record*, a name written in a hand Theo had known since before he could read.

**E. ZHOU**

* * *

He nearly breathed water.

The reflex was pure animal — the body's revolt against what the mind had just taken in — and he mastered it the way you master any fall, by relaxing into it, by letting the shock pass through rather than fighting it. He hung there in the black water with the ledger pressed to his chest and let his heart hammer itself out, and around him the drowned tower held its silence, and somewhere far above, distorted into a soft green shimmer, the surface of the Vell moved like the ceiling of another world.

Elias Zhou. His father. Surveyor of Record for a town that did not exist.

Not a visitor. Not a witness. *Surveyor of Record.* The man whose name the state would hold legally responsible for every line, every boundary, every elevation in Vellmark Township. You don't assign that name lightly. You assign it to the engineer who walked every meter of the ground, who signed every sheet, who stood in front of the commission and answered for the numbers. Which meant his father hadn't stumbled onto this place. His father had *made* this place — on paper, at least. Had drawn the town into official existence with his own hand.

And then the state had drawn it back out.

Theo thought of the compass in his pocket, the broken compass, the heirloom that pointed everywhere except north. His father had carried it on every job. Had taught him to read it at nine years old, kneeling in the gravel of a closed spur line, saying: *the needle knows things the map doesn't, boy; your job is to find out what the map is hiding.* He'd taken it for a joke, a piece of railroad folklore, the kind of thing men say to make mystery out of tedium. Now he turned it over in his mind and felt the ground shift under the memory. A surveyor who suspected his work would be erased might well build himself an instrument that remembered what the archives forgot.

An instrument that pointed, not north, but to something else.

The thought was too large to hold underwater, with his air gone gray at the edges and his fingers numb around the ledger. He kicked upward, through the doorway, past the carved name, up the outside of the tower along the rope, hand over hand, the book sealed inside his jacket against his heart like a second heartbeat, and the light widened around him from blue to green to gold, and then his head broke the surface and the evening air went into him like a blade and he was coughing and alive and Maren was hauling on the rope, hauling him onto the wet stone shelf of the foundation, shouting his name again, that rare and dangerous word.

"You found something." It wasn't a question. She'd seen his face.

He knelt on the stone, dripping, shaking — from the cold, he told himself, only the cold — and unwrapped the ledger from his soaked jacket. The gilt lettering caught the last of the light. Maren read the title, and then the name beneath it, and Theo watched something move behind her eyes that he couldn't name: not surprise. Recognition. As if she had known, in some corridor of herself, exactly whose name would be in that book, and had been waiting days for the confirmation the way you wait for a diagnosis.

"Your father," she said quietly.

"My father surveyed this town." Theo heard his own voice come out level, which surprised him, because inside he was nowhere near level; inside he was a man standing in a room where the floor has turned out to be a painting of a floor. "Officially. By name. And then the state erased the town, and nobody ever mentioned it to me, not once, not in twenty years, not even—" He stopped. Started again. "There were nights he'd sit with his maps after I'd gone to bed and I'd hear him talking to himself. Working something through. I thought it was just the job. They're all like that, surveyors. Married to the lines."

"And now?"

"Now I think he was talking to this." He put his palm flat on the ledger's cover. "To a town he helped draw into the world and then watched them drown. Eleven hundred people, Maren. There were eleven hundred people in Vellmark, and somebody decided the valley needed water more than it needed them, and my father's name is on the paperwork for both halves of that sentence."

The bell above them stirred in the wind. No clapper, no sound — but the tower hummed faintly, a note below hearing, and Theo felt it come up through the stone into his knees.

Maren crouched beside him. When she spoke, her voice had dropped into a register he hadn't heard from her before — lower, unarmored, the voice of the person underneath the agent. "Theo. Listen to me. If your name is in that book, then your name is in other books. Files. The ones that matter are not down here." She glanced upstream, toward the narrowing gorge, where the last light was going red on the water. "They're at the source."

"The source of what?"

"Of everything." She stood, and for a moment she looked out at the drowned street below the surface — the cobbles, the doorways, the rooftops like cupped hands — and her expression was unreadable in the failing light. "We move at first light. Upstream."

Theo stayed kneeling a moment longer. Below him, under four meters of returning river, Elias Zhou's handwriting waited in the dark, page after page of measurements and margins and decisions, and Theo understood that he would not sleep tonight until he had read every word of it — that some part of him had already begun reading, had been reading since the moment he saw the name, and would go on reading for the rest of his life.

The needle of the compass, he noticed, had stopped pointing down the drowned street.

It was pointing upstream.
