## 8. Signals

The tunnel announced itself before they saw it, the way grief does — a change in the air, a coolness on the skin, a sound where there should have been silence.

Theo heard it first as a rhythm beneath the river's murmur: metal on stone, faint and arrhythmic, like someone tapping out a message they'd half forgotten. He raised a hand and Maren stopped beside him on the overgrown railbed, her breath fogging in the gorge's early shadow. Above them the cliffs leaned together, and between them the Vell ran green and quick over stones it had been polishing for a century.

"You hear that?" he asked.

Maren tilted her head. "Water."

"Under the water." He crouched, laid his palm flat against the rail. The steel was cold and carried a vibration too regular to be natural. "Someone's hitting a rail joint. Deliberately. Three taps, pause, two taps."

"Signal code?"

"Old survey code." His mouth was dry. "It means *present, armed, do not approach*. Which means whoever's down there is still alive, still scared, and still following protocol nobody's enforced in forty years."

Maren's hand had drifted to her coat pocket, where she kept the pistol Theo had pretended not to notice since the second day of their journey upstream. "Or it means bait," she said quietly.

"Everything means bait to you."

"Everything *is* bait to me. That's why I'm still alive." But she didn't draw the weapon, and when he started forward along the railbed she followed without argument, which was its own kind of answer.

The tunnel mouth opened in the cliff face like a held breath. Black spruce crowded down to its threshold; decades of needle-fall had buried the rails to their webs, and the timber lintel above the bore was furred with moss so thick it looked upholstered. Someone had dragged brush across the entrance — recently. The broken stems were still pale at the joints, weeping sap.

Theo stood at the edge of the dark and did the thing his father had taught him before he'd taught him anything else about surveying: he waited. Stillness, the old man used to say, is just another instrument. If you hold it long enough, the landscape tells you what it's hiding.

He counted sixty heartbeats. Then seventy.

From inside the tunnel, a voice — cracked, disused, furious with itself for speaking at all: "That's far enough. State your name and function."

"Theo Zhou. Railroad engineer, mapping division." He hesitated, then decided the truth was the only currency that might be accepted here. "Son of Elias Zhou."

The silence that followed had texture. It had weight. Somewhere in the dark, something metallic clattered against stone — dropped, then snatched up again.

"Say it again," the voice said.

"Elias Zhou. My father. He surveyed this valley in—" 

"I know what he surveyed." The voice was closer now, or maybe it had only gathered force. "Show me your hands. Walk forward slowly. And tell your friend to keep her hand out of her pocket or I'll put a spike through it."

Theo glanced sideways. Maren's face was unreadable, but she withdrew her hand from her coat and let it hang empty at her side, and something in the set of her shoulders told him she was cataloguing every word, every angle, every escape route, the way she always did.

They walked into the dark.

* * *

Their eyes adjusted slowly. The tunnel ran straight into the mountain for perhaps three hundred meters, and daylight behind them shrank to a grey coin, then a grey thread. The air smelled of creosote and cold iron and, underneath it, something organic and wrong — the smell of people who had lived too long in one small space.

A lantern flared at the tunnel's midpoint, hooded, its light cut down to a blade. Behind it crouched a figure that resolved by degrees into a woman: gaunt, hollow-cheeked, her hair hacked short with what looked like scissors or possibly a knife, wearing a survey team jacket gone grey with grime. She held a track spike in one fist like a weapon, and she held it competently, which told Theo she'd had practice.

Behind her, deeper in the dark, two more shapes stirred. One of them moaned softly, continuously, like wind through a crack.

"Petra Osei," the woman said. It wasn't an introduction. It was a challenge, as if daring him to make something of the name. "Team lead, hydrographic survey, Vell River expedition. Serial number—" and she recited it, and Theo felt the compass in his breast pocket grow suddenly heavy, because the number matched the stamp on its case exactly.

"You're the team," he said. "You're the missing team."

"We're what's left of it." Petra's eyes moved past him to Maren and narrowed. "Who's she?"

"Maren Kessler. She—"

"She's government." Petra said it flatly, without inflection, the way you'd say *the water's rising*. "I can smell the ministry on her. The boots. The way she stands with her weight even, like she's waiting for orders. Who is she to you?"

"Her department sent the notice," Theo said. "Surrender all maps of the Vell. She conscripted me to find you."

"To find us." Petra laughed, and it was a terrible sound, dry as bone meal. "To find our bodies, you mean. To file us under *resolved*." She shifted her grip on the spike. "How many people did they send with you? How many are on the banks right now?"

"It's just the two of us."

"Nobody sends two people." But doubt had entered her voice, thin as wire, and Theo understood that she wanted to believe him — that three months in a tunnel had made belief a currency she could no longer afford, and that wanting it was its own agony.

"Petra." He kept his voice low and even. "Your serial numbers. They're stamped on my father's compass. The one he carried on the original survey, thirty years ago. Do you understand what that means? Your expedition and his were issued from the same program. Whatever sent you up this river was built on whatever buried him."

The lantern trembled. Not her hand — the whole lantern, hung from a spike driven into a timber support, swinging with some draft from deep in the tunnel. Or maybe it was her hand after all.

"Come and see," she said finally. "Both of you. Slowly. And if either of you reaches for anything, Ansel will stop you." She nodded toward the deepest dark, where a third shape sat propped against the tunnel wall, and Theo saw now that the shape held a length of pipe across its knees and that its breathing was wet and labored.

* * *

Their camp was a museum of interruption.

Instruments lay on a canvas groundsheet with the care of things arranged for an inventory that never happened: a theodolite with its lens cap on, a level, a chain box, notebooks stacked and weighted with stones. Beside them, three tin plates held the fossil remains of meals — rice gone to concrete, the ghost of beans welded to the tin. A fourth plate was absent entirely, and Theo noticed the absence the way you notice a missing tooth.

"How long?" he asked.

"Eleven weeks." Petra fed a handful of dry grass to a tiny stove whose flame barely qualified as fire. "We went dark in the third week. After Halloran."

"Halloran being the fourth member."

Something crossed her face — not quite pain, more like a door closing on a room already full of pain. "Halloran being the fourth member. He found something upstream. Something the survey wasn't supposed to find, because the survey was never a survey." She looked up at Theo, and in the lantern light her eyes were fever-bright and absolutely steady. "Do you know why your government sends engineers to map a river valley it officially doesn't have?"

"To erase it properly," Theo said. "You can't unmake a place until you've documented everything you're unmaking. I've spent six years closing lines. I know how it works."

"Then you know more than we did when we came up here." She pulled one of the notebooks from under its stone and opened it, and her hands shook now, openly, the steadiness having cost her everything it cost. "Three weeks in, we found the drowned town. Low water. Bell tower standing in the middle of the river like a grave marker nobody visits. We dove it. We brought up records." She tapped the notebook. "And the records didn't match our orders. Our orders said *verify structural integrity of impoundment*. Verify. Like the dam was a bridge. Like the town was a load calculation."

Theo sat very still. In his pocket, the compass pressed against his ribs.

"What did the records say?"

"They said the town was surveyed before it was flooded. Formally, legally, with signatures." She turned a page toward him, and there, in faded ink, in a hand Theo recognized with a vertigo that dropped through him like an elevator with no floor, was a name he had known his whole life and never seen in this context: *E. Zhou, resident surveyor.*

"He protested it," Petra said, watching him read. "There's a letter. Four pages. He documented every family, every holding, every grave that would go under. He wrote that the impoundment was unnecessary and that the town could be saved by rerouting the intake two kilometers downstream. The letter was filed. The town was flooded anyway. Six weeks later, your father resigned. Six months after that, he vanished."

"Not vanished," Theo heard himself say. "Erased. That's the word I've been looking for my whole life."

Behind them, near the tunnel mouth, Maren hadn't moved. She stood with her back nearly to the light, and Theo couldn't see her face, only the line of her shoulders, which had gone rigid in a way he'd learned to read as calculation. He thought: *she knew*. He thought: *of course she knew; she was sent because someone feared exactly this — that the son would come looking.* He thought, less certainly: *but she left the notice where I'd find it. She chose me. Why choose me if you want the past to stay dead?*

He filed the thoughts away the way he filed everything, in the ledger-keeping part of his mind that never slept, and turned back to Petra.

"You said Halloran went upstream. To open what?"

Petra closed the notebook. For a moment she seemed to weigh whether to answer, and Theo watched her decide — watched trust get weighed against starvation and eleven weeks of dark and the memory of a friend's empty plate.

"There's a structure at the source," she said. "The old surveys call it an archive. Sealed. Built into the rock above the spring. Halloran became obsessed. He said the documents we recovered were copies — that the originals were up there, that the whole flooding was archived, ordered, signed, provenanced, top to bottom. He said a sealed room full of proof was worse than useless. Proof has to move. Proof has to breathe." Her voice roughened. "We told him to wait. We were low on food; the plan was to walk out, publish what we had. He wouldn't wait. He said if he didn't open it, someone would seal it forever, and everyone in that town would stay a rounding error."

"And you let him go?"

"*Let* him." She laughed again, that awful dry sound. "He left while we slept. Took half the rations and the good rope. Left a note." She reached into her jacket and produced a scrap of paper, soft as cloth from handling, and handed it to Theo.

Four words, in a careful engineer's print:

*I'll make it undeniable.*

Theo read it twice. The lantern swung. From the deep dark, the sick man — Ansel — moaned, and a second voice, younger, hoarse, murmured to him in a language Theo didn't know, soothing, rhythmic, hopeless.

"That's Devi," Petra said, following his glance. "Our tech. Ansel took a fall in the gorge coming back from the bell tower. His lungs filled. There's nothing in a tunnel that fixes lungs." She said it without self-pity, reporting conditions, engineer to engineer. "Which is why I'm going to ask you the question I've been dreading for eleven weeks, Zhou. How did you get here? Handcar? On foot?"

"Handcar, most of the way. It's stashed eight kilometers down the line."

"Eight kilometers." Petra looked at Ansel, at Devi, at the instruments laid out like the skeleton of their abandoned purpose. "Ansel can't walk eight meters. Can your car carry four?"

"It can carry four badly."

"Badly is a unit of measurement I've become very familiar with." She rubbed her face with both hands, and Theo saw how the bones stood out under the skin, how eleven weeks had spent her down to the studs. "Here's what I know, and you can check every word of it against your own experience. The ministry sent us to verify a lie. When we found the truth instead, they stopped answering our signals. When we signaled again, a patrol boat came up the river — not a rescue boat, a patrol boat, with men who shot at us on the bank. We've been in this hole ever since, eating lichen and boiled bark, because going downstream means dying politely and staying here means dying slowly." She fixed him with those fever-bright eyes. "So before I put my people on your handcar, I need to know what she is to you. Because if Kessler's here to tidy us into a report, I will burn this tunnel down around all of us before I let it happen. Tell me you understand."

"I understand."

"Then answer."

Theo looked over his shoulder. Maren stood at the edge of the lantern light, and for once she didn't look like a woman waiting for orders. She looked like a woman listening to her own verdict.

"She conscripted me with a threat," Theo said slowly. "She's lied to her superiors at least once that I know of — reported a village empty that wasn't, bought us a night we needed. She carries a gun she's never pointed at anyone on this river, including me, including days when pointing it would have solved her problems." He exhaled. "I don't know what she is. I know what she's done. Right now that's the only ledger I trust."

Petra studied him for a long moment. Then she looked past him at Maren, and the two women regarded each other across the dying lantern light like opposing counsel who've discovered they're arguing the same case.

"Kessler," Petra said. "Your report. When you find three survivors of the Vell survey. What does it say?"

There was a silence long enough to hold a bullet.

"It says," Maren said at last, "that I haven't written it yet. And that when I do, it will depend entirely on what happens in the next ten minutes."

Petra barked a laugh — real this time, startled out of her. "God. A bureaucrat with a survival instinct. Fine." She pushed herself upright, swaying, gripping a rail joint for balance. "Devi, Ansel — we're moving. Pack the instruments, leave the rest. Zhou—" she tossed him the notebook, the one with his father's name in it, and he caught it against his chest like something alive. "You carry that. You're the only one of us it belongs to."

They were twenty minutes into packing — wrapping the theodolite, breaking down the stove, coaxing Ansel upright between Devi and the tunnel wall while he coughed in wet, tearing spasms — when Devi stopped mid-motion and went rigid, her head cocked toward the tunnel mouth.

"Petra," she whispered. "The signal. Someone's answering it."

Theo froze. From outside, faint but unmistakable, rolling up the rails from downstream, came three taps. A pause. Two taps.

*Present. Armed. Do not approach.*

Petra's face went grey. "Nobody knows that code," she said. "Nobody except the survey program."

Maren had already moved to the tunnel mouth, flattening against the wall, and when she looked back at Theo her expression had shed every ambiguity it had ever carried.

"Patrol boat was four days ago," she said quietly. "Downstream. Which means whatever's signaling us didn't come up the river." Her eyes met his. "It came down the line. The same way we did. Someone's been following us since the last station."

Outside, the taps came again — patient, professional, unhurried.

Three. Pause. Two.
