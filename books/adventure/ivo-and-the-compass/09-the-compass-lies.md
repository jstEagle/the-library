## 9. The Compass Lies

The tunnel smelled of creosote and old water, of iron left too long in the dark. Theo sat with his back against the cold curve of the wall, the compass open on his knee, and watched the needle do what it had been doing for three days now: nothing sensible.

Outside, the Vell ran loud through the gorge, a sound like a crowd murmuring behind a wall. Inside, the three survivors of the Halloway survey team had given him a corner of their camp — a dry stretch near the old signal alcove where someone had once bolted a lamp bracket to the rock. Petra Osei sat across from him, sharpening a knife against a stone with slow, patient strokes, watching him watch the instrument.

"You've been staring at that thing since sundown," she said.

"It's broken."

"Is it."

The needle didn't tremble. That was the wrong part. A broken compass wandered, hesitated, sulked. This one was certain. It held its bearing with the confidence of a machine that knew exactly what it was doing, and what it was doing was pointing south-southwest — upriver, into the gorge wall itself, at a slope of solid granite that no magnetic pole on earth could explain.

Theo turned it ninety degrees. The needle swung smoothly, obediently, back to south-southwest. He turned it again. Again it returned, settling like a bird coming home to roost.

His father's compass. Brass case worn silver at the edges from forty years of a thumb worrying it. The maker's mark Ivo Brandt had named without hesitation — *Aldous Rehn, Basel* — stamped small beneath the hinge. And stamped beside it, in characters so faint Theo had needed firelight and squinting to find them: a serial number. The same serial number he'd photographed on the survey team's equipment manifest, on the crate stencils, on the sealed instrument cases laid out neat as coffins in an abandoned camp two hundred miles downstream.

His father's compass had been government property. His father had been carrying government property when he vanished, and nobody had ever come looking for it, because nobody had ever admitted it existed.

"I need light," Theo said. "Real light. Not this."

Petra nodded toward the back of the tunnel. "There's a maintenance niche past the third crosscut. We rigged a lantern there when we first came in. You'll want to be careful what you take apart in front of me, Zhou. I've spent six weeks deciding whether to trust you, and I'm not finished."

He almost smiled. Six weeks starving in a tunnel would make anyone careful about strangers; it had made Petra careful the way a blade is careful, all edge and intention. She was a compact woman gone hollow-cheeked with rationing, her engineer's hands still precise, still clean. She did everything precisely. It was how she stayed alive.

The maintenance niche was barely wider than a coffin. Theo wedged himself in with the lantern hooked above him and opened the compass fully for the first time — not just the lid, but the whole instrument. There was a second hinge along the base, hidden under a lip of brass so fine he'd taken it for wear. His thumbnail found it. Pressed. The bottom plate dropped open like a jaw.

Inside, where a cheap compass would have been hollow, there was mechanism.

Not much of it. A ring of fine gearing no larger than a pocket watch, a cam cut into a disc of hard rubber, and a second needle — a sliver of magnetized steel mounted on jeweled bearings, independent of the first. Someone had built a lie into the instrument deliberately, carefully, with love or with malice, and Theo couldn't yet tell which. The outer needle, the one every eye would check, pointed wherever the earth told it to point. The inner needle answered to something else entirely. And the visible needle — the one he'd trusted across four hundred miles of closed country — was geared to follow the hidden one.

It wasn't a compass that lied. It was a compass that had been made to tell one truth and only one.

He thought of every night he'd slept by it. Every morning he'd checked it out of habit, the way other men crossed themselves. North, always north, give or take the honest wobble of any magnetized needle — and he'd never once questioned it, because it agreed with the sun, with the rails, with the world. But now, in this niche, with the mechanism exposed, he understood: the agreement had been engineered. Somewhere in the gearing was a correction factor, a fixed offset, and the offset had been set so that the false bearing and the true bearing would read identically at some particular place on earth. Wherever his father had calibrated it. Everywhere else, it drifted — so slightly no man walking a line would notice, so certainly that over distance it became a road.

A compass that pointed home. Not north. Home.

And home, for this instrument, was south-southwest. Upstream. Into the gorge wall.

"The archive," Theo said aloud, to no one, and the word came out of him like something he'd swallowed weeks ago finally rising.

Behind him, in the dark of the tunnel, Petra Osei stopped sharpening her knife.

* * *

She made him bring it out into the open. She wouldn't touch it herself — he noticed that, noted it the way he noted everything now, the way a man who has learned the ground can swallow whole towns learns to note things — but she looked at it for a long time, turning it with a pencil she'd whittled to a point, never letting her skin meet the brass.

"Aldous Rehn," she said. "You know who that is?"

"Ivo Brandt says he was the best instrument-maker in the country. Retired thirty years ago."

"He didn't retire." Petra set the pencil down. "He was retired. There's a difference, and the difference is that men who retire get to keep their tools. Rehn worked for the Bureau of Hydrographic Survey before there was a Bureau of Hydrographic Survey. Back when it was called something else and did things it doesn't put in its charter." She looked up at him, and in the lantern light her eyes were flat and tired and absolutely clear. "Your father commissioned that compass from him. We found the correspondence in the team's records before we burned half of it. Eleven letters. Your father paid for the mechanism himself — his own money, which is why it never appeared on any inventory. The Bureau thinks your father carried a standard field compass. The Bureau is wrong."

"Why?" Theo heard his own voice go quiet, the way it went quiet over bad ground. "Why build a key into a compass?"

"Because a key you can see gets confiscated. A key that looks like a tool gets carried everywhere." She drew a breath. "Zhou, I'm going to tell you what our orders were, and then you're going to want to hit something, and I'd ask you to do it on the far side of the tunnel, because we don't have food to spare for stupid injuries."

* * *

What she told him took less than ten minutes. It took him most of the night to stop hearing it.

The Halloway survey team — four engineers, full instrumentation, a mandate signed at the deputy-minister level — had never been sent to map anything. Their brief, the one Petra had memorized and then destroyed page by page, was a verification survey. The town at the head of the gorge — the drowned town, the town whose bell tower had risen out of the low water like an accusation, the town that appeared in no census, on no map, in no ledger the state would acknowledge — was supposed to stay dead. And every eleven years, the Bureau sent a team upstream to confirm it.

To confirm that the flooding had held. That the site was unrecoverable. That the records were unrecoverable. That the depth of the water and the remoteness of the gorge and the legal fiction of the town's nonexistence were, together, sufficient to keep whatever lay in the archive chamber beyond the reach of anyone who might go looking.

"We weren't surveyors," Petra said. "We were auditors of a grave. We came to certify that the murder was still concealed." Her voice didn't shake. Theo suspected it had done all its shaking months ago, alone, in the dark, and had come out the other side into this flatness. "And when we got here — when we found the source, and the keeper, and the chamber — Halvorsen read the original brief to the end. All of it. There was a final section. Contingency language." She paused. "If verification failed — if the archive was found intact, if the keeper was alive, if anything suggested the site could be recovered — the team's standing orders were to correct the failure. Permanently. The chamber, the records, the keeper. Us, if we objected."

"And you objected."

"Halvorsen went upstream instead. To warn them, or to destroy it himself — he never said which, and I've stopped guessing. The rest of us ran." She gestured at the tunnel, the dark, the murmur of the river beyond it. "This is objecting. This is what objecting looks like after six weeks."

Theo sat with the compass in both hands and felt the weight of it change. It weighed the same. It weighed exactly what it had weighed in his father's coat pocket, in his own rucksack, through every town he'd never slept twice in. But it was heavier. It was the weight of a man who had known — who had *known*, decades ago, sitting in some office or some kitchen, reading a brief or refusing to sign one — that the state had drowned a town full of people and meant to keep it drowned, and who had responded not with a shout but with a piece of precision engineering. With gears. With a hidden needle. With a secret sewn into the lining of an ordinary life and handed, casually, to a son who liked maps.

*He never told me.* The thought arrived and Theo let it pass through without arguing, because of course he hadn't told him. Telling was death. Telling was what had gotten the survey team starved into a tunnel. His father had built the message into the instrument instead, the way engineers built messages into everything — into bridge loadings and rail gauges and the spacing of ties — trusting that someday, someone with the right eyes would read it.

But it meant his father had known. Had known about the town, the flood, the archive, the killing — and had chosen to carry the knowledge silently for years rather than spend it. Had chosen survival, and secrecy, and a son raised on restlessness instead of truth.

Or had chosen the only move available on a board where every other square was mined.

"Do you know what happened to him?" Theo asked. "My father. Really."

Petra was silent long enough that the lantern hissed twice.

"No," she said finally. "But I know what the file said, because Maren Kessler quoted it to us the day she conscripted you — she thought it would buy your cooperation, and she was right, and I've disliked her ever since for the efficiency of it. The file says your father resigned his commission and disappeared voluntarily. Off the record, in the corridor, the night before we went upstream, our team lead told me the same file used to say something different. It used to say he died. Then it changed. Files that change are files someone is maintaining, Zhou. Somebody upstairs has kept your father's story current for twenty-five years. Ask yourself why a dead man needs ongoing clerical work."

* * *

He walked out onto the ledge above the tunnel mouth while the others slept, because the walls had started to feel like a throat.

Dawn came gray up the gorge, the river running high and green below him, and somewhere south-southwest — behind that wall of rock, under that water, at the end of a bearing his father had machined into brass — the archive waited, and the keeper waited, and whatever remained of the truth waited with them.

Theo opened the compass one more time. The needle held its line, patient as gravity. Pointing home. Pointing at the thing his father had wanted found by exactly one person, in exactly one lifetime, and had trusted a jeweled bearing and a cam of hard rubber to accomplish what no letter, no confession, no dying word ever could.

"They'll have another team coming," Petra said behind him. She'd followed him out without sound; he was losing his touch, or she was better than he'd credited. "Verification failed. The Bureau will know within the month — Kessler's reports will see to that, whatever she's writing in them. When they come, they won't send auditors. They'll send men whose contingency language is the whole document." She stood beside him, arms folded against the cold, and looked at the compass rather than the river. "So the question isn't whether you're going upstream, Zhou. I can see that you are; it's written all over you, same as it was written all over Halvorsen. The question is what you're going to do when you find out your father's last correspondent is still alive, and what she'll do when she sees that compass in your hand."

"She'll know what it means."

"Yes. And so will whoever's following us." Petra turned to go back into the dark, then stopped. "One more thing. The fourth member of my team — the one who went upstream. Halvorsen. Before he left, he asked me for the serial number off our instrument manifests. All of them. I gave them to him because he was my lead and I was frightened and it seemed like bookkeeping." Her jaw tightened. "Yesterday I remembered why a man running to warn the enemy of his own government would want a list of serial numbers. He wasn't checking inventory, Zhou. He was checking whether your father's compass was still in circulation. Whether the key was still loose in the world."

The wind came down the gorge and the river answered it, and Theo stood on the ledge holding the one object in the country that two governments' worth of men might already be hunting — and understood, with the calm of a man reading a grade marker before a collapsed bridge, that they were no longer following the river at all.

The river, and everyone on it, was following the compass.
