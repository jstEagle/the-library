## 10. Maren's Report

The cipher machine weighed more than it should have. Maren Kessler had carried field units before — the standard-issue K-4 with its brass rotors and its smell of hot bakelite — but tonight, in the derelict signal shed two miles downstream of the tunnel where three living people were pretending to be dead, the thing felt like a stone she was being asked to swallow.

She had set up on a workbench still scarred by solder burns, under a window boarded from the inside so that only threads of moonlight came through. The handcar sat outside in the weeds. Theo was asleep against its frame, or pretending to be; he had not slept properly since the tunnel, and neither had she. Petra Osei's people were keeping their distance, watching her the way one watches a dog that might be trained to bite.

Maren opened the report folder and read her own orders for the fourth time.

*Recover all records pertaining to Survey 7-Vell. Confirm status of personnel. Allow no witnesses upstream of Marker 61.*

The words hadn't changed. What had changed was everything around them — the half-eaten meals at the abandoned camp, the ledger in Theo's wet hands, the way Petra Osei had looked at Maren across the tunnel dark and said, *You're the cleanup crew, aren't you?* Not an accusation. A diagnosis.

She took out her notebook and began to draft.

---

The first draft was true. That was the trouble with drafts; they always started honest.

*Survey 7-Vell located alive at kilometer 44 tunnel. Three survivors: Osei, Brandt-von Leyen, Achterberg. Fourth member (Voss) proceeded upstream alone. Records partially recovered. Recommend extraction and debrief.*

She looked at that last line for a long time. *Recommend extraction.* She knew what extraction meant when the directive said *allow no witnesses.* It meant a second team arriving on the night line with no lights, and three starving engineers marched to the edge of the gorge, and a report filed afterward with the word *resolved* in it. She had seen resolved reports before. She had never asked what they resolved, because asking was how careers ended and how people ended up assigned to places like the Vell River in the first place.

Except now she had asked. Now she had watched Theo Zhou hold his father's compass like it was a wound he'd been carrying since childhood without knowing the shape of it. Now she had stood in a drowned town's bell tower shadow while he read a name off a water-swollen ledger — *Zhou, E., surveyor* — and heard him say, very quietly, "He wasn't supposed to be here. Nobody was supposed to be here."

Her handler, Director Ilse Varga, had once told her over brandy in a warm office: *We don't erase people, Kessler. We erase paperwork. People do the rest themselves.* It had sounded almost gentle at the time. Almost reasonable. Everything Varga said sounded reasonable if you drank enough and didn't look downstream.

Maren fed the first draft into the stove and watched it curl.

---

The second draft took an hour, and it was the hardest writing she had ever done, harder than any coded dispatch, because it required her to kill three people she had spoken to that evening.

She wrote slowly, in plain pencil first, transcribing to cipher after:

*Report follows. Survey 7-Vell confirmed deceased. Remains located in flood debris at gorge narrows, consistent with spring surge event. Recovery compromised by terrain; identification by effects and documents. Records recovered: field books (partial), instrument log, personal effects of four members. No evidence of unauthorized disclosure. Fourth member Voss unaccounted for; presumed taken by river. Recommend area be closed pending seasonal survey. Team Kessler out.*

Three lies per sentence, she thought, feeding the tape into the machine. Osei alive. The records scattered and hidden, not recovered. The river blamed for what the state would otherwise do itself. She turned the rotors and felt each letter go through her hands like something being buried.

There was a craft to lying well, and she had been trained in it, but training assumed you lied to enemies. This was different. This was lying to the machinery that paid her wages, sheltered her, gave her purpose after the war had chewed up every other purpose she'd tried. Lying to it felt less like deception and more like amputation — clean, deliberate, and hers.

She keyed the transmission in bursts, as protocol demanded, and when the last rotor clicked home she sat in the dark listening to the river. It was louder here than at the camp. Or maybe she was just hearing it properly for the first time.

*Allow no witnesses upstream.* She had not disobeyed, technically. She had reported witnesses who no longer existed. Somewhere between those two sentences was the person she used to be, and she could not find the exact spot where she'd stepped over the line — only that the ground on this side felt different under her boots.

---

Theo found her at dawn.

She heard him coming — he moved like a surveyor, always testing the ground before trusting it — and she had time to close the cipher case and put her hands flat on the workbench and arrange her face into something neutral. When he ducked through the shed door, carrying two tin cups of Ivo Brandt's terrible chicory, she saw that he hadn't slept either. His eyes had the particular redness of a man who has spent the night reading instead.

"You sent something," he said. Not a question.

"I filed my report." She took the cup because refusing it would have been a confession. "Standard procedure."

"Standard." He sat on the bench opposite her, unhurried, and looked at the cipher machine the way he'd looked at the flooded farms along the closed line — reading it, cataloguing it, filing it somewhere behind his eyes. "Petra says your kind doesn't file standard anything. Petra says your kind files people."

"Your friend Petra has a talent for summary."

"She also says you could have called us in already. You've had two days and a working radio link." He blew on the chicory. Steam curled between them. "So I'm going to ask you plainly, Kessler, because my father taught me that rivers and governments both get more dangerous the longer you let them meander. What did you tell them?"

Maren held his gaze. She was good at holding gazes; it was half her profession. But there was something in his face that made the professional version feel obscene — not fear, exactly, and not anger. He simply wanted to know. He had spent his whole life wanting to know things, and everyone above him in the chain of command had spent their whole lives making sure he couldn't.

"I told them you were dead," she said. "All of you. Flood debris, spring surge, remains unrecoverable. I told them the records were in my possession and the area should stay closed."

The silence went on long enough that she counted her own heartbeats. Seven. Eight.

"Why?" he said finally. Not *thank you*. Not *you're lying*. Just *why*, in the flat tone of a man checking a bridge rating before he drives a train across it.

Because I watched you dive into cold black water for a ledger with your father's name in it. Because Petra Osei shared her last real food with me without being asked. Because I have spent eleven years believing the erasures were administrative accidents, and I can't believe that anymore. Because someone has to be the first witness who refuses.

"Because the order was to allow no witnesses," she said, "and I've decided I'm not the kind of officer who interprets that order correctly."

Theo studied her for a moment longer, then drank his chicory. "That's the most honest sentence anyone in your organization has ever said to me."

"It's the only one I've got left. Spend it carefully."

---

The reply came at midday, and it came wrong.

She knew before she decoded it. Priority traffic to a field unit arrived in neat blocks, routine and dry. This transmission arrived as a single dense burst, flagged twice, which meant Varga had written it herself rather than delegating to a clerk. Maren carried the tape down to the shed, checked the boards over the window out of habit, and ran the decrypt with her hands steadier than she felt.

*Received and noted. Discrepancy: recovery of full records inconsistent with reported condition of remains. Clarify inventory. Further: cross-reference initiated on auxiliary instruments associated with Survey 7-Vell. Be advised — historical precedent exists. File excerpt follows.*

Then, in the flat institutional voice of a document that had killed someone just by existing:

*Subject: ZHOU, EDMUND. Engineer, Grade II. Attached Survey 7-Vell, original commission. Subject authored dissenting memorandum re: reservoir proposal, classified Annex D. Subject did not resign, defect, or disappear. Subject was administratively removed, 19 March, forty-one years past. Removal authorized at Deputy Directorate level. All subsequent references to subject expunged from civil registries. Note for current operations: subject's next of kin (son, T. Zhou) employed in adjacent directorate. Flag: proximity risk. Recommend assessment.*

Maren read it three times.

The shed was cool and smelled of creosote and old oil, and outside a wood pigeon was making its idiot two-note call, and none of it seemed real. *Administratively removed.* She knew the phrase. Everyone in the service knew the phrase, the way farmers know certain weather signs — you hoped never to see it applied to anyone you'd met. It meant a man had been walked into a room and out of history. It meant his son had grown up telling people his father vanished, because *vanished* was the only word left in the language after the state finished its editing.

And Varga had sent it to her deliberately. That was the part that made her hands go cold. The Director didn't quote files for education. She quoted them as messages, and the message here was legible even through the cipher: *I know who is traveling with you. I know why he matters. Choose accordingly.*

It was a leash and a threat braided together. Report honestly about the Zhou boy, or become a discrepancy yourself.

Maren thought about Edmund Zhou, forty-one years dead, whose only surviving crime was a memorandum. She thought about the compass stamped with a survey serial number — the son carrying the father's instrument into the same valley, following the same river, pulled by the same current the state had dammed with paper. She thought about her own file, wherever it lived, and how thin the margin was between *officer* and *administratively removed*, and how Varga had just shown her the margin on purpose.

Then she did something she had never done in eleven years of service. She took out her notebook and wrote, in her own hand, not for transmission: *They erased him. They are going to try to erase the boy too. Not while I'm drawing breath.*

It wasn't doctrine. It wasn't loyalty to the crown or the directorate or the flag on her commission papers. It was smaller than that and larger — loyalty to a specific tired man drinking bad chicory, to a specific stubborn engineer hoarding canned peaches in a rail tunnel, to the dead man whose name was finally going to be said out loud in daylight.

Her institution had just become the enemy. The transfer happened quietly, somewhere between the third reading of the file excerpt and the pigeon's call, and afterward she could not remember which side of it she'd stood on that morning. It hardly mattered. Rivers change course the same way — not all at once, but one flood season, one collapsed bank, one night of high water — and then the old channel is just a dry memory in the landscape.

---

She burned the file excerpt. Then, after a moment, she burned her original orders too, the ones with *allow no witnesses upstream* in Varga's own dictated phrasing. If the second team came and found her, let them find nothing to prove she'd ever known the terms.

In the afternoon she climbed to the tunnel mouth. Petra Osei was on watch, sitting on a fallen timber with a rifle across her knees she must have scavenged from the camp, and she watched Maren approach with the flat patience of a woman who had decided not to trust her and saw no reason to revise.

"Theo tells me you killed us on paper," Petra said. "Drowned us in the narrows. Efficient. My mother gets a letter eventually?"

"No letters. Your names go into a closed annex until this is over."

"Then we're ghosts."

"You're ghosts with copies," Maren said. "Which is more than the state usually allows." She crouched so they were level, and made herself say the rest of it, because these people deserved the arithmetic. "My reply came back this morning. Varga isn't satisfied. She wants a full inventory, and she's cross-referencing the compass. And she sent me Edmund Zhou's file — the real one. He didn't vanish. He was removed. Authorized at deputy-directorate level, forty-one years ago, for writing a memo against the reservoir."

Petra was quiet for a long moment. Down in the tunnel, faintly, someone was laughing at something — the small, rationed laughter of people who have decided to keep living anyway.

"So the boy is walking his father's river," Petra said at last, "carrying his father's key, toward the thing his father died objecting to. And the office that killed him knows the son is here." She spat neatly off the timber. "How long do we have?"

"A second team is already being assembled. Days, not weeks. Maybe less, once Varga decides my report smells wrong."

"And what will you do when she asks you again?"

Maren looked upstream, toward the gorge, toward the source, toward whatever sealed room held a protest letter and forty-one years of other people's memos. The river ran low and silver in the afternoon light, patient as a filing system.

"I'm going to lie to her again," she said. "Better this time. And then I'm going upstream with the rest of you, and when the second team arrives, they'll find a dead survey, a missing officer, and no witnesses — because everyone worth protecting will be beyond Marker 61."

Petra studied her the way she'd study a bridge: load-bearing members, signs of stress, whether it would hold under weight. Whatever she saw, she nodded once and stood, slinging the rifle.

"Then you'd better learn to shoot," she said. "Ghosts travel armed."

Behind them, deep in the tunnel, the laughing stopped, and Theo's voice called out — sharp, urgent, the voice of a man holding something up to the light. Maren turned toward the sound, and the river behind her kept running, low and quiet, carrying everything downstream toward strangers who didn't yet know what was coming to them.
