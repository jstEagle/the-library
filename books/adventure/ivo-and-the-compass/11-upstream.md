## 11. Upstream

The gorge began where the maps ended, which Theo had learned to read as a kind of sentence.

Not a blank — the state never left blanks. Blanks invited questions. What they left instead was a lie: a neat blue thread for the Vell River running through terrain marked *unproductive*, *unstable*, *no value*. He'd traced that thread on Maren's field copy the night before, holding it near the fire while Petra slept with her boots on and Okafor kept watch with a rifle across his knees. The river in the drawing ran straight. Rivers did not run straight through gorges. Someone had drawn it from a desk, from memory, or from an order.

"They didn't survey this," he said. "They surveyed around it."

Maren looked up from cleaning her sidearm. "That's what we're for now."

"That's what we're for," Theo agreed, and folded the map away, because there was no version of this conversation that ended anywhere good.

They broke camp before first light. Four of them now — himself, Maren, Petra Osei, and Anselm Okafor, who had spent eleven days starving in a rail tunnel and still walked like a man with somewhere to be. The fourth member of the original team was somewhere ahead of them, upstream, carrying whatever he carried toward whatever he meant to open or destroy. Behind them, Theo knew without turning around, was the second team.

He'd seen their sign at dawn yesterday: boot prints along the old towpath, fresh enough that water hadn't yet pooled in the heels. Six men, moving light, moving fast. Government boots were identifiable by their soles — issued tread, replaced on schedule, worn evenly because government men walked on pavement more than stone. These prints were two days old at most.

"They're not trying to catch us," Petra said when he showed her. She crouched over the tracks, her face thin and brown as a nut. "They're herding. Watch — they'll cut the high trail by noon."

She'd been right. By noon the high trail was gone, collapsed into the river in a rockfall that might have been natural and might not have been, and the four of them dropped to the waterline with the gorge walls rising on either side like the inside of a throat.

* * *

The Vell in the gorge was not the polite river of the farmlands downstream. It moved fast and green-black over shelves of slate, loud enough that conversation happened in fragments, shouted sideways. The walls narrowed and widened without rhythm, opening into scoured amphitheaters where floodwater had torn out everything living, then pinching into slots where the four of them walked in single file with the current sucking at their ankles.

Theo found himself reading the walls the way he read all closed lines — as a ledger.

There had been a road here once. You could see it in the cuts: a shelf carved into the north face, wide enough for a cart, running level above the flood line for half a mile before it simply stopped, truncated by a newer shelf that had been dynamited off its own footing. Two roads, one gorge, and neither permitted anymore. Below the road cuts, half-drowned in the shallows, lay the stumps of a bridge — iron bollards, mortared footings, the ghost of a crossing that connected to nothing on either side.

"Forty years," Petra said, following his gaze. "Maybe fifty. My grandfather talked about a valley road. Said you could drive a wagon from Hessler's Ford to the source country in a day." She said it flatly, but her jaw was tight. "Nobody drove it. Nobody could tell you why."

"Because nobody's left who remembers wanting to," Theo said.

It was the kind of thing he thought constantly and said rarely, and he regretted it immediately, because Maren heard it. She was walking behind him — she'd insisted on rear guard, and none of them trusted her enough yet to argue — and he felt her silence change quality, the way a room changes when someone stops pretending not to listen.

"I'm not apologizing," he said, not turning around.

"I didn't ask you to."

"You drew some of those erasures."

"I filed some of those erasures," Maren said. "There's a difference. Filing is paperwork. Drawing means you decided." A pause, filled by the river. "I've been trying to work out which one I am since the tunnel."

Ahead of them, Okafor laughed — a short, cracked sound, the laugh of a man whose humor had been rationed for eleven days. "Welcome to the survey," he called back. "Everyone finds out what they are upstream. That's the whole trip."

The slot canyon swallowed them again, and the talk was over.

* * *

They made the crossing at dusk on the second day, and that was where Ivo Brandt found them.

Theo saw him before anyone else — a shape standing on the far bank where the gorge opened into a bowl of grey scree, beside a mule that stood with the patience of an animal that had done this many times. The old man was dressed the same as at the station: heavy coat, canvas hat, hands folded on the strap of a pack like a man waiting for a train that had stopped coming decades ago. Except no train came here. No train had come here in forty years, and yet Ivo stood at the ruined crossing as if he'd checked the timetable that morning.

"He can't be here," Maren said. "This is three days' walk from the station."

"It's one day if you know the paths," Petra said slowly. "And he would."

They waded the crossing single file, cold to the waist, and came up dripping onto the scree, and Ivo watched them come with eyes that went from face to face and settled last on the compass case at Theo's belt.

"You're being followed," Ivo said. No greeting. His voice was gravel over the river's noise. "Six men. They passed the ford below you this morning. They have dogs."

"Dogs," Okafor repeated, and spat.

"The handler walks them on the towpath so the scent stays near the water. They'll lose you at the slate shelves unless you cross the river twice more tonight." Ivo said it the way he'd called the compass by its maker's name at the station: plainly, as fact, requiring nothing. Then he shrugged the pack off the mule and began unbuckling straps. "Rice. Salt pork. Dried apples. Cartridges for the rifle — I don't know the caliber, I brought three kinds. There's a stove flask. Drink it before it goes cold."

They ate like animals and pretended not to. Theo sat close enough to the old man to share the flask, and the liquor in it was harsh and clean and burned a channel of warmth down through him that the fire couldn't reach.

"Why are you here?" Theo asked.

Ivo took his time answering. He poured a measure of the liquor into the cap, held it, drank half.

"When your father came through," he said, "he asked me the same thing about a different man. Why does anyone stand at a crossing? Because somebody has to be there when the ones who matter come through." He handed the cap back. "You're going to the source."

It wasn't a question. Theo didn't insult him by making it one.

"Then hear me." Ivo set the flask down and leaned forward, and in the last grey light his face was all planes and shadow, older than Theo had ever allowed himself to see. "The keeper is alive."

Petra's head came up sharply. "That's impossible. The fourth expedition—"

"The fourth expedition found an empty house and reported an empty house, because the house was empty. She wasn't in it." Ivo's mouth did something that was almost a smile. "She hasn't been in it for thirty years. She moves. She has paths the way I have paths, only better, because hers were made by people who needed them to be invisible. Your man—" he nodded upstream, toward the fourth member, toward whatever waited at the river's beginning, "—is walking to a place where nobody lives, looking for a woman who isn't there. And when he doesn't find her, he will do something desperate. Desperate men with explosives do not search patiently."

"Explosives," Maren said quietly. "You know that for certain."

"I know the second team carries demolition charges, because I sold the mule that hauled them to the depot at Kessel's Landing, and I count my mules." Ivo picked up the flask, screwed it shut, and stood with the unhurried economy of a man who had never once wasted a motion in his life. "So. The keeper is alive, and she is the only person alive who can open what your father sealed, and the men behind you intend to end both facts permanently. That is why I am here."

He turned to the mule and began re-saddling it, and the conversation, as far as Ivo Brandt was concerned, was over.

* * *

They crossed the river twice that night, exactly as Ivo had told them, and lost the dogs in the black water and the slate. On the far side of the second crossing there was a path Ivo had described — a goat track stitched into the south wall, invisible until you were standing on it, climbing steadily away from the river into pine and shadow. From its height, near midnight, Theo looked back down the gorge.

Far below, tiny in the dark, lanterns moved along the towpath. Six points of light, patient, methodical, sweeping the banks where the four of them had walked that morning. As he watched, the lights stopped, clustered, and one rose higher than the rest — a man climbing, checking, descending. Then the cluster moved on, upstream, following the river the way Theo had followed it, the way everyone in this story followed it, because the river was the only road the state had left open and the state had left it open on purpose.

"They're not chasing us," he said to Maren, who had climbed up beside him without a sound. "They're following the same map we are. We're just faster."

"For now."

"For now." He watched the lanterns another moment. "Maren. When they catch us — and they will catch us, or corner us, or blow the gorge with us in it — what happens to you?"

She was quiet long enough that the river filled the silence twice over.

"I don't know," she said finally. "That's the honest answer. Three weeks ago I'd have told you exactly what happens to me: I complete the assignment, I file the report, I go home. Now—" She shook her head, and he saw her hand move briefly to her ribs, where she still carried something flat and wrapped in oilcloth. Her reports. Or what used to be her reports. "Now I'm carrying documents I should have destroyed and lying to a handler who signs his orders with the same stamp that erased your father. Whatever happens to me, I chose it. That's more than the town got."

Below them the lanterns went out one by one, doused for the night march, and the gorge went dark as a closed drawer.

* * *

They climbed through the remaining dark and came out at dawn onto a high bench of ground where the pines stood apart and the air smelled of snow that hadn't fallen yet. The river was visible far below now, smaller, faster, whiter — working harder the closer it came to its beginning. Petra spread Ivo's provisions on a flat stone and divided them with a surveyor's precision, and Okafor slept the dreamless sleep of the starved catching up, and Theo took out the compass.

He'd stopped pretending it pointed north days ago. In the open air of the bench, with the whole gorge laid out below, he let the needle speak. It swung, steadied, and fixed on a bearing slightly west of upstream — into the high country where the maps gave up even pretending, where the source was, where the archive was, where his father had built a key out of brass and glass and grief and hidden it in plain sight in his son's inheritance.

His father had known. All those years, sitting in that workshop with the boy who would grow up to map dead rail lines, winding the compass, oiling it, teaching him to trust it — he had known that someday the needle would be needed, and that the only safe place to keep a key was in the hands of someone the state had already decided was harmless.

"He planned for me to find it," Theo said aloud, to no one.

"No," Petra said, from across the stone. She was watching him with those tired, unsparing eyes. "He hoped. Planning is what the other side does. Hope is what costs nothing and everything." She tied off the provision bundle and slung it. "Come on. We've got light for six hours, and after what Brandt told us, I want to find that woman before a man with explosives decides she's a rumor."

They walked. The gorge fell away behind them, and the pursuit with it — for now, for hours, for however long the slate and the crossings bought them. Ahead the land rose into country Theo had never seen named on any map, and the compass needle held its bearing steady as a heartbeat, pointing at a living woman, a sealed room, and the end of every line he'd ever drawn.

Behind them, far down the dark throat of the gorge, the first faint crack of demolition charges echoed off the stone — the second team, giving up on the chase, and starting instead to close the door.
