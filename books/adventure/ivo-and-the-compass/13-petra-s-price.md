## 13. Petra's Price

Petra Osei had signed her name to a great many documents in her career as an engineer. Bridge certifications. Load tolerances. Grade calculations for lines that would carry ten thousand tons of freight a day over ground that wanted to swallow them. She had learned early that a signature was never just ink — it was a promise that you had looked, truly looked, at what you were certifying, and found it sound.

The verification report she had signed in the spring, three weeks before the Vell River swallowed her team's camp whole and left nothing but instruments laid out like offerings, was the only document of her life she had signed without looking. Or rather — she had looked, and seen exactly what it said, and signed it anyway.

She sat now on an ammunition crate in the mouth of the rail tunnel, the fire small enough to hide behind a windbreak of corrugated tin, and watched Theo Zhou turn his father's compass over in his hands like a man trying to read a letter written in a language he almost knew. The needle did not point north. It pointed upstream, steady and patient, toward whatever waited at the river's source. Toward the archive. Toward the thing the state had sent four surveyors into the gorge to confirm could stay buried forever.

Maren Kessler stood at the tunnel's edge with her back to them, keeping watch on the dark, though Petra suspected she was also giving them privacy — or taking it for herself. The two survivors, Anselm and Duarte, slept in their bedrolls against the far wall, hollow-cheeked and quiet, men who had spent five months eating river fish and fear.

Theo hadn't asked yet. He'd been circling it all evening, the way water circles a stone before it goes over the edge. *Why did they send you? Surveyors don't get hunted. Surveyors don't vanish.* And Petra had answered everything except the question itself, because answering it meant saying the words aloud, and once said aloud they would be true in a way they weren't true inside her chest, where she had kept them folded up and hidden since April.

He looked up from the compass. His face in the firelight was his father's face, older than it should have been, worn by the same restlessness. She had known Elias Zhou for eleven years. She had eaten dinner at his table. She had helped him carry his wife's coffin when the state sent no one else.

"Petra," he said quietly. "What did you verify?"

The fire ticked. Somewhere back in the tunnel, water dripped off a rib of rock with the patience of something that had been counting longer than any of them had been alive.

She could have lied. She was good at lying now; five months of hiding taught you that. But Anselm stirred in his sleep and murmured something, and Duarte's breathing hitched, and she understood suddenly that the lie wasn't hers alone anymore. It belonged to everyone in this tunnel. It had been sitting on all their chests like wet gravel since the day she put pen to paper.

"I'll tell you," she said. "But I want you to understand that I've told myself a different version every night, and none of them are true."

* * *

It began, she told him, with a letter.

In February, the Ministry of Water and Reclamation had issued their team its commission: a hydrographic survey of the upper Vell, closed territory, forty years unvisited. Standard language. Standard seals. But tucked into the commission, in an annex so dry it might have been written by a machine, was a clause requiring the team to certify — not survey, not assess, but *certify* — the structural integrity of the reservoir basin at the river's source. To confirm, in writing, under signature, that no settlement remained within the flood zone. That no structures, no records, no human remains presented a risk to the integrity of the impoundment works.

"We thought it was boilerplate," Petra said. "You sign forty pages of boilerplate on every commission. It's how they get you. The poison is always in the page nobody reads twice."

They had gone up the gorge in late winter, when the river ran low and mean between walls of black stone. And on the third day, where no map showed anything but wilderness, they had come around a shoulder of rock and seen the town.

Not drowned. Not yet. The state had drawn the reservoir line on paper decades ago, had flooded the lower valley and called it progress, but the source itself — the high basin where the Vell came out of the earth cold and clear — had never been drowned after all. The town of Vellmark still stood there, whole and impossible, its bell tower dry, its streets empty, its windows staring down at the water that was supposed to have taken it. Forty years of official erasure, and the town had simply been left, sealed behind closure orders and falsified maps, waiting for someone to remember it existed.

"There were papers," Petra said. Her voice had gone flat, the way it went when she read load figures for a bridge she already knew was failing. "A whole archive. In the old station house. Records of the flooding — the real one, the lower valley. Dates, requisitions, engineering assessments. Proof that they knew the valley wouldn't drain the way they claimed. Proof that they flooded it anyway, with people in it, because delaying the reservoir another season would have cost a deputy minister his career." She paused. "And protest letters. Dozens of them. From the surveyor who mapped the town before they erased it."

Theo's hands had stopped moving on the compass.

"Elias Zhou," she said. "Your father filed a formal objection six weeks before the gates closed. He documented every household in the valley. He named names. He demanded the evacuation be carried out properly or not at all. And then the gates closed, and his objection went into the archive instead of into history, and his name went onto a list, and one day he simply wasn't anywhere anymore."

The fire cracked. Down the tunnel, the dripping continued its long arithmetic.

"The annex," Theo said slowly. "Certify that no settlement remains."

"They didn't want us to find the town," Petra said. "They knew it was there. They've always known. They wanted us to stand in it, look at the archive, and sign our names saying it could safely stay buried — that no one would ever come looking, that the records posed no risk, that the dead could keep their silence another forty years. It was a test dressed as a survey. If we signed, we were theirs. If we refused—" She spread her hands. "You've seen what happens to people who refuse."

"And you signed."

The question landed the way a dropped tool lands on stone. Petra made herself hold his eyes. That was the discipline of shame: you did not look away, because looking away was the second lie, and the second lie was the one that rotted you.

"I signed," she said. "I stood in that station house with my hand shaking and I signed the certification, and I told myself it was the price of getting my team out alive. Four signatures on that report. Mine, Anselm's, Duarte's. And Joss."

"Joss."

"Joren Vasch. Our fourth. The best of us, if I'm honest — the one who noticed the annex in the first place, who read every page twice because that's who he was. He signed too. We all signed. And we walked out of that valley carrying our lives like contraband, and we told ourselves we'd done what anyone would do." Her jaw tightened. "We got as far as the lower camp. And then Joss broke camp in the night and took the pack radio and half our food, and left a note pinned to the tent flap with his knife."

She had the note still. She kept it in the waterproof sleeve with her own copy of the certification, the two documents bound together like a wound and the blade that made it. She handed the sleeve to Theo and let him read by firelight.

The note was short. Engineers wrote short notes; there was never time for anything else.

*I can't be the seal on this. Someone has to open it before they decide it's easier to drown it again. Don't follow me. One man moving is invisible. Five are a target. — J.*

"He went upstream," Petra said. "Back toward the source. Back toward the archive. Alone, in March, with the river still carrying ice." She looked at the fire. "That was five months ago. We stayed in the tunnel because following him meant dying on open ground when the hunters came, and staying meant living with what I'd signed. So we lived with it. Anselm stopped talking for six weeks. Duarte counts things — rivets, stones, drips — anything, constantly, under his breath. And I re-read my own signature every night like it might change if I caught it being written."

Theo set the note down. When he spoke, his voice was careful, the way a man's voice is careful walking across ice.

"You could have told me this at the last station. You could have told me when we found your camp."

"Yes."

"Why didn't you?"

"Because you were his son," she said, "and I was going to need you. Because the truth is a tool and I've learned to ration my tools. And because—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "Because I hoped you'd never ask. That's the honest answer. Every coward's answer is hope wearing a coat."

Maren had turned from the tunnel mouth at some point without Petra noticing. She stood at the edge of the firelight with her arms crossed, and her face held something Petra recognized from mirrors: the expression of a person doing arithmetic with their own complicity.

"You reported us dead," Maren said. "I know you did. Your false position reports matched mine for three weeks. That's not cowardice, Osei. That's coordination."

"It's survival," Petra said. "Don't dress it. I coordinated with a government handler because a government handler was the only cover I had. If she'd turned out to be what her file says she is, I'd have led my team into her gun and called it prudence." She looked at Maren steadily. "We're both standing in the same river, Kessler. The only difference is which bank we started on."

For a moment the two women regarded each other across the fire, and Petra felt something shift — not trust, nothing so clean as trust, but the grudging recognition of shared weight. Two people who had each, in their own way, signed something they shouldn't have, and were now spending everything they had to unsign it.

Theo picked up the compass. The needle trembled once and settled again, pointing upstream, pointing at the source, at the keeper, at the room full of paper where his father's voice had been shouting for forty years into a silence built to contain it.

"Joss went to destroy it," Theo said. It wasn't a question.

"He went to open it first," Petra said. "Destroying it was the fallback. He believed — God help him, I think he was right — that a truth locked in a box is just leverage waiting to be used. The state doesn't fear the archive. The state fears the archive in the wrong hands. Their plan was always to retrieve it eventually, quietly, and use it however it served them. Joss wanted it *out*. In the open. Belonging to everyone, so it could belong to no one's purposes." She shook her head. "But he was one man, and the lock on that station house is the same kind of lock as everything else they build — it doesn't open for honesty. It opens for keys."

Theo looked down at the compass in his palm. The brass casing, the maker's stamp worn nearly smooth, the needle that had never once pointed north in all the years he'd carried it, blaming the flaw on age, on damage, on his own carelessness. His father had built it as a key and given it to his son the way you bury a seed — trusting the ground to do the rest.

"If Joss couldn't open it," Theo said, "he may still be there. At the source."

"Or his bones are," Petra said. "Five months. Winter coming out of the gorge. Hunters behind him." She made herself say the rest, because the discipline of shame was also the discipline of courage, and they were the same muscle. "If he's alive, he's dying. And if he's dying, then the last thing he'll hear about me is that I hid in a tunnel while he walked back into the valley I certified as empty."

The fire burned low. Anselm murmured in his sleep and went quiet. Out beyond the tunnel mouth, the Vell ran dark and constant, going about its ancient work of carrying everything downhill — silt, seasons, secrets — toward the sea.

Petra stood, joints stiff from the crate, and began kicking dirt over the fire. There was no more night left to waste on confession. Confession was fuel, and fuel was for burning toward something.

"Then we go upstream," Theo said.

"We go upstream," she agreed. "All of us. And Theo—" She waited until he looked at her. "When we find whoever the state sends next — and we will, they'll have tracked Kessler's silence by now — you let me stand in front. I've spent five months hiding behind my signature. I'm done paying interest on it. Whatever this costs, I pay first."

She shouldered her pack and checked the straps with hands that had signed away a piece of themselves and meant, starting tonight, to sign it back.

Behind them, deep in the tunnel, the dripping went on counting. Ahead, upstream, the compass needle held its line — steady, patient, and true at last — toward the source, toward the keeper, toward whatever remained of the fourth man who had chosen the open wound over the comfortable lie.

And downstream, far below in the dark, a telegraph line hummed with the first queries of a ministry that had just begun to wonder why its handler had gone silent.
