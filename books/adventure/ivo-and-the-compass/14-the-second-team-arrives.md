## 14. The Second Team Arrives

The keeper's name was Halina Voss, and she made tea the way other people kept time.

Theo learned this in the three days they'd been at the source — three days of copying documents by lamplight while the old woman moved through her stone rooms with a kettle and an unhurried certainty that seemed, to Theo, almost obscene given what was coming. She had lived above the archive for forty years. She had fed his father's letters to no one. She had watched the river rise each spring over the drowned town and had written down everything the water tried to take, and now she poured tea for four fugitives as if the state were merely weather.

"Drink," she said, pressing a tin cup into Petra Osei's hands. "You copy faster when your hands are warm."

Petra didn't argue. None of them argued with Halina Voss. She was small and bent and her hands shook slightly when she held anything lighter than a ledger, but there was something in her stillness that reminded Theo of deep water — the kind that looked calm because it hadn't yet decided what it wanted on the bottom.

They were nearly done. Three copies of the core documents: the flooding order with its forged safety assessment, the compensation ledgers where the dead were listed as *relocated*, and his father's protest letter, eleven pages in a draftsman's hand, each page stamped RECEIVED and then DEFERRED and then, finally, DESTROYED — except that someone had not destroyed it. Someone had carried it upriver instead, into the gorge, into a room cut into the rock behind a waterfall, and had spent a lifetime keeping it dry.

His father's letter. His father's handwriting. Theo had read it four times now and each time he reached the same line — *I surveyed every foundation in Vellmark myself; I know which families were home* — and had to set the page down and walk to the mouth of the cave and look at the river until his eyes stopped burning.

Maren found him there on the third evening. She came up beside him without speaking and stood shoulder to shoulder, watching the water fall past the cave entrance in a grey-green curtain. Below them the source pool churned, and beyond it the gorge ran down toward Ivo Brandt's station, toward the last station, toward everything they'd left behind and couldn't go back to.

"The fourth route leaves tomorrow," Maren said quietly. "Petra takes the high trail at first light. The others split at the ford."

"I know."

"And us?"

Theo turned the compass over in his pocket. It was warm from his body heat, which felt wrong for a thing made of brass and glass and his father's secret. "We're the last copy out. After that—"

He never finished the sentence, because that was the moment the dog barked.

Not a wild dog. A working dog, trained, disciplined — one sharp bark, cut off, the sound of an animal corrected by a handler who didn't want to be heard. It came from downstream, from the direction of the crossing where Ivo had met them with supplies two days ago, and it carried up the gorge the way sound carries over water: too clearly, too close.

Halina appeared between them so fast it seemed she'd been standing there all along. Her tea towel was still over her arm. "How many?" she asked Maren.

"At least six. Maybe more." Maren was already moving back inside, voice low and fast. "Kessler's team moves in pairs with a dog handler. If we hear one bark, the rest are within a kilometer." She caught Petra's eye across the lamplit room. "You leave tonight. Now. All three routes, tonight."

Petra was on her feet before Maren finished, stuffing documents into oilcloth with hands that didn't shake at all — Theo noticed that, noticed how the starving woman from the tunnel had become someone else entirely under pressure, someone precise. "The chamber," Petra said. "Can you seal it?"

"It seals from inside," Halina said. "That is the design. That was always the design." She looked at Theo, and her eyes were steady and very old. "But it needs the key to open again. You understand that. Whatever happens tonight, the key cannot fall into their hands."

Theo's hand closed around the compass. "It won't."

"Good." She said it simply, the way she said *drink*, and went to bank the fire.

* * *

They moved in the dark like people who had practiced being ghosts.

Petra and the two survivors went first, down the goat trail on the far side of the falls, carrying copies wrapped against their chests. Maren walked them to the edge of the light and stood watching until the river noise swallowed their footsteps. When she came back her face was set in the expression Theo had learned to read as her version of fear — jaw tight, eyes doing arithmetic.

"Second team will have the trail covered by dawn," she said. "Which means they'll be here within the hour. We can't run the gorge in the dark; the water's up with the melt. We hold here."

"Hold where? There's one way in and one way out."

"There's the chamber."

Theo stared at her. "You want to seal ourselves inside a tomb."

"I want to not die on a ledge." She said it flatly, without drama, and he understood she had already done this math several times and hated every answer equally. "Halina?"

The old woman had not stopped moving. She was gathering things into a satchel with terrible calm — the lamp, flint, a bundle of letters Theo recognized as his father's correspondence, the originals she had never let out of her keeping. "I am not going into the chamber," she said.

"Halina—"

"They will want to know where the documents went. They will ask me." She tied the satchel closed. "And I am very good at making men believe an old woman knows nothing. I have been practicing since before you were born, engineer." She paused at the inner door, the low arch cut into living rock that led down to the archive room, and for a moment her composure flickered — just once, like a lamp in wind. "Forty years I have kept this room. Do you know what I have learned about keeping things? You do not keep them by hiding. You keep them by giving them away faster than anyone can burn them. Go. Seal it behind me. And when you come back — and you will come back, because you are his son and his son finishes things — knock twice and wait. The mechanism is slow."

Then she was gone, out into the dark, a small shape with a small satchel, walking toward the dogs.

* * *

Inside the chamber, the air smelled of stone and lamp oil and forty years of careful hands.

Theo had thought, the first time Halina led him down, that an archive would feel like a vault. It didn't. It felt like a kitchen, or a chapel, or the parlor of someone who expected visitors — long wooden shelves cut into the rock, documents in oiled wrappers labeled in a fine spidery hand, a table, two chairs, a basin for water. Everything arranged so that a stranger could find the truth in minutes. She had built it to be *read*, not hoarded. He understood now why the state feared it more than any weapon. Weapons killed people. This room unmade lies, and lies were load-bearing.

Maren barred the outer door — a slab of timber on iron pivots, older than the railroads — and then stood with her ear against it while Theo worked by lamplight, copying the last pages. His father's protest letter. He saved it for last deliberately, the way you save the last of anything, and his pen moved slower with each line.

The knocking started twenty minutes later.

Not on the outer door. On the rock itself — a dull, rhythmic concussion that Theo felt in his teeth before he understood it. Boots. Many boots, on the shelf of stone outside, and beneath them the dog again, closer now, its barking no longer corrected but encouraged.

Maren blew out the lamp.

In the dark, Theo's world shrank to sound: boots distributing themselves across the ledge, a voice — Kessler's voice, he was almost sure, though he'd only heard it through a wall once — giving quiet orders. Metal on stone. Something being wedged against the timber door.

"They're breaching," Maren whispered. "Get behind the shelves."

"We should help her—"

"If you open that door, we both die and the compass goes into a government safe." Her whisper was razor-flat. "She knew the cost when she walked out. She's known it for forty years. Don't insult her by pretending otherwise."

He hated her for saying it. He hated her because she was right.

The timber door groaned. Once. Twice. Then a crack of splintering wood, loud as a rifle shot, and torchlight spilled down the stairwell in ragged yellow bars. Theo pressed himself into the gap between two shelves with the compass crushed in his fist and watched shadows detach themselves from the light — four, five, six men, rifles first, moving with the economy of professionals. The last one through carried a crowbar still bright with fresh-splintered wood.

A hand grabbed Halina's satchel off the table and tore it open. Papers fanned across the floor. One of the men knelt, gathered them, read by torchlight, and laughed — a short, ugly sound.

"Letters," he said. "An old woman's letters."

"Search the shelves," said the tall man Theo now recognized fully — Kessler's second, a man named Dreyse whom Maren had described once, without adjectives, the way you describe a tool. "All of them. And find the mechanism. Kessler wants this room intact. She wants it *theirs*."

They searched. Torchlight crawled along the shelves, over the wrappers, across the table where Theo's copied pages had lain minutes ago — he saw one of the men pick up a blank sheet, sniff it, drop it. The compass in Theo's fist felt like a coal. He could hear his own pulse. Beside him, Maren had gone absolutely still in the way of someone who had done this before, whose body remembered how to be furniture.

Dreyse crossed to the inner wall, the one with no visible seam, and put his palm flat against the stone.

"The report says a counterweight mechanism," he said. "Somewhere in this wall. Find it."

A man pried at a shelf bracket. Another tapped along the joints with a knife handle, listening. And Theo, in the dark behind the shelves, did the worst arithmetic of his life: the mechanism Halina had mentioned — *knock twice and wait, the mechanism is slow* — was somewhere in this room, and six armed men were looking for it, and if they found it they would open the chamber from inside, and the compass would stop being a key and become a souvenir.

Then Halina's voice came from the stairwell, thin and clear and furious.

"You are standing on my floor."

The torches swung. In the doorway, framed by the last of the outer light, stood the old woman, satchel gone, hands empty, spine straighter than Theo had ever seen it.

"Madam," Dreyse began, "you need to—"

"I have buried three governments' secrets in this rock," Halina Voss said, "and I will tell you exactly where the mechanism is, and exactly what it opens, and exactly nothing else, unless you put those rifles down and speak to me like men and not like burglars." She took one step into the room. "Well?"

It was magnificent. It was suicide. Every head turned toward her, every torch, every rifle muzzle drifting down and sideways, and Theo felt Maren's hand close on his wrist — *move* — and he didn't move, because in the shuffle of attention he had seen it: the shelf bracket the first man had pried at had shifted a finger's width, and behind it, in the stone, a slot. A brass-edged slot, shaped — he knew the shape before his mind finished naming it — shaped exactly like the back plate of his father's compass.

One of the soldiers got tired of talking. Theo heard the safety click off, heard Dreyse snap *"Hold, you idiot, Kessler wants—"*, and heard Halina say, in a voice that did not tremble at all:

"My name is Halina Voss. Write it down correctly. No one else ever has."

The shot went off in the stone room like the end of the world.

* * *

Afterward, Theo could never assemble those seconds into order. What remained were fragments: Maren's weight hitting him, driving both of them down behind the table as the room erupted — not into surrender but into chaos, because the shot had been answered, somehow, by falling rock; because the counterweight Halina must have released — or tripped, or broken with her last step — had let half a ton of dressed stone drop across the stairwell, sealing it, crushing the torchlight to nothing, burying the doorway and the shouting and the dog and the old woman together under a wall of rock that had waited forty years for exactly this.

Darkness. Dust. Somewhere a man screaming that his leg was pinned. Rifles firing blind, bullets sparking off stone, and then a voice — Dreyse, coughing — snarling the order that told Theo everything: *"Out! Fall back to the ledge! Nothing in here is worth digging for tonight!"*

Boots receding. The dog, whining, dragged away. Then silence, total, except for the fine hiss of settling dust and, after a long time, the sound of Maren striking a match.

The flame showed a room transformed. The stairwell was gone — replaced by a raw face of fallen masonry, cracked but holding, packed solid from floor to ceiling. The table was split. The shelves had shed half their wrappers. And on the floor near where the doorway had been, half-buried in rubble, lay a single tin cup, dented, still faintly warm with the ghost of tea.

Theo picked it up and could not put it down.

"She bought us the room," Maren said. Her voice was scraped hollow. "She walked in there knowing — she *told* us she knew — and she bought us the room." She crouched, retrieved the scattered papers one by one, squared their edges with mechanical care. "Six men, Theo. Six rifles. And she walked in first."

"She didn't trip anything." His own voice sounded foreign to him. "There was no lever. I was looking right at the wall. She stepped on the floor — the floor *is* the trigger, it has to be, she knew exactly where to stand—" He stopped. His throat had closed. "She practiced. Forty years, she said. She wasn't talking about lying."

Maren said nothing. There was nothing to say, and she was too honest to pretend otherwise, and Theo loved her a little for that even as grief went through him like a freight car — rage and guilt braided into one load, because he had followed the river, he had followed it upstream with his father's compass in his pocket, and everywhere it led, the ground ended in somebody's body. The survey team starving in a tunnel. The fourth man gone mad at the source. His father, erased. And now Halina Voss, who had kept the truth alive for forty years with a kettle and a label-maker's patience, dead under her own archive, buying strangers ninety seconds with her life.

*Following the river has a body count.* The thought arrived complete, cold, undeniable. And the compass was still in his fist.

He opened his hand. In the matchlight the instrument lay in his palm, brass worn bright at the edges by sixty years of thumbs, its needle swinging slowly — not north, never north — until it settled, pointing at the sealed wall, at the slot behind the shifted bracket, at the room full of truth that six men would come back to dig for the moment they brought tools and time.

"They'll return," Maren said, following his gaze. "Tomorrow, maybe the day after. With drills. Kessler doesn't quit and now she's angry."

"Then we don't wait for tomorrow." Theo stood. The grief didn't leave him — he understood suddenly that it never would, that it would ride in his chest the way the compass rode in his pocket, permanent, pointing — but it hardened into something he could use. "The mechanism works from inside. Halina said knock twice and wait. Which means the way out isn't the stairway. There's another door. There's always another door — his father designed it, and his father never built a room with one exit."

Maren looked at him, at the compass, at the wall. "And when we're out? Where do we go? Every road down is theirs."

Theo slid the compass into its slot. The brass fit the brass with a click so soft it was almost tender, and somewhere deep in the rock, weights began to move.

"We go down," he said. "Tonight. Before the water drops and before Kessler gets her drills. Because there's one thing she doesn't know yet, Maren." The wall shivered; a seam of darkness opened where no seam had been. "Flood season starts tomorrow. And the river is about to do our running for us."
