## 16. What Theo Burns

The fire pit at the keeper's source was still warm when Theo knelt beside it and opened his satchel.

He had carried maps for twenty-two years. Not the government's maps — those belonged to the state, stamped and numbered and surrendered on demand — but his own. The private ones. The ones he drew by lamplight in rented rooms and never showed anyone: hand-inked tracings of every closed line he'd ever walked, annotated in a shorthand only he could read. A spur abandoned in '61 with a culvert that still sang in the rain. A junction erased from the gazetteer where two farmers had kept a crossing gate out of stubbornness long after the rails were pulled. The Vell River valley itself, mapped three times over the years, each version showing the water a little higher, the towns a little fewer, as if the river were eating the country's memory one page at a time.

He had told himself once, in the early years, that he was keeping them for history. Then, later, that he was keeping them because they were all he had. Both explanations had been lies of convenience. He kept them because making them was the only way he knew how to move without arriving anywhere. A man with a map always has somewhere else to be. A man with a full satchel never has to sit down and ask himself what he's running from.

Maren found him kneeling there. She didn't say anything at first. She had learned, over these weeks, that Theo needed silence the way other men needed answers, and she stood at the edge of the firelight with her coat pulled tight against the gorge wind, watching him lift the first folio out of the bag like a man lifting something wounded.

"You don't have to do this," she said finally.

"Yes I do." His voice came out flat, matter-of-fact, which surprised him. He'd expected it to shake. "They're not following us anymore. They're following *it*." He tapped the leather cover of the folio. "Every map I've ever drawn has my hand in it. My notation. My habit of marking water sources with a little arc, my way of dating things in the corner. Petra saw one of my old survey sheets back at the tunnel and recognized me before I said my name. If the second team gets hold of even one of these, they can read me like a timetable. They'll know where I've been, where I'd go, how I think. And worse—" he turned the folio over in his hands, "—they'll know what I know. Every closed line I ever documented is a place they haven't finished erasing yet."

"And if you burn them, you're just a man in the mountains."

"I'm nobody in the mountains." He almost smiled. "It's the best offer I've had in years."

Maren crouched down across the fire from him. In the orange light her face looked older than it had on the handcar, harder around the eyes, but the hardness sat differently now — not the institutional set of a woman following orders, but the weathered look of someone who had chosen her side and was living inside the choice. "You understand what you're actually doing," she said. It wasn't quite a question.

"They're maps, Maren."

"They're your father."

The words went through him like a spike driven between ties. He looked down at the folio in his lap and saw it was true. The earliest drawings in here were copies of copies — pages his father had made before the flooding, before the erasure, before whatever had happened to him on the banks of this same river. Theo had traced them at nineteen, sitting cross-legged on a boarding-house floor, learning his father's line weight the way other sons learned a father's handwriting or his laugh. Every map since had been an argument with those originals. Every mile he'd walked was a conversation he'd been having with a dead man who couldn't answer.

And now he was going to feed the conversation to a fire.

* * *

He started with the oldest ones.

That felt right, though he couldn't have said why. The tracing of the drowned town's original street grid — the one drawn from his father's field notes, showing the church, the schoolhouse, the bell tower that had risen out of the low water like an accusation. Theo held it up to the light one last time. He had copied it so many times he could draw it blind, and he would be able to draw it blind until the day he died; that was the strange mercy of memory, that it kept what the hands gave away. But the paper itself, the physical artifact with its foxed edges and its coffee ring from some night twenty years ago — that had to go.

He fed it into the coals. For a moment the flame seemed reluctant, licking up the edges in brown curls, and then the whole sheet caught and bloomed orange, and his father's careful linework curled inward on itself like a hand closing.

Theo watched it with the detachment of a man watching someone else's funeral. That was the trick of it, he realized. Grief, real grief, required a body, a grave, a name spoken aloud. This was different. This was amputation. There was no pain at first because the nerve hadn't caught up yet, and somewhere down the line — tomorrow, next week, standing in some doorway some ordinary morning — the absence would arrive all at once and he would reach for a satchel that wasn't there.

Petra came out of the cave mouth behind him. She moved quietly for a woman who'd spent six weeks starving in a rail tunnel; the tunnel taught people to move like that, she'd said, like anything louder than breathing might bring the rock down. She stood beside Maren and watched the fire eat.

"The Osei family kept everything," she said, apropos of nothing, or so it seemed. "My mother. Photographs, letters, my grandfather's discharge papers, receipts from 1953. When we fled the north she carried two suitcases and both of them were paper." Petra crouched, warmed her hands. "I used to think it was weakness. All that weight, for paper." She glanced at him. "Then I got older and understood she wasn't carrying the past. She was carrying the evidence that we'd existed. You burn that, and anyone can tell you any story about yourself they want."

"That's exactly why I'm burning them," Theo said.

Petra tilted her head.

"Because right now the hunters' story about me is the only one that matters. As long as my maps exist, I'm legible. I'm a file. Zhou, T., railway engineer, habitual mover, known associates, known methods, known destinations." He pulled out another folio — the Vell valley, third revision, water lines marked in blue where the reservoirs were planned to rise. "A man they can't read is a man they can't predict. And a man they can't predict is a man they have to stop chasing and start guessing about. Guessing takes time. Time is the only thing we've got that they want."

"And after?" Maren asked. "When this is over. When the copies are downstream and the state's version collapses. What's left of you?"

Theo considered the question honestly, which cost him something. He thought about the room above Ivo's station, the one with the good window. He thought about the sound the river made at night, patient as a heartbeat. He thought about the fact that for the first time since he was nineteen years old, he had not spent last night planning tomorrow's departure.

"I don't know," he said. "I've never been a man without maps. Maybe there's nothing left. Maybe that's the point — maybe you have to be nothing before you can be someone in particular."

"Or maybe," Petra said, standing, brushing ash from her knees, "you find out the moving was never the thing. It was just the shell around the thing." She touched his shoulder once, briefly, and went back inside to the others.

* * *

The fire burned for hours.

He worked through the satchel methodically, and the methodicalness was its own strange comfort — one last survey, one last ledger, entries in the order he'd made them. The spur outside Harrow's Bend, where he'd slept in a signal box and heard the rails sing in the wind. The rerouted bridge at Callow Ford, drawn twice because the first drawing had been rained on. Forty-one closed lines in total, forty-one small funerals, each one a place where somebody's farm or shop or grandmother's house had been subtracted from the official world and Theo had been the only person left who bothered to write it down.

That was the part that hurt, when the hurt finally arrived. Not losing his own work. Losing theirs. Because his maps had been, in the end, the only monument those places had. No plaques, no archives, no graves with names — just a folded sheet in a drifter's satchel, inked by a stranger who passed through once and never came back. He had told himself for two decades that this made him their historian. Now he understood it had also made him their hostage-taker. As long as he carried them, the dead towns belonged to him, and he belonged to the road, and neither of them was ever free.

He fed the last of the Vell surveys into the flames and watched the blue water lines flare green as the ink burned.

Maren stayed the whole time. She didn't help — he wouldn't have let her, and she seemed to know it — but she sat with her back against a stone and kept watch on the gorge trail, her rifle across her knees, and once, when a night bird startled up from the scree, her hand went to the bolt and Theo saw her glance at him first, checking, before she checked the dark. Checking on him. Somewhere along this river the handler had become the guarded, and the guarded had become the handled, and neither of them had marked the exact date of the exchange. He suspected neither of them would ever say it out loud either. Some things survived better unspoken.

Near dawn, when the satchel was empty except for the compass, he took it out and held it.

His father's compass. The key. The needle that didn't point north but pointed, patiently and treacherously, toward a sealed door in the mountain behind him. He turned it over. The maker's stamp — the same stamp Ivo Brandt had named at the derelict station, spoken like a prayer — caught the firelight.

"You could burn that too," Maren said quietly. "End it completely. Nothing left to follow, nothing left to open."

"No." The word came out of him before thought did, and he let it stand. "This isn't mine to destroy. It's the only honest thing my father ever gave me. Everything else — the silence, the vanishing, the life built on top of a flooded town — that was the lie. This needle pointing at the truth is the only inheritance I trust." He closed his fist around it. "Besides. We still need it. Tomorrow we open that chamber again, and the day after, three people walk out of these mountains in three directions carrying the truth in oilcloth, and none of them will be me. But tonight—" he looked at the fire, at the grey drifts of ash where forty-one closed lines had been, "—tonight the only map left of Theo Zhou is the one in other people's heads. And most of those heads are about to scatter in three directions too."

Maren studied him for a long moment. "You're not the man from the handcar," she said. "The one who wouldn't sleep in the same town twice."

"He burned tonight too."

"Who's left?"

Theo looked east, where the sky was going pale above the gorge rim, where the river ran silver and cold toward the lowlands and the strangers downstream who would soon hold his father's protest letter in their hands. He felt lighter. He felt hollowed. He felt, for the first time in twenty-two years, like a man who could stop.

"Ask me in a week," he said. "If we live."

But the answer was already forming, and he knew it, and he suspected she knew it too. It had a shape. It had a window with a good view of the water, and a station being rebuilt board by board, and an old caretaker who asked nothing and a woman who had crossed a line for him and never once asked for it back.

He stood, brushed the ash from his knees, and shouldered the empty satchel — empty, gloriously, terrifyingly empty — and turned toward the cave where Petra and the survivors waited with the copied documents and the first grey light of the last morning.

Behind him, the fire settled into white ash, and the wind off the source began, very gently, to carry it away.

Inside the cave, Petra looked up from the oilcloth bundles, and her face told him before she said a word: they had a problem. The fourth man's trail led upstream — and someone else had followed it there ahead of them.
