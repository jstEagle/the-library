## 18. The Fourth Man

They found him where the river was born.

Not at the spring itself — nothing lived that close to it, not even moss — but a hundred meters below, in the shallow bowl of stone where the water gathered its first strength and the air smelled of iron and old snow. He had built himself a shelter of tarp and rail spikes and driftwood from a forest that no longer existed anywhere downstream. He had been there a long time. The evidence was written in his body: the way he'd learned to move slowly, the way his fire pit had been rebuilt so many times its stones had gone black through their centers, the way he watched them come without surprise, as if he'd been rehearsing this meeting for thirty years.

Petra saw him first. She stopped so abruptly that Maren nearly walked into her back.

"That's him," Petra said. Her voice came out flat, stripped of everything. "That's Aldous Wren."

Theo knew the name the way you know a scar — not because you remember getting it, but because it has always been part of you. *Wren, A.* It appeared twice in his father's field notebooks, once in a margin note about bridge load tolerances, once beside a sketch of a bird with the caption *namesake, met on the Vell line, argues too much.* His father had drawn him arguing. Theo had grown up thinking Wren was a joke between men who never existed.

The man in the shelter was not laughing. He was dying, and he knew it, and he had arranged himself accordingly — propped against his pack with his legs straightened out in front of him like a man posing for a photograph he intended to be found in. One hand rested on his knee. The other held a tin cup of snowmelt he was clearly too weak to lift.

"Four of you," Wren said. His voice was gravel poured slow. "I counted four coming up the gorge since yesterday. I thought they'd send soldiers."

"They did," Maren said. "We outran them. Barely."

"Maren Kessler." He said her name like a verdict, then looked at Theo, and something moved across his ruined face that Theo would spend the rest of his life trying to name. Not recognition. Recognition happens in a moment. This took longer — a whole weather system passing over him. "You have his chin," Wren said finally. "And his compass. I can see the case from here. He never let anyone touch it. Not even me." A pause. "*Especially* not me."

"You knew my father."

"I was ordered to kill him."

The words fell into the bowl of stone and lay there. The river made its small beginning-noise behind them, patient as arithmetic. Petra sat down on a rock as if her knees had been quietly removed. Maren's hand went to her sidearm and then, deliberately, away from it.

Wren watched all of this with the detached interest of a man reading his own obituary. "Sit down," he said. "It's a long story and I've only got enough voice left for one telling. I've been saving it. Thirty years is a long time to save a breath, but I'm a stubborn man. Ask anyone. Ask the river."

They sat. Theo did not sit near him. He sat where he could see the compass in his own hand and Wren's face at the same time, and he waited, because he had learned on this river that waiting was the only honest form of asking.

* * *

"He wasn't erased the way they erase people now," Wren began. "No forged embezzlement, no invented disgrace. They were more elegant then. They simply removed him from the record and let everyone assume he'd run. A surveyor who abandons a project — worse than a traitor, in the eyes of other surveyors. Traitors at least believe in something. Deserters just stop believing." He coughed, wet and deep, and waved off Maren when she moved toward him. "Your father believed in things. That was the problem.

"The town — Corvel, it was called, though you won't find that name anywhere now — was surveyed three times before they flooded it. First time, standard work: elevations, parcels, a relocation plan for every family. Your father did half the parcel maps himself. He drank tea with those people. He knew which widow kept goats in the churchyard and which boy was going to be an engineer someday if anyone bothered to teach him. Second survey was after the order changed. The order changed because the reservoir wasn't for irrigation anymore. It was for a smelter contract, and the smelter didn't care where the families went, only that the valley filled on schedule.

"Third survey was mine. I was young. I thought I'd been given an honor — verify the earlier work, certify the numbers, close the file. Nobody told me what closing the file meant. Nobody tells you. You find out standing in a churchyard measuring how much water will stand over the graves."

He lifted the tin cup with both hands, managed two swallows, set it down again. His hands shook, but his voice steadied, as if the story were the only thing holding the rest of him together.

"Your father filed a protest. A real one — forty pages, with appendices. He proved the flood calculations had been falsified to hide that the valley wouldn't drain properly, that the water table would poison three towns downstream within a decade, that the relocation plan had been abandoned entirely. He sent it up the chain. And the chain did what chains do. It passed the letter upward until it reached someone whose entire career depended on the smelter, and that person did not write back. That person wrote a different kind of letter. Downward.

"I received it. Because I was junior, because I was reliable, because I had signed the verification report and they wanted my signature on the ending too. The instruction was simple. Zhou's protest could not exist. Neither could Zhou. An accident on the Vell line — rockfall, high water, take your pick. They gave me a week and a travel authorization and the quiet understanding that my own file would grow very thin or very thick depending on the outcome."

The fire cracked. Somewhere above them, loose stone ticked down the gorge wall — the second team, still working their way up, hours behind but not stopping. No one looked up. There was nowhere to look that mattered more than this.

"And?" Theo asked. His voice sounded strange to him, younger than he wanted it to.

"And I went to meet him." Wren closed his eyes. "I told myself I'd talk first. Reason with him. Get him to withdraw the letter voluntarily — that was the version I could live inside. But I packed the rope and the climbing gear anyway, and I knew what the packing meant, and he knew it too the moment he saw me. We walked up the line together toward the high trestle, both of us knowing why we were there, neither of us saying it, and I have never in my life been so tired as I was on that walk. Fear tires you. Shame tires you worse.

"At the trestle he stopped and put his hand on the rail and said, *Aldous, tell me the truth. Did they send you to kill me?*

"And I said yes. Because lying to him seemed like the one thing I couldn't do. And do you know what he did?"

Wren opened his eyes and looked directly at Theo, and Theo understood that whatever came next had been carried upstream for thirty years, through drowned valleys and burned files and every mile of this forbidden river, specifically to arrive at this moment, in front of this face.

"He laughed," Wren said. "Not bitterly. Actually laughed. And he said, *Thank God. I was afraid they'd send someone I couldn't trust.*"

The river ran on. Theo found he had stopped breathing and made himself start again.

"He sat down on the ties," Wren went on, softer now, "and he opened his pack, and he took out the compass — the one you're holding — and he said, *This doesn't point north. It points at the archive. I built it that way. If anything happens to me, someone will eventually follow it, because engineers can't leave a broken instrument alone, and whoever follows it will find everything.* Then he handed it to me and said, *You can't kill me, Aldous. You've already failed. Look at your hands.*

"And I looked. And I had. I don't know when — somewhere on the walk, somewhere in the deciding, my hands had already refused. So he stood up and shook my hand like we'd finished a day's work, and he walked into the gorge alone, upstream, toward the source, toward the keeper, carrying his protest letter to the one person in the country who might keep it alive. And I went back down and reported that the rockfall had taken him, and I signed the paper, and I have been signing that same paper in my head every single day for thirty years."

"Why here?" Petra asked. Her voice was rough. "Why come back to the source?"

"Because the fourth member of your team — the one who went upstream to destroy the archive — was following orders I recognized." Wren's mouth twisted. "Same chain. Same downward letter. I've spent my life watching that letter travel. When I understood what Osei's missing man had been sent to do, I came to stand between him and the door. Old man against young one. I figured I could at least slow him down." He gestured vaguely at his own body, at the ruined leg Theo now saw had been broken and badly set. "Slowed him down. He went around me in the end, but I cost him two days, and two days is why your keeper still had time to seal the chamber. Everything costs. That's the whole lesson of this river. Everything costs somebody."

* * *

Maren built up the fire while Wren talked about smaller things — the tea his father had drunk, the argument they'd had about bridge load tolerances that had lasted eleven years by mail, the sketch of the bird. Theo listened with the compass open in his lap, its needle swinging gently off true, pointing always at the sealed room upstream where his father's forty-page protest lay in the dark beside the names of the drowned.

He had spent his whole life believing his father had abandoned him. That was the engine under everything — the restlessness, the never sleeping twice in the same town, the habit of leaving before dawn so he'd never have to see a place in full daylight and admit he'd stayed. His father had walked out. His father had chosen the river over the family. Every closed line Theo ever mapped, he'd mapped with that wound talking to him.

And the wound had lied.

His father hadn't run. He'd been hunted, spared by a man too decent to obey, and had chosen — deliberately, at cost of everything, including his son's good opinion — to carry the truth to the only safe place rather than carry himself home to a house that would have been watched, a family that would have been leverage. He hadn't abandoned them. He'd made them unfindable by making himself unfound. It was the cruelest possible mercy and the most merciful possible cruelty, and Theo sat with it while the fire burned down, and felt thirty years of motion come loose inside him like a knot finally slipping.

"You hate me," Wren said quietly, when the others had drifted to the edge of the firelight. "You should. I signed the paper that let them drown a town and call it geology."

"I don't hate you." Theo heard his own voice and was surprised by it — steady, tired, certain. "You were the last person to see him make a choice. Do you know what that makes you? Everyone else in my life has told me he vanished. You're the only one who can tell me he *arrived*."

Wren's eyes filled, and the old man did not wipe them, as if he'd decided tears were part of the testimony. "He arrived," he said. "I promise you. He arrived."

* * *

It was Maren who heard the sound first — the far-off knock of a handcar on the old spur below the gorge, rhythmic and wrong, too steady to be water. Then a flare of light against the northern ridge: lanterns, several, moving in formation.

The second team. Done with patience.

Wren tried to rise and failed, and Petra caught him, and he gripped her arm with sudden ferocious strength, the last strength of a dying man spending his final coin. "The chamber," he rasped. "The keeper sealed it, but seals are just doors with better manners. They'll breach it by morning unless—" He stopped, coughing, and looked at Theo, at the compass in his hands, and finished differently. "Unless someone who understands what it really is gets there first. Your father built it as a key, boy. Keys lock as well as open. Go. And when you're inside — read the last page of his protest first. He wrote it for you. He told me so. He said, *if my son ever comes, tell him to read the last page.*"

Theo stood. The needle swung in its housing, faithful to a dead man's design, pointing up the black throat of the gorge toward the source.

Behind them, Wren settled back against his pack and asked Petra for his tin cup, and asked her, almost politely, to leave him the pistol and the lantern.

"For slowing them down," he explained. "Everything costs somebody. Let it cost me."

They climbed. The lanterns below grew brighter, and the river ran thin and cold beside them toward the room where his father's last page waited, and Theo finally understood what he had been walking toward his entire life — not the truth about a vanished man, but the last words of a father who had never once stopped writing to him.
