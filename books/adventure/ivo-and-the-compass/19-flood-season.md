## 19. Flood Season

The sound reached them before the news did — a low, rhythmic thudding from downriver, like a heart beating inside the mountain. Theo stood at the mouth of the gorge with his cup of tea going cold in his hands and counted the intervals between the blasts. Four minutes. Then three. Then two and a half.

"They're not surveying," Petra said beside him. She had her binoculars up, aimed at the bend where the Vell narrowed to a throat of grey rock. "Survey crews drill slow. This is a demolition schedule."

Through the glass Theo could see them: orange points of men on the far bank, a crane arm swinging over the water, flatbed trucks backed up to the old haul road that hadn't carried anything heavier than foxes in thirty years. The state had come back to the river it had already drowned once, and this time it wasn't pretending.

Maren came up the trail at a run, breath ragged, a folded signal flimsy crushed in her fist. She'd been down at the relay hut since before dawn, listening on the dead line, coaxing traffic out of wires that officially didn't exist anymore.

"It's confirmed," she said. "Gorge closure order, signed yesterday. They're calling it *stabilization works*." Her mouth twisted around the word. "Three charges at the narrows, two at the source approach. When they're done, the whole upper valley floods. Permanently. The archive, the keeper's house, the tunnel — everything under forty feet of new water."

Theo looked at his hands around the cold cup. He thought about how many times he'd stood on some forgotten spur at dawn and read a landscape like a ledger: here a farm bought out, here a bridge rerouted, here a town struck from the rolls so thoroughly that even its name had been reassigned to a drainage culvert. He had spent his whole life mapping erasures. He had never once watched one being made in real time, with dynamite, on a schedule posted for efficiency.

"How long?" he asked.

"Last blast is scheduled for tomorrow, oh-six-hundred. After that they open the coffer dams upstream." Maren's voice was steady the way a plank is steady over a drop. "We have one night."

* * *

They gathered in the keeper's chamber, the room of documents that had cost an old woman her life, and Theo laid out what they had on the long table under the lamp. Three canvas satchels. Two hundred and eleven copied pages, wrapped in oilcloth, sealed in wax against the water that was coming for them one way or another. The original protest letter — his father's handwriting, cramped and furious across four pages — went into the smallest bag, the one Theo would carry himself.

Petra traced the river on the keeper's old chart with one finger. "Downstream is the problem. The gorge road is crawling with their people. The rail line through the tunnel is watched since we came through. And once the dams open—" she tapped the narrows, where the Vell compressed itself between walls close enough to shout across, "—this becomes a chute. Whitewater, debris, whatever the blasts shake loose. A raft through there isn't a journey. It's a coin toss."

"Then we don't all go the same way," Theo said.

He'd been turning it over since dawn, and now he said it plainly, because there was no time left for the luxury of hesitation. Three bags. Three routes. Ivo knew the fisherman's path along the north rim, the one the government had never bothered to close because no map showed it. Maren still had contacts on the freight line two valleys east who owed her more than she liked to admit. And the river itself — the river was the fastest way down, and the most dangerous, and the only one that would put the documents in Theo's own hands at the end.

"You're splitting the truth into pieces," Maren said quietly. "If any one of us is caught—"

"Then two-thirds of it survives." Theo met her eyes. "That's the arithmetic. It's the same arithmetic the state uses, except ours adds instead of subtracts."

Nobody laughed. Nobody argued either. That was how he knew they'd all done the math already, alone, in the dark hours when the blasting thudded through the stone like a countdown.

* * *

Ivo arrived at dusk, as if the old caretaker could smell a deadline the way he smelled rain. He came down the rim trail with a mule loaded with tarps, rope, pitch, and two barrels of resin, and he took one look at the chart, one look at their faces, and started issuing instructions in the tone of a man who had spent fifty years keeping a station alive against the indifference of everyone else.

"The raft first," he said. "Then the routes. You don't get to be brave until the work's done."

They built it in the shallows below the keeper's house, by lamplight, while the blasts kept their rhythm upriver. Ivo's timber — seasoned pine from the station's stores, planks he'd been hoarding against a winter that never asked for them — lashed to empty resin barrels, cross-braced with the survey team's spare tripod legs because nobody had time to fell anything. Theo worked with his sleeves rolled, pitch burning his fingers, and found that his hands remembered things his mind had never learned: knots his father must have tied in front of him before he was old enough to hold the memory. The compass rode in his breast pocket, and every time he bent over the lashing he felt it knock against his chest like a second heartbeat, pointing, always pointing, toward the room behind him full of paper that people had died to keep dry.

Petra tested the lashings by hanging her whole weight off each joint, grunting approval or curses. The three survivors worked without speaking much; they had been starving in a tunnel together for months, and they had passed beyond the need for small courage-talk. One of them, the young one — Halloran — kept glancing upriver at the flashes of the demolition crews, and Theo saw him mouthing something, counting maybe, or praying, or just naming the hours as they went.

By midnight the raft sat in the shallows, ugly and seaworthy, looking exactly like what it was: desperation given a hull.

* * *

They divided the routes by lamplight after that, and it was there, over the chart, that the night turned heavy.

"I'll take the rim path," Ivo said. "North side. I've walked it since before your father was born. Nobody stops an old man with a mule and a fishing rod." He said it lightly, but his eyes went, just for a moment, to the dark shape of his station far downstream — the last station, the warm place, the kettle and the stove and the forty years of quiet he'd built out of other people's forgetting. Theo understood what the old man was leaving on the table by volunteering, and couldn't find words for it, and saw that Ivo preferred it that way.

"Maren takes the freight line," Petra said. "East valley, the ore spur. There's a morning train if her friends are still her friends."

"They're still my friends," Maren said. "That's the one thing in my life I never falsified a report about."

Which left the river, and the river left Theo. He said so before anyone could arrange it otherwise. Petra started to object — *you're the one they want, you're the compass-bearer, you're the son* — and he cut her off with a flatness that surprised even him.

"That's exactly why. They'll expect me to run for the hills like everyone they've ever erased. Let me come down the middle of the water instead, carrying my father's letter, on the river that drowned his town. If they want to stop the truth, they'll have to shoot at a man in a boat in floodwater, and do it in front of witnesses on both banks." He looked around the lamplit circle. "And someone has to see it through. All the way. Not to a newspaper office — to the people. The families. The ones who were told their town never existed. Paper doesn't mean anything until a living person reads it out loud."

Silence, except the distant thud of the charges, closer now. Two minutes apart. Maybe less.

Maren touched his arm, briefly, in front of everyone, which was more than she'd ever allowed herself. "Oh-six-hundred," she said. "Don't be late for the end of the world."

* * *

He slept for ninety minutes, sitting up against the archive door with his hand flat on the wood, and dreamed of nothing, which he took for mercy. He woke to Halloran shaking his shoulder and the smell of the river rising — that green, mineral breath it gave off when it was gathering itself for weather.

The sky was the colour of wet slate. Downstream, somewhere past the bends, the coffer dams were already groaning; you could feel it through the ground, a deep structural unease, the mountain clearing its throat. The demolition crews had paused for the night shift change, and the silence between blasts was worse than the noise — a held breath, a drawn bow.

They loaded the raft in the grey half-light. Oilcloth bundles lashed low and centered, wrapped twice more in tarpaulin. Oars — paddles really, Ivo's idea, quieter than oarlocks. The compass in Theo's pocket. His father's letter in an inner wallet, waterproofed, against his ribs.

At the edge of the shallows they said the things that needed saying, quickly, the way people do when ceremony would be an insult to the hour. Petra gripped his forearm hard enough to leave marks. "You get it downstream," she said. "I don't care how wet you get. Wet is fine. Dead is not."

Ivo pressed something into his hand: a small tin whistle, dented, ancient. "River signal. One long, two short means you're alive and need help at the fish weir. Don't use it unless you mean it." The old man's voice cracked, just slightly, on the last word, and then he turned away to check a lashing that did not need checking.

Maren stood closest. For a moment neither of them spoke, and the whole unsaid country of the last weeks lay between them — the reports she'd falsified, the handler she'd defied, the bullet she hadn't yet taken but both of them somehow knew was waiting somewhere in the geometry of the next twelve hours.

"Strangers downstream," she finally said. "That's who it goes to. Strangers who'll do the right thing because nobody told them not to."

"That's the plan."

"It's a terrible plan."

"It's the only one left." He almost smiled. "Like us."

She hit his shoulder, not gently, and turned to her mule and the east road, and did not look back, which was its own kind of promise.

* * *

Theo pushed off as the sky went from slate to bruise.

The river took the raft the way it took everything — without malice, without mercy, with the enormous indifferent strength of a thing that had been burying towns longer than any government had existed to name them. The first mile was almost gentle, the current sliding them past the drowned bell tower's tip, past the keeper's house standing dark on its bank, past everything they had paid for in blood and were now entrusting to the current. Theo knelt amidships with the paddle and steered by instinct and by the white noise of the rapids ahead, and behind him the gorge walls rose higher and higher, closing over the sky like a book.

Then the narrows came into view, and he heard the water change its mind.

It wasn't whitewater yet — that was still downstream, past the bend, where the charts showed the chute. But here the river compressed, accelerated, began to speak in a lower register, and on both banks he saw them: lanterns. Orange points of men. The demolition crews, working the dawn shift now, rigging the final charges on scaffolds bolted to the rock, and among them — moving with a purpose that had nothing to do with dynamite — soldiers. Field glasses catching the first light. A voice calling out, thin across the water, too distorted by the current's roar to parse but unmistakable in intent.

They'd seen him. Of course they'd seen him. A raft in the gorge on the last morning of the world was not a subtle thing.

Theo dug the paddle in and steered for the seam of faster water, the channel Ivo had described, the thread of survival stitched down the middle of the roar. Behind him, upstream, a sound rolled over the cliffs that was not thunder and not blasting — deeper, older, the sound of a dam giving up its argument with gravity. The coffer gates, opening early. The river behind him began to rise even as he fled down its spine, lifting the raft, quickening everything, the flood coming to collect the gorge one last time.

Ahead, the narrows waited — a gap barely wider than the raft, walled in rock rigged with enough explosive to move mountains, men on both sides raising rifles against the grey light.

Theo put his father's letter against his chest, felt the compass knock once against his ribs like a knock at a door, and paddled straight into the throat of the river.
