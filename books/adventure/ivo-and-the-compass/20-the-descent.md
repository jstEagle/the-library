## 20. The Descent

The gorge filled faster than anyone had promised.

By midnight the water had climbed the first three steps of the drowned bell tower and swallowed them again, and by two it was lapping at the old flood line — the stain on the rock that Theo had once believed was permanent, a scar the river wore like a signature. Now the river was writing over itself. The dynamite charges were already stacked at the narrows upstream; he could see the surveyors' lanterns moving along the rim like slow fireflies, men measuring a thing they intended to unmake. Come dawn, or shortly after, the whole upper gorge would come down in one deliberate roar, and everything the keeper had died to protect would be silt and rumor.

Which meant the copies had to go downstream tonight, on water that had never been navigable even when it was calm.

The raft was Ivo's design — three railway gangway planks lashed across two empty fuel drums from the station cellar, a rudder made from a signal lever, rope soaked in tallow so it wouldn't stiffen. It looked like something children would build and drown on. Petra tested the lashings twice, then a third time, and said nothing, which from Petra was a eulogy for the plan's dignity.

They loaded the documents into oilcloth bundles — nine of them, each wrapped around a core of wax-sealed tin, each sewn with a different name inside the lining: newspapers, the families of Vell Hollow, a junior archivist in the capital whose letter Petra had kept folded against her heart for eleven days. If any one bundle reached its name, the truth survived. That was the arithmetic they had built their hope on. Nine chances, no single point of failure.

Except the river didn't care about arithmetic.

* * *

Ivo stood at the top of the station steps while they carried the raft down to the water, and he was dressed for travel, which was how Theo knew, before anyone said anything, what the old man intended.

"You're not coming," Theo said.

"I'm too heavy for that raft and too old for that water." Ivo's voice was as level as it had been the night he'd named the compass by its maker's name, back when all of this had still seemed like a mystery instead of a debt. "But the hunters on the bank are watching this station. They've watched it since the day you arrived. A light burning here is worth more than a light anywhere else."

"Ivo—"

"The station has stood sixty years," he said. "It has done its work. Trains stopped coming, and it kept the lamp lit anyway, because a station's job is to tell people where they are." He pressed a canvas bag into Theo's hands — bread, a flask, a storm lantern with a shuttered hood. "I'll light every window and walk the platform with a lantern until you're past the second bend. They'll think you're still here. By the time they know better, you'll be somewhere they can't shoot straight."

"They'll burn it."

"Probably." He said it the way he might have said *probably rain*. "Buildings can be rebuilt. I rebuilt this one once already, after the flood. I know where every beam goes." Then, quieter, with his hand briefly on Theo's shoulder: "Your father stayed here one night, thirty-one years ago. Slept in that chair by the stove. Left before dawn, the way you people always do. I'm glad one of you finally came back."

Theo wanted to argue. There was no time to argue. Downstream, the water was rising through the dark with a sound like a freight train that never passed.

Maren came up beside him as Ivo walked back toward the station, and she watched the old man's lantern bob along the platform, and she said, softly, "He knows exactly what he's doing."

"He always did."

"That's what frightens me about him."

* * *

The gorge at night, flooded, was a country that did not exist on any map Theo had ever drawn.

The walls closed overhead until there was only a ribbon of sky scattered with stars, and the water beneath them moved in layers — a fast brown current on top, and beneath it, visible in the lantern's spill, the pale shapes of things the river had taken decades ago: fence posts, a wagon bed, the ridge of a roof sliding past six feet under the surface like the spine of some enormous sleeping animal. The drowned town of Vell Hollow passed beneath them in silence. Theo held the lantern low and watched the streets go by underneath, doorways and garden walls and the arch of the well where he had found the ledger with his father's name in it, all of it lit for a moment and then gone behind them, a neighborhood of ghosts waving off a departing train.

Petra handled the rudder. The two surviving team members — Aldous at the bow with a boat hook cut from a signal pole, Renn bailing constantly with a dented kettle — worked without speaking. Maren sat amidships with the document bundles strapped in a lattice around her body, her arms threaded through the ropes, because she had insisted: *if I go in, they stay with me.* Theo knelt beside her with the compass open on his knee.

It didn't point north. It never had. The needle swung slowly, patiently, toward the source they had left behind — toward the sealed chamber, the room of paper, the protest letter that had cost his father everything and the keeper who had given her life for an hour's head start. But now, with the chamber sealed and the hunters swarming above it, the needle trembled, uncertain, as if the thing it pointed toward were being shaken apart.

"Stop looking at it," Maren said.

"It's the only map we have."

"No." She nodded forward, into the dark. "That's the river. And the river doesn't like being followed — but tonight it likes us better than it likes them. We're going the way it wants to go."

The first shot came off the eastern bank a moment later — a flat crack that echoed three times off the stone, and then a splash a yard short of the bow.

* * *

They ran the rapids blind.

There was no other word for it. The flood had erased every feature Theo had memorized on the way up: the ledge that should have marked the millrace was ten feet underwater, the strainer of fallen pines at the second bend now floated and shifted with the current like a living gate. Petra steered by sound — by the change in the water's voice when it went shallow, by the hollow boom under the hull that meant a submerged wall — and Theo fed her what little the lantern showed, hooded, angled down, a coin of light on black glass.

Bullets came in flurries now, from both banks, hunters running the rim trail with lamps of their own. Theo could see them between the trees: small, jerking figures, silhouetted against the last stars, stopping to fire and running again. Once, close on the western bank, a lantern swung in a wide arc — signaling, coordinating — and Theo understood they weren't shooting to kill anymore. They were herding. Driving the raft toward the narrows below the third bend, where the gorge pinched to twenty feet and the water funneled through with a sound like tearing cloth, and where a dozen men with rifles could wait all night.

"We can't run the narrows," Aldous called back. "Not loaded like this."

"We can't stop," Petra answered.

Behind them, far upstream, a light bloomed against the clouds.

Theo turned. The glow came from the direction of the station — not a fire yet, but the whole building lit at once, every window blazing, the platform lamps lit, the great station lantern hoisted up the mast as it hadn't been since the line closed. Ivo's decoy, working exactly as designed. On both banks, the running figures stopped. Lamps converged toward the glow. The shooting faltered, then ceased, the hunters' attention pulled upstream like iron filings to a magnet.

"He's giving us the bends," Maren breathed. "All of them. Go — *go*, Petra, while they're watching him—"

Petra put the rudder hard over and the raft leapt.

For ninety seconds the gorge belonged to them alone. The water took them through the second bend at a speed that made speech impossible, spray sheeting over the bow, the drums groaning, Renn bailing in a frenzy, the bundles lashed to Maren's chest slapping wetly against the oilcloth. Theo laughed once — a single involuntary bark of pure animal relief — and Maren looked at him, and in the hooded lantern light he saw her actually smile, the first true one he'd ever seen on her, and he thought: *we're going to make it,* which was, he would understand later, the exact thought the river was waiting for.

The narrows opened ahead anyway. Of course they did. The hunters had left two men on the pinch point — two was enough — and as the raft swept into the funnel of stone, muzzle flashes strobed from both walls, close enough now that Theo could hear the rounds crack past rather than merely snap.

"Down! Everyone down!" Petra threw herself flat across the rudder. Aldous dropped the boat hook and covered his head. Theo grabbed Maren's shoulder and hauled, and she came down beside him — but not before turning, deliberately, deliberately, putting her back to the gunfire and her body over the lattice of bundles, curling around the documents the way a person curls around a child.

He heard the round hit her.

Not a crack this time — a dull, dense sound, like a fist on wet wood, and Maren made a noise he had never heard her make, small and utterly surprised, and her weight came against him all at once.

* * *

Time broke into pieces after that, and Theo lived in the gaps between them.

The raft through the narrows — a shriek of drums on stone, a wall of spray, Renn thrown half overboard and hauled back by his collar. Calm water beyond, black and broad, the gorge opening its throat. Maren in his arms, her breathing wrong, a wet heat spreading under his hands at her side, high, under the ribs. Her eyes open and fixed on him with ferocious clarity.

"The bundles," she said.

"Forget the bundles."

"Don't you dare—" She coughed, and it cost her visibly, her face going gray in the lantern light. "Nine names. Nine. You don't get to spend them on me."

"You're one of the nine," Theo heard himself say, and didn't know until he said it that it was true, that it had been true for longer than he'd admitted. "You're on the list. You're one of the things that has to survive tonight."

Aldous had the tiller now; Petra was pressing her jacket against Maren's side, hands slick, voice steady in the way of someone performing steadiness at full cost. The eastern bank slid past, dark, empty — the hunters drawn upstream, all of them, everyone except the two at the narrows, and those two were behind them now, firing at a target already shrinking into the dark.

And then the raft hit the submerged fence.

No one saw it. The old boundary of some farm the river had eaten forty years ago rose out of the flood like a reef, and the portside drum struck it with a sound like a bell, and the lashings — triple-knotted, tallow-soaked, Ivo's careful work — held, but the plank above them split lengthwise with a crack like a rifle shot, and the raft began, slowly, horribly, to fold.

The bundles' lattice strap was tied to that plank.

Theo saw it happen in the lantern light: the strap going taut, then fraying, the oilcloth bundles shifting, sliding toward the gap where the raft was opening like a mouth. Maren's hand shot out and caught the strap — her wounded arm, the arm on the side the bullet had torn through — and she held it, screaming through her teeth, holding nine names above the water with the arm that had just been shot.

And Theo understood, in one white-hot instant, the choice the night had been building toward. The raft was failing. It could carry weight or it could carry speed, not both. In the water, the bundles would sink within minutes even in oilcloth; the tins might hold, might not. Maren could not hold the strap and be hauled clear; hauling her meant letting go of the documents or dragging her under with them. Save her or save them. The river had arranged it neatly, the way it arranged everything, and every instinct he had inherited from his father — the man who had chosen the truth and lost everything, the man whose grave had no marker for thirty years — every instinct screamed that this was the moment the story demanded, the clean terrible arithmetic, the choice.

He refused it.

"There's no choosing!" he shouted, to Maren, to the river, to the hunters he knew were listening on the banks. "Nobody taught you people anything? You *refuse!*"

He tore the storm lantern from its bracket and wedged it between the splitting planks — a spar, a shim, flame licking the tallow-soaked wood and holding it together the way a stitch holds skin. He looped the fraying strap twice around his own forearm and took Maren's weight onto it, the rope biting to the bone, and he braced his boots against the drum and pulled her bodily across the widening gap while Petra hauled the rudder side flat and Aldous threw everything movable — the bail kettle, the bread bag, the boat hook — onto the high side for ballast. Renn got a shoulder under Maren's knees. Between the four of them, swearing and bleeding and bailing, they dragged her over the documents without letting either touch the water.

The raft limped. It did not die. The lantern burned down through the split plank like a candle through a knuckle of ice, and the river carried them, and the gorge walls fell away behind, and the sky opened wider than Theo had seen it in weeks.

Maren lay with her head in his lap, her hand pressed over the wound, conscious, furious, alive.

"You absolute idiot," she whispered.

"Yes."

"You could have lost everything."

"I nearly did." His forearm was striped red where the rope had cut it. He found her free hand and held it. "Both halves. That's the trick nobody tells you. Sometimes you just refuse the choice and pay for both, and sometimes — *sometimes* — the river lets it stand."

She looked up at him for a long moment. Then, weakly, with the ghost of the smile: "Your father would have made the noble choice."

"My father made the noble choice," Theo said. "Look where it got him. An unmarked grave and a son who couldn't sleep in the same town twice." He looked down at her, at the bundles lashed safely between their bodies, at the needle of the compass in his pocket trembling toward a source that would be rubble by morning but was, tonight, still pointing. "I'm done making choices the way this country teaches them. Somebody has to learn to want two things at once and refuse to give up either."

Ahead, around the final bend, the gorge ended. Beyond it lay the lowlands, the living towns, the world — and somewhere in it, nine names waiting for their letters.

Behind them, far upstream, the glow over the station changed color. Theo saw it and his chest seized — orange now, deepening, the silhouette of the water tower standing black against it like a headstone.

Ivo had said buildings could be rebuilt. Theo held onto that. He held onto it the way Maren had held the strap, with everything he had, because it was all there was.

Then the current took them out of the gorge, into the wide dark delta of the lower river, and the first lights of the downstream world appeared through the trees — and with them, moored across the channel like a drawn bolt across a door, a government patrol launch, its searchlight sweeping the water.

Renn whispered, "Oh, God."

Petra's hands tightened on the rudder.

Theo reached down and closed his fingers around his father's compass, and waited to see which way the needle would turn.
