## 1. The Mood of Sandbars

The river told you its temper before you ever saw the water. Quentin Joubert had learned that lesson thirty years ago, and he learned it again every morning, standing at the wheel of the *Joubert* with his coffee going cold in one hand and the other resting on the brass rail worn smooth by a decade of his own grip.

This morning the sandbars were in a mood.

You could read them from a mile off if you knew what to look for — and Quentin knew. A bar lying quiet showed itself as a long, pale seam just under the surface, the water sliding over it like silk over a knee. But a bar that meant trouble broke the current into a stutter, a chop, a brown confusion where the water argued with itself. This morning, three kilometers below the Nam Khan confluence, the water was arguing.

"Bar's shifted," Sefa said from the engine room hatch behind him. The old engineer's voice came up through the deck plates with the low rumble of the engine underneath it, two sounds braided together so long they'd become one voice. "Flood moved it east. You'll want the far channel."

"I see it."

"You always say you see it."

"And I always do."

Sefa grunted, which from him was an entire conversation, and disappeared back down into the heat and grease of the engine room. Quentin turned the wheel two spokes to starboard and felt the *Joubert* answer — slow, heavy, obedient in the way of old women and older boats, willing but not eager.

She was forty meters of riveted iron, built in Saigon before the war for a colonial shipping concern that no longer existed, and she carried her history the way he carried his: in patches, in welds, in the places where new metal had been laid over old wounds. Her cargo hold was stacked this morning with rice in sealed drums, diesel in tins, roofing tin, a crate of medicine bound for the mission upriver, and forty sacks of cement that would turn to stone if the rain got at them. Ordinary freight. Ordinary run. Vientiane to the trading posts above Luang Prabang and back, ten days if the river was kind, fourteen if it wasn't.

The river was never kind. It was only sometimes indifferent.

Quentin set his coffee on the chart shelf and let both hands have the wheel. Ahead, the channel narrowed between a bar that had migrated overnight and a limestone bluff that hadn't moved since the world was made. He threaded the gap with the particular attention of a man threading a needle while wearing mittens — the *Joubert* drew a meter and a half loaded, and the channel here drew two, which sounded generous until you remembered that rivers lie.

"Port side's shallow," came a voice from the bow. One of the twins — Kham, by the angle of him, though at distance they were interchangeable and they knew it and exploited it shamelessly. He stood at the rail with a bamboo sounding pole, reading the water ahead the way other men read newspapers. "Green water to the mark, then brown. I'll call the line."

"Call it."

Kham called it. His brother Noi echoed it from the stern, where he minded the depth gauge and the steering lines and, when he thought nobody watched, smoked thin cigarettes he wasn't supposed to have. The *Joubert* slid through the gap with half a meter to spare on either side, and Quentin allowed himself the small luxury of exhaling.

That was the whole art, really. Reading the argument in the water. Choosing the channel. Keeping the boat moving.

He'd had a rule about that for eleven years now, and it was painted into him deeper than the boat's name was painted onto her stern: *never stop moving*. Not because stopping was dangerous — though it was, moored boats attracted thieves and inspectors and worse — but because stopping gave a man time to think, and thinking, in Quentin Joubert's experience, was the most expensive activity on the river.

And the second rule, written nowhere because it needed no paint: *never take passengers*. Cargo didn't talk. Cargo didn't die on you. Cargo couldn't be carried in your arms to a mudbank grave while its eyes asked you why.

Ivo was testing the second rule again. Quentin could feel it the way he felt a bar shift — not seeing it yet, but reading the argument in the water.

* * *

The boy was sixteen now, though he'd come aboard at five, fished out of floodwater like a piece of driftwood that happened to be breathing. Eleven years, and Quentin still caught himself watching the boy the way he watched weather — alert for the change coming off him.

Ivo worked the deck without being told, which was the highest compliment Quentin paid anyone and the only one he ever said aloud, and rarely even that. This morning the boy was coiling line near the forward hatch, and he was coiling it badly, and he was coiling it badly *on purpose*, because bad coils took longer, and long coils meant staying near the hatch, and the hatch was where he'd hidden something.

Quentin had raised the boy himself, more or less, in the way a man raises anything on a working boat: by proximity, by example, by shouting when shouting was required and silence when it wasn't. He'd taught him knots and channels and how to read a bar's mood. He had not taught him what to do with tenderness, mostly because Quentin had mislaid his own somewhere around the year his wife stopped writing back, and you can't teach from an empty hand.

But the boy had found tenderness anyway, growing wild in him like banyan in a wall. And every few months, it flowered into exactly this kind of trouble.

At midday they tied up at Ban Pak-O, a fueling stop that was four stilt houses, a leaking pump, and a dog with opinions. While Sefa argued with the pump and the twins hauled diesel tins, Ivo vanished aft. Quentin, pretending to check the mooring lines, pretended not to follow.

Behind the stack of empty rice drums, in the shade that collected there like water in a hull's bilge, sat a child. A girl, maybe eight, maybe nine, hollow-cheeked, wearing a man's shirt belted with rope, sitting with her knees drawn up as if she'd been folded there and forgotten. She had the stillness of something that had learned not to be seen.

Ivo crouched in front of her with a bowl of rice and dried fish — galley rice, Sefa's rice, rice that would be missed and counted and asked after.

"You're doing it wrong," Quentin said.

Ivo didn't jump. That was new. A year ago he'd have jumped. Now he just looked up over his shoulder with those dark, level eyes and said, "The coil?"

"The rice."

"It was going to be thrown out."

"It was not."

"It should have been." The boy held his gaze, unflinching, and Quentin recognized the stubbornness in it because he saw it in the mirror every morning. "She's been on the pilings since last night. She came down from the hills. There's nothing up there right now — the soldiers burned the terraces in spring."

Quentin looked at the girl. She looked at him with the flat, calculating patience of the very young and very hungry, the look of someone deciding whether he was weather or shelter.

"Where are her people?" he asked.

"She says an aunt in Luang Prabang. Maybe. She says it like she doesn't believe it."

"Luang Prabang is two days upriver."

"I know where it is. I've been there." A pause, and then the boy played the card he'd been holding since dawn; Quentin could see it coming like a squall line. "We're going there anyway."

"No."

"We haven't even—"

"No passengers, Ivo. You know the rule."

"I know the rule." The boy rose, slowly, bowl still in his hands, and Quentin hated how tall he'd gotten, how the top of his head was nearly level with Quentin's chin now, how the arguments had stopped being a man talking down to a boy and started being two men talking across a gap. "I know the rule better than anyone. I've heard it my whole life. Never stop moving. Never take passengers. Don't get attached. Don't—" He stopped himself, jaw tightening, and Quentin watched him decide whether to say the rest of it. He said it. "Don't love anything, because everything either leaves or sinks."

The words landed harder than the boy probably intended. Or maybe he intended them exactly. Sixteen-year-olds aimed well and apologized never.

"That's not what I teach," Quentin said quietly.

"It's what you live."

Down at the pump, Sefa's voice rose in negotiation with the pump's owner, and the dog added its opinion, and the ordinary noise of the river went on around the two of them standing in drum-shade over a bowl of rice and a rule.

Quentin looked at the girl again. She hadn't moved. She was watching the bowl now, not the men — hunger narrowing the whole world to a single point. He knew that look. He'd worn it once himself, on a dock in another country, in another life, before the river, before the disgrace, before the name *Joubert* became something he wore instead of something he was given.

"Feed her," he said. "Then put her ashore at the headman's house. They'll see she eats tonight."

Ivo's face did something complicated — victory and disappointment fighting for the same ground. "And tomorrow?"

"Tomorrow we're gone before light."

"She can't walk to Luang Prabang."

"Not our problem."

The boy flinched as if struck. Then he nodded, once, and crouched, and put the bowl in the girl's hands, and stayed there while she ate, saying nothing, doing nothing, just being a shape between her and the rest of the world. Quentin watched from the edge of the shade and understood, with the tired clarity of a man who has been losing an argument for years, that he was watching himself — the self he'd been before, the one who pulled a drowning child out of brown water without asking whose problem it was.

He turned and walked back toward the wheelhouse, and his chest did the thing it did whenever the armor got tested — a slow ache, like a seam taking water.

* * *

They cast off at dusk, running the last hour of light to reach the anchorage below the rapids. The girl stood on the headman's steps as they pulled away, small against the lamplit stilts, and Ivo watched her until the bend took her, and then he stood looking at the bend a while longer, and Quentin let him.

Supper was fish soup and rice at the folding table in the galley, all five of them knee to knee, the lamp swinging gently with the current. The twins argued about whether a crocodile seen at Chom Cheng had been four meters or five, each inflating the number when the other wasn't listening. Sefa ate in silence with the efficiency of a man who considered meals maintenance. Ivo ate with his eyes on his bowl, quiet in a way that wasn't sulking but was adjacent to it.

"You did right today," Sefa said suddenly, to no one, to the table, to his soup. "Putting her ashore. Headman's a decent man. She'll eat."

Ivo looked up. "For tonight."

"For tonight," Sefa agreed. "Tonight counts. On this river, tonight is most of what anybody gets." He mopped his bowl with the last of his bread and stood, patting Ivo's shoulder once on the way out — a gesture so rare the twins exchanged a glance over it.

Later, when the others had turned in, Quentin found the boy on the foredeck, sitting against the winch housing with his knees up, exactly the way the girl had sat. Watching the water go black under the first stars.

Quentin lowered himself onto the hatch cover beside him, joints announcing their decades. For a while neither spoke. The river spoke enough for both of them — water on iron, the far cough of a night bird, the anchor chain's occasional complaint.

"When I found you," Quentin said, "the water was over the rooftops at Ban Sen. Whole village gone under. Bodies in the trees." He kept his eyes on the dark. "I was running cargo through it because cargo pays double in a flood. I wasn't looking for anyone. I wasn't looking for anything." A pause. "You were hung in a mango tree, holding on. You'd been there two days, by the marks on your hands. You didn't cry when I lifted you down. You just looked at me like you were deciding something."

"What was I deciding?"

"If I was weather or shelter."

Ivo was quiet a moment. "What did you decide I decided?"

"You decided I was shelter. Worst decision you ever made." He said it dry, but the boy heard the thing underneath it, because the boy always heard the thing underneath it, and that was the whole trouble with raising a child on a boat — eventually they learn to read you the way you read the river.

"Why do you keep moving, Captain? Really."

Quentin took his time. The honest answer sat in his chest like ballast, and he'd never once brought it up on deck. "Because a boat that stops starts taking on questions," he said finally. "And some questions sink you faster than any hole in the hull."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I'm selling."

Ivo almost smiled. Almost. Then he lay back against the winch housing and looked up at the stars coming through the monsoon haze, and said, to the sky more than to Quentin: "There was a man at Pak-O this morning. Before we docked. In a canoe, at the pilings. Sick-looking. He asked me if the *Joubert* was going upriver, and I said yes, and he said—" the boy frowned, retrieving it, "—he said, 'Tell your captain I'll pay whatever passage costs. Tell him I'm paying with gold.'"

Quentin turned his head. "Gold."

"That's what he said. He was feverish, shaking. He said he'd be at the landing at Muang Ngoi. Two days from now." The boy shrugged, elaborately casual, a performance so transparent it was almost touching. "I told him we don't take passengers."

"Good."

"I told him that." A pause. "I also told him we'd be there."

Quentin closed his eyes. Somewhere below them, in the dark water, a sandbar lay in wait, patient as everything else on this river, and the current ran on toward Muang Ngoi carrying a boat, a crew, a boy who fed strays, and a promise Quentin hadn't made and already knew he wouldn't keep.

"Two days," he said, to no one in particular.

The river said nothing. It never did. It just kept arguing with itself around the bars, and kept moving, and expected everyone aboard to do the same.
