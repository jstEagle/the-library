## 2. A Dying Man's Fare

The landing at Ban Houayxay announced itself by smell before sight — mud and woodsmoke and the sweet rot of fruit nobody would buy. Quentin brought the *Joubert* in against the current with the engine idling low, Sefa's hands doing their quiet arithmetic below, and let the bow nudge the mud bank the way an old man leans into a doorframe he has leaned into ten thousand times.

"Ten minutes," he called down to the deck. "Cargo out, cargo in, no conversation."

The twins, Kham and Noi, were already moving, barefoot on wet planks, passing sacks of dried fish and bolts of cloth down to the men waiting on the bank. They worked like two halves of one animal, never speaking, never needing to. Ivo stood at the rail with the manifest board under his arm, ticking items in his careful schoolroom hand, and Quentin watched him the way he watched weather — without appearing to.

The boy was fourteen now. Three years since the flood, three years since Quentin had hauled a half-drowned child out of a brown torrent that had taken everything else the boy had ever owned, and in that time Ivo had grown long in the limbs and quiet in the mouth, which was the opposite of what most boys did and exactly what Quentin had hoped for. Quiet boys didn't ask questions. Quiet boys didn't make promises. Quiet boys stayed on a boat that kept moving.

Mostly.

Quentin stepped onto the landing himself, because he always did — a captain's boots on a captain's business — and was negotiating the price of diesel with a fuel seller whose smile had too many teeth in it when he saw the man at the far end of the planks.

He'd been there a while, Quentin guessed. Long enough for the mud to have climbed him to the knees. He sat propped against a mooring post like laundry someone had forgotten, a canvas roll clutched to his chest the way a drunk clutches a bottle, and his skin had gone the color of old paper left out in the rain. Fever. The bad kind, the kind that came up from the forest and didn't leave until it had emptied you.

As Quentin watched, the man tried to stand, failed, and tried again, and on the second attempt managed to get one hand out toward the boat.

"Captain." His voice was gravel dragged through water. "Captain. Passage."

Quentin turned back to the fuel seller. "You said forty."

"I said forty-five."

"You said forty before you looked at my boat and decided I could afford forty-five." He counted out the notes slowly, letting the man watch each one go, and made him sign the chit with a pencil stub Quentin carried for exactly this purpose. Behind him, the prospector's voice came again, thinner.

"Captain. Please. Upstream. As far as you'll take me."

"We're not taking passengers," Quentin said, without turning around.

"I can pay."

"Then pay the ferryman at the public dock. They take passengers."

"They take passengers who can walk to the public dock." A cough then, deep and wet, that bent the man double over his canvas roll. When he straightened, there was blood on his lips and something bright and desperate in his eyes that Quentin recognized and hated, because he had seen it in mirrors once, years ago, in another life. "I don't need comfort. Floor space. A corner. I'll be dead inside a week either way — all I'm asking is which riverbank it happens on."

That landed harder than it should have. Quentin finished tying off the last of the cargo lines and made himself look at the man properly. Sixty, maybe, or fifty worn down to sixty. A French army coat gone green with mold, cut down and resewn by someone who'd cared about him once. Boots held together with wire. And the canvas roll, which he shifted as Quentin approached, not hiding it but guarding it, the way a mother guards a child even when she's too weak to lift it.

"My name is Vann," the man said. "I was a surveyor. Before. During." He gestured vaguely upstream, at the whole war-soaked length of the river, as if it were a country he could point to on a map. "I know things about this river that the French paid me to find and then paid other men to forget."

"Not interested."

"I haven't told you what they are yet."

"Don't need to. Whatever a dying man knows about this river, it's worth nothing to me except trouble, and trouble is the one cargo I don't carry." Quentin crouched so they were eye to eye, because he wasn't cruel, whatever else he was. "There's a dispensary two streets up. Tell them Joubert sent you. They'll give you quinine whether or not you can pay, because the nun who runs it doesn't believe in debts."

"The nun will give me a bed to die in." Vann's laugh turned into another cough. "I've had beds. What I want is water moving under me. You spend thirty years walking rivers and see if you don't understand." His hand shot out and caught Quentin's wrist — surprisingly strong, the grip of a man whose body hadn't yet told his mind the news. "One day. One day upriver and put me off wherever you like. I'll pay you more than your boat earns in a season."

"With what?"

Vann fumbled at the canvas roll with shaking fingers, and Quentin should have walked away right then. He knew it even as he stood there. But there's a kind of curiosity that operates below decision, below discipline, in the flooded cellar of a man where he keeps the things he doesn't look at, and Quentin stood still while the roll unrolled across the mud-stained planks.

Not gold. Not money. Paper. Dozens of sheets of paper covered in fine drafting work — contour lines, soundings, channel marks — and one sheet in particular that Vann smoothed flat with both palms as gently as if it were skin.

"One map," Vann said. "Drawn from memory, but memory is the only honest cartography left. Nineteen fifty-three. A colonial barge went down in the Nam Ou gorge with a cargo the French never entered in any ledger. Gold bullion, coin, ingots being pulled out ahead of the collapse. It sank. They wrote it off. Nobody living knows where except me, because I surveyed the wreck site and then I kept my mouth shut for twenty years, which is why I am dying poor in a place like this instead of rich in a place like anywhere else."

Quentin looked at the map. He couldn't help it. Even upside down, even at arm's length, he could read water the way other men read faces, and the drawing was good — the work of a real hand, a patient hand.

And every instinct he owned, every scarred-over lesson of the last fifteen years, stood up at once and said: *walk away.*

"There's no such thing as sunken treasure that costs less than it pays," Quentin said. He rolled the map closed and pushed it back against Vann's chest. "Gold sinks boats. Not just the barge. Every boat that goes looking afterward. Keep your map. Die somewhere soft."

He stood and turned and walked back up the planks, and behind him Vann said nothing, which was worse than arguing. Silence from a dying man has weight. It follows you.

* * *

Loading took eight minutes longer than it should have because the twins found a crate of tinned milk that had to be argued over, and by the time Quentin got back aboard the sun had climbed high enough to turn the landing into a griddle. He was checking the stern line when he heard Sefa's voice from the engine room hatch, pitched low in that way the engineer had of saying something important while pretending not to.

"Captain. Your boy's gone over the side."

Quentin's stomach dropped the way it always did, three years of parenting compressed into one cold second. "Gone where?"

"Down the bank. Toward the end of the landing. Carrying the water jug." Sefa wiped his hands on a rag that had been black since before Ivo was born. "Thought you'd want to know before you found out some other way."

Quentin found them at the far end of the dock, in the strip of shade under a collapsed awning where the mooring posts threw their shadows like bars. Ivo was sitting cross-legged in the mud beside Vann, holding the tin cup to the old man's mouth with both hands, careful as a nurse, tilting it just enough. The prospector drank in small sips, his eyes closed, and between sips he talked — and Ivo listened.

Quentin stopped at the edge of the shade and did not announce himself.

He heard fragments. Something about a daughter. *Linh.* Something about Luang Prabang, said twice, the name handled like something fragile. Something about *they followed me from Vientiane,* and then Vann's voice dropping, and Ivo leaning closer, and whatever was said next was for the boy alone.

The water jug sat between them, already half empty. Quentin had rationed that water himself that morning, marked the level with a grease pencil, because monsoon was late this year and the tributaries were running low and a captain who didn't count his water didn't deserve his boat.

He should have shouted. Should have marched down there, taken the cup, hauled the boy back by the collar, and delivered the speech he'd delivered before: *we don't stop, we don't carry strangers, we don't take on other people's dying.* The speech had kept them alive. The speech had kept *him* alive, which was different and more important, though he'd never say so.

Instead he stood in the heat and watched a feverish old man tell stories to his son — because that was the word, whatever the papers never said, whatever the adoption had never quite formalized; the boy was his son the way the river was his road — and watched Ivo's face do something it almost never did. Soften. Open. The boy laughed once, a small startled bark, at something Vann said, and the old man smiled with bloody lips like he'd been given a gift.

Compassion, Quentin thought. That's the crack in the hull that sinks you. Not rocks, not storms. Rocks and storms you can read. It's the soft thing, the warm thing, the moment you let somebody's dying become your business.

But he didn't shout. He stood there until the cup was empty and Vann's eyes drifted shut and Ivo eased the old man's head down onto the folded canvas roll — the maps, Quentin noted, still clutched there, guarded even in sleep — and then the boy gathered the cup and the jug and stood, and turned, and saw him.

Ivo didn't flinch. That was new too. There was a time the boy would have shrunk like a plant under a hot sky. Now he just stood in the mud with the water jug against his chest and met his captain's eyes and waited for judgment.

"He's dying," Ivo said. Not pleading. Stating. As if that settled something.

"People die on land all the time. It's where dying happens."

"He asked for the river."

"The river doesn't want him. The river has enough." Quentin crossed the planks, took the jug, weighed it in his hand, and felt the missing water like a debt. "You gave away our ration."

"Yes."

"You didn't ask."

"No." The boy's chin came up a centimeter. "You'd have said no."

"I would have said no."

"So I didn't ask." And there it was — not defiance, exactly. Something steadier. The boy had done the arithmetic himself and accepted the cost in advance, which was more than most grown men Quentin had shipped with ever managed. "He has a daughter. In Luang Prabang. She thinks he's been dead for six years. He wants—" Ivo stopped, and Quentin watched him decide how much to give away, and watched him choose honesty anyway. "He wants to get home before he dies. That's all. Not gold. Just home."

"That's not what he offered me."

"It's what he wants."

Quentin looked past the boy, down the dock, at the shape under the collapsed awning. The fever had him now, visibly — even at this distance Quentin could see the shivering, the way the man's hands plucked at the canvas roll like a bird rearranging its feathers. Two days, maybe three. Less, if the fever was the bad strain.

If he took him aboard, the man would die on his deck. That was near certain. And a death on the *Joubert* meant questions — police, paperwork, a customs officer with a rubber stamp and an appetite. Fifteen years of keeping his head below the skyline, undone by one act of charity.

If he left him here, the boy would remember it. That was also certain. Boys keep ledgers too, quieter ones, and Ivo's ledger had a page for every kindness Quentin had shown and would now show a blank page where mercy should have been.

"Back aboard," Quentin said finally. "Both of us. We cast off in five minutes."

Ivo's face fell — and then Quentin added, "We'll take him as far as Pak Beng. Put him off at the mission there. They have a doctor who owes me." He held up a finger before the boy could speak. "That is the whole distance. Not Luang Prabang. Not wherever the map says the gold sleeps. Pak Beng, and done. And he rides in the hold with the cargo, and if he dies aboard, we bury him at the first bank and log it as a drowning, because I am not spending a week explaining a corpse to customs men. Are those terms understood?"

Ivo blinked. Then, slowly, he nodded — and the look on his face was so nakedly grateful that Quentin had to turn away from it, the way you turn from a lamp after a long dark.

"And Ivo."

"Captain?"

"The water comes out of your share for a week. Rationing means something or it means nothing."

"Yes, Captain." But the boy was smiling as he said it, trying to hide it and failing, and Quentin walked back up the dock ahead of him so he wouldn't have to see it, because seeing it made something move in his chest that he had spent fifteen years boarding up.

* * *

They got Vann aboard by main strength, the twins taking an arm each while Sefa cleared a space between crates of tinned milk. The old man weighed nothing. That was the worst part — lifting him, Quentin felt the bones through the rotten coat like carrying a bundle of kindling, and thought: *this is what the river does to the men it finishes with. It weighs them first.*

They got him laid on a mat in the hold, in the corner where the hull was driest, and Sefa appeared with a thermometer that had no business working and a bottle of quinine tablets he claimed he'd been saving, though Quentin suspected the engineer simply hoarded medicine the way he hoarded everything, against a future only he believed in.

"Fever's at the top of the scale," Sefa reported, squinting at the glass. "Whatever's left of him, the fever's eating it. Two days. Maybe."

"Maybe three," Quentin said. "Pak Beng is thirty hours at cruising speed if the current cooperates."

Sefa looked at him over the thermometer, and there was something in the engineer's face Quentin couldn't read — or could, and didn't want to. "Thirty hours," Sefa repeated, noncommittal, and went back below.

By evening they were underway, the *Joubert* pushing upriver through water gone amber with the first runoff, and Quentin stood at the wheel long past his own watch because standing at the wheel was the one place where thinking wasn't required. The banks slid by. Fishing lights came on downstream like a constellation falling into the water.

Once, near full dark, he went down to the hold to check the lashings and found Ivo there, sitting on an overturned crate beside the mat, reading aloud. Not a book — there were no books. He was reading the manifest, item by item, in his serious voice, and Vann's eyes were open and fixed on the ceiling beams, following the sound the way a man follows music, and every few items the old man would murmur something — *ah, condensed milk, my wife lived on that during the evacuation* — and drift, and surface, and drift again.

Quentin stood in the shadow of the stairwell and didn't interrupt.

"—forty sacks of dried fish, consigned to Tha Suang," Ivo read, "twelve bolts of indigo cloth—"

"A good color, indigo," Vann murmured. "It hides the stains. Everything important on a river should be indigo." A pause, long enough that Quentin thought the man had gone under again. Then, faintly: "Boy. Come here. Closer."

Ivo leaned in.

"What I told you on the dock. About my daughter. About the rest of it." The old man's hand found the canvas roll and drew it against his chest. "You remember it all? Word for word?"

"Word for word."

"Good." Vann's eyes closed. "Because paper burns, and men like the ones following me are very good with fire. Remember it in your head, where they can't reach." His breathing changed, going shallow and fast, and then, on the edge of sleep, so quietly Quentin nearly missed it: "She has her mother's patience. She'll wait for you. Tell her... tell her the current keeps what the current takes..."

The words trailed into fever-muttering, into French, into a language Quentin didn't know, and Ivo sat back on his crate and looked at the old man for a long time with an expression Quentin recognized because he wore it himself, years ago, in another life, looking at another dying man.

It was the expression of a boy who had just been handed something heavy and had decided, silently, in the flooded cellar where he kept the things he didn't look at, to carry it.

Quentin climbed back to the wheelhouse and put the *Joubert*'s nose at the dark water and told himself the tightness in his chest was just the engine noise.

Upstream, thirty hours to Pak Beng. He'd put the old man off at the mission and be rid of the whole affair — the map, the fever, the daughter, the followers Vann claimed were behind him, the words the boy was memorizing in the dark.

He was already lying to himself, and some part of him, the part that read water, knew it. Because he'd seen the tracks on the landing that afternoon, before they cast off. Boot prints at the end of the dock. Two sets. Fresh. Facing upriver.

Somebody was asking after Vann. And somebody was asking in the direction the *Joubert* had gone.
