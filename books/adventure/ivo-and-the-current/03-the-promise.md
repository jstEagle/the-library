## 3. The Promise

Ivo did not sleep. He told himself it was the heat, the way the river held the day's warmth against its skin and breathed it back at them all night, thick as a hand over the mouth. But that was a lie, and he was old enough now — fourteen, nearly fifteen — to know when he was lying to himself.

The prospector lay in the stern under the cargo tarp, where Quentin had grudgingly allowed him to be carried as far as the next landing and no farther. *No farther*, Ivo had heard him say through the bulkhead, in the voice he used for rules: flat, final, like a door closing on a room you were not invited into. Vann had been brought aboard at dusk with his fever burning so high Ivo could feel it from a foot away, radiating off him like the engine room bulkhead after a long run. His skin had gone the color of old paper. His lips had cracked open and wept a thin pink fluid.

Sefa had looked at him for exactly three seconds and then said, quietly, to no one in particular, "This man is dying."

Quentin had said nothing. That was worse than anything he could have said.

Now the boat sat moored against the bank below a stand of bamboo, engines cold, because Quentin would not run the night water with a dead or dying man aboard and no moon to read the water by. Even his rules bent a little, Ivo thought. Even iron has a temperature at which it softens.

Ivo slid out of his bunk barefoot. The deck boards were warm under his feet, then cool where the tarpaulin shadow fell. He knew every board of this boat the way other boys knew their own hands. He knew which one creaked under the twins' weight even when they tried to sneak, knew the spot near the rail where the varnish had worn through to gray wood from years of Quentin standing there, watching the water come at him and never once looking back.

Vann was awake. His eyes found Ivo in the dark with an accuracy that startled him — feverish men were supposed to wander, but this man's gaze came straight to him like a fish rising to a fly.

"You," Vann whispered. "The boy who gave me water."

"You should sleep."

"I have done my sleeping." He tried to laugh and it turned into a cough that bent him double, and Ivo held the tin cup to his mouth until the spasm passed. When Vann lifted his head again there was blood on his chin, small and bright as berries. "Sit. Sit down, boy. My eyes are bad but not so bad I can't see a boy who wants something."

"I don't want anything."

"Everyone wants something." Vann's voice was a dry rasp, but underneath it Ivo could hear something else — a patience, a stillness, the way Sefa went quiet around machinery he respected. "You want to know why your captain refused me. You want to know what I carry that made two men chase me down a river with knives. And you want—" he paused, and his cracked lips pulled into something almost like a smile, "—you want permission. To care about a stranger. And someone taught you that permission is never given."

Ivo sat down on the deck across from him. The river moved past them in the dark, making its endless low argument with the bank.

* * *

Vann talked for a long time, in pieces, the way dying men talk — a sentence here, a silence there, the story surfacing and diving like something swimming beneath ice. He had been a prospector for thirty years, first for the French, then for himself. He had worked the tributaries no map bothered to name. He had a daughter in Luang Prabang, Linh, whom he had not seen in four years because four years ago he had found something in the water and had not been able to stop looking for more of it.

"There is a barge," he said. "Sank in the war years, up past the second gorge. Everyone knows the story — colonial gold, French pay chest, lost with all hands. Treasure hunters have dragged that stretch of river for twenty years. They find beer bottles and old boots." His breath caught. "The gold is real. But the gold is not what matters."

"What matters?"

"The barge carried more than coin." His hand crept along the deck planking toward a oilskin packet tucked inside his shirt, and stopped, as if the last distance were too far to cross without help. "It carried ledgers. Sealed ones. Records of everything the customs service confiscated in those years — and records of what never reached the warehouses. Men signed those pages. Men whose names are still powerful. There are families on this river today whose houses were built out of goods taken from boats like this one, from people who drowned asking questions."

Ivo felt the hair rise on his arms. Not from cold.

"And you have proof? Where it sank?"

"I spent six years finding it. Six years of bribing old pilots, of diving in water that wanted me dead, of marking depths and landmarks on paper." The smile again, terrible this time. "And then I made the mistake of telling one man. One. A partner I thought I could trust. By morning everyone who deals in secrets knew there was a map, and the men who need those ledgers to stay drowned began to follow me." He coughed again, longer, and Ivo watched the blood come brighter. "They will keep following. Until the map burns or until I do."

"Then give it to me," Ivo said, before he knew he was going to say it. "Give it to me and get off at the next landing and disappear somewhere they can't—"

"Boy." Vann's hand closed weakly around Ivo's wrist. His grip was astonishing, for a dying man — or maybe it wasn't strength at all, maybe it was just that Ivo didn't pull away. "Look at me. Do I look like a man who disappears?"

The truth of it sat between them. Ivo looked at the paper-colored skin, the breath whistling now with a wet rattle at the bottom of each inhale, and could not lie to him.

"My daughter," Vann said. "Linh. She teaches at the school by the temple in Luang Prabang, on the street with the frangipani trees. She thinks I am a fool and she is right, but she is my fool's daughter and she deserves the truth of how her father died. Not the version the customs office will write." His eyes were very clear now, clearer than they'd been all day, the way a lamp burns brightest just before the oil is gone. "The map goes to her. No one else. Not to buyers, not to officials, not to heroes. To Linh. Can you do that?"

"Why me?"

"Because you gave me water when your captain said no. Because you listened. Because—" and here his voice dropped to almost nothing, and Ivo had to lean close to catch it, "—because you have the face of a boy who was pulled out of a flood, and I think you know better than anyone what it means when something valuable comes out of the water alive."

Ivo's throat closed. Nobody talked about that day. Quentin least of all. It was the one sealed room in the house of their life together, and this stranger had put his hand on the door within hours of coming aboard.

"How do you know about—"

"A riverman hears things. Even a dying one." Vann's grip tightened. "Will you promise? Say it plainly. Dying men can't afford riddles."

The river argued with the bank. Somewhere upstream a night bird called, three notes, like a question asked twice and then abandoned.

"I promise," Ivo said. "The map goes to Linh. In Luang Prabang. To her hands only."

Something left Vann then — Ivo saw it go, saw the tension drain out of his shoulders and his head settle back against the coiled rope behind him, and for a moment Ivo thought it was already over. But Vann fumbled at his shirt with fingers gone clumsy, and Ivo helped him draw out the oilskin packet. It was smaller than Ivo expected, lighter, tied with waxed cord. It fit in his palm like a bird.

"Inside. The map. Depths, landmarks, the bend where she lies." Vann's breathing was shallow and fast now. "There is a mark in the corner — a circle with a line through it. That is mine. Show it to Linh and she will know it is true, because I drew that same mark on every letter I ever sent her, and she keeps them all, even the ones that only said *still alive, don't wait for me.*"

He pressed the packet into Ivo's hands and folded Ivo's fingers shut over it, both of his hands around the boy's one, and for a moment the fever-heat of him seemed to pass entirely into the packet, into Ivo's palm, and stay there.

"You didn't ask your captain's leave," Vann said. There was no accusation in it. Almost approval.

"No."

"Good. Some promises are too heavy to carry through a crowd. You have to pick them up alone, or not at all." His eyes drifted toward the dark water, and his voice went dreamy, distant. "My father took me out on the river when I was your age. Told me the current doesn't remember anyone. I hated him for saying it. Spent my whole life trying to prove him wrong." A pause. The rattle in his chest was louder. "Turns out he was right. But he forgot to say the current remembers what we *carry*. That part sinks slow. That part waits."

His eyes came back to Ivo one last time, focused, urgent.

"Hide it well. Not where you'd hide it. Where *he* wouldn't look. Your captain is a good man wrapped in a locked door — he'll take it from you to protect you, and the taking will break something in you both. Don't let him. Promise me that too."

"I promise."

"Then I am done." Vann smiled, and it was the first whole smile Ivo had seen on him, unbroken by coughing or pain. "Water," he said. "You gave me water. That was enough. Most people don't even do that much."

Ivo gave him water again, holding the cup while he drank in small, painful swallows, and then Vann slept — really slept, or something like it — and Ivo sat in the dark holding the packet against his chest, listening to the labored rhythm of the dying man's breath and the easy rhythm of the river, and could not tell which sound frightened him more.

* * *

Dawn came up gray and slow, the mist lying on the water in long ropes. Ivo had not moved from the stern. At some point the breathing beside him changed — the rattle smoothed out, the spaces between breaths lengthened, and then, in the moment when the first light touched the water and turned it briefly to hammered tin, the spaces became everything, and there were no more breaths at all.

Ivo sat very still.

He had seen death before — fish, birds, once a deckhand on another boat, years ago, before his time aboard the Joubert. But he had never been the one sitting next to it, keeping watch, being the last person a man spoke to. It felt less like an ending than like a change of current: the man was still moving, but now he moved somewhere Ivo could not follow, and the packet in Ivo's hands was the only thing that had crossed over with him.

Quentin came aft as the light strengthened. Ivo heard him before he saw him — the particular weight of his step, unhurried, deliberate, a man who never arrived anywhere he hadn't already decided to arrive. He stood over them for a long moment. Ivo watched his captain's face do something complicated and quick and then become nothing at all, smooth as closed water.

"He went in the night?" Quentin asked.

"At dawn. Just now."

Quentin crouched. He touched two fingers to Vann's throat anyway, out of some old professional habit, and held them there longer than he needed to. Then he straightened and looked downriver, toward the landing they'd reach by midmorning, where there would be forms to fill and questions to answer and a body to surrender to strangers.

"We'll turn him over at the next post," he said. "Customs will want a statement. You'll say he came aboard dying, we did what we could, nothing else. Nothing else, Ivo. Do you understand me?"

*I promised him,* Ivo thought. *I promised him you'd try to take it, and I promised him you wouldn't.*

"Yes, Captain," he said aloud.

Quentin's eyes came down to him, sharp, and for one suspended second Ivo was certain the packet's weight showed through his shirt like a bone through skin. But Quentin only nodded once and went forward to wake Sefa, and the morning continued, and the river continued, and Ivo stood at the rail with a dead man's promise folded against his ribs.

* * *

They buried the problem of the body quickly — the landing had a customs clerk with a rubber stamp and no curiosity, and money changed hands, and Vann became a line in a ledger, which Ivo thought was probably the cruelest thing that could happen to a man who had died carrying the truth in his shirt. Quentin paid for a proper cloth for wrapping. He didn't say anything about it. He didn't have to.

By afternoon they were running again, the banks sliding past, the twins laughing about something forward, Sefa's engine beating its steady heart-note under the deck. Everything ordinary. Everything exactly as it had been yesterday, except that Ivo now carried a secret the size of a river.

He had spent the whole day deciding where to hide the packet. Not where *he'd* hide it, Vann had said. Where Quentin wouldn't look. He considered the bilge — no, Sefa lived down there. Under his mattress — no, Quentin inspected bunks; he called it fire safety but Ivo knew it was a habit from whatever prison-shaped years Quentin never spoke of. The flour tin — too obvious. The toolbox — too obvious.

In the end he hid it inside the thing Quentin trusted most and therefore never examined: the ship's log itself. He slipped the oilskin packet flat between the pages at the back, beneath a year of Quentin's own entries — depths, sandbars, fuel counts, weather. *The current remembers what we carry,* Vann had said. Well. Now the log carried it too, right under the captain's hand, in the one book on this boat Quentin opened every single day and never once suspected.

That night, at supper, Quentin looked at him across the table for a long time.

"You barely spoke today," Quentin said. "You're barely eating."

"I'm tired."

"Mm." Quentin turned his cup slowly on the wood. "The man in the stern. Did he talk to you?"

The question hung there. Ivo felt the packet's absence like a tooth — the constant pressure of the space where it used to be, hidden in the chartroom, waiting.

"He talked," Ivo said. "About his daughter. About the river." He met Quentin's eyes and did not look away, and hated himself for how easily the lie came, and understood suddenly that this was what growing up meant: not doing what you were told, but learning exactly what it cost. "Mostly he just wanted water."

Quentin studied him a moment longer, and Ivo watched something move behind his captain's eyes — suspicion, maybe, or memory, or the old locked-door sadness — and then Quentin let it go, the way he let most things go, by pretending they had never existed.

"Sleep," Quentin said. "We make the lock at Vientiane tomorrow. Long day."

Ivo slept badly. Twice he woke and went to the chartroom just to touch the logbook, to feel the flat shape of the packet through the canvas cover, to confirm it was real and still there and still his to carry. Both times the boat breathed around him in the dark — hull timbers ticking, water slapping the strakes, Sefa's banked engine clicking as it cooled — and both times he stood there in the blackness and made the promise again, silently, word for word as he'd sworn it: *to her hands only.*

On the third waking, near dawn, he heard the twins whispering forward and Sefa's footsteps overhead, and none of it mattered, because none of them knew. He was alone with it, the way Vann had said he'd have to be. Alone with a dead man's map and a living man's rules, and tomorrow they would tie up at Vientiane, where the customs officers wore clean uniforms and asked questions in soft voices.

Ivo lay in his bunk and stared at the deck above him and thought: *they're going to ask about him. Someone always asks.*

And far downstream, though he couldn't have known it yet, someone already was.
