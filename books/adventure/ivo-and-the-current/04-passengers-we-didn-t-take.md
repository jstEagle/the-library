## 4. Passengers We Didn't Take

Vientiane announced itself the way it always did — a smear of smoke over the left bank, then the long concrete apron of the customs quay sliding out of the haze like the jaw of something patient. I cut the engine to half and let the current carry us in sideways, the way I liked to arrive anywhere: slow, uncommitted, ready to leave.

"Fuel first," Sefa said at my shoulder, wiping his hands on a rag that had been black so long it was more oil than cloth. "Then water. Then we go."

"We go when the paperwork's done."

"The paperwork's done. It's always done. You sign your own name and we're ghosts again."

I almost smiled. Sefa had been making me into a ghost for eleven years, and he was good at it. The *Joubert* ran cargo up the tributaries with her papers clean and her history scrubbed, and no one on any landing between here and the Chinese border could tell you where she slept at night, because she didn't.

Ivo was on the foredeck coiling line with the twins, and I watched him without meaning to. He'd been quiet since the muddy landing two days back. Quiet in a particular way — the way water is quiet above a submerged snag. He'd given that dying prospector water against my orders, sat with him through the night, and now he moved like a boy carrying something heavier than rope. I told myself it was grief. Boys his age caught grief easily; it was one of the few things they could catch from strangers.

The mooring lines went out. The twins took them, Kham forward and Khone aft, moving like mirror images the way they always did, and I stepped down onto the quay with my manifest folder under my arm and my face arranged into the expression customs men expect: bored, honest, slightly resentful of paperwork.

There were two men waiting at the head of the gangway.

That was the first wrong thing. Customs at Vientiane worked from the office — you came to them, hat in hand, folder open. They didn't stand at your boat before you'd tied off. But these two had been standing there long enough that their shadows had shifted, and the taller one held a leather folio the way a man holds a weapon he's licensed to carry.

"Captain Joubert." Not a question. The tall one said my name like he'd practiced it, or like he'd read it off a file. "Customs and Excise. We'd like to look at your boat."

"You can look at my papers." I held out the folder. "Boat's busy."

The shorter one laughed — a quick, wet sound, like he found everything funny because nothing was ever going to touch him. The tall one didn't laugh. He took the folder, opened it, and read it slower than there was anything in it to read. I used that time to do what I do, which is look at people properly.

The short one was muscle gone soft, a pistol-shaped bulge under his jacket that he wanted noticed. Local hire, or hired local, boots wrong for the river. The tall one was different. Tall, gray at the temples, a face built for offices but worn at the edges like he'd spent years outdoors anyway. Sunburned in an old pattern. Hands that had done fieldwork once and resented desks ever since. And a voice —

I knew the voice before I placed the face. That happens sometimes. A voice gets filed deeper than a face, because a voice is what follows you out of a room.

"— manifest lists rice, diesel drums, roofing tin," the tall man was saying. "And before that? Vientiane southbound, then north again. You run this stretch often, Captain?"

"Often enough to eat."

"And passengers?" He looked up then, and I made the mistake of meeting his eyes, and eleven years fell on me all at once.

Ravelin.

He'd put on weight and lost hair and gained the kind of authority that comes with a stamp, but the eyes were the same — pale, unhurried, the eyes of a man who has never once needed to raise his voice because other men raised it for him. Ravelin, who had stood on a dock in another life and watched them take my license, my boat, my name, while he adjusted his cuffs. Ravelin, whose signature was on the conviction that killed my wife as surely as any fever, though no court would ever write it that way.

"Captain?" he said.

"No passengers." My voice came out level. Eleven years of practice went into that levelness. "Never take them. Passengers complain, passengers sue, passengers drown and their families ask questions. Cargo doesn't talk."

"A wise policy." He handed the folder back, and our fingers didn't touch, and I hated how carefully he made sure of it. "Though I'm told you broke it recently. At a landing downriver — Ban something, the mud flats. A man came aboard. Sick man. Prospector, by trade."

The river went very loud inside my head. *Vann.* His body was forty kilometers behind us, buried in a shallow grave Ivo and the twins had dug at dawn while I stood watch and pretended not to count the shovelfuls.

"I've had no passengers," I said. "Sick or otherwise."

"He would have offered you something." Ravelin's tone didn't change. It never changed. That was the thing about him — he spoke about everything, even ruin, in the voice of a man discussing freight rates. "Men like that always have something to sell. Maps, usually. Stories dressed up as maps. Did he offer you a map, Captain?"

"There was a sick man on the landing," I said. "Begging passage from every boat that stopped. Half the river saw him. He wasn't mine."

"But you talked to him."

"I talk to everyone who talks to me. It's cheaper than fighting."

Something flickered at the corner of Ravelin's mouth. Not a smile. Recognition, maybe — the pleasure of a man finding an old tool still sharp. "May we board? Just a walk through. Standard inspection."

"It's not standard," said the soft one, grinning. "But we're doing it anyway."

I thought about the layout of my boat. The engine room where Sefa slept among his machines. The hold with its false floor I'd built the year after my conviction, deep enough to hide a man, a crate, or a past. The foredeck where Ivo had been sitting ten minutes ago — and where Ivo was not sitting now, and hadn't been since the twins first called down that men were waiting. Where was Ivo? What was he doing below decks with that careful, guilty quiet of his?

*No,* I told myself. *He's a boy. He's grieving. He isn't hiding anything.*

But I had lied smoothly for eleven years, and lying smoothly means knowing exactly which lies are load-bearing, and right now I could feel the whole structure trembling on one beam: whatever Vann had pressed into that boy's hands, whatever promise Ivo had made over a dying stranger's water cup, it was below my feet, and Ravelin wanted it, and Ravelin did not come to Vientiane quays on rumor alone.

"Inspection's fine," I heard myself say. "Mind the bilge boards, they're loose. My engineer bites."

* * *

They walked my boat like men appraising a coffin.

Ravelin went slowly, touching nothing, which was worse than touching. He stood in the galley and looked at the tin cups hanging on hooks and asked who cooked. He looked at the bunks and asked how many crew. Four, I said. Five counting the captain. He looked at the fifth bunk — Ivo's, narrow, blanket folded with a soldier's corners I'd taught him — and let his gaze rest there one beat too long.

"Your deckhands are twins," he observed.

"Kham and Khone. Good boys. Strong."

"And the fifth?"

"My son." The word came out before I could weigh it, and I let it stand, because taking it back would have cost more than spending it. "Adopted. Pulled him out of a flood six years ago. He's crew."

"That's generous of you."

"I owed the river a debt. It collected."

We came down into the engine room last, and Sefa rose out of the machinery like a spirit of grease and iron, huge and calm, and gave Ravelin a look of such profound, unblinking stupidity that I nearly loved him. Sefa can perform ignorance the way other men perform sincerity. He answered three questions with four shrugs and a grunt, wiped his hands on the rag, and offered it to the soft one as a handshake. The soft one declined. Ravelin looked at the pumps, the fuel lines, the patched seam near the keel — and here something moved behind his pale eyes, some small recalculation, as if he were noting the boat's condition the way a wolf notes a limp.

"She leaks," he said.

"They all leak. Water's the business. Some of it comes along."

"Mm." He turned a full circle, slow, cataloguing. Then: "Captain, I'm going to tell you something, because I believe you're a reasonable man. There was a passenger on this route recently. A thief. He stole government property — documents of a sensitive nature — and died of his own foolishness somewhere downriver. The property is still missing. When it surfaces, it will embarrass certain officials. Embarrassment makes men careless, and careless men make rivers dangerous." He paused at the ladder, one hand on the rail, and looked back at me with those unhurried eyes. "If it surfaces on your boat, Captain, there won't be a fine. There won't be a hearing. Do you understand the difference?"

"I understand you don't like paperwork either."

For the first time, Ravelin smiled. It was the worst thing I'd seen all day, and I'd spent the day being inspected by him. "I remember you," he said quietly, too low for the soft one to catch. "From the old days. You've gotten careful, Joubert. Careful men live long lives. It's the ones who take passengers that end up in the current."

He climbed the ladder. I stood in the engine room between the pumps and listened to his footsteps cross my deck, and I understood that the past had not merely caught up with me. It had bought a ticket, boarded, and was deciding where to sit.

* * *

They stood on the quay until we cast off. I felt them watching the whole length of the channel, two small figures by the customs shed, the tall one not waving. Sefa took the wheel while I stood at the rail and did arithmetic.

A dead prospector. A missing document. An officer with an old grudge and new power, asking questions he already knew the answers to. Men like Ravelin don't investigate; they confirm. Which meant someone had talked — a witness on the mud flats, a fisherman paid in kip, someone who'd seen a sick man carried up a gangplank in the dark, or believed he had.

"Sefa," I said. "Where's Ivo?"

"Forward. Being quiet."

"Get him. Get the twins. All hands, below, now."

When they were assembled in the saloon — Ivo with his chin up and his eyes down, the twins flanking him like bookends, Sefa filling the doorway — I shut the door and told them the truth, because a crew that lies to each other drowns together.

"The man who just walked our decks is named Ravelin. Six years before Ivo was born, he ruined me. He'll tell you I was a smuggler. The court said so, so it must be true. What actually happened is I hauled a load I didn't know was his, and when it went wrong, he stepped back and let the river take the blame onto me. My license. My name. My—" I stopped. "My wife. She didn't survive what came after. So when I say I know that man, understand what I mean. I know him the way a scar knows the knife."

Nobody spoke. The river thumped softly against the hull.

"Now he says a dead man stole documents. Government documents. And he thinks they're on my boat." I looked at Ivo, and I kept my voice gentle, because I'd learned the hard way that you catch more truth with gentleness than with rage. "Ivo. Whatever that man gave you at the landing — whatever you promised him — I need to know. Not because I'm angry. Because Ravelin just measured my boat for a coffin, and I can't protect what I can't see."

Ivo's jaw worked. The twins looked at the floor with the synchronized guilt of boys who shared everything, including knowledge. Sefa studied his rag.

"Captain," Ivo said finally, and his voice cracked in the middle and he pushed through it, "I promised a dying man."

The hull ticked. Somewhere ahead, dark water waited, and the monsoon was building its first thunderheads over the mountains like a debt coming due.

"I know," I said. "I know you did. God help us both — so did I, the moment I lied to that man on my own deck."

I turned for the wheel, and behind me I heard Ivo exhale, and Sefa murmur something low, and I understood that whatever the boy was carrying below my feet, the time for pretending it wasn't there had just run out with the tide.

Ravelin's men would search again. Next time they wouldn't ask.
