## 5. What Ivo Keeps Below

An engine tells you everything about a person, if you listen long enough. Sefa had learned that in a shipyard in Colombo, thirty years and three names ago, when he was still the man his mother named him and not yet the man who slept in the belly of a riverboat with grease under his fingernails. Engines don't lie. They knock when they're hurting, they sing when they're loved, and they go quiet — that terrible, deliberate quiet — when someone is hiding something near them.

The *Joubert*'s engine was singing on the third night out of Vientiane, a low two-stroke hymn in D minor, which meant the fuel line was clean and the bearings were happy and Sefa could allow himself the luxury of sitting on his stool with a cup of tea going cold beside him. He liked this hour best. The twins were asleep forward, tangled together in their hammock like rope left carelessly coiled. Quentin was up top with the wheel and the stars, doing whatever it was Quentin did up there — talking to the river, or to God, or to nobody, in that flat voice that never gave anything away.

And Ivo was below.

Sefa heard him before he saw him: the particular carefulness of bare feet on steel plating. A boy trying to be silent makes more noise than a boy walking normally, because silence is a performance and performances have edges. Ivo came down the ladder one rung at a time, holding something folded against his chest the way you'd hold a bird you weren't sure was alive.

Sefa didn't turn around. He lifted his tea, found it cold, drank it anyway.

"You're dripping river water on my deck plates," he said.

Ivo froze halfway between the ladder and the shadowed space behind the spare cylinder housing — the little alcove where Sefa kept rags and the parts nobody used anymore. "I was rinsing off."

"At midnight."

"The heat woke me."

"Monsoon's coming," Sefa agreed pleasantly. "Heat wakes everyone." He turned then, slow, and looked at the boy over the rim of the cup. Fifteen years old, all elbows and watchfulness, with that way of standing like a man braced for a wave that hadn't arrived yet. Sefa knew that stance from the inside. "You want to tell me what you're putting behind my cylinders?"

"I'm not—"

"Ivo." Sefa set the cup down on the workbench with a small ceramic click. "I have spent forty years listening to machines lie to me so that I could catch them before they lied at the worst moment. You are not a machine. You are much worse at it."

The boy's jaw worked. Behind him the engine sang its steady note, indifferent, honest, filling the dark with the only truth Sefa had ever fully trusted.

"It's nothing," Ivo said. "It's paper. It's mine."

"Paper is never nothing. Paper is the most dangerous cargo on any boat." Sefa picked up a rag, wiped his hands though they were already clean, an old ritual, buying time the way other men bought drinks. "I once watched a man get shot in Rangoon over a bill of lading. Not gold. Not opium. Paper. Because paper says what happened, and there are always men who need what happened to be something else."

He watched the boy hear that. Watched it land somewhere behind the ribs.

"Sit," Sefa said, and nodded at the low stool by the workbench, the one with the cushion made from an old life vest. "Or stand. But stop hovering like smoke."

Ivo sat. For a long moment neither of them spoke, and the engine filled the silence, and above them the deck boards creaked under Quentin's slow pacing, wheel to rail, rail to wheel.

Then Ivo reached into his shirt and brought out the oilcloth packet, and unwrapped it on his knees with the tenderness of a boy handling a relic, which, Sefa supposed, was exactly what it was.

* * *

The map was drawn in faded ink on paper gone soft as cloth, the kind of paper that had been carried against skin through fever and rain. Sefa held it under the workbench lamp and said nothing for a while, because the drawing deserved the silence. It was good work — better than good. River channels marked with soundings. A bend of the Mekong south of Luang Prabang rendered in careful profile, cliffs noted by height, a submerged shelf drawn in cross-section like a surgeon's diagram. And there, in a hand that had pressed hard enough to tear: *the barge*. Depth figures. A bearing taken off a rock shaped, according to a small confident sketch, like a sleeping dog.

"This is Vann's," Sefa said. It wasn't a question. The whole crew had seen the prospector die at the muddy landing two days back — had stood in a respectful half-circle while Quentin dug the grave himself, refusing help, shoveling the red earth with a face like a closed door.

"He gave it to me." Ivo's voice was low and fierce at once, the way voices get when they've been rehearsing an argument alone in the dark. "Not to Quentin. To me. He asked me for water and I gave it to him and he talked, and at the end he took my hand and put the map in it and said—" The boy stopped. Swallowed. Started again, quieter. "He said, *take it to my daughter. Linh. In Luang Prabang. Tell her her father wasn't a liar.*"

"And you hid it from Quentin."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because Quentin would say no." Ivo looked up, and there it was — the thing underneath, the real cargo. "Because Quentin would look at it the way he looks at everything that isn't cargo, and he'd say the river's too high and the man's dead and dead men's errands sink boats. And maybe he'd be right. But I promised, Sefa. He was dying and he was scared and nobody else would even sit with him, and I promised."

Sefa folded the map along its old creases, carefully, and handed it back. Then he got up, took the kettle hanging by the exhaust manifold — it stayed warm all night, a small luxury of his kingdom — and poured two cups of the coarse green tea he drank instead of sleeping properly.

"Forty years ago," he said, giving Ivo his cup, "I was a fitter in the Colombo yards. Good work. Steady. My father had been a fitter, and his father before him, brass and iron all the way back. And then there was a strike, and the company brought in men to break it, and one of those men died in a yard accident that was no accident, and the police needed someone to blame who wasn't useful to anyone." He blew on his tea. "They blamed me. I had a name, a trade, a mother. None of it mattered, because a piece of paper said I'd done it, and paper beats people every time unless another piece of paper fights back. Nobody fought back for me. So I became someone else. Worked tramp steamers. Lost the name, kept the hands." He flexed them, scarred and capable, in the lamplight. "That's why I listen to engines. Machines are the only witnesses that can't be bribed."

Ivo was watching him with that total attention boys give when they realize an adult has opened an actual door instead of just pointing at one.

"What happened to your name?"

"Nothing. That's the point. It's still there, in a file, in a country I can't go back to. Waiting. Names keep." Sefa drank. "So here is what I know about promises and paper, boy, and you can take it or leave it. A promise made to a dying man is the strongest kind there is, because the man can't release you from it. It sits on you like ballast. You can throw it overboard, but the boat rides wrong ever after. I've seen it. Men who dropped their dead men's errands — they run rivers fine, but they flinch at their own reflections."

"Quentin—"

"Quentin has a reflection he doesn't look at either," Sefa said, and immediately wished he'd swallowed the words, because Ivo's eyes sharpened like a hook finding current.

"What do you mean?"

"I mean the captain has his own map hidden somewhere, and it isn't drawn in ink." Sefa waved it off. "Another time. Maybe never. Not mine to tell. But understand this about him: he'll be angry, yes. He'll talk about risk and cargo and the rule about passengers. And underneath all of it, if you watch closely — and you're good at watching, I've seen you — underneath, some part of him will be glad. Because a man who keeps a promise for a stranger is proof that such men exist, and Quentin has spent years needing proof."

The engine changed key — a faint new harmonic, nothing wrong, just the river shallowing somewhere ahead, silt singing in the intake strainer. Sefa listened to it the way other men listened to conversation, then reached over without looking and made a half-turn adjustment on the fuel valve. The song smoothed out.

"Here's what happens now," he said. "You put the map back where you had it, but not behind the cylinders — too obvious, that's the first place anyone searches, I found it in four minutes and I wasn't even looking. There's a false bottom in the tool chest, second drawer, under the spare gaskets. I built it for documents during a bad year in Cambodia. No one knows it's there. Now one person does."

Ivo stared at him. "You're helping me hide it? From Quentin?"

"I'm helping you keep a promise," Sefa said. "From whoever comes asking. Which may include Quentin, and may include worse." He let that sit. "Because here's the other thing I know, boy, and I learned it the expensive way. A dying man doesn't give away treasure maps to random children. Vann chose you. Out of everyone on that landing — the captain, the twins, me, an old exile with strong hands — he chose the boy who brought him water. Think about why. Men who are dying see clearly. Whatever's on that map, it isn't money. Money, he'd have sold. This is something he needed delivered by someone whose price couldn't be met."

Ivo's hand had closed around the oilcloth packet without his seeming to notice. "His daughter."

"Start with the daughter," Sefa agreed. "But keep your eyes open past her."

Above them, footsteps crossed the deck — Quentin, moving toward the bow rail, pausing there. Through the open engine-room hatch drifted the smell of the river at night: mud and rain-that-hadn't-fallen-yet and the green rot of the flooded banks sliding past in the dark. Somewhere far upstream thunder muttered, testing its voice.

"Does it frighten you?" Ivo asked suddenly. "Having secrets again. After what they cost you."

Sefa considered the question honestly, because the boy had earned honesty. "Terrifies me," he said. "Every day since Colombo, I've kept exactly one secret — my own name — and it's a light burden because no one wants it. This is different. This secret has weight because other people will want it. Bad people, probably; good maps attract bad men like lampblack attracts flies. And now I'm carrying it with you." He smiled, and it was an old smile, worn smooth. "But I'll tell you what my mother told me when I left home with a warrant behind me and nothing in my bag. She said: *a man running from a lie travels lighter than a man running from the truth, but only the second one arrives anywhere worth being.*"

Ivo looked down at the packet in his fist. When he raised his head, something had settled in his face — not peace, nothing so easy, but resolve finding its footing, a keel taking hold in water.

"I'm going to Luang Prabang," he said. "Whatever Quentin says."

"I know," Sefa said. "I've known since you came down the ladder pretending to be quiet." He nodded at the tool chest. "Second drawer. Under the gaskets. Go on, before the captain comes down and asks why my engine room smells like conspiracy."

Ivo slid off the stool, crossed to the chest, and Sefa watched him find the false bottom on the first try — good hands, quick mind, the boy learning the boat's body the way Sefa had learned it, seam by seam. The map disappeared into the dark beneath the gaskets. The drawer closed with a sound like nothing at all.

Then Ivo paused, one foot on the ladder, and looked back.

"Sefa. Thank you."

"Don't thank me yet." Sefa picked up his cold tea and drained it, grimacing. "Thank me in Luang Prabang, if we get there. And boy — if the captain finds out before we arrive, you let me do the talking. I've had forty years of practice explaining things to angry men."

Ivo grinned — quick, bright, gone again — and went up the ladder into the night.

Sefa sat alone with his engine after that, listening to it sing, and did not sleep. He thought about names in files across the sea. He thought about a prospector pressing paper into a child's hand. He thought about Quentin up there at the wheel, steering them steadily toward a city none of them should be eager to reach, carrying a passenger he didn't know he had.

Toward morning, the first fat drops of monsoon began ticking against the engine-room hatch, and far downstream — though Sefa could not have known it, and would not learn it for two more days — a customs launch was turning into the current with two men aboard who asked questions about a dead prospector.

The engine sang. The rain came harder. The river ran on toward Luang Prabang, carrying all of them.
