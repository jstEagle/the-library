## 6. Running the Night Water

Quentin Joubert found out about the map the way he found out about most things on his boat — too late, and from Sefa.

The engineer came up to the wheelhouse just after noon, wiping diesel from his hands with a rag that had been black for years, and stood there in the doorway not saying anything. Quentin had learned to read those silences. Sefa did not come to the wheelhouse to talk unless something was wrong, and when something was wrong he circled it like a heron circling a fish, patient, until the moment came.

"Spit it out," Quentin said.

Sefa spat it out. "The boy's got a map. Vann gave it to him before he died. It's in the engine room, wrapped in oilcloth behind the spare injector pump."

Quentin set down the mug of coffee he'd been nursing since dawn. He didn't slam it. He never slammed anything; slamming was for men who hadn't learned that a boat punishes sudden movement. But his voice came out low and flat, the way it did when the twins were wrestling near the winch.

"You searched his bunk?"

"I searched my engine room. He asked me if he could keep something there. Something dry." Sefa folded the rag over his belt. "I looked. I'm the one who keeps that room dry."

"And you let him."

"I talked to him first." Sefa leaned against the doorframe, arms crossed, and there was something in his face Quentin didn't want to look at directly — a kind of settled certainty, the certainty of a man who had already decided he was right. "He made a promise to a dying man, Quentin. You know what that is. You've made promises you didn't keep. So have I. The boy kept his."

"The boy disobeyed me."

"The boy did what you wouldn't." Sefa said it without heat, which made it worse. "You turned Vann away at the landing. Ivo gave him water. You refused the map. Ivo took it. One of you is going to be able to live with himself in ten years, and it isn't going to be you."

Quentin felt his jaw tighten. Ten years of running this river had taught him every argument Sefa knew how to make, because they were all variations of one argument: *you can't outrun being a man.* And ten years of running this river had taught Quentin the answer to that argument, which was that you could try, and trying kept everyone alive.

"He's fourteen," Quentin said. "He doesn't get to decide what we carry. A map to French gold isn't cargo, Sefa. It's a target. Whoever killed Vann will want it, and whoever wants it will come aboard this boat, and everything I built here —" he gestured at the wheelhouse, the brass rails, the chart table where the twins' homework sat half-finished under a stone — "everything floats on the rule that we carry rice and salt and machine parts and nothing anyone would kill for."

"You think rice keeps you safe?" Sefa pushed off the doorframe. "I ran from a country that burned. You ran from a courtroom. The twins ran from a village that sold them. Nobody on this boat is safe, Captain. We're just moving." He paused at the stairs. "The map stays below. Talk to the boy. Don't break him doing it."

Then he was gone, down into the engine's heat, and Quentin stood alone at the wheel with the current pushing against the hull and the monsoon clouds stacking up along the western ridgeline like grey freight.

* * *

He waited until evening. That was deliberate. Rage handled at noon becomes shouting; rage given six hours to settle becomes something you can use.

He found Ivo aft, coiling line in the fading light, working with the quick economy the boy had learned without anyone teaching him — the way he'd learned everything, by watching and needing. Quentin took the coil from his hands and held it.

"Vann," he said. "Tell me."

Ivo told him. All of it. The water at the landing, the fever, the voice going thin as paper, the map pressed into his hands with fingers that shook. *My daughter. Luang Prabang. Tell her I tried to come home.* And then the promise, made in the dark, without permission, the way all the important promises are made.

"He was dying," Ivo finished. "What was I supposed to do? Ask you?"

"Yes." Quentin heard how it sounded even as he said it. "No." He looked downriver, at the water going copper and then ash. "You're supposed to ask me because asking me is how you stay on this boat, and staying on this boat is how you stay alive. Do you understand what that map is? It's not gold to us. It's a reason for men with guns to stop us. Ravelin's men were at Vientiane asking about Vann. They'll be at every landing between here and Luang Prabang. They'll board us. They'll search us. And when they find it —"

"They won't find it. Sefa hid it good."

"Sefa hid it *good*," Quentin repeated, and laughed, once, a hard sound with no humor in it. "Boy, I've been boarded by customs men who could find a smuggled letter inside a sealed tin of opium. There is no *good*. There is only luck, and luck runs out at the worst lock on the worst night of your life."

Ivo stood his ground. That was the thing about the boy that both maddened Quentin and — though he'd have cut off his own hand before admitting it — steadied him. Ivo didn't flinch. He'd been pulled out of a flood at eight years old, coughing river water into Quentin's palms, and ever since he had treated fear as weather: real, worth noting, not worth obeying.

"Then what do we do?" Ivo asked. "Throw it in the river? Break the promise?"

And there it was, the question Quentin had spent fifteen years building his whole life to avoid. What do you do with a promise that costs more than keeping it?

"We go to Luang Prabang," Quentin said finally, "because that was always the run. We deliver our cargo, we keep our schedule, and nobody so much as breathes wrong until we're past the last checkpoint. And when we get there, you give that map to Vann's daughter, and we wash our hands of it. Until then it stays in the engine room, and you don't touch it, and you don't speak of it, and if any man in a uniform asks you a question, you look stupid. Can you look stupid?"

Ivo almost smiled. "I've seen the twins do it."

"It's harder than it looks." Quentin handed back the line. "Coil that properly. We make the Pak Ou lock by tomorrow midnight if the current holds."

They didn't make the lock by midnight. The river decided otherwise, as rivers do.

* * *

The storm caught them an hour short of the gorge, coming down the tributary valleys in walls, turning dusk into a brown darkness where the rain hit the deck sideways and the sandbars Quentin had memorized for a decade went somewhere else. He ran blind on sound and memory, the wheel heavy in his hands, Sefa's engine answering every call like a heart that had never once considered stopping. Somewhere below, the twins were lashing cargo and singing one of their terrible improvised songs about the rain, because the twins sang when they were frightened, and everyone pretended not to know that.

They came around the last bend above the lock at half past eleven and Quentin saw the lights.

Not the lock lights. The lock lights were amber, steady, boring, beautiful. These were white, sweeping, two of them, mounted on a launch moored against the guide wall, and beside them a floodlight had been rigged on the lock platform itself, throwing a hard cone across the water where every vessel coming down had to slow to nothing and present itself like a plate of food.

A man in a poncho stood on the platform with a clipboard under his arm, and even at this distance, even through rain, Quentin knew the posture. Head slightly tilted, weight on one heel, the patience of someone who has never once been in a hurry because the machinery hurries for him.

Ravelin.

Or Ravelin's men wearing Ravelin's manner. It hardly mattered. Fifteen years ago Quentin had watched that same tilt of the head take his name, his license, his wife, and hand him back a conviction and a river to hide in. Now it stood under a floodlight waiting for his boat, and somewhere below his feet, wrapped in oilcloth behind a spare injector pump, was the reason it was waiting.

"Quentin?" Sefa's voice up the engine tube. "That's a customs launch. They're signaling us to tie off."

"I see them."

"Inspection at night, in a storm, at a lock? That's not procedure. That's a trap with paperwork."

Quentin looked at the floodlight cone. He looked at the rain hammering the gorge walls. He thought about tying off, standing straight, letting them search, watching some clerk in rubber boots pull an oilcloth bundle from behind an injector pump while Ivo watched from the rail with his whole future in his face. He thought about the alternative, which was the weir channel on the far side of the lock island — the spillway the guides called the Night Water, runnable maybe twice a year, runnable never with a loaded boat, runnable tonight or not at all.

Every rule he had. Every rule he'd built the boat on. *Never stop moving. Never take passengers. Never carry what men kill for.* And underneath them, older than all of them, the rule he'd actually lived by since the courtroom: *never be where they can reach you.*

Tying off was where they could reach him.

"Twins topside!" he shouted down the hatch. "Life vests on! Sefa — full pressure, everything she has!"

Sefa's voice came back without hesitation, which told Quentin the engineer had been waiting for exactly this order since sundown. "She's got everything she has. Where are we going?"

"Around."

* * *

The weir channel opened on their port side like a black throat. In daylight, at low water, it was a chute of broken water forty meters wide between concrete wing walls, dropping three meters in a hundred, ending in a standing wave train that could fold a barge like paper. Tonight, with the monsoon feeding it, it wasn't a chute. It was a waterfall pretending to be a river, and the roar of it reached them a full minute before the entrance did.

Kiet and Somchai came up the ladder two seconds apart, soaked, grinning the way they grinned at funerals, and Quentin gave them the only briefing there was time for: "Port side rail. Hands on. If I say jump, you hold the line and you jump together, and if you go in the water, you go in holding each other, because nobody finds one twin in the dark."

Somchai looked at the channel and stopped grinning. Kiet looked at Quentin and started.

"You've run this?" Kiet shouted over the roar.

"Once. Daylight. Empty. Fifteen years ago." Quentin put the wheel over. "It's grown since."

The bow entered the white water at 23:40 by the wheelhouse clock, and the world became noise.

The first drop took them like a hand taking a coin. The deck went steep, the cargo shifted with a groan of timber, and the floodlight swept across them from the lock platform — one long white slash through the rain, catching the wheelhouse glass, catching Quentin's face, catching, he was sure of it, the exact moment the officer with the clipboard understood what the boat was doing and began to shout orders no one could hear. Then the second drop swallowed the light, and there was only water.

Sefa's engine screamed and answered. The Joubert — ninety tons of riveted iron that Quentin had nursed through fifteen years of never asking too much — dug her stern down and rode the surge the way she'd been built to ride it, by a colonial shipwright dead fifty years who had probably never imagined this. Quentin steered by the sound of the water against the hull: smooth on the starboard quarter, chaos ahead, a deep grinding note off the port bow that meant rock, meant reef, meant the wing wall's drowned cousin reaching up to claim a toll.

"Starboard!" he roared. "Hard starboard!"

The wheel fought him like a living thing. The boat slewed, the port rail went under green water to the second stanchion, and the twins — lashed to the rail, hands hooked on the lifeline — went horizontal, legs kicking free over thirty thousand cubic meters per second of monsoon river.

Kiet's grip tore loose.

Quentin saw it happen in the strobe-flash way the mind saves things: the boy's hand sliding along wet rope, the rope end whipping, Kiet's body swinging out over the torrent with Somchai still locked to the rail beside him, the two of them joined at the wrist by the safety line Quentin had made them double up — *if you go in the water, you go in holding each other* — and the line snapping taut, hauling Kiet back against the hull like a fish on a jigging line while the river tried to peel him off and keep him.

"Hold him!" Quentin bellowed. Somchai had him — fist in his brother's collar, cheek pressed to the deck, screaming something that wasn't a word. Ivo was there before Quentin could move, flat on his stomach on the flooded deck, both arms around Somchai's waist, anchoring the anchor, and the three of them were one shape clinging to the rail while the Joubert climbed the next standing wave and hung there, bow skyward, propeller thrashing air, for one endless second —

— and came down.

The impact went through the hull like a bell struck underwater. Quentin's knees buckled. Somewhere below, he knew, Sefa was braced against the engine, riding it, coaxing it, giving them revolutions when the screw broke surface and torque when it bit again. The channel widened. The standing waves flattened into the long grey rollers of the tailrace, and the roar fell away behind them like a crowd going quiet, and the Joubert ran out into the open dark below the lock with her decks streaming and her crew gasping.

Quentin counted heads. Twins, dripping, tangled in their own safety line, laughing now — actually laughing, high and cracked, the laugh of people who have been handed back to themselves. Ivo, soaked to the skin, helping them unwind. Sefa's face at the engine-room hatch, asking nothing, needing nothing said.

Behind them, upstream, the floodlight still swept the empty lock approach. No launch could follow through the weir. No clerk with a clipboard could follow through the weir. For tonight, they were past.

Quentin took the wheel again and steered into the black water, and his hands shook, and he let them shake where no one could see, which was its own old rule.

* * *

At two in the morning they tied up against a flooded willow bank twenty kilometers downriver, engines cold, watch set. The rain had thinned to a steady soak. The twins slept like the dead, side by side in the wheelhouse bench, still in their life vests, still faintly smiling. Sefa had gone below to listen to his engine the way other men listen to music.

Quentin found Ivo at the stern rail, looking back upriver at the water they'd come down, the water that had nearly taken his friends.

"You could have died tonight," Quentin said. "All of you. Because of a piece of paper."

"Because of a promise," Ivo said.

"Don't dress it up. Paper. Ink. A dying man's wish." Quentin heard himself and hated the sound, because it was the sound of a man arguing with the truth. "I spent fifteen years making sure nothing on this boat was worth dying for. Rice doesn't get anyone killed. Salt doesn't. Tonight a piece of ink nearly did."

Ivo turned and looked at him, and in the lamplight spilling from the wheelhouse the boy's face was calm in a way that made Quentin feel old and transparent.

"You said whoever killed Vann would come aboard," Ivo said. "They were at the lock. Before they ever knew we had the map. They were there because of *him*, because of what he knew. Running didn't bring them, Captain. They were already coming." He paused. "The map didn't change that. It just means we know why."

Quentin stood with that. The rain ticked on the wheelhouse roof. Downstream the river ran wide and brown and patient toward Luang Prabang, and somewhere on it, behind them, a customs launch was putting men ashore at the lock to telegraph ahead — he was certain of it, certain the way you're certain of a wound. By morning, every landing on the middle river would have their description. By afternoon, someone would be waiting at every jetty with a clipboard and a floodlight and Ravelin's tilted head.

There was no more running the edges. There was no more carrying nothing worth killing for, because the thing worth killing for was already aboard, had been aboard since a feverish man pressed oilcloth into a boy's hands, and the only question left — the question Quentin had refused for fifteen years, at landings and checkpoints and in the mirror — was whether he carried it as cargo or as captain.

He looked at the boy who had pulled the truth out of him the way Sefa pulled truth out of engines.

"All right," Quentin said. "We carry it. But we carry it my way. Nobody touches it but you and Sefa. Nobody speaks of it on the water. And when we reach Luang Prabang, it goes to Vann's daughter and out of our lives forever. That's the deal. That's the whole deal."

"That's the deal," Ivo said.

Quentin went back up to the wheelhouse and woke the twins for the midwatch, and as Kiet took the wheel, rubbing sleep from his eyes, the radio crackled with the river traffic band — the ordinary chatter of barges and ferries reporting stages and snags — and then, threaded through it, a message in the flat official cadence that Quentin had learned, fifteen years ago, to hear in his nightmares:

*"All vessels, all stations. Customs notice. Inspection points established at Ban Houei Sai, Chom Khao, and the Luang Prabang approaches, effective dawn. Vessel Joubert, iron hull, ninety tons, master J. Q., to be detained and searched on contact. Master is advised that cooperation will be noted favorably."*

Favorably. Quentin knew exactly what favorable cost. He'd paid it once, in a courtroom, with his name.

He switched off the radio. Ahead, the gorge country rose out of the rain-dark, black ridges stacked against blacker cloud, and the river narrowed between them, gathering itself for the run down to Luang Prabang — and somewhere in that narrowing water, in the next day, in the next bend, something was waiting that would crack the hull of everything he had.

"Come about," he said quietly. "New heading. We're going to need more river than the map shows."
