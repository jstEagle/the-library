## 7. The Leaking Hull

I heard the river change before anyone else did.

That was my job now, or it had become my job without anyone saying so — to stand at the rail in the grey hour before dawn and listen. The Mekong talked all night if you let it, muttering against the hull, slapping the paddles of driftwood, and after three years aboard I had learned its moods the way Sefa had learned the engine's: by failure first, then by love. So when the water below us went from a low conversation to a long, grinding hiss, I knew we were running over something that wasn't water.

I went down the ladder two rungs at a time.

Quentin was already at the wheel, hatless, his hair plastered flat with river damp. He hadn't slept. None of us had, not really, since Vientiane — since the night run through the rapids that Quentin had made with his jaw set like a cleat and his eyes fixed on water he could only half see. The twins were curled on the cargo deck under a tarpaulin like two commas, still wearing their life vests out of habit. Sefa was somewhere below, asleep in the engine room because she always slept there after a hard run, one hand on the main bearing as if the boat were a patient and she its nurse.

"Sandbar," I said. "Long one. We're dragging."

"I hear it." Quentin didn't look at me. He was reading the water ahead — the smooth glassy tongue where current poured over a submerged bar, the boil downstream where it came up angry again. "We're light enough. We'll grind across and be glad of it."

We weren't light enough.

The *Joubert* shuddered, a long low vibration that climbed through the deck plates and into my teeth, and for a moment the whole world was the sound of steel hull against wet sand — a sound like a giant drawing a blade very slowly. Then we came off the bar with a lurch, dropped into deeper water, and everything seemed fine.

Everything seemed fine for about an hour.

* * *

It was Sefa who found it, because Sefa found everything wrong with the boat before the boat itself knew. She came up the engine room ladder with her face doing something I had never seen it do, which was nothing at all — no scowl, no grin, just a flat blankness that frightened me more than shouting would have.

"How much water?" Quentin asked her. He'd seen her face too.

"Bilge is at eighteen centimeters and climbing," she said. "Pump's keeping pace. Barely. It won't by afternoon."

"Where?"

"Aft seam, port side. Where we kissed the bar at the narrows last night — don't give me that look, Quentin, you threaded that water like a man with a debt to pay, and the river took its fee. Cracked a seam plate. Not torn. Cracked. Which means it opens wider every time the hull flexes, and this old girl flexes plenty."

Quentin stood very still at the wheel. I watched him do the arithmetic I couldn't yet do — the distance to the next landing, the state of the sky, the weight of the cargo, the fact that behind us somewhere, sooner or later, Ravelin's men would come looking for a boat that had refused to stop.

"Options," he said finally.

"Beach her on the next high sandbar and patch the seam properly. That's the only option. Everything else is a way of choosing when we sink instead of whether."

"And the next high sandbar is—"

"Six hours. Maybe seven, if the current holds."

Quentin looked aft, toward the sky. I looked too, and my stomach went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the morning chill. The clouds had been building since Vientiane — great stacked towers of them, white and gold and bruise-purple, standing on the horizon like a city that had no business existing. Everyone on the river knew what they meant. The monsoon wasn't due for another week. But the river doesn't read calendars, and neither does the sky.

"That storm comes early," Quentin said, "and we're bailing water in the middle of it."

"Then we'd best hope it's polite enough to wait," said Sefa, and went back down to her engine room.

* * *

I spent the morning learning how a boat dies.

That sounds dramatic, but it's the truth, and I've decided — writing this now, with dry feet under me and a roof that doesn't leak — that the truth deserves the drama. A boat doesn't die all at once. It dies the way a man dies of a fever: in increments, each one survivable alone, none of them survivable together.

By mid-morning the pump was falling behind. Sefa rigged the second pump, the backup, the one that was supposed to be for emergencies, and the two of them together held the line at twenty-five centimeters. The twins took turns in the bilge with buckets, not because the buckets helped but because Quentin wanted hands on the problem, hands feeling the water rise, hands that would know before any gauge did. I took my shift at noon. The bilge water was warm and brown and smelled of diesel and rust, and when I knelt there in the dark with a bucket, scooping and passing and scooping, I could feel the hull breathing around me — flexing with each wave, and with each flex, somewhere aft on the port side, a faint tick like a clock counting down.

I told myself it was the cooling metal. I told myself a lot of things that day.

Quentin ran us hard. Harder than he liked, harder than the boat liked; I could tell by the way he stood at the wheel, leaning forward slightly, the way a man leans into wind. Every hour we gained was an hour between us and deep water in a storm. Behind us the cloud towers had grown all morning until they filled the southern sky, and their shadows moved across the water ahead of us like the shadows of enormous birds.

It was the twins who named what we all felt. They always did that — said the thing out loud that the rest of us were carrying silently, because they were twins and between the two of them they only needed half the courage each.

"The sky looks like the flood year," Kiet said.

"The bad one," Somchai said.

They said it together, the way they said everything, and nobody answered, because everybody on that boat remembered the flood year. That was the year Quentin pulled me out of the water. That was the year I learned that rivers take houses, and fields, and sometimes whole villages, and give back almost nothing.

* * *

At four in the afternoon the rain arrived, and it did not arrive politely.

There was no gentle beginning, no scattered drops, no warning patter on the deck. The storm simply reached down and took hold of the river. One moment I was watching the leading edge of the cloud wall come at us like a moving country; the next, the world was white noise and horizontal water, and the *Joubert* was plunging into waves that came from directions waves had no right to come from, because the wind was fighting the current and the current was winning and losing by turns.

I lost my footing on the cargo deck and slid until I caught the cargo netting. The tarpaulins snapped and cracked like whips. Somewhere forward, Quentin's voice cut through the roar — not words, just commands, short and hard, the voice he used when the river left no room for sentences.

Sefa came up the ladder soaked through, hair flattened to her skull, and shouted into Quentin's ear because shouting was the only language left.

"Thirty centimeters! Both pumps maxed! She's opening!"

"Time to the bar?"

"Twenty minutes if we live that long!"

Quentin turned the wheel a few degrees and the whole boat leaned, groaning, into the turn. Through the curtain of rain I saw it — or rather I felt it before I saw it, the way the current ahead went suddenly confused, backing and eddying in the lee of something big and shallow. The sandbar. Our salvation, six hours away that morning, arriving exactly when the river chose to deliver it.

"Stand by the bow lines!" Quentin roared. "Twins — forward! Ivo — aft! When I put her nose in, she'll swing. Don't let her swing broadside. If she goes broadside in this, we roll."

I went aft along the rail with both hands locked on, the rain hitting my face like thrown gravel. The stern was rising and falling through a range that made my stomach float free at the top of each lift. Below my feet, through the deck plates, I could feel the water inside us answering the water outside us, sloshing in counterpoint, the boat growing heavier with every minute, riding lower, answering the helm slower.

This is what I understood then, kneeling at the stern cleat with the bow line coiled and frozen in my fists: the *Joubert* was not a thing that carried us. The *Joubert* was us. It was Sefa's hands and Quentin's eyes and the twins' laughter echoing in the cargo hold and my own bunk with its thin mattress and the notch I'd carved in the doorframe to mark my height each birthday, because Quentin pretended not to notice and measured it himself when he thought I wasn't looking. And it was dying under our feet, and there was nowhere else in the world for any of us to be.

The bar came up fast.

Quentin brought us in with the kind of helmsmanship I have never seen since and expect never to see again — reading water that was half foam, feeling the bottom through the wheel, putting the bow onto the sand at the exact angle where the hull would bite and hold instead of slide. The impact threw me against the rail. The twins were already over the side, ropes around their waists, wading and swimming in the churn to run lines ashore to the stunted trees at the bar's upstream tip. Sefa killed the engine to save what remained of the fuel, and in the sudden relative quiet — the storm still screaming, but the engine's heartbeat gone — I heard the sound I will hear in dreams for the rest of my life:

Water, inside the hull, still moving. Still finding its level. Unhurried. Certain.

* * *

We fought for the boat all night.

I don't remember it as separate hours. I remember it as one long continuous effort, lit by the storm's internal lightning and the single battle lantern Sefa rigged under the tarp, our small orange island in a world of black water. Sefa led the patching — a wooden wedge driven into the cracked seam, then sheet lead hammered over it, then canvas saturated in pitch, then a timber shore braced against the frame to take the flex out of the plate. She worked with a calm that I finally understood wasn't calm at all. It was fury, refined. She had been exiled from one home already. She was not going to be exiled from this one.

The twins ferried gear and bailed and argued cheerfully about which of them was working harder, because even at the edge of drowning they were themselves. Quentin stood in the shallows holding the bow line against the current for two full hours, until his arms shook, refusing help, because the bow line was the boat's life and he trusted no other hands with it — though later, much later, he admitted the truth, which was that he needed to be holding something, and the boat was the truest thing in reach.

And I bailed. I bailed the bilge by lantern light while the storm tried to tear the tarps off above me, bucket after bucket after bucket, and somewhere in the third or fourth hour of it, with my shoulders burning and the brown water still coming, I stopped being afraid.

Not brave. Just past fear, the way you get past a wall you've been pushing against so long your arms forget why. I knelt in the flooding belly of our home and I thought about Vann, the dying man on the muddy landing, and the map folded against my skin where it had lived for days now, dry and secret. I thought about his daughter in Luang Prabang, waiting for a father who was already dead. I thought: this boat has to live, because the promise lives on this boat, and I am the only one who knows it, and if the boat dies tonight the promise drowns with me.

So I bailed. And the water rose anyway.

* * *

Near dawn the storm spent itself down to a steady grey rain, and Sefa called a halt, and we gathered under the tarp like survivors of something larger than weather, which we were.

"Talk to me," Quentin said.

Sefa wrung out her shirt with the grim efficiency of a woman wringing a chicken's neck. "The patch is holding. Barely. It's a finger in a dike, Quentin. The seam's cracked clean through the plate, and no wedge and lead is going to make that right. She needs careening — beach her, roll her, weld or rivet a proper doubler over the seam. Real work. Days of work."

"We can't stay beached for days."

"No," Sefa agreed. "Because we're sitting on a sandbar in monsoon season with a rising river, and in three days this bar will be a rumor. We need a yard. A real one. Or—" she paused, and I watched her decide to say the thing, "—or a village. There's Ban Pak Xang, twelve kilometers downstream. Good mud bank, deep approach, people who know boats. People who owe you nothing and remember everything."

Something crossed Quentin's face then, quick as a fish under the surface, and was gone. I'd seen it before — at the mention of certain names, certain stretches of river. A flinch with history in it.

"They'll not welcome us," he said.

"They'll not welcome anyone, in flood season, with strangers. But they'll let us repair, because that's what river people do, and because you—" Sefa stopped herself, which she almost never did. "You'll manage it. One way or another."

Quentin looked out at the river. The rain had thinned enough to see the far bank, drowned and green, and the water running high and yellow-brown with the first flush of the monsoon, carrying branches and whole trees south like a fleet in retreat. His boat sat under him listing two degrees to port, patched with lead and prayer, holding the river out by stubbornness alone.

"Twelve kilometers," he said. "On a cracked seam. In flood water."

"On a cracked seam in flood water," Sefa confirmed. "Or we stay here, and in three days the river takes the decision away from us."

Nobody spoke. The twins, for once, had nothing to say. I kept my hand flat against the deck plates, feeling the hull breathe, feeling the tick of the wounded seam underneath the patch — slower now, but still counting.

Quentin nodded once, the smallest nod I ever saw him make.

"Pump watch in shifts," he said. "We run at first light. Twelve kilometers." Then, quieter, almost to himself, looking downriver toward a village he had not spoken of in all the years I'd known him: "Mai's people. Of all the landings on this river."

He said the name like a man touching an old scar to check whether it still hurts.

I lay in my bunk that morning with the map dry against my chest and the boat rocking gently on the bar, and I listened to the river rise around us, and I understood that we were sailing toward more than repairs. Whatever waited for us at Ban Pak Xang, Quentin feared it more than the storm we'd just survived — and Quentin Joubert did not fear storms.

The seam ticked beneath me like a second heart. In twelve kilometers, we would find out which debts the river collects first.
