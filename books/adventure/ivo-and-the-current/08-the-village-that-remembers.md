## 8. The Village That Remembers

The *Joubert* came into Ban Houayxay's reach at dusk, listing like a drunk man leaning on a fence.

Quentin stood at the wheel and watched the village assemble itself out of the river haze — fishing stakes first, then the black shapes of drying racks, then lamplight spilling from stilt houses onto water gone the color of old tea. Behind him the engine beat its wounded rhythm, a knock in every third stroke that Sefa had been fighting for two days without winning. The bilge pumps ran on their own schedule now, coughing up what the cracked seam let in, and the twins had stopped joking about it somewhere past the second hour of bailing.

He knew this bend. He knew it the way a man knows the road to a house he swore he'd never visit again — not with his mind but with his hands, which had already eased the throttle back before he'd decided anything, already begun reading the current for the deep channel past the fish traps.

"You know this place," Ivo said. Not a question. The boy had come up beside him the way he always did, silent as river fog, and now he studied Quentin's face with those eyes that missed nothing and offered nothing back.

"Ran supplies here during the flood year." Quentin kept his voice flat, working-voice, the one that carried no weather inside it. "Before your time."

"Before I was pulled out of the water, you mean."

"Before a lot of things."

Ivo didn't push. That was the boy's gift and his weapon both — he simply stood there, and silence did his asking for him, and Quentin found himself filling it whether he wanted to or not.

"There was a flood," Quentin said. "Bad one. Whole valley under water. Government sent nothing for nine days. I was running relief cargo — rice, medicine, fuel — three boats' worth of it, and mine was the only one drawing shallow enough to make the last stretch." He turned the wheel a degree, feeling the sandbar's pull through the wood like a hand on his wrist. "I made four runs. On the fifth, the customs men were waiting at the landing. Ravelin's men. They said my papers were irregular. They said they'd impound the boat."

"And you left."

"I left." The words came out of him like splinters. "There were people on that bank who'd carried their grain down to the landing on their backs, waiting for me to carry it up to the villages cut off above the gorge. Old people. Children. A woman named Kham who'd organized the whole thing, who'd fed my crew every night we were there." He shook his head once, a small violent motion. "They told me if I docked again they'd take the boat and my license and probably my freedom. So I took the boat and went, and I told myself the people upstream would find another way. They didn't. Eleven drowned that season trying to cross to the relief point on rafts. I read the names a year later in a newspaper I bought in Vientiane, and I read them standing in a hotel lobby, and then I ordered another drink and never came back to this stretch of river again."

Ivo said nothing for a long moment. The engine knocked. Water chuckled against the hull, finding its way in through the wound they'd given it running the night rapids.

"So why are we here now?" the boy asked finally.

Quentin looked at the gauge board. Fuel low. Pump margin thinner than he'd admit aloud. Ahead lay two days of river they could not run with a hull drinking like this and an engine missing beats, and behind them lay whatever Ravelin's men would do next now that the inspection had slipped through their fingers.

"Because the river doesn't give you a choice about coming home," he said. "It just waits until you're too broken to go anywhere else."

* * *

They tied off at the community landing below the temple, where the pilings wore rings of pale scar tissue from twenty years of mooring lines. Quentin jumped ashore first — an old habit, testing the boards before letting his crew trust them — and the moment his boots hit the planks he felt it: the village noticing him. Doors opening a hand's width. Faces in the lamplight, unreadable. Somewhere a dog started up and was hushed.

A girl of maybe ten stood at the end of the walkway, staring at the boat with open hunger, and when she saw the twins come down the gangway she grinned and shouted something over her shoulder, and suddenly there were children everywhere, swarming the rail, demanding to know if the twins were really twins, if the engine was really alive, if the tall quiet one — Ivo — was really the captain's son.

"He's not my son," Quentin said automatically, and then heard how it sounded, and said nothing more.

The crowd parted the way water parts around a piling, and Mai came through it.

She was older. Of course she was older; eleven years sits differently on a woman who runs a village than on a man who runs from one. Her hair had gone silver at the temples and she wore it short now, practical, and her hands were still the hands he remembered — broad-knuckled, net-scarred, hands that had hauled drowning people out of brown water while he sat in a hotel bar reading obituaries. She stopped six feet away and looked at him the way a fisherman looks at a fish he's already decided to throw back.

"Quentin Joubert," she said.

"Mai."

"You got old on the river."

"You got old here."

"I got old doing the work." No heat in it. That was the worst part — no heat at all, just the flat accounting tone of a woman balancing books she'd balanced a long time ago. Her eyes went past him to the boat, cataloguing the list, the stains along the waterline, the exhaustion in his crew's faces. "Your hull is cracked."

"A seam opened. We need two days, maybe three. Timber, pitch, a place to careen her. We'll pay."

"We don't want your money."

"Mai—"

"We don't want your money." She said it louder, and the children went quiet, and the adults pretending not to listen stopped pretending. "Money is what you gave us last time, Joubert. Money is easy. Money is what a man sends instead of himself." She turned and walked toward the village, and after a moment Quentin understood he was meant to follow, and hated that he understood.

* * *

Her house stood on the high ground above the flood line, the same house, though the stilts had been replaced and the woven walls re-woven many times. Inside, a single lamp burned. She poured rice whiskey into two glasses without asking whether he drank, set one before him, and sat across the table with her own untouched.

"Say it," she said.

"Say what?"

"Whatever speech you built on the way up the hill. I can see it sitting in your mouth. Say it so we can get past it."

Quentin looked at the whiskey. He'd rehearsed nothing, actually — he'd spent the approach to the landing rehearsing nothing, which was its own kind of cowardice — but she was right that something wanted to come out, some arrangement of words that would make eleven years sit easier in the room.

"I abandoned you," he said. "That's all of it. There's no version where I didn't. Ravelin threatened me and I chose the boat, and eleven people died who wouldn't have died if I'd been braver or stupider or both. I've known it since the day I read the names. I'm not here to be forgiven. I'm here because my boat is dying and your landing is the only one I trust, and I'll take whatever contempt comes with the timber."

Mai studied him for a long time. Outside, the frogs had started up, and beneath them, faintly, the sound of Sefa's voice in the engine room, teaching Ivo the names of parts.

"Do you know what we called you, after?" she said. "Not to your face. You weren't here to have a face. We called you *the man who counted.* Because when the customs men came, you stood on your deck and you counted. We could see you doing it. Counting the cost of staying against the cost of going, and the going cost less, so you went." She lifted her glass but didn't drink. "I don't say it to wound you. I say it because you should know that shame has a name here, and it isn't a curse. It's a measurement. It tells us exactly what you are worth, and what you owe."

"The debt," Quentin said. "Name it."

"Eleven dead is not a debt a man pays. If you'd said *eleven saved*, maybe. But they're dead, and whiskey won't raise them, and neither will timber." She set the glass down. "So here is what I name instead. You will careen your boat on my beach, and my people will help you, because your crew looks like they haven't slept in three days and one of them is a child who should eat. And when your boat is sound, you will not simply leave. There is a thing coming down the river toward you — I hear things, Joubert, the river tells this village everything — and when it arrives, you will stand. Here. With us. Whatever it costs you. That is the payment. Not money. Presence. The thing you withheld."

Quentin felt the words land somewhere below his ribs, in the place where he kept the memory of counting.

"You know about Ravelin's men."

"I know a customs launch passed downstream yesterday flying a flag it doesn't usually fly. I know a fuel seller in Pakxan sold information about a riverboat to a man with soft hands. I know that the officer who ruined you is the same officer whose name comes up whenever goods vanish from the customs sheds and witnesses stop answering their doors." She leaned forward. "You think you brought your trouble to my village. Your trouble has been my neighbor for eleven years. The difference is I never had anywhere to run."

* * *

They worked through the next day in a rhythm Quentin hadn't felt since the flood year itself. The villagers hauled the *Joubert* onto the sloping beach at low water with ropes and rollers and a hundred years of inherited technique, tilting her until the cracked seam stood clear of the river, and Sefa walked the length of the exposed planking with a mallet and a candle flame, reading the hull like scripture. The twins became instantly, permanently famous among the village children, and bore their fame with the modesty of two boys who had spent their lives being identical and had long ago decided fame was the only compensation. Ivo worked the pitch kettles without being asked, sleeves rolled, forearms scalded pink, and Mai's granddaughter appointed herself his supervisor and paid him in mangoes.

Quentin hammered planks and tried not to look at the beach.

But the beach looked at him. Every load of timber came from trees the flood had felled. Every rope-hand was the son or grandson of someone who'd waited at that landing. At midday an old man with a clouded eye sat down on the sand near the work site and began, quietly, to sing — a flood song, the kind women sang during the high-water year, and Quentin recognized the melody because he'd heard it every night for five nights while he loaded rice, and he drove his mallet harder and pretended the singing wasn't a knife.

Sefa found him at dusk, sitting on the keel with a shared bottle between his knees.

"The seam will hold," the engineer said, lowering himself onto the sand. "Two days of curing and she's sounder than before. Better timber than the original, honestly. These people build for floods."

"They do."

"Mai came by the engine room today." Sefa turned the bottle in his hands. "Asked questions. About you, about the map, about the man who died at our landing. I told her what I knew. She listened like a judge."

"She told me her price. She wants me to stand when Ravelin comes. Here."

"Will you?"

Quentin looked out at the river. Evening had turned it to hammered copper, and the fishing boats were coming home in a scattered fleet, lamps beginning to glow along their gunwales like a procession of small souls. Somewhere out there, downstream, a customs launch with the wrong flag was moving at its own pace, patient as silt.

"Eleven years ago I counted," he said, "and the count told me to run, and I've been running so long I forgot the count was ever a choice. She's right. Running isn't safety. It's just a slower way of drowning." He took the bottle from Sefa and drank, and the whiskey burned all the way down like a decision. "When he comes, I'll stand."

"Good." Sefa stood, brushed sand from his trousers, and paused. "One more thing. The girl — Linh. Ivo's been writing to her from every landing since Luang Prabang. Letters forward and back through the post offices, slow as monsoon mail. Today a letter came upriver with a trader." He held out a folded paper. "Ivo read it and went very quiet and hasn't spoken since. Thought you should know."

Quentin unfolded the page. The handwriting was quick, urgent, pressed hard enough to emboss the paper:

*They know what my father carried. It was never gold. Ravelin's ledgers are in that chest — names, dates, payments, the ships he sank and the men he drowned to hide them. He cannot let it surface. And Quentin — the trader who brought this letter says a gunboat has been assigned to the gorge. Three days.*

Quentin read it twice. Down on the beach, the work lamps were being lit one by one, and Ivo stood among them holding a pitch brush he'd stopped using, looking upriver at the darkening water as if he could see the future assembling itself out of it.

Three days. Quentin closed his fist around the letter and felt, for the first time in eleven years, that he was not running toward something or away from something, but standing on ground he might actually defend.

"Get the twins," he said. "Wake Mai. We're going to need more than pitch."
