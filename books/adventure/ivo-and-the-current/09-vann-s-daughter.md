## 9. Vann's Daughter

Luang Prabang came out of the morning mist the way a memory comes out of sleep — all at once, and older than you expected.

I was at the rail before Sefa called me, watching the town assemble itself through the fog: the long stair of the temple roofs, gold going dull in the wet air; the palms along the embankment; the market already shouting itself awake on the far bank. The *Joubert* slid in against the current like she'd been here before, which she had, though not with me at the rail. Quentin kept us out of the main anchorage. He always did. We took a mooring below the old ferry landing, where the fishing boats clustered and nobody asked a cargo boat why she sat so low in the water or why her captain wouldn't meet the customs launch that came sniffing down the channel.

"Two hours," Quentin said. "Sefa and I take the manifest to the depot. Twins, you're on deck. Ivo—" he looked at me a beat too long, the way he'd been looking at me since the night water, since he learned what I'd been keeping folded in oilcloth under the floor plate of the engine room— "Ivo stays aboard."

"Yes, Captain," I said.

He knew I was lying. I knew he knew. Neither of us said it, because that's how things worked between us: we carried each other's silences like ballast, and the boat stayed level.

The launch puttered off toward the depot and the twins immediately began arguing about who would win a fight between a monitor lizard and a dog, which is how they talked when they were nervous, and I waited until they were deep enough into it — the twins could argue anything for an hour if you let them establish terms — and then I went below.

* * *

The map was where it always lived now, wrapped in its second skin of waxed cloth, wedged behind the spare injector pump where the heat was worst and no one reached without a reason. I took it out and held it a while without unwrapping it. I didn't need to look anymore. I had drawn it twice from memory in my head during the night runs, tracing the river's bends against Vann's ink lines until the two matched so well I sometimes forgot which one was real.

Vann's hand had shaken when he drew it. Fever does that. But the river doesn't forgive shaking hands, and neither had Vann — he'd redrawn the stretch above the gorge three times, muttering depths to himself like prayers, marking the drowned barge's position against a rock shaped like a sleeping ox. *She sits in thirty feet of green,* he'd told me, the last night, his voice coming apart at the edges. *Thirty feet, boy, and everything the French ever stole from this river sitting in her hold.*

I had promised him. That was the whole of it. A man dying on our deck, and I gave him water, and then I gave him my word, and somewhere between those two gifts he stopped being a stranger and became something else — a debt, maybe, or a father-shaped hole that happened to be my size.

His daughter was in Luang Prabang. Her name was Linh. She taught letters at a school above the market, he said, near the tamarind tree that leaned over the wall. *Tell her I'm sorry about the years. Tell her the map is hers. Tell her—* and then the fever took the rest, and by dawn the river had taken him too, and we buried him on the bank above the flood line because Quentin said even strangers get a grave.

I tucked the map back behind the pump and went up to find the twins.

"Kham," I said. "You know the market."

"I know every market."

"You know *this* market?"

He grinned at me over the rail of the argument he was losing to his brother. "Better than I know my own face."

"Then walk me past the school above it. The one with the tamarind tree."

Both twins looked at me then, and for once their faces did the same thing at the same time, which meant they understood exactly what I was asking and exactly what it would cost me if Quentin found out. Kham's grin faded into something almost gentle.

"He said stay aboard."

"He said that to me. Not to you."

"Noi," Kham said to his brother, "the boy has learned to argue like a customs man."

"That's insulting," Noi said. "Customs men don't argue. They just take." But he was already untying the skiff.

* * *

The school sat where Vann said it would, above the market noise, behind a wall the color of old bone, and the tamarind tree leaned over it exactly as a dying man remembers trees leaning — more than they really do, because memory wants shade. Children's voices came through the gate, chanting numbers, and I stood outside it longer than I should have, holding nothing in my hands, because I had left the map hidden and didn't know yet whether I was a boy delivering a message or a thief deciding what to steal.

Kham nudged me. "You want me to knock, or do you want to stand there growing roots?"

"I'm thinking."

"You've been thinking since the landing. I watched you think the whole way up the hill. It looks heavy. Let someone else carry it a minute."

So I knocked.

A woman answered — not old, not young, with chalk dust on her fingers and a tiredness around her eyes that looked earned rather than worn. She saw two river boys in salt-stiff shirts and her face did the thing faces do when they're deciding whether you're trouble.

"We're looking for Linh," I said. "Daughter of Vann. Of—" I almost said *of nowhere*, because that's what Vann had sounded like, a man belonging to no place, but I caught it. "Of the river. Prospector. He was working the tributaries above Vientiane."

Something moved behind her eyes. Not surprise. Recognition, arriving late, like thunder after lightning.

"You'd better come in," she said. "Not the children's room. The side veranda."

Her name was Linh. She was Vann's daughter, and she poured us tea on the veranda with steady hands, and only the way she held the cup — too tight, knuckles pale — told me what the name had done to her.

"When?" she asked.

"Eleven days ago. We buried him above the flood line, on the east bank. There's a cairn. I stacked it myself."

She nodded, and drank her tea, and looked out at the tamarind leaves moving, and I understood she was doing what I did on the boat when the work stopped — filling the silence with something, anything, so the feeling couldn't get in. I knew that trick. I'd invented half of it myself.

"He wrote to me twice a year," she said finally. "Always the same letter with different dates. *Still looking. Still short of money. Still sorry.*" Her mouth bent, not quite a smile. "I stopped answering three years ago. I told myself it was because the letters hurt. That was a lie. I stopped answering because I decided he'd chosen the river over us, and I wanted him to feel that choice." She set the cup down. "And now some boy I've never met climbs my wall to tell me he's dead, and I find out the last thing he did with his life was try to give me something. So tell me, river boy. What did he give you to bring me? Because I know my father. Whatever it is, it isn't simple."

"It's a map," I said.

And then, because she was looking at me the way Quentin looks at a sandbar that has moved overnight — measuring, recalculating everything — I heard myself keep talking.

"It's a map to a sunken barge. Colonial era. Above the gorge, below the sleeping-ox rock, thirty feet down. He drew it himself. He traded it to us for passage, and the Captain refused, and I gave him water instead, and he died anyway, and I promised him I'd bring it to you." I made myself stop there. Some part of me wanted to say more — wanted to confess the hiding, the lying, the weeks of carrying it like a coal in my chest — but the words ran out on their own, and I sat there with my tea untouched and my heart going like Sefa's engine at full steam.

Linh was quiet for a long time.

"A barge," she repeated. "The *Mekhong Star*."

I blinked. "You know it?"

"Everyone who grew up on this river knows the story, the way children know ghost stories. A colonial transport lost in the gorge floods, forty years ago. Officially it was carrying tin and machinery. Unofficially—" she glanced toward the classroom window, lowered her voice— "unofficially, everyone said it was carrying the administration's real accounts. And my father spent twenty years and two marriages chasing it, because he believed the unofficial version, and because he believed it would finally be enough. Gold. Enough to come home as a rich man instead of a beggar with a story." She laughed, softly, terribly. "He used to say, *Linh, when I find her, you'll have shoes that fit.*"

"What's wrong with that?" I asked, because something in her voice said everything was.

"Nothing. Everything. It depends what's actually in the hold." She stood, walked to the veranda rail, came back. "River boy. Ivo, you said your name was?"

"Ivo."

"Ivo. My father wasn't a prospector. Not really. Prospectors dig. My father *remembered*. Before the war he was a clerk — a cargo clerk, in the customs house at Vientiane, under the colonial administration, and after it, under whoever replaced them. He saw manifests. He saw what went onto barges and what got written down as having gone onto barges, and the difference between those two lists was worth more than any mine." Her eyes were bright and hard now. "He was dismissed for asking questions. He was lucky that's all it cost him. Two other clerks who asked the same questions were transferred to river postings, and their boats sank, and nobody investigated, because the men who sign the investigation orders are the men being investigated."

The courtyard sounds fell away. The children chanting numbers, the market roaring below the wall, the tamarind leaves — all of it went distant, the way sound goes when the engine cuts and suddenly you can hear the water itself.

"The ledgers are still sealed in the strongroom of the barge," Linh said. "My father told me once, drunk, the only honest conversation we ever had. Confiscated goods sold off the books. Payments recorded. Names. And worse than the goods — the disposals. Men who knew too much, listed as *lost overboard*, with dates and witnesses. Witnesses who later drowned too." She turned to face me fully. "It isn't treasure, Ivo. It's testimony. Every page of it. And the man who signed those pages — the young officer whose initials are on every disposal order — he's still alive, still serving, still powerful. Do you understand what my father handed you? Not a fortune. A weapon. Aimed at a man who kills people for knowing less than you now know."

I thought of Ravelin. I hadn't known his name then — I'd only seen him at the landing in Vientiane, the smooth man with the customs authority and the questions, the man whose voice made Quentin go quiet in a way I'd never heard before. I thought of the search at the lock. The night rapids. The seam we cracked escaping them. All of that had happened because two killers and a corrupt officer thought my oilcloth bundle was a treasure map.

They'd been right about the map. They'd been wrong about everything else.

"There's a man," I said slowly, "who boarded us. Customs. He asked about Vann. He lied about it — he said Vann owed duties, but he asked about him like a hunter asks about blood. Our Captain knows him. From before. From something bad."

Linh went very still. "Describe him."

"Tall. Gray at the temples, but not old. Speaks softly. Wears his uniform like it's a costume he finds slightly embarrassing. One glove, always, on the left hand — even in this heat."

"*Ravelin.*" She said the name like spitting out a fishbone. Then she sat down across from me, and took both my hands in her chalk-dusted ones, and her grip was stronger than I expected. "Listen to me. If Ravelin is hunting that map, then he knows what's in the barge better than my father ever did. Those initials on the disposal orders — Ivo, they aren't just initials. They're his. He was the junior officer on that route. The barge didn't sink by accident. It was scuttled to bury the evidence, and everyone connected to loading it was scattered or silenced, and my father survived because he was small and careful and grieving and nobody thought a clerk's son would spend his whole life swimming back toward a wreck." Her voice dropped. "Ravelin has spent thirty years making sure that barge stays buried. You are carrying the one thing in the world that can end him. Do you see now why I asked what he gave you? It isn't simple. It was never going to be simple."

* * *

We walked back down through the market with the chant of the school still in my ears, and I carried Linh's words the way I carried the map — close, hot, dangerous. She was coming to the boat at dusk, she said, when the depot closed and Quentin returned. She wanted to speak to the Captain herself. She wanted to see the map with her own eyes, to read her father's handwriting one last time, and I found I wanted that too — wanted to watch her unfold it and make it hers, so that it stopped being only my promise and became ours.

At the landing, Kham bought sugarcane from a woman with a baby on her hip and chewed it thoughtfully all the way to the skiff.

"You know what this means," he said.

"That we're in trouble."

"That we were *already* in trouble, and now we know its name." He spat a fiber into the water. "Noi owes me ten kip. I said the map was worth killing for. He said it was just paper."

"It is just paper," I said.

Kham looked at me.

"Just paper," I said again, "with thirty drowned men written on it."

The *Joubert* sat low and patient at her mooring, and as we pulled alongside I saw Quentin standing at the rail, back from the depot early, watching us come in with an expression I'd learned to read the way I'd learned to read water — the flat calm that means something underneath is moving fast.

He didn't ask where we'd been. He just looked at me, and then past me, at the woman in the teacher's clothes already descending the embankment stairs with a cloth bundle under her arm and her chin set like a woman walking into weather.

Quentin's jaw tightened.

"Ivo," he said quietly. "Who is that?"

"That," I said, "is Vann's daughter. And Captain — the gold isn't gold."

Below us, the river slid past, brown and patient, carrying everything it had ever swallowed somewhere in its dark.
