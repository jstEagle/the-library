## 11. The Twins' Reconnaissance

### Kiet

My brother snores like a bilge pump with a stone in it, which is why I was awake when the fuel seller's boy came running past the boat at midnight, barefoot, breathless, headed for the customs house with his shirt buttoned wrong.

I lay in the dark of the fo'c'sle and did the arithmetic I always do at night: tide, current, the creak of the hull telling me where the water wanted us. Then I added a new sum. A boy running to the customs house after midnight is not a boy going fishing.

I put my hand over Noi's mouth before he could inhale his next snore.

"Up," I whispered. "Trouble's walking."

He came awake the way he always does — all at once, no fog, eyes open before his body finishes moving. That's the difference between us that nobody can see because we look the same from any distance you care to name. I wake slow and mean. He wakes fast and grinning.

"Whose trouble?" he breathed.

"The kind that wears a uniform."

We went out through the forward hatch, quiet as oil, and dropped onto the dock planks still warm from the day. The moon was a fingernail paring over the river. Down the landing, lamplight burned in the customs house, and two silhouettes stood on its veranda talking to the fat duty officer — Ravelin's men, the ones who'd come aboard at Vientiane smelling of cigarettes and other people's fear. One of them had a paper in his hand. Even at this distance I knew what kind of paper it was by how he held it: flat, official, the kind of thing men use instead of guns until the guns arrive.

Noi touched my elbow. Two taps. *Follow.*

One tap back. *Wait.*

That's our whole language most nights. We learned it hauling cargo before we had words for anything but hunger, back when we were small enough to hide in the rope locker and steal oranges from the hold above. Two taps means move. One tap means think first. Quentin thinks we invented it for work. We invented it for exactly this.

The silhouettes finished their talk. The one with the paper folded it away into his breast pocket, and both men walked down the landing toward the town, not hurrying, the walk of men who own every hour they're in.

We followed them into Vientiane.

* * *

### Noi

Here is what my brother will never admit: he needs me. Not for muscle — we're twins, we're the same size, whatever he eats goes to my ribs too. He needs me because Kiet sees everything and understands half of it, and I understand the other half. Together we make one whole person who should never be allowed near a card table.

Vientiane at midnight smells of charcoal and drain water and frangipani gone over-ripe in the heat. The two customs men walked like tourists who didn't want to look like tourists — too casual, checking reflections in shop glass. Kiet and I split without discussing it, the way water splits around a post. He took the far side of the street. I took the shadows under the awnings, close enough to hear their voices carry.

"—three days," the tall one was saying. "Not before. The *Penghou* comes up from Pakse on the third morning, and she doesn't run at night, so it has to be daylight at the gorge."

"And if the Joubert runs early?"

The tall one laughed. It was an ugly sound, the laugh of a man describing weather to someone standing in it. "Then she runs into the surge with no pilot chart and a boy at the wheel. Ravelin says let the river do the work if it wants to."

I felt that sentence go into me like a fishhook. *A boy at the wheel.* They meant Ivo. Everyone on this river had started meaning Ivo lately, the way weather starts meaning rain.

They turned into a teahouse that was still lit, the kind of place that stays open for men whose business keeps late hours. I couldn't follow them inside without becoming a mirror in their tea. So I did the next best thing, which is the thing Kiet taught me before either of us could swim properly: I found the back wall and listened through it.

Teahouse walls in Vientiane are bamboo and plaster. Plaster is generous.

Inside, chairs scraped. Someone poured. And then a voice I hadn't heard since the deck of our own boat said, "You're certain it's still aboard?"

Ravelin. In person. In a room I could have put my fist through if I'd been three years older or considerably more stupid.

"Certain enough," said the tall man. "The old engineer's been coaling like a woman preparing for a birth. They're provisioning heavy. They run soon."

"They run when I say they run." A cup clicked down. "The gunboat takes them at the gorge. Nobody boards the Joubert before that, nobody frightens the crew, and above all—" the pause was long enough that I could count my own heartbeats, four of them, five— "nobody touches the boy. If the map passes hands, I want to know whose. Understood?"

Understood, they said. Chairs again. I was already moving along the alley behind the teahouse, counting windows, marking doors, doing the other half of the job — because hearing a plan is worth nothing unless you can bring home the shape of it whole.

Three days. The *Penghou*. Daylight at the gorge.

I slipped out the alley's mouth and there was Kiet, leaning against a spirit house with his arms crossed like he'd been waiting all century and found the wait acceptable.

"Well?" he said.

"Gunboat at the gorge. Three days. They want Ivo untouched and the map watched." I grinned at him, because that's my half. "Your turn. Tell me something I don't know."

Kiet smiled the way he does when he's about to ruin someone's week. "The fuel seller took money tonight. From the customs house. His boy ran the message — the Joubert's berth, our draft, everything. Somebody on this dock sold us."

* * *

### Kiet

Here is what my brother will never admit: I had already known about the fuel seller. I'd watched old Thong count his money twice at closing, which no honest man does, and I'd seen the bruise on his knuckles that wasn't from work. But I needed Noi to find the gunboat himself, because a thing you discover sticks in you like a splinter, and a thing your brother tells you slides off like rain off tin.

We are not sentimental, the twins. But we are a single instrument, and an instrument plays best when both strings are struck.

We ran the rooftops route home — low roofs, tile, the ones that don't announce you — and dropped to the dock on the downstream side where the pilings shadow out the moon. The Joubert sat at her moorings riding light, and I looked at her the way you look at a parent you've just realized can be hurt.

She's an old riverboat, iron-boned, wooden-skinned, patched in nine places Sefa will tell you about if you ask and eleven places he won't. She's carried us since before we had a last name anyone would use. And in three days, if we did nothing, a gunboat would come up the gorge and take her apart looking for a dead man's map.

Noi beat me through the hatch, which meant he'd cheat-running, which meant he was scared. Good. Scared is useful. It's calm that gets people drowned.

Quentin was awake. Of course he was awake; the man sleeps like a heron, one eye on the water. He sat in the deckhouse with the lamp turned low and a chart open on his knees, and when we came in he didn't startle, just looked at us over the top of it with those gray river-stone eyes.

"You were out."

"We were out," Noi agreed.

"You were followed back by nothing, which means you were careful, which means you found something worth being careful about." He closed the chart. "Sit. Both of you. Start at the beginning and don't decorate."

So we told it. Noi gave the gunboat, the *Penghou*, the three days, the daylight-at-the-gorge arithmetic. I gave the fuel seller, the money, the message boy. We handed Quentin the night piece by piece the way you pass bricks down a line, and we watched him build the wall in his face while he listened.

When we finished he was quiet a long time. The lamp hissed. Somewhere down the dock a dog decided against barking.

"Three days," he said finally.

"Two and a half," I said. "Dawn's already spent."

"And they know our draft." He rubbed his jaw, and I saw the tiredness in him that he shows to no one, the tiredness of a man who has been running for fifteen years and has just learned the road ahead is blocked. "They know how much water we draw. They'll pick the crossing point to suit it."

"So we don't cross where we draw," Noi said.

Quentin looked at him.

"Sefa's been saying the monsoon surge is coming early," Noi went on, faster now, the idea arriving as he spoke it, which is how his ideas always arrive. "If the surge comes, the gorge isn't a channel anymore, it's a argument. A gunboat draws too much to argue. But we—"

"We're shallow," I finished. "We're the shallowest thing on this river that can still float a cargo. We could go where the *Penghou* grounds herself."

The silence after that had a different texture. Quentin looked at the chart, then at the dark window where the river made its endless small noises against the hull, and I watched him do the thing he does — weigh every course not in fathoms but in names. The twins. Ivo. Sefa. Himself.

"No one speaks of this yet," he said at last. "Not to Ivo, not to Sefa, not to the village. Tomorrow I talk to Mai about the fuel seller — quietly, her way, not ours. And tomorrow night we provision like we're running south, so Thong sells Ravelin a lie worth the money he took." He rolled the chart closed and tapped it once against his palm. "You did well. Both of you. Now sleep, because in three days I'm going to need hands that don't shake."

We went aft. At the hatch Noi stopped me with a hand on my shoulder and pointed down the deck.

Ivo was sitting on the cargo cover in the moonlight, knees up, awake, looking at the black water with the map-shaped secret still living somewhere behind his ribs. He didn't know yet. None of them below decks knew yet.

"He should hear it from us," Noi whispered. "Not from the gunboat."

I thought about it, one tap's worth.

"Yeah," I said. "But not tonight. Tonight let him have his last easy dream. Tomorrow the whole crew learns what we learned — and tomorrow night, we stop being passengers on somebody else's plan."

Below us, the river slid past in the dark, patient as a debt, carrying the news of the surge down from the mountains toward the gorge where, in three days, everything we loved was going to be waiting.
