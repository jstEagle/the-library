## 12. Mai's Price

Mai counted the boats twice before she let herself believe the number.

Eleven. Eleven fishing hulls drawn up on the mud below the village, their nets hung to dry on bamboo racks like the wings of tired birds. She had counted them every morning for thirty years, since her husband was alive and the fleet was his pride and hers by marriage, and in all those years the count had never once come back wrong. A woman who could not count her own boats could not protect them, and a headwoman who could not protect her people was only a name with a house attached.

Eleven this morning. She stood on the rise above the landing with her hands folded into her sleeves and watched the river do what it always did — carry its silt, its secrets, its strangers — and she thought about the boat moored at her dock with its patched seam and its haunted captain.

The *Joubert* had been there four days now. Long enough for Sefa to pull the cracked plate and weld it. Long enough for the twins to charm half the children in the village and offend the other half. Long enough for Ivo to help mend nets without being asked, his fingers quick and patient, learning knots the way other boys learned songs. And long enough for Quentin Joubert to stand at her fire three nights running, saying nothing, watching the water as if it owed him an apology he hadn't yet decided how to collect.

She had known him before, of course. That was the trouble with rivers. They carried everything back eventually — driftwood, corpses, debts.

* * *

The men came at dusk on the fifth day, and they did not come quietly.

Mai heard the engines first — two longboats running fast against the current's grain, which meant power, which meant money, which meant trouble. By the time she reached the landing the boats were already swinging in, six men in them with the flat, well-fed look of men who worked for someone rather than themselves. Their leader stepped onto the mud without waiting for permission, and Mai marked that. A man who steps onto your ground uninvited is telling you whose ground he believes it is.

"You're the headwoman." It wasn't a question. He was younger than she expected, soft through the middle, with a government-issue pistol riding his hip like it had never been fired and never would be. "We're looking for a riverboat. Cargo vessel, patched hull, runs under a French name."

"There are many boats on this river."

"Not many with a captain named Joubert."

Mai kept her face still. Behind her, down the beach, she could see the *Joubert*'s mast above the tree line, could see the twins' laundry strung from its yard like flags of a very small nation. There was no hiding it. There was no pretending. The question was not whether these men would find the boat but what it would cost to make them leave satisfied.

"Joubert," she said. "I don't know the name."

The man smiled. He had good teeth and bad eyes. "That's fine. We'll look anyway." He turned to his men and said something low, and two of them started up the path toward the village while the others drifted along the shore — and one of them stopped, and looked out at the water, and then looked at the boats drawn up on the mud, and smiled the way a man smiles when he has found something better than what he came for.

"Nice fleet," the leader said, turning back to her. "Lot of fish comes off this stretch, I'd guess. Lot of tax owed on fish, too. Fishing licenses. Landing fees. Hard to keep track, in a place like this." He let the words sit in the air between them like smoke. "Hard to keep track, unless a man's memory gets refreshed."

"I have nothing to refresh it with."

"Then we'll be back tomorrow," he said pleasantly, "and the day after, and every day until somebody remembers something. Enjoy your evening, grandmother."

He touched the brim of his cap — mock courtesy, the kind Ravelin's people were famous for, though she didn't know the name Ravelin yet, wouldn't learn it until later that night when Quentin told her — and went back down to his boat. The engines tore the dusk apart and then the river closed over the sound, and Mai stood on her landing counting eleven boats in the failing light, and felt the shape of what was coming the way old women feel storms in their knees.

* * *

They came back before dawn, but not to the landing.

Mai woke to orange light on her ceiling and knew before she reached the door. Down the beach, where the fleet lay drawn up on the mud, three of the boats were burning — not smoldering, burning, kerosene-fed flames standing ten feet tall against the last of the night, and men were running from the village with buckets and wet blankets and screaming, and the fire was answering them with a sound like tearing cloth.

She ran. Of course she ran; she was sixty-one years old and she ran anyway, down the slope, through the wet grass, her lungs burning worse than the boats. The heat drove her back twenty feet from the waterline. Three hulls — she counted them through the smoke, because counting was all she had ever had — three of her eleven, including the *Nang Kham*, the oldest, the one her husband had built with his own hands the year they married, the one every girl in the village had learned to pole from.

Old Somchai was on his knees in the mud in front of the fire, and he was not screaming. That was worse. He was just kneeling there, watching forty years of his life go up in black smoke, and his grandson had his arms around the old man's shoulders and the boy was crying hard enough to shake them both.

By full light the fire was out and the damage was done and everyone knew what everyone else knew: this was not banditry. Bandits take. This was a message, and messages are written in whatever is closest to hand. The fishing fleet was closest to hand.

Mai stood in the char and ash with her people around her, and she made herself say the thing that had to be said.

"No one speaks to them again. No one sells them food, no one sells them fuel, no one tells them anything, no matter what they offer or threaten. We are done being polite." She looked at the faces, young and old, and saw fear in most of them and anger in some and in a few — the fishermen, the ones who had lost boats — something harder than either. "And I will speak to the captain."

* * *

She found him on the *Joubert*'s deck, already awake, already dressed, watching the burned stretch of shoreline through the rail. He had the look of a man who had watched fires before and knew exactly what they meant and hated himself for knowing.

"They came for you," Mai said. No greeting. The time for greetings was over.

Quentin didn't turn. "Yes."

"And you knew they might. You knew when you brought that boat behind my dock that whoever hunted you might follow the smell of your engine smoke straight to us."

"Yes." He turned then, and she gave him credit for not flinching, for meeting her eyes. His face was gray in the early light. "I told you the risk when I asked to tie up. I told you plainly."

"You told me they wanted a map." Her voice stayed level, which cost her more than shouting would have. "You told me a dying man's paper. You did not tell me the man hunting it burns fishing fleets to make grandmothers talk. You did not tell me his name was Ravelin, or that he is customs chief at Vientiane, or that he has gunboats. You gave me the shape of the danger but not the weight of it, Captain, and there is a difference, and my people are standing in that difference right now, in the ash."

Behind him, at the rail, the boy appeared — Ivo, silent as a cat, drawn by the voices. Mai saw him look past her at the blackened beach and saw something move through his face that she recognized, because she had worn it herself once, years ago, watching another fire from another shore. Guilt that hasn't learned yet that guilt is useless without action.

"Three boats," Quentin said quietly. "I'm sorry. I'll pay for—"

"Pay." Mai laughed, and it came out ugly even to her own ears. "With what? With gold? Do you have gold, Captain? Or do you have a map to gold, which is the same as having nothing?" She stepped closer. "You will not pay me money. Money is how men like Ravelin settle things, and look what their settling looks like. My husband built that boat with his hands. You cannot pay for it. There is no price. That is what I have come to tell you — that the debt has changed."

"The debt."

"When you ran supplies here during the flood year, you fed us, and then you left when we needed you to stay, and I called that a debt and I let you work it off with a patched roof and a bag of rice and I told myself it was settled." She shook her head slowly. "I was wrong. Debts between people are not settled like accounts. They are carried. And now you have carried a new one into my village and set it down on my beach and it has caught fire."

"Mai—"

"Listen to me." She raised one hand and he stopped, because he was a man who understood authority when he heard it, whatever else he'd lost. "Those men will come back. Not tomorrow, maybe. But they will come back, and next time it will not be boats. Next time it will be a house, or a granary, or a person. They have shown us what they are. There is no neutral ground left on this river, Captain. Neutral ground is just ground that hasn't burned yet."

Down the beach, Somchai's grandson was dragging the black ribs of the *Nang Kham* out of the shallows, piece by piece, stacking them like bones. Mai watched him do it and let the silence stretch until Quentin broke it.

"What do you want from me?"

"Now you ask the right question." She turned to face him fully. "Ravelin has a gunboat. Sefa told my son it patrols the gorge, and that it will be looking for you there within days. You mean to run it — I can see it in you, you've already decided, you're only deciding how. So here is my price, and it is not rice and it is not roofs." Her voice dropped, and she let every year of it into the words. "Help us. Not with money. Stand with us. When Ravelin falls — and he will fall, because men like him always build their houses on the river bottom — this village needs to be standing on the right side of that fall, openly, visibly, so that no man ever again thinks our boats are cheap to burn. Take our divers with you when you raise what you mean to raise. Put our names in whatever you put in those ledgers' place. Make us part of it, Captain, or take your boat and your map and your ghosts and go, and be remembered here as the man who ran twice."

The words landed. She watched them land, watched his jaw tighten and his hands close on the rail, watched the boy Ivo go very still beside him.

"You're asking me to fight," Quentin said.

"I'm asking you to stop running. Fighting may follow. It usually does." Mai allowed herself one small, bitter smile. "You've spent years being nobody, Captain. I understand it. Nobody can't be hurt. Nobody can't lose anyone. But nobody also can't help anyone, and the flood year taught me that the hard way, and now the fire has taught my people the same lesson. The river doesn't care whether you run toward something or away from it. It carries you either way. The only choice you get is what you're carrying when you arrive."

For a long moment neither of them spoke. The river moved beneath the dock, brown and patient, carrying its silt, its secrets, its strangers. Somewhere down the beach a child was singing a mourning song for a boat, the old one, the one mothers sang when the water took someone, and the sound of it rose thin over the char.

"I need to talk to my crew," Quentin said finally.

"Talk to them tonight. At my fire. All of you." Mai turned to go, then stopped, and looked back at him, and softened by exactly one degree — no more; she had earned the right to be hard, but not the right to be cruel. "The boy mended nets well. Better than my own sons. Whatever else happens, Captain — whatever you decide — know that you raised him right, even if you did it by accident."

She walked back up the slope through the wet grass, past the stacked bones of the *Nang Kham*, past Somchai kneeling in the mud, and she did not look back at the boat again. She had said what there was to say. Now the river would do what the river always did.

Carry everything back, eventually. Even Quentin Joubert, if he let it.

That night, at her fire, she would hear their answer. But even before the crew gathered, even before Quentin opened his mouth, she saw the way the boy Ivo sat down among her people as if he already belonged, and the way the twins fell to arguing with her sons about fish traps, and the way Sefa quietly began inspecting her neighbors' engines without being asked — and Mai, who had counted boats for thirty years and never once been wrong, counted heads around her fire and understood that the answer had already arrived.

It remained only for the captain to catch up to his own crew.
