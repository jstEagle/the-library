## 13. Storm Season Logic

Sefa had spent thirty years listening to engines, and in that time she had learned that machines told the truth the way rivers did — slowly, and only to those who stayed long enough to hear it. The Joubert's engine was talking to her now. It said: *I am tired. I have a cracked seam two meters aft of my bedplate, water in places water should never be, and if you ask me for one more hard run before you patch me properly, I will make you regret it.*

She wiped her hands on a rag already black as tar and sat back on her heels in the engine room's amber light. Above her, through the deck plates, came the sounds of a boat at anchor pretending not to be afraid: the twins arguing about cards, Ivo's footsteps crossing and recrossing the aft deck like a man pacing out a cell, Quentin up top where he always went when a decision was chewing on him, smoking with his back to the wheel.

Three days. That was what the twins had brought back from their reconnaissance, breathless and grinning and pale all at once. Three days until a gunboat came down from the north station and closed the gorge like a hand closing around a throat.

Sefa looked at the crack again. She ran her thumb along it the way you'd touch an old scar. Then she climbed the ladder, because the plan had been forming in her chest since midnight, and plans like that didn't keep.

* * *

Quentin was at the rail, watching the village lights come on across the brown water. Mai's village smelled of woodsmoke and drying fish, and tonight it also smelled of grief — the burned fishing fleet still lay along the shallows downstream, hulls split open like seed pods, their char drifting on the current in slow black commas.

"You've been down there four hours," Quentin said without turning. "Tell me the truth about the hull."

"The truth is a thing with teeth," Sefa said. She leaned beside him. "You want it anyway?"

"I married a woman who told me truths with teeth. I can take one more."

Sefa almost smiled. Almost. "The seam is worse than I told the twins. I can hold it with oakum and prayer for maybe two hundred kilometers of easy water. The gorge is not easy water. If we run the gorge with this hull, we are betting every life aboard on my patch holding against pressure it was never meant to hold."

Quentin exhaled smoke. "Then we don't run the gorge."

"We have three days."

"Then in three days we're gone, upstream or overland, and Ravelin's gunboat can guard an empty river."

"Where?" Sefa asked. It wasn't cruelty. It was arithmetic. "Overland with a boatload of cargo and no road? Upstream into the next patrol station? Quentin. You know me. You know I don't say impossible things lightly." She turned to face him fully. "So I'm going to say one now, and then I'm going to tell you how it might not be."

He looked at her. In the last light his face was all planes and shadow, a man carved by weather and refusal. "Say it."

"Ride the surge."

The word hung there between them. Downriver, thunder muttered somewhere past the mountains, the monsoon clearing its throat.

"The storm front coming down from the north hits the highlands tomorrow night," Sefa said. "When it does, the gorge fills. Not floods — *fills*. The whole river compresses into that canyon and runs three times its normal volume, and the surface rises six, seven meters, enough to drown the worst of the rocks. For perhaps four hours, the gorge isn't a rapids. It's a single moving thing. One wave train, end to end, running downhill faster than anything with a propeller should go."

"And no boat alive can follow us into that," Quentin said slowly.

"No boat with a sane captain," Sefa agreed. "A gunboat draws too much, rides too high, has too much freeboard to control in standing waves. Their commander will know it. He'll wait at the mouth for the surge to pass and pick up whatever's left of us after." She let that sit. "But here is the tooth in the truth, Quentin. The surge doesn't care what we're carrying. It doesn't care about your skill or my patch or Ivo's promise. For four hours we are cargo in the river's hands, and the river has never once been gentle with what it carries."

* * *

They gathered in the saloon that night — all of them, because Sefa refused to propose mutiny against physics to a partial crew. The lamp swung gently with the current. Rain began against the windows, soft at first, testing.

Sefa laid it out the way she laid out an engine problem: parts first, then assembly, then the ways it could fail.

"One: the hull. I re-pack the seam tomorrow with everything I have, and I rig a second pump off the main shaft so we pump while we run. Two: timing. The surge crest reaches the gorge head roughly six hours after the storm makes landfall — I've ridden it twice, years ago, in a boat smaller than this one and dumber than me. We enter at the crest, not before, not after. Before, and we're fighting full rapids. After, and the water drops out from under us onto the rocks it was hiding. Three: steering. In that much water there is no steering, not really. There is only trim and nerve. We point the bow down the throat, we keep her square to the waves, and we do not — *do not* — try to fight the current when it takes us sideways. Fighting it is how boats fold."

"And how do we know where the throat is, in the dark, in that?" Kiet asked. One twin, then the other; Sefa had stopped trying to tell them apart in lamplight years ago and simply addressed the pair as one creature with two mouths.

"Linh," Ivo said quietly.

Everyone turned. The boy sat near the lamp with Vann's map flattened under his palm, and something in his face had changed since Luang Prabang — some boy-thing burned away, some man-thing set in its place. "Her father ran the gorge eleven times. She said he used to talk about it like other men talk about weather. She remembers what he said. The landmarks, the bends, where the old channel runs. And—" Ivo hesitated, then pushed forward. "And she wants to come. She says the ledgers are half hers, and she won't wait ashore to find out if we sink."

"It's a child's memory of a dead man's stories," Quentin said.

"It's more than anyone else has," Ivo said. Not defiantly. Just flat, the way facts are flat.

Silence. The rain thickened against the glass. Sefa watched Quentin look at the boy — watched him see, perhaps, the shape of himself thirty years younger, all promise and no permission — and watched him lose the argument with himself before he opened his mouth.

"She rides in the pilothouse," Quentin said finally. "Lashed to something solid. And if she's wrong about even one bend—"

"She won't be," Ivo said. "Vann didn't survive twenty years on this river by being wrong about water."

* * *

Later, when the others had gone, Sefa found Quentin alone in the pilothouse. He stood at the window with the wheel loose in his hands, not steering, just holding it the way a man holds a railing over a long drop. Lightning walked along the far ridgeline, silent, counting down.

"You haven't said yes," she observed.

"I haven't said anything."

"You've been standing there for an hour saying nothing very loudly."

That earned her half a laugh, ground out of him like a reluctant gear engaging. He was quiet a while longer. Then: "Do you remember Phnom Thiek?"

Sefa went still. He almost never said the name of the place. "I remember you don't talk about it."

"My wife and I ran a flood year there. Much like this one. The water was high, the money was good, and I was young enough to think the river owed me something for my courage." His voice was level, which was how Sefa knew the cost of it. "There was a ferry crossing below the town. Families waiting on the bank because the regular ferryman wouldn't go out in it. I told myself I'd make one trip. One trip, good pay, everyone safe." He turned the wheel a few degrees, uselessly. "I made the trip fine. On the way back, empty, going too fast because I wanted my supper and my bed, I hit a submerged tree the flood had moved that morning. Swamped her. Eleven people were on that crossing the next day, waiting for a boat that was on the bottom. The flood took four of them before anyone else could reach the bank."

"That wasn't cowardice, Quentin. That was haste."

"It was both. They always travel together." He looked at her, and behind his eyes Sefa saw the machinery of him turning over, the great flywheel of his fear, spinning thirty years without a bearing change. "Every time I've run hard water since, I've felt him back there. That younger man. Telling me I owe the river a debt I can't ever pay, so I'd better be perfect, so I'd better never want anything, because wanting is what sank the boat." A muscle worked in his jaw. "Ravelin knows that about me. He's counted on it since the day he ruined me. He thinks I'll run forever rather than stand anywhere, and he thinks I'll refuse the gorge because refusing is what I do."

"Is he right?"

Quentin was silent so long that the rain filled the silence for him. When he spoke, his voice had changed — lower, stripped of performance.

"When I was in that prison camp after the trial, there was a man there, an old engineer, doing ten years for a bridge that fell. He told me something I've carried like a stone in my boot ever since. He said: *a machine fails in one of two ways. Either it was built wrong, or it was asked to do nothing for too long.*" Quentin's hands tightened on the wheel. "I've been asked to do nothing for thirty years, Sefa. Run cargo, keep moving, feel nothing, owe nothing. And I can feel it — the failure coming. Not in the hull. In me."

Lightning struck close now, close enough to whiten the whole river, and the thunder arrived like a door slamming.

"If we ride the surge," Quentin said, "and the patch holds, and Linh's memory holds, and the twins lash everything that isn't nailed down, and Ivo keeps his head — if all of that holds, we come out below the gorge ahead of the gunboat with the ledgers' site a day behind us and Ravelin's whole scheme a day from dying. If any part of it fails, we die in the dark in the loudest water on this river, and the last thing most of us hear will be each other."

"Yes," Sefa said simply. "That is the plan."

"And you'd stake the boy on it."

It was not a question, but she answered it as one. "I'd stake him on staying, too. Because Ravelin's men are already in the village asking after us, and Mai cannot hold them off twice, and a gunboat doesn't board politely. There is no version of the next week where Ivo is safe, Quentin. There is only the version where he's safe *and free*, and that version goes through the gorge."

Quentin closed his eyes. Sefa watched him do it — watched him stand in the dark behind his own eyelids with the ghost of Phnom Thiek and the ghost of his wife and the ghost of the man Ravelin had made him, and choose, deliberately, to open them again.

"Tomorrow at dawn, you start on the seam," he said. "I want the second pump rigged by noon. Twins on the lashing detail. Ivo and Linh at the charts until they can recite the gorge in their sleep." He set the wheel amidships, straightening it as though the whole river could be squared away so easily. "We enter at the crest."

Sefa nodded and turned for the ladder. At the top step she paused.

"For what it's worth," she said, "the engine agrees with you. Machines always know when a man has stopped lying to himself. They run better for it."

* * *

Dawn came gray and boiling, the storm front visible now as a wall of purple-black stacked on the northern horizon, eating the mountains one ridge at a time. The village woke to the sound of hammers — Sefa and the twins tearing into the hull patch, oakum and pitch and canvas, working the seam like surgeons racing a fever. Ivo and Linh spread the map and Vann's remembered words across the saloon table, the girl's voice steady as she traced the channel: *the throat, the elbow, the widow-maker shelf, the long chute.* Twice, Ivo made her repeat a bend until he could draw it blind.

At midmorning, Quentin appeared at the engine room hatch, sleeves rolled, and picked up a mallet without a word. Sefa handed him a second one. They drove caulking side by side, and neither spoke, and none needed to.

By noon the barometer had fallen so far the twins joked about it, and then stopped joking. By early afternoon the first outer bands of the storm swept the village, rain falling sideways, and Mai herself came down to the boat with oilcloth bundles of food and a look on her face that dared anyone to call it farewell.

"The crest reaches the gorge head at moonrise," Sefa called up to the pilothouse, wiping her hands one final time. "Six hours. Say the word, Captain."

Above her, Quentin Joubert stood at the wheel with the storm wall rising before him like a country neither of them had ever visited. For a long moment he said nothing. Then his hand closed around the brass, and his voice came down through the rain, clear as a bell over water:

"Get us to the gorge head. We ride the surge tonight."

And far to the north, unheard beneath the thunder, a gunboat lay at anchor at the gorge's mouth, its crew playing cards, its captain certain that nothing on earth would be fool enough to enter the water that was coming.
