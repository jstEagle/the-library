## 15. The Betrayal at the Landing

The fuel seller's name was Outhai, and he had hands like cracked rubber — the kind of man who'd spent thirty years hauling drums of diesel down mud ramps and never once washed the smell off. Ivo liked him. That was the thing that would sit in his chest afterward like a swallowed stone: he had *liked* him.

Outhai kept a fuel depot at Ban Pak Xang, a straggle of houses where the river bent wide and slow and the landing was nothing but old planks and mooring posts gone soft as cheese. The *Joubert* had stopped there four times in two years. Outhai always came down to meet them, waving before they even touched the bank, always had cold sugarcane juice waiting for the twins, always asked after Sefa's engine with genuine interest, because once upon a time he'd been an engine man himself on a cargo barge out of Vientiane before the river took his hearing in one ear.

"He's honest," Sefa had said, when Quentin sent Ivo ashore with money and a list. "Mostly. Honest enough."

Ivo stepped onto the planks and felt the difference in his legs immediately — the way you always did after days aboard, the ground seeming to tilt against you, the whole world suddenly too still. The *Joubert* sat at anchor fifty meters out, low in the water even after Sefa's patching, her hull wearing a fresh smear of black bitumen over the cracked seam like a scar covered with soot.

"Little captain!" Outhai called from under his corrugated awning. He was already dragging a drum across the boards. "You want the good diesel or the diesel that wants to be diesel?"

"The good kind. Captain says if it smokes gray we're coming back to haunt you."

Outhai laughed, a dry bellows sound. "Tell Quentin Joubert ghosts don't pay their bills." He kicked the drum upright and began working the pump handle with one hand while he squinted at Ivo's list with the other. "Gaskets. Packing. You're leaking."

"We're leaking."

"I can get gaskets. Not here — at my cousin's shop, up past the temple. Twenty minutes, maybe thirty." He waved toward the village, where the roofs went up the hillside among banana trees. "Walk with me. It's better than standing here watching your boat sink."

Ivo hesitated. Quentin's rules lived in him now like bones: never leave the boat unguarded, never split up, never stop moving. But Quentin himself had sent him ashore alone this time — because the twins were too recognizable, because Sefa couldn't leave the pumps, because Quentin needed to stay with the boat and the map hidden beneath the floor plates of the wheelhouse, wrapped in oilcloth, sleeping like something alive.

"Twenty minutes," Ivo said.

They walked up through the village. Women were spreading rice on mats to dry ahead of the weather; the sky had been building all morning, great stacked towers of cloud marching up from the south, and the air tasted like metal and green things rotting fast. Dogs trotted alongside them and lost interest. A boy no older than Ivo had been when Quentin pulled him from the flood water stared at him from a doorway, and Ivo found himself doing what he always did — checking the child's feet, his color, whether someone was watching him. Old habit. The flood had taught him that every child on a riverbank is either about to be saved or about to be lost, and nobody knows which until it happens.

Outhai talked the whole way, the way lonely men do. About his cousin who watered down paint thinner and sold it as fuel. About the monsoon coming early this year, the river already a hand's width above the dry-season marks. About his brother.

That was new. In four visits, Ivo had never heard about a brother.

"Kham," Outhai said, and his voice changed — flattened, careful. "Younger than me by ten years. Stupid the way young men are stupid. He got work in Vientiane, customs warehouse, carrying crates. Good pay, he thought. Then the crates turned out to be full of things that shouldn't have been in crates, and the man whose crates they were needed someone small to blame." He spat neatly off the path. "Kham's been in the lockup eight months. Waiting for trial that will never come, because the man who put him there likes having him there. Likes knowing he can be moved whenever it's useful."

Ivo felt the words arrive one at a time, each one taking its place, and somewhere behind his ribs a door began, very quietly, to open.

"Which man?" he asked. His voice sounded far away to himself.

Outhai didn't answer right away. They had reached the cousin's shop, a tin-roofed room smelling of solvent and hot rubber, and Outhai busied himself talking to the cousin in a low voice while Ivo stood in the doorway and watched the storm build over the river and understood, with the terrible clarity that comes a moment too late, exactly which man.

*Two men claiming customs authority board at Vientiane.* One of them had done the talking — smooth, patient, a voice like poured oil. The other had mostly watched, and written things down. And later, at the lock, Ravelin's men had searched the boat, and Quentin had run the rapids rather than let them finish, and ever since then they had been running, and running men need fuel, and fuel comes from landings, and landings are full of men with brothers in lockups.

"You told them," Ivo said. He wasn't asking.

Outhai's back was to him. The cousin had gone very still, the way people do when they realize they are standing inside something that is about to happen.

"They came three days ago," Outhai said quietly. "Before you did. The officer himself — not his men, the man. He sat at my table and drank my tea and told me Kham would be released Friday. Told me all I had to do was send word when a certain boat passed, and describe who came ashore." He turned around. His face was gray. "He knew your name, boy. He knew *your* name, and you weren't even born when—" He stopped. Shook his head. "I'm sorry. I'm sorry. But he has my brother, and you have—"

"What?" Ivo said. "What do we have?"

And that was when the men came out of the trees.

There were four of them, and they had been waiting in the shade behind the cousin's shop long enough that the mosquitoes had found them, and they moved without hurry because hurry wasn't necessary. The biggest one took Ivo's arm — not cruelly, which was somehow worse, just firmly, the way you'd take a crate you'd already paid for — and the smallest one patted him down with quick professional hands and found the money pouch and the list and nothing else, because the map wasn't on him.

The map was under the floor plates of the wheelhouse.

The map was on the boat, and he was here, and Outhai was looking anywhere but at him, and the biggest man said, "The officer wants the paper. Where is it?"

"I don't know what paper."

The blow came without warning and without anger, a short backhand across the ear that rang his skull like a struck pot and put him on his knees in the dust. The world went white, then reassembled itself sideways: the cousin's tin roof, the stacked clouds, Outhai's shoes turning away.

"The officer said you'd say that," the big man said, almost kindly. "He said the boy would say that, and the old man would cry, and neither of those things matters, because we'll take you to the boat and the captain will give us the paper to make us stop hurting you. So you can save everyone time, or you can save yourself pride. Pride is heavier. Take it from me."

They put him in a longtail boat that had been waiting at the downstream end of the landing, hidden behind the fish traps, and they ran upstream toward the *Joubert* with the throttle wide open, and Ivo sat in the bilge water between two men who smelled of cigarettes and waited, and did the only thing left to do.

He closed his eyes and opened the map.

Not the paper. The paper was oilcloth and ink and it slept under the floor plates fifty meters away. But Vann had drawn it twice — Ivo had watched him do it, that last feverish night, because the dying man's hand shook so badly the first attempt was useless, and he'd torn it up and started again, and Ivo had held the lamp. And something had happened to Ivo in that lamplight, something he hadn't understood until later: he had memorized it. Every bend, every depth mark, every crooked little symbol Vann scratched for the sunken barge lying in the silt off Don Khong's northern channel. The second map was the true one, and it lived now behind Ivo's eyes, complete, permanent, more permanent than paper. Paper burns. Paper gets taken. Paper gets traded for brothers.

He had known, in some wordless way, that this day might come. He hadn't planned it — planning would have felt like betrayal of his own crew, preparing to lose. But three nights ago, unable to sleep with the pumps chucking below, he had taken a sheet of Sefa's scrap paper and drawn the map from memory, and then drawn it again, and compared the two drawings against each other, and they matched. And then, because he was Ivo, because he was the boy who fed stowaways and broke rules for kindness, he had done one more thing: he had copied Vann's first, ruined attempt — the wrong one, the torn-up one, which he had also seen, also remembered — onto a fresh sheet, aged it with tea, folded it along the same creases, and slipped it into the oilcloth roll where the true map lived.

A decoy. He hadn't let himself call it that. He'd told himself it was just caution, just tidiness. But his hands had known.

Now the longtail swung alongside the *Joubert*, and the big man shouted up at the rail, and Quentin appeared there with the twins flanking him, and Ivo saw the exact moment Quentin understood — saw it hit him like the river hitting a piling. Saw him look at Ivo's bleeding ear, at the four men, at the gun the smallest one now wore openly on his hip.

"The paper," the big man called up. "Or we take the boy apart slowly, starting with the parts he uses for steering."

Quentin's face did something Ivo had never seen it do. It went completely empty — not calm, empty, like a house with the furniture carried out — and then, piece by piece, the captain came back into it, and what assembled itself there was worse than rage. It was arithmetic.

"Lower him a line," Quentin said. "I'll bring the paper."

"Don't—" Ivo started, and the big man cuffed him almost absently, and the deck tilted as someone aboard threw down a rope.

What happened next happened fast, and Ivo missed half of it because he was being hauled up the side hand over hand by the twins, both of them gripping him, Noi swearing continuously under her breath in a rhythm like an engine, and by the time his feet hit the deck Quentin was already back from the wheelhouse holding the oilcloth roll, and Sefa was at the engine hatch with a wrench in his hand looking like a man deciding how much trouble murder was worth, and the four boarders had spread out across the deck with the ease of men who had done this before.

Ravelin's man took the roll, weighed it, looked inside. His face gave nothing away. He whistled once, short, and the longtail backed off, and the four of them went over the side and down to it, and the last thing the big man said, looking up at Ivo, was:

"The officer says thank you. He says tell the captain the gorge is closed to traffic as of tomorrow. Officially, for the storms." He smiled. "Unofficially, there'll be a gunboat in it."

Then they were gone, the longtail shrinking downstream, and the rain arrived all at once like a dropped curtain, and the *Joubert* rocked in the sudden wind with her crew standing frozen on her deck around an empty spot where a map used to be.

It was Ivo who moved first. He pushed past Quentin, dripping, ear ringing, and knelt at the wheelhouse floor plates, and lifted them, and pulled out the oilcloth roll, and unrolled it with shaking hands.

Vann's ruined map. The false one. The decoy his own hands had made without letting his heart know why.

"It's fake," he said. His voice cracked and he hated it and kept going anyway. "Captain — the one they took — it's the drawing Vann threw away. Wrong channel mark, wrong bend. I copied it. I memorized the real one months ago, I've drawn it a dozen times, I can draw it right now, I can draw it in the dark—" He was crying, he realized, and couldn't stop, and didn't try. "I'm sorry. I'm sorry I didn't tell you. I'm sorry I got taken. But they don't have it. They don't have it."

Quentin crouched down in front of him. Rain ran off the captain's jaw. For a long moment he said nothing, and the boat groaned at her mooring, and the storm leaned on them like a hand.

"You gave them a lie," Quentin finally said, "and kept the truth in your head where no one could take it." Something moved through his face — not quite a smile, but the place where a smile would go if a man hadn't forgotten how. "Boy, that's the coldest thing anyone on this boat has ever done. Including me."

Behind them, Sefa was already at the winch, shouting over the rain that they had maybe an hour of light and a storm coming and a gunboat waiting at the gorge, and the twins were hauling in the mooring lines, and the river rose dark and fast around the hull.

Ivo wiped his face, stood up, and went to help.

* * *

They cast off into the teeth of the building storm, because staying meant capture and moving meant a chance, and any chance was better than none. But as the *Joubert* turned her bow upstream, Ivo stood at the rail and looked back at Ban Pak Xang shrinking in the rain — at Outhai's awning, at the fuel depot, at the man who had given them sugarcane juice and sold them for a brother.

And Ivo understood, in the way you understand things at fifteen that you will spend the rest of your life re-understanding: that Outhai hadn't betrayed them at all. He had chosen between two people he loved, and whichever way he chose, he would lose one. Ravelin built his power out of exactly that — out of forcing decent men to choose which of their own to feed to the river.

Well. Ravelin had the fake map now. And in three days, he would stake everything on it — his gunboat, his gorge, his whole rotten architecture — waiting at a bend of the river where no sunken barge had ever lain.

Ivo flexed his aching hand and thought: *Let him wait.*

Ahead, the storm swallowed the channel whole.
