## 16. Gorge of the Surge

The storm arrived the way Sefa had promised it would — not as weather but as a decision the sky had already made.

Quentin stood at the wheel an hour before dawn and watched the monsoon come down the valley like a wall being pushed over. The rain didn't fall so much as travel sideways, a gray curtain dragging itself across the water toward them, and beneath it the river had gone the color of wet iron. He could hear it before he could see it: a low grinding roar from upstream where the gorge began, the sound of the Nam Khong squeezing itself through rock too narrow for its temper.

"Surge is building," Sefa said beside him, one hand flat against the engine room bulkhead as if she could read the river's pulse through the steel. "Two meters in the last hour. Maybe three by the time we reach the narrows."

"And the gunboat?"

The twins had come back soaked to the bone at midnight with their report, breathless and grinning in the way that made Quentin want to shake them and feed them at the same time. *Three days out,* they'd said. *Patrolling below the falls. But they've got a fast launch at the mouth of the gorge, and Ravelin's man on the radio.* The launch was the problem. A launch could run ahead of them, could sit in the slack water past the first rapids and wait like a spider while the Joubert came down the throat of the current with no room to turn and no speed to flee.

Unless the launch couldn't stay there either.

"You're sure about this," Quentin said. It wasn't a question he asked often, and Sefa knew it.

"No," she said. "I'm sure about the numbers. The surge will lift us over the teeth of the first rapid that would otherwise open this hull like a tin of sardines. I'm sure the launch can't hold station in that water — no boat that size can. And I'm sure that if we wait, we die slowly instead of quickly." She looked up at him, oil-streaked, calm as a woman discussing the price of diesel. "I prefer quickly."

Behind her, the twins were coiling lines on the foredeck with the economy of people who had done ten thousand things twice. Kham had rigged extra lashings across the cargo hatch; Som had wrapped the spare fuel drums in netting and chained them to the rail. They moved without speaking to each other, which was how you knew they were serious. When the twins talked, it was theater. When they were silent, it was work.

And Ivo —

Ivo stood at the base of the wheelhouse ladder, holding the chart case against his chest, watching Quentin with those eyes that had learned years ago not to ask for things but never quite stopped hoping for them.

"You'll be on the deck," Quentin said. "Lashed to the mast step. That's not a request."

"I know the channel," Ivo said. "You drew it for me. Twice."

"I drew it on paper. Paper doesn't drown." Quentin turned back to the wheel because looking at the boy made something in his chest pull tight, and a captain with something pulling tight in his chest was a danger to everyone aboard. "You'll be my eyes on the soundings if the gauge floods. Nothing else."

"Yes, Captain." But there was something in the boy's voice — not defiance, exactly. Certainty. As if Ivo already knew how the morning would go and was being patient with him about it.

* * *

They entered the gorge as the light failed entirely, swallowed by rain.

It was like running into a throat. The cliffs rose sheer on both sides, black walls streaming with water, and between them the river had abandoned every pretense of being a river and become something else — a single muscle flexing downhill, ridged with standing waves, pocked with holes that opened and closed like breathing mouths. The surge lifted the Joubert and dropped her, lifted and dropped, and each drop landed with a concussion Quentin felt in his back teeth.

"Sefa! Give me everything she has!"

"She's giving!" Sefa's voice came up the speaking tube, ragged with engine noise. "If she gives more, she comes apart!"

The first rapid hit them at the bend. Quentin had run this gorge eleven times in his life, in flood and in drought, and he knew its moods the way he knew sandbars — but he had never seen it like this. The channel markers were gone, drowned or swept away. He steered by memory and by the white noise of the water, reading the surface the way a blind man reads a face: the smooth dark tongue of deeper current along the left bank, the broken water starboard where the rocks waited just under the swell, hungry.

"Hard left," he said, mostly to himself, and put the wheel over.

The Joubert leaned until the rail kissed the water. For one long second Quentin was steering a boat on her side, cargo groaning somewhere below, and then the surge caught them and flung them upright and forward, and they were through — into the short black pool between rapids where the rain hammered down hard enough to bruise.

"Soundings!"

"Fathoms showing four," Ivo called from the mast step, lashed in, shouting over the storm. "Then three! Rocks off the bow, port side, fifty meters!"

He could hear them. Of course he could. The boy had ears like a bat and nerve like his dead father must have had, whoever that man had been, because Ivo's voice carried no panic at all — only information, clean as a bell.

The second rapid was worse. It always was. The river narrowed to less than half its width and fell two meters in a hundred, and in surge it didn't fall so much as pour, a cataract disguised as a chute. There was a line through it — there was always a line — a thread of green water threading between two standing waves, and you found it or you died finding it.

Quentin found it. Or thought he did. He brought the Joubert around in the pool, squared her up, and drove her at the thread with the engines screaming behind him.

For ninety seconds the world ceased to exist. There was no boat, no crew, no storm — only water coming at him from all directions at once, only the wheel fighting his hands like a living thing trying to escape, only the knowledge, cold and absolute, that the next thing he saw would tell him whether he had guessed right or guessed wrong.

He guessed right. Barely. The Joubert shot down the chute sideways, half-broached, the hull shrieking along something that might have been rock or might have been the river's own bed, and then the pool below took them and spun them and spat them out still floating.

"Report!"

"Hull's singing but she's holding," Sefa called. "Pumps are gaining. We're fine. We are *fine*."

She said it twice because once hadn't convinced either of them.

* * *

The third rapid was where the river tried to take the twins.

It came without warning — a hole in the middle of the pool that shouldn't have been there, a whirlpool born of the surge colliding with some submerged ledge, and it opened under the starboard bow like a drain. The Joubert lurched. The lashings Som had rigged on the fuel drums parted with a crack like a rifle shot, and both twins went over the rail into the water before anyone could shout.

Quentin felt it happen more than saw it — the sudden lightness of the boat, the absence of two bodies that had been part of its balance since they were children hauling nets on their uncle's barge.

"MAN OVERBOARD! Port and starboard — both sides —"

He threw the wheel hard over, swinging the stern toward the churn, and the river took the twins the way it takes everything: completely, instantly, without malice. He caught one glimpse of Kham's head above the white water, one arm raised, and then nothing of Som at all.

"Lines! Throw lines! Ivo —"

But Ivo was already moving. Quentin heard the snap of the boy's knife cutting his own lashing, heard bare feet hit the deck, and then Ivo was at the rail with a coil of line in his hands, feeding it out with a fisherman's timing, letting the current do the work instead of fighting it. Som surfaced twenty meters downstream and Ivo's throw caught him across the chest on the first pass — a cast any deckhand would have been proud of in flat water, made blind, in a monsoon, at night.

Kham was harder. Kham had been pulled toward the hole again, circling it, and every second the whirlpool held him was a second closer to the ledge below where bodies went and didn't come back. Quentin brought the Joubert around in a full circle, using the boat herself as a shield to break the whirlpool's rotation, and when the eddy finally shuddered and collapsed, Kham shot out of it like a cork from a bottle — straight into Ivo's second line.

They hauled them aboard at the tail of the pool. Both twins coughed river water and lived. Som lay on the deck gasping and laughing at the same time, which was somehow the most frightening sound of the entire night.

"That," Kham wheezed, "was the worst swim of my life."

"Your best," Som corrected. "We'll argue later."

Quentin gripped the wheel and discovered his hands were shaking. Not from cold. From the image his mind kept returning to: the empty rail, the missing weight, the river closing over two boys he had fed and scolded and taught to splice line, gone in half a heartbeat, and how little it would have cost the river to keep them.

*Captain.* Ivo stood beside him, soaked through, the line still in his hands. His eyes were bright and steady. *Both lines held.*

*Yes,* Quentin said. *They did.*

* * *

The gunboat launch appeared at the mouth of the lower gorge just as the sky began to go the gray of old ash.

Quentin saw its searchlight first — a pale blade sweeping the water a kilometer behind them, hunting. Then the shape of it, low and armored, riding the edge of the surge the way Ravelin's men rode everything: as if the river belonged to them.

"They're following us in," Sefa said, coming up onto the wheelhouse wing, binoculars pressed to her eyes. "They're insane. That draft is too deep for the narrows in surge."

"They think we're insane," Quentin said. "They think anything we can do, they can do with more horsepower." He watched the light swing and settle on the Joubert's stern, pinning them. "Sefa. How much does she have left?"

Sefa lowered the binoculars and looked at him. Whatever she saw in his face made her smile — a small, fierce, entirely un-Sefa-like expression.

"She has one more hour of everything," she said. "After that, she has scrap metal."

"Then let's spend it."

The last stretch of the gorge was the longest and the fastest — a straight chute two kilometers long where the whole valley's water funneled between walls close enough to touch with a boathook, falling the entire way. In dry season it was a wild ride. In surge it was a waterfall wearing a river's clothing.

Quentin drove the Joubert into it at full throttle.

The world became noise and speed. The cliffs blurred past on either side, streaming black. The bow wave rose until it towered over the wheelhouse windows, a green mountain of water that should have swamped them and instead became the thing that carried them, the surge lifting the hull and hurling it downhill faster than the boat had ever moved in her life. Behind them the searchlight swung wildly, lost them, found them, lost them again — and then Quentin heard it, even through the roar, a sound he would remember for the rest of his days: a grinding, tearing crunch of steel on stone, distant and enormous, followed by a silence where an engine had been.

He couldn't look back. Looking back was death in the gorge. But Sefa had the binoculars, and after a moment she began to laugh — a real laugh, helpless and delighted.

"She's grounded," Sefa shouted. "High and dry on the mid-channel bar, right where the map says the rocks are! She'll be there until the water drops — maybe until the water drops in *October*!"

The twins, wrapped in blankets on the deck below, raised their fists in the air and roared.

* * *

They came out of the gorge at dawn into a world washed clean.

The storm had spent itself against the mountains; here the rain fell soft and ordinary, and the river spread wide and brown and almost gentle, carrying them along at a walking pace as if nothing had happened. Mist stood in the trees on both banks. A heron lifted off a sandbar as they passed, unhurried, unimpressed.

Quentin handed the wheel to Ivo.

The boy took it without ceremony — no surprise, no protest, just his hands settling onto the spokes as if they had always belonged there. Quentin stood back and watched him hold the channel, small corrections, patient ones, reading the water the way Quentin had tried to teach him and the way Ivo had really learned, which was by watching and waiting and knowing things without announcing them.

"You threw those lines like you'd done it a thousand times," Quentin said.

"I've thrown them a thousand times," Ivo said. "At buoys. At driftwood. At Som, mostly."

Som, within earshot, raised a hand in salute without turning around.

Quentin looked at the boy at his wheel — the boy he had pulled from a flood, the boy who had promised a dying stranger a promise Quentin himself had refused to make, the boy who had cut his own lashings and gone to the rail without being ordered — and understood, with the simple finality of a lock opening, that the Joubert had not been his alone for some time now. She belonged to everyone who had held a line this night. She belonged to the village that would mend her hull. She belonged, in whatever way mattered most, to the boy steering her.

"The ledgers," Ivo said quietly, eyes on the channel. "The barge site is two days downstream. Linh will meet us at the fishing stakes."

"Then we'll be there," Quentin said.

But the river had one more thing to say. Sefa came up from the engine room slowly, wiping her hands on a rag that was more red than black, and her face told him before her mouth did.

"We're taking water," she said. "Not fast. But the seam that cracked in the second rapid — it's opened wider, and the pumps are losing now. Slowly." She met his eyes. "We have perhaps six hours of river left in this hull. And the barge site is in thirty meters of water."

Quentin looked at the mist on the water, at the boy at his wheel, at the crew that had followed him into the throat of the gorge and come out the other side.

Six hours. Thirty meters. One chance.

"Get me Mai's village on the radio," he said. "Tell them to bring every diver they have."
