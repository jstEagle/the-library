## 17. Raising the Barge

**QUENTIN**

The storm came up the gorge like something with a grudge.

Quentin stood at the wheel of the *Joubert* and watched it arrive — a wall of gray-green water and wind that bent the rain sideways before the first drops even fell. Behind him, the village boats had scattered to their moorings hours ago; ahead, past the drowned bend where the old French barge had gone down forty years ago, the river was already white.

"You're sure about this," he said. It wasn't a question he was asking Linh so much as asking himself, out loud, one last time.

Linh stood at his shoulder with her father's map flattened under her palm on the chart table, weighted at the corners with four brass engine parts Sefa had donated without being asked. She was Vann's daughter in more than name — she had the same fever-bright certainty in her eyes Quentin had seen in the man's last hours at the muddy landing.

"The channel marker my father drew is the split rock," she said. "He used to fish this stretch with my grandfather before the war took everything. The barge sits in the eddy behind it, thirty feet down, on her starboard side. The cargo hold is the third hatch from the bow." She tapped the map twice. "He spent eleven years finding this. We are not leaving without it."

Thunder answered her, rolling down the gorge like applause from an unfriendly crowd.

Quentin looked at his crew. Sefa at the engine hatch, arms crossed, oil-stained to the elbow, nodding once — the nod of a man who had already decided and was only waiting for the captain to catch up. The twins, Kiet and Noi, coiling rope with the easy synchronized grace they brought to everything, even things that might kill them. Mai's divers were already ashore pulling on their gear, three men and a woman named Oun who had been diving this river since she was twelve and who claimed, with a straight face, that she could count fish by feel in the dark.

And Ivo. Sixteen years old, holding the map's copy against his chest like a shield, looking at Quentin with an expression that said: *you promised Vann nothing, but I promised him everything, and you taught me what a promise costs.*

"Anchor off the split rock," Quentin said finally. "Stern into the current. If the storm builds past what I say it will, we pull everyone out and we come back when it passes. Nobody argues with me on the water. Agreed?"

"Agreed," said five voices, and one of them — Ivo's — was steady enough that Quentin almost believed the boy wasn't terrified.

Almost.

* * *

**IVO**

The rain hit like gravel thrown by God.

Ivo crouched under the awning over the stern winch, watching the divers' support boat buck its way toward them through the chop, and tried to remember everything Vann had told him in those last grey hours at the landing. *The chest is iron, boy, double-sealed, French army surplus. It will not float. It will not open. It will fight you all the way up.* He'd laughed then, coughing blood into a rag. *Everything worth raising fights you.*

The support boat came alongside and Oun climbed aboard first, hauling herself over the rail with water already streaming off her wetsuit, though she hadn't been in yet — that was just the sky doing her work for her.

"This is madness," she said cheerfully, in the tone of a woman announcing dinner. "The visability down there will be nothing. Black tea. We work by line and by touch, nothing else. Your engineer says your winch can lift how much?"

"Two tons at slow speed," Sefa called from the engine room doorway. "If the drum holds. If the drum doesn't hold, we'll know immediately, because the cable will come through the deck like a harpoon."

"Wonderful," said Oun. "Give me the grapnel."

They worked fast because they had to. Kiet and Noi rigged the lifting bridle while Oun talked them through it — she'd raised engines, sunken boats, once an entire water buffalo, she said, though Noi refused to believe the buffalo story and made her swear on her grandmother. The plan was simple in the way desperate plans are simple: drop a grapnel line onto the wreck, let the divers walk it down in the black water, find the hold, find the chest, lash the bridle to it, and pray the winch could do what Sefa promised.

Simple. In monsoon water. At the bottom of a flooded gorge.

Ivo took his place at the stern rail with the tag lines, because that was where Quentin had put him, and because arguing with Quentin on the water was a rule even Ivo didn't break twice.

Oun went in first. Then her brother-in-law Thong. Then the third diver, a quiet young man called Phet who had been a monk until two years ago and who, according to the twins, did not breathe so much as *suggest* to his lungs that they wait.

Then there was nothing but rain, and the hiss of the river, and the air hose lines paying out over the side, and the small brass bell signals — one tug for okay, two for coming up, three for trouble.

For a long time, three tugs never came.

* * *

**SEFA**

Sefa kept one hand on the winch brake and one on the engine telegraph and gave both of them his full attention, which was more than either deserved.

Below his feet the *Joubert* groaned in the swell. They were anchored bow and stern off the split rock, riding the eddy Linh had promised, and the eddy was real — a pocket of relative calm behind the rock, maybe fifty meters across, where the flood current went strange and slow. But the edges of that pocket were a knife. A boat that drifted ten meters downstream would be in current that could roll a loaded barge, and Sefa had seen the anchors drag twice already, the chain shuddering through the hawse like something trying to get loose.

He thought about exile, the way he always did in bad weather. The ferry he'd fled on, twenty years ago, in a different country with a different river. How he'd sworn never again to trust a vessel with his life, and how he'd broken that oath within a year because the alternative was land, and land had people on it.

The winch drum trembled. Cable tension crept up on the gauge — the divers had found something and were rigging.

"Stand by," he called. "Weight coming."

Quentin appeared beside him, soaked through, saying nothing, just being there, which was its own kind of language between them after all these years.

The bell signal came: one long series of tugs. Ready.

Sefa eased the brake. The drum turned. The cable came tight, singing a low note through the fairlead, and the whole stern of the *Joubert* squatted down as if the river itself had grabbed the transom.

"Two hundred kilos," Sefa read off the gauge. "Three. Three-fifty—" The needle climbed and hung there, trembling. "It's moving. It's coming up slow."

"It's fighting," Quentin said.

"Everything worth raising does," said Ivo, from the rail, and Sefa laughed despite himself, despite the storm, despite the gauge now reading half a ton and climbing.

* * *

**NOI**

Noi had the tag line wrapped twice around her waist and her boots braced against the combing, and she was thinking about her brother, which was dangerous, because thinking about Kiet during work was how people got hurt.

Kiet was on the support boat above the dive site, tending Oun's air line, and every time the swell lifted that little boat Noi's stomach lifted with it, and every time it dropped she saw — uselessly, vividly — the line snagging, the boat shipping water, her brother going under in the black.

"Stop making faces," Phet's wife shouted at her from the wheelhouse window, where she was relaying bell signals. "You look like your grandfather."

"My grandfather was handsome," Noi shouted back, and hauled the tag line another meter.

The chest broke the surface at 4:17 in the afternoon, in the middle of the worst squall yet, and Noi would remember the moment for the rest of her life: the black water bulging, swirling, and then the iron box rising through it like a head coming up to breathe — streaming, furred with forty years of silt and weed, trailing the lifting bridle and two divers' safety lines, and Oun surfacing right beside it with one arm hooked over the top, spitting river water and laughing so hard she had to be dragged aboard.

"It's sealed!" she roared over the storm. "It's sealed, it's whole, it weighs like sin — get it aboard before I lose my grip!"

They got it aboard. It took all of them — winch, tag lines, hands, curses. When the chest finally thumped down on the *Joubert*'s aft deck, the whole boat rolled to port and rolled back, and everyone stood around it in the hammering rain, breathing hard, staring at forty kilograms of rusted French iron as if it might speak.

Linh knelt and put both palms flat on the wet metal.

"Hello, Papa," she said quietly, and Ivo, watching, felt something in his chest crack open like ice going out in spring.

* * *

**MAI**

Mai had not wanted them to go out. She had told Quentin so, standing on the village dock with the storm building behind the hills, and he had told her the truth: that waiting would cost more than weather, because Ravelin's gunboat was coming upriver and every hour the chest stayed on the bottom was an hour someone else might raise it, or seal the site, or make the whole question disappear the way Ravelin made questions disappear.

So she had given them Oun and Thong and Phet, her best divers, and she had watched them go, and then she had done what headwomen do, which is stand in the rain and refuse to leave the dock until her people came home.

When the *Joubert* came back up the eddy with the chest lashed to her deck and six exhausted faces along her rail, Mai felt something loosen in her that had been tight since the fishing fleet burned. Not forgiveness — that was a debt still owed, and debts were settled at tables, not docks. But something adjacent to hope.

She waved them in through the rain, and the village came down to the water behind her, dozens of them, holding oilcloth over lanterns, and the light ran gold across the flooded river all the way to the boat.

* * *

**IVO**

They opened the chest in Mai's largest house, with the storm still screaming outside and half the village pressed into the room to watch.

It took Sefa and the twins twenty minutes with pry bars and a borrowed bolt cutter, working carefully, because Linh had insisted — *if the seals are intact, the documents inside may matter in a court someday; break nothing you don't have to.* The iron lid came up with a groan and a breath of cold, mineral-smelling air, the smell of deep water and long time.

Inside, packed in tarred canvas and wax-sealed lead sleeves, lay paper.

Not gold. They had known — Linh had told them, and Ivo had believed her — but knowing and seeing were different things. Ledgers. Shipping manifests. Letters in French and Lao and Vietnamese, some signed, some stamped with customs seals that Ivo recognized because he'd seen their like on Ravelin's own men's papers at Vientiane. Names. Dates. Tonnages of confiscated goods sold back into private hands. Payments recorded. And margins full of annotations in a clerk's careful hand — including, on three separate pages, the same word written beside entries that ended in drownings: *réglé.* Settled. Dealt with.

Witnesses dealt with. Cargo dealt with. Forty years of it, dry as bone, sealed against the river that had swallowed the barge rather than surrender it.

Linh lifted one ledger with both hands, the way you lift a sleeping child, and set it in front of the lamp.

"My father found the site eleven years ago," she said to the silent room. "He couldn't raise it alone. He couldn't tell anyone, because anyone he told would sell him to Ravelin. So he drew maps. He waited. He got sick." Her voice held steady the way a wire fence holds — thin, taut, unbreakable. "He gave the map to a boy on a riverboat because the boy was the first person in eleven years who asked him for nothing."

Every face in the room turned to Ivo.

He hadn't planned to speak. But the words were there, waiting, the way Vann's story had been waiting in him since the landing, since the dawn Vann died with Ivo's hand around his.

"He asked me to carry it to his daughter," Ivo said. "That was the whole promise. Carry it to Luang Prabang." He looked at Linh. "I'm sorry it took the long way."

Linh crossed the room and took his hand, and pressed it flat against the ledger's cover, the way she had pressed her palms to the chest.

"The long way is the only way anything true travels," she said. "My father knew that. That's why he chose a riverboat."

Outside, the storm reached its peak — thunder cracking directly overhead, rain hammering the thatch like a mob at the door — and inside, Mai began giving quiet orders: fire the lamps brighter, bring rice wine, wake the man who could read old French handwriting. There would be no sleeping tonight. There were copies to be made, and decisions to be made after that, and somewhere downriver a gunboat was moving through the dark toward all of them.

Quentin stood apart, near the door, arms folded, watching the ledgers circulate hand to hand through the lamplight. Ivo went to him.

"Well?" Ivo said. "We have it. What happens now?"

Quentin was quiet a long moment. Rain drummed. Somewhere in the village a dog barked at the thunder and gave up.

"Now," Quentin said slowly, "we find out whether paper is heavier than iron." His eyes went to the dark doorway, out toward the river, toward the south, where the storm was and where, three days behind it, a man with a pistol and nothing left to lose was coming up the current. "Because Ravelin is about to learn what we're carrying. And when he does—"

He didn't finish. He didn't have to. Everyone in the room heard the rest of it in the thunder:

*he'll come for it himself.*
