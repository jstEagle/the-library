## 18. Justice Outside the Law

The chest sat on the deck of the *Joubert* like a drowned man finally given a funeral, and nobody wanted to be the first to open it.

We had moored in the shallows below Mai's village, where the water ran slow and brown over the sandbar, and the storm had passed off downriver to drown somebody else's week. The divers — three of Mai's men with rope soles and lungs like bellows — had brought it up from forty feet of flood-dark current, off the ribs of the old colonial barge that Vann had spent his last feverish days mapping. The chest was iron-banded teak, crusted white with river lime, and when Sefa cracked the lock with a cold chisel and a prayer to whatever engineer's god he kept, the smell that came out was paper and rot and something older than both.

Linh reached in first. She was Vann's daughter, and she had a right, and everyone on that deck knew it without my saying so.

She lifted out a bundle wrapped in oilcloth, tied with tarred string, and unwound it with hands that shook only a little. Ledgers. Seven of them, bound in government calfskin, stamped with the customs seal of the territorial office — Ravelin's own office. Beneath them, wrapped separately, a tin dispatch box, and inside that, letters. Correspondence. Signatures.

I stood at the rail and watched the river go by and felt nothing I could name, which is how I feel about most things that matter.

"Well," Sefa said, into the silence. "That's not gold."

"It's better than gold," Linh said. Her voice was steady. She'd been steadier than any of us since Luang Prabang. "Gold you can spend once. This—" she held up a page, and even upside down and ten feet away I could read the columns of figures, neat as scripture, "—this spends forever."

* * *

We read them all night under the lamp in the wheelhouse, because there was nowhere else to put them and no one willing to let them out of sight. The twins took turns reading aloud while the other two slept in shifts, and Ivo sat cross-legged on the deck plates with a ledger open across his knees, moving his lips over the French, translating for Sefa in a low voice.

The arithmetic was simple, the way evil usually is. Confiscated goods — rice, opium, timber, boats themselves, whole boats taken from families who couldn't produce papers Ravelin's men had invented — entered the customs ledgers as destroyed or seized-for-state-use. Then they reappeared, sold upriver, sold across the border, sold back sometimes to the very families they'd been taken from. The margins were recorded. The shares were recorded. And where a name appeared too often, where a merchant or a boatman asked one question too many, that name acquired a habit of traveling on the river at monsoon season and not arriving.

There were eleven such names. Linh found her father's on the fourth ledger, dated six years back. *V., prospector, inquiry closed — subject lost in flood.*

He hadn't been lost in a flood. He'd been put in one.

I went out onto the deck after that and stood in the dark a long time, listening to the water work against the hull. Behind me, through the wheelhouse glass, I could see Ivo still bent over the pages, and I thought about a boy pulling a dying stranger out of the rain because nobody told him not to, and what that stranger's stubborn ghost had just handed us.

You can't sink this, I thought. You can't sell it either. Both of those are just different ways of drowning.

* * *

In the morning I called a council on the cargo deck, which is where we hold councils, because the cargo deck has room for everyone and no chairs, so nobody gets comfortable enough to talk long.

Ravelin would know by now that the barge had been raised. His gunboat sat aground in the gorge with its belly stove in, but gunboats have radios, and radios have friends, and somewhere downriver a man who had spent twenty years building a wall of paper around his crimes had just heard the paper was in our hands. He would come. Not with the gunboat — with lawyers, with soldiers, with offers, and if those failed, with men whose faces we hadn't met yet.

So the question was simple: what do we do with seven ledgers and a box of letters?

"Sell them back to him," said Kiet, the elder twin, who always argued the practical position first so someone else wouldn't have to. "Name a price. He pays, we run, we're rich, the river forgets."

"The river doesn't forget," said Mai, who had come aboard for the council and stood at the gangway with her arms folded like she owned the deck, which morally she did. "It just moves things downstream."

"Then give them to a magistrate," Kiet tried again.

"Which magistrate?" Linh asked quietly. "Show me one between here and the capital whose appointment didn't pass through Ravelin's office. We hand him the evidence and he hands us a receipt and a shallow grave."

Silence. The twins looked at each other, that twin shorthand they do, half a second of whole conversation.

"So we burn them," said Noi, the younger twin, and there was no playfulness in it at all. "Sink them back where they came from. Some things cost more to carry than they're worth."

"They're worth eleven dead men," Ivo said.

Everyone turned. He hadn't spoken yet, and he was sitting on the winch housing with his knees drawn up, fourteen years old and wearing my old rain jacket two sizes too big, and when he spoke he sounded like a man reading a verdict he'd been practicing.

"Vann carried that map for six years. Six years running the tributaries, sick, alone, hunted, because he couldn't get to the barge and couldn't make himself leave it. He gave it to me because I promised. If we burn it, then the flood got him twice." He looked at me. He has his mother's eyes, whoever his mother was; I never learned hers either. "Captain. What do we do?"

And there it was, the question I'd been carrying since a muddy landing two months ago, since a feverish man held out a grease-stained map and I said *no passengers, no exceptions.* Every road I'd refused had led here anyway. The river doesn't ask your permission. It just carries you.

"We copy them," I said.

Sefa frowned. "Copy?"

"Every page. Every figure, every signature, every name. We don't sell it, we don't trade it, we don't hand it to any single man with an office and a seal — because anything one man holds, one man can bury, and anything one man buries, Ravelin can buy. So we don't give it to power." I looked around the deck, at Mai, at the twins, at Sefa, at Linh, at the boy on the winch housing. "We give it to everybody."

* * *

It took four days.

Mai's village had a schoolhouse with a duplicating machine left over from the mission years, a crank-driven thing that groaned like Sefa's engine in a headwind, and the schoolteacher — a thin, serious woman named Oun who had once, Mai told me, refused to falsify attendance records for a customs man and paid for it with her posting — ran copies until her hands were inked to the wrist. The village turned out. Old men who remembered the confiscated boats took turns at the crank. Children carried wet pages to the veranda to dry in rows like laundry, hundreds of them, flapping in the river wind, and I stood watching a page dry that contained the record of a man's murder and thought: this is what justice looks like when it isn't wearing a uniform. It looks like laundry.

We made thirty-one complete sets.

Then we made the distribution plan, and this was Sefa's contribution, and it was beautiful the way only an engineer's solution to a human problem is beautiful. "A thing fails," he said, tapping the engine-room schematic he'd drawn on the back of a chart, "when it depends on one part. So build it with no critical parts. Any one courier can be stopped. Any one office can be bought. Any one newspaper can be shuttered — Ravelin owns two already. But if the same document arrives in forty places in the same week—"

"—then stopping it means stopping everything," Linh finished. "And everything, everywhere, at once, is the one thing a secret can't survive."

We split the sets. Linh took the southern route with two of Mai's cousins, by road and ferry, to the provincial newspapers and the magistrate at Pakse who had a reputation for being either honest or very careful, and in that country the two look identical. The twins took the northern run in a borrowed pirogue with an outboard Sefa rebuilt from spare parts, hitting the mission stations and the consulate agent at Luang Prabang, where Linh's own sworn statement was already waiting. Ivo and I took the middle river ourselves, the *Joubert* running light, calling at every landing with a telegraph office and a postbox.

At each stop, the same ritual. Ivo would carry the packet ashore — I insisted on that, and he knew why, though neither of us said it: it was his promise, and promises should be delivered by the person who made them. He'd hand it to the postmaster, or the missionary, or the newspaper clerk, and say the same words each time, words we'd agreed on in the wheelhouse one night:

"This is evidence concerning customs officer Étienne Ravelin. Copies are going today to thirty other places. You are asked to publish, file, or forward within the week."

No threats. No demands. Just the weight of it, and the arithmetic — thirty other places — doing the threatening for us.

The postmasters' faces were the best part. Men who had spent their careers learning exactly how much not to see suddenly being handed a package they couldn't unsee, with thirty witnesses to the handing. One old postmaster at Ban Keun weighed the packet in his hand, looked at Ivo, looked at me, and said, "Heavy," and stamped it anyway.

* * *

But Sefa's design had one more component, and that one was mine, and it was the part I'd lain awake two nights deciding whether I had the stomach for.

On the fifth day, we put in at the customs station above the falls — Ravelin's own station, the district headquarters, a stilted building flying the territorial flag with a fuel depot and a radio mast and a dozen men on the payroll. Ravelin himself wasn't there; he'd gone downriver to salvage his gunboat and his story. But his men were there. His sergeants, his clerks, the boat crews who ran his confiscations, the young officers who signed the forms without reading them because reading them would have meant resigning.

I walked up the landing with a canvas satchel over my shoulder and Ivo at my heel, and the sergeant of the guard — a broad, gray-templed man named Dara whom I'd traded diesel with for years, back before I understood whose money bought his uniforms — met me at the foot of the stairs with his hand resting easy on his holster.

"Joubert. You've got nerve, coming here."

"I've got mail," I said, and set the satchel on the rail, and took out the packets.

Dara looked at them. Then at me. Then, briefly, at Ivo, standing small and straight beside me, and something moved behind his face — because Dara had a son about Ivo's age, and every man on that station had seen the ledgers' kind of arithmetic touch his own life somewhere, a brother's boat, a cousin's shop, a stretch of river suddenly closed.

"What is it?" he said, though his voice had already guessed.

"Your paymaster's books," I said. "Every entry. Every share, every sale, every man who traveled at monsoon season and didn't arrive. There's a copy for the captain of each boat crew, one for the clerks' room, one for the mess. And one for you, Sergeant, because your name appears in the third ledger — as a witness. To the Vann inquiry. A witness who signed a statement saying he saw nothing."

His jaw worked. "You're trying to turn us."

"No," I said. "Ravelin turned you years ago. He just never told you the price. This is the receipt." I held out the packet. "Read it tonight. All of you. Because copies of these went out five days ago to every magistrate, every paper, every consulate on this river. By Friday, the whole territory will know what's in them. The only choice left anybody has is whether they knew beforehand — and stood there anyway — or whether they read it and stepped clear. That's not turning, Sergeant. That's just gravity. Things fall."

For a long moment Dara didn't move. The station behind him had gone quiet, men gathering on the veranda without seeming to gather, the way soldiers do. Somebody had already recognized the customs seal on the packets. Somebody had already started reading aloud, low, the way you read something you can't believe.

Dara took the packet from my hand.

"You know what he'll do to us if—"

"He'll do it to you regardless," I said. "The only question is whether you're standing behind him when it comes, or standing somewhere the river can carry you clear." I turned to go, then stopped. "One more thing. When he comes back — and he'll come back alone, eventually, because by Friday he won't have anything else — remember that you outnumber him about twelve to one. That's all. That's the whole message."

* * *

We ran downriver that evening with the light going copper on the water, and Ivo stood beside me at the wheel, quiet the whole first hour.

Finally he said, "Do you think they'll read them?"

"Some will burn them unread. Some will read them and do nothing. Some will read them and be sick, and some of those will find that being sick is the beginning of something." I trimmed the wheel for the channel. "You can't make a man decent, Ivo. You can only make sure he knows exactly what he's choosing. That's more than most people ever get."

"And Ravelin?"

I thought about it honestly, because he deserved honesty and because lying to Ivo had stopped being possible somewhere around the gorge.

"He built his whole life on being the only one who knew everything. The ledgers, the shares, the names — he held them all, and holding them made him untouchable, because everyone who feared him also needed him. What we did this week wasn't attack him. It was take away his only possession." I watched a heron lift off the far bank, slow and prehistoric. "A man like that, stripped of his secrets, isn't dangerous anymore. He's just a man in a uniform that everyone's read the fine print on. And men like that, when they finally understand it — they either run, or they come aboard."

The radio in the corner of the wheelhouse crackled. Sefa had rigged it to scan the working channels, and we'd kept it low all week, but now it coughed and spat and resolved into a voice — official, clipped, the territorial band.

*"...all stations, all stations. Warrant issued this date for the arrest of customs officer Étienne Ravelin on charges of corruption, theft of state property, and the deaths of eleven persons. Subject considered armed. Last vessel association: river steamer Joubert, master Q. Joubert..."*

Ivo and I looked at each other.

They weren't hunting us. They were warning us — in the clumsy, public way a bureaucracy warns, by announcing to every ear on the river exactly where the desperate man was likely to point himself.

I cut the throttle to half and turned the *Joubert'*s bow toward the narrows below Mai's village, where the channel runs deep and dark and a boat can lie quiet against the bank.

"Get Sefa," I said. "Full watch tonight. And Ivo—" he was already halfway to the engine hatch, and I caught his shoulder, and he turned back, and I saw he'd already worked it out, the way he works everything out lately, ahead of me and without telling me. "He'll come at night. Alone. He hasn't got anyone left to send."

"I know," Ivo said. "I'll be on deck."

And the river carried us home through the falling dark, toward whatever was waiting at the landing, patient as water.
