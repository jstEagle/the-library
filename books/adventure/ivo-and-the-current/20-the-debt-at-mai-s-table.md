## 20. The Debt at Mai's Table

The drums began at dusk, and Mai felt them in her chest before she heard them properly — that low animal heartbeat the old men beat out on skins stretched over hollowed logs, the sound her grandmother had said was the river's own pulse borrowed for an evening. She stood in the doorway of her house above the landing and watched the village arrange itself below her: lanterns going up along the palisade posts one by one, fires kindled in the sand pits, long tables carried out from every house and set end to end until they ran nearly the length of the beach. The whole village was becoming a single room with a roof of monsoon cloud.

Debt feasts were rare. Debt feasts that ended well were rarer still.

Mai had called this one three days ago, after the ledgers went out on the current — copied by lamplight in a dozen hands, sealed in oilcloth, sent downriver with traders and pilgrims and one very fast courier boat to every magistrate, newspaper office, and consulate that mattered between here and the delta. After Ravelin's men had come to the village not as raiders but as deserters, hats in hand, asking to be fed. After the gunboat in the gorge had grounded itself chasing a ghost of spray and wake, and the *Joubert* had come through the surge like something born of it rather than built by men.

The debt was blood. That was what she had told Quentin Joubert, standing on this same landing with the smoke of her burned fishing fleet still in everyone's nostrils. *Help us or leave, and be remembered as the man who ran twice.* She had meant it as a blade. She had not expected him to take it and turn it into a bridge.

She descended the steps slowly, because a headwoman descends slowly, and because her knees had opinions about stairs these days. Children ran past her carrying skewers of river fish. Old Somchai was already at the largest fire, arguing with his nephew about the proper amount of chili in a festival curry, which was to say the festival had truly begun.

And there, moored at the landing where burned boats used to be, sat the *Joubert* — repainted, recaulked, her hull seams closed with new teak and her name picked out fresh in white against the black of the bow. Mai had watched Quentin work on that lettering himself for two evenings, kneeling on the deck with a brush too small for the job, redoing each stroke until it pleased him. A man does not repaint a boat's name unless he intends to keep the boat. A man does not keep a boat unless he has decided, somewhere deep in the scarred country behind his ribs, to stop running.

She intended to test that decision tonight. Kindly. But thoroughly.

* * *

The feast opened the way all her grandmother's feasts had opened: rice first, offered to the elders, then to the river itself — a handful scattered on the water for those the water had taken. Mai spoke the names. Her husband, gone eleven years now. The fishermen lost when Ravelin's men fired the fleet. Vann, the prospector who had died on a stranger's deck with a promise in a boy's keeping. And last, quietly, her own son, drowned in the flood year, whose face she no longer needed the lantern light to see.

When the naming was done, the eating began, and the noise rose like water coming back into a dry channel.

Mai seated Quentin at her right hand, which was either an honor or an interrogation depending on how the evening went, and she suspected he knew it. He sat stiffly in the borrowed chair, a big weathered man made awkward by ceremony, his hands around a clay cup of rice whiskey he hadn't drunk from. Sefa was farther down the table, deep in animated argument with two of the village engine-menders about injector timing, gesturing with a fish skeleton as though it were a piston rod. The twins had already vanished into the crowd of children, teaching them some card game that involved cheating, and the laughter coming from that corner of the beach suggested the cheating was being taught well.

Ivo sat across from Mai. The boy — no, she corrected herself, watching him; the young man — ate steadily and listened to everything, the way he always did. She had marked that about him from the first day the *Joubert* limped into her landing with a cracked seam and a captain wearing guilt like a wet coat. The boy listened the way the young of every species listen when they have decided the world is worth learning. It was a quality she had seen perhaps five times in her life, and it had never once belonged to a fool.

"You've fed us twice now," Quentin said to her, low, under the drumming. "Once when we didn't deserve it."

"Twice," Mai agreed. "The second time you'd earned it. Barely." She let him hold her gaze a moment, then relented enough to smile. "Your engineer fixed our pump. Your twins fixed my grandson's bicycle, which I am told did not need fixing before they touched it. Your boy—" she paused, deliberately, "—your boy has been hauling nets with the fishing crews since dawn. The old women have adopted him. You may have difficulty getting him back aboard."

Something moved through Quentin's face at that — quick, quickly mastered, but she saw it. Fear. The specific fear of a man who has kept everything he loves on one small floating world and is suddenly shown how large the shore is.

"He works hard," Quentin said. "Always has."

"Mm," said Mai, and drank her whiskey, and let the evening do its work.

* * *

The gifts came at moonrise, because debts are spoken aloud only after the stomachs are full — that was the order of things, and order mattered on nights like this.

One by one, villagers rose and named what was owed between them and the crew of the *Joubert*. Somchai named the pump. The net crews named the hands that had hauled beside them. An old widow named the twins, who had apparently spent an entire afternoon retrieving her goat from a mango tree, an act Mai chose not to investigate too closely. Each gift given was answered with a gift returned — fish, fruit, mended tools, a coil of good rope from the *Joubert*'s stores — until the space between the tables became a slow circulating river of its own, and the ledger of the village balanced itself in public, in firelight, where everyone could see.

Then Quentin stood.

He stood the way he did everything, Mai thought — like a man braced against a wind only he could feel. The drums quieted. Even the children stilled. He looked out at the faces for a long moment, and Mai saw him gather words the way a helmsman gathers a swell: carefully, with respect for what they could do to him.

"I owe this village a debt I can't repay with rope or repairs," he said. His voice carried without effort; a river voice, built for shouting over engines. "Eleven years ago I ran supplies up this stretch during the flood. Then the water got high and my nerve got low, and I turned back at the bend below the temple hill. I told myself the current wouldn't let me make it. That was a lie. The current would have let me. I wouldn't let me." He paused. "People counted on me. I left them counting."

No one spoke. In the firelight Mai watched the old ones nod, not in forgiveness — forgiveness wasn't hers to give on their behalf — but in acknowledgment. A truth spoken aloud is a plank laid over a gap. You still have to walk it.

"So here is what I can offer," Quentin went on. "Every flood season, while I live and the *Joubert* floats, this boat runs your relief route free. Cargo, evacuation, mail, medicine — whatever the water demands. No charge, no conditions, no exceptions. Not as payment." His jaw worked. "Payment implies the account closes. This doesn't close. This is just what I should have been doing for eleven years, and will keep doing so I don't forget it."

He sat down. The silence held for three heartbeats — Mai counted them — and then Somchai banged his cup on the table and roared approval, and the drums came back up like a tide, and the moment broke into noise and warmth.

But Mai raised her hand, and the village quieted again, because they knew she had not yet spoken, and the night was not done.

* * *

"The debt of blood is paid," she said, standing. She said it formally, in the old cadence, the way her grandmother had taught her: *the blood cried out, and the answer came, and the river heard.* "Ravelin's men burned our boats, and this crew ran the gorge to bring us the means to end him. Our dead are named. Our account stands open no longer." She looked at Quentin. "Captain Joubert. Before this village, before the river and the drum: the debt is paid. You owe us nothing. We owe you nothing. What passes between us from tonight forward passes freely."

She saw it land in him — saw the tension go out of his shoulders by degrees, like a line paying out. For eleven years, she guessed, the man had carried every debt in the world on his own back, because carrying them was easier than setting them down among people who might see what was underneath. Now the weight was lifted, and he looked briefly unmoored by it, a man whose anchor rope had been cut by kindness instead of storm.

Then she turned to Ivo.

The boy straightened. Around the fire, the village leaned in without seeming to; Mai felt them doing it, forty pairs of ears sharpening.

"Ivo," she said. "You came to us a stranger on a stranger's boat. You leave nothing undone, take nothing unearned, and give away water to dying men even when your captain forbids it." A ripple of laughter — Quentin's ears went red — and she let it pass. "You kept a promise to a dead man at cost to yourself. You steered a boat through the gorge when the man who taught you was thrown from the wheel. And you hauled nets beside our crews as if you'd been born to it."

She took from her neck the cord she had been wearing since sundown: a small carved hook of river hardwood, old and dark with handling, strung on braided cotton. Her grandmother's. Her mother's. Hers. Every household in the village had one like it; it was nothing and it was everything — the mark that said *this one eats at our fire.*

"Our houses are small and our roofs leak," Mai said, "but there is a place at our table that has been empty a long time. Flood season or dry season, monsoon or starlight — whenever the *Joubert* ties up at this landing, there is a home here with your name on it. Not a debt. Not a duty. A door." She held out the cord. "Will you take it?"

The silence was enormous. Out on the black water, the river made its endless patient sound against the pilings.

Mai watched the boy look at the hook, and then — this was the part she would remember — look at Quentin. Not for permission. For something else. Asking, silently, whether taking it would break anything. Whether a found family could hold more than one belonging without splitting its seams.

Quentin met his eyes for a long moment. Then he nodded, once, and Mai saw him say — barely a movement of his lips, but she read it, having raised sons and buried one — *Go on. Take it. It doesn't take anything from me.*

Ivo took the cord. He bowed to her the way the young bow when they mean it, all the way, and when he straightened his eyes were bright and his voice, when it came, was steady.

"Thank you, Grandmother Mai."

*Grandmother.* The word went through the assembled village like current through wire. Somchai whooped. The twins, materializing from nowhere, seized Ivo by both arms and shook him gravely, announcing that they had known all along he was family, mostly because he laughed at their jokes, which no sane person did. Sefa raised his cup and shouted something in his island tongue that needed no translation. And Quentin — Quentin sat very still at Mai's right hand, looking at the boy with an expression she recognized from her own mirror on certain mornings: the look of a man discovering that the thing he fled from all these years had followed him anyway, caught up at last, and was standing quietly beside him wearing a boy's face.

The drums thundered. The dancing started. Mai sat back down and poured whiskey for two, and this time Quentin drank.

"To doors," she said.

"To doors," he said, and for the first time since she'd known him, the man smiled with his whole face, and it changed him entirely, and she understood that under all that weathered iron there had been this all along — a man who could be glad, waiting all these years for permission.

* * *

Late, when the fires had burned to orange hearts and most of the village slept where they'd fallen, Mai walked the landing alone, as she did after every feast, checking lines and lanterns out of habit older than sense.

The *Joubert* lay quiet at her moorings, riding the gentle swell, her new paint catching the last lantern light. On her aft deck, a lamp still burned in the wheelhouse. Mai saw two silhouettes through the glass — the captain and the boy, bent together over the chart table, heads close, the boy's finger tracing the river's course while the captain talked with his hands. Planning, she thought. Or remembering. Or both; on this river the two were often the same cargo.

She was about to turn back when she noticed the third thing, and stopped.

At the foot of the gangplank, half-hidden in shadow, a man stood watching the wheelhouse light. Not one of hers. Not one of the crew. A lean figure in a soaked traveling coat, mud to the knees, who had come up the landing path without a drum announcing him — impossible, on a feast night, unless he had come by water and quietly, and wanted very much not to be seen.

As she watched, the figure stepped forward into the lantern glow, and Mai saw the face beneath the hood, and her breath caught.

It was Linh. Vann's daughter. And she was alone, exhausted, half-drowned-looking, and moving fast — up the gangplank toward the wheelhouse light, toward the two silhouettes, carrying something wrapped in oilcloth clutched against her chest like a child.

Behind her, far downriver, where the gorge swallowed the current, a sound rolled up through the dark that Mai had not heard since the war years: the flat, repeated crack of a signal gun, someone calling a name across the water.

Calling, she would learn soon enough, the *Joubert*'s name.
