## 22. The Current Goes On

The sandbar below the old ferry crossing had a new mood this year.

Ivo noticed it before anyone else — a long pale shoulder of sand where last monsoon there had been only brown water and the drowned tops of bamboo. It sat maybe forty meters off the left bank, crescent-shaped, its downstream tail curling like a question mark. The river had built it out of silt carried down from some hillside it had eaten in the spring floods, and it was already teaching itself to be permanent: two herons stood on it with the patience of landlords.

He wrote it down in the captain's book, because that was his job now, or part of it. *New bar, left bank above Ban Houayxay ferry crossing. Crescent. Herons. Will shift after next big rain.* He had learned from Quentin never to write *will* about the river without also writing *unless*, but the book was getting crowded with unlesses, and Ivo had decided that was honest. A river pilot who wasn't uncertain was a man who hadn't been paying attention.

"Reading it aloud or just admiring your own handwriting?" Sefa called up from the engine hatch, her voice half-swallowed by the throb of the diesels. She had grease on her forearms to the elbow and a look of deep contentment, which for Sefa meant the engine was misbehaving in an interesting way rather than a fatal one.

"Admiring," Ivo said. "The handwriting's better than yours."

"My handwriting is a code," she said. "Developed over decades to confine information to those worthy of it." She wiped her hands on a rag that had given up being a rag sometime last season. "Number three cylinder's running hot again. Tell the captain we'll want to back her down at the landing and let me look at it while the twins unload."

Ivo relayed it aft, where Quentin stood at the wheel with the particular stillness he wore when the water was talking to him. A year ago Ivo would have waited for the captain to finish listening before speaking. Now he knew you could talk during the listening; Quentin kept one ear on the river and one on his crew, and both ears worked at once.

"Sefa wants the landing for twenty minutes. Number three."

Quentin nodded without turning his head. "Tell her she can have fifteen. The twins will have the cargo off in ten if I threaten them."

"They'll hear you threatening them?"

"I don't need to. They can smell a threat forming. It's how they were raised." There was the ghost of a smile under the gray of his beard. A year ago the smile had been a rumor. Now it was weather — not constant, but forecastable.

The *Joubert* came around the bend and the village opened up ahead of them like a hand unclenching: the stilt houses along the bank, the fish-drying racks, the new roof on the schoolhouse that Mai had bullied three districts into funding, the long wooden stairs cut into the clay going up from the landing. Smoke from cooking fires stood straight up in the windless afternoon. Somewhere a dog was barking at the boat with the ceremonial hostility reserved for boats it actually liked.

And on the landing, already waiting, Linh — a rolled chart under one arm, the other hand shading her eyes, standing in exactly the spot where a year ago Vann had knelt in the mud and traded a dying man's map for a promise.

Ivo felt that memory the way you feel an old scar in cold water: present, but no longer sharp. He brought the boat alongside himself now — Quentin let him take the lines in close water, watched with arms folded and said nothing unless the nothing needed saying — and the fenders kissed the pilings soft as a held breath.

* * *

Linh came aboard before the twins finished tying off, which was tradition now, and went straight to the chart table in the wheelhouse, which was hers.

"The survey boat came through yesterday," she said, unrolling the chart of the gorge reach. "They've moved the marker buoy at the second narrows. Or lost it. With those people it's the same thing." She tapped a pencil mark she'd already made, fresh ink over faded ink, layers of the river's history accumulating on the paper like rings in wood. "If it's gone, the channel runs closer to the right wall than anyone remembers. We should run it tomorrow morning, early, before the surge builds."

"You've been up since when?" Ivo asked.

"Since the survey boat's wake rocked my hammock at four," she said, "which is why I'm useful and you're decorative."

This was their liturgy. It had taken Ivo most of the first month to stop being flustered by her and most of the second to realize the flustering was the point — that Linh talked the way she navigated, fast and precise, testing every channel for depth. Underneath it she carried her father's map in her head the way Ivo did, except she'd grown up with the original, had traced its lines as a child while Vann told her stories about a barge that went down with French gold on it, gold that turned out to be ledgers, testimony, the drowned names of witnesses. She had spent a year learning what her father had spent a lifetime hiding, and she wore the knowledge lightly, the way you wear a tool you intend to use rather than a wound you intend to keep.

Quentin appeared in the wheelhouse doorway with two cups of coffee, gave one to Linh without asking, and studied the chart over her shoulder. He did this every time they docked. Ivo understood now that it wasn't checking her work. It was the ritual of a man who had spent years navigating alone learning to navigate in company — confirming, each time, that the person beside him could be trusted with the river.

"Right wall, then," Quentin said. "First light. If the buoy's gone we put out our own."

"We don't have a spare buoy."

"We have the twins, a barrel, and a lantern. That's a buoy with ambition."

Linh laughed — short, real — and marked the channel. Ivo looked at the two of them bent over the paper, the old captain and the navigator who was younger than the boat, and thought about what Vann had bought with his last coins of strength: not passage, exactly. Passage had been the currency. What he'd bought was this — a table with people around it, arguing about buoys.

* * *

Mai found him at dusk on the foredeck, where he sat with the captain's book open on his knees, copying the day's log into fair form. She climbed aboard the way she did everything, without asking permission and without apology, and lowered herself onto the cargo hatch with the sigh of a woman whose knees had opinions.

"Show me what you wrote about my river today," she said.

"It's not your river."

"Everything within sight of my stairs is my river. Show me."

He showed her. She read slowly, lips moving slightly, and he watched her face do what it always did — the stern set softening by degrees until she looked less like a headwoman passing judgment and more like a grandmother reading a letter from far away.

"*Herons,*" she said. "You write about birds."

"Quentin says the birds tell you what the bar intends. If they're nesting on it, it's staying. If they won't land, it's going to wash out with the next rain."

"Mm." She handed the book back. "Your captain is a man who learned to read the river because reading people hurt too much. And now look at him. He has a boy writing poetry about herons in his logbook and he pretends it's hydrology."

"It is hydrology."

"It is a boy who was pulled out of a flood learning to name the things the flood leaves behind." Mai stood, joints cracking like small gunfire, and looked down at him with an expression he had learned meant affection delivered at combat strength. "The feast is ready. Your captain will say he has engine work. Sefa will say the engine work is done. The twins will already be eating. Come."

At the feast — long tables under the great house, fish grilled over coals, sticky rice in baskets, the whole village plus crew plus the survey boat's embarrassed engineer whom Mai had adopted out of spite toward his employers — Quentin sat at Mai's right hand, which had become custom, and argued with her about the schoolhouse roof, which had also become custom. They argued the way old friends argue, which is to say about something small while meaning something large. The roof argument was really about whether the village could accept more help than it gave, and the answer, reached anew every few weeks, was that debts between family aren't debts at all.

Ivo sat across from the twins, who were conducting their usual post-voyage debrief, conducted entirely in overlapping sentences and aimed at no one.

"—and then he says, *that's a buoy with ambition*, like—"

"—like he didn't once make us row a barrel two kilometers upstream in the rain—"

"—in the *rain*, Kham—"

"—it was drizzle, Khone, there's a difference—"

"—there's a *difference*, he says, the man who made us lash cargo in a hailstorm—"

"That was one time," Quentin said, without looking up from his rice, "and it was graupel, and you were fine."

The table roared. Ivo laughed with them and felt the strange double vision he sometimes got now: seeing this table as it was, and seeing it as it had almost never been. A year ago tonight he might have been alone on a dark deck holding a dead man's map, guilty and certain, keeping a promise he'd made without permission. The map was framed now, hanging in Mai's great house — the decoy, the one he'd surrendered to Ravelin's men, drawn from memory with deliberate errors, the false channel that had helped ground a gunboat. Linh had the true route in her head. The chest of ledgers sat in a magistrate's strongroom in Vientiane, its copies circulating through every newspaper office and consulate on the river like blood through a body. Ravelin was a case number. His men were other men's men now.

And the *Joubert* ran the monsoon route, home ported at a village that remembered Quentin's worst day and loved him anyway, which Ivo had come to understand was the only kind of love worth having — the kind that knows the whole story.

* * *

Later, when the fires burned low and the twins had finally run out of grievances against the weather, Ivo walked the landing alone. The river lay black and enormous under the stars, moving with that patient muscular purpose it never lost, even here in the wide slow water. He stood at the edge of the boards and listened to it breathe against the pilings.

A year ago he had stood on a deck very much like this one and believed the current only took things away. It had taken his first family, the flood year. It had taken Vann. It had nearly taken the twins in the night rapids, nearly taken the boat in the gorge, nearly taken Quentin when the wheel kicked and threw him and Ivo had steered through the worst hundred meters of his life with his captain shouting helm orders from the deck below, trusting a boy with the whole weight of the river.

But the current also carried things. It had carried a feverish prospector to a muddy landing where a boy broke a rule and gave him water. It had carried a map downstream to a daughter. It had carried a boat full of liars and exiles and orphans into a gorge and out the other side, and carried the truth of Ravelin's crimes into every port on the river faster than any gunboat could chase it. The current took and carried in the same motion, all the time, and the only choice — Quentin had said this once, drunk on one beer, which was his version of drunk — the only choice was whether you fought the carrying.

Ivo went back aboard quietly. In the wheelhouse, the captain's book lay open on the chart table where he'd left it. He picked up the pen.

Below the day's entries — the new bar, the herons, the missing buoy, Sefa's cylinder — he added the line he added every night now, the line Quentin pretended not to know about and had never once told him to remove:

*Home port: Ban Houayxay. All hands accounted for.*

Then, because tomorrow they would run the second narrows at first light on a channel nobody had surveyed, and because the river was already whispering about rain in the hills, he added one more:

*Bar below the ferry crossing — watch it. It's building. So are we.*

Outside, the current went on, dark and certain, carrying them home.
