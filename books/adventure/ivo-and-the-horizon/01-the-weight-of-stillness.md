## 1. The Weight of Stillness

The thief had made three mistakes before noon, and Casper Hartwell had counted every one.

The first was water. Out here, on the salt flats south of the Verrik line, you didn't drink in the open. You drank at dawn, in the shadow of your own body, and you buried the wet ground behind you. This man had knelt at a seep at midmorning, plain as a prayer, and the damp patch he'd left was already telling Casper everything: how long ago, how thirsty, how far he thought he'd get.

The second mistake was direction. He was cutting straight for the broken aqueduct, because that's what everyone did. The aqueduct meant shade, and shade meant survival, except it also meant every other desperate soul in forty miles had the same idea. Casper didn't go to the aqueduct. He went to where the aqueduct would push the man — the narrows where two collapsed spans leaned together like drunkards, leaving a gap barely wide enough for a mule.

The third mistake was the dog. A thin yellow thing trotting ten paces behind its master, too honest to hide its tracks. Casper could have followed the dog alone and saved himself the sun.

He walked anyway. That was the job. Nine years of walking, and the walking had become the only part of him that still worked without complaint. His knees ached, his eyes burned behind the smoked goggles, and his mind — his mind did what it always did out here, which was nothing. Stillness. The flats were good for that. No trees to remind you of anything, no ruins older than your grief, just heat shimmering off white ground until the horizon dissolved and you were walking inside a bowl of light.

Rangers called it the weight of stillness, the way the desert pressed down on a man who stopped moving. Casper had learned there was another kind, the kind that lived indoors, in towns, in rooms with chairs. He'd tried it once, three years back. Six weeks in Colder's Ferry with steady pay and a bed that didn't smell of sand. By the fifth week he was drinking before noon. By the sixth he'd punched a man at the ferry office for no reason he could name, and the reason, when he finally sat with it, was simple: sitting still, the grief caught up. Out here it couldn't. Out here he moved faster than it did.

So he tracked the water-thief, and he was grateful for the work, and he hated himself a little for the gratitude.

* * *

He took the man at the narrows an hour past midday, coming up from behind with the sun at his back so the first warning was Casper's shadow falling across him, long and dark on the pale stone.

The thief spun, dropped the clay jug he'd been cradling like a child, and grabbed for a knife he clearly hadn't used in years. It was a kitchen blade, honed to a sliver, the handle wrapped in rag.

"Don't," Casper said.

The man looked at the knife in his own hand as if surprised to find it there. Then he looked at Casper — at the ranger's coat, sun-bleached to the color of bone; at the rifle slung easy across his chest; at the badge, a stamped tin circle showing a hand holding a line, the mark of the Verrik Water Authority — and the knife came down.

"They said the seep was dead," the man said. "They said nobody patrolled this far."

"Who's they?"

"A man in Halven's Rest. Sold me the map." He laughed, and it was a bad sound, cracked down the middle. "Sold me a map to a seep that's been dry since the war."

Casper looked at him properly then. Forty, maybe. Sun-scorched past that. Clothes that had been good once, a merchant's cut, now stiff with salt sweat. The dog crept up and pressed against the man's leg, and the man's hand found its ears without looking, the way you touch the only thing left that's yours.

"You've got family back east," Casper said. It wasn't a question. Men who crossed the flats alone for a dry seep weren't running toward something.

"A wife. Two girls." The man's jaw worked. "The farm went under. I thought — I heard stories, about water out here. About men who came back rich."

"The stories are lies."

"I know that now."

The wind moved across the flats, carrying grit that ticked against the fallen jug. Casper crouched, picked it up. Empty. Of course empty. He turned it in his hands and felt something old move in his chest, something he kept buried under nine years of miles.

Because he knew this man. Not the face — the arithmetic. The arithmetic that said *I have nothing, so anything is worth the crossing.* Casper had done the same crossing once, just in the other direction. When Tomas died, Casper hadn't grieved. He'd signed on with the Authority, taken the worst posts, walked the emptiest ground. Everyone said he was dedicated. Nobody said he was running, because running implies a destination, and Casper's flight had none. It was motion as anesthesia. Keep the legs busy and the heart has nothing to say.

"Where's your water?" Casper asked.

The man swallowed. "Gone."

"Gone how?"

"Drank it. First day. Panicked." His voice dropped to almost nothing. "I know what you have to do. Fine me. Take the coat. Whatever the law is."

The law was a fine the man couldn't pay, confiscation of gear that would kill him by tomorrow night, and a walk to the nearest magistrate post that he wouldn't survive. Casper knew the regulations better than most. He'd written half of them into practice himself, out here, in the years when rules felt like something solid to hold.

He looked at the man's hands. They were shaking. Not from fear. From thirst, already working.

Casper unslung his canteen and held it out.

The man stared at it. "You're arresting me."

"No."

"But the seep—"

"Dry, like your map said. Nothing stolen from a thing that doesn't exist." Casper pushed the canteen into his hands. "There's a well at the old waystation, six miles north-northeast. Marked stone, iron ring on top. Water's bitter but clean. From there, the caravan road runs east to Colder's Ferry. You can beg passage with what's left of your pride or sell the dog. Either way, you'll live."

The man clutched the canteen like it might be revoked. "Why?"

Because stopping costs more than moving, Casper thought. Because I know what it is to be hollowed out and pointed at the horizon. Because someone once gave my brother water in a worse place than this, and Tomas never forgot it, and I've spent nine years trying to forget everything and failing at that too.

"Get moving," Casper said. "Before I change my mind."

The man went. At the edge of the narrows he turned, opened his mouth, closed it again, and raised one hand — not a wave, more like a salute, or an apology — and then he was gone across the white, small as a scratch, the yellow dog trotting faithful behind.

Casper stood alone in the gap between the fallen spans and felt the stillness settle back over him like ash.

* * *

The courier found him at dusk, which told Casper the man was good, because finding one ranger on the open flats required either luck or money, and couriers who carried what this one carried were paid in the second.

Casper saw him first — a dot against the dying light, riding a dun mare at a pace that respected the horse. By the time the rider reined up at Casper's camp, the fire was lit and the coffee was bitter, and the man swung down with the stiffness of someone who'd been in the saddle since dawn.

"Casper Hartwell?"

"That depends on who's asking."

The courier smiled without warmth and produced a leather tube, sealed with black wax. "Sera Voss asks whether the answer is still no."

Casper didn't take the tube. He looked at it the way you look at a snake you know is venomous but beautiful anyway.

"She sent you all the way out here for a no?"

"She sent me because she said you'd want to read it before you said it." The courier set the tube on the flat rock between them, next to the fire, and stepped back. "She also said — her words — 'Tell him the map is real.' And that she'd wait in Halven's Rest until the season turns. After that, no buyer, no contract, no second letter."

Then he mounted up and was gone, hooves fading into the dark, leaving Casper alone with a cylinder of black wax and nine years of silence.

He didn't open it that night. He sat by the fire and drank his coffee and watched the coals and told himself all the reasons, and there were so many reasons, and every one of them was true.

Qeth-Amar was death. Every ranger worth their badge knew the name the way sailors know a reef. The drowned city beneath the deep waste, where the first expedition went in with nineteen souls and came out with eleven, and the eight they lost included Tomas Hartwell, who was twenty-six years old and laughed like the world was built for it. Casper had sworn off the job in front of witnesses. He'd sworn it in front of Sera Voss herself, both of them standing over the guild hall door in Halven's Rest where the dead got their names carved, and he'd said *never*, and she'd said *understood*, and neither of them had believed the other, because that's what rivals do — they keep accounts.

And now here was her seal, black wax, her mark pressed into it like a thumbprint on a wound.

*The map is real.*

That was the hook of it. She knew him well enough — nine years of rivalry teaches a kind of intimacy that friendship never could — to know that those four words were the only ones that mattered. Not the pay, though the pay would be ruinous. Not the salvage rights, though the rights to Qeth-Amar would set up a crew for life. The map. Because if the map was real, then the city was reachable, and if the city was reachable, then the place where Tomas died was a place a man could stand.

Casper slept badly, which was normal, and woke before dawn, which was also normal, and opened the tube with cold fingers while the sky went gray to pink.

Inside: a contract, drafted in the tight legal hand of some Halven's Rest scrivener. Terms, shares, hazard clauses. And folded separately, a single sheet in Sera's own writing, blunt as a hammer:

*Hartwell. I need a tracker who knows the deep waste and won't panic when it goes wrong, because it will go wrong. I'm not going to pretend this isn't the job we swore off. It is. But the buyer is real, the map is real, and the monsoon seals the wastes in five weeks. After that, Qeth-Amar closes for another lifetime. I'd rather do it with you watching my flank than anyone alive, including the people I trust. Come or don't. But come decided. — V.*

*Come decided.*

He read it four times. Then he sat with it while the sun came up over the flats, turning the white ground gold, then white again, indifferent as ever.

Here was the truth he circled and could not land on: he should burn it. Any sane man would burn it. The sane arithmetic said the first expedition killed eight of nineteen, and the second would kill worse, because the first had gone in blind and this one would go in knowing exactly what waited.

But sanity was a room with chairs, and Casper had learned what happened in rooms with chairs.

He thought about the water-thief, kneeling at a dry seep because a stranger sold him a lie about riches. He thought about the man's shaking hands, and the dog, and the wife and daughters waiting east of nowhere. Desperation wears many coats. Sometimes it's a merchant's jacket crossing the flats. Sometimes it's a ranger's badge, nine years worn, tracking strangers across the emptiness because the alternative is sitting still and letting the grief arrive.

Stopping costs more than moving. It always had. And Sera Voss had put her name to paper saying the map was real, and somewhere under the dread — under the cold weight that had settled into his stomach the moment he saw the black wax — there was a smaller, meaner ember that whispered: *the place where he died is a place you can stand.*

Casper took out his pen. The ink was half-frozen from the night; he warmed it in his fist like a small captured bird.

He signed.

Then he rolled the contract, sealed it with the spare wax stick every ranger carried, and wrote the courier's drop-mark on the outside, and set it under the stone where the riders would find it, and stood looking east toward Halven's Rest, five days' hard walk, where Sera Voss was waiting with a crew he didn't yet know and a door with nine names carved into it that he'd sworn never to look at again.

The wind came up off the flats, hot and dry, smelling of nothing at all.

Five weeks, she'd said. Then the monsoon, and the city closed, and whatever waited under Qeth-Amar stayed shut in the dark with its secret.

Casper broke camp and started walking, faster than the grief, the way he always did.

It wasn't until the third mile that he admitted what the dread in his gut really was. Not fear of dying. He'd made peace with that years ago, out here, alone under stars that owed him nothing.

It was fear that this time, going back, he might finally stop running — and find out what was left of him when he did.
