## 2. Nine Names on a Door

Halven's Rest smelled of tallow and wet stone, the way border towns always did — like a place holding its breath between one country and another. Casper came in from the flats at midmorning, dust still riding his shoulders, and stopped at the edge of the market square to look at what he'd agreed to.

The crew was assembling outside the Wayfarer's Rest, an inn with a crooked sign and a stable yard full of camels that looked better fed than most of the people. He counted them without meaning to. Old habit. Nine figures, plus Sera Voss standing at the head of them with her arms crossed, watching him watch her.

She'd gone grey at the temples. That was the first thing. The second was that she didn't smile, and he realized he'd been braced for a smile for nine years, some flicker of the woman who used to race him across the salt pans and laugh when they both fell. What stood there instead was harder than anything he remembered, and it looked at him the way you look at weather coming in.

"Hartwell." She said his name like she was checking a seal on a crate. "You got my letter."

"I got your contract." He stepped into the shade of the inn's awning. "The letter I burned."

"Sentimental."

"Practical." He looked past her at the crew. "Nine. You said eight."

"One dropped out this morning. Cold feet." She shrugged. "You're the ninth."

He didn't answer that. There was nothing to answer. They both knew why he'd come, and it wasn't the pay, though the pay was obscene — enough to buy the kind of quiet most men only found in the ground.

* * *

Sera ran the introductions like a muster, quick and unsentimental, and Casper catalogued faces the way he catalogued terrain: which ones would hold, which ones would crack.

Dree Osk, the salvage master, a broad woman with burn-scarred forearms who'd worked wrecks along the coast before the coast stopped being worth working. She shook his hand once, hard, and asked no questions, which he appreciated more than she could know.

Ilex Marrin, the navigator — young, maybe thirty, with ink-stained fingers and a habit of drawing maps on anything that held still. She'd charted the route from fragments: survey scraps, a dead prospector's journal, half a tablet someone had smuggled out of Qeth-Amar forty years ago. When Casper mentioned the wastes south of the Verren line, she lit up like he'd handed her a gift.

"You've been through," she said. "Actually through?"

"Once."

"And?"

"And I'm here." He let that sit. She didn't push. Smart.

Then the old hands. Brannic Hale, who'd been a rope-handler on the first expedition, older now, quieter, with a tremor in his left hand he kept folded out of sight. He met Casper's eyes across the yard and neither of them said Tomas's name, and the not-saying was louder than shouting. And Pell Wicker, who'd been nineteen on the first run and was twenty-eight now and had never gone back into the deep waste since — until now. He had a wife in Halven's Rest and a baby he talked about the way other men talk about religion.

The rest were strangers. A water engineer named Sofi Danat who asked precise questions about cisterns. A pair of brothers, Tev and Rold Ammen, hired muscle who watched everything and trusted nothing. And a quiet man called Merrek Ostrow, listed as quartermaster, who counted crates with the focus of a man memorizing them.

Casper noticed that. Filed it. Said nothing.

* * *

They spent two days in Halven's Rest loading and arguing. Sera argued like she always had — fast, certain, willing to be wrong later if it meant moving now. Casper argued the way grief had taught him: slow, from behind cover, picking apart every assumption until someone lost their temper.

By the second evening they'd both drawn blood.

"You're doing it again," Sera said, cornering him in the stable yard after a planning session that had ended with Ilex storming off to redraw her route. "Poking holes because holes are safer than roads."

"I'm poking holes because the last road killed four men and my brother."

"The last road failed because we were young and stupid and I made a call with bad information. I've had nine years to be less stupid. You've had nine years to be—" she waved a hand at him, "—whatever this is. This careful, hollowed-out version."

"That version's alive."

"So am I." Her voice didn't rise. That was the worst part; she'd learned to fight cold. "You think I don't know what Qeth-Amar cost? You think I signed this contract standing in a field of flowers? I know exactly what's down there, Hartwell. Better than you do. And I'm going anyway, and I needed the best tracker in the territory beside me, and that's you, and I hate that it's you, but here we are."

For a moment the old rivalry flared clean between them — not the corroded thing, the original one, two people who'd spent a decade trying to outrun each other across every flat and ridge in the waste. It almost felt good. Then it settled back into ash.

"Why me?" he said. "You could have hired anyone."

"Because everyone else would follow me." She turned toward the gate. "You'd tell me when I'm wrong. You're the only man alive who will."

* * *

On the last morning, walking to the guild hall to file their expedition bond, they passed the door.

It was a heavy thing, black ironwood banded in bronze, set into the face of the guild hall where the territory's rangers and salvagers recorded their dead. Names were cut into the door itself, and when a company lost someone, they added the names and sealed the panel with wax and wire. Nobody opened a sealed panel. That was the whole point. The door only grew.

Casper had walked past it a hundred times over nine years and never once looked directly at it. Today Sera slowed, and he understood she meant to stop, and he hated her for it.

Eight panels down, near the bottom, wax gone amber with age: the Voss-Hartwell expedition, nine years past. Four names.

Brannic Hale had carved them himself, kneeling on these stones with his hands shaking. Casper knew because he'd been there, standing three feet back, unable to touch the chisel.

TOMAS HARTWELL. The letters were deep and even, cut by a man who loved him and couldn't say so either.

Casper looked at the name the way you look at the sun — a fraction of a second, then away, then the afterimage burning for hours.

"Say something," Sera said quietly.

"What do you want me to say?"

"Anything. You've said nothing for nine years. Not to me, not to the door, not to anyone. You track water-thieves across the flats and let them go and call it mercy, and you haven't said his name out loud since the funeral. I've watched you do it."

"You don't know what I do."

"I know exactly what you do." She faced the door, not him. "I come here twice a year. Every year. I stand here and I read all four names and I tell them what I've done and I ask them if it was worth it, and they don't answer, and then I leave. That's the difference between us, Hartwell. We both buried him. Only one of us visits the grave."

His jaw ached. He hadn't realized he was clenching it.

"He'd have hated this job," Casper said finally. "Coming back. He told me once — the wastes give you back exactly what you bring. Nothing more."

"Then we'd better bring something worth getting back." Sera touched the bronze band below the sealed panel — not the names, never the names, just the metal beneath them, the way you touch a shoulder — and walked on.

Casper stayed a moment longer. The street moved around him: carters, children, a woman selling melons, all of them passing the door of the dead without a glance, because that was what living people did.

"Tomas," he said. Just the name. It came out rough, like something hauled up from depth.

Then he went after her.

* * *

They cleared the last well at noon and turned south, and the town shrank behind them to a brown smudge against the haze. Ilex rode point with her charts wrapped in oilcloth. Dree checked the load rigging twice. Merrek Ostrow counted the water cases again, third time that day, lips moving slightly as he tallied.

Casper fell in beside Sera at the column's rear, where he could see everyone at once.

"How much did the buyer offer?" he asked.

"Enough."

"For what? Salvage rights, or the site itself?"

Her silence lasted four strides. Five.

"Casper—"

"There's a case in your baggage," he said. "Sealed, lead-lined, marked for return trip only. I saw the manifest. So did Dree. We're not carrying tools out there, Sera. We're carrying a container. Which means somebody knows what's in Qeth-Amar, and wants it brought back alive."

Sera looked at him for a long moment, and whatever she saw in his face made her turn forward again, toward the heat shimmer and the empty south.

"Monsoon's in six weeks," she said. "Ask me again when we get there."

That wasn't an answer. It was worse than an answer. It was a promise that the answer existed, and that she already knew it, and that she'd decided he'd go faster with the truth behind him than in front.

He rode, and watched the horizon darken in the east where no storm should be, and thought about a name cut deep into ironwood, and how far a man could travel before the ground started giving back what he'd brought.
