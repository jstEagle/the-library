## 3. Salt and Second Chances

The waste did not forgive hesitation, and neither did Sera Voss.

She rode point on a dun courser named Brine, three lengths ahead of the column, counting shadows the way other people counted money. Nine days out of Halven's Rest and the land had already stopped pretending to be land. It was a rumor of land — cracked pans, salt crust white as bone meal, ridges of black stone that rose out of the flat like the spines of drowned animals. The sun came up mean and stayed meaner.

Behind her: eleven riders, two wagons, forty days of water if nobody spilled any, and Casper Hartwell riding drag with his hood up like a man attending his own funeral.

She'd hired him because he was the best tracker in the borderlands and because she was a coward about exactly one thing, and it wasn't this desert.

*Debts,* she thought, and let the word sit in her chest where it lived now, permanent as a scar. The Voss salvage company had been dying for six years — slow, undignified, the way her father had died, the way everything she touched seemed to die eventually. There were creditors in Halven's Rest who smiled at her too kindly. There was a warehouse full of rusted equipment worth less than the paper it was stored on. And there was the buyer.

She didn't let herself think of him by name. Names gave things weight, and she couldn't afford the ballast.

He'd come to her office eight months ago, past midnight, past every lock she'd ever installed, and sat down across from her desk like an old friend. He'd known about Qeth-Amar. Not the legends — everyone knew the legends, the drowned city, the towers that drank light. He'd known the *coordinates*. He'd known the descent shaft survived. He'd known things about the first expedition that only two living people could have told him, and one of them was sitting across from him trying not to shake.

"Your debts," he'd said, "are arithmetic. I'm offering you a solution."

"And what does it cost?"

"Nothing you haven't already lost."

She should have put a knife in him then. Instead she'd taken the map, because the map was real — she'd spent four months verifying it down to the last seam of stone — and because a woman drowning will grab at anything, even a hand reaching out of the deep.

Now the buyer's map rode folded against her ribs, and his instructions rode somewhere worse, behind them, and every night she checked the crew's faces for the one she knew was embedded among them. *An agent,* the buyer had said. *For your protection.* She hadn't been able to pick him out yet. That frightened her more than the storm she could see building on the southern horizon.

Because there was a storm. Had been for two days — a brown smear along the world's edge, patient as debt itself.

"Hartwell." She didn't turn around. "How long?"

His horse came up beside hers without sound. Nine years and the man still moved like a shadow with opinions. "Storm's faster than we are," he said. "We don't make the ridge."

"The caves do."

A pause. She felt him recalculating, reading the land under its skin the way he read everything. "Caves," he said finally. "You've been holding that back."

"I hold back everything. It's why I'm still alive."

Something that wasn't quite a smile crossed his face and died there. "Two miles," he said. "Northeast. Blackstone shelf — there's hollows under it. Tomas and I sheltered in one in—" He stopped. The name hung between them like a dropped blade.

"Tomas and you," Sera said quietly. "Say it or don't, but stop choking on it."

Casper looked at her then, and for a moment she saw nine years of grief with nowhere to go, packed down into a man who'd turned himself into a tool to survive it. Then he wheeled his horse northeast and started shouting orders, and she hated how good he was at it, and hated more that she was relieved.

* *

The blackstone shelf rose out of the flats like a shipwreck, and the caves beneath it were older than any map — wormholes of smooth dark stone, breathing cool air that tasted of minerals and deep time. They got the wagons under cover with twenty minutes to spare, and then the world ended politely, all at once.

Sera stood at the cave mouth and watched the storm come. It didn't look like weather. It looked like the desert standing up — a wall of red-brown a thousand feet high, rolling toward them with a sound like the whole earth being ground finer. The light went orange, then brown, then nothing. Sand found every gap in her clothes, her teeth, her thoughts. Somewhere behind her, horses screamed and were soothed. Somewhere behind her, men laughed too loudly, the way people did when they wanted the dark to know they weren't afraid.

She wasn't afraid either. She was doing arithmetic again. Water spent sheltering: two days. Days remaining before monsoon sealed the wastes: shrinking. And somewhere in that brown roar, the buyer's agent was watching her, and she still didn't know his face.

That was when the screaming started — not horse-screaming. Human.

She turned into the black and followed the sound, hand on her knife, boots slipping on sand that poured through the cave mouth like water running backward. Twenty feet in, the dark broke apart around a lantern: three crew clustered at the edge of a fissure she hadn't marked on any survey, a crack in the shelf floor wide as a wagon, breathing cold air upward. And down in it, half-visible, a man.

Dario. One of the strangers — young, quiet, good with the camels. He'd gone back for a loose animal and the floor had opened under him. He'd caught a ledge. He was not going to keep catching it.

"Rope," someone shouted uselessly.

"There's no anchor—"

"There's me." Casper.

He came through the lantern light already moving, and Sera watched him work and understood, with a sick lurch, that she was watching something rehearsed. He didn't run to the edge and look down — he stopped short, threw sand across the lip until he found where it drained inward, mapped the fissure's throat in two seconds flat. He pulled off his sash, knotted it around his own chest in a harness hitch she recognized because Tomas had invented it, showed it to her once across a campfire in another life, laughing, saying *if you fall, Voss, this is what saves you, so be nice to me.*

"Casper," she said. "The walls are slick. If the sand shifts while you're on the line—"

"Then pull fast." He fed the rope around a spur of stone, took two wraps, and stepped backward over the edge into the dark like a man stepping off a dock into familiar water. "Dario! Look at my hands. Don't grab the rope. Grab my wrists. The rope is for me. You're for me."

The descent took four minutes and lasted a year. Sand hissed down the fissure in thin ropes. The stranger below had stopped screaming, which was worse than screaming — Sera had seen that silence before, on the first expedition, on men who had decided the math wasn't in their favor. But Casper kept talking down into the dark, low and steady, a rhythm more than words, and later Dario would tell anyone who asked that Hartwell had recited something the whole way, some cadence, like a song without a tune.

When they came up — Casper last, hauling the half-frozen man over the lip with hands that were bleeding freely and didn't seem to notice — the crew made the noise crowds make. Relief, awe, the beginning of a legend. Sera stood apart from it, arms crossed, and watched Casper kneel in the lantern light checking Dario's fingers for frost-crack, talking to him gently, and felt something in her chest turn over like a stone in a streambed.

Those techniques were Tomas's. Every one of them. The sand-reading, the harness hitch, the cadence — Tomas had learned it from a salt-diver in Qeth-Amar's outskirts and worn it like a second skin, and Casper had absorbed it the way brothers absorb each other, without knowing, without permission. Watching him use it was like watching a dead man move through a living body.

"You knew that fissure was there," Casper said, coming to stand beside her. Not accusing. Worse — tired. "You scouted this shelf two years ago. You knew, and you camped us on top of it anyway, because it was the fastest shelter and the schedule mattered more."

"The schedule always matters."

"Not more than that." He nodded at Dario, who was wrapped in blankets by the fire now, alive, staring at his own hands like they belonged to someone else. "You want to tell me what's chasing us, Sera? Because it isn't monsoon season. Monsoon doesn't make you cut corners like a thief."

She almost told him. The buyer, the debts, the agent in their midst, the promise — the promise she'd made to a dying man in a tent outside Qeth-Amar nine years ago, holding his hand while he bled out, swearing she'd finish what the expedition started, swearing it because he asked and because refusing a dying man anything is the one cruelty a person can't commit.

Instead she said, "Get your hands bandaged, Hartwell. You bleed on my rope again and I'm docking your pay."

He held her gaze a long moment. Something passed between them that wasn't forgiveness — she hadn't earned that — but was maybe its cousin, its poor relation. Grudging respect, surfacing like bones in shifting sand: there all along, just buried.

"Nine years," he said quietly, "and you're still the same liar."

"And you're still the same fool who follows liars into the deep waste." She turned away before he could see her face do anything honest. "Sleep. We move when the storm breaks."

* *

It broke near dawn, the way they did — all at once, the wind dying mid-breath, the sky rinsing to a pale bruised gold. Sera climbed out of the cave mouth into a world remade: dunes shifted, the flats re-skinned, the horizon scrubbed clean and lying about its intentions.

That was when she saw it, far to the south, where the storm had come from and gone.

Towers. Thin as needles, black as the shelf they'd slept under, catching the first light and bending it wrong — drinking it, the old stories said, and the old stories, for once, were underselling it. Even at this distance, even as a smudge, Qeth-Amar pulled at the eye like a hole in the world.

Behind her, she heard the crew gathering, heard the murmurs, heard Casper's boots stop beside hers.

"That's not on the map," he said slowly. "The map puts the city east of here. Two days east."

Sera looked at the towers, and at the map against her ribs, and understood with cold clarity that the buyer had given her a true map to a false place — or a false map to a true one — and that somewhere among the eleven riders behind her, the man who'd drawn it was watching her realize it.

"No," she said. "It isn't."
