## 4. The City That Drinks Light

We came over the last ridge an hour before sundown, and the whole column stopped without anyone ordering it to.

Qeth-Amar sat in the basin below us like a thing that had been waiting. Nine towers, black stone gone gray with age, rising out of the flats in a crescent — and the light was going into them. I don't know how else to say it. The sun was low and red behind us, and its last rays should have lit the western faces of those towers gold the way they'd lit every ruin I'd ever stood in. Instead the stone drank it. The walls stayed dark. The shadows fell the wrong way, toward the sun.

"Tell me you're seeing this," Odessa said beside me. She was one of the strangers, a demolition woman out of Corrin's Reach with burn-scarred forearms and no patience for superstition. Her voice had gone quiet anyway.

"I'm seeing it," I said.

Sera rode up the line, her duster snapping in a wind that smelled like hot metal. She didn't stop at the ridge. "It'll be dark in forty minutes," she called back. "We make the plaza tonight or we camp on open sand and explain to the monsoon why we were late. Move."

So we moved. Down off the ridge, across three miles of ground so flat and salt-crusted it rang faintly under the wheels of the water cart, and into the shadow of the towers. And I want to be honest about what that was like, because I've told this story badly ever since: walking into Qeth-Amar felt like walking into a mouth. Not menacing, exactly. Patient. The air changed ten yards inside the crescent of walls — cooler, stiller, tasting faintly of ozone, like the moment before lightning. The horses hated it. The camels hated it more. We had to hobble them outside the walls with double lines and let Rennick sit with them, and even he, who feared nothing I'd ever seen, kept his rifle across his knees the whole first night.

The plaza at the city's center was paved in the same black stone as the towers, fitted so precisely there wasn't a seam you could slip a knife into. No sand had collected on it. Nine years of wind, centuries of wind, and the plaza was clean as a swept floor. Sera walked out to its middle and stood there a long time, turning slowly, reading the ground the way she read everything — like it owed her money.

"It should be under here," she said.

"You've been here before?" Odessa asked.

"No." A beat. "Tomas did."

Nobody said anything. The wind moved through the towers and made a sound I didn't like — not whistling, not moaning. More like breathing. Low and even, in and out, like something asleep that could wake.

I found it because my boot told me. Twenty paces from where Sera stood, the temperature changed underfoot — a thin band of warmth crossing the plaza like a river under ice. I crouched, pulled off a glove, and pressed my palm flat to the stone.

Warm. Not sun-warm. Sun doesn't reach through six inches of black basalt and keep it blood-heat at dusk. This was coming from below, from a line running straight as a surveyor's chalk toward the base of the central tower.

"Sera."

She was beside me in four strides. I pulled my knife and worked it into the joint between two paving stones. It sank half an inch and stopped against something that gave — not stone, some kind of sealant, ancient and glassy. Around the slab I'd chosen, faint as breath on glass, a circle showed itself once my hand brushed away the dust: a ring of characters in no alphabet I knew, worn nearly smooth by hands touching it long before the wastes were wastes.

"The shaft," Sera said. "Tomas described it. He said—" She stopped. I watched her decide not to finish the sentence, and I let her, because I'd spent nine years getting good at letting people not finish sentences about Tomas.

It took Odessa and her charges two hours to crack the seal without cracking what was underneath. What opened was a shaft maybe eight feet across, walled in smooth black stone, descending farther than our lamps could argue with. Warm air rose out of it, steady as a chimney draft, carrying that ozone smell and something else underneath — something organic, like the air of a room where someone has been living a very long time.

"Still warm," Odessa said, hanging her head over the edge. "After how long? Nothing stays warm for—"

"Centuries," Sera said. "I know."

"And we're going down into it."

"That was always the job."

* * *

We made camp in the lee of the eastern tower, twelve souls and a water cart, with the shaft mouth ringed in warning lamps and Rennick's watch rotation set for the night. Supper was hardtack and smoked goat and water that tasted of canvas. Nobody talked much. Even the strangers had gone quiet, the way people do when a place is bigger than their vocabulary. Ilex sat apart with her charts, tracing routes by lamplight, mouthing numbers. Dree and Kasten — the two big quiet ones Sera had hired out of the southern pits — cleaned weapons that were already clean.

I took first watch myself. I wanted my eyes on the towers while the moon came up, because I didn't trust them. By full dark they'd stopped drinking light and started doing something worse: nothing. They didn't reflect the moon either. They were nine holes cut out of the sky, and my eyes kept sliding off them like water off wax.

Around midnight I heard footsteps behind me and knew the stride before I turned. Sera carried herself like a drawn blade; she had since she was nineteen.

"You felt it too," she said, sitting down on the cart bench beside my post. Not a question.

"Felt what?"

"Don't do that. You've been doing it since Halven's Rest — standing apart, cataloguing, waiting for the job to turn into the last one." She looked up at the tower holes. "You felt it. The city's awake. Or something in it is."

I didn't answer right away. Below us, the lamp ring around the shaft burned steady, and the warm air came up out of the dark in that slow rhythm, in and out.

"Tomas said the towers drank light," I said finally. "That's what he wrote in his last letter. Everyone thought he was being poetic. Tomas wasn't poetic. He was precise. If he wrote *drank*, he meant it."

Sera was quiet a while. "There's a lot he said that I haven't told you."

"There's a lot everybody hasn't told me. It's starting to feel like a profession."

"Casper—"

"Save it." I said it harder than I meant to. "When we're out. When there's distance between us and this place. Down here everything true sounds like a lie anyway."

She almost smiled. Almost. "Still the most stubborn man in the wastes."

"Second most. You never quit anything in your life, including arguments you lost."

That got the real smile, brief as a struck match, and then it was gone and she went to check the perimeter, and I watched her go and hated how much of my attention went with her.

* * *

I found the case an hour before dawn, and I will spend the rest of my life knowing I should have found it sooner.

It was Odessa's watch. I'd come down off the wall to warm my hands at the coals, and I saw the light — a lantern glow spilling from under the supply wagon's canvas where no glow should be. Someone crouched at the wagon's tailgate, working at something with a pry bar, moving careful and slow.

I came around the wagon with my rifle up and my voice low. "Step back. Hands where I can see them."

Kasten straightened up slow, palms out, that broad pit-fighter's face carefully empty. On the tailgate behind him lay a case I knew. Every one of us had signed for it in Halven's Rest — the sealed case, iron-banded, wax-stamped with the buyer's mark, the one Sera had told us in plain words was not to be opened until the return trip. Salvage protocol. Chain of custody. We'd all nodded like men who believed things.

The wax was broken. The bands were cut. The lid stood open.

Inside, packed in wool, lay no tools and no trade goods. A cradle of padded iron held a single object: a sphere the size of a fist, black as the towers, veined with hairline lines of dull silver. It didn't reflect the lantern light. It took it. Looking at it was like looking down the shaft — depth with no bottom, warmth with no source. As I watched, the silver veins brightened, just slightly, the way a coal breathes when the wind finds it.

And beneath it, folded into the wool, papers. A letter bearing the buyer's seal. I didn't touch it. I didn't need to. I could read enough upside down: instructions. Coordinates. The phrase *upon successful extraction of the core* — and a name. Not the buyer's name. A name I didn't know, written with the care people give names that matter.

"Who told you to open it?" I asked.

Kasten's eyes flicked past me. Behind me, boots on stone — Dree, and then Sera, drawn by the lantern light, and I heard her breath change when she saw the case.

"Nobody told me," Kasten said. "It opened itself."

"That's not a thing cases do."

"Look at it, ranger." For the first time his voice cracked, and under the crack was fear, real fear, the kind men from the southern pits don't show. "It's been humming since the ridge. Three nights now. Tonight it got loud. Like it knew we'd arrived. Like it was *waiting*." He wiped his mouth with the back of his hand. "I didn't break the wax. I swear on the pits, I swear on my mother — the wax was already broken when I climbed up. I only lifted the lid."

Sera stepped past me and looked into the case, and I watched her face do something I'd never seen it do in twenty years of knowing her. It went blank. Total blankness, the face of a woman reading her own death warrant.

"Sera," I said quietly. "What is retrieval? Because this isn't salvage gear. This is a delivery. We didn't hire on to carry treasure out of Qeth-Amar. We hired on to carry *this* to somebody. Somebody specific. Somebody who built a case that fits it like a coffin."

She didn't answer.

In the case, the sphere pulsed once — slow, soft, unmistakable — like a heartbeat, or like something knocking politely from the inside of a door. The warm air rose from the shaft across the plaza, in and out, in and out, and for the first time I understood that the rhythm matched.

The engine knew we'd brought it a piece of itself. And somewhere below us, in the dark, something began — very faintly, very patiently — to hum along.
