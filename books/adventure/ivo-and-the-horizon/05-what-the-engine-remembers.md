## 5. What the Engine Remembers

Sera had led eleven expeditions into the waste, and she had learned to read a site the way other people read faces. Qeth-Amar's face was wrong.

The descent shaft breathed. That was the only word for it — a slow exhalation of warm air rising from the dark, carrying a smell like hot copper and something sweeter beneath it, something almost floral. The stone around the shaft's lip was warm to the touch after nine hundred years of burial. She'd pressed her palm against it that morning and felt a pulse, faint and regular, like a sleeper's heartbeat through a wall.

The crew felt it too. She watched them cluster at the shaft's edge with the particular stillness of people standing at the border of a country they'd only heard about in stories. Even the strangers — the riggers, the water-hand, the quiet man named Doss who never ate with the others — even they understood that they had not come to a ruin.

"Ropes in pairs," she said. "Torches burn strange down there, so nobody moves on their own light. Ilex, you're second. Casper, you're with me."

Casper said nothing. He'd said very little since the plaza, since they'd found the shaft still warm. He checked her harness knots without asking, the way he always had, and Sera let him, because his knots were better and because it was easier than talking about everything else that sat between them.

They went down into the dark in pairs, and the dark went down a long way.

* * *

The engine chamber opened like a throat.

Sera's torchlight found the far wall first — a curve of worked stone inlaid with metal that had not corroded, running in channels from floor to ceiling like the veins of something enormous. Then the floor, a shallow basin of black glass. Then, at the center, the engine itself.

It was smaller than she'd imagined. That was the first obscenity of it. Nine centuries of legend, the drowned city, the buyer's obscene money, and at the heart of it all sat a thing the size of a wagon bed: a lattice of dark metal and pale crystal, suspended a hand's width above the basin, turning so slowly she could watch it move only by looking away and back. No gears. No smoke. It turned the way a thought turns.

The warmth came from it. So did the pulse. So, she realized, standing at the basin's edge, did the sweetness in the air.

"It's alive," said the water-hand, a boy called Ferren who'd never been underground before.

"It's a machine," Casper said. "Machines can be alive. That's what makes them dangerous."

Sera held up a hand and the crew went quiet. She walked the basin's rim, counting the inlaid channels — twelve of them, running from twelve points around the wall, converging on the engine. At the base of each channel was a depression in the stone, roughly the size of a hand.

She'd seen the buyer's documents. She knew what the depressions were for.

"Nobody touches it," she said. "Nobody touches the walls, the basin, nothing. We survey, we mark the core housing, and we get out. That's the job."

"Then why'd the old-timers build twelve doors to it?" Doss asked from the back. His voice was flat as slate. "Man doesn't build twelve handles on a thing he doesn't want turned."

Sera didn't answer. She was looking at the nearest depression, at the faint polish on its stone, as if nine hundred years of hands had worn it smooth.

* * *

They made camp in the antechamber above, and it was Ilex who broke the rule.

Sera heard it before she understood it: Ilex's voice, rising from the chamber below, talking fast, talking to herself. She went down the ladder with her torch and found the navigator standing at the basin's edge with her hand pressed flat against one of the twelve depressions, her eyes wide and unfocused, her lips moving.

"Ilex." Sera kept her voice level. "Step away from it."

"I can see it." Ilex didn't turn. Her voice had a strange, doubled quality, as if two women were speaking in unison and one of them was slightly ahead of the other. "The whole route. Every dune, every fall, the dry riverbed and where it forks, the well at Sereth's Thumb that isn't on any map — Sera, I can see the way out like I'm *flying* over it. Every step. Every—"

"Ilex. Let go."

Ilex let go. She swayed, caught herself on the basin's rim, and laughed — a bright, cracked, joyful sound that had no business being in that chamber. "Nine years I've been navigating off half-remembered routes and dead men's sketches. Nine years. And now—" She turned, and her face in the torchlight was the face of a woman who had been given something she'd wanted her whole life.

"Your daughter," Sera said. "Maretta. She's what, six now? You said you'd—"

"Maretta." Ilex frowned. The frown lasted a heartbeat, two, a small crease between her brows like a hairline crack in glaze. "Maretta. Yes. She's—" She stopped. She looked at Sera with perfect, terrible calm, and Sera watched the crease smooth itself away, watched the woman decide the missing thing wasn't missing at all. "She's fine. She's home. What matters is the route. Sera, I can get us out through the monsoon. *Through* it. Nobody's ever—"

"Get up top," Sera said. "Now."

Ilex went. At the ladder she paused and said, "It doesn't take, Sera. It *trades*. That's what the old inscriptions say, the ones by the shaft. It doesn't take anything you don't hand it." And she climbed, humming.

Sera stood alone in the chamber with the turning thing and the pulse in the walls, and she understood that Ilex was right, and that being right made it worse.

* * *

By morning, three more had touched it.

Ferren went down at first light and came up knowing how to read the old script that covered the shaft walls — came up not knowing his mother's face. He described the loss the way a man describes a tooth coming out: there one moment, a gap the next, the tongue going to it again and again. He was weeping and smiling while he did it.

Doss went down alone, without asking, and came up with nothing visible changed at all. He ate breakfast with the others and looked at Sera across the fire and smiled, and it was the first time she'd seen him smile, and she liked it less than anything else that had happened in the drowned city.

The rigger Ovist went third, and after, he stood at the chamber's edge for an hour, and when he came back he asked Sera, gently, politely, when they were going to cut the core free and take it to the buyer. As if it had already been decided. As if it were the most reasonable thing in the world.

"It's not the job," Sera said.

"It's the *point* of the job," Ovist said, still gentle. "You know it is. Ask yourself why the buyer sent an agent all this way and paid what he paid. Ask yourself why you said yes." He walked away before she could answer, and Sera stood in the antechamber feeling the floor tilt under certainties she'd thought were load-bearing.

* * *

Casper found her at the chamber's rim that evening.

She'd gone down alone, telling herself it was reconnaissance, knowing it was nothing of the kind. The engine turned. The pulse beat in the walls. And the pull — she had told herself there would be no pull for her, that she was too old and too hard and too much in debt to be tempted, and the pull was there anyway, patient as water, wearing at the stone of her.

She hadn't touched it. Her hand was a foot away from the depression. That was all. A foot of air and nine years of discipline.

"What did you see?" Casper asked, from behind her.

She hadn't heard him come down. She didn't turn. "Nothing. I haven't touched it."

"That's not what I asked."

Sera looked at her hand. The engine turned. And she lied.

"I looked," she said. "That's all. There's nothing to see unless you give it something, and I'm not giving it anything." She turned then and made herself meet his eyes, and the lie went out of her mouth smooth and clean, the way the best lies do, because it was built on a true thing: *I haven't touched it.*

Casper studied her for a long moment. He had a face built for suspicion and she'd spent nine years reading it, and in all that reading she had never once caught him catching her. He nodded slowly.

"Good," he said. "Because whatever it gave Ilex, it took the price out of her without her feeling the blade. That's not a trade. That's a *thief* with good manners." He looked past her at the engine, at the turning, at the twelve worn-smooth depressions, and his voice dropped. "My brother used to say the old world didn't fall. It *spent* itself. Every wonder they built, they paid for with something they couldn't spare." He was quiet a moment. "Tomas would have touched it inside a minute. You know that."

The name went through her like a hook. She kept her face still. "I know that."

"So we mark the core, we don't cut it, and we walk out with nothing." He said it like a man testing a rope. "That was the job."

"That was the job," Sera agreed.

But she was thinking about the buyer's letter, the one line she'd never shown him: *The core is to be removed intact. The agent will make the arrangements.* She was thinking about Ovist's gentle voice and Doss's first smile. She was thinking about the promise she'd made at a graveside nine years ago, to a dead man, about his brother, a promise that had put her in debt to the buyer in the first place — and about how debts, in her experience, always came down the mountain to collect.

Above them, faint through the stone, someone laughed. It sounded like Ilex. It sounded like a woman who had forgotten something important and was happier for it.

Casper's head came up. "How many are down here right now?"

Sera counted the torches at the chamber's mouth. Then she counted them again.

"Too few," she said. "Casper — too few."
