## 6. A Bargain Struck in the Dark

Casper slept badly in the engine's shadow, and woke to a silence that had weight to it.

Not the silence of the waste, which was really a thousand small sounds braided together — wind gritting over stone, the tick of cooling metal, someone's cough three tents down. This was the silence of people holding still. He knew it from ambushes, from the moment before a dune gave way. Nine years of desert work had taught him that the desert never went quiet on its own. When it did, something had made it.

He came out of his tent with his knife already loose in its sheath and found the camp arranged wrong. The fire had been rebuilt — bigger than it needed to be, throwing light all the way to the shaft mouth. And standing at the edge of that light, where the glow gave out and the dark of the descent began, were three figures who should not have been standing together.

Dree Marrow. Osk Vane. And Corren Hale, who for eleven days had been nothing more than the crew's quiet man — the one who hauled rope without complaint, who cooked when it was his turn, who asked careful questions about water margins and never once about Qeth-Amar itself.

Casper had counted him as ballast. Steady, forgettable ballast.

"Evening, Hartwell," Hale said. His voice was the same mild voice he'd used over every camp meal. That was the worst part. "You sleep?"

"Badly." Casper let his hand rest on his belt, casual, deliberate. Across the fire he could see Sera's tent, flap open, empty. His stomach dropped a slow inch. "Where's Voss?"

"Talking with Ilex." Hale turned so the firelight caught half his face, and for the first time Casper looked properly at him and saw what he'd trained himself not to see: a man carrying weight like a mule carries cargo, evenly, without opinion. "I'd like to show you something before she comes back. Something I showed Dree and Osk last night."

"You showed them the engine," Casper said.

"I showed them what it does." Hale reached into his coat — slowly, palms visible, a man who understood how fast rangers drew — and brought out a strip of pale metal no longer than a knife. It was dull, unremarkable, except that the firelight seemed to land on it slightly late, as if it arrived a half-second after everything else. A sliver of the core. Shaved off, somehow, during their first day below. "Touch it."

"No."

"It doesn't take much. Not like the chamber. Just a taste." Hale smiled, and there was something almost gentle in it. "Osk can tell you the rainfall in Halven's Rest for the last six years. Every storm, every dry month. Dree remembers her mother's face now — she'd told me she'd lost it years ago. Small things. Fair trades."

*Fair trades.* Casper heard Tomas in the words, or rather heard the shape of the argument Tomas would have made, because Tomas had always believed the world ran on fair trades and died learning otherwise. "And what did you pay?" he asked.

Hale's smile didn't move. "Nothing I needed."

Behind him, Dree shifted her weight. She was a good climber, a better cook, and until this moment Casper would have sworn she was one of the honest ones. Now she wouldn't meet his eyes, and her hand kept moving to her temple, touching it the way people touch a sore tooth.

"The buyer," Casper said. "There is no salvage contract. There never was."

"There's a contract," Hale agreed. "You've been paid against it. So has Voss. But the deliverable isn't scrap and old-world alloy, no. The deliverable is the core itself, cut free and carried out whole. My employer has been waiting a long time for it. Longer than you've been alive, probably." He said it lightly, but underneath the lightness Casper heard certainty — the flat, bottomless certainty of a man who had stopped asking whether a thing was possible and only asked how. "The monsoon seals the wastes in nine days. We cut the core free in five, we're out with margin. Everyone gets rich. Everyone gets home."

"And everyone forgets something," Casper said. "That's the toll. You know that. You've felt it yourself — don't tell me your employer's man walked into that chamber and paid nothing."

For just a moment, Hale's composure flickered. Casper watched it go and come back, quick as heat lightning, and understood two things at once. First: Hale had touched the engine, deeply, and lost something he'd decided he could live without. Second: whatever he'd lost, he'd already spent it buying this lie.

"Sera made a promise," Casper said. "To my brother. Before the first expedition ever broke ground. Didn't she."

Hale studied him. "She told you that?"

"She didn't have to. I've watched her circle that chamber for three days like it's a grave." Which it was. Casper had known it since the second night and hadn't let himself say it, because saying it would mean deciding what to do about it, and deciding meant choosing between the job and the truth, and he'd built nine years on never having to choose. "The core stays in the machine. That was the promise. That's why she took the job — not to cut it free. To make sure nobody else did."

"Then she's defrauded her crew and her buyer both," Hale said, "and you along with her." He held up the sliver between two fingers, letting the delayed light crawl across it. "Here's the bargain, ranger, and I'll make it plain because I respect plain men. Help us cut it free, and you walk away with double shares and your memories intact — we do the cutting, we carry the cost. Refuse, and you're welcome to walk back across four hundred miles of flats alone and explain to the guild why their salvage fee bought nothing. Or—" and here his voice dropped into something almost kind, "—you stay, and you watch, and when it's done you'll understand. Men like you always do, in the end. The engine gives back more than it takes. That's the whole secret of the old world, Hartwell. They didn't fall. They *ascended*, and we've been crawling in their dust ever since, too frightened to follow."

Casper looked at the sliver. He thought about Tomas's name, which he still owned, still said to himself some nights like a man counting coins. He thought about Ilex, who had traded her daughter's name for a route home and now recited bearings in her sleep. He thought about the sealed case someone had opened, and the buyer waiting on the far side of the sands, and the word *retrieval* sitting in his gut like a swallowed stone.

The mission was the corruption. It had been from the first signature. He'd signed anyway, because stopping had cost more than moving, and now the bill had come due with interest.

"No," he said.

Hale sighed, genuinely regretful, an actor giving the scene its proper beat. "I hoped you'd be smarter."

"So did I," said Sera Voss, from the dark behind him.

Casper spun. She stood at the shaft mouth with Ilex a step behind her, and she had clearly been there long enough to hear all of it — long enough, maybe, to hear Casper refuse, though her face gave nothing away. She walked into the firelight with her rifle slung loose and her eyes on Hale like a woman reading a ledger she'd suspected was crooked for years.

"Dree," she said quietly. "Osk. Step away from him."

Neither moved. Dree's hand went to her temple again. Osk stared at the middle distance, and when he spoke, his voice had the dreamy fluency of a man reciting something learned rather than chosen. "Six years of rainfall," he said. "Every storm. Do you know what that's worth to a water-broker? I could buy back my father's farm. I could—"

"You could remember your father's *face* instead," Sera snapped, "if you put that thing down and never touch it again."

"He's already touched it," Hale said mildly. "They both have. Last night, while you slept. There's no walking that back, Voss. Only forward." He turned to Casper, and the kindness drained out of his face, leaving something older and colder underneath. "Last chance. Stand aside."

"I told you. No."

What happened next happened fast, the way bad things do — faster than decision, faster than regret. Hale moved toward the shaft mouth, not toward anyone, and it was so obviously a feint that Casper read it even as his body answered it, stepping left to cut the angle, and that was the mistake, because Dree wasn't feinting. She came off his blind side with a climbing axe — a tool, nothing more, that was the horror of it, a tool for anchoring ropes on the descent — and swung it at the back of his knee.

Nine years of instinct saved his leg. Nothing saved the moment. He twisted, caught the haft, and drove his forehead into her nose, and she went down bleeding and screaming words he couldn't parse, and then the night tore open — Osk shouting, Ilex screaming Sera's name, Hale drawing a blade that had no business in a salvage crew's kit, a long curved thing that caught the firelight and held it.

Sera fired once, into the air, and the crack of it flattened everything.

"Enough!" Her voice carried the full authority of nine years of command, and for one heartbeat it actually worked — Osk froze, Dree sobbed into the sand, even Hale stilled with the curved blade low at his side. In that heartbeat Casper got his feet under him, blood running warm from his brow into his eye, and put himself between Hale and the shaft.

Below them, faint through forty fathoms of stone, the engine hummed. Casper realized he could feel it in his boot soles, patient as a tide.

"Hale," Sera said. "You're done. Take your two, take your sliver, and start walking. The wastes will do the rest."

"They won't," Hale said. "Because I'm not leaving without the core, and neither are they — ask them, if you think they can still answer for themselves. And because my employer doesn't send one man to do a job worth kingdoms." He smiled again, and this time it reached his eyes, and what lived in his eyes made Casper's skin crawl. "You should hear the rest of the crew coming down the shaft right now. Shouldn't you? Listen."

And Casper listened, and beneath the wind and the fire and Dree's crying, he heard it: boots on iron rungs, many of them, descending out of the dark above — the rest of the crew, who had gone topside at dusk to cache the surplus water, coming back down into the earth, into the light of a fire that was burning far too large, around strangers standing in attitudes of violence.

Sera's rifle came level. Casper's knife came free. And somewhere below them all, the engine hummed a little louder, the way a sleeping animal does when it smells blood.

* * *

The rungs rang under the returning crew, and Casper understood, with the cold clarity that arrives only after the first blood is spilled, that the bargain had already been struck — days ago, in the dark, while the rest of them slept — and that every choice left to him now was only about the price.
