## 7. Monsoon Season

The clouds came in from the west like a debt collector.

Casper saw them first from the rim of the plaza, a bruise-colored wall stacked against the horizon, and for a long moment he just stood there watching it build. Nine years in the waste and he'd never seen the monsoon arrive early. It was supposed to be six days out. The buyer's charts had said six days. Now the sky was eating itself and the air had gone thick and sweet, the way it did before the wastes drowned.

Behind him, someone shouted his name. He didn't turn.

* * *

It happened fast, the way bad things do — no warning worth the name, just a gap in the watch and then the sound of the core's cradle scraping stone.

Casper came awake with his knife already in his hand and knew before he reached the engine chamber that he was too late. The chamber was empty of everything but the machine itself, still humming its low hungry note, and Dree Vask standing over the open floor panel where the core had been, holding a lantern, wearing an expression that was almost apologetic.

"Where is it," Casper said.

"Down." Dree tipped his chin toward the shaft mouth. "Hollis and Merrin went with him. The agent. They took the descent rig and half the water."

"The agent has a name?"

"He never gave one I believed." Dree set the lantern down carefully, like it might bite him. "He said you'd understand. He said the job was always this. Said Sera knew."

Casper's blood went cold and then hot. "Where's Sera?"

"Chasing him." Dree's voice cracked on it. "Went down the shaft alone not two minutes after they ran. Casper — she's got nothing. No rope, no light worth the name. She just *went*."

Of course she did. Casper was already moving, grabbing the spare coil, the flare sticks, the water skin. Dree caught his arm at the shaft mouth.

"The storm—"

"Will come whether we're down there or up here." He looked at the man a moment longer. Dree had signed on in Halven's Rest knowing none of the history, and he'd held his nerve through everything since. "Get the rest topside. High ground at the plaza rim. If we're not back by full dark tomorrow, seal the shaft and go home."

"And if you're back?"

Casper dropped into the dark. "Then we all go home."

* * *

The dry riverbed ran northwest out of the buried city's bones, a scar of pale stone winding between ridges that had once been seawalls. The splinter faction left a trail a child could follow: boot prints in the silt, a discarded ration tin, the faint wrongness in the air where the core's hunger bled through the rock.

Casper caught Sera two miles down the channel, crouched behind a fallen pillar, breathing hard, her face streaked with dust and something darker.

"You went alone," he said.

"You were asleep."

"So you decided to die without me."

She looked up at him then, and there was fury in it, but underneath the fury something rawer. "I made a promise, Casper."

"To Tomas."

She didn't answer. She didn't have to. He sat down beside her behind the pillar and checked the flares and said nothing else, because some conversations needed darkness to happen in, and the darkness was coming whether they chased it or waited for it.

They moved at dusk. The riverbed gave them cover and took it away in turns; the splinter three were making good time, unburdened by conscience or caution, and twice Casper caught sight of their lantern glow bobbing ahead like a will-o'-the-wisp. The core was changing them already. He could see it in how they moved — too smooth, too certain, men who had traded something essential for the perfect memory of every step they'd ever taken, and who no longer quite remembered why the steps mattered.

The clouds ate the last of the light. The first thunder rolled down the channel like a door slamming somewhere enormous.

"We camp," Sera said.

"They'll keep moving."

"They'll stop." Her voice was flat with certainty. "The agent's careful. Careful men don't run blind in a flood channel with a storm coming. He'll make camp on the high bank and wait for dawn." She paused. "And we need to talk, Casper. Really talk. Before one of us dies without saying it."

* * *

They made their own camp in a side wash, a shallow overhang with a view of the channel, no fire. The rain arrived an hour after full dark — not the monsoon proper yet, but the outriders of it, fat warm drops that struck the stone like thrown gravel and multiplied until the whole world was hissing water.

Sera sat with her knees drawn up, watching the channel flood by degrees. Casper watched her instead.

"Tomas reached for it," she said.

The words came out of her like teeth pulled. Casper didn't move. Didn't breathe.

"The first expedition. Nine years ago. Everything we told the guild, everything written in the record — it's a lie. There was no collapse, no cave-in, no heroic rearguard action." Her voice stayed level the way a wire stays level under load. "We found the engine. We found it in the first hour, Casper. And the crew started touching it, and it started *giving*, and everyone forgot what they were paying. Everyone except Tomas."

"What did he pay?" Casper heard his own voice from very far away.

"Nothing. That's the thing." She turned to face him fully now, rain-light silvering the edge of her jaw. "He wouldn't touch it. He stood at the edge of the chamber and he told us all to walk away, and we wouldn't listen, because it showed each of us exactly what we wanted most. And when the feeding started — when people began forgetting things mid-sentence, forgetting *people* — he went in to pull them out. Physically. With his hands." Her breath shuddered once and steadied. "And the engine read him. Just from contact. Just from trying to save them. And it offered him—"

"*What.*"

"It offered him you." Her eyes were wet and she let them be. "It read his memories and it built your face out of them and it said *come here, brother, I'll give you back the years you lost*. And Tomas — God forgive him, Casper, he was so tired, he'd carried all of us for so long — Tomas reached for it."

The rain hammered the overhang. Somewhere down the channel, thunder detonated against the ridge line.

"He didn't die a hero," Sera said. "He died reaching. And I have spent nine years telling everyone including myself that it was otherwise, because the truth would have broken you, and because I promised him — his last coherent words, Casper, before the engine finished taking him — he said *don't tell my brother what I almost did*. And I kept that promise until keeping it started killing other people."

Casper sat very still. Nine years of grief rearranged itself around the new shape of it, and the grief became something else, something with edges. Not at Tomas. Never at Tomas. At the machine that had taken a good man's exhaustion and worn his love as a lure. At himself, for building a shrine out of a lie.

"I spent nine years," he said finally, "not following you into the waste. Because following you meant Qeth-Amar, and Qeth-Amar killed him." A sound came out of him that was almost a laugh. "And he died reaching for the thing I'm about to destroy."

"Yes."

"And you let me hate the mission instead of the truth."

"I let you stay alive." Her voice cracked clean through. "That was the duty. That was always the duty, Casper. Not the guild, not the contract, not the buyer. Him. He asked me to keep you moving, and I have hated every mile of it, because keeping you moving meant being near you and never—" She stopped. Pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "Never anything else."

The rain came down harder. The channel below was running brown and fast now, and the far bank was dissolving into the dark.

"Sera." He said her name and it came out wrecked. "Say the rest."

"Against every rule I have," she whispered. "Against the promise, against the duty, against nine years of good reasons." She lowered her hand and looked at him, and there was nothing left in her face of the rival, the contractor, the woman who'd handed him a map across a table in Halven's Rest. Only the girl who'd stood beside his brother at the edge of a chamber and watched him reach. "I love you, Casper Hartwell. I have loved you since before the first expedition, and I chose the promise over you because I thought it was the same thing, and it wasn't, and Tomas is dead either way, and I am done choosing wrong."

He crossed the space between them. Under the overhang, with the world drowning, he kissed her — not gently, not carefully, with nine years of withheld everything behind it — and she kissed him back like a woman putting down a weight she'd carried alone across a desert.

When they broke apart, her forehead rested against his.

"The core," she murmured. "The agent. Tomorrow."

"Tomorrow," he agreed. "Tonight the world can drown without our help."

But even as he said it, Casper was looking past her shoulder, down the flooded channel, to where a light had appeared on the high bank opposite — the splinter camp, yes, but wrong somehow. Too many lights. Too much movement.

And then the wind shifted and brought him the sound: voices raised not in argument but in a strange, flat chorus, three throats speaking words in unison, and beneath them a hum he knew in his marrow.

The core wasn't waiting for dawn.

It was calling across the water — and something in the drowned city behind them was answering.
