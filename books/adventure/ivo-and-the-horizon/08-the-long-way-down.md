## 8. The Long Way Down

Sera counted the splinter faction's fires from the ridge above the dry riverbed and got four. Four fires, six people, one core.

The math was wrong in a way that made her teeth ache. Six had gone out from Qeth-Amar with the agent — Drenn, Osk, the twins Vey and Marn, the mule-driver Pell, and Corrin, who'd been Casper's pick, a steady man with steady hands. Six. But she'd watched them through her spyglass at dusk, and what moved around those fires didn't move like six people. It moved like fewer. Like something that had started consolidating.

"They're not posting watches," Casper said beside her. He'd been still so long she'd almost forgotten he was there, which was his particular gift. "Look. Nobody's watching the north approach."

"Nobody's watching anything."

"That's worse."

It was worse. She'd led crews through the waste for eleven years and she knew every way a camp could be wrong — too loud, too bright, too casual — but she'd never seen one this wrong: fires burning unattended, gear strewn like the owners had set it down mid-thought and never come back. One figure sat alone by the largest fire, rocking slightly, and even at this distance Sera could see he was talking to something in his hands. The core, probably. Or to nothing. With that thing, the distinction stopped mattering fast.

"We take them tonight," she said. "Before they reach the cisterns. Once they're underground—"

"You think they're going underground?"

"I think they don't know where they're going anymore." She passed him the glass. "Watch the tall one. Vey. She's been walking in circles since noon. They're following the core, not a route. And the core wants to go home."

Casper lowered the spyglass slowly. In the last light she could see the change in his face — not fear exactly. Recognition. He'd been carrying his own hollowed-out places for nine years; he knew the shape of an empty man when he saw one.

"The cisterns," he said. "Under Qeth-Amar. The flood system."

"The engine drank a city once. Something had to feed it." She stood, knees cracking, and hated how old the waste had made her. "We go down the scree on the east side, quiet, and we take them while they're still human enough to surrender."

"And if they're not?"

She didn't answer that, and he didn't make her.

* * *

They came down through broken terraces older than any map Sera had ever bought or stolen, stone steps worn to shallow curves by feet ten centuries dead. Ilex went first — the navigator, the one who'd touched the engine and traded her daughter's name for a perfect map of the way out. She moved through the dark like water finding its level, and Sera hated how useful she was, how calmly she pointed out each collapsed arch and unstable shelf as if she were reading it off a page inside her head. Which, Sera supposed, she was.

They caught the splinter faction at the mouth of the cisterns, in a chamber where moonlight fell through a shattered ventilation shaft and turned everything the color of drowned things.

Corrin was dead already. That was the first thing Sera understood, though it took her a moment to accept it. He lay against the wall with his eyes open and his hands folded, arranged almost neatly, and no one had killed him. There wasn't a mark on him. He'd simply been emptied — a man used up like a wick, set down when the light went out.

Pell stood over him, or near him, swaying. When Sera called his name he turned and looked straight through her.

"Pell. Mule-driver. You signed on in Halven's Rest. You have a sister in—" She couldn't remember the town. The name wouldn't come, and she realized with a cold slide of horror that it wasn't her memory failing. "You told me about her. At the well outside Halven's Rest. You said she kept bees."

"She did," Pell said. His voice was flat as the riverbed. "Did she?"

Osk came out of the dark with a knife, and Casper took him down without killing him — three moves, efficient, almost gentle — but Osk fought like a man who'd forgotten what fighting was for, mechanically, without anger. When Casper pinned him and asked where the core was, Osk smiled up at the ceiling and said, "It remembers me," in a voice full of wonder, and said nothing else, ever again, as far as Sera ever learned.

The twins were deeper in. Sera heard them before she saw them — two voices in the flooded galleries ahead, calling back and forth, except they weren't calling each other's names anymore. They were reciting. Dates, distances, water-table levels, the whole buried hydrology of Qeth-Amar pouring out of them in perfect, useless detail, the engine's knowledge wearing their mouths like masks.

And between them, on a stone ledge above black water, sat the core.

It was smaller than Sera expected. A lattice of some dark metal, fist-sized, veined with a faint amber light that pulsed slow as a sleeping heart. It didn't glow so much as drink — she could feel it pulling at her attention, at her, a soft constant tug like current under calm water. *Come closer. Remember with me. I'll hold it all. You'll never lose anything again.*

She thought of her debts. Of the buyer who knew too much. Of Tomas, nine years dead, whose promise she'd carried like a stone in her chest — *don't let them cut it free, Sera, whatever happens, don't let them* — and she understood, finally and completely, why he'd made her swear it. Not because the core was dangerous to carry.

Because it was kind. That was the horror of it. It gave you exactly what you were starving for, and it fed on the part of you that wanted.

"It has to go back in the chamber," Casper said behind her. "Sealed. Or destroyed. Those are the only—"

Vey came out of the gallery at a dead run.

Sera would replay it for years afterward: the tall woman, half-consumed, most of herself already poured into that amber light, charging past all of them toward the ledge — not to take the core, Sera realized. To give herself to it. To finish the trade. Vey hit the water's edge and reached, and the core flared, and Sera saw the woman's face open like a door, everything behind it going out room by room—

Ilex moved.

Later, Sera could never say whether the navigator had planned it or simply understood, in the way of people who have already paid the engine once, what the moment required. Ilex crossed the chamber faster than anyone should have been able to, took Vey by the collar, and threw her clear — and then instead of retreating, she stepped onto the ledge, over the core, and put her shoulder against the load-bearing pillar above it. The cracked one. The one Sera hadn't even noticed.

The pillar that held up the gallery roof. The pillar that, if it went, brought down fifty tons of ancient masonry between them and the deep cisterns — between the core and everyone in the world.

"No," Sera said, and it came out of her like a reflex, stupid and human. "Ilex—"

Ilex looked back at her. In the amber light her face was calm, and young suddenly, all the cost of the engine's bargain visible at once — the smooth places where a daughter's name had been, the perfect architecture of routes and distances built over the ruin of everything personal. She smiled. It was the worst thing Sera had ever seen, that smile: peaceful, grateful, a woman glad to be done paying.

"Tell my daughter—" Ilex started, and stopped, and laughed softly at herself. "I almost did it again. Asking someone else to carry what I sold." She set her feet. "Get them out. All of them. Even Osk. Especially Osk."

The pillar groaned.

"Go," Ilex said, and pushed.

The crack ran through the stone like lightning finding ground. Sera grabbed Vey's arm and ran, Casper hauling Osk's dead weight across his shoulders, and behind them the gallery came down in a long rolling thunder that filled the world — dust and darkness and the scream of ten centuries of stone giving up at once. The last thing Sera saw, turning in the collapse-light, was Ilex standing on the ledge with her arms spread, holding the pillar an extra second, two seconds, buying them the margin with her spine —

— and then the wall of rubble sealed the passage, and the amber glow was gone, and the silence afterward rang worse than the noise.

* * *

They regrouped in the antechamber where Corrin lay. Someone — Sera, though she didn't remember doing it — closed his eyes. Vey sat against the wall, breathing, alive, and wept without sound, and every few minutes asked her own name as if checking whether she still owned it.

Casper set Osk down gently and came to stand beside Sera. For a long time neither of them spoke. Above them, faint through the ventilation shaft, the stars were going out one by one behind a rising bank of cloud.

"Monsoon," Casper said finally.

"Two days. Maybe less."

He nodded slowly. Then he said, in a voice carefully stripped of everything, the voice of a man reading a supply manifest: "The core survived. Didn't it."

It wasn't a question. Through fifty tons of fallen rock, Sera could still feel it — faint now, distant, a pulse under the floor of the world. Patient. Undamaged. Waiting for someone to dig.

"Yes," she said.

Casper looked at the rubble wall for a long moment. Then he looked at her, and she saw him arrive at the same place she'd arrived years ago, standing over a grave in Halven's Rest, making a promise to a dying man: that some things you don't salvage, you don't sell, and you don't leave for the next fool to find.

"There's another way into the cisterns," he said. "Through the engine chamber."

"I know."

"I have to go back in."

"I know," Sera said again, and this time her voice broke on it, and she let it, because the monsoon was coming and there was no more time to spend pretending they weren't already lost.
