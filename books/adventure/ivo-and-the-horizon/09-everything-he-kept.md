## 9. Everything He Kept

The sky over Qeth-Amar had gone the color of a bruise by the time Casper made his decision.

He stood at the mouth of the descent shaft with Sera's hand still warm on his arm from the argument they'd just finished — the one where she'd told him no, told him she'd go down instead, told him every reason that was a lie dressed as an offer. The wind was coming up out of the south now, wet and wrong, carrying the smell of a rain that hadn't fallen in this country for a year and was about to fall all at once.

"Hours," Sera said. "Maybe less."

"Then I'd better not waste them arguing with you."

She didn't laugh. She never laughed when he needed her to, only after, like payment rendered late. She pressed the last of the water skins into his hands and said, "Tomas would have—"

"Don't." He said it gently, which was worse. "Don't finish that sentence. Not today."

* * *

The shaft swallowed him in stages. First the heat of the surface, then the cool of the stone, then something older than either — a warmth rising from below like breath, like the city itself exhaling after four hundred years of holding it. His lamp threw his shadow long against the walls, and the walls were carved with figures whose faces had been worn smooth by hands touching them in passing, generation after generation of people who had come down here needing something.

He knew how they felt.

The chamber opened around him the way a cathedral opens — sudden, vertical, meant to make you small. And there at its heart stood the engine, exactly as Ilex had described it before she forgot her daughter's name: a column of dark metal veined with light, humming at the edge of hearing, patient as a well. The core the splinter faction had cut free was gone, dragged off into the flooded dark with three people who no longer remembered why they were running. But the engine remained. The engine always remained. It was the kind of thing that remained.

Casper set his pack down at the base of the first step and took inventory, because that was what rangers did, what Hartwells did, what Tomas had drilled into him on their first expedition when Casper was nineteen and certain he'd live forever. Water: half a skin. Lamp oil: enough. Rope: enough. Reasons to go back up: one, standing at the top of the shaft with the monsoon coming, refusing to leave without him.

He thought about that reason for a long moment. Then he began to climb.

* * *

The engine read him before he touched it.

He felt it the way you feel someone reading over your shoulder — a pressure behind the eyes, a sorting. The light in its veins shifted color, amber to deep red, and the hum climbed into a register he felt in his teeth. When he finally laid his palm flat against the metal, it was warm the way a sleeping animal is warm, and it did not show him power. Not first. First it showed him the flats outside Halven's Rest, nine years ago, the day he'd signed the contract that brought them all here the first time.

It showed him Tomas laughing at the guild hall door, chalking the ninth name onto the wood while Casper held the ladder and complained about the angle.

"That's crooked," Casper said, in the memory, in the chamber, aloud, to no one.

The engine drank the words.

He understood then, the way you understand things in dreams — completely and too late — what the machine wanted. Not touch. Touch was just the door. It wanted everything. Every memory was fuel to it, and it burned clean, and it burned hot, and the old world had built it to run on the one resource every civilization produces in abundance: what people can't bear to keep.

Ilex had fed it a daughter's name and gotten perfect recall of the route home. A fair trade, if you didn't look too closely at what *fair* meant to a thing like this. The splinter faction had fed it whole years and gotten nothing but hunger. The engine didn't cheat. It just never lost.

"You killed my brother," Casper said.

The light pulsed. Somewhere in the deep of the machine, something turned over, considering the accusation, finding it imprecise. *He reached for me,* the pulse seemed to say. *They always reach.*

And that was the truth Sera had carried alone since the night camp, the truth she'd finally given him two days ago with both hands, watching his face while she said it: Tomas hadn't died heroically holding a line or hauling a wounded man up the shaft. He had died reaching for the engine, greedy for what it offered, and the reaching had killed him, and Casper had spent nine years building a brother out of the parts that were left because the whole brother was unbearable.

The engine knew that. Of course it knew that. It was showing him now — Tomas at the edge of the chamber, lit gold, turning back with that grin, saying something Casper could almost hear, something about—

No.

"No," Casper said again, and pressed his hand harder against the metal, and this time he pushed instead of received.

* * *

There was no manual for destroying a thing like this. There was only what the old carvings said, the ones near the base that the salvage crew's scholars had argued over for two nights: *it feeds, therefore it burns; starve it and it sleeps; fill it and it dies.* Fill it. Give it everything until there was nothing left to give, until the furnace choked on the weight of a single man's entire life.

So Casper gave it.

He gave it his mother's kitchen and the smell of bread and the specific way she said his name when she was angry versus disappointed. He gave it his first expedition, the good parts, the bad parts, the taste of recycled water at altitude. He gave it every woman he'd ever failed to love properly, every friend buried under cairns across three hundred miles of waste, every night he'd lain awake listening for sounds that weren't there.

The engine glowed brighter with each offering. The chamber grew hot. Sweat ran into his eyes and he didn't wipe it because wiping meant lifting his hand.

And then it came for Tomas.

Not the polished grief-version Casper had been carrying for nine years, the martyr, the name on the door. The real one. The engine reached past all of it, down to the bedrock, and started pulling up the load-bearing memories one by one: Tomas's voice, hoarse from singing badly on watch. Tomas's face at fourteen, gap-toothed, daring Casper to race him to the river. Tomas teaching him how to read a storm front, how to save a stranger in a sandstorm, how to be a man who kept moving because stopping cost more.

The reason Casper kept moving.

That was the last of it, and the engine took it last, savoring, and Casper felt it go the way you feel a tooth leave the jaw — a horrible loosening, a socket suddenly empty, a tongue returning again and again to the absence. He tried to hold on. He braced himself against the column with both arms now, wrapped around it like a man clinging to a mast, and he said the name. He said it once, twice, a third time, gripping it the way you grip a rope in floodwater.

*Tomas. Tomas. To—*

The word came apart in his mouth. He could feel the shape of it, the weight of it, the way you can feel the shape of a room you grew up in after the house has burned. He knew there had been a person. He knew the person had mattered more than anything. He knew he was standing in a hot room destroying himself to honor someone he could no longer —

Someone. Someone he could no longer picture.

The engine screamed. There was no other word for it — the hum climbing past hearing into a frequency that cracked the carvings on the walls, the light going white, then blue-white, then a color Casper had no name for because he was losing names, all of them, the machine gorging and failing to digest, choking on the sheer undivided mass of one stubborn ranger's refusal to stop feeding it. Veins of light burst along the column like frost spreading across glass. Heat rolled off it in waves that blistered the paint on his lamp, and still he held on, because letting go now meant surviving this hollow, and he would rather burn.

*Fill it and it dies.*

The core went first — a soundless implosion, a place in the world briefly becoming no place at all. Then the column folded inward, slow as a closing hand, and the light drained downward into the floor and was gone, and the silence that followed was so complete Casper could hear his own blood.

He let go. He fell. The stone caught him.

* * *

He woke to thunder.

For a disorienting moment he thought it was the engine, still screaming somewhere below, and he scrambled upright in the dark with his dead lamp and his empty hands. Then he understood: the storm. The monsoon, arriving early, arriving enormous, the sky above the shaft opening like a wound.

He climbed. His arms worked. His legs worked. Names surfaced as he climbed — Sera, Ilex (dead, the cisterns, the collapse), Halven's Rest, the flats, the guild hall — ordinary words, load-bearing words, all intact. Only one socket stood empty, and he kept returning to it with his tongue, probing the absence, unable to stop.

At the top of the shaft, rain was falling in sheets, and Sera was there, soaked through, having refused — he learned later — every order to take shelter. She caught him as he came over the lip, and he was crying without knowing why, great silent wrenching sobs that had nothing left attached to them, and she held his face in her hands and asked him nothing.

"What did it take?" she whispered.

He opened his mouth to say his brother's name.

Nothing came.

"I don't—" He stopped. Started again. "There was someone. I know there was someone. I can't—"

Sera pulled him against her, into the rain, and said the name for him, once, quietly, like setting a stone on a grave. And the name sounded right, and it sounded foreign, and Casper held on to her and wept for a man who was now nothing but a word in another person's mouth.

Above them the storm broke fully open, and below them the drowned city drank the rain in silence, its engine cold forever, and the survivors gathered at the plaza's edge with nothing worth selling and no story anyone would believe — ready, at last, to walk home.
