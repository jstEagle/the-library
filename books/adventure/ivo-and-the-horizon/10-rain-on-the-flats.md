## 10. Rain on the Flats

The rain came an hour before dawn, and Casper felt it before he heard it — a change in the air's weight, a coolness on his skin like a hand laid gently on a fevered brow. He stopped walking. Behind him, the survivors kept moving, boots dragging through the sand, until they noticed he'd halted and turned to look at him, and then at the sky.

The clouds had been building for two days, a bruised wall along the southern horizon that none of them had spoken about because speaking it would make it real. Now the wall was overhead, black and low and full, and the first drop struck Casper's cheek with the weight of something thrown rather than fallen.

Then the sky opened.

It came down in sheets, warm rain, the first in over a year across the whole of the waste. It hammered the sand flat, raised steam from the stones, filled the dry washes with a sound like applause. The survivors stood in it without sheltering, faces tipped up, mouths open, letting it wash the dust and salt and dried blood from their skin. Dree wept openly and didn't try to hide it. The mule driver Kell sank to his knees in the flooding sand and stayed there, hands pressed flat to the ground as if checking whether the world was still solid.

Casper watched them and felt nothing he could name. There was a space where something should have been — he'd known that since he walked out of the chamber, since Sera had looked at him and he'd looked back and neither of them had said anything, because there was nothing left in him to say it with. He knew he had lost something enormous. He could feel the shape of the absence the way you feel a missing tooth: the tongue keeps going to the gap, finding nothing, going back again.

He just couldn't remember what had been there.

They walked out of the waste under the rain. Fourteen had gone in. Seven came out, and one of those was Ilex, who came out only as a name spoken over a cairn of stones at the mouth of the flooded cisterns, and another was the agent whose body they'd left where the engine's hunger had finished with him, unburied, unmourned, worth nothing now to anyone.

They carried nothing worth selling. The salvage cases went into the flood channels on the second day, Sera giving the order without ceremony, the crew watching the water take them. Whatever the buyer had wanted, whatever contract had started this, it ended in the mud. Sera tore the map in half somewhere past the salt flats and let the wind have it, and no one asked her why, because everyone already knew.

On the fourth day out, Kell asked him.

"Hartwell. What happened down there? In the chamber?"

Casper thought about it. He turned the question over the way he'd been turning it over every night since, feeling its edges, finding no purchase.

"I don't know," he said, and it wasn't a lie, and it wasn't the truth, and Kell looked at him a long moment and then nodded, because men who work the waste learn when a question is a door better left shut.

* * *

Halven's Rest smelled of wet wood and woodsmoke when they came down off the flats, the town's gutters running loud for the first time in living memory, children standing in the streets with their palms up. The guild hall door was dark with rain, and Casper stopped in front of it the way he always did, the way he'd done nine years ago and never again after.

Nine names. He'd read them a hundred times in his head and never once aloud. The first expedition's dead, cut deep into the oak by the guild's carver, each letter clean and final. *Tomas Hartwell.* Third from the top. He read it now and felt what he always felt, which was grief — but grief with a hole in the middle of it, grief like a ring, like a stone arch with nothing standing inside it anymore. He grieved, and he could not have said for whom. He knew the name belonged to someone he'd loved. He knew it the way you know a room was once furnished, from the pale rectangles on the walls where pictures hung.

Sera stood beside him. Rain ran off her hood in threads.

"You don't have to do this," she said.

"Yes, I do."

She didn't ask what he meant. That was the thing about Sera Voss, the thing he'd hated for nine years and loved for longer and would love now until the ground took him: she never asked for what she wasn't prepared to hear. She'd taken his hand on the third day out, crossing the flats, without looking at him, without asking what he'd lost, and she hadn't let go until the town walls rose up out of the rain. She understood the arithmetic. Some debts you pay in silence or not at all.

The carver's shop was closed. Casper used his own knife.

The oak was old and hard and the rain softened it not at all, and he worked by feel more than sight, kneeling in the gutter water, cutting letter by letter while the survivors waited behind him and the town's few early risers watched from doorways and said nothing. When it was done he sat back on his heels and read all ten names, top to bottom, and the tenth was his own.

*Casper Hartwell.*

Not because he was dead. Because the man who had walked into Qeth-Amar was. Whoever came out of that chamber had burned everything he was to feed the fire that killed the engine, and what remained was a man with a brother-shaped hole and a woman's hand remembered against his palm and nothing else worth carrying. Duty had built him, expedition by expedition, grave by grave, and duty had nothing left to take. So he gave the name to the door and left it there, among the honored dead, and stood up as someone new who owed the old one nothing.

Dree came forward and touched the letters, all ten, the way you'd touch a coffin lid. Then she turned and walked into the guild hall and didn't come back out, and Casper understood that some of the seven were going to stop here, and that was right. You didn't walk out of Qeth-Amar and keep walking forever. Some doors you went through and stayed behind.

Kell shook his hand. Two others did the same. Then it was Sera and him alone in the rain, and the street empty, and the day beginning around them with the ordinary sounds of a town that had never heard of Qeth-Amar and never would.

"So," she said.

"So."

She looked at the door, at his name under his brother's, and her jaw worked once, twice, and then she said, quietly, "I promised him I'd bring you home. Nine years ago. Before either of us knew what home meant." She wiped rain from her face with the heel of her hand. "I'm late, but I'm here."

"I know," Casper said. And he found he did know — not the promise itself, not the moment she'd made it, but the truth of it, sitting in him like a stone worn smooth by water. "I don't remember him," he said. "Tomas. I know I should. I know I did. But it's gone, and the strange thing is—" He stopped. Started again. "The strange thing is it doesn't hurt the way I thought it would. It hurts like weather. It passes through."

Sera took his hand again, there in the street, in front of the door with the ten names, and this time she looked at him while she did it.

"Then we start from zero," she said. "No history. No rivalry. No debt." A ghost of the old crooked smile, the one that had infuriated him across three expeditions and half a desert. "Just two people standing in the rain in Halven's Rest with nowhere in particular to be."

"First time in my life that's ever been true."

"Feels good?"

Casper considered it honestly. The restlessness that had driven him for nine years — the tracking, the moving, the refusal to stop anywhere longer than a job required — was quiet. Not gone. Quiet. Like a river gone underground. Somewhere ahead of him were days that didn't require him to outrun anything, mornings with no horizon to chase, and the prospect terrified him less than stillness used to, which was its own kind of miracle.

"It feels," he said, "like rain."

She laughed — short, surprised, real — and pulled him by the hand away from the door, down the street, toward the tavern with the light in its windows and the smell of bread coming out of its chimney. Behind them the ten names darkened in the wet, waiting for the sun to dry them, and Casper didn't look back, because for the first time in nine years there was nothing behind him he needed to check on.

At the tavern door she paused. "One condition," she said. "Whatever we are now — whatever this is — no contracts. No maps. No buyers."

"No maps," he agreed.

And then, because the habit of nine years dies hard, he glanced once at the eastern flats, where the rain was easing and the light was breaking gold through the last of the cloud — and found, to his surprise, that the horizon looked exactly like what it was: the edge of the land, and nothing more.

He turned away from it and went inside.
