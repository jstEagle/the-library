## 1. The Last Inspection

The rain had been falling on the Kestrel River trestle for eleven days, and Casper Amberdale had been counting every one of them.

He stood now at the base of the third pier, collar turned up against the drizzle, watching water thread down through a crack in the timber that should not have been there. The crack was the width of his thumb at the top and widened as it descended, fanning out into the wood like frost on a windowpane — the particular bloom of rot that no amount of paint or tar could disguise from a man who knew what he was looking at. And Casper knew. He had been looking at it for six days, in good weather and foul, with a mallet and an auger and a lamp on a wire, and every hour of looking had told him the same thing: the pier was hollow. Not all the way through, not yet. But hollow where it mattered, in the heartwood that carried the load when a hundred-ton locomotive came across at speed, and the heartwood did not grow back.

Above him, the morning freight whistled at the crossing two miles east. He heard the low thunder of it building, felt it arrive in the soles of his boots before it arrived in his ears, and watched the trestle take the weight. The whole structure shivered. A fine gray dust sifted from the crack above him and drifted onto the black water below, where it vanished without a ripple.

Casper wrote nothing down. He didn't need to. The number was already in him, the way numbers always were — 4,200 tons of rolling stock per day over a pier that would fail within the season, probably under winter ice load, possibly sooner if the river rose again. He had measured it three ways because his father had taught him to measure everything three ways, and all three ways agreed, and there was no version of arithmetic anywhere in the world that made this trestle safe.

He climbed back up to the deck, unhurried, letting the ladder's cold iron bite through his gloves. At the top, waiting under the oilcloth awning they'd rigged over the inspection table, stood Mr. Aldous Vane.

Vane was not a large man, but he occupied space the way a filing cabinet occupies a room — completely, and with the implication that everything in it belonged to him. He wore a good coat that the rain hadn't dared soak through, and he held Casper's inspection ledger open in both hands, squared at the corners, as though it were a document of state rather than a working engineer's field book with mud on its cover.

"Six days," Vane said. "You've spent six days on a bridge that takes a competent man two."

"A competent man who wants to sign it," Casper said. He set his mallet down on the table and began, methodically, to dry his hands on a rag that had long since stopped being dry itself.

"And you don't want to sign it." It wasn't quite a question. Vane's voice had the smoothness of something machined rather than spoken.

"I don't want to sign it *falsely*."

The word sat between them. Vane let it sit. That was the thing about Vane that Casper would remember later, in the bad nights, turning the morning over and over like a stone with a flaw in it: Vane never flinched, never blustered, never did any of the honest ugly things an honest ugly man would have done. He simply waited, patient as erosion, for the world to arrange itself around his convenience.

"The division engineer's report," Vane said at last, "calls the Kestrel trestle sound. Timber treated '86, serviceable life fifteen years minimum. The roadbed is scheduled for heavy traffic through the winter season. The company has commitments." He turned a page of the ledger with one finger, delicately, as if afraid of catching something from it. "Your notes here say *pier three, internal decay, probable failure under ice load*. Do you understand what happens when a report like this reaches the provincial office? There's an inquiry. There are delays. There are men standing idle in Greywater and Harrow Junction drawing wages for nothing, and there is a very specific person whose name appears at the bottom of the report that caused it all."

"I know whose name appears at the bottom. I put it there."

"You could put a different name there." Vane closed the ledger gently. "That's rather the point of the exercise, Mr. Amberdale. The form requires a signature. It does not require your signature. There are assistant engineers on this division who would be pleased to find the trestle sound. Some men have a talent for finding things sound."

The rain ticked on the oilcloth. Downriver, somewhere out of sight, a kingfisher made its rattling cry, indifferent to the affairs of men.

"My father inspected this line," Casper said. "For nineteen years. He taught me that the signature is the only part of the job that can't be corrected later. Everything else — the measurements, the calculations, even the bridge itself — you can go back and fix. The signature goes forward in time. You're writing a letter to a day you'll never see, telling someone whether they live or die crossing this span."

Vane smiled. It was a small smile, economical, the smile of a man conserving his expressions for better investments. "Your father," he said, "is dead."

The words landed precisely where he intended them to. Casper felt them go in, cold and clean, and knew — with the strange clarity that sometimes arrives in the worst moments, the clarity that feels almost like calm — that this was the moment. Not the signature. This. The pause afterward, the half-second in which a man decides what he is.

"Yes," Casper said. "He is. Which means the only thing left of him that still works is what he taught me. I'd like to keep it working."

He took the ledger out of Vane's hands — Vane let it go, which was somehow worse than if he'd held on — and opened it to the certification page. In the space marked *Condition*, he wrote: **UNSAFE. PIER THREE INTERNALLY DECAYED. RESTRICT TO TEN MILES PER HOUR OR CLOSE ENTIRELY PENDING RECONSTRUCTION.** Then he signed beneath it, C. R. AMBERDALE, ASSISTANT ENGINEER, in letters as steady as he could make them, which was steady enough.

"There," he said. "Now it's true in writing."

Vane looked at the page for a long moment. When he raised his eyes, there was no anger in them at all, and that frightened Casper more than anger would have.

"Young man," Vane said mildly, "do you know how many bridges there are between here and the coast?"

* * *

They dismissed him before noon, which was efficient even by railway standards.

It was done in the division office at Harrow Junction, a brick building that smelled of coal smoke and ink, in the office of Superintendent Grieves, a heavy man with a mustache the color of wet ash who would not look directly at Casper while he spoke. The language was careful, the way such language always is. *Reduction in force.* *Consolidation of engineering staff.* *Nothing personal, Amberdale, you must see how it looks.* A clerk appeared with an envelope containing four weeks' pay and a letter of reference so bland it could have been written about anyone, or no one. Casper read it standing up. *Conscientious. Thorough. Perhaps overly cautious in his assessments.*

*Perhaps overly cautious.* Somewhere, a train was going to cross the Kestrel trestle at forty miles an hour because a report had been rewritten by a man with a talent for finding things sound, and *perhaps overly cautious* was what the record would say about the one person who'd tried to stop it.

"Mr. Grieves." Casper folded the letter once and put it in his inside pocket, where it lay against his chest like a splinter. "For my own understanding. Who countersigned the revised condition report?"

Grieves's mustache moved. Underneath it, his mouth might have tightened; it was hard to tell. "That information isn't generally circulated."

"No. I imagine it isn't." Casper picked up his hat from the chair. "Then I'll ask differently. If that pier fails under a passenger train this winter, will the information circulate then?"

The silence that followed had texture. It had weight and grain and temperature. Two clerks in the outer office had gone very quiet, pretending to work, listening to every word.

"Take care of yourself, Amberdale," Grieves said finally, which was not an answer, and both of them knew it was not an answer, and both of them let it stand as one anyway, because some answers cost too much to give and some questions cost too much to press.

Outside, the rain had stopped. The streets of Harrow Junction steamed gently in the thin light, and everywhere Casper looked there were signs of the company's confidence: new rails stacked in gleaming rows, a fresh coat of paint on the freight shed, a poster pasted to the telegraph office wall announcing HARROW LINE — EXPANSION AND PROSPERITY, with an engraving of a locomotive charging toward a sunrise. Men nodded to him as he passed. Most of them had heard already; news traveled faster than trains in a junction town. Their nods had a particular quality, apologetic and relieved at once, the nods of men glad it wasn't them and sorry enough to mention it.

By evening, the story had acquired its finished shape, the shape stories acquire when they're useful to someone. He heard it in the taproom of the Station Hotel, sitting alone with a glass of beer he didn't want, eavesdropping on a conversation two tables over without meaning to, the way you can't help hearing your own name.

"—the young Amberdale, yes, the inspector's son—"

"—condemned a perfectly good bridge, from what I hear. Six days poking at it and then writes up a death sentence for the thing—"

"—well, blood will tell. His father was the same, wasn't he? Always finding trouble. Remember what happened out on the old Rivergate survey, back in—"

"I remember nobody ever came back, is what I remember."

A pause. The clink of glasses. Then, lower, with the relish people reserve for other families' disasters: "Runs in the family, maybe. Going off into the mountains after ghosts."

Casper finished his beer and paid and walked out into the dark without hurrying, because hurrying would have meant they'd touched him, and he found, walking the long way around the freight yard with his collar up, that they hadn't. Something had happened instead that he couldn't yet name — something like a door closing behind him and another one, unlit, standing open ahead. The humiliation was real. He catalogued it honestly, the way he'd been trained to catalogue everything: the heat in his face, the itch between his shoulder blades where their eyes had been, the sour taste of a reputation dissolving in a single afternoon after nine years of careful work. All of it real. And underneath it, quieter but far more durable, something else was setting like concrete.

They could take the job. They could take the reference, the wages, the name on the door of the division office. What they could not take was the fact that pier three was rotten, and he knew it, and he had written it down, and the writing was true.

Truth, he thought, was the only asset that couldn't be countersigned away.

* * *

His rented room was on Cordwainer Street, third floor, north-facing, twelve shillings a week, and it contained a bed, a washstand, a chair, a table, a lamp, and a tin trunk with a broken latch that he kept closed with a leather strap. He lit the lamp, hung his wet coat over the chair back, and knelt on the cold floorboards to open the trunk.

Everything he owned that mattered was in it: two spare shirts, his drafting instruments wrapped in flannel, a photograph of a woman who had died believing her husband would come home, and — at the bottom, wrapped in oilcloth tied with string gone brittle with age — his father's maps.

There were many of them. Nineteen years of inspections breed paper the way rivers breed silt. But Casper wasn't after the routine ones, the division surveys and bridge schedules with their neat printed borders. He lifted those aside and took out the one that had no border at all, drawn on a sheet of linen-backed paper soft as cloth from handling, covered edge to edge in his father's hand.

Elias Amberdale had been, by all accounts the son ever heard, the best line engineer on the coast division. He had also been, by the same accounts, a difficult man — a man who asked questions in meetings, who wrote memos, who would not let a discrepancy rest. Casper had been fourteen when his father went out on the last survey, twenty-two years ago, up the coast into the range beyond Greywater, along the corridor the companies called the Rivergate lines. Three crews had worked that country over a decade, driving grades through the gorge country, and then the work stopped — officially because of cost, unofficially because of the collapses, and actually, in the way these things were actually, because of reasons no one committed to paper. Elias Amberdale had gone out to make the final assessment of the abandoned corridor. Eleven men went with him. None of them returned. The company searched for nine days, called it a loss to weather and terrain, sealed the file, and paid the widow a settlement with a clause she signed because she had a son to feed and no strength left to fight.

Casper spread the map on the table and weighted its corners with the ink bottles and the brass paperweight shaped like a sleeping cat that had been his mother's.

The map showed the whole corridor. He knew it as well as he knew his own hands — the coastal approach at Greywater, the climb through the foothills, the long traverse where the old grade clung to the shoulders of the range, the descents toward the interior valleys. His father's annotations crawled over every mile: gradients, curve radii, notes on rock quality, water sources, snow sheds needed, culverts washed out. The handwriting was small and exact, an engineer's hand, each letter built rather than written.

And then, in the middle of the sheet, where the map crossed the high gorge country — where the Rivergate itself should have been, the narrow defile every surveyor mentioned and none described — there was a gap.

Not blank paper. Blank paper would have meant nothing. This was different. The contour lines ran confidently up to the edges of the gap and stopped, cut off clean, as if trimmed. The old rail alignments approached it from the coast side and simply ended, mid-line, the little cross-tick symbols of the track terminating in empty space. On the interior side, the same: rails running toward the gorge and stopping short, like sentences interrupted.

Someone had removed a piece of this map. Cut it out with a blade, carefully, taking the gorge and everything in it, and leaving the wound behind.

Casper bent close, tilting the paper under the lamp. Along the margin of the cut, almost invisible unless the light raked across it, his father had written something in pencil so faint it might have been erased and re-ghosted a dozen times. Casper read it twice before he trusted himself to have it right:

*They will not let me chart what is in the middle. Chart it anyway. Chart it in your head if nowhere else.*

Below that, smaller still, nearly worn away by twenty-two years of folding and unfolding:

*If anything happens, the boy should look where the tide runs lowest.*

Casper straightened up slowly. The lamp hissed. Out on Cordwainer Street a cab horse clopped past, and somewhere a dog was barking at nothing, and the ordinary sounds of the city went on around him while he stood over the wounded map with his father's voice in his ears, faint as graphite, saying words the man had probably never expected anyone to read.

*The boy should look where the tide runs lowest.*

He thought: I have no job. I have a letter of reference that says *perhaps overly cautious*. I have four weeks' pay and a room I can't keep past the month. Every door on the coast division is shut, and the men who shut them are drinking downstairs and laughing about my family.

And then he thought: there is exactly one contract in the province that nobody else wants.

He had seen the notice pinned in the railway office that morning, among the postings he'd read every day for nine years without seeing. It was a small notice, an orphan of paperwork, renewed automatically each season by clerks who had never given it a thought. *Provincial Concession 44-C: survey and condition assessment, closed Rivergate corridor, Greywater approach to Interior Divide. Permits expire at first snowfall. No renewal. Applicants should note the corridor's history and govern themselves accordingly.*

Nobody wanted it because the corridor had eaten three crews and his father, and because the permits died with the first snow, and because whatever was in the middle of that country had been cut out of the map.

Casper stood for a long time in the lamplight, one hand flat on the linen paper, feeling through his palm the ghost of the missing piece. Then he rolled the map carefully, tied it with the old string, and slid it inside his coat, against his chest, where the useless letter of reference had lain all day. One document out. One document in. It seemed to him, in a way he could not have defended before a board of inquiry, like a fair exchange.

He blew out the lamp and began, in the dark, to pack.
