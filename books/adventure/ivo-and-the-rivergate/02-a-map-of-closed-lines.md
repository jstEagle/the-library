## 2. A Map of Closed Lines

The room above the Ferryman's Rest cost four shillings a week and smelled of tallow, damp plaster, and the ghost of a hundred men who had slept badly in it before him. Casper Amberdale paid for three weeks in advance because he had learned that morning that his money now had edges — that every coin spent was a coin that would not be spent again, and that the arithmetic of a dismissed man was a colder discipline than any he had ever kept on a bridge crew.

He set his father's map on the table beneath the window's gray light and did not touch it for a long while.

It had come to him, as these things do, sideways. Not willed to him with ceremony, not pressed into his hands with last words, but found — folded into the back of an inspection manual he had carried unopened through two postings and one marriage, a book heavy enough to be useful as a doorstop and dull enough never to invite reading. He had discovered the map eleven years ago, the winter after the funeral, when grief had made him go through his father's things like a man searching a wreck for survivors. He had looked at it once, understood almost nothing, folded it away again, and told himself he would study it properly when he had time.

There had been time. There had never been the willingness.

Now the willingness had been forced upon him by a man named Hobb with a soft voice and a signature line, and Casper sat down across from the map the way one sits down across from a stranger who might turn out to be family.

* * *

The paper itself told the first story. It was surveyor's linen-backed stock, the kind that outlasted its owners, but someone — his father, surely — had reinforced the folds with strips of brown paper glued along the creases, the way a man reinforces a thing he intends to open often. The glue had yellowed. The strips were worn soft at their centers from handling. His father had opened this map hundreds of times, and closed it hundreds of times, and each folding had been done carefully enough that the corners still met.

Casper unfolded it fully for the first time.

It covered the whole of the northern corridor: the coast range rendered in fine ink hachures like the stubble on a sleeping giant's jaw, the rivers in blue so faded it had gone the color of weak tea, and over all of it, in red pencil, the lines. Rail lines. Some solid — built, ballasted, running. Some dashed — chartered but unfinished. And some drawn in a hand that grew lighter as it went, tentative as breath on cold glass, trailing off into the blank margins where the cartographer had simply stopped.

His father's hand. Casper knew it the way he knew his own reflection — not because he had studied it, but because it had been around him all his life, on lunch pails and ledger covers and the flyleaf of the Bible nobody read. *E. AMBERDALE, ASSISTANT ENGINEER,* written in that upright, slightly compressed script, the letters leaning forward like men walking into wind. The same lean was in every annotation on this map. His father had leaned into this work. Whatever it had been.

Casper poured himself the dregs of the inn's coffee, cold now, and began at the beginning, which was the coast.

* * *

The southern third of the map was almost conventional. Here were the lines Casper had run himself or walked beside: the Greywater spur, the coastal trunk, the branch that climbed toward the Kettle Falls quarry. His father had annotated them not with engineering notes but with small, human marginalia that made Casper's throat tighten in a way he resented.

Beside the Greywater junction, in tiny letters: *Marta's stew worth the delay.* So Marta had fed his father too. The innkeeper had been young then; Casper tried to imagine her with dark hair, laughing at some dry remark E. Amberdale had made over a bowl of stew, and could not quite manage it, and the failure hurt more than the image would have.

Beside a trestle crossing at mile forty-one: *Rebuilt 3rd time. Timber spec is a lie. Told them. They know.*

Casper read that twice. *Told them. They know.* Four words containing an entire career — the same career his own had just become a copy of, the same refusal inherited like eye color. He had thought, signing nothing at that rotten trestle yesterday, that he was making a choice. Now he saw he was keeping an appointment made thirty years before he was born.

He drank the cold coffee anyway. It tasted like the room felt.

* * *

By midmorning the light had strengthened enough to show him the middle country, and here the map changed character.

The annotations thickened. The neat surveyor's shorthand gave way to longer passages crammed into whatever white space the linen offered — up the sides of mountains, along river bends, even written vertically in the margin like a man talking faster and faster. His father had spent years, it seemed, on a question the official maps did not ask: whether the range could be crossed.

Everyone knew it couldn't, not honestly. The corridor between the coast and the interior plateau was forty miles of collapsed tunnels, washed-out cuts, and the famous ruin of the Rivergate viaduct, whose broken spans still stood in the gorge like the ribs of something that had died mid-stride. Every company that had held the concession had surveyed the corridor, pronounced it impassable at acceptable cost, and quietly let the charter lapse. The lines on every official map simply stopped at the western wall of the gorge and started again on the eastern side, forty miles later, as if the mountain were a word too long to spell.

But his father's pencil had gone straight through the word.

Casper traced it with one finger, not quite touching the paper. The red line left the western railhead, climbed the valley of the Greywater's north fork — and then, instead of stopping at the cliffs, it turned *inward*. Into the rock. The line ran through the massif itself in a series of ruled segments, each segment annotated with distances in chains, grades in percentages, and small symbols Casper recognized only dimly from his apprenticeship: ventilation marks, drainage marks, and one symbol repeated at intervals that he had to dig through three reference books to identify.

A gauge notch. The mark surveyors cut into tunnel walls to verify clearance for a given rail gauge.

His father had marked gauge notches inside a mountain that no company had ever tunneled.

Casper set down the reference book and sat very still. Outside, a gull argued with another gull about something neither would win. Downstairs, Marta was arguing with a fishmonger about something she would win. The ordinary world went on being ordinary, and on the table in front of him, in red pencil, his dead father claimed there were passages through the range that had never appeared on any chart, in any filing office, in any engineer's dream.

Unless they had appeared in one place. Unless the cave the locals wouldn't name —

No. He was getting ahead of himself, and he knew the habit. An engineer who got ahead of his data ended up explaining to widows why the bridge sang before it fell. He made himself go back to the beginning of the middle section and read every word.

* * *

It took him until the light began to fail, and what he assembled, piece by piece, was less a route than a diary disguised as one.

The earliest annotations were dated — his father had dated everything, a habit Casper had mocked as a boy — and the dates ran backward through the years of the great push, when three companies at once had believed the corridor could be won. In those entries the tone was confident, almost joyful. *Gauge checks true. Air moves clean through the whole run — there must be a second mouth somewhere east. Found flowstone floor firm enough to ballast if we drain the sump at chain 220. The men are calling it the Tollgate. I've asked them not to. Names like that get believed.*

Casper read that last sentence aloud to the empty room, and heard in his father's voice — a voice he had not truly heard in eleven years — a warning that had gone unheeded, because here, further east, in a different ink, the name appeared anyway: *Tollgate*, underlined twice, beside a symbol Casper now understood to mark a cavern entrance on the seaward cliffs.

The locals called it that still. The old woman on the dock yesterday had crossed herself when he'd asked about the shore caves, and her grandson had pulled him away by the sleeve, and no one would say why. He had filed it under superstition, the way an educated man files away everything that frightens him politely.

He was no longer sure it belonged in that drawer.

* * *

Toward evening he reached the section that broke him, though he did not know it was coming until it had already happened.

The dates had continued, year over year, and the confidence had thinned the way a river thins toward its source — gradually, then suddenly. The engineering notes remained precise; his father's mind had never stopped being excellent. But threaded among them now were other sentences, shorter, harder, written in a pressure of pencil that had nearly torn the linen:

*Steel delivered to the viaduct site fails bend tests. Rejected. Delivered again anyway.*

*Asked Harrow's man directly. Smiled at me. That smile will cost lives.*

*Filed objection with the provincial office. Receipt No. 4471. Keep this number.*

*They have reassigned me. "To better use my experience." I am to inspect bridges I have already condemned.*

And then, in the largest handwriting on the entire map, boxed in red as if his father had needed to shout on paper:

*I WILL NOT PUT MY NAME ON IT.*

Casper put the map down. His hands were not steady. He walked to the window, opened it, and breathed the cold harbor air until his eyes stopped burning, and told himself the burn was the wind.

Eleven years he had believed the story everyone told: that Edmund Amberdale, walking out to the Rivergate works one October morning with his crew, had been taken by the mountain — a collapse, a flood, bad luck, the toll such country exacts. An accident. A tragedy with no author. He had been fourteen, and he had needed the mountain to be the villain, because the alternative was unbearable.

The map was offering him the alternative. His father had found rotten steel in the viaduct. Had refused it. Had been reassigned, sidelined, mocked — and had kept working anyway, alone, on a secret route through the range that no charter covered, gathering evidence the way other men gathered firewood against a winter they could see coming.

And then the dates stopped.

* * *

Not gradually. Not thinning. Stopped. The last dated entry was six days before the collapse that killed him.

After it came only this: a final stretch of red line, drawn eastward from the deep interior of the passage, heading for the high plateau — and dying halfway across the page.

It did not fade, exactly. Fading would have meant doubt creeping in. This was different. The line ran strong and certain for perhaps three inches, and then the strokes simply ceased, mid-course, the way a sentence stops when the speaker stops believing in the sentence. There was no arrowhead. No terminal annotation. No *route impracticable*, no *survey abandoned*. Just absence — a pencil lifted from the paper by a man who had, at the end, put it down.

Casper stared at that unfinished line for a long time, long after the light had gone bad enough that he was reading memory more than ink.

His father had not stopped believing in the route. Casper was sure of that, the way he was sure of load calculations — by feel, below argument. A man who had fought steel mills and provincial offices for years did not surrender to geography. No: the line ended because the man ended. Because six days after that last confident stroke, Edmund Amberdale had walked into the Rivergate country with his crew, and the country had kept them.

Which meant the unfinished line wasn't an ending at all. It was a sentence interrupted. And an interrupted sentence, in Casper's experience of ledgers and inspection reports and the lies men wrote in both, was not silence. It was testimony waiting for whoever came next.

He thought of the gap on the official maps — the forty-mile blank where the gorge should be, the word too long to spell. His father had spelled it. Had spelled most of it. And the part he hadn't finished lay somewhere inside a mountain whose sea caves the people of Greywater would not look at, behind a tide that ran lowest, the old woman said, in three days.

Three days. The number arrived in his chest with the weight of a scheduled train.

Casper cleared the cold cup aside, took out his notebook, and began, in his own upright hand, to transcribe his father's annotations in order — dates, chainages, gauge marks, receipt numbers, all of it — because evidence copied is evidence that survives fire and flood and the goodwill of railway companies. He worked by candlelight past midnight. When he reached the final entry he copied that too, the interrupted line and all, and then, after a moment, drew its continuation himself: three inches of faint dotted red, reaching eastward across the blank page toward the plateau.

A question, not a claim. A son asking his father a question across eleven years of silence.

He folded the map along its worn brown-paper creases — carefully, the way his father had — and set it by the door for the morning. Below the window, the tide was going out over the mudflats with a long whispering sound, like a crowd settling before something begins.

Three days, he thought. Low enough to show what wants showing.

He blew out the candle and, in the dark, listened to the sea pulling back from the land as if making room.
