## 3. The Concession Clerk

The Provincial Concession Office occupied the ground floor of the old Customs House on Wexler Street, a building that had been built to impress and had since settled into merely enduring. Its granite steps were hollowed at the center by a century of boots. Its windows, tall and begrimed, let in a light the color of weak tea. Inside, the air smelled of dust, sealing wax, and the particular staleness of paper that has been filed and forgotten — the smell, Casper thought, of decisions made by men who never had to stand on the things they decided.

He arrived at ten minutes past eight, having learned long ago that government offices opened their doors at nine but began their real work whenever the first desperate applicant appeared. He was not the first. Two men in canvas coats sat on the bench outside the wicket, one of them asleep with his hat over his eyes, the other picking at the seam of his glove with the concentration of a man trying not to think about something. Neither looked up when Casper came in. Men who came to this office did not come for good news, and they had stopped expecting it from each other.

Casper took the third seat on the bench and set his satchel between his feet. In it: his surveyor's license, folded soft at the corners from handling; a letter of reference from a foreman who had since moved west and could no longer be reached; and his father's map, wrapped now in oilcloth against damp. He had slept badly. Twice he had risen to look at the map again by candlelight, tracing the gap in the gorge where the lines simply stopped, as though the paper itself had flinched away from whatever happened there.

At half past nine, a bell behind the wicket rang once, thin and officious.

"Next."

The clerk was a narrow man named Pellworth — the name was stenciled on a little brass plate beside the window, beneath the words CONCESSIONS & RENEWALS, as if he were a piece of equipment rather than a person. He had the complexion of someone who worked under gaslight year-round, and hands that moved through papers with the brisk, bloodless efficiency of a man shuffling cards he had shuffled ten thousand times before. He did not ask Casper to sit. There was no chair on the public side of the wicket anyway, which Casper suspected was deliberate.

"Purpose of application," Pellworth said, without looking up.

"Survey permit. Closed corridor, Rivergate division." Casper slid his license under the grille. "Full traverse. Engineering assessment of bridges, tunnels, and viaduct structures along the abandoned main line."

That got the clerk's attention, briefly. His eyes came up — pale, quick, unimpressed. "Rivergate." He said the word the way a man names a disease. "You understand the status of that corridor?"

"I understand it's been closed eleven years."

"It's been closed," Pellworth said, "since the Amberdale collapse. Which makes you either very brave or very ignorant, and I've found the two are difficult to tell apart at this window." He turned to the license, read the name stamped there, and something flickered across his face — recognition, quickly suppressed, the way a man suppresses a cough in church. *Amberdale.* Of course. The name would be in every file in this building. It was probably carved into the lintel somewhere, in the province's memory, next to the words *structural failure* and *eleven dead.*

"You're aware of the association," Pellworth said. It wasn't quite a question.

"My father's name was on the project," Casper said evenly. "It's part of why I want the corridor. I'd like to know what's actually there."

"Hm." Pellworth drew a ledger toward himself and opened it with a crack of binding. For a while there was only the sound of his pen moving, and the breathing of the sleeping man on the bench, and somewhere deeper in the building the slow tick of a clock that had been wrong for so long it no longer mattered what time it kept. Casper stood and waited. He had gotten good at waiting over the past months — waiting outside foremen's offices, waiting for telegrams that didn't come, waiting for the word *no* dressed up in its various official costumes: *regrettably*, *at this time*, *the board has determined*.

Pellworth pulled out a printed form and began filling it in. "Ninety days," he said. "Standard term for closed-corridor survey. You'll have access rights to the right-of-way, station houses where they still stand, and nothing else. No blasting without separate authorization. No timber cutting. No interference with Harrow Line markers."

"Harrow Line markers," Casper repeated. "On a corridor Harrow doesn't hold yet."

"On a corridor Harrow *will* hold," Pellworth said, and here he set down his pen and looked at Casper directly, and for the first time the man seemed less like furniture and more like a messenger carrying news he personally disliked delivering. "That's the part you need to hear clearly, Mr. Amberdale, because people come through this window thinking ninety days is ninety days. It isn't. The concession lapses at first snow. Not at day ninety — at first snow, whichever comes sooner. After that, the corridor reverts to the Harrow Line Company under the consolidation act. Every rail, every tunnel, every bridge abutment, every inch of surveyed or unsurveyed grade. Anything you find in there after the snow falls belongs to them. Anything you find in there and haven't *filed* before the snow falls might as well belong to them too."

"The act specifies snowfall?" Casper asked. "Not a date?"

"The act specifies 'the commencement of winter conditions,' which the commissioner interprets as first measurable accumulation within the corridor boundaries." Pellworth's mouth did something that was almost a smile and gave up halfway. "Last year that was the fourteenth of October. Year before, the second. The mountains don't consult the calendar, Mr. Amberdale. They consult themselves."

Casper felt it then — the thing he would carry out of this office and feel ticking in his chest for weeks afterward. Not fear exactly. Fear was a cold thing, and this was hot: a small iron weight settling behind his sternum and beginning, faintly, to swing. Ninety days. Fewer, probably. Perhaps far fewer. A season measured not in pages of a calendar but in the mood of weather a hundred miles north of anyone who cared.

"And renewal?" he asked, though he already knew.

"There is no renewal." Pellworth said it flatly, the way a doctor says *there is nothing more we can do* — not cruelly, but with the practiced weariness of a man who has said it many times and watched it land many times. "The consolidation act is final. When the window closes, it closes. I'll stamp your permits today, but I'll tell you what I tell everyone who takes a closed corridor: do not build your plans around day ninety. Build them around day sixty and thank me later."

He stamped the forms. The sound of the stamp — a dull, decisive *thump*, twice, once for the permit and once for the duplicate — sounded to Casper absurdly like a starting pistol.

"Sign here. And here. Initial the margin — that clause indemnifies the province against injury, loss, or death arising within the corridor. Yes, including death. We're thorough." Pellworth slid the papers under the grille along with two stiff yellow permits, each bearing an embossed seal and a number hand-inked in the corner. Casper signed. His signature looked strange to him on the form — smaller than usual, as if even his hand understood the modesty of what was being offered.

"One more thing." Pellworth gathered the completed file and squared its edges against the desk, and for a moment his briskness faltered into something almost human. "The Rivergate division. You'll find the grade washed out in places. The maps the province holds are eleven years stale and were incomplete even then — the final survey was never filed. Whatever your reasons for going in there, personal or professional, keep them separate. The corridor doesn't care about grief, and neither does the snow."

Casper looked at him. "Did you know him? My father?"

For a moment the clerk's pen hovered over the ledger, motionless. Then it came down and resumed its work. "I process applications, Mr. Amberdale. I don't know people." But he said it a half-second too late, and a half-tone too carefully, and Casper — who had spent his life reading trestles and trusses for the flaw hidden inside sound-looking timber — heard it plainly. Pellworth knew something. Whether it was much or little, whether it was about the corridor or about the man who had vanished in it, the brittle little clerk knew *something*, and he was not going to give it up at a wicket window to a stranger with a dead man's name.

"Your permits," Pellworth said. "Valid from today. Good luck, such as it is. Next!"

Casper stepped aside. The sleeping man on the bench woke with a snort, took off his hat, and approached the window with the gait of a man walking to his own sentencing. Behind Casper, the tea-colored light lay in long bars across the floor, and the wrong clock went on ticking its wrong time, faithful to nothing.

Outside, Wexler Street was loud with ordinary morning — carts, gulls, a fishmonger crying his prices, two boys chasing a hoop down the cobbles. All of it suddenly seemed to Casper like a country he was leaving. He stood on the hollowed granite steps with the yellow permits in his hand and read them twice, front and back, committing the numbers to memory the way he'd commit a signal code: *Rivergate Division, Corridor Survey, Permit 4471-C and 4471-D, holder Casper J. Amberdale, licensed engineer, term subject to termination upon commencement of winter conditions.*

First snow. Somewhere beyond the range, autumn was already leaning into winter. He thought of the map in his satchel, the lines that stopped mid-paper, the gorge with its missing years. He thought of the letter he would need to write to his mother, and did not yet know how to begin it.

Sixty days, then. He would build around sixty and pray for eighty.

He buttoned his coat against the harbor wind and started for the railway yard, where the morning train would carry him north and west, to the end of the line and the fishing village called Greywater — the last place a man could stand on solid ground before the mountains took over the argument.

Behind him, in the Customs House, the stamp thumped again for somebody else's future. Casper didn't look back. He could feel the clock in his chest now, swinging steady as a pendulum, keeping the only time that mattered.

* * *

The train left at noon, and Casper rode it standing at the open platform rail for the first hour, watching the city thin into suburbs and the suburbs into marsh. He had bought a third-class ticket with most of what remained of his savings, and the carriage smelled of coal smoke, wet wool, and someone's unwashed apples. He didn't mind. Third class was honest. First class pretended the world was better than it was, and Casper had lost his appetite for pretense of any kind.

At a water stop outside Tarnmouth, a conductor came through checking papers, and when he reached Casper's permits he paused, holding the yellow slips up to the grey afternoon light.

"Rivergate," the conductor said. "Haven't seen one of these in years." He handed them back and lingered, rubbing his jaw. "You know there's folk out that way won't go near the shore caves? Fishermen, mind — men who'll take any water God offers. They won't touch that stretch below the cliffs. Say the tide runs strange there."

"So I've heard."

"Mm." The conductor looked at him a moment longer, with the frank curiosity of a man deciding how much a stranger ought to be told. "Well. You're an engineer. You'll make your own assessments." He touched his cap and moved on down the swaying aisle, and Casper turned back to the window.

The marsh gave way to scrub, the scrub to stone. By late afternoon the land was rising in long folds toward the distant white shoulders of the range, and the light came slantwise and gold, laying the shadow of the train out ahead of it like a spear thrown at the mountains. Casper took out his father's map and spread it on his knees, and there it was again — the gap, the silence in the paper where the Rivergate gorge should have run. Eleven years ago his father had drawn these lines with a surveyor's confidence, mile after mile of sure, ruled ink. And then, at the gorge, the pen had lifted and never come down.

*What happened there, Dad? What did you find that made you stop drawing?*

The train rocked. The light failed by degrees. Somewhere ahead, past the last stations and the last telegraph poles, the sea was working at the base of the cliffs, patient as a clerk, keeping its own books, waiting for the tide to run low enough to show what wanted showing.

And in Casper's chest, the clock swung on — sixty days, maybe fewer — counting down toward the snow.
