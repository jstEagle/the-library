## 4. Tide Tables

The coach from Marrow Junction let him off where the road gave up being a road and became a suggestion — two ruts full of rainwater winding down toward a grey smear on the coast that the driver called Greywater without looking at it. Casper stood with his carpet bag and his father's map tube while the coach turned around, the horses steaming in the cold sea wind, and for a moment he watched the last connection to the interior shrink to nothing between the hills.

Then there was only the village, the sea, and ninety days.

Greywater was built the way driftwood is arranged: by accident, by weather, and with no expectation of permanence. Houses leaned together along a single crooked street that followed the curve of the shingle beach. Nets hung from every available beam like the shed skins of enormous fish. Boats sat hauled up above the tideline on wooden rollers, their hulls patched with tar and tin, their names painted over so many times they had become palimpsests — *Diligent* under *Providence* under something older still. The air smelled of salt, tar, kelp rot, and woodsmoke, all of it cut through by the clean cold iron smell of the sea itself.

Casper walked down the street with his engineer's coat and his city boots and felt the village take his measure before he'd taken ten steps. Curtains shifted. A man mending a net looked up once, held his gaze a beat too long, and went back to work with the deliberate indifference of someone who has decided not to decide about you yet.

The inn was called the Anchorless, which struck Casper as either a joke or a confession. Inside it was warm and dim and smelled of fish stew and lamp oil. Behind the bar stood a broad woman with forearms like capstans and grey hair pinned up with what appeared to be a sail needle. She looked at him the way the net-mender had, but she was quicker about her arithmetic.

"Room?"

"Room, supper, and information, if you sell the last."

"Depends what kind." She wiped the bar in front of him as if clearing space for a negotiation. "Marta Voss. I keep this place."

"Casper Amberdale. I'm a surveyor."

Something moved behind her eyes — small, quick, gone. "Surveyor," she repeated, tasting the word. "For the railway."

"For whatever's left of it. The closed lines through the Rivergate corridor. I hold permits from the provincial concession office." He set the folded permit papers on the bar, weighted them with a coin, and slid them across.

Marta read them longer than their contents required. When she handed them back, her face had settled into a careful blankness that Casper recognized, because he had worn one himself for most of the morning after he'd refused to sign the trestle report — the face of someone deciding how much truth a stranger can be trusted with.

"There's a room at the top of the stairs," she said. "Supper's at six. Stew or stew."

"I'll take the stew."

"And the information?" She almost smiled. "That's free, but you get what you pay for. Ask your question."

Casper hesitated. He had meant to ask about supply routes, packhorses, the condition of the old coast track north toward the gorge. Instead what came out was the question that had been sitting in his chest since the cliffs outside town, since he'd seen the dark mouths in the rock below the headland and felt something in his blood lean toward them like a compass needle.

"The caves. Under the cliffs south of here. The locals call one of them the Tollgate, I've heard. Can a man walk in at low tide?"

The inn went quiet in a way that was more than quiet. It wasn't that conversation stopped — it was that the two fishermen at the corner table stopped pretending to talk, and the boy stacking firewood by the stove stopped moving, and Marta's rag stopped on the bar. The lamp flame itself seemed to lean.

"No," Marta said.

"It's a simple question—"

"It's a simple answer." She resumed wiping, but slower now, her eyes on his face rather than the wood. "Nobody goes in the caves, Mr. Amberdale. Not the Tollgate, not the little ones, none of them. Not for crab, not for shelter, not for anything. You can ask anyone in this village and they'll tell you the same, and then they'll tell you not to ask again."

"Why not? Drowning? Rockfall?"

"Those are reasons people stay out of caves everywhere." Marta set the rag down. "We have other ones."

She didn't elaborate. The boy by the stove picked up his armload of wood and carried it out back with unusual haste. One of the fishermen muttered something to the other that Casper couldn't catch, and the other one laughed without any humor in it at all.

* * *

His room was small and cold and had a window that framed the sea like a painting hung slightly crooked. Casper lit the lamp, set his father's map tube on the table, and found among his papers the letter his mother had pressed into his hands at the Marrow Junction platform, three days ago now, her breath smoking in the cold, her grip on his sleeve tighter than she knew.

*You'll think me foolish,* she'd said, *but write when you can, and come home before the snow.*

He broke the seal now and read it standing up, because sitting down made it worse.

*Casper my dear,*

*I have packed your winter coat whether you took it or not, because I know you didn't. Mrs. Halloran asked after you and I told her you were working, which is true, though I did not tell her where, because I don't entirely know. Your father never told me either, in those years. I used to think it was secrecy. Now I think it was shame, or fear, or both wearing each other's coats.*

*I dreamed of him again. In the dream he was standing on a bridge that wasn't finished, over water that wasn't moving, and he kept looking back at me as if he wanted to say something and the words were too heavy to lift. I woke with his name in my mouth. I am not telling you this to burden you. I am telling you because you are going where he went, and I want you to carry one thing with you that isn't grief. Carry this: whatever he found out there, whatever happened to him and those men, it was not weakness. Your father was the most stubborn honest man I ever loved, and stubborn honesty is exactly the thing that gets people hurt in this world. You have it too. I could see it in you at four years old. So go carefully, and be less honest than he was if you must, but come home.*

*There is bread in the green tin. Eat it before it goes stale, which knowing you will be never.*

*Your mother,
E. Amberdale*

Casper read it twice. Then he folded it along its original creases, put it inside his coat — the wrong coat, the one she hadn't packed — over his breast pocket, and stood a while at the window watching the light die off the water.

*Over water that wasn't moving.* His mother dreamed in symbols the way engineers thought in loads and stresses; everything in her dreams meant something structural. A bridge unfinished. Water unmoving. Words too heavy to lift.

And beneath it all, the map in the tube on his table, ending mid-line in the middle of the Rivergate gorge, as if his father had stopped believing in his own hand.

* * *

Supper was stew, as promised, and better than expected — barley, fish, some root vegetable Casper couldn't name, all of it cooked long enough to surrender. He ate alone at a corner table while the common room filled with men coming off the boats, and he listened the way surveyors listen: not to words but to gaps.

They talked weather, prices, a wedding, an engine boat that wouldn't start. They talked about everything except the cliffs to the south, which were visible through the window, black against the last grey light, and except the caves beneath them, and except — he noticed — the railway. Not one word about the railway, in a region whose whole history was written in rail. It was as if the subject were a hole in the floor everyone had learned to step around in the dark.

When he paid for his meal, he tried once more. "The old line north," he said to Marta. "The coast track. Is it passable on foot?"

"Passable." She counted his coins into the till. "If you call walking on forty years of frost-heaved ballast passable. Keep off the viaduct spans. Some of them are standing on memory alone."

"Whose memory?"

"Ask the mountain." But her mouth tightened around the words, and he understood that she knew exactly whose, and that she was not going to say the name Amberdale in her own common room, and that her silence was a kindness or a warning or both.

Upstairs, he unrolled the map by lamplight and worked until midnight, tracing the closed lines in pencil, calculating distances, marking the collapsed sections Sefu-and-Brigid-shaped holes in his plan — no, not yet, he hadn't hired anyone yet, that was tomorrow's problem. Tonight the problem was simpler and worse: the corridor ran forty miles through country with no road, no track, and one obstacle — the gorge — that had swallowed a construction crew whole and kept them.

Unless the gorge could be bypassed. Unless the stories were true.

He fell asleep with the lamp burning and dreamed of a lantern swinging in darkness, its light catching letters stamped into rusted metal, and woke before he could read them.

* * *

In the morning the tide was far out, exposing a wide apron of wet sand and black rock, and Casper walked south along the shore with his notebook, doing what surveyors do: measuring, sketching, asking the land questions in the only language it answers. The cliffs rose sheer on his left, banded sandstone and shale, seamed with the mouths of caves — small ones mostly, waist-high, weeping groundwater. But southward they grew, and the cliff grew with them, until he stood beneath the headland the charts called Gate Point, and understood why.

The rock here was different. Older. It came down to the sea in a single vast wall, and set into the wall, half-hidden behind a shoulder of fallen stone, was an opening tall as a church door — barred, at present, by the tide itself, white water boiling across its threshold. Even from twenty yards he could see that the opening was not natural. There was geometry in it. Cut faces. And along the lower edge, just visible above the foam, a row of square notches spaced with a regularity no wave would ever produce.

Gauge notches. Or something like them.

His heart was doing something unprofessional. He made himself stand still and record observations: bearing, height, estimated width of opening, state of tide. His hand was steady. His breathing was not.

"You'll want to step back from there."

The voice came from behind and above — dry as driftwood, unhurried. An old woman stood on the shelf of rock where the beach ended and the cliff scree began, gathering bladderwrack into a basket with hands knotted like tree roots. She wore a man's oilskin coat thirty years too big for her and boots wrapped in twine, and she was looking at him with pale eyes that had spent eighty years looking at weather.

"I'm not going in," Casper said. "The water's up."

"The water's always up. That's not why." She came down the rocks with a sureness that embarrassed his own footing, and stopped an arm's length away, close enough that he could see her face was seamed like cracked varnish and that her expression held neither hostility nor welcome — only assessment, the same arithmetic Marta had done, done slower and more thoroughly. "You're the surveyor. The Voss woman's lodger."

"News travels fast."

"In Greywater news walks. We're small enough that it doesn't have far to go." She shifted her basket to her hip. "I'm Tamsin Reeve. My grandfather helped blast that cutting you're staring at, back when there was work. He never went inside it. None of them did, after."

"After what?"

She looked at the cave mouth for a long moment. The surf filled the pause, throwing spray against the cut stone.

"After the first crew went in and the second crew went to look for them, and the sea closed the door behind both." Her voice was flat, reciting, the tone of a story told so often it had worn smooth. "That's the tale, anyway. The true version's longer and nobody agrees on it. What everybody agrees on is the tide."

"The tide?"

"Tide tables." She nodded at his notebook. "You're a measuring man. So measure this: the spring tides run low twice a month, and once in a great while — a few times a year, when the moon's right and the pressure's low and the sea's in a mood — they run lower than any table prints. Lower than living memory expects. Three days from now will be one of those. The water will fall back farther than it has since last winter, and that door—" she tipped her chin at the cave, "—will stand open past its threshold, dry enough to walk into, for maybe two hours. Maybe less."

Casper felt the clock in his chest — the one the clerk's warning had wound — give an extra turn. Ninety days. And now a door that opened twice a year.

"You know the tide tables well," he said carefully.

"I've read them my whole life. Reading them and trusting them are different trades." Tamsin Reeve bent, pulled a strand of weed free of the rock, and dropped it in her basket without looking at it. "Three days, Mr. Amberdale. Low enough to show what wants showing." She straightened and fixed him with those pale eyes. "That's the phrase my grandmother used, and hers before her. Low enough to show what wants showing. Notice she never said low enough to show what *you* want shown. The sea decides what comes up. It always has. Men who argue with it end up in the ledger of this coast, and the ledger's pages are mostly names and dates and no explanations."

"What do you think it will show?"

"I think," she said, turning back toward her weed-gathering with the finality of a door closing, "that you already know. I think that's why you came to a village where nobody wanted the railway back, holding permits nobody asked for, asking about a cave everybody's afraid of. I think you're not here for the province at all."

Casper said nothing. The surf boomed against the cut stone, and somewhere deep under the sound — or perhaps only in his own chest — he thought he heard an echo with a shape to it, like the ghost of a whistle dying in a long gallery.

Tamsin paused, her back to him, and spoke without turning around.

"My grandfather left one saying about that place that the family still keeps. *The gate takes a toll from everyone who passes.* He meant it plain. But he also wrote, in the last letter he ever sent us, a second line that nobody in my family has ever managed to explain." She gathered her basket and started up the scree. "He wrote: *the toll is not what you pay to enter. It's what you learn you were carrying all along.*"

She climbed out of sight among the rocks. Casper stood alone on the wet sand with his notebook open to a page of neat figures that suddenly seemed beside the point, watching the white water seal the doorway, and began, methodically, to count the hours until the tide turned.

Three days.

Above the cave mouth, gulls wheeled and screamed against the grey sky, and the sea kept its own books, and waited.
