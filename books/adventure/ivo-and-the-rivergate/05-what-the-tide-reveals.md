## 5. What the Tide Reveals

The tide came in like a held breath let out slowly, and Casper Amberdale stood at the edge of Greywater's shingle beach and watched it go.

He had been there since before dawn, wrapped in his father's old wool coat with a thermos of tea gone cold in his pocket, because the old woman — Nettie Corrow, the one who sold mussels off a cart by the harbor wall — had told him three days ago, in the flat tone of someone reciting scripture: *The tide runs lowest on the third morning. Low enough to show what wants showing.*

He hadn't known what that meant. He suspected, even now, standing on wet stone with the sea pulled back a quarter mile like a curtain drawn by some enormous indifferent hand, that he still didn't know what it meant. But he had learned in three days of asking careful questions around Greywater that the villagers did not go to the cliffs at low tide. Not the fishermen, who knew every reef and current for twenty miles. Not the children, who swam in the harbor shallows all summer. Not the dogs, who whined and turned back at a certain line of kelp-strewn rock as if it were a fence only they could see.

*We don't go under the cliffs,* Marta had said at the inn, drying a glass with more force than the glass required. *Not a law. Just a thing nobody does. My grandmother said the sea takes its toll there. Hence the name, if you believe old stories.* She had laughed, but it hadn't reached her eyes. *The Tollgate, they used to call it. Nobody calls it much of anything now. Nobody goes.*

Nobody goes. Casper had spent thirty-one years in the company of men who went — into cuttings, into tunnel headings, into the black wet mouths of mountains that had killed better engineers than him. He had learned to read the particular quality of silence that meant *men died here and we agreed not to discuss it.* Greywater's silence had that quality. It hung over the whole village like fog.

So on the third morning, with the tide at its lowest in nineteen years according to the almanac he'd bought from the chandlery, Casper walked alone down the shingle toward the cliffs.

* * *

The exposed seabed was a country unto itself.

Kelp lay in great bronze ropes across the rocks, still dripping, still moving faintly in the residual swell as though the sea had only stepped out of the room and might return without warning. Tide pools glowed like scattered coins — green, violet, the startling orange of anemones. Gulls walked the strange new landscape with proprietorial strides, picking at crabs. The air smelled of iodine and cold stone and something older, something mineral and deep, like the inside of a tunnel.

Casper walked out onto it with the careful, rolling gait of a man who had spent his life on ballast and ties, testing each stone before committing his weight. The engineer in him catalogued even as he marveled: the strata of the cliffs laid bare at their base, the way the rock shelved in steps, the scour patterns in the gravel showing him exactly how the water moved when it came back. You could read water's intentions in the ground it touched, if you'd spent enough years reading ground. That was the whole trade, really. The rest was arithmetic and nerve.

The cliffs rose two hundred feet above him, black and green with lichen, seamed with the nests of seabirds that screamed down at him in indignant spirals. He walked along their base for the better part of an hour, finding nothing but sea caves of the ordinary kind — shallow throats, choked with boulders, roaring emptily with each spent wave. He was beginning to think Nettie Corrow's tide had shown him nothing at all, that the legend was just a legend and the villagers' avoidance just superstition wearing the costume of caution —

— when he saw the notches.

They were cut into the cliff face itself, at the base, in a band of harder stone that had resisted the sea's filing better than the rock around it. Casper stopped. Squinted. Walked closer, boots crunching on shell gravel.

Notches. Regular. Spaced. Cut square, with a chisel or a star-drill, in a pattern his hands knew before his mind caught up. He had seen that pattern a thousand times — in tunnel walls, in shaft headings, in the faces of cuts where surveyors had set their instruments. They were datum marks. Survey marks. Someone had stood exactly here, at the foot of these cliffs, with instruments, and had measured.

And below them, half-buried in the gravel, ran a line of something that made his breath stop in his chest.

Iron. A rail. Or what was left of one — a length of perhaps six feet, flanged foot down, web up, corroded to a lace of orange rust, but unmistakable. Rail. Laid at the base of a sea cliff, pointing directly into the rock.

Casper crouched beside it and put his hand on the cold web of the rail the way you'd touch a sleeping animal. The gauge — he measured it by eye, by knuckle-span, out of habit — was wrong. Not standard. Not any of the three common widths he could name. Narrower. An experimental gauge. He knew of only one man in the province who had ever argued for narrow-gauge mountain lines in print, who had written letters to the *Provincial Engineer* that the journals had published and then quietly stopped publishing replies to.

His father.

The thought arrived not as a thought but as a physical event — a tightening behind the sternum, a sudden loudness of his own pulse in his ears. He made himself stand up. Made himself walk on along the cliff base, following the ghost of the grade — because it *was* a grade, he could see it now, a cut bench running along the rock at a steady two percent, the sea's erosion having softened but not erased it — until the cliff wall swung inward in a great concave sweep, and there, where no cave had any right to be, was the mouth.

It was not a natural opening. That was the first thing he understood, standing before it with the wind pulling at his coat. Natural sea caves were keyholes, irregular, choked. This was an arch — a true arch, cut, its springline level, its voussoirs dressed stone. Someone had built a tunnel portal into the cliff at sea level, and the sea had spent the years since trying to take it back, and had only partly succeeded. The rubble of that argument lay heaped in the entrance: fallen rock, drifted gravel, the gray skeletons of crab pots and a rowboat ribcage, all of it cemented together with kelp and salt into a rampart. But above the heap, the arch stood open. Dark. Breathing.

Because it *was* breathing. Casper felt it before he heard it — a slow movement of air out of the dark, cool and mineral-smelling, with a rhythm that was not the rhythm of the waves behind him. The cave was exhaling. Somewhere inside, the mountain was breathing through some other opening, some vent, some throat, and the air came out of this portal in long slow draughts that stirred the wet gravel at his feet.

He stood there a long time.

Every instinct he owned told him to turn around. He was an engineer, and engineers died in exactly these places — uncharted, unventilated, unsupported openings in rock that the sea had been undermining for decades. He had signed death certificates with causes of death that began *entered an abandoned working.* He had, eleven days ago, refused to sign a piece of paper that would have sent trains across a rotten trestle, and it had cost him his name, and he was not going to throw the name away on a hole in a cliff that an entire village had agreed, without discussion, never to approach.

But the datum marks were cut by a surveyor. The rail was his father's gauge. The portal was dressed stone, engineered, *built* — and his father had come to Greywater thirty years ago with a contract to survey closed lines and had walked into the mountains with a crew of eleven men and had never walked out, and the official record said *lost in the collapse of the upper viaduct works* and the official record had been signed by men from the Harrow Line, and Casper had spent his whole life swallowing that sentence like a stone.

His father's map was in his coat pocket. He took it out. Unrolled it against the wind, weighting the corners with stones. The hand-annotated lines ran up the valley, over the pass, and stopped — stopped mid-stroke, the ink trailing off, in the gorge country above Greywater. He had stared at that gap for three nights in his rented room. Now, standing at the sea-level end of a rail grade that pointed into the mountain, he understood with a certainty that felt like vertigo: the map didn't stop because his father stopped believing in the line. The map stopped because the line went *underground,* and his father had stopped drawing what he had started keeping secret.

Casper folded the map. Put it away. Took out his lamp — a good brass storm lantern, bought in Greywater, filled and trimmed that morning because he had known, in the way you know a thing without admitting you know it, that he would need it today.

He struck the match. The wick took. The little flame stood up straight in the draught from the cave, leaning toward the dark as if pointing.

"Low enough to show what wants showing," he said aloud. His voice sounded small and foolish against two hundred feet of cliff.

He climbed the rubble rampart.

* * *

Inside, the world changed its rules.

The first twenty yards were the sea's territory — wet stone, weed-slick, the roar of the surf behind him bouncing off the walls until it seemed to come from everywhere. His lamp threw a small trembling circle, and beyond the circle was a darkness with weight to it, a darkness that pressed on the flame. He moved slowly, testing the floor with each step, an old habit from inspection crawls, and the floor under the weed was — he stopped and knelt and scraped with his thumb — ballast. Crushed stone, hand-broken, still graded. Under thirty years of tide-wrack, someone had laid a roadbed into this cliff.

Then the grade began to rise, gently, and the sea's roar fell away behind him like a door closing, and he was walking on dry ballast in a tunnel that no record in any office in the province admitted existed.

The walls closed in to a single track width, then widened. His lamp found the datum notches continuing at eye level, marching into the dark at precise intervals — and then found something else. Cable hooks. Iron rings, rusted, set into the rock at intervals, the kind used for hauling haulage rope in a working heading. And then, set into a widened chamber where the rock had been cut back to make a passing place, the wall carried a line of characters chiseled deep and clean:

**R.G. — MILE 0.**

Rivergate. Mile zero. The beginning of a line that had never been chartered, never surveyed on any public record, never existed — except in the mind of a man who had believed a railway could go through the range by going *under* it, and had come to the coast to prove it, and had not come back.

Casper stood in the passing place with his lamp held high and let the grief and the wonder arrive together, because they would not come separately. This was his father's handwriting in stone. This was the man himself — the patient chisel work, the careful grade, the ballast graded by someone who cared about drainage even a quarter mile into a cliff above the tide line. Casper had spent his childhood watching those hands: sharpening pencils with a knife in one long curl, leveling a spirit vial by eye, tapping a rail to hear if it rang true. All of it was here. The man had not simply vanished. He had *worked.* He had built. And then, at some point, he had stopped.

The tunnel went on. The lamp showed it bending gently left, climbing, dry, breathing its slow breath. It could go for miles. It could go through the range. It could be the thing the survey contract needed, the shortcut nobody owned, the answer to ninety days and first snow and the Harrow Line's lawyers — it could be all of that, and some part of Casper's mind was already running the arithmetic, the ventilation figures, the grade percentages, the engineer's cold bright joy of *a way through.*

He walked on. And thirty yards past the mile-zero stone, the lamp caught something at the edge of the ballast that stopped his feet as if he'd walked into glass.

A lantern.

It lay on its side in a niche in the wall, tucked there deliberately — placed, not dropped — and it was the twin of the one burning in his own hand. Brass storm lantern, the same pattern, the same make, the same little chimney and wire guard. Rust had taken it whole; it had been wet and dry and wet again for three decades, and it wore its corrosion like armor. But on the base, where the maker stamped every lantern that came out of the foundry at Corvess, the stamp was legible under his thumb when he knelt and rubbed the rust with his sleeve:

**E.A.**

Edmund Amberdale. His father's initials, punched into the brass in the private mark the man put on all his tools, the mark Casper had grown up tracing with a finger — on levels, on chains, on the watch he now carried in his own vest pocket and had carried every day since he was twelve.

Casper did not move for a long time.

The flame in his lamp hissed softly. The tunnel breathed. Somewhere far back, the sea worked at the portal rubble with its patient stone-cold patience, and the sound came down the passage like something being said very slowly, over and over, that he could almost but not quite understand.

His father had set this lantern here. Tucked it into this niche, on this ballast, at this mile — set it down the way a man sets down a tool he means to pick up again in an hour. And then had not picked it up. Had walked out of this tunnel, or been made to walk, or been carried, and the lantern had waited in the dark for thirty years with the initials punched in its base, waiting to be found by the only person in the world who would know what those two letters meant.

*He was here. He was right here, and then he wasn't.*

Casper lifted the rusted lantern with both hands. It was lighter than it should have been — the fuel long evaporated, the reservoir empty, everything in it that could rot or run out long since gone. Only the brass and the stamp remained. Only the name.

He held it up beside his own burning lamp, the two lanterns side by side in the dark, one lit and one dead, and understood that he had crossed a line somewhere in the last hundred yards and could not cross back. Not the portal behind him, not the tide, not the contract — some other line, older and less visible. His father had walked into this mountain and the mountain had kept him, and Casper had just walked in after, thirty years late, carrying the same name on the same kind of lamp.

Wonder curdled slowly into dread as he stood there, because the thought that finally arrived, arriving the way the worst thoughts arrive — quietly, completely, without argument — was this: *the villagers don't go under the cliffs. The dogs turn back at the kelp line. An entire village agreed, without a law, without a discussion, to forget this place. And men who are only forgotten are usually silenced.*

The lamp flame leaned toward the tunnel's inner dark, where the grade climbed on toward the gorge his father's map refused to finish.

And from somewhere up that dark — far off, faint, impossible — came a sound.

A whistle. Two short blasts and one long.

The signal, in the code every rail man knew, for *someone is here.*
