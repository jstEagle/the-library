## 6. The Boy Who Lives in the Gate

The man came in with the tide.

Ivo heard him long before he saw him — the particular slap of boots on wet stone that no gull and no seal ever made, too heavy on the heel, too certain of itself. He was crouched in the shadow of the second gallery, where the light from the cave mouth bent around a shoulder of rock and died, and he listened the way he had learned to listen: with his whole body, his breath held down small in his chest like a coin in a pocket.

One set of footsteps. That was new. The fishermen never came past the mouth — they crossed themselves at the cliff path and told their children the Tollgate ate people. The Harrow men who had come sniffing along the shore two summers ago had been three, then four, and they had carried lamps and iron bars, and Ivo had driven them out with falling stones and a whistle that echoed through six galleries until they fled believing the mountain itself was screaming at them. But this one was alone. Alone was either brave or stupid, and both kinds ended up dead in the water if they went where they shouldn't.

Ivo uncoiled from his crouch and moved along the gallery wall without a sound. He knew every inch of this stone the way other boys knew the streets of towns he'd never seen. Here the floor dipped where the old flood had scoured it; there a shelf of granite jutted at exactly the height of a man's temple. He passed the third notch — *three short blasts, line clear* — cut into the wall by hands long gone to dust, and slid into the passage that let him come out ahead of the stranger, above him, looking down.

The man had stopped at the lantern niche.

Ivo's stomach tightened. That niche was his. Everything in the gate was his — not because he owned it, nobody owned it, but because he kept it, and keeping was a kind of owning that mattered more. The old brass lantern hung there where he'd polished it only yesterday, and the man stood before it with his hat off, holding it up to what little light reached that deep, turning it so the stamped letters caught.

Ivo read the man's face the way he read weather. He wasn't a fisherman. His coat was good wool gone shabby at the cuffs, and his hands were wrong for a laborer — long-fingered, ink-stained at the nails, one knuckle scarred white. A clerk's hands. A surveyor's hands. And when he looked at the letters on the lantern — *E.A.* — something moved across his face that Ivo recognized without understanding, the way you recognize a storm color on the horizon before you know its name.

Grief. The man looked at the lantern like it was a grave.

He shouldn't have been able to see it at all. Ivo had checked the tide tables scratched into the keeper's slate himself; the low water hadn't been due for another day. But the sea kept its own books sometimes, and today it had opened the door wide, and this man had walked through it as if the door had been left open for him.

Maybe it had. That was the thought that made Ivo's skin prickle. The last keeper had said things like that, near the end, in his half-voice with his cough: *the gate shows what wants showing, boy, mind you believe it.*

The man took one step further in. Toward the inner passage. Toward the galleries where the ledgers lived, where the cot stood made up under its oilcloth, where the walls were carved with gauges and dates going back sixty years. Where no living soul but Ivo had walked since the keeper stopped coming back.

No.

Ivo moved.

* * *

Casper Amberdale had taken perhaps ten steps into the dark when the floor tried to kill him.

It happened without warning — one moment stone underfoot, the next his ankle wrenched sideways and upward with a vicious jerk, and he was falling, arms wheeling, shoulder striking rock hard enough to fill his skull with white noise. Something had closed around his boot. Not rope — wire, thin and taut and cunningly anchored, hauled up from some hidden fulcrum so that his foot swung clear of the ground and every struggle cinched it tighter. He hit the wall, caught himself on hands that immediately began to bleed, and hung there half-inverted while the pain in his ankle sang up his leg.

"Hello?" His voice came back at him doubled and tripled, thrown from gallery to gallery, mocking. "Is someone there?"

Nothing answered. The cave breathed around him — that vast slow tidal respiration, air drawn in from cracks high in the mountain and exhaled through the sea mouth, carrying the smell of salt and cold stone and something else, something older, like iron and lamp oil. Somewhere far above, water dripped with the regularity of a clock.

He'd been a fool to come in. He knew it even as he hung there. Every instinct he owned had screamed at the threshold — engineers are taught to respect enclosed spaces the way sailors respect weather — and he had stepped inside anyway, drawn by the lantern with his father's initials, by the notches cut into the walls at rail gauge, by the impossible suspicion taking root in him that the Rivergate gorge's missing forty miles ran *through* this mountain, and that Edmund Amberdale had known it.

Now he dangled from a snare set by person or persons unknown, in a cave the locals wouldn't enter, holding a dead man's lantern.

A pebble clicked somewhere above him.

Casper went still. In the blackness overhead — a blackness so complete his eyes had given up entirely — something shifted. Not a fall of gravel; gravel didn't move with intent. This was weight redistributing itself, a body settling into a better position, the way a cat adjusts on a fence post.

"I can hear you," Casper said, trying to keep his voice level. It came out rougher than he intended. "I'm not here to take anything. I'm an engineer. I'm surveying the closed lines. I have permits."

Silence. Then, from the darkness, a sound he couldn't place at first — two short notes, whistled, sharp and clean, followed by a longer lower one. It took him a moment, hanging there with blood running down his fingers, to translate it. Railway code. Whistle signals. Two short, one long.

*Stop. Danger.*

"I know it's dangerous," Casper said. "That's rather why I'm here."

Another whistle, and this one had a different shape entirely — a rising, derisive sweep that needed no translation at all. Whoever it was, they thought he was an idiot.

"Look," Casper said, "my foot is caught in your snare, my ankle is likely sprained, and I'm bleeding on your floor. If you're going to rob me or worse, could we get on with it? If not, I'd be grateful for the wire being slackened."

More silence. Longer. He had the strange sensation of being measured — weighed like freight, assessed like a bridge under load, tested for what he could bear. It was not an unpleasant feeling, exactly. It was thorough. Whoever watched him from the dark was taking no chances and making no assumptions, and Casper found, absurdly, that he respected that.

Then the darkness spoke.

"You crossed the green line." The voice was high — a child's voice, unmistakably, though flat and unchildlike in its cadence, each word placed like a tie in ballast. "Green means go. You went past the green. Green is the last warning. After green comes red."

Casper blinked into the blackness. "The green—"

"Moss." A pause. "On the archway. Green moss means go. Red lichen means stop. I put them there. I keep them fresh." Another pause, shorter. "You walked past the red too."

"I couldn't see it."

"That's why it's there." The voice managed, without inflection, to convey that this explained everything and that Casper was hopeless. "Everyone who comes in sees the green. Only people who don't belong miss the red."

"And do many people come in?"

"No." The word dropped like a coupling slamming home. "Nobody comes in. This is the gate. I keep the gate."

Something detached from the ceiling of the world.

Casper heard it coming — a soft rush of displaced air, the whisper of bare feet finding holds he couldn't see — and then a shape resolved out of the blackness directly in front of him, close enough that he flinched. A boy. Twelve years old at most, small and wiry, dressed in layers of mismatched cloth that might once have belonged to a dozen different men: a railwayman's canvas jacket cut down and re-stitched, wool leggings, boots two sizes too large stuffed with something to make them fit. His hair was a pale untamed thatch, and his eyes in the gloom were the grey-green of the sea before weather.

In one hand he held a lantern, lit, its flame turned low. In the other, a length of driftwood shaped and worn smooth into something between a club and a tool.

He did not point the driftwood at Casper. He simply held it, the way a signalman holds a lamp — not threatening, merely authoritative.

"Name," the boy said.

"Casper Amberdale."

The reaction was instantaneous and strange. The boy went rigid — not afraid, Casper thought, but *alert*, the whole small body snapping to attention like a telegraph key under a fast fist. His eyes narrowed, and he leaned in, and he looked at Casper's face with an intensity that was almost painful, hunting for something in it.

"Amberdale," the boy repeated. He said it carefully, tasting each syllable, the way you'd test a rail for a crack with a hammer — listening for the true ring or the dull one. "There was an Amberdale."

"Yes," Casper said slowly. "My father. Edmund Amberdale. Did you know him?"

The boy didn't answer. He crouched, and with two efficient movements — no wasted motion, the economy of someone who had done this a hundred times — released the snare wire from Casper's boot and coiled it away. Then he stood, raised the lantern, and held its light up full against Casper's face, studying him with the frank, total scrutiny of someone who had never learned that staring was rude.

"You have his hands," the boy said finally. "Same hands. Same scar." He touched his own knuckle, mirroring the white scar on Casper's. "He fell on the ice coming up from the lower gallery. Winter before the winter I came." He whistled something then, very softly — four short notes, almost tender. Casper didn't know the code, but he understood it anyway. It sounded like a name spoken kindly.

"You knew him," Casper said. His throat had gone tight. "You *know* him. Is he—"

"The keeper stopped coming back." The boy said it the way he seemed to say everything: flat, final, a fact filed in its proper place. "Sixty-one months ago. I count in months. Nobody counts days in a cave." He turned, lifting his lantern toward the inner dark, where the passage climbed away into the mountain's heart. "You'll want the ledger."

"The ledger?"

"He wrote everything down. That's what keepers do." The boy glanced back over his shoulder, and for just a moment the flat authority cracked, and underneath it Casper saw something young and wary and desperately alone. "If you're an Amberdale, you can read it. If you're not—" The driftwood shifted in his grip. "Then you walked past the red, and the gate keeps what walks past the red."

* * *

They went up together, the boy ahead with his lantern held high, Casper limping behind with his sprained ankle wrapped in a strip of the boy's spare cloth — offered brusquely, without eye contact, in the manner of someone feeding a stray animal they haven't yet decided to adopt. The passage climbed in long switchbacks, and the walls changed as they rose: raw sea-carved granite giving way to dressed stone, to timber cribbing gone silver with age, to iron brackets bolted into the rock at precise intervals.

Rail brackets. Casper stopped at the fourth one and put his palm flat against the cold metal, and his heart was hammering in a way that had nothing to do with the climb.

"These are standard-gauge mountings," he said. "But the spacing—" He measured with his hands, engineer's hands, automatically. "This isn't any gauge I know. What ran here?"

The boy looked back at him. "His trains," he said simply. "The quiet ones. Night ones." He whistled two short notes — *proceed with caution* — and walked on.

And Casper, holding his father's lantern in his bleeding hand, followed a twelve-year-old stranger deeper into the mountain, toward a ledger written by a dead man, knowing with every step that he was walking past the red — and that whatever waited at the top of the passage would change the shape of his life the way the tide changes the shape of the shore.

Behind them, far below, the sea sighed against the cave mouth, beginning its slow return.

The tide was coming in.
