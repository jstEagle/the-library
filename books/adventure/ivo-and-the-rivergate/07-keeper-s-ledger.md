## 7. Keeper's Ledger

The man followed where Ivo led, which was the first good thing about him. Most grown men who came into the Gate wanted to lead themselves. They pushed ahead with their lanterns held high like they were carrying the sun in a tin cup, and they stepped on the wet stone where you must not step, and they breathed too loud, and they died — no, not died, that was only one of them, and Ivo did not think about that one — they turned around and went back out and told the village lies about what lived in the caves.

This one walked behind. He kept his lamp low, the way the keeper had taught Ivo to keep his own, low so the light stayed on the ground where your feet needed it and didn't go shouting at the dark. He had said his name was Casper Amberdale, and he had said it strange, like the name was a coat that no longer fit him, and when Ivo's snare had caught his ankle he had not cursed or thrashed but had gone still and looked at the rope and said, "Good anchor point," which was such an odd thing for a caught man to say that Ivo had decided not to kill him.

Not that Ivo killed people. But he thought about it, sometimes, the way you think about the sea.

"Where are we going?" the man asked. His voice echoed wrong in this gallery — too flat, swallowed by the old timber lining the walls.

"You'll see." Ivo put two fingers to his lips and blew a short note through them, high and thin, and listened. The echo came back off the far wall clean, no rattle, no drip interfering. Dry passage. Good. "Mind the third sleeper. It's rotten."

"There are sleepers here?" The man's lamp swung down. Underfoot, half-buried in cave gravel, ran a line of blackened timbers at perfect intervals, and set into them, furred with rust, two rails of iron going off into the dark like the ribs of something enormous that had lain down here to die and never got up again.

Ivo watched the man's face in the lamplight. He had learned faces down here, where there was little else to learn. The keeper's face had been weathered and kind and closed, all three at once. The fishermen's faces were afraid. This man's face did a thing Ivo had never seen: it went hungry. Not hungry for food. Hungry the way the tide was hungry, pulling back and pulling back before it came rushing in.

"Somebody laid track in a sea cave," the man said quietly. "Who?"

"The keeper."

"What keeper?"

Ivo didn't answer that. Some questions you answered by walking, and he walked, ducking under a fallen prop, stepping over a pool as still and black as poured ink, following the rails deeper into the mountain while behind him the man's breathing quickened with something that wasn't fear exactly, or wasn't only fear.

* * *

The keeper's station sat in a chamber the size of the church in Greywater, if the church had been carved by water out of the inside of a mountain and lit by a single lamp instead of God. Ivo struck a match off his thumbnail — the keeper had taught him that too, thumbnail, never the box, the box wasted matches — and touched it to the wick of the big storm lantern that hung from an iron hook driven into the rock. Light bloomed slow and yellow, and the chamber came up out of the dark the way the shore came up out of the tide: gradually, then all at once.

There was a desk. Not a fine one — a carpenter's knock-together of crate wood and shelf plank, but square and true, because the keeper had built nothing crooked in his life. On the desk lay ledgers, seven of them, their spines cracked and their corners soft with damp, stacked in order. There was a rack of tools on the wall: gauges and calipers, a spirit level in its oiled case, hammers, chisels, a hand drill with its crank worn bright. There was a cot against the far wall, blanket folded at its foot with military corners, pillow plumped, waiting. There was a shelf of tinned food, dusted, rotated, the oldest tins at the front. There was a stove, cold now, its pipe running up into a crack in the ceiling that breathed the smoke away somewhere Ivo had never bothered to find.

And there was the chair, behind the desk, and Ivo did not look at the chair, because looking at the chair was a thing he had stopped doing years ago, mostly.

"This is—" The man called Casper Amberdale stopped just inside the light. His voice had gone strange. "This is a station. A working station. Someone *lived* here."

"The last keeper stopped coming back," Ivo said. It was the sentence he had made for this, the one he used, the only one, because the whole truth was a heavy load and he had carried it alone so long he'd forgotten how to set it down. "So I keep it."

The man turned slowly, lamp raised, taking in the tool rack, the cot, the tins. "How long since the last keeper—"

"Long."

"How long is long?"

Ivo counted the way he counted everything, in tides and winters. "Nine winters. Maybe ten. The winters run together down here."

The man set his lamp on the desk, carefully, squaring it with the ledger stack the way a particular sort of person squares things, and Ivo saw his hands. Big hands, scarred across the knuckles, a railroad man's hands, and they were shaking. Not much. Just enough.

"May I?" he asked, gesturing at the top ledger, and Ivo blinked, because nobody asked. The fishermen didn't come. The few men who had ever come this deep hadn't asked permission for anything; they'd grabbed, or they'd run. The keeper had asked permission of everything — of the cave, of the tide, of Ivo, small as Ivo was, as if a boy could refuse a grown man and have it mean something.

"It's yours to look at," Ivo said, and then, because the words felt wrong coming out, "I mean — I keep it. For whoever comes back. If someone comes back, it should be all here."

"And if no one comes back?"

"Then it's mine." Ivo heard how that sounded and lifted his chin. "It's mine already. Nine winters is enough. That's the rule of wrecks. Anything left past nine tides belongs to whoever salvages it. Marta's husband said so, before he drowned."

But the man wasn't listening anymore. He had opened the top ledger, and whatever was written on the first page had hit him like a wave hits a swimmer who has misjudged the depth, because he made a sound — small, punched-out — and sat down in the chair without seeming to decide to sit, and Ivo watched a grown man come apart quietly in the keeper's seat and understood, with a cold sliding feeling in his stomach, that he had made a mistake. He had shown this stranger the heart of the Gate. You didn't show strangers the heart of the Gate.

Then the man turned the ledger around so the lamplight fell on the page, and said, in a voice scraped down to bare wire:

"That's my father's handwriting."

* * *

Ivo knew the writing, of course. He couldn't read most of it — the keeper had begun teaching him letters, A through maybe M, before the teaching stopped — but he knew the shape of it, the strong forward lean of it, the way numbers marched across the columns like men in step. He had spent nine winters keeping those ledgers dry and squared and safe, oiling the shelf boards beneath them, turning them end for end each season so the damp wouldn't settle into one corner. He knew every stain. He knew the page where a drop of something brown had fallen, long ago, and the writer had blotted it and continued without comment. He knew the last entries best of all, though he could read almost none of the words, because the last entries were in the newest book, and the newest book stopped.

He had never once wondered whose hand it was. That was the thing. The keeper had been the keeper, the way the tide was the tide. You didn't ask the sea for its surname.

Now a stranger sat in the keeper's chair with the keeper's ledger open under the keeper's lamp, and the stranger was saying, "Elias Amberdale. Elias R. Amberdale, engineer of lines. That's — he signed everything like that, even my mother's birthday cards, even the—"

The man stopped. Pressed the heel of his hand hard against his eyes. Ivo stood very still by the tool rack, one hand resting on the handle of the spirit level case, not because he meant to use it but because touching the keeper's tools steadied him the way holding a rail steadied you when the ground felt like it might move.

"He had a son," the man said, to no one, to the ledger. "Me. Casper. He'd — I was six. Seven. He went out on an inspection along the coast division and never came home, and everyone said the sea took him, or the collapse took him, or he ran off, and my mother waited three years before she let the word widowed into the house, and I—" He laughed, one dry note with nothing in it. "I grew up hating him a little. For leaving. And now he's been *here*. He's been down here the whole time, keeping books, keeping a *station*, and I've spent my life—"

He couldn't finish. Ivo watched him try twice and fail, and something moved in Ivo's chest that he didn't have a word for, because there was a boy in that story, a boy of six or seven, and Ivo knew what it was to be left by a grown man who had matters more important than a child. The difference — the enormous, unbearable difference — was that this man's leaving had lasted twenty-some years and ended in a chair in a cave, and Ivo's had lasted nine winters and ended nowhere at all, because the keeper had never once come back, and Ivo had fed the stove and rotated the tins and turned the ledgers anyway, every winter, in case.

"When did he stop coming back?" the man asked. His voice had found some floor to stand on. "Exactly. What happened? There would be a record — he recorded everything, look at these columns, he recorded the *weather*—"

"He did." Ivo came forward slowly, drawn despite himself. "Weather, tides, rail measurements, food stores. Everything. Every day. He taught me the letters while he wrote. He'd say, *this is a B, river-boy, B for bridge, remember it*, and I'd remember it." Ivo pulled the newest ledger from the stack and set it beside the first one, and the man's breath caught again at the sight of them side by side, the same hand spanning years. "Then one day he went down to the lower gallery. He went down there sometimes. There's a place down there he cared about. He told me to stay above the flood line, and I stayed, because I always stayed, and he didn't come back up."

"How long ago?"

"I told you. Nine winters."

"No — I mean, how long after he left the station? Hours? Days?"

Ivo frowned. The question had edges he'd never turned over. "I don't know. He didn't come back. After three days I went down looking, and the lower gallery was—" He stopped. The memory rose the way bad water rises, and he pushed it down the way he always pushed it down. "The way down was blocked. Rockfall, I thought. I shouted for days. I burned the lamp in the window slot every night for a year so he could find his way back if he was lost. Then I kept the station, because that was the job, and the job doesn't stop just because the keeper does."

The man was staring at him. In the yellow light his eyes were wet and wide and full of a terrible arithmetic, and Ivo felt himself becoming part of somebody else's sum, and didn't like it, and couldn't leave it.

"You were here," the man said. "Alone. Since you were — how old are you?"

"Twelve. Nearly."

"Since you were three?"

"Nearly four. I remember him. I remember everything." Ivo said it flatly, daring the man to make it pitiful. "He fished for us both, off the rocks below the mouth at low tide. He sang songs with numbers in them. He called me river-boy because he found me — I washed down after a flood, in the reeds, and he said the river delivered me, so the river's claim on me came first, before any other claim anyone might think to make." He lifted his chin higher. "That means the Gate is mine to keep. Whatever your name is, whatever he was to you — the river's claim came first."

"I'm not contesting the river's claim," the man said, and something in the way he said it — gentle, immediate, no argument in it at all — knocked Ivo slightly sideways, like a wave you'd braced for that turns out to be warm. "Ivo. Is that your name?"

"The keeper gave it to me. Out of a book. He said names should be short if you're going to shout them across water."

"Elias Amberdale," the man said, "pulled a drowning child out of a flood and raised him in a railway station inside a mountain, and neither of you ever told a soul in Greywater."

"Marta knows. Marta brought flour. She thinks we're lighthouse keepers' kin. I let her think it." Ivo hesitated, then asked the question that had been building in him since the man had spoken his father's name, the question that had teeth. "If he was your father, why did he never send word? Nine winters. He wrote in the book every single day and he never once sent a letter out with Marta. I asked him once, near the end. I asked, *when do we tell people who we are?* And he said, *soon, river-boy. When the line is finished and the steel is proven, then everyone will know everything.*" Ivo's voice dropped. "What line? What steel? What was he proving?"

The man had gone still in the chair. Slowly, he reached out and turned the newest ledger toward himself, and began, with fingers that trembled less now than they had, to turn pages backward through time — backward through measurements and tide tables and supply lists — hunting, Ivo realized, for the beginning of the ending. Hunting for whatever his father had been doing down here in the dark all those years, singing songs with numbers in them to a river-found child, recording weather no one would ever see, laying track to nowhere in the belly of a mountain.

"I don't know yet," Casper Amberdale said. "But I'm going to read every word."

* * *

They read until the lantern burned low. Or rather, the man read, aloud, in a low rough voice, and Ivo sat on the cot — the keeper's cot, which he had never once allowed himself to sleep in, though he had straightened its blanket a thousand times — and listened, and the chamber filled up with the keeper's voice coming out of a stranger's mouth, and it was the closest thing to hearing him again that nine winters had brought.

Most of it was numbers. Rail elevations. Gauge widths — and here the man paused, frowning, checking figures against figures, muttering that the gauge was wrong, impossible, no company's standard, wider, experimental, *what were you building, Father?* Tide tables copied fair. Notes on the rock itself, on which galleries sweated in summer and which froze in winter, on the vent shafts, on the sound of the sea in the deep places during storms. And threaded through it all, growing more frequent in the later months, a second kind of entry — shorter, harder, pressed down onto the page with a pen pushed nearly through the paper. Entries the man read silently first, his jaw tightening, before reading aloud.

"*Sampled the Harrow shipment at the coast spur. The steel is wrong. Flawed at the core — painted over, stamped honest. If this went into the viaduct piers—*" The man stopped. Swallowed. "*If this went into the viaduct piers,*" he repeated, "and the viaduct is the one over the gorge, the one that came down with thirty-one men on it, the one they blamed on the surveyor—" He looked up, and his eyes were terrible and bright. "They blamed the surveyor. They ruined him. And my father *knew*. He was down here building the proof, entry by entry, measurement by measurement, and he never got to—"

He didn't say *he never got to testify*. He didn't say *he never got to come home*. He put both hands flat on the ledger, flat on his father's words, and bowed his head over them, and Ivo sat on the edge of the dead man's cot and watched grief arrive in the station like a traveler finally reaching shelter, and felt the strangest ache of envy underneath his pity — because this man at least knew. Knew the worst, and the worst had a shape, and a shape could be fought. Ivo's worst was a blocked gallery and a lamp burning in a window slot for a year and a silence that had never once explained itself.

"The lower gallery," the man said suddenly, lifting his head. "You said the way down was blocked. Rockfall, you thought." He was already rising, already reaching for the storm lantern, already the engineer again, grief converted to fuel the way a locomotive converts fire to motion. "Show me. Tonight, if the tide allows. My father went down there and didn't come back, and I have spent my whole life being told he abandoned us, and I am not—" His voice cracked and he mastered it. "I am not leaving this mountain until I know what's down there."

Ivo stood too. Fear moved through him, old and familiar, the fear of the lower gallery that he wore the way other children wore a coat. But underneath the fear, something new was stirring — something that felt dangerously like hope, and dangerously like company.

"The tide turns in four hours," Ivo said. "After that, the lower approach floods to the roof. We go now, or we wait three days." He took the keeper's spare lantern down from its hook and lit it, and his hands were steady, because the keeper had trained them to be steady, whoever the keeper had been, whoever he belonged to. "Follow me. And mind the third sleeper. It's rotten."

At the mouth of the passage leading down, where the rails bent away into the dark toward the place Ivo had never dared, the man paused and looked back once at the station — at the cot, the tools, the ledgers stacked in their nine-winter order — and said, quietly, as if making a vow to the room:

"He kept it beautifully."

And Ivo, who had kept it beautifully, alone, for nine winters, for a return that had never come, felt the words land somewhere deep and unguarded, and had to turn quickly toward the dark so the man wouldn't see his face.
