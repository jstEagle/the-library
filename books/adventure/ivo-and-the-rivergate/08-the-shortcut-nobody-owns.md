## 8. The Shortcut Nobody Owns

Casper woke on the keeper's cot with the sea breathing somewhere below him.

That was the first impossible thing. He had slept in survey camps and boarding houses and once, memorably, in the cab of a stalled locomotive during a November storm, but he had never slept inside a mountain with an ocean for a heartbeat. The sound came up through the stone itself — a long exhale, a pause, a long inhale — as though the cliff were a lung and the tide its breath. He lay still for a while and let it work on him, because an engineer learns early that some sounds are information. This one told him the galleries above the waterline were dry, that the rock was sound enough to hold its shape against pressure twice a day, every day, without complaint.

The second impossible thing was the boy.

Ivo was already awake. Of course he was. Casper had learned in one day that the child slept like a gull — lightly, in snatches, one ear always turned toward the water — and that he rose before dawn to do things Casper could not yet catalogue: checking snares, hauling brackish water up from a seep for boiling, running his fingers along certain stones the way other boys ran fingers along fence rails. Now Ivo sat cross-legged near the cold iron stove in the keeper's station, turning pages of the oldest ledger with a care that would have shamed most archivists, and when he noticed Casper watching, he did not stop or apologize. He simply looked at him the way he looked at everything: directly, without ceremony, as if human attention were just another kind of weather to be endured.

"Two bells," Ivo said, and tapped the wall twice.

Casper had spent the previous evening learning the boy's language. It was not quite speech and not quite signal; it was rail telegraphy translated into gesture and clipped phrase. Two taps meant all clear. A flat palm dragged downward meant water rising. When Ivo wanted to say *stay*, he made a fist and held it against his sternum, and Casper had understood it immediately, the way you understand a lamp hung at a crossing — not through translation but through recognition. Somewhere in the boy's short life someone had taught him the grammar of the line, and when the teachers stopped coming, the grammar had stayed, growing wild like ivy over an abandoned station.

"Two bells," Casper agreed, and sat up. His back reported its objections about the cot's narrowness. He ignored them.

The keeper's station occupied a chamber perhaps forty feet below the cliff top, reached by a stair cut into the living rock — a stair with a handrail, Casper had noted, and the handrail was set at the height a working man would want it, which meant the men who built this place had expected to climb it tired, carrying loads, in the dark. Everything in the cave said the same thing. The ledges were squared. The drainage channels were cut with a slight fall toward sumps. There were iron rings bolted into walls at intervals, for rope lines, and the bolts were good ones, ordered from a foundry, paid for by someone. This was not a smuggler's hole or a hermit's fancy. This was infrastructure. Somebody had surveyed this passage, graded it, provisioned it, and then — the ledgers said so plainly — stopped coming.

Casper crossed to the table where the maps lay and unrolled his father's sheet beside Ivo's copies, and there it was again, the thing that had kept him awake half the night: the gap.

His father's map covered the whole Rivergate concession in confident ink — grades, curves, mileposts annotated in a small precise hand Casper knew better than his own, because he had grown up tracing it on kitchen tables while his mother complained, fondly, that the man drew in his sleep. The map ran east from Greywater along the coast bluffs, turned inland at the gorge, climbed the switchbacks toward the plateau. Except at the gorge it didn't turn. At the gorge the line simply ended, mid-stroke, the pen lifted, and in the blank space where forty miles of collapsed viaduct and dead trestle should have been drawn, his father had written nothing at all. No note. No *survey incomplete*. Just absence, shaped exactly like a secret.

And now Casper knew what filled the absence. It was under his feet.

* * *

They spent the morning walking the passage.

Ivo led with a lantern held low, the way harbor pilots carry lights so the glare doesn't kill their night vision, and Casper followed with a notebook, a tape, a clinometer, and the growing, vertiginous conviction that he was walking through the answer to a question he'd been asking since the morning he'd refused to sign a rotten inspection and lost everything but his name.

The main gallery ran inland at a grade so gentle Casper checked it three times before believing it. One point four percent, falling toward the sea. Forty feet wide at the shoulder, benched level, with a low retaining wall along the seaward side where the floor dropped away into flooded lower chambers. Old tie stubs still projected from the ballast in places, rotted to soft black cores, laid to a gauge he couldn't yet measure accurately but which was narrower than standard — narrower than anything in the provincial code. And the air moved. That was the detail that made his hands go cold on the tape. Air moved through the passage from the inland end, steady and cool, which meant the tunnel breathed from somewhere beyond the gorge. Which meant it went through.

"How far?" he asked Ivo, for perhaps the sixth time since yesterday.

Ivo considered the question with the seriousness he gave all questions, lips moving slightly as he converted it into his own units. "Sleeps," he said finally. "Two sleeps walking slow. One sleep if we don't stop." He held up fingers, then folded them down one at a time. "Dark water here." A tap at his ankle. "Falling rock there." A tap at his shoulder. "Then light." He spread both hands wide, palms out, and for a moment his face did something young and unguarded — wonder, plain as bread. "Big light. Sky."

Two days' walk through the mountain, against forty miles of collapsed line that would take a full crew a season to rebuild even if the province could be persuaded to fund it. Casper stood in the lamplight doing arithmetic that felt almost criminal in its simplicity. The concession expired at first snow. First snow, by every almanac and every fisherman's squint, was six weeks out, maybe less. A conventional survey of the closed corridor — clambering over washouts, sounding trestle footings, chaining around slides — would consume every one of those weeks and probably fail anyway, because half the route no longer existed except as drawings. But a survey that documented a viable passage *through* the range, bypassing the collapse entirely? That wasn't a salvage report. That was a discovery. That was a corridor worth chartering, worth fighting for, worth keeping open past the deadline because no clerk in the provincial office would dare let a working shortcut revert to Harrow Line while the ink on the findings was still wet.

If he could chart it. If he could prove it. If—

"You're making the face," Ivo said.

"What face?"

"The counting face." Ivo tapped his own temple. "Men make it before they ask for something big."

Casper huffed. "Is that so."

"Marta makes it when she waters the beer." Ivo said it without malice, as pure observation, and Casper laughed despite himself — the first real laugh since Greywater, since the dismissal, maybe since the funeral. The sound went up the gallery and came back changed, flattened by stone into something older.

He crouched then, to be nearer the boy's height, because there was a thing that needed saying and it deserved eye level. "Ivo. Listen to me. What you've shown me — this passage, the ledgers, all of it — if I write it down and send it to the engineer's office, it changes things. Big things. Trains might come through here someday. Men with instruments and opinions. It won't be yours anymore, the quiet part of it." He paused. "I won't do that unless you say yes."

Ivo looked at him for a long moment. Then he walked ten paces up the gallery, stopped at a rusted iron plate bolted to the wall — Casper hadn't even noticed it — and rapped it with two knuckles. Under the corrosion, letters: a serial number, a foundry mark, and a date thirty-one years old.

"Gate was never mine," Ivo said. "Gate was never anybody's. Keeper said—" and here the boy's voice did something complicated, thickened and thinned at once, "—keeper said a gate belongs to what walks through it. You walk through it now. So it's yours too." He shrugged, one shoulder, the way children shrug when they have said more than they meant to. "But the snares stay mine."

"The snares stay yours," Casper agreed solemnly, and offered his hand, and after a suspicious pause Ivo shook it with a grip like a dockhand's, and that was that. Contracts had been executed with far more ceremony and far less honesty all across the province that morning, Casper was sure of it.

* * *

The trouble — and Casper made himself look at it squarely, because self-deception was the disease that had killed the Rivergate once already — the trouble was everything else.

He spent the afternoon at the table with the ledgers open and his notebook filling, and the picture assembled itself out of numbers, and the numbers were honest and therefore merciless. Yes, the passage bypassed the collapse. But the eastern portal, the one Ivo called *big light, sky*, opened onto the high plateau via a vent shaft and a rubble slope that Ivo described with gestures suggesting a scree field the size of a church. The drowned galleries flooded on every spring tide — Ivo demonstrated with the flat-palm drag that Casper was learning to dread — and the flood reached within eight feet of the main gallery floor at its lowest lip. Eight feet. One storm surge. One unusual tide like the freak that had exposed the sea mouth in the first place, and the whole route became a pipe organ with the ocean playing it. Any survey report he wrote would have to say so, because he had lost one job refusing to lie about water and timber, and he would not purchase another by lying about stone and tide.

And the deeper trouble was smaller than any of that and heavier too. It fit in a lantern.

The lantern sat on the shelf where Ivo kept it polished, brass housing stamped with the initials E.A. — Edmund Amberdale — and a service number Casper had checked against his father's old employment card twice, as if the letters might rearrange themselves overnight. His father's lantern. His father's ledgers, in his father's hand, ending mid-sentence. His father's cot, worn to the shape of a man broader through the shoulders than Casper. Every object in this station was a piece of evidence in a case nobody had ever been allowed to file, and Casper understood now that charting the passage was not merely a survey. It was an excavation. Somewhere ahead, in the dark the boy wouldn't speak of, lay the answer to what had happened to eleven men and one stubborn keeper, and Casper would have to walk toward it with his eyes open, because the alternative was to walk away and spend the rest of his life hearing the sea breathe through a mountain.

Trusting a legend, he could manage. Legends didn't drown anyone who read the tide tables. Trusting a twelve-year-old with his life and his father's ghost and the last six weeks of his professional existence — that was the harder ledger entry. And yet. He watched Ivo across the room, showing Sefu's future duties to nobody, rehearsing, and thought: the boy has kept this gate alive alone for longer than most adults keep a promise. Competence doesn't always come of age. Sometimes it comes of necessity, wearing bare feet.

There was also, he admitted, writing carefully in the margin of his notes, the matter of Vane. Aldous Vane did not strike Casper as a man who left questions unanswered. Marta's nervousness at breakfast, the way she'd refilled his coffee unasked and looked at the door — something was moving in Greywater, and he'd been in the industry long enough to know that when a rival started asking pointed questions about your permits, he wasn't gathering information. He was building a case. Which meant speed mattered, and secrecy mattered, and the cave — hidden, unregistered, owned by nobody — might be the only office in the province where a man could work without being watched.

Hope arrived wearing caution, his mother used to say about second marriages. Casper wrote it in the margin and underlined it twice.

* * *

Near dusk — dusk being a rumor down here, deduced from a faint cooling of the air moving through the gallery — Casper climbed the cut stair to the sea mouth and stood in the throat of the cavern looking out.

The tide was coming back. The great slab of exposed seabed that had granted him entrance two days ago was already drowning, channels filling, kelp lifting and swaying like hair in wind, and the cave mouth's lower arch hissed and swallowed white water. In an hour the door would be shut. In fourteen hours it would open again, briefly, on the next low. The mountain kept banker's hours, and everyone inside lived on its schedule whether they liked it or not.

He got out the letter.

It had come with the coastal steamer, forwarded through three hands, his mother's small upright script on the envelope. He'd read it on the beach and hadn't answered it yet, partly from busyness and partly because answering it truthfully would require explaining where he was sleeping, and *inside a cliff, Mother, with a feral child and my dead father's dishware* was a sentence that would put her on the next boat. He read it again anyway, because the sea was loud and the words were warm.

*Your father used to say the rivergate country was the finest engineering problem on the coast,* she had written, *and that whoever finished it would finish it honestly or not at all. He was a difficult man to love and an easy man to believe. Be both, if you can.*

Below him, the last channel filled. The exhale of the surf deepened into the long inhale, and the cave drank the tide, and somewhere in the flooded dark below his boots the lower galleries took the sea into their lungs and held it, patient as everything else in this place.

Casper folded the letter, went back down the stair, and found Ivo waiting with the ledgers stacked and the lamp trimmed, and the boy's expression was the closest thing to eagerness Casper had seen on him — a stillness so intense it vibrated.

"We start tomorrow," Casper said. "Full chain survey, gallery by gallery. Grades, widths, clearances, flood levels, everything the office will demand and half of what they won't know to ask for." He tapped the map, the gap, the silence his father had left there. "By the time the snow flies, this line is going to exist on paper if it exists nowhere else. And nobody — not Harrow, not Vane, not the tide itself — is going to close this gate while I'm its keeper's son."

Ivo nodded once, businesslike, and then ruined the solemnity entirely by holding up one finger — *wait* — and disappearing into the back chamber. He returned with a second lantern, polished until the brass threw lamplight like struck gold, and pressed it into Casper's hands.

"Yours," Ivo said. "Keeper's son gets a light."

Casper held it a moment too long to pretend his eyes were dry, and the boy pretended not to notice, which was its own kind of mercy, and outside the mountain the tide turned and began, slowly, to give the shore back.

It was two hours later, both of them drowsing over the tables, that Ivo went suddenly rigid — head cocked toward the sea mouth, nostrils flared, the whole body become one listening instrument. Casper had learned that posture yesterday and had hoped never to see it aimed at anything but gulls.

"Smoke," Ivo whispered. "On the wind. From the village side." His eyes found Casper's in the lamplight, and they were not a child's eyes at all. "Somebody's burning something they shouldn't."

Far off, muffled by stone and distance, came a sound Casper knew from his years on the crews and would know in his grave: the flat, doubled crack of a blasting charge, rolling in off the water like a knock at a door.

Someone had just fired a shot into the Rivergate's front porch.
