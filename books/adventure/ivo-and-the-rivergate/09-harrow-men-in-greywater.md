## 9. Harrow Men in Greywater

Marta Quill had run the Salt and Anchor long enough to read a man by the way he came through her door. Fishermen entered like men apologizing for existing — shoulders down, eyes on the floorboards, already smelling of their day's failure. Farmers from up-valley came in loud, wanting to be heard over a sea they didn't trust. Government men arrived with their hats still on, as if removing them might constitute a concession.

The three who walked into her common room on the last Tuesday of October were none of these. They came in the way weather comes in off the water: no announcement, no apology, just a change in the pressure of the room.

The one in front was lean as a surveyor's chain and carried himself with the particular neatness of a man who had never once been caught out. Grey coat, grey gloves, grey eyes that went around her common room the way an auditor goes around a ledger — not looking at things so much as pricing them. Behind him came two younger men in matching oilskins, Harrow Line buttons at their collars polished bright enough to catch the lamplight, which told Marta they were new to the coast. Nobody who'd worked Greywater kept his buttons bright past the first week of salt air.

"Mrs. Quill," the grey man said, before she'd said a word. "Aldous Vane. We'll want rooms. Three, if you have them, and dinner that doesn't come out of the sea."

"We've got fish," Marta said, "and we've got mutton that walked here from up-valley, and we've got rooms enough." She wiped her hands on her apron though they weren't wet. It was a habit she'd never broken, and it made her look busier and stupider than she was, which had served her well for nineteen years. "You'll be with the railroad, then."

"Harrow Line," Vane said, and let it sit there like a coin on a counter. "Concession work. The corridor reverts to us after first snow, but there are matters of record to settle before then."

"Matters of record." Marta nodded slowly, the way she nodded at tide reports and death notices. "We don't get much record down here."

"No," Vane agreed pleasantly. "I noticed."

He took the chair nearest the fire without being shown to it, and his two juniors arranged themselves behind him like punctuation. Marta fetched the mutton because it was the expensive thing and she wanted him gone before he thought to ask what else the village had. While the kitchen did its work she stood at the bar and watched him watch her common room, and she felt the particular cold that comes not from wind but from attention.

There were four other people in the Salt and Anchor that evening. Old Tam Breech, asleep in his corner with a pint going warm beside him. Two crabmen from the north shore, arguing about prices in the low murmuring way of men who'd argued about the same prices for thirty years. And in the window seat, half-hidden behind the salt-crusted glass, a girl of maybe sixteen doing sums in a schoolbook, which was odd in itself, because the school had closed two winters back and nobody local did sums for pleasure.

Vane's eyes passed over all of them and dismissed them, and then came back to the girl, and stayed.

"That your daughter?" he asked Marta when she set down his plate.

"She's nobody's daughter. She does letters for people who can't. Reading, writing, figures." Marta shrugged elaborately. "Coast people aren't much for books."

"Mm." He cut his mutton into precise squares. "Useful trade. Does she do telegrams?"

"There's a wire office at Port Ellen. Forty miles."

"I know where it is." He smiled, and it was a good smile, the kind that had been practiced against better mirrors than any Greywater possessed. "Eat your dinner, Mrs. Quill. I'll have questions after, and you strike me as a woman who answers better on a full stomach."

* * *

He waited until the crabmen had gone and Tam Breech had been shaken awake and sent home, until the girl had packed her sums away and slipped out the side door with a nod to Marta that meant paid-in-full, until the common room held nothing but the three of them and the fire working through damp driftwood.

Then he laid a paper on the table between them, square and flat, the way a man lays down a winning card.

"You know this name?"

Marta looked. CASPER AMBERDALE, written in a clerk's hand, with a permit number beneath it and the words RIVERGATE CORRIDOR — SURVEY AUTHORIZATION. Her heart did something complicated and painful, and she kept her face doing nothing at all, which was the hardest work she'd done since her husband's funeral.

"Amberdale," she said, tasting it. "No."

"He took the coastal road through here. Three weeks ago, perhaps four. Tall man, forty or near it, carries his gear like an engineer — everything strapped and squared. He'd have asked about the closed lines. About the gorge."

"Half the coast asks about the gorge. It's the only thing we've got that strangers find interesting." She picked up his empty plate and began stacking it with unnecessary care. "Railroad men mostly. They come, they look at the cliffs, they go away shaking their heads. Nothing runs through there. Everybody knows it."

"Everybody knows it," Vane repeated softly. "And yet the province issued a permit for a survey of it, which means somebody believes otherwise. Men who believe otherwise are worth finding, Mrs. Quill. My company pays for information. Good information." He produced a second card, plain white, with a number written on it in the same grey hand that seemed to be the only hand he owned. "If a tall man comes back through — if anyone mentions him, if anyone remembers a stranger asking after tides or caves or the old viaduct road — you send word to Port Ellen. There's money in it. Honest money, for honest remembering."

"And if I don't remember anything?"

"Then you'll have lost nothing but a stamp." He rose, buttoned his coat with two economical movements. "One more thing. Your village has a reputation for keeping its mouth shut. That's usually a virtue. In this case it would be a mistake. The Harrow Line will hold this corridor within the year, Mrs. Quill. When it does, there will be a depot, a spur line, wages, traffic. Every village on this coast will get a piece except the ones that were unhelpful during the transition." He put on his hat. "I'd hate to see Greywater left off the map twice."

He said goodnight as though he meant it, which was somehow worse.

* * *

Marta did not sleep. She sat in the kitchen with the banked stove ticking and turned the permit paper over and over in her hands, because he'd left it — deliberately, she understood now, the way you leave a hook in the water — and she couldn't decide whether burning it would be loyalty or evidence.

Casper Amberdale had eaten at her table eleven times. She'd counted without meaning to, the way she counted everything that mattered. He'd taken the corner room, the cheap one, and paid for a week at a stretch and then another week, and he'd asked her questions she'd answered badly on purpose: whether locals went into the shore caves (no), whether anyone fished the north cliffs below the old keeper's path (nobody living), whether the tide ever ran low enough to walk under the headland (once in a generation, and God keep anyone fool enough to try).

She'd lied to him too, in her way — lied by omission, by shrug, by the elaborate stupidity of her apron-wiping. But she'd also told him the one thing that mattered, the thing the whole village knew and never said to outsiders: *the tide runs lowest Thursday next. Low enough to show what wants showing.* She'd told him because he'd stood in her kitchen with his father's map spread on her table, and she'd seen the gap in the map where the gorge should be, and she'd seen a man looking at a hole where his own history ought to be, and Marta Quill had buried a husband and a son and could not abide watching another person dig alone.

Now three grey men sat upstairs in her beds, sleeping on her linen, and in the morning they would fan out along the coast asking their questions, and somewhere across the water — or under it, or inside the mountain, she didn't know which and that was the worst of it — Casper Amberdale was walking around with a target painted on him that he didn't know about.

She got up, lit a candle stub, and went to the drawer where she kept things that weren't hers to keep. At the bottom, wrapped in oilcloth, was a telegram form. Blank. She'd taken it from the wire clerk at Port Ellen on her last trip for supplies, months ago, telling herself it was for emergencies, telling herself a great many things.

She held it a long time. Then she put it back, unmarked, and blew out the candle, and lay in the dark listening to the house settle and the sea work the rocks below, and she thought: *not yet.* Not yet, because she didn't know where he was, and a telegram is a shout, and shouting in the dark tells the wolves exactly where you are. Not yet, because Vane's men were sleeping ten feet above her head and she was a bad liar in daylight and a worse conspirator under pressure, and if she moved wrong now she'd hang them both.

But she memorized the permit number. She'd always had a memory for numbers; it was how she'd kept the inn's books through two depressions without losing a penny. CASPER AMBERDALE, and the string of digits beneath, and the date of issue, and the clerk's initials. She laid them out in her mind like spoons in a drawer, each in its place, ready to be counted out when counting was needed.

Outside, the tide was coming in. It always came in. That was the whole lesson of the coast, if you lived on it long enough: whatever goes out comes back, and whatever the water takes, the water keeps, and the only wisdom is knowing which night the low tide falls and being standing on the right sand when it does.

Thursday next, the old woman had said. Low enough to show what wants showing.

Marta fell asleep near dawn with the permit paper still under her pillow, like a stone from a riverbed, smooth on one side and sharp on the other.

* * *

In the morning Vane's men ate eggs and asked nothing, which frightened her more than questions. They split up at the door with the unhurried competence of men following a plan drawn before they arrived — one up the valley road toward the old quarry, one along the north cliff path, and Vane himself standing a moment in her doorway, adjusting his grey gloves finger by finger.

"We may stay through the week," he said. "Tides are interesting on this coast, I'm told. Once in a generation, someone says."

"Do they," said Marta.

"They do." He settled his hat. "Keep the kettle hot, Mrs. Quill. And think about what I said — about being helpful. The map gets redrawn once. Only once."

She watched him go down the lane toward the shore path, the direction of the caves, the direction of the headland, walking at the pace of a man who already knew what he would find and was merely confirming the arithmetic.

Marta went inside and took the blank telegram form out of its oilcloth wrapping and laid it flat on the bar, and stared at it, and did not write on it yet.

But her hand was steady, and the number was in her head, and the tide, she knew, was going out.
