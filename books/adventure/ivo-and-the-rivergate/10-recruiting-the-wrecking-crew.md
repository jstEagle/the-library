## 10. Recruiting the Wrecking Crew

The labor office in Greywater smelled of coal smoke and old paper, and the man behind the counter looked at Casper the way men look at a bill they've already disputed.

"Rivergate survey," Casper said again. "I need two hands. Experienced ones."

"You need a miracle," the clerk said, not unkindly. "Every man who can hold a transit is already on Harrow's payroll. Vane signed forty of them last month." He slid a ledger across the counter, its pages thin with names crossed out in red ink. "What's left is what nobody else will take."

Casper ran his finger down the column of struck-through names until it stopped on one that wasn't struck at all — because it had never been entered. A card sat loose in the binding, yellowed at the edges, as if someone had pulled it out and pushed it back a dozen times.

**MALLEY, B. — powder work. Blacklisted.**

"What did she do?"

"She made a speech," the clerk said. "At the Kettle Creek yard. About the blasting caps that were coming through wet, and how somebody ought to say so before somebody's son came home in a bucket." He shrugged. "She said so. Loudly. With witnesses."

"And?"

"And now she's blacklisted from every line between here and the coast." The clerk tapped the card. "You want her, you take her whole. She comes with the blacklist attached."

Casper thought about trestles he'd refused to sign. About reputations, and what they weighed, and what they were worth when the mountain didn't care about either. "Where do I find her?"

* * *

He found her at the end of the fish-drying racks, north of the village, where the shacks leaned together like old men sharing a secret. The woman splitting cod on the third rack didn't look up when his boots crunched the gravel, but her knife paused — half a heartbeat, no more — and Casper understood she'd heard him coming and catalogued him already.

"You're the surveyor," she said. Her voice was low and even, the voice of someone who'd learned that shouting was a luxury. "Amberdale. Marta told me you'd come sniffing."

"Marta talks fast for an innkeeper."

"Marta talks careful. There's a difference." Brigid Malley straightened. She was perhaps forty, built like something forged rather than grown, with forearms scarred in pale ladders — burn scars, Casper guessed, the kind powder men collected the way sailors collected salt. Her hair was bound back in a strip of red cloth that might once have been a union ribbon. "You want dynamite work done."

"I want rock moved where I say, when I say, and nowhere else. And I want someone who can tell me honestly if I'm asking for something that'll kill us."

That got him a look — quick, appraising, gone again. "Most men hiring powder want the opposite. They want yes-men with steady hands."

"I had a career full of men who wanted my signature more than my opinion," Casper said. "I'm not interested in collecting any more of them."

Brigid set down her knife. Wiped her hands on her apron with the deliberateness of a woman buying time to think. "They say you wouldn't sign the Millbrook trestle. That you stood in front of the superintendent and called his steel rotten to his face."

"It was rotten. It fell anyway, three weeks after they fired me. Two section hands broke their backs."

"They say that too." She studied him the way she might study a fuse — checking the cut, the crimp, the length. "Here's what I am, Amberdale, so you can't say later you weren't warned. I talk back. I count charges twice and other people's arithmetic three times. If you tell me to fire a shot I think is wrong, I'll refuse, and I'll refuse loud, and if you sack me for it I'll walk up that mountain on my own two feet rather than apologize." She picked the knife back up. "And I haven't touched a charge in fourteen months, so my hands will be slow the first week. After that, there isn't a rock face on this coast I can't open like a letter."

"The first week we're charting, not blasting," Casper said. "You'll have time to remember your hands."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth. Not quite a smile. The place where a smile would live, kept swept and ready. "Pay?"

"Standard survey rate. Plus hazard, plus a bonus if we finish before snowfall."

"Plus," she said, "you put in writing that I was fired for telling the truth, and that you hired me knowing it. Not for me. For the next fool who makes a speech."

Casper took out his notebook and wrote it standing up, right there among the fish racks, while gulls screamed over their heads like a jury that couldn't reach a verdict. When he handed her the page she read it twice, folded it, and put it inside her shirt, against her skin.

"All right," Brigid Malley said. "I'm yours till the snow flies. God help us both."

* * *

Sefu Okonkwo was harder to find and easier to convince, which Casper would learn was the shape of most things worth having.

The bridge carpenter lived in a room above the cooperage, and Casper found him by following the sound of hammering that was too precise to be repair work. Through the window he saw a tall man building something small — a model, jointed and pegged, of a truss span so elegant it looked less like engineering than calligraphy. Every stick of it was scrap wood. Every joint was perfect.

When Casper knocked, Sefu opened the door on a chain, looked at him through the gap, and said, "You're limping slightly on your left. Long walk from the village. You're the Amberdale surveyor, and you want me, and you've already been to see Malley, because she's the only other name on the list and you strike me as a man who works a list in order."

Casper blinked. "How—"

"Marta told me yesterday you'd be along today, probably mid-morning, probably after Malley, because Malley's shack is on the way and you don't seem like a man who doubles back." He unlatched the chain and swung the door wide. "Come in. Mind the table. Everything on it is load-bearing."

The room was a cathedral of small things: models of bridges, sections of rail, a wall of notebooks bound in oilcloth, each spine dated. Casper lifted one and opened it at random and felt his breath catch. It was a record of every span Sefu had ever built or inspected — dimensions, timber sources, ironwork suppliers, the names of foundries stamped into every plate, cross-referenced in a hand so neat it could have been typeset.

"You memorize all this?"

"I remember everything," Sefu said simply. He lowered himself onto a stool with a particular care that Casper recognized — the choreography of a bad leg, learned so thoroughly it had become invisible. "It's not a talent. It's just that nothing leaves. Which sounds like a gift until you spend nine years remembering exactly how a bridge failed, and whose steel was in it, and who signed off anyway."

Casper set the notebook down gently. "Whose steel?"

"Harrow's." Sefu's eyes were dark and level and very still. "The Kestrel Viaduct. Eleven years ago. I built the falsework; I watched the span come down. They blamed my falsework. My leg was under it when it went, so I was in no condition to argue, and besides—" he smiled, and it was the saddest thing in the room, which was saying something, "—who argues with a cripple? The inquiry took my statement, thanked me, and buried it. But I remember the plates, Mr. Amberdale. I remember the foundry marks. I could draw them for you tomorrow, every one, because nothing leaves."

Casper sat down slowly on the one chair that wasn't holding anything up. Outside, the cooperage hammers rang their patient rhythm, and somewhere below a gull argued with the tide.

"Sefu," he said. "I'm going to tell you why I'm really here. Not the permit version. The real one."

He told him about the cave. The gauge-notches in the stone. The lantern with his father's initials. He told him about the map that ended mid-line, and the keeper's station he hadn't yet seen but now believed in with a certainty that frightened him. He watched Sefu listen the way masons listen to a story about stone — weighing every word for load.

When he finished, Sefu was quiet for a long moment. Then he reached to the wall, took down a notebook dated eleven years back, and opened it to a page near the middle.

"Kestrel Viaduct," he said. "Ironwork supplied by Harrow Consolidated, rolled at their Meridian works. Here are the plate stamps. And here—" his finger moved down the page, stopped, tapped twice, "—is the name of the site engineer who accepted the shipment without testing it. Read it."

Casper read it. His mouth went dry.

"Aldous Vane," he said.

"Aldous Vane," Sefu agreed. "Who was a junior engineer then, and is a chief surveyor now, and has never once in eleven years been asked about it, because everyone who could ask was paid not to." He closed the book and held it against his chest like a shield, or a weapon being decided upon. "So. You want a carpenter with a bad leg and a good memory on your survey of a closed line through country Harrow wants forgotten."

"Yes."

"Then you have one." Sefu stood, and this time he didn't hide the effort, and Casper understood that was its own kind of trust. "But I build true, Mr. Amberdale. Whatever we cross this mountain on, it holds or it doesn't, and I'll tell you which before we step on it, even if the answer stops the job. That's the whole of my terms."

"That's not terms," Casper said. "That's the job description."

* * *

Marta's inn was nearly empty at the supper hour — fishing season dying, travelers rarer than honest weather — which meant the long table by the fire belonged to them, and the stew was generous, and the bread was still warm enough to steam when Brigid tore it.

Three strangers sat down to eat.

For a while nobody talked. Casper was tired in the marrow-deep way that comes from a day spent persuading people to bet their lives on him, and Brigid ate like a woman who'd learned never to waste a meal, and Sefu arranged his plate with geometric precision before touching it. The fire cracked. Rain started against the windows, soft at first, then committed.

It was Brigid who broke first, because Brigid always would — Casper could see that already, could see it would be both the trial and the salvation of them.

"Fourteen months," she said, to her bowl as much as anyone. "You know what I've been doing? Fish. Mending nets. Being polite." She said the word *polite* like it was a disease she'd recovered from. "There was a morning last spring when I caught myself apologizing to a gull. An actual apology. To a bird." She looked up. "Today I held a piece of det cord for the first time since Kettle Creek and my hands remembered everything in about four seconds. Fourteen months of politeness, gone in four seconds. I nearly laughed out loud on the beach."

"What did the gull say?" Sefu asked gravely.

Brigid pointed her spoon at him. "Don't. I liked you better when you were quiet."

"No, you didn't," Sefu said. "Nobody does. It's a documented problem."

And Casper laughed — a short, surprised sound, torn out of him before he could vet it — and the laugh changed the temperature of the table. He saw it land on both their faces: the flicker of recognition, the almost-embarrassment, the way three people who have just discovered they can make each other laugh all pretend they haven't noticed yet.

"My turn, is it?" Sefu turned his plate ninety degrees, aligning it with the table edge. "Eleven years ago I could walk twenty miles of rough grade in a day. Now I plan my routes around stairs. Do you know what the industry does with a builder who can't climb? Nothing. That's the cruelty of it. No hearing, no blacklist, no speech required. It simply stops seeing you. I sat in that room up there for eleven years building bridges out of kindling that will never carry anything, and telling myself it was practice." He looked at the fire. "It wasn't practice. It was waiting. I just didn't know what for."

"For a surveyor with a limp of his own," Brigid said.

"I beg your pardon?"

"You favor the left," she said. "Same as him. You two are going to argue about whose knee predicts rain more accurately, I can see it from here, and I want it noted now that I want no part of it."

"We'll settle it empirically," Sefu said.

"Oh, worse. An empirical argument. Between two men who keep records." She groaned and reached for the bread. "This is what I get. This is what happens when you make speeches."

Casper found himself smiling into his stew, and beneath the smile, something was quietly rearranging itself — some load shifting onto new bearings. He had walked into Greywater alone with a dead man's map and a clock ticking in his chest, and he had told himself that was manageable, that surveys were done by solitary men with instruments and nerve. Sitting here, listening to a powder monkey and a crippled carpenter bicker about knees across a shared loaf, he understood he'd been lying to himself the way the industry had lied to all of them: politely, and with paperwork.

A crew wasn't a roster. It was this. Bread, fire, rain, and the slow discovery that the people across the table had been broken in places that matched yours.

"So," Brigid said, wiping her bowl with the last crust. "Tomorrow. The cave. What do we actually know, and what are we pretending to know? I like to keep those lists separate. It's saved my life maybe four times."

Casper laid his father's map on the table between the bowls, weighting the corners with salt cellars and a spoon. The firelight made the old ink lines look freshly drawn, and the gap where the gorge should be looked like a wound in the paper.

"We know this much," he said, and began to tell them.

He was halfway through — at the part about the gauge-notches, the ones cut into living stone to a width no company on record ever used — when the inn door banged open behind them, letting in a blade of cold air and the smell of wet wool.

Three men in oilskins stood dripping in the entryway. Their boots were wrong for fishermen, too heavy, too new. The one in front was lean and gray-eyed and carried himself with the unhurried confidence of a man who had never once needed to hurry, and when his gaze swept the room it passed over the fire, over Marta frozen behind her bar, and settled — briefly, precisely, like a transit finding its mark — on the map spread out on Casper's table.

"Well now," the man said pleasantly. "An Amberdale. I was told you'd washed up here."

Behind Casper, very quietly, Sefu closed his notebook. Beside him, Brigid's hand drifted off the table and came to rest, casually, on the canvas roll leaning against her chair — the one that clinked, faintly, when it moved.

The gray-eyed man smiled, and it was the smile of someone reading fine print he intended to enforce.

"Aldous Vane," he said. "Harrow Line. I wonder — might I buy you gentlemen a drink?"
