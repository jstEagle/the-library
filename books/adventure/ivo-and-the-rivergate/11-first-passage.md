## 11. First Passage

The tide gave them three hours, and Ivo meant to spend every one of them learning whether these land-people could be trusted with the inside of the mountain.

He watched them from the shadow of the second gallery while they assembled at the gate mouth with their lamps and their rope and their loud careful voices. Four of them now. The engineer, Casper, who moved like a man reading a page even when the page was stone. The woman, Brigid, who smelled of black powder and woodsmoke and kept touching her coat pocket as if checking on a sleeping animal. The carpenter, Sefu, whose bad leg made the shingle stones chatter under his boot but whose eyes never stopped moving, cataloguing everything — the struts of old timber bracing the entrance, the waterline stains on the walls, the notches cut in gauge along the lower seam. And behind them all, Ivo himself, half a turn back, close enough to catch them if they fell, far enough to run.

That was how you walked with strangers. That was how the keeper had taught him without ever saying it: *never give them your back until they've earned it, and never expect them to earn it fast.*

"Rules," Ivo said, and the word came out rusted. He didn't use many words when the crew was listening. Words were for the ledger and for Casper, sometimes, in the lamplight after supper. "You walk where I walk. You stop where I stop. If I say *back*, you go back. If I say *down*, everyone down flat, no questions."

"And if we don't like your rules?" Brigid asked. She wasn't hostile. She was testing him the way he was testing her, and some animal part of Ivo respected that.

"Then you can go out the way the water comes in," Ivo said. "At high tide."

Sefu laughed — a short, surprised bark — and Brigid grinned, and Casper said quietly, "We'll follow your rules, Ivo." And that was that. That was the strange thing about this crew, the thing Ivo still hadn't gotten used to: when someone said a thing, the others acted as if the thing were true.

He turned and led them into the dark.

* * *

The upper galleries were Ivo's country the way the shore was the gulls'. He knew them by more than sight — by the taste of the air, which changed at every junction, salt giving way to cold stone giving way to the faint mineral bite of the deep seep; by the sound of footsteps, each passage returning its own particular echo, so that he could tell where he was blindfolded by the shape of his own breathing; by touch, the walls worn smooth in places where the keeper's hand had trailed for twenty years, rough where no one had ever gone.

He also knew where the wrong turns were. Every gallery had them — passages that looked promising and went nowhere, or worse than nowhere. And this morning, moving through the grey half-light of the first chamber with its ceiling lost somewhere above the lamp reach, Ivo made a decision he did not fully understand himself until later:

He would test them. Not with questions. With turns.

The keeper had done it to him once, years ago, when Ivo had first followed him past the threshold and refused to leave. The old man had walked into the third gallery and taken the left fork instead of the right, and Ivo, proud and certain, had said nothing, and they had ended up in the dead crawl above the tidal slot with the sea booming under them like a heart in a giant's chest, and the keeper had looked at him and said, "You saw me take the wrong way and let me take it. Why?"

And Ivo had said, because he was small and honest before he learned to be small and careful, "Because you're the keeper."

"No," the old man had said. "Because you'd rather be polite than be right. In here, boy, polite gets people drowned. Next time, tell me I'm wrong."

So Ivo took the left fork.

It was a good wrong turn — plausible, wide enough for four abreast, sloping gently downward the way a person would want a route to slope. He heard them behind him: boots, breath, the clink of Sefu's tool roll. He counted forty paces, fifty, sixty. The passage narrowed. The air thickened, went stale, dead — no current, no sea-sound threading through it.

Seventy paces. Eighty.

Nothing.

Ivo stopped and turned. They stood in the lamp glow looking at him, waiting. Waiting. Not one of them had said a word.

His chest did something complicated. "You knew," he said. It came out almost angry, though he wasn't angry, he didn't know what he was.

Casper met his eyes. "Knew what?"

"This is wrong. It goes to a dead crawl. You knew." He pointed back the way they'd come. "Back there, the right fork has air moving through it. You can hear the sea in it. This one has nothing. It's been nothing for a hundred years. You *knew* and you let me walk us into it."

"I didn't know," Casper said. "Not about forks, not about air. I've never been in here. I don't know anything about this place that you haven't shown me."

"Then why didn't you say something? Anything?"

"Because I had nothing true to say." Casper held up one hand, ticking fingers. "I could have guessed. I could have flattered you — told you I trusted your judgment completely, that I felt good about the left fork. But I didn't feel anything about the left fork. I know rails and loads and what rotten timber sounds like under a hammer. I don't know caves. If I pretended otherwise, you'd start believing my guesses, and someday one of my guesses would kill us." He lowered his hand. "You're the only one in this company who knows this mountain. My job isn't to seem like I do. My job is to know exactly what I don't know, and ask."

The silence after that was long. Somewhere below them, muffled through fathoms of stone, the tide worked at its endless arithmetic.

Brigid broke it, dry as tinder: "Well. That's the first time I've heard an engineer admit ignorance without being drunk first."

Sefu snorted. "First time I've heard anyone admit it at all. Usually they just promote their guess to a fact and put it in a report."

Ivo looked at Casper for a long moment. Something in his chest was loosening, reluctantly, like a knot worked free by patient fingers. The men from before — the ones in his memory, the ones in Harrow coats — had never once said *I don't know*. They had come into the mountain certain of everything, certain enough to seal a man behind stone, and certainty was the thing Ivo feared most in the world, more than the deep gate, more than the flooded galleries.

This man was uncertain. Loudly, deliberately uncertain. It might be a trick. It probably wasn't.

"The right fork," Ivo said finally. "Stay between the white marks. Some of the floor is shell sand over void. Step where I step."

* * *

He tested them twice more that morning, and both times the pattern held.

The second wrong turn was subtler — a fork where both passages breathed, but the left one breathed too easily, a through-draft that meant it opened somewhere onto the cliff face, which in winter meant wind and ice and a drop. Ivo took it without hesitation, walking fast, daring someone to object. Sefu lasted thirty paces.

"Beg pardon," the carpenter said, in the tone of a man apologizing to a foreman he liked, "but that draft's got teeth in it. Air that moves that free in a cave means a hole to outside, and holes to outside in October mean weather. I'd want to know where it goes before I marry it."

Ivo stopped. Turned. Stared at him.

"It goes to the cliff face," he admitted. "Forty feet up. There's a ledge. In summer it's a shortcut. In winter it kills people."

"There you are, then," Sefu said comfortably, and waited.

"You *smelled* it?" Ivo couldn't keep the wonder out of his voice.

"Fifty years of bridges," Sefu said, tapping his temple. "Wind tells you where a structure's open. Same as water tells you where it's rotten. Everything talks if you know its language." He shrugged. "I don't speak cave. But I speak draft."

Ivo led them back to the right-hand passage. His mind was working fast now, rearranging itself around a new idea: that these people were ignorant of his country but not stupid in it, that ignorance and foolishness were different animals, and that a crew full of people who spoke their own languages honestly — draft, powder, load, rail — might actually be worth leading.

The third test was the cruelest, and he did it without quite deciding to. Near the Gallery of Bells — so named by the keeper for the dripstones that rang faintly when the wind found them — the passage split three ways, and Ivo, tired now, hungry, wanting the morning to be over, took the middle path and then simply stopped walking and stood there, silent, lamp low, watching what they would do with an unmoving guide.

They nearly failed it. Brigid wanted to push on — "kid's resting, we can scout ahead" — and Casper overruled her, but softly, and there was a friction there, two strong wills rubbing, and Ivo crouched in the dark listening to his crew argue about him in low voices and felt the old loneliness rise up in his throat like bile. *They'll decide things about you. They'll always decide things about you. You're the river's boy, you're the wild child, you're the problem to be managed—*

But then Casper said, "No. We wait. He stops for a reason. Every signal he's given us so far has been true, and I'm not going to spend the capital he's built on a guess about why he's standing still." A pause. "Also — Ivo. If you're testing us, I'd rather you told me. Not because I mind. Because I'd rather know the rules of the game I'm losing."

Ivo stood up out of the dark.

"How did you—" he started, and stopped, and started again. "The keeper used to test me. Wrong turns. He said polite people drown." He looked at his boots. "I wanted to see if you were polite."

"And?"

"You're not," Ivo said, and it was the closest thing to a blessing he had ever given anyone, and he saw Casper understand that, saw it land in the man's face like rain on cracked ground.

* * *

They came up out of the upper galleries near noon, into the great vault the keeper had called the Cathedral — a chamber so tall their lamps couldn't find its roof, where the sea had carved arches like the ribs of a drowned church and the light came down in one pale column through a vent a hundred feet overhead, falling on a floor of white shell sand. Even the crew went quiet. Even Brigid, who Ivo suspected feared nothing on earth, stood with her head tipped back and her mouth slightly open.

"Lord," Sefu murmured. "Fifty years driving spikes, and I never once suspected there was a room like this under anybody's mountain."

"It's not under anybody's," Ivo said. "It's under nobody's. That's the point of it."

Casper turned slowly, and Ivo watched him do what he did — read the chamber like a document. His eyes went to the old drill scars in the walls, to the rusted iron rings set at intervals, to the ghost-line of a bench cut into the rock at rail height, running off into the dark on the experimental gauge. Then to the far wall, where a smaller opening breathed cool air from the deeper galleries.

"That passage," Casper said. "Where does it go?"

Ivo felt the morning's warmth contract suddenly in his chest. Because he knew that passage. He knew it the way you know a door in your own house that's always been locked — the way you know it by avoiding it.

"That's the way down," he said. "Toward the lower galleries. Toward the deep gate."

"Can it be charted?"

"Maybe." The word tasted like cold iron. "Nobody's charted it. The keeper wouldn't go down there. At the end, he wouldn't go." Ivo hesitated, and then, because the morning had taught him something about these people, he gave them the truth whole: "There's a ledger entry. The last one. It stops in the middle of a sentence. After that, the keeper never came back to the station, and I kept the lamps filled alone, and I never went down that passage either. Three years. I feed the lamp at the top of those stairs and I don't go past it."

The pale column of light fell between them. Casper looked at the dark opening for a long time, and Ivo watched his face and understood that the man was thinking about his father, though he said nothing of fathers.

"We don't have to go today," Casper said at last. "Today we chart what we've walked. Today we build trust with our legs." He glanced at Ivo. "But eventually the survey has to go down there, doesn't it? The route doesn't work unless the deep gate works."

"Yes," Ivo said. "Eventually."

He did not say: *and the last keeper's sentence is still unfinished down there, and I am afraid of it, and I think you are too.* He did not need to say it. He was beginning to learn that this crew could hear the things a person didn't say, if the person stood still long enough.

Brigid broke the spell, practical as a hammer: "Then let's get today's numbers while the light holds. Boy — which way out of your cathedral?"

Ivo looked at her, and at Sefu, and at Casper, standing in the fallen column of light with his father's map already unrolled against his knee, and he felt the strange new thing settle into place in his chest, heavy and warm at once, like a lamp being filled.

"Follow me," he said. "And this time, if I take a wrong turn — *say so.*"

Sefu grinned. "Oh, we will."

They started toward the far passage together, four sets of footsteps finding a single rhythm on the shell sand, and behind them the Cathedral held its silence, and ahead the dark of the lower galleries waited — patient, cold, and keeping, at the end of an unfinished sentence, the truth about what had happened to the last keeper of the Rivergate.
