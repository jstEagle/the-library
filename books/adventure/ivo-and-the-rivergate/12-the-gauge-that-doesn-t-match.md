## 12. The Gauge That Doesn't Match

The passage narrowed for the better part of an hour, and then, without warning, it opened like a held breath released.

Casper felt the change before he saw it — a shift in the air against his face, the way sound stopped crowding in from all sides and began to travel. Ivo had gone ahead with his lamp held low, the way he always did when the ground was about to speak to him, and now the boy's light swung wide, sweeping across a space too large for the beam to fill.

"Careful here," Ivo called back, his voice arriving strangely flattened, as though the room itself were absorbing it. "Floor drops. Old ballast. Don't trust your eyes — trust your boots."

They came down a slope of broken stone into what Casper's mind refused, at first, to name. His profession named it for him whether he willed it or not: a cut. A rail cut, hewn through living rock, twenty feet high at its crown and running dead straight into the dark ahead of them until the lamplight gave out. The walls bore drill scars in regular rows. The floor was graded, cambered, drained by a ditch cut along its eastern side that still ran clear and cold over smooth stones.

It was a tunnel. Not a cave wearing the shape of one — a *built* thing. Surveyed, blasted, ballasted, finished.

Brigid came up beside him, her dynamite satchel creaking, and let out a low whistle that the dark swallowed whole. "Well," she said. "Somebody's been busy."

Sefu leaned on his good leg, his bad one braced against a boulder, and said nothing at all. Casper glanced at him and saw the carpenter's head tilted, listening to the space the way another man might read a page.

"How far does it go?" Casper asked.

Ivo shrugged, which from the boy was an answer of a particular kind. He only shrugged about things he knew perfectly well. "Longer than my galleries," he said. "I don't go past the third bend. The echo changes there." He said it without drama, the way he'd say *the tide comes in*, but Casper had learned to hear what lived underneath the boy's flatness. The echo changed. And whatever changed it had kept Ivo out.

"We'll go to the third bend," Casper said. "No further. Today."

Ivo looked at him for a long moment, lamp raised, and Casper watched the boy perform the arithmetic he performed on every adult who entered his territory: what did this one want, how much would he push, could he be turned if he had to be turned. Then Ivo nodded once and started walking, and the crew followed him down the throat of the mountain.

* * *

They walked through engineering.

That was the phrase that kept rising in Casper's chest as they went, mile upon measured mile of it, and he could not have stopped the thought any more than he could have stopped his own pulse. Every element of the cut spoke of a hand that had known exactly what it was doing. The grade never exceeded two percent — he checked it three times against his clinometer because he did not believe it, and three times the instrument told him the same impossible, beautiful truth. The drainage ditches ran true. The timbering, where the rock required it, was set in pairs at precise intervals, adzed smooth, pegged rather than nailed, and seasoned so well that after — he counted the rings on a broken offcut, and stopped counting, and stood very still for a moment — after better than thirty years, it had not rotted.

Thirty years. The numbers assembled themselves unbidden, the way they always did, and Casper let them: thirty years ago his father had been a young engineer with a reputation and a crew and a contract on the Rivergate corridor. Thirty years ago the viaduct had fallen and taken eleven men with it, and the line had been closed, and the corridor had been struck from every charter and map in the province. Thirty years ago, give or take a season, someone had driven a rail line through the heart of the range that appeared on no survey, no ledger, no drawing office record anywhere in the dominion.

And not a rough track. Not a smuggler's tramway. This was work of a quality that would have made the chief engineers of the Harrow Line weep with envy.

"He built this," Casper said aloud, before he knew he meant to speak.

Ivo stopped. "Who?"

"My father." Casper's voice sounded strange to him, thinned by the stone. "Or his crew. Someone with his hands."

"The keeper didn't build things," Ivo said, with the mild certainty of a boy correcting a guest's manners. "The keeper *kept* things. He said so. He wrote it in the book." He resumed walking, and the conversation was, as far as Ivo was concerned, concluded.

But Casper walked the next half mile with his father's name lodged in his throat like a fishbone. *Kept.* The word would not sit still. A man keeps a station, a ledger, a lamp. A man does not keep forty miles of secret railroad unless the railroad is something that must be kept *from* someone.

* * *

At the third bend, the echo changed.

Casper understood why within ten steps. The cut opened onto a gallery so vast that their four lamps together could not find its ceiling; the beam simply climbed and failed, and above the failing light there was only blackness that seemed less like absence than like weight. Water dripped somewhere far off with the regularity of a clock. Underfoot, the ballast gave way to something smoother, and Casper knelt, and brushed away a film of mineral dust with his sleeve, and his heart stopped.

Steel.

A rail. Running straight as a sermon into the dark, both rails, set in ties of blackened oak, the whole of it furred with rust but sound beneath — he struck it with his knuckle and heard the long clean bell-note of healthy metal, not the dull dead knock of corrosion eating through.

He should have stood up then. He should have done what any surveyor in the province would have done: paced the gauge, logged it, moved on. Instead he knelt there with his palm flat on the cold steel and felt, absurdly, that he was touching a wrist. Checking for a pulse.

"Sefu," he said. "Tape."

Sefu came hobbling, unrolled the linen tape with the economy of long habit, and hooked it inside the left railhead. He drew it across. He looked at the number. He looked again.

"That's wrong," Sefu said quietly.

"What is it?" Brigid asked.

Sefu read it out anyway, because Sefu's memory was the kind that could not leave a number unread: "Four feet, nine and a half inches." He said it twice more, slower, tasting it. "Standard gauge is four-eight-and-a-half. Broad gauges run five foot or wider. Narrow runs three-six or less. Four-nine-and-a-half—" He shook his head. "There is no company on this continent, on *any* continent I've ever heard tell of, that lays four-foot-nine-and-a-half."

Casper took the tape himself and measured it again, because the number demanded to be disbelieved. Four feet, nine and a half inches. An inch wider than standard. One inch. Not enough to be a different class of railway. Far too much to be an accident — no crew in history had drifted an inch per rail by carelessness over miles of cut. He walked fifty yards and measured again. The same. He walked back and measured a third time, and the same, and somewhere in the repetition of it the disbelief burned off and left something else standing in its place, hot and bright and slightly terrifying.

Deliberation. This gauge was chosen. Chosen by someone who understood exactly what standard gauge was for — interoperability, the sharing of rolling stock, the quiet consolidation of power in the hands of whoever owned the trunk lines — and had decided, deliberately, to build a railway that could never be absorbed, never be bought, never be quietly swallowed into somebody else's empire. A railway that would have to stand or fall on its own.

"It's a declaration," Casper heard himself say. "Whoever laid this wasn't hiding a shortcut. He was founding a company."

"In a hole in a mountain," Brigid said. "With no charter, no maps, no rolling stock I've seen yet."

"The maps exist," Casper said. "I've been carrying half of one since I was nineteen years old." He told them then — quickly, roughly, because the words wanted to come fast — about the gap in his father's old survey map, the place where the Rivergate gorge should have been drawn and instead the ink simply stopped, mid-line, as if the pen had lifted and never come down. He had thought for years it was grief. Now he wondered if it was discretion. If the missing section hadn't been lost at all, but withheld — drawn once, copied once, and hidden where only a keeper could keep it.

"And the viaduct fell," Sefu said slowly, "and eleven men went into the gorge, and the province closed the corridor, and nobody ever asked why the dead man's private railroad ran an inch wider than everyone else's."

"Nobody asked," Casper said, "because asking would have meant admitting it existed."

The drip of water counted seconds in the dark. Brigid crouched and put her hand flat on the tie, reading the grain the way she read everything, and said, "This oak's older than me. They weren't building this *after* the collapse. They were building it before. Maybe while the main line was still running." She looked up at Casper, and her face in the lamplight had gone very still. "Which means the viaduct and this passage aren't two stories. They're one story."

Casper felt the thought land in his chest like a dropped tool. His father's crew had died building a viaduct across the gorge — the official line, the chartered line, the line whose collapse had ended everything. And all the while, under the mountain, out of sight of every inspector and shareholder and rival in the province, the same hands had been laying a second road. A road that answered to no one.

Why? What had his father known — what had he *feared* — that made a secret railway seem like prudence?

He had no answer. But for the first time since Greywater, since the clerk's brittle warning, since the tide had peeled back the cliff and shown him the lantern stamped with his own name, Casper felt something move in him that was not dread and not grief. It was older and simpler than either. It was the feeling he'd had at seven years old, holding a spirit level for the first time while his father watched: the certainty that somewhere under all the noise and rot of the world there was a true line, and that finding it was the only work worth doing.

"This isn't just a way to finish the survey," he said. "This is the survey. Forty miles of collapsed charter, and underneath it a working road that predates the cover-up. If I chart this — grades, gauge, timbering dates, all of it — it's not evidence for a permit renewal. It's evidence, full stop. For everything."

"You're talking about digging up the dead," Brigid said. There was no judgment in it. She was, Casper realized, checking his nerve.

"I'm talking about letting them testify."

Sefu laughed — short, surprised out of him — and then grew serious in the way that only Sefu became serious, folding his arms over his knee. "Then you'll want the measurements exact," he said. "Because exact is the only language a hearing understands. And you'll want them witnessed. All four of us sign the log. Dates, positions, instruments used. No man's word alone carries a mountain."

Ivo had been standing apart through all of this, at the edge of the lamplight, where the boy always stood when the adults were saying things too large for him to want. Now he came forward a few steps, and his voice, when it came, was careful.

"The keeper had instruments," he said. "Better ones than yours. In the station. He showed me where they were, before—" The boy stopped. He did that, Casper had noticed; he arrived at the edge of certain sentences and stepped back from them as from a drop. "Before he stopped coming back. He said, *when the engineers come, show them the transit.*"

The silence that followed had texture. Casper heard his own blood in it.

"When the engineers come," he repeated.

Ivo nodded once, matter-of-fact, and turned to lead them back the way they had come, and Casper stood a moment longer with his boot on the tie, his hand on the rail, the strange gauge humming faintly under his palm like a struck string that would not quite stop ringing.

An inch. One deliberate inch between his father's road and every other road in the world. Enough to keep it free. Enough, perhaps, to keep it *his* — and enough, Casper thought, following the boy's lamp back up the cut, for someone else entirely to have wanted it buried.

Behind them, in the vast dark of the gallery, the water dripped on, keeping time.

And far ahead, deeper than the third bend, deeper than Ivo had ever dared go, something shifted in the mountain — a soft settling of stone, like a man turning over in his sleep — and none of them heard it at all.
