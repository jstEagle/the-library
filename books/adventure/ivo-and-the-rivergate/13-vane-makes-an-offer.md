## 13. Vane Makes an Offer

The storm came in off the water two days after the first passage, and with it came Aldous Vane.

Casper saw him before he heard him — that was the kind of man Vane was, the kind who arrived already inside your sightline, occupying it as though he'd always been there. Casper stood at the window of Marta's inn, watching rain stitch the harbor grey, and there in the glass, superimposed over the fishing boats and the drowned light, was a figure in a city coat walking up the mud street with the unhurried gait of a man who had never once been late for anything or early for anything either.

He knew the face from the provincial gazettes and from ten years of railway gossip: Aldous Vane, chief surveyor of the Harrow Line, thirty years in the service of the company his father had helped charter. A narrow man, dry as a ledger page, with silver at his temples arranged so precisely it looked surveyed. He carried no instrument case. He carried nothing at all except an umbrella he hadn't opened, because men like Vane did not flinch from weather; they simply noted it for the record.

Casper's hand went to the window frame and gripped it.

Below, Vane stopped at the inn door, shook the rain from his hat brim with two economical motions, and went inside. Casper counted to twenty. His heart counted faster.

* * *

Marta met Vane in the common room with her arms folded and her chin set at the angle she reserved for tax collectors and men who ordered oysters out of season. Casper came down the stairs slowly, giving himself the length of the staircase to decide what kind of conversation this would be — and understanding, by the time his boot touched the last tread, that the decision was not his to make. It never had been. Vane had crossed forty miles of bad road in the first storm of the season to find him. That was not a negotiation arriving. That was a verdict.

"Mr. Amberdale." Vane pronounced the name carefully, the way a man handles a document he intends to quote later. "You're harder to find than your reputation suggests."

"My reputation is easy to find," Casper said. "It's been posted in every hiring office on the coast line."

"Yes." Vane smiled without warmth, the smile of a man acknowledging a chess move rather than an insult. "The trestle business. I read the file. Fascinating reading. You refused to certify a structure rated for twelve tons that your own figures showed carrying fourteen."

"The figures showed it carrying fourteen *once*," Casper said. "And then not again."

"And Harrow's engineers reviewed your review and found it conservative."

"Harrow's engineers found what they were paid to find. We've both read that file too, I expect."

For a moment something moved behind Vane's eyes — not anger. Recognition, maybe. The look of one professional meeting another across a table where all the money sat on one side. Then it was gone, filed away, and Vane gestured to the corner table by the cold hearth with the courtesy of a host in a room that wasn't his.

"May I buy you a drink, Mr. Amberdale? Or dinner? Marta tells me the mutton is honest, if nothing else here is."

"Marta tells you nothing," said Marta, from behind the bar, "because I tell you nothing. And the mutton's lamb, and it's better than you deserve."

Vane inclined his head to her as if accepting a compliment. Casper almost laughed. Almost. There was something steadying about Marta's hostility, something that reminded him which side of the ledger he stood on. He sat down across from Vane because refusing to sit would be a performance, and performances were Vane's native country. Better to stand on his own ground, even if his own ground was a chair in an inn that smelled of lamp oil and wet wool.

"No drink," Casper said. "Say what you came to say."

* * *

What Vane came to say, it turned out, took a long time to say, because Vane was a man who built his approaches the way railroads were built — grade by grade, cut by cut, each sentence laid on ballast so the final offer arrived feeling inevitable.

He began with the weather. He observed that the passes north of Greywater closed early this year, that the provincial office had already logged frost at the summit stations, that the concession corridor would revert to Harrow's charter at first snow as surely as water ran downhill. He said this not as threat but as geography, as if the snow itself were a neutral party whose testimony could not be disputed.

He moved on to history. He spoke of the Rivergate lines — closed now eleven years, a wound in the company's balance sheet that successive boards had preferred to bandage with silence. He spoke of the collapse of the viaduct with a smoothness that made Casper's jaw ache, calling it "the regrettable incident," as though steel had failed of its own accord, as though mountains simply reached up sometimes and took nineteen men for reasons no one could name.

"Nineteen men," Casper said quietly.

"I beg your pardon?"

"The regrettable incident had names. I can recite them, if it helps your survey. My father taught them to me. He said a crew isn't a line item."

Something flickered across Vane's face then — the first true thing Casper had seen in it — and was smoothed away so fast that afterward Casper wondered whether he'd imagined it. "I'm not here to litigate the past, Mr. Amberdale. I'm here to discuss the future. Yours specifically."

"There it is," Casper said. "Forty minutes of scenery and finally the tunnel."

Vane opened his coat — a small theatrical gesture, deliberate as everything else about him — and drew out a leather folio. He did not open it. He set it on the table between them, square with the table's edge, and rested two fingers upon it.

"You've found something in the mountain," Vane said. "Don't insult us both by denying it. Your crew bought rope, pit candles, and blasting caps in quantities that suggest more than a surface survey. Your carpenter has been asking about timber spans. And three days ago a boy was seen on the cliffs at low tide, near the caves, carrying a lantern." He paused. "A lantern, Mr. Amberdale. In a corridor that has been sealed to traffic for eleven years."

Casper kept his face still. It cost him. Somewhere behind his ribs, the thought *they've been watching since the shore* turned over like a stone in surf.

"Greywater is a village of forty people," he said. "Everyone watches everyone. It doesn't mean what you think it means."

"It means exactly what I think it means, because I have spent thirty years learning what things mean, and it is the only part of this profession I have ever truly mastered." Vane leaned forward, and for the first time his voice dropped its public register and became something quieter, almost intimate, which was somehow worse. "So let me save us both the theater. Harrow will pay you for your permits. Both of them. Full value plus a premium for your time — say, four hundred dollars, cash, today. You sign a release assigning any findings, surveys, charts, or discoveries within the corridor to Harrow Line interests, and you take the next boat south. In exchange, whatever is inside that mountain becomes Harrow's to develop, or Harrow's to leave undeveloped, as the board sees fit. Which is a polite way of saying it disappears."

"And my father's name along with it."

The words came out of Casper flat and cold, and he watched them land. Vane didn't flinch. That was the terrible thing — he didn't flinch at all.

"Your father," Vane said, "was a fine engineer with a talent for finding problems, and the misfortune of finding this one." He tapped the folio once. "There are men on the current board who were junior officers when the viaduct went down. They have spent their entire careers making certain the question of that steel was never asked in a room with a stenographer in it. You are proposing, whether you know it or not, to ask it. And the answer, Mr. Amberdale, is worth more than four hundred dollars to them. It is worth everything. Which is why I am authorized to go higher, if the premium of your cooperation requires it. Name a figure."

"The figure is a tribunal," Casper said. "With a stenographer in it."

Vane exhaled through his nose — not quite a laugh, but the ghost of one, the sound of a man watching a card he'd predicted come up. "Yes. I told them you'd say that."

"*Them.*"

"The board. The committee. Call them what you like." Vane finally opened the folio, and Casper saw that the paper inside was already drawn up — his own name printed in the assignee's blank, the permits' numbers already entered in a fair clerk's hand. Numbers Casper had given to exactly one brittle man in a provincial office, behind a door marked Records. The sight of them went through him like a spike through a tie. Someone had sold those numbers, or copied them, or never locked the drawer at all. The clock in his chest, which had been ticking since the day the clerk warned him about first snow, seemed suddenly very loud.

"You see the difficulty," Vane went on, mild as milk. "You imagine yourself racing me to the snowline. But I don't need to race you. I need only to arrive after you, holding papers that say everything under that mountain belongs to Harrow. Every chart you draw, every gauge you measure, every testimony you gather — it becomes our property the moment we file, and your word against a corporation's thereafter. You've seen how that arithmetic goes. You lived it on the trestle."

"So why buy me at all? If the arithmetic is settled, why cross a mountain range in a storm?"

"Because," Vane said, and here he did something Casper had not expected. He closed the folio, aligned it with the table's edge again, and looked at Casper with an expression stripped of all its varnish — tired, almost, and old. "Because I read the trestle file twice. Because your father's inspection notes on the viaduct were the cleanest engineering documents I have ever seen, and I have seen thousands. And because a man who refuses four hundred dollars is a man who cannot be bought — and a man who cannot be bought is a man who must be *handled*, and I have grown tired, Mr. Amberdale, of handling things." He rose, buttoning his coat. "The offer stands until the snow flies. After that, the corridor reverts, and what happens inside it stops being a negotiation and becomes an operation. I would prefer the negotiation. For both our sakes."

* * *

Casper walked him to the door. He couldn't have said why, except that some animal instinct told him it was safer to keep Vane in front of him than at his back.

Rain had softened to a fine drifting mist. Down the street, two men waited beneath the eave of the chandlery, wearing the flat nondescript coats of company men, and they fell in behind Vane without a word, the way tools follow a hand. Vane put on his hat. At the edge of the lamplight he paused and half-turned, and the smile he gave Casper then was the worst thing Casper had seen all day — worse than the folio, worse than the permit numbers in a stranger's hand. It was a small, satisfied, patient smile. The smile of a man who had asked a question he already knew the answer to, not because he needed the answer, but because the answer itself was data. *Refusal confirmed. Subject unbuyable. Proceed to contingency.*

"Give my regards to your crew," Vane said. "The blaster, the carpenter, and the boy. Do give particular thought to the boy. Children complicate operations terribly. So much harder to account for them afterward."

Then he was gone into the mist, and the street swallowed him, and Casper stood in the doorway of Marta's inn with the cold coming off the water and dread settling into him the way silt settles in a harbor — gradually, and then all at once, and then everywhere.

"Casper." Marta's voice, low, from just behind his shoulder. She had followed him to the door, and her hands were wringing her apron, and her face was grey. "That man asked me questions yesterday. Before you came down. About your boat, your stores, which cove you launch from. I told him nothing, but I—" Her voice caught. "I laughed at his jokes. God forgive me, I laughed, because that's what you do with gentlemen, and he wrote something down when I did."

Casper covered her hand with his own. "Marta. Listen to me. Whatever he asks you, whoever comes after him — you tell them the truth about yourself and lies about us, in whichever order keeps you safe. Do you understand? Nothing we're doing is worth your skin."

"And what is it you're doing?" she asked, searching his face. "That makes a man like that cross a mountain in October?"

Casper looked out at the water, at the place beyond the point where the cliffs dropped and the tide ran out and the sea pulled back its curtain every few weeks to show the mouth of the Tollgate. Somewhere behind that stone lay a rusted lantern with his father's initials, ledgers full of a dead man's careful hand, rails of a gauge no company owned, and a boy who called himself the Gate.

"He thinks I'm surveying a shortcut," Casper said. "I'm carrying a witness statement forty miles long. And he knows it."

He went back inside, climbed the stairs two at a time, and began packing by candlelight. Not in the morning. Tonight. Because the smile at the lamplight had told him the one thing he needed to know: the offer had never been an offer. It had been a measurement. And somewhere down that dark street, a man was already writing down the results.

Outside, far off toward the high country, thunder rolled — or something that was not thunder, a deep percussive echo rolling down out of the range, felt in the floorboards more than heard.

Brigid's head appeared at his door, eyes sharp. "Tell me you heard that too."

Casper was already reaching for his coat.
