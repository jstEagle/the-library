## 14. Sabotage at the Portal

Brigid O'Rourke had heard a great many explosions in her life, and she had learned to read them the way other people read letters — by the hand that wrote them.

A charge was a signature. The powder you chose, the tamping you packed, the depth of the bore, the angle of the cut against the grain of the stone: all of it spoke. A sloppy man left a ragged mouth and a cough of unburnt powder hanging in the air like an apology. A careful man left clean rock and silence. And Brigid herself — she'd been told this by foremen who hated her and by the men who loved her both — left a particular sound, a flat *crack* with no echo behind it, because she drilled her holes to the inch and stemmed them with wet clay and never, ever wasted force. Her blasts didn't argue with the mountain. They persuaded it.

So when the ground under the keeper's station shivered at half past four in the morning, and the sound came rolling down the galleries — flat, clean, persuasive, no echo — Brigid sat up on her bedroll with her heart already going cold in her chest, because she knew that voice as well as her own.

"That's mine," she said aloud, to nobody. "That's my pattern."

The second blast came before she'd finished lacing her boots. Then a third, smaller, deeper in — the seaward gallery, by the sound of it, down where the cave opened its throat to Greywater Cove. The floor jumped. Dust sifted from the ceiling in grey ropes. Somewhere back along the passage, a stack of survey stakes went over with a clatter, and Ivo's voice rose out of the dark, high and sharp, not words but a signal — two long hoots, three short — the one he used for *danger, come to me*.

Casper was already moving. Of course he was. The man slept like a cat over a mousehole, one ear open for the whole world. He came out of his alcove buttoning his coat, lantern already lit, and his face in the lamplight was the face of a man doing arithmetic he didn't want finished.

"The portal," he said. "That was the portal."

"I know." Brigid took up her own lamp and her kit roll, hands steady, which surprised her. "And I know whose work it looks like."

They ran. Sefu came hobbling after them, bad leg dragging but stubborn as ever, and Ivo flowed past all of them through the dark like water finding its level, barefoot, calling signals ahead of himself so they could follow the sound of him.

The seaward gallery ended at a wall.

Not a wall of the mountain's making. A wall of rubble — great slabs of ceiling rock brought down deliberate and neat, packed tight from floor to roof, the fracture faces still weeping fresh dust. Brigid put her palm against it and felt the warmth of the blast still living in the stone. Behind it, muffled and far away, she could hear the sea working at the shingle outside, patient as ever, indifferent as ever.

She walked the face of it slowly, lamp held high, and what she saw made her sick in a way she hadn't been sick since the trial.

Three cuts. Boreholes angled fifteen degrees off vertical, spaced even as fence posts, stemmed proper, fired in sequence — crown first, then the flanks, then the key. It was textbook. It was *her* textbook. She had taught this exact pattern to forty men in the Kestrel pits, and every one of them had learned it from diagrams drawn in her own hand, because she'd been fool enough to write a manual once, back when she believed the industry might be worth teaching to.

"Vane," Casper said quietly. It wasn't a question.

"Vane, or Vane's money in somebody else's pocket." Brigid crouched and touched the spoil heap at the base of the wall. Fine stuff, well-sorted. Whoever had done it had carried their drill and their powder in and out again without leaving so much as a boot print worth reading — or had known the tide would scrub the approach clean within hours. "But look at the cut angles. Look at the stemming. That's my pattern, Casper. My pattern, near enough that if the provincial inspector ever saw it, he'd say *Brigid O'Rourke blew this wall*."

Sefu, leaning on his stick, sucked air through his teeth. "Framing you."

"Framing me." She stood. Her knees ached suddenly, all the old pit injuries waking up. "And doing more than that. Casper — how long is the tide cycle?"

Casper blinked. "What?"

"The tide. You've got the tables memorized, don't tell me you don't. How long until high water?"

He closed his eyes. She watched him do it — watched the numbers move behind his face the way they did when he checked a load calculation. When he opened his eyes, the arithmetic had gone badly.

"Eleven hours," he said. "Maybe twelve. At high water, the lower galleries flood to the third marker. Ivo showed us the marks."

"Aye." Brigid turned and looked back down the passage, toward the deep of the mountain, where the drowned gallery waited with its black water and its echo-navigable dark. "So here's our situation, gentlemen. Somebody has shut the front door, signed my name to it, and left us eleven hours to find a window."

Ivo had gone very still at the edge of the lamplight. He was looking at the rubble wall the way a man looks at a grave.

"They came in from the sea," he said. His voice was low, roughened from disuse; he spoke little and each word cost him. "At night tide. In a boat. There's a shelf below the cliff — the sealers use it. Nobody watches it."

"You saw them?"

"No." His jaw worked. "But the gulls would have told me. They always tell me. Last night they were quiet. Gulls go quiet when men are quiet." He looked up at Casper, and there was something in his face Brigid hadn't seen before — something old and ashamed. "I should have listened."

"Boy." Brigid crossed to him and crouched to his height, the way she'd learned to do with frightened pit ponies and green miners alike. "You're twelve years old and you've kept this mountain better than any company ever kept anything. If the gulls failed you, that's the gulls' shame, not yours. Understood?"

Ivo looked at her for a long moment. Then he nodded, once, the way he did everything — economical, exact.

* * *

They gathered in the keeper's station, all five of them around the scarred table, and Brigid laid it out plain, because panic was a fire and the only thing that killed it was structure.

"Right. Here's what we know. One: the seaward entrance is sealed, and sealed well — I'm not cutting through that in a day, let alone eleven hours, and I'd need powder I haven't got. Two: the lower galleries flood at high water, which means anything below the third marker becomes river. Three—" she tapped the map Casper had spread on the table, his father's map, the one with the gap in it, now annotated in four different hands, "—we came in through the upper galleries, and the upper galleries have exactly one other way out that anybody knows of. Which is the way we came in. Which is a vent shaft on the plateau side that Ivo says he's never used."

"It's narrow," Ivo said. "And it goes up through loose rock. And I've never been past the first bend, because—" he stopped.

"Because why?" Casper asked gently.

"Because the last keeper wrote about it." Ivo's eyes went to the ledger shelf, to the row of oilcloth-bound books that were becoming, entry by entry, the most important documents in the province. "In the early pages. He wrote that the shaft breathes wrong. That the air comes down it sometimes smelling of snow and sometimes of nothing at all, and a shaft that smells of nothing is a shaft that doesn't know where it ends."

Brigid felt the hair rise on her arms. She'd spent thirty years underground; she knew exactly what the boy meant, and she knew it was superstition, and she knew superstition kept men alive underground anyway because it made them careful.

"Well," she said briskly, "a shaft that smells of nothing is also a shaft nobody's using, which right now sounds like a virtue. But before we commit to climbing up a chimney in the dark, I want options. Sefu. The drowned gallery — how fast does it fill?"

Sefu had been sitting with his eyes shut, and Brigid knew what he was doing: walking the route in his memory, measuring it step by step, the gift that had gotten him blacklisted from three companies because a carpenter who never forgot a measurement made foremen nervous. Now he opened his eyes.

"From the third marker to the junction chamber, four hundred yards, give or take. Floor slopes down going seaward, so the water comes at us uphill, if you follow me. At spring tide it reaches the junction in about six hours after it starts rising. We waded it coming in — knee to thigh on me, waist on the boy. Going out the same way, before the rise, is nothing." He paused. "After the rise starts, it's a different animal. Water moving in a confined gallery has push to it. Thigh-deep and pushing is enough to take a man off his feet, and once you're down in that dark with current on you—"

He didn't finish. Nobody needed him to.

"So the drowned gallery is open to us for maybe five hours," Casper said. "Then it closes for six. Then it opens again. And if we're not out of the mountain by tomorrow's low-water window—"

"We're not drowning," Brigid said. "We're just choosing which dark we trust. The flooded gallery we know. The vent shaft we don't. And there may be a third door none of us knows about, because there's a boy at this table who's lived in this mountain seven years and knows passages the maps never heard of."

All eyes went to Ivo. He sat with his hands flat on the table, fingers spread, and Brigid watched him wrestle with something — not fear exactly, or not only fear. Possession. The mountain was his, and being asked to give up its secrets was being asked to give up a piece of himself.

"There is the chimney room," he said at last. "Above the second gallery. It goes up and up and it forks, and one fork goes nowhere and one fork — I think — comes out in the scree below the north ridge. But I've only gone partway. The bats live in it. Where the bats live, the air lives. That's what the keeper said. Air means a way through." He looked up. "But it's a climb. Hands and feet. Your leg—" this to Sefu, blunt as a hammer, "—your leg can't do it."

"My leg," Sefu said mildly, "has opinions, but it doesn't get a vote."

"And the instruments," Casper said. "The theodolite, the levels, the chain — half our legal evidence is in those cases. If we go up a chimney, we carry them or we lose them."

"We carry them," Brigid said. "Split the weight. And we take the ledgers too — wrapped in oilcloth, strapped to chests. Paper drowns same as men."

Casper was quiet a moment, looking at the map, at the small penciled x where the vent shaft breathed its nothing-smelling breath. Then he looked around the table, at each of them, and Brigid recognized the look. It was the look of a man taking attendance of his own soul.

"Ivo," he said. "You know this mountain better than any of us will ever know anything. So I'm asking, not telling. Which way?"

The boy didn't answer at once. He got up, went to the ledger shelf, and took down the oldest book — the one with the water-stained cover, the one whose final entries trailed off mid-sentence in a handwriting Brigid now knew belonged to Casper's father. He didn't open it. He just held it, both hands, like a rail held for balance.

"The keeper wrote that the gate gives you the door you're ready for," he said. "I used to think that meant the mountain chooses. Now I think it means you choose, and the mountain agrees or it doesn't." He set the book down in front of Casper. "The flooded gallery is the door we know. The chimney is the door we don't. But the flooded gallery leads out to the sea, and the sea is where Vane's men are, and the chimney leads up to the plateau, and the plateau is where we have to go anyway to finish the survey." He looked at Casper. "We choose the hard door that points the right direction."

Brigid laughed — short, involuntary, surprised out of her. "Listen to him. Twelve years old and talking like a chief engineer."

"He talks like a keeper," Casper said, and there was something in his voice Brigid couldn't name, grief and pride tangled together, and she let it be.

* * *

They worked for two hours, and the work was the best medicine any of them could have prescribed, because hands busy with honest tasks have no time for dread.

Brigid inventoried the powder remaining in the station's store — the keeper had kept a modest magazine, dry and orderly, and she blessed him for it across the years between them. She wasn't going to blast anything; there was nothing left worth blasting and no target worth the risk. But she redistributed the charges into waterproof canvas bags, rewrapped the fuse in waxed linen, and split the lot between packs so that no single accident could rob them of everything. As she worked, she found herself talking, because Brigid O'Rourke always talked when her hands were busy, and what came out was the story of the pattern — her pattern — the one somebody had stolen and signed with her name.

"First charge I ever fired, I was nineteen, and my father stood behind me and said, 'Bridie, the rock doesn't care about your temper. Give it a reason and it'll agree with you. Give it a fight and it'll bury you.' Everything I know is that sentence stretched out over thirty years." She tied off a bag. "And now some Harrow hireling has taken my sentence and used it to try to bury *me*. Well." She cinched the knot hard enough to hurt her fingers. "There's a conversation coming about that."

"You're certain it's Vane?" Sefu asked, coiling rope with the economy of a man who'd spliced a thousand lines.

"I'm certain it's my pattern, and I've taught it to perhaps fifty men alive, and I can account for forty-nine of them. The fiftieth is a lad named Corrigan who took Harrow's coin two winters back when the pits locked out, and I'd bet my blacklist he's standing on that sealer's shelf right now, pleased as punch with himself." She shouldered the bag. "Which means Vane didn't just close our door. He closed it with a signature, so that when they find this cave — and they'll find it, Casper, once the permits lapse and Harrow moves in — the provincial man will pull a core sample, read the cut angles, and write down *O'Rourke, B., unauthorized demolition*. They're not just stealing a route. They're building me a cell."

Casper stopped packing. He crossed the room and stood in front of her, and for a moment neither of them said anything, and the lamplight moved a little in the draft from the deep galleries.

"Then we make sure the truth gets out ahead of the lie," he said. "The ledgers. The gauge. The viaduct notes. Every hour we're in this mountain, we're collecting the evidence that clears you along with my father. That's not a consolation prize, Brigid. That's the job."

She nodded, and found she couldn't speak for a moment, and covered it by checking a strap that didn't need checking.

* * *

They reached the foot of the flooded gallery as the first grey suggestion of dawn filtered down through cracks in the mountain's bones — not light really, just the absence of full dark, enough to show them the water's surface lying flat and black and patient.

Ivo went first, as he always went first, wading in without ceremony, and his voice came back along the rock in that strange music he made — a click of the tongue, a slap of the palm, and then the listening silence, reading the echoes like a page. *Clear. Clear. Drop-off, left side, mind it.* Sefu followed with the instrument cases lashed high on his shoulders, cursing steadily and creatively under his breath, which everyone understood to be how he prayed. Then Casper with the ledgers. Brigid took the rear, because rearguard was where she'd always been happiest — the position of the person who watches the dark behind so the others can watch the dark ahead.

The water rose as they went, as the tables promised it would. By the halfway mark it was at Sefu's hip and Casper's waist, and the current had begun to make itself known — a long insistent pull toward the sea, toward the sealed door, as if the mountain itself were trying to hand them back to their enemy. Brigid planted each step with her staff and counted her breathing, and behind her own counting she heard the others: Sefu's curses thinning into silence as the water climbed his ribs; Casper's steady, deliberate exhale, the breath of a man lifting a load he refuses to set down; and ahead, always ahead, Ivo's small bright signals, *clear, clear, clear*, threading them through the dark like stitches through cloth.

At the junction chamber the water was chest-high and pushing, and Sefu stumbled.

It was nothing — a rounded boulder, a treacherous foothold, the kind of thing that happens ten thousand times in ten thousand galleries. But he went down sideways with eighty pounds of instruments on his back, and the current took the case straps and rolled him, and for three seconds — three seconds that lasted a year — Brigid watched a good man disappear under black water and understood, with total clarity, that the mountain had just offered to keep him.

Casper moved. She never properly saw how — one moment he was six feet upstream, the next he had Sefu by the collar and the instrument strap together, hauling, and Brigid threw herself flat against the current and caught Sefu's arm, and between the three of them they walked him up out of the deep channel onto the higher shelf of the junction, where he knelt coughing lake-water and fury in equal measure.

"Instruments?" Casper gasped.

"Dry." Sefu spat. "Cases are sealed. Leg's fine. Pride's drowned, though — leave it."

Ivo appeared out of the dark, dripping, eyes enormous. He looked at the three of them — the dynamiter, the engineer, the carpenter, kneeling in a heap on a wet rock in the belly of a mountain — and he made a sound Brigid had never heard him make before. A laugh. Small and rusty, like a hinge unused for years, but unmistakably a laugh.

"You dropped him," Ivo said to the mountain, gravely, "and we picked him up. That's how it works here. Nothing stays lost unless you let it."

* * *

The chimney room was everything Ivo had promised and worse: a vertical throat of rock going up beyond the reach of any lamp, breathing its slow cold breath, alive with the papery rustle of bats somewhere far above. They roped up. Brigid went second, behind Ivo, calling holds downward to Sefu, who climbed like a man settling a debt with the rock personally, jaw set, bad leg braced and pushed and cursed upward inch by inch. Casper carried the ledgers against his chest in a sling, and twice Brigid heard him stop and press his forehead to the stone, just breathe, just hold on, and start again.

Halfway up, the air changed. It came down the shaft colder, cleaner, tasting of pine and distance and — faintly, impossibly — of snow.

Ivo stopped. Brigid, a body-length below him, saw him lift his face into that moving air the way a man lifts his face to rain after drought.

"It's true," he whispered. "The keeper wrote it and it's true. The shaft breathes. It goes all the way through." He looked down at her, and in the lamplight his face was stripped of everything — all the wariness, all the territory, all the seven years of solitary keeping — and what was underneath was just a child about to see the sky from a place he'd feared his whole life. "Brigid. What if it smells of nothing higher up?"

"Then we'll smell of something," she said. "We'll bring the smell of us up there — coal smoke and tea and Sefu's terrible pipe. Mountain'll learn the scent soon enough."

Above them, faint as a memory, the first grey light of the real morning began to gather at the top of the shaft — and with it, drifting down through the cold clean air, came a sound that stopped every hand on the rock.

A whistle. Far off, high and thin across the plateau. Not a train — no train ran within forty miles. A human whistle, three notes, repeated. The kind of signal a lookout makes to other men below.

Vane's crew was on the plateau. Ahead of them. Above them. Waiting.

Ivo looked down the shaft at the four faces turned up toward him, and Brigid watched the boy take stock — the water rising in the galleries below, the strangers above, the narrow dark between — and she saw him arrive, calmly and completely, at the same conclusion they all did.

There was no way back. There was only through.

"Climb," Ivo said. "Quietly. And when we come out — we come out as the mountain comes out. Slow. Listening. Like the tide."
