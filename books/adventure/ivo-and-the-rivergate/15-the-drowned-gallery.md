## 15. The Drowned Gallery

Sefu had built bridges for nineteen years, and in all that time he had learned one rule above every other rule: water always wins. You could argue with stone. You could negotiate with timber. But water did not argue and did not negotiate. Water simply arrived, patient as arithmetic, and took what it had decided to take.

He was thinking about this — thinking about it the way a man thinks about a debt he cannot pay — as he stood at the lip of the drowned gallery with cold air rising off black water like breath off a dead man's chest.

The gallery had been a tunnel once. That much was plain even now, even flooded to within a hand's width of its crown. The arch of it was still there overhead, the old masonry still shouldering the mountain's weight, and along both walls Sefu could see the corbels where a walkway had run, back when this passage had been somebody's pride instead of nobody's grave. The water itself was the color of wet slate, moving — not fast, not yet, but moving — with a low, muscular current that made a sound against the walls like a slow exhale.

"How deep?" Brigid asked behind him. Her voice came out flatter than usual. Even dynamite women, Sefu thought, went quiet in front of standing water.

"Over my head," Sefu said. "Over yours too."

"And mine?" Casper asked.

Sefu looked at him. Casper Amberdale was not a big man — lean, weathered, built more like a surveyor's tripod than a bridge crew's foreman — but he was the tallest of them by two inches, and his shoulders were honest ones.

"Maybe not," Sefu admitted. "Maybe to your chin. Maybe past it. Depends on the bottom." He crouched, wincing as his right leg folded under him the way it always did, the knee refusing the last few degrees of bend, and put his palm flat on the water. It was cold enough to make his teeth ache through his jawbone. "And the bottom," he added, "is not a bottom. It's rubble. Rails, ballast, God knows what else fell off the walkway when they abandoned this place. A man steps wrong down there and he goes into a gap up to his ribs, and then the current decides for him."

The tide was coming back. They all knew it; they had been running from it for an hour, since the blast at the seaward portal had sent the sea pouring into galleries that had been dry for forty years. Ivo had led them inland and upward through passages so narrow that Brigid had to uncoil from her pack and pass it ahead of her, and then the passages had stopped going up, and now here they were: a drowned throat between them and whatever lay beyond, with the ocean filling the lower galleries behind them at a rate none of them could calculate but all of them could feel, the way you feel a debt collector getting closer without hearing his boots.

Ivo stood at the water's edge, barefoot — he had been barefoot since Sefu first saw him, and seemed to regard boots as some kind of affliction — with his trousers rolled to the knee and one foot in the flood, testing it. The boy's face in the lamplight was calm in a way that Sefu found almost worse than fear. Fear you could share. Calm like that belonged to someone who lived here.

"It rises," Ivo said. "Fast after the third hour. We have maybe two hours before it takes the whole gallery." He said it the way he said everything, in that clipped, careful voice of his, each word set down like a rail spike driven true. "There is a way through. There is always a way through. The keeper taught me that. But we have to go now, and we have to go quiet, and nobody panics when the light goes under."

"When the *what* goes under?" Brigid said.

"The lamp," Ivo said. "Partway across, the ceiling comes down low. Not the water coming up. The rock coming down. You'll have to swim a short stretch in the dark, holding the guide rope, and come up on the far side of the pinch. If you keep your lamp lit while you're swimming, you'll drop it, and then we'll have no light at all, and no way to find the rope again."

Nobody said anything for a moment. Sefu looked at the water and felt his leg speak to him — not pain exactly, more like a lawyer's letter, formal notice of terms and conditions. His leg had been crushed under a trestle bent six years ago on the Kessel Line, and the bone had knitted crooked because the company doctor had knitted it cheap, and ever since then the leg had opinions. It could carry him. It could carry him all day, most days. What it could not do was swim, or climb, or trust itself on wet rubble in the dark. In water over his waist, the good leg would be pushing against nothing and the bad leg would be a dead weight swinging on him like a sack of wet sand.

"I can't swim it," Sefu said.

It cost him something to say it out loud. He heard the cost in his own voice, the flatness of it, and he hated that. He had crossed half a continent on that leg. He had walked out of a hospital ward on it three weeks after the doctors said six months, because the company had stopped paying and the ward stopped being free. He had built a bridge over the Ash River with a fever, because if he hadn't, forty men would have stood idle and idle men get dismissed. He had spent six years making that leg invisible, and now, in front of these three people he had known for four days, it stood up and announced itself.

Casper turned around. He didn't say anything at first. He just looked at Sefu the way he looked at a span he was deciding whether to trust — not with pity, Sefu noticed, and he would remember that later, in the dark, how grateful he was for that. Not pity. Assessment.

"How's it on flat ground?" Casper asked.

"On flat ground it's a leg."

"In the water?"

"In the water it's an anchor."

Casper nodded slowly, and Sefu watched him do the arithmetic — he could see it happening behind the man's eyes, the same arithmetic Sefu himself ran every time he sized up a load: weight, distance, margin, failure. Then Casper looked at Ivo.

"The pinch. How long?"

Ivo considered. "Twenty lengths of a man. Maybe twenty-five. After the pinch, the floor rises. Waist-deep, then knee-deep, then dry rock to the far stair."

"And the rope?"

"There is a rope. The keeper strung it. I check it every month. It holds." A pause, and then, with the boy's strange precision: "I have swum it alone in the dark eleven times. To know it. So that if anyone ever needed to know, I would be the one who knew."

Sefu felt something shift in his chest at that — something small and uncomfortable, like a coal settling. Eleven times. Alone. A child, swimming blind through a mountain, practicing for people who might never come.

"All right," Casper said. "Here's how it goes. Brigid first — she's the strongest swimmer and she carries the charge bag, and if anything happens to the charges we're all finished anyway, so they go with the person most likely to keep them dry. Then Ivo, who knows the way. Then me, with Sefu. Sefu puts his arm around my neck, I hold him under the arms, and we kick together. Twenty-five lengths. One length at a time."

"I'm not being carried through a flooded tunnel like a—" Sefu started.

"You are," Casper said, not unkindly, not loudly, in the tone of a man reading a level. "Because the alternative is that you drown, and then I stop to pull your body out of a gap in the rubble, and then we all drown, because the tide doesn't care which of us it takes first. This isn't charity, Sefu. It's engineering. I don't send a cracked girder out onto a live span. I shore it up and I carry it."

Sefu shut his mouth. There was no argument in him anywhere, and what surprised him most was that the silence didn't feel like defeat. It felt like being included in a calculation. Like mattering.

Brigid snorted — a short, hard sound, her version of tenderness. "One length at a time," she said. "Fine. But if any of you touches my charge bag I will haunt this tunnel personally."

* * *

They went in.

The cold was the first thing — a full-body fact, immediate and total, the kind of cold that erased every other sensation and replaced it with itself. Sefu gasped once, involuntarily, and heard Brigid gasp too, and heard Ivo say, close beside him, "Breathe. Slow. The cold passes. Panic doesn't." And then they were moving.

The water rose as they went — shin, knee, hip — and the floor beneath it was exactly what Sefu had feared: not a floor at all but a field of fallen things. Ballast stone that rolled underfoot. Timber ends sharp as teeth. Once, horribly, his good foot came down on something long and round and yielding, and it took him a full second to understand it was a rail, still on its chair, furred with forty years of silt, and another second to understand that the chairs were the only reliable footing in the whole gallery. After that he walked the rails. Left foot forward along the iron, right foot dragging its crooked arc, one hand on the wall, following the line his own trade had laid and some other men's neglect had drowned. There was a bitter joke in that, and he held onto the joke the way you hold onto a rope, because the joke was doing the work of courage.

Behind him he could hear Casper's breathing, steady as a metronome. Ahead, the small splash-splash of Ivo's feet, quick and light, and the boy's voice every thirty paces, pitched low: "Rubble on the left. Step wide. Good. Now the ceiling drops — heads down — good."

Then the water reached Sefu's waist, and his leg left him.

Not gradually. All at once, like a colleague resigning mid-shift. The current found the joint — that crooked, badly-knit hinge — and pushed, and the leg swung out sideways, and for one lurching moment Sefu was standing on one limb in a black river inside a mountain, and the river was winning.

He grabbed the wall. His fingers found old iron — a ring bolt, some forgotten mooring point — and he hung on it and did not move and did not speak, because speaking would have meant admitting what was happening, and the water was at his sternum now and rising.

"Sefu." Casper's voice, very close. "Take my arm."

"I've got the ring—"

"The ring is forty years old and so is the mortar around it. Take my arm."

Sefu let go. The instant his fingers opened, the current took his whole weight and slammed him against Casper, and Casper took it — took all of it, a grown man's full weight plus the drag of the flood — and did not go down. Sefu got his arm over Casper's shoulder and locked his hands together at the man's chest, the way he'd carried injured navvies off spans in another life, and Casper turned side-on to the current, presenting it his shoulder like a man leaning into wind, and began to walk.

It was not dignified. It was not graceful. It was a fight, stroke by stroke, Casper's boots finding the rail chairs by feel, Sefu's good leg kicking when it could find purchase and finding nothing when it couldn't, the two of them moving as one clumsy animal against a river that wanted them gone. Somewhere behind them Brigid was swearing steadily and creatively, a continuous low mutter like a fuse burning, and somehow that helped. As long as Brigid was swearing, Brigid was alive and vertical.

Then the ceiling came down to meet the water, and Ivo said, "Pinch. Lamps out. Rope is on your left. Follow it. Swim when it pulls down. Come up when it pulls up. Count your strokes — twenty-five and you're through."

Darkness, total, the kind that has texture. Sefu felt the rope slap into his free hand — hemp, wet, taut, alive — and then Casper said, near his ear, "On three. Breathe in. One. Two. Three," and the world ended.

He swam in the dark with a man's shoulder under his arm and a rope in his fist and a dead leg trailing behind him like something he was towing rather than wearing, and he counted. He counted because counting was a rail to walk on. Four strokes, seven, twelve — at fifteen the rope dipped and pulled downward and he followed it down and the water closed over his head and there was nothing in the universe but cold and rope and count. Eighteen. Nineteen. At twenty-two his lungs were screaming and a small treacherous voice in him said *let go, just stand up, just rest,* and he answered it aloud — he actually said it, bubbles streaming past his ear, "*No*" — because his father had been a man who answered voices like that with words, out loud, on principle, and Sefu had inherited the habit along with the bad knee and the perfect memory.

Twenty-five.

The rope pulled up. He pulled up. And his head broke water into a space where a lamp was already relit — Ivo's lamp, cupped in the boy's two hands like a bird — and the light showed stone rising out of the flood ahead, real stone, dry-shouldered stone, and Brigid hauling herself onto it with the charge bag held high above her head like a rescued infant, and Casper surfacing beside him, coughing, gripping his collar, saying, "You counted. Good man. You counted."

Sefu tried to answer and found he was shaking too hard to shape words. He got a hand on the rock. Casper got a hand on his belt. Between them, and then with Brigid's fist closing on his wrist, and then Ivo's small strong grip under his arm — the boy astonishingly strong, tow-headed and ferocious as a river otter — they got him out of the water and onto the stone, where he lay on his back with the bad leg twitching and his heart slamming and the lamp swinging overhead, and laughed.

He didn't decide to laugh. It came up out of him like water out of a struck well, ragged and unstoppable, and Brigid laughed too, lying flat beside him, and then Ivo laughed — a bright, startled sound, high and young, the first time any of them had heard it — and even Casper, wringing out his coat, let go of his careful foreman's face and grinned like a man half his age.

"We're through," Brigid said. "We're actually through."

"Not yet." Ivo was already standing, looking back the way they'd come, into the dark throat of the pinch. His laughter had stopped as quickly as it started. "Listen."

Sefu listened. At first there was only their own noise — breath, dripping water, the tick of cooling lamps. Then, underneath, faint but unmistakable, rising from the flooded gallery behind them: a change in the sound of the water. A deepening. A gathering. The sound a river makes when it stops being a river and starts being a decision.

"The third hour," Ivo said quietly. "It's early. Or we were slower than I hoped." He turned to them, and in the lamplight his face was grave and certain at once. "The gallery we just crossed will be full to the crown in twenty minutes. Everything below us will follow. We have to climb now, and we can't stop, and I can't carry anyone." He looked straight at Sefu, and there was no apology in it, only honesty, which Sefu found he preferred. "How is the leg?"

Sefu sat up. He assessed the leg the way he assessed any structure: honestly, without hope of flattery. It had carried him through a flood. It had nothing left. It was, structurally, finished.

"It's done," he said. "For today, it's done."

"Then we rest five minutes," Casper said, "and after that—" He stopped. Looked at Sefu. Looked at the dark stairwell Ivo was already pointing toward, a slot of deeper black in the rock, angling up. Sefu watched him arrive at the same arithmetic he'd arrived at in the water, and this time there was no debate in the man's face at all, only the answer.

"After that," Casper said, "I carry you."

* * *

Five minutes became four because none of them could sit still that long with the water talking behind them. Then Ivo went first up the stair with the lamp, and Brigid went second with the charges, and Casper crouched at the bottom step with his back angled, and Sefu climbed onto him the way you climb onto a horse you trust — arms over the shoulders, wrists crossed at the man's chest, trusting him with the whole humiliating truth of his weight.

"Tell me if I'm crushing you," Sefu muttered.

"You weigh less than a coil of half-inch cable," Casper said. "Hold on."

And then Casper Amberdale climbed. Stair by stair, in the dark, with a grown man on his back, following a boy's lamp and a woman's swearing, up through the mountain's interior while the flood rose behind them filling gallery after gallery with a sound like the end of the world played slowly. Sefu kept count of the stairs because counting was what he did, what he was — two hundred and ten, two hundred and forty — and somewhere past three hundred his head dropped onto Casper's shoulder and he stopped counting and simply held on, and it occurred to him, in a way that had nothing to do with stairs or water or legs, that he had not been carried by anyone since he was a child. That he had spent his entire adult life being the man who carried things, because the one time he had asked to be carried — the hospital, the Kessel Line, the claim papers — the company had answered with a dismissal and a locked door.

This was different. This was a man carrying him because the man had done the math and the math said *both of us or neither*, and that was not charity. That was a crew.

Four hundred and twelve stairs, by Ivo's count, though the boy counted differently than Sefu did, in some private rhythm of his own. Near the top the air changed — Sefu felt it before he saw anything, a thinning, a coldness, a taste of sky — and then gray light appeared above them, actual daylight, weak and silvered and unmistakably outdoors, and Ivo's head and shoulders rose through a vent shaft onto the high plateau, and the boy turned back down to them with the wind moving his hair and said:

"Come out slow. Like the tide."

Casper lifted Sefu through last, setting him down on the shelf of rock at the shaft's mouth with a gentleness that undid Sefu more than the flood had. And there they lay and crouched and knelt, four of them, spilled out onto the shoulder of the mountain like cargo from a wrecked train, while behind and below them the drowned galleries filled in the dark with the patient, victorious sound of water taking what it had decided to take.

Sefu looked east, because Brigid was pointing east, and his memory — the perfect memory, the curse and gift of it — served him the map instantly: the plateau, the ruins of the laborers' shantytown two miles on, the viaduct country beyond. And above the eastern ridgeline, where the sky should have been the clean hard blue of late autumn, there was a cloud. Low. Gray-white. Unmoving. Sitting on the mountains like a lid on a pot.

"That's not rain," Brigid said.

"No," Ivo said. He was standing at the edge of the shaft with the wind pulling at him, and his voice, when it came, was very quiet. "That's snow. And it wasn't supposed to come for weeks."

Sefu looked at his leg, stretched out crooked on the stone, and then at the cloud, and did the only arithmetic that mattered anymore.

"Then we'd better start walking," he said.
