## 16. Out Through the Mountain's Teeth

The vent shaft was a secret Ivo had kept even from himself.

He had known about it the way he knew about things he did not want to know — the way he knew the sound the river made when it found a new crack in the mountain, or the way he knew that some of the ledgers in the keeper's station had pages torn out. The shaft was marked on no map. It was not on the old keeper's charts either, which meant it was either unimportant or forbidden, and Ivo had spent four years deciding it was both.

It opened off the third gallery, past the place where the walls went white with mineral bloom, a vertical throat of stone barely wide enough for a man's shoulders, breathing cold air downward like something sleeping. He had put his head into it once, years ago, when he was small enough to think the mountain would forgive curiosity. The cold had come up out of it and touched his face, and he had heard — or thought he heard — a far-off rattle of loose stone falling a long way down, and he had climbed back into the warm dark of his galleries and told himself: *not mine.* Every passage in the gate was his except that one. That one belonged to whatever lived above.

Now he stood at its mouth again, older by four years and by one flooded gallery and one drowned crossing that had nearly taken Sefu, and the cold came up out of the dark and touched his face the same way, patient as ever.

"It goes up," he said. "All the way."

Casper crouched beside him, lamp held low. Behind them the drowned gallery still dripped and sighed, the flood receding down passages they had no intention of seeing again. Brigid sat against the wall wringing out her second sock, and Sefu lay propped on a coil of rope with his bad leg straight out before him, gray-faced but watching everything, the way he always watched everything.

"How far up?" Casper asked.

"I don't know." It cost Ivo something to say it. Honesty was the toll this crew charged — Casper had taught him that without meaning to, back in the upper galleries, admitting what he didn't know until Ivo had started doing the same. "I never went. It breathes, so it has to open somewhere. But it's narrow, and it's loose, and—" He stopped. The rest was harder. "The last keeper never wrote about it. Everything else, he wrote about. Not this."

Casper was quiet a moment. In the lamplight his face looked carved and tired, forty years of worry pressed into thirty. "Then maybe he didn't know about it," he said. "Or maybe he did, and left it for whoever needed it after him."

"Or maybe it's how they got in," Ivo said, and did not say *who*, and Casper did not ask, because they had already had that conversation once, down in the deep dark, and neither of them had finished it.

Brigid snorted from the wall. "Well, whichever corpse used it last, they're done using it now. Give me the rope."

* * *

They went up in the order Ivo chose, which was himself first, then Casper, then Brigid, then Sefu, because Sefu argued loudest that he should go last so nobody would have to watch him climb, and Ivo understood the argument was really about dignity and let him have it.

The shaft took him the way water takes a stick — slowly, with a great deal of grinding. He climbed by feel, wedging knees and shoulders, the rope humming faintly below him each time someone moved. The stone was slick with condensation and broke into flakes under his fingers, and twice he dislodged pieces that fell away into the dark with a sound like slow applause. Above him the circle of black stayed black. Below him Casper's lamp made a small trembling sun.

Halfway — or what he guessed was halfway, because there was nothing to measure against — the shaft narrowed, and Ivo had to turn his head sideways to breathe, and for three long minutes he hung in the throat of the mountain thinking about the tide tables, about how much water was still down in the lower galleries, about whether the sea could climb this high if it decided to. Then the narrowing passed, and the stone around him changed — drier, colder, smelling of frost instead of salt — and he knew he had crossed some border the mountain kept between its two lives.

Light came next. Not daylight yet, but a gray suggestion of it, seeping down through rubble near the top like water through a roof. Ivo climbed toward it faster than was wise, hands burning, and then his head came up through a jumble of broken rock into air so cold it felt like a different element, and he was out.

He lay on the slope a moment, cheek against frozen grass, and let the sky pour into him.

It was enormous. That was the first thing. Four years underground had shrunk his idea of the world to lamp-radius, and now the sky went on forever in every direction, bruise-colored and low, streaked with cloud the color of dirty wool. Wind moved over the plateau in long slow pushes, combing through a sea of dead gold grass that ran away north until it dissolved into mountains. Somewhere far off a hawk said one dry word and got no answer.

Below him, through the vent's rubble mouth, came voices — Casper counting Brigid's movements, Brigid telling Casper exactly where he could put his counting. Ivo almost laughed. The sound came out of him rusty, like a door in the keeper's station he'd never oiled.

When all four of them were out — Sefu hauled up the last ten feet on a rope over Casper's shoulder, swearing steadily and creatively the whole way — they stood together on the shoulder of the mountain and looked at what the world had been doing while they were underground.

Autumn was dying.

Not fading — dying, the way it dies at altitude, all at once and without apology. The grass was bone-gold going gray at the roots. The stunted pines along the ridge wore brown like borrowed coats. And high above them, on the peaks to the north, the snowline had come down to meet them — not the soft seasonal dusting the fishing villages talked about, but a hard white edge, sharp as a gauge line, sitting a thousand feet lower than any almanac said it should sit in this month.

"That's wrong," Casper said quietly. He said it the way he read bridge inspections — flat, factual, and full of doom. "That's weeks early. Weeks."

"The mountain doesn't read almanacs," Brigid said. She was shivering and pretending not to. "Mountain reads wind."

Sefu had gone very still, staring north, and Ivo watched his lips move as he counted something — ridges, maybe, or years. When he spoke, his voice had the careful evenness of a man laying track across bad ground. "My grandmother kept weather in her bones. She'd take one look at that sky and start burying potatoes." He turned to Casper. "How many days to the viaduct?"

"Four, if the ground's kind."

"And the ground," Sefu said, looking at the dead grass, "is not looking kind."

* * *

They walked while the light lasted, because none of them suggested stopping and none of them wanted to be the one who did. The plateau rose in long swells, treeless and exposed, the wind coming off the northern peaks with a smell like iron and coming winter. Ivo led them along contours he half-remembered from the old keeper's surface notes — the man had charted the plateau too, in a cramped hand, marking water sources with little crosses, and Ivo had memorized those crosses the way other children memorized prayers.

They found the first cross at dusk: a spring running thin and glassy out of a rock seam, skin-cold. They drank, filled every vessel they had, and ate half of what remained of their food, which was the arithmetic nobody spoke aloud.

They camped in a fold of ground with the wind over their backs. Brigid got a fire going from wet grass and sheer professional stubbornness, feeding it twigs like charges in a fuse sequence, and they huddled into its small orange argument with the dark.

Ivo sat apart, as he always sat, close enough to share the warmth and far enough to keep his edges. But tonight Casper shifted over until their shoulders nearly touched, and held out the last strip of dried fish, split unevenly so the bigger piece faced outward, and Ivo took it without comment because refusing would have been a kind of lying.

Above them the clouds were tearing apart, and through the gaps the stars came hard and close, and Ivo picked out the ones the keeper had named for him — the Driftwood, the Two Lanterns, the Long Signal — and felt the old ache of being the only person alive who knew those names.

"We'll make the viaduct in four days," Casper said, to the fire more than to anyone. "Cross it or find another way over the gorge. Then it's the high survey line, six days to the eastern portal if the weather holds."

"And if it doesn't hold?" Brigid asked.

Casper was quiet. The fire popped. Far off on the northern peaks, where the white edge sat, something glittered that might have been starlight on fresh snow.

"Then we walk faster than the weather," Casper said finally. "We've been racing a clock since Greywater. This is just the clock catching up." He looked around the fire at each of them — Brigid with her burned fingers, Sefu with his leg stretched stiff, Ivo with his fish — and something in his face settled, the way a span settles onto its abutments. "We don't stop. We don't scatter. We count our days honestly and we spend every one of them moving. That's all a schedule is. That's all anything is."

Sefu grunted, which from Sefu was applause. Brigid fed the fire another twig. And Ivo looked north, at the white line on the peaks, and felt the thing he had felt at the mouth of the vent shaft — the cold touching his face, patient as ever, waiting to see what he would do.

Only now, for the first time in four years, he was not alone in front of it.

* * *

The snow came on the third day.

Not the real snow — not yet — but its herald, a fine dry sifting out of a colorless sky, falling so lightly it seemed undecided, dusting the dead grass silver and erasing their backtrail behind them like a hand smoothing a bedsheet. They walked in it all morning, four figures in a whitening world, and by noon the plateau had begun to look like somewhere else entirely, somewhere older, somewhere that had never heard of railroads or permits or men named Vane.

It was Sefu who stopped first, mid-stride, one hand raised.

Ahead of them the land dropped away — gently at first, then all at once — into a gorge so wide and so deep that the far side seemed a rumor. The river ran along its bottom, thin as wire from this height, and spanning the gap, or having once spanned it, stood the viaduct.

What remained of it.

Broken piers marched down into the gorge like the vertebrae of something enormous that had died mid-step. Between them hung fragments of deck, sagging on twisted steel, furred with frost. The whole ruin lay silent in the sift of snow, and even the wind seemed to lower its voice passing over it.

Nobody said anything for a long time. Ivo watched Casper's face do several things at once — grief and fury and a surveyor's cold measuring eye, all fighting for the same ground.

"There it is," Casper said at last, very quietly. "My father's bridge."

And beneath his feet, half-buried in the fresh dusting of white, Ivo saw the corner of something square and dark protruding from the scree at the gorge rim — a rusted tin box, its latch hanging open, as if the mountain itself had been waiting to hand them what it had been keeping.

Casper hadn't seen it yet. Ivo stepped toward it.
