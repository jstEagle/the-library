## 17. Camp of the Unwanted

The shantytown announced itself before they saw it — a smell of old creosote and rotting spruce riding down the wind off the plateau, and then the ruins themselves, rising out of the frost-killed grass like the ribs of something that had once breathed.

Brigid stopped at the edge of it and took off her cap without meaning to. She'd lived in places like this one. She'd been born in a place like this one, or near enough — a construction camp on the Kestrel cut, forty families in tar-paper shacks, her father swinging a hammer for a company that docked his pay for the nails he bent. The ruins here were older and colder, but the shape of them was the same: rows of foundations gone green with moss, stove chimneys standing alone like headstones, a plank walkway half-sunk into the earth where hundreds of boots had worn it hollow on their way to work.

"Nobody told me there was a camp up here," Casper said behind her.

"Nobody tells anybody," Brigid said. "That's how camps work. You build them, you live in them, you die in some of them, and when the line's done the company burns the rest and puts it out of its memory." She walked forward through the grass, which crackled underfoot with the first thin skin of ice. "This one they didn't even burn. They just left."

Sefu came up beside her, favoring his bad leg on the uneven ground, and looked out over the foundations with an expression she couldn't read. Ivo ranged ahead the way he always did, low and quick, checking the ruins the way other boys would check a henhouse for eggs.

"There's a chimney that still stands whole," Ivo called back. "And a cellar. Dry inside. And there are things." He said *things* the way he said most words — flat, precise, like a signal flag run up a pole.

The things turned out to be the leavings of lives: a boot sole, a tin cup fused to a stone by rust, a child's slate with one corner broken, a hairbrush with most of its bristles still in it. Brigid set the cup upright on a foundation stone, not because it needed doing but because leaving it face-down in the dirt felt like a kind of agreement with whoever had walked away from it.

They made camp in the lee of the standing chimney, which had once warmed a shack big enough for a family of six and now sheltered four travelers and a cook fire. Casper got the fire going with a competence that surprised no one; Sefu dragged timbers from a collapsed bunkhouse and built a windbreak with the economy of a man who'd built a hundred of them; Ivo vanished and returned with two ptarmigan he'd taken from the rocks above the camp with a sling he'd made himself from a bootlace and a strip of inner tube, and if anyone thought it strange that a twelve-year-old could dress game faster than a hotel cook, nobody said so, because by now nobody thought anything about Ivo was strange except how long it had taken them to stop being surprised.

They ate. The light went blue and then black over the plateau, and the cold came down out of the sky like a lid closing, and they pulled closer to the fire until their knees nearly touched, four people who had known each other eleven days and had already, between them, drowned a gallery together, crossed a gorge on a rope span, and lost a case of survey instruments to a hundred feet of empty air.

It was Sefu who started it, though Brigid would remember afterward that she'd been thinking it.

"You know what I keep noticing," he said, turning his cup in his hands. "Every one of us has a story about how we ended up available to take this job. And none of us has told it."

"We don't need to," Casper said.

"I know we don't need to." Sefu smiled, slow, his eyes catching the firelight. "I want to anyway. It's been rattling around in my head since the gorge. You carry a man across a flooded gallery and you don't even know what he was doing the day the world threw him away." He looked around the fire. "I'll go first, if it makes it easier."

Brigid fed a stick to the flames. "Go on, then."

* * *

"I was the best bridge carpenter on the Coast Division," Sefu said, without any boast in it — the way you'd state the load rating of a truss. "Fifteen years. I could look at a bent and tell you what it was carrying and what it wished it wasn't. And then my leg." He knocked his knuckles against his knee, and the sound it made was wrong, a dry click instead of the dull thud of flesh. "Trestle at Miller's Bend, seven years back. Green timber in the stringers — Harrow-supplied, as it happens, though nobody wrote that part down. The walkway let go under me. Twenty-two feet onto rock and river gravel."

He was quiet a moment. The fire popped.

"The company doctor set it fast and crooked and cleared me for light duty in nine weeks, because the schedule didn't have room for anything slower. I went back. I worked three more years on that leg, and I'll tell you honestly, I worked well — I learned to do everything sitting down or leaning, and I got so I barely thought about it. Then the new division superintendent came through on an inspection tour, watched me climb a falsework ladder, and decided a crippled carpenter was a liability on his books. Not unsafe. *Liability.* His word. He said it like he was doing the books a favor." Sefu turned his cup again. "So they retired me. At thirty-eight years old. With a letter thanking me for my service and a pension calculated on a formula so mean it wouldn't keep a dog in bones."

"And your memory?" Brigid asked. "You've never said where that comes from."

Sefu laughed, short. "From being the man they never wrote anything down for. If the plans were wrong — and they were often wrong — I was the one who had to hold the right numbers in my head, because there was no budget for redrawing them. So I held them. Every span I ever built is still up there." He tapped his temple. "Every measurement, every camber, every mistake and every correction. The industry threw me away, but it couldn't take the ledger out of my skull." He glanced at Casper. "Which is why, when a dismissed engineer offered me a job walking a dead line with a boy for a guide, I said yes before he finished asking. A man doesn't get many chances to build something true after the books close on him."

* * *

"My turn, I suppose." Brigid stretched her hands toward the fire. She'd told this story before, in hearing rooms and union halls and once, memorably, to a magistrate who fined her five pounds for the telling, and it had worn smooth with repetition, like a stone in a current. But tonight, in the ruins of a laborers' camp, it came up rough again.

"I was a powder monkey at fourteen. Blaster's apprentice at nineteen. Full charge certificate at twenty-three — youngest woman in the province ever to hold one, and the examiner told me so with a face like he'd swallowed a wasp. I could read rock. That's the whole art of it, reading rock — knowing where it wants to break and helping it along, and knowing where it *doesn't* want to break and leaving it be. Most men blast by the book. The book doesn't know your mountain."

She paused. Out past the firelight, the plateau lay silent under the first stars, and somewhere below them the gorge they'd crossed held its dark like a held breath.

"Two years ago I was blasting the north portal at Cairn Hollow, and the rock told me the face was live — a seam full of water pressure, the kind of thing that turns a routine shot into a burial. I refused to fire it. My foreman ordered me to fire it. I refused again, in front of the crew, and told them why, and told them what the company's bonus schedule was doing to our judgment — how we were all being paid to hurry, and hurry is what kills powder men. Somebody repeated what I said to somebody else, and it got back to the district office, and two weeks later I gave a speech at the lodge hall about it. Union business. Straightforward, decent, ordinary union business — the kind of thing men had been saying in that hall for thirty years." Her mouth twisted. "Except I wasn't a man, and I said it better than most of them, and there were newspapermen in the room."

"They blacklisted you," Casper said quietly.

"They blacklisted me so thoroughly that the blacklist has a nickname for me. Ask for Brigid Doyle at any rail office west of the mountains and watch the clerk's face go white. I can't buy dynamite legally anymore. Do you understand what that means? I have a charge certificate and ten years of perfect safety record and I cannot legally touch a stick of powder in my own province. I've been living on odd jobs and friends' couches for two years, and I was about three weeks from eating my tools for scrap when a tall engineer with a haunted face walked into Marta's inn and asked if anyone knew a blaster who didn't ask questions." She looked at him across the fire. "I asked questions anyway. Good ones. You answered all of them straight, which is more than any employer's done for me since I was fourteen."

"That's why you checked Vane's charges so fast," Sefu said. "At the portal. You knew your own signature."

"I knew my own signature," Brigid agreed, and the memory of it — the collapsed entrance, the charges cut in her own pattern, the cold certainty that someone had used her hands like a forged check — moved through her again like weather. "Somebody out there studied my work well enough to counterfeit it. When I find out who taught them, there will be a conversation."

* * *

They looked at Casper then, all of them, even Ivo, who had been listening from his place at the edge of the firelight with the absolute stillness he brought to everything that mattered.

Casper took his time. He was a man who took his time, Brigid had learned — not from caution exactly, but from a habit of weighing words the way he weighed loads, as if a misspoken sentence might bring something down on top of everyone.

"You both know the outline," he said finally. "The trestle at Marrow Creek. I inspected it and found the bents rotten to the core — Harrow steel in the ties, Harrow timber in the stringers, same suppliers, same cheapness, different year. My superior told me to sign the inspection clean. I told him I'd sign it honest or not at all. He chose not at all, and by evening I was dismissed for incompetence, which is the word they use when a man's honesty inconveniences the timetable." He fed the fire a piece of split shake. "What you don't know is that I almost signed it."

Brigid kept her face still. Beside her, Sefu did the same.

"I stood there with the pen in my hand for — I don't know how long. A minute. Five. My rent was due. My mother's letters were getting thinner, the way letters get when the writer is choosing which troubles not to mention. And the trestle wasn't going to fall tomorrow. Probably not that month. There's always a probably, and the probably does the persuading, and that's how it works — that's how it works on everybody, all the time. The rot doesn't kill anyone today. The signature just buys time. Everyone signs. Everyone has always signed." He looked into the fire. "My father signed things. That's what I was thinking, with the pen in my hand. Whatever happened to him out here, whatever took his crew — before that, he was a man who signed. And I thought: if I put my name on that paper, I am signing myself into the same company he kept, and I will never get out of it."

"So you didn't sign," Ivo said. It wasn't a question. Ivo's voice did something Brigid had noticed it only did with Casper — softened at the edges, the way ice softens at the edge of a current.

"So I didn't sign." Casper's mouth moved toward a smile and didn't arrive. "And I lost my name in the trade. That's the part nobody warns you about. You think you're risking your job. What you actually risk is your name — the way people say it, the weight it carries. 'Casper Amberdale' used to open doors. Now it closes them, and it closes them politely, which is worse." He spread his hands. "So here I am. Walking a dead line with a blaster nobody will hire, a carpenter the books retired, and a boy who owns a mountain. The industry discarded all four of us. It seems to me the industry has poor judgment."

"It seems to me," Sefu said, raising his cup, "that it discarded us into excellent company."

Brigid touched her cup to his. Even Ivo lifted his tin mug an inch off his knee, solemn as a christening.

* * *

The fire burned down to a bed of orange and they fed it again, and the cold deepened, and the stars over the plateau thickened until the sky looked flayed open. It was Ivo who spoke next, unprompted, which was rare enough that all three of them went quiet to make room for it.

"I don't have a discard story," Ivo said. "Because I was never in the industry. I was never anywhere. The keeper found me — or I found him, I don't remember which way it goes — and he kept me, and then he stopped coming back, and I kept the gate." He turned his mug slowly. "But I know what you mean. About being thrown away. The village people throw me away with their eyes. When I come down to Greywater for salt, they look at me like I'm weather. Something that happens to them. Something to be endured until it passes." He said it without self-pity, which somehow made it worse. "The gate never threw me away. The gate keeps things."

"The gate keeps things," Brigid repeated softly, and found she couldn't tell whether it was comfort or warning, and suspected Ivo couldn't either, and suspected that was the truest sentence spoken all night.

They sat with it. The fire settled. Somewhere out in the ruined camp, a loose sheet of tin shifted in the wind and clanked twice, like a distant signal, and Ivo's head came up at the sound and then lowered again, satisfied.

It was later — the fire down to coals, Sefu asleep sitting up with his bad leg stretched toward the warmth, Casper staring into the embers with the ledger open on his knees though he wasn't reading it — that Brigid heard herself ask the question she'd been carrying since the gorge.

"Ivo. The keeper. Did he ever say his name? Your keeper?"

Ivo was quiet so long she thought he'd gone to sleep too.

"He had names," the boy said at last. "He had a name on the ledgers. He had a name the village used. But he told me names were like stations — a name is just where a person stops and lets themselves be known." He looked into the coals, and the light moved on his face. "He called me the river's boy. That was the only name he gave me. I never asked for another."

Brigid opened her mouth — because Brigid always opened her mouth, it was her gift and her affliction — and then closed it again, because she saw Casper watching the boy with an expression she recognized from her own mirror: the look of a man about to offer something he isn't sure he has the right to give.

And beyond the firelight, far out over the northern rim of the plateau where the land fell away toward the high passes, the sky had begun to gather itself into a color that Brigid Doyle, daughter of the Kestrel cut and veteran of twenty winters in camp country, knew the way she knew the smell of fuse smoke.

Snow-clouds. Weeks early. Maybe days.

She said nothing yet. But she marked it, the way she marked a live seam in a rock face, and waited for morning to tell the others.
