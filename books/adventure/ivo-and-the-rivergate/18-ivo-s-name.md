## 18. Ivo's Name

The fire in the ruined shantytown burned low and orange, throwing long shadows across the fallen walls of the laborers' barracks. Ivo sat at the edge of the circle, where he always sat — close enough to feel the warmth, far enough to keep his back to stone. Even here, on the open plateau with autumn dying around them and snow coming weeks early, he chose his seat like a creature choosing a den. Wall behind him. Fire before him. Exits counted.

He had counted them since they'd made camp. Two gaps in the tumbled masonry to the north, one to the south where a doorway had rotted into a black mouth, and the low ridge beyond, which any man could cross in four strides if he were running. He knew this without thinking about it, the way he knew the sound of water rising in the lower galleries, the way he knew the difference between wind moving through the gate and something heavier moving through it.

The others had told their stories tonight. That was Brigid's doing. She had a way of asking questions that weren't really questions — she'd say *so what did they get you for* the way another person might say *pass the bread*, and somehow you found yourself answering before you understood you'd been asked. Sefu had gone first, telling about his leg and the bridge foreman who'd ordered green timber used anyway, and how Sefu had written the date and the timber mark on the inside of his own forearm in ink so he'd never forget, and how the company had decided a carpenter with a memory was more dangerous than a carpenter with a limp. Brigid had told about the union speech, standing on a crate in the rain, and how she'd known even as the words left her mouth that she was signing her own blacklist, and how she'd do it again tomorrow.

Then Casper had spoken. Quietly, in his flat engineer's way, about the rotten trestle and the signature he wouldn't put on paper, and about his father, and the map that ended mid-line. Ivo had listened with his chin on his knees, watching the fire through the gap between his boots, and had felt something strange move in his chest when Casper talked about the ledger — *my father's handwriting*, Casper had said, *I'd know it anywhere* — because Ivo knew that handwriting too. Better than Casper did, maybe. He had spent years tracing those letters with his finger in the dark, teaching himself to read by the shape of a dead man's discipline.

And then Brigid had turned to him.

She didn't do it right away. She let the fire crackle and the wind worry the ruins, and she fed another stick to the flames, and Ivo thought — hoped — that she'd skip him. That she could see he wasn't like the others, that there was nothing to tell because there was no story, only a boy and a cave and a keeper who stopped coming back.

But Brigid looked across the fire at him, and her eyes were kind and terrible all at once, and she said, "And you, river-boy. What's your family? Who raised you besides the old keeper?"

Ivo looked at the fire.

The question sat in him like a swallowed stone. Not because it hurt, exactly. Because he had never once tried to answer it, and now that he turned it over he found there was nothing inside it. No name to give. The keeper had called him *boy*, mostly, or *river's boy* when he was feeling soft, and Ivo had never asked why there was no other word, because asking would have meant admitting he'd noticed it was missing.

"I don't have one," he said.

"Have one what?" Sefu asked gently.

"A family-name." He picked up a twig and turned it in his fingers. "The keeper called me the river's boy. That's all. There wasn't—" He stopped, started again. "Nobody came to leave me. Nobody came looking after. I was just there, and then I was his."

The silence that followed was not the bad kind. Ivo had learned every kind of silence in the gate — the silence of rising water, the silence of men's boots on stone, the silence of the deep gallery where the air went stale and wrong. This was none of those. This was the silence of three people deciding not to look at him too directly, because they understood that being looked at too directly can feel like being cornered.

It was Casper who spoke. "My father kept a ledger," he said. "Every man who worked for him is named in it. Every day they worked. If you lived at the station, you'd be in it."

Ivo shook his head. "I'm not in it. I looked. Years ago. I read every page." He said it without bitterness; it was simply a fact, like the depth of the tide pool or the number of steps down to the keeper's cot. "There's no page for me."

Brigid made a small sound, half breath, half word, and pressed her knuckles briefly to her mouth.

"So write yourself in," Casper said.

Ivo looked up. Across the fire, the engineer's face was lit orange and steady, and there was something in it Ivo couldn't name — not pity, which he hated, and not the sharp measuring look adults gave him when they were calculating what he was worth. Something else. Something that waited.

"You could take mine," Casper said. "Amberdale. It's only a word, but it's a word with weight. Men trust it, or they did. It would open doors for you that—" He stopped himself, and Ivo watched him hear his own sentence and understand what was wrong with it. Doors. As if Ivo were behind one. As if the boy who owned every passage of the mountain needed doors opened for him.

Casper started again, slower. "What I mean is: you shouldn't have to go through the world without a name. Not because a name makes you anything. But because the world is stupid, and it won't let you pass without one."

Ivo turned the twig over in his fingers. He thought about the registry office in Greywater, the one time Marta had taken him there to buy lamp oil, and the clerk who had asked his full name and looked at him like a hole in a form when he couldn't answer. He thought about the way the word *Amberdale* sounded — solid, load-bearing, a beam set across two posts.

And he thought about the gate.

"I'm not an Amberdale," he said.

He said it gently, the way you decline food you're grateful for. He saw Casper take it without flinching, which Ivo noted and stored away, because most grown men flinched.

"The Amberdales are a line," Ivo said, trying to make the words carry what he meant. They came slowly; he was used to signals, not sentences. "A line has two ends. It goes from somewhere to somewhere. Your father drew his line on the map and it stopped, because—" he hesitated, because he didn't want to wound him, "—because the line stopped. But the gate doesn't stop. The gate isn't going anywhere. The tide comes in, the tide goes out, and the gate stays. I'm not a line. I'm the place the line goes through."

Sefu laughed softly, not mocking — marveling. "Listen to him. He talks like a bridge."

"He talks like a keeper," Brigid said.

Ivo felt heat climb his neck and was grateful for the firelight, which hid everything. He looked down at the twig in his hands and snapped it, quietly, and dropped both halves into the flames.

"My name is Ivo," he said. "That part's true. The keeper gave me that much, or I took it, I don't remember which. But the rest—" He lifted his chin toward the black mass of the mountain behind them, toward the hidden mouth of the plateau vent they'd climbed out of that morning, toward everything below it: the galleries, the drowned hall, the keeper's station, the ledger with its last entry ending mid-sentence. "The rest of my name is that. All of it. The stone and the water and the rails nobody owns. If I have to be called something, call me that."

"And what's that, exactly?" Brigid asked, her voice softer than he'd ever heard it.

Ivo opened his mouth to say *the gate*, and then closed it again, because saying it out loud suddenly felt like giving it away, like marking the entrance on a map for men with rifles. Instead he said:

"You'll know it when the train comes through."

Nobody laughed. He hadn't meant it as a joke, and they knew it. Sefu nodded slowly, the way he nodded at timber he'd just tested and found sound. Brigid reached over and knocked her tin cup against his, a small dull bell-ring, a toast to something unnamed. And Casper — Casper just looked at him for a long moment, and then reached into his coat and brought out the folded map, his father's map, the one that ended mid-line, and laid it on the ground between them, smoothing it flat with both palms.

"Then we'll need a new map," Casper said. "One that starts where the old one stops. And it should be drawn by someone who knows the territory." He tapped the blank space in the gorge, the gap where the Rivergate should have been. "When this survey's done — if it's ever done — I want you to draw this part. Your hand, not mine. A map isn't a record of the land. It's a record of who truly saw it."

Ivo stared at the empty space on the paper. In all his years in the gate he had never once thought of the passages as something that could be put down on paper — they were body-knowledge, echo-knowledge, the kind of knowing that lives in the legs and the ears and dies with the body. But looking at the blank space now, he felt a strange pulling sensation, as if the blankness itself were a door standing open.

"I can't draw letters good," he said.

"We'll fix that," Sefu said. "Man who can navigate by echo off wet stone can learn to make an S face the right direction."

The fire settled, collapsing inward in a shower of sparks, and the cold pressed closer around the ring of light. Somewhere out on the dark plateau, wind moved through the ruins of the shantytown, moaning low through broken window frames, and Ivo catalogued it automatically — *wind, north gap, hollow frame, harmless* — and then let the catalogue go, because for once he wanted to listen to a sound without sorting it.

Brigid stood, stretched, and announced she was taking first watch, and Sefu grumbled himself down into his bedroll with his bad leg propped on his pack. Casper banked the fire carefully, the way the keeper used to, and Ivo watched his hands do it and felt the ache and the comfort of that at once.

Before Casper lay down, Ivo spoke, quiet, almost lost under the wind.

"Casper."

The engineer looked up.

"When we go in again. When we go to the deep gate." Ivo held his eyes. "You should know the keeper's last entries stop there. Mid-sentence. Whatever your father found down there, he found it at the end, and he wrote until he couldn't write anymore." He paused. "I've never gone past that page. In six years, I never went past it."

Casper was silent a moment. The firelight moved on his face.

"Then tomorrow we go past it together," he said.

Ivo lay back on his bedroll with his back to stone and the fire dying warm at his feet, and above him the first stars came out over the plateau, hard and white as signal lamps. He was the Gate. He had said it aloud, and the sky had not fallen, and the mountain had not objected, and somewhere below them in the dark the deep gate waited with its unfinished sentence.

Tomorrow, he thought, they would find out how it ended.

And tomorrow, he realized as sleep came up to meet him like rising water, tomorrow he would have to walk into the one room in his whole kingdom he had never dared enter — and he would not be walking in alone.

* * *

He dreamed, that night, of the ledger.

In the dream the pages turned themselves, and the keeper's neat hand marched down each one, and Ivo walked along the margin of the writing like a boy walking a rail. The entries got shorter as the pages turned. *Inspected west brace. Sound.* *Tide high, galleries dry.* *Men from Harrow came again. Said nothing worth writing.* Then the pages began to blur, the letters swimming like fish, and the last clear words stood alone on the final page, and in the dream Ivo leaned close to read them, and woke.

The fire was embers. Brigid sat watch with her rifle across her knees, and beyond her the eastern sky had gone the grey of old pewter, and the wind had changed — Ivo felt it before he was fully awake, a new edge in it, a smell like iron and distance.

Snow-smell.

He sat up. Far off to the north, the peaks above the gorge had worn their first white caps overnight, thin and bright, like the mountain had begun signing its name in chalk.

"Winter's early," Brigid said quietly, following his gaze.

Ivo nodded, and touched the blank space on the map where it lay folded in Casper's coat, though Casper still slept and could not see him do it.

*The gate doesn't stop,* he reminded himself. *The gate stays.*

But even gates, he thought, looking at the white peaks, have depths they've never shown anyone.
