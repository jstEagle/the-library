## 19. The Collapsed Viaduct

They smelled it before they saw it.

Brigid named it first, because Brigid named everything: "Old iron," she said, wrinkling her nose against the wind that came down the gorge. "Rust and river rot. That's a dead bridge breathing."

Casper said nothing. He had known for half a day already — had felt it in the way the survey line bent toward the gorge instead of around it, in the way Ivo walked faster as they climbed the last ridge of scree, quick and light, the way a boy walks when he is going somewhere he has been told never to go. The keeper's map — his father's map, the one with the gap torn clean out of the Rivergate gorge — ended its final penciled bearing at a point Casper could now fix with his eye: a notch in the far canyon wall where two stone abutments should have stood.

Should have. Past tense was the whole geography of this place.

He crested the ridge at midmorning, breath smoking in the thin cold, and stopped.

The viaduct lay across the gorge like the skeleton of something enormous that had knelt down to drink and never gotten up. Nine piers of cut granite marched across the chasm from wall to wall, and six of them still stood, square-shouldered and patient, holding up nothing but sky. Where the deck had been there were only stubs of timber, blackened by forty years of weather, jutting from the pier caps like broken teeth. Two spans had fallen entire — great latticed ribs of iron and wood now lying along the riverbank below, half-buried in gravel bars, their rivets bleeding orange streaks down the stone. The remaining three spans sagged between their piers in slow catenaries of ruin, hanging by geometry and stubbornness alone.

The river ran beneath it all, green and indifferent, the same river that ran through the Tollgate, that had run through every chapter of his father's life and death.

"That's it," Sefu said quietly. He had come up beside Casper without sound, bad leg and all, and stood leaning on his walking stick, reading the ruin the way other men read scripture. "That's Amberdale's crossing. I've seen the drawings. In the office at Calder Junction, when I was an apprentice — they kept the old plans in a flat drawer nobody opened." He shook his head slowly. "I used to think the drawer was locked out of respect."

"Out of shame," Casper said.

Below them, on the near bank, Ivo had already begun to climb down.

* * *

They made camp on the shelf of flat ground above the western abutment, where someone had camped before them — long ago. Casper found the evidence within the first hour: a ring of stones blackened on the inside, a rusted tent peg, the neck of a bottle gone opalescent with age. His father's crew had lived here. Had eaten here, argued here, sung here, on ground Casper now stood on with his own boots. He crouched at the fire ring for a long time, saying nothing, while behind him Brigid and Sefu pitched the tents and Ivo vanished down the talus slope with the sureness of an animal returning to a den.

It was Ivo who found the strongbox.

The boy came up the slope at a dead run near dusk, breath ragged, eyes enormous, and grabbed Casper's sleeve — the first time he had ever touched him unasked — and pulled. *Come come come,* in the clipped whistle-tone he used for urgency, three short notes like a signal flag snapping in wind.

The strongbox was under a slab of fallen masonry on the downstream side of the second pier, wedged in a crevice where floodwater couldn't reach. It was an engineer's field chest, iron-bound, its lock long since corroded past function; Ivo had worried it open with a pry bar that looked older than he was. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth that crumbled at the touch, lay paper.

Casper knelt in the failing light and unwrapped his father's last work.

Field notebooks, seven of them, their covers stamped with the crest of the Rivergate Concession Company. A bundle of survey plats, drawn in the fine confident hand Casper knew better than his own — he had grown up watching that hand move across kitchen tables. And beneath everything, wrapped separately, a leather folio marked in block letters: **INSPECTION RECORDS — MATERIALS RECEIVED — DO NOT REMOVE FROM SITE.**

His hands were steady. He noticed that about himself, distantly, the way one notices the behavior of a stranger. He carried the folio back to the fire, and he sat down with it on his knees, and he began to read.

* * *

The entries began in the spring of what would have been the viaduct's second construction season. His father's handwriting, meticulous, each inspection dated and countersigned:

*April 14. Delivery of structural steel, lots 7 through 12, per Harrow Ironworks contract. Mill certificates attached. Visual inspection: lot 9 shows laminar scaling at flange edges inconsistent with certified grade. Sampled for tensile testing.*

*April 19. Tensile results, lot 9: 41,200 psi ultimate. Contract specification: 55,000 psi minimum. REJECTED. Returned to Harrow agent with written notice.*

*April 22. Lot 9 redelivered with new mill certificates, grade restamped. Restamp observed over original stamping. Rejected again. Copy of notice enclosed.*

*May 3. Received directive from Concession head office, countersigned H——: "Accept materials as certified. Further rejections will be considered grounds for removal."*

The fire cracked. Somewhere down in the dark, the river went on talking to itself. Casper turned the page.

His father had not obeyed. That was the thing that broke something open in Casper's chest — not the corruption, which he had half expected, which he had spent twenty years building a life sturdy enough to stand inside of without looking at — but the refusal. Entry after entry, through May and June, the same war fought in ink: steel rejected, steel redelivered with forged papers, steel rejected again, each rejection followed by another directive from above, each directive more thinly veiled, until the language stopped pretending:

*June 30. Instructed that continued objection will be "managed." I have shipped duplicate samples and copies of all records to [name illegible — water damage] at the provincial office. If these lots are used in the main spans, the structure will fail. Not may. Will. I am writing this so that no one can say afterward that it was not known.*

*July 11. Lots 9, 10, and 12 erected in spans four, five, and six over my written protest. I have signed nothing. They have signed for me.*

Then the handwriting changed. The last entries were hurried, the pen pressed hard enough to tear the paper in places:

*August 2. Span five showing deflection under test load beyond tolerance. Ordered crew off. Head office orders load test repeated "to confirm." I will not repeat it. I am taking the records off site tonight.*

And then, on the final page, in a different rhythm entirely — smaller, faster, the writing of a man who knew how little time the lamp had left in it:

*If the span goes while men are on it, it will be called weather, or workmanship, or my name. It will not be called Harrow steel. Whatever happens, the record must survive me. Bury it deep. The gate keeps what the gate keeps.*

Casper read that last line four times. *The gate keeps what the gate keeps.* His father had known about the cave. Had hidden the proof inside it — or meant to — in the keeper's station, in the place where ledgers were kept by whoever kept the gate. And Ivo had been keeping that station ever since, sweeping its floor, trimming its lamp, tending papers he couldn't read, waiting for a man who had walked into the mountain and never walked out.

The grief arrived first, old and familiar, the grief he had carried since he was nine years old and learned the word *vanished*. But underneath it, rising through it like water through limestone, came something hotter and less forgiving.

Rage.

Not the clean rage of a wrong done to him — he had made his peace, more or less, with what the world had done to Casper Amberdale. This was rage on behalf of the dead. Men had died on that span — eleven of them, the village said, though the village had lost count of the exact number the way villages lose count of things that make the counting unbearable. Eleven men had died because a company bought cheap steel and forged the certificates and told the one honest inspector to shut his mouth, and when the span came down they had buried the inspector's name under the word *vanish* and let the river do the rest.

And Harrow Line still existed. Still held charters. Still sent men like Aldous Vane out into the world with clean collars and clean contracts, buying permits, asking questions, smiling.

"Casper."

Brigid's voice, low. He looked up. All three of them were watching him — Brigid with her arms crossed against the cold, Sefu with his stick across his knees, Ivo sitting cross-legged closest of all, close enough to touch the folio, staring at the pages he couldn't read as if the shapes themselves might confess.

"You've gone white as the scree," Brigid said. "What's in it?"

Casper found that his throat had to be unlocked, joint by joint, before the words would come. "Proof," he said. "Forty years of proof. My father rejected the steel three times. Harrow forged the certificates. Head office ordered him to accept it anyway. He wrote it all down — dates, lot numbers, test results, names." He looked down at the folio, at the water-blurred signature on the June page. "They didn't just let the bridge fall. They built it knowing. And then they erased him."

Sefu exhaled slowly through his nose, the sound of a carpenter seeing a joint that was cut to fail. "Spans four, five, and six," he said. "Those'd be the ones down." He nodded at the fallen iron on the riverbank. "Cheap steel cracks cold. First hard autumn, loaded span, brittle fracture — whole rib lets go at once. No warning anyone could hear." He paused. "No warning anyone was allowed to hear."

"They'll say it's worthless," Brigid said. She said it flatly, not cruelly — she was the one who always said the hard thing first so it couldn't ambush anyone later. "Paper's forty years old. Names are dead. Half a page's water-eaten. Vane's lawyers will call it forgery before he finishes his first cup of coffee."

"It's not worthless," Casper said. "It's a map. Every lot number leads to a mill record. Every mill record leads to a purchase order. Paper chains, Brigid. You don't need one link to hold; you need enough links that breaking them looks like what it is." He heard his own voice harden and did not soften it. "My father built a chain and buried it. I'm going to finish laying it."

Ivo touched the folio then, one finger, careful as touching a sleeping animal. He looked up at Casper and asked, in his whistle-and-word speech, the question that went through Casper like a fishhook: *"This is why they took him?"*

"Yes," Casper said. "I think he was carrying this into the gate when they caught him."

Ivo withdrew his hand. For a moment the boy's face did something complicated and old, and then he stood, and whistled the low descending note he used for *enough*, and pointed down the slope toward the second pier, where in the last gray light something glinted — a shape he had apparently been saving.

"There's more," Casper translated, though no translation was needed. "He says there's more down there."

* * *

They went down at first light, all four of them, roped together on the talus because Brigid insisted and because Casper had learned, over these weeks, that insisting was how Brigid loved people. The fallen span lay along the bank like a beached leviathan, its lattice cells big enough to walk through, furred with orange rust and pale lichen. Sefu moved through it like a man moving through a cathedral, touching nothing at first, reading everything.

"Look at the fracture faces," he said, kneeling at the break where the bottom chord had parted. He beckoned Casper down. The exposed metal was rough, crystalline, granular — nothing like the fibrous tearing of good steel giving up its strength grudgingly. "See how it's all shiny facets? That's brittle failure. Cold crack. Good steel bends and complains before it dies. This stuff just — quits." He looked up, and his face, usually so mild, held an expression Casper had never seen on it. "I inspected bridges for eleven years. I know what killed this bridge. Any man who ever held a hammer knows."

"Knowing and proving," Brigid said from above, "are on opposite sides of a courtroom."

"Not anymore." Casper stood in the shadow of the fallen span with the folio under his arm and felt the rage settle in him, condense, take on weight and structure — become, in fact, something very much like his father's handwriting: dated, specific, unerasable. "Lot numbers match steel we can sample right here. Test results match fractures we can photograph. And somewhere in Greywater or Calder Junction there's a mill ledger with a Harrow purchase order for lots nine, ten, and twelve." He looked up at the standing piers, at the three sagging spans still clinging between them. "Vane wants this corridor buried under Harrow's charter. Instead I'm going to hang his company's own signature on it."

It was Ivo, scrambling along the top chord of the ruin, who found the last of it — a plate, bolted to the iron, that the boy cleaned with his sleeve and then called them to with a rising note of pure discovery. Cast into the metal, half obliterated by rust, a maker's mark:

**HARROW IRONWORKS — LOT 10**

Sefu laughed — a short, disbelieving bark. "They didn't even grind it off. Forty years, and the confession's been bolted to the murder weapon the whole time."

"Nobody looks at dead bridges," Brigid said. "That's the point of killing them."

But Casper was looking. He stood beneath the ruined span with the morning coming up gold behind the eastern rim, and he understood, finally and completely, what the price of vindication would be. Not danger — he had priced in danger since the clerk's pen scratched ninety days onto his permit. Not labor, not cold, not even the mountain itself. The price was this: to prove what happened here, he would have to drag his father's ghost through every hearing room in the province. Would have to let strangers read the last frightened lines of a dying man's handwriting. Would have to make the vanishing public, dissected, argued over by lawyers who had never met the man whose name they'd be handling. Grief, once entered into evidence, belonged to everyone.

He found he was willing to pay it. That surprised him most of all.

Above them, on the abutment shelf, a whistle sounded — Ivo's alarm note, two sharp highs, *strangers* — and Casper's blood went cold a full second before he raised his glasses to the southern ridge.

Figures. Four of them, maybe five, small with distance, moving along the ridgeline with the unhurried economy of men who were not lost and not hurrying because they did not need to. Men pacing a quarry.

One of them raised something that flashed — glass catching sun. A spyglass, aimed straight down at the viaduct. At them.

"Vane," Brigid said, and spat.

"No," Casper said slowly, lowering the glasses. His heart was hammering, but his mind had gone suddenly, terribly clear. "Vane doesn't sight us and wait. Vane moves." He turned to look at the three sagging spans overhead, at the cheap Harrow steel that had waited forty years for one excuse to finish falling. "Someone else does the waiting."

As if in answer, deep in the gorge, the river changed its note — and high above, with a sound like a giant clearing its throat, the fifth span shed a single rivet that rang off the granite and dropped, spinning, into the green water below.
