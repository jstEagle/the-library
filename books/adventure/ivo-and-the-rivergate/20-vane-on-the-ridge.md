## 20. Vane on the Ridge

The wind came off the plateau the way Aldous Vane imagined a verdict might come — cold, formal, and carrying no interest whatsoever in what a man had to say for himself.

He stood at the lip of the ridge with his field glasses raised, watching four figures move along the river of scree below. Amberdale walked in front, of course. The man had the gait of someone who believed the ground would hold him if he only kept his accounts straight with it. Behind him came the woman — Brigid Doyle, formerly of the Caledonian works, formerly of a union hall, formerly employed anywhere at all — hauling a coil of rope like it was a sleeping child. Then the carpenter with the ruined leg, keeping pace through sheer stubbornness of rhythm. And last, small and quick as a wren, the boy.

Vane adjusted the focus. The boy was carrying the lantern.

* * *

"Two miles back," said Hollis, his junior surveyor, crouched beside the theodolite case with a pencil frozen mid-note. "We've held that distance since dawn. If they look up—"

"They won't look up." Vane lowered the glasses. "People being followed don't look up. They look behind. There's nothing behind them but us, and we're careful."

"That's not what I meant."

"I know what you meant."

Hollis was twenty-six, with the kind of face that hadn't yet learned which thoughts to keep off it. He'd been with Harrow two years, hired out of the provincial college with first-class marks and a letter of recommendation describing him as *scrupulous*. Vane had read that word at the time and thought: *trainable*. Now he watched the young man's pencil hover over the page and understood that some words were warnings.

Below, the four figures stopped at a bend in the scree. Amberdale crouched, touched something — a cairn, perhaps, or a mark left by men thirty years dead — and then they moved on.

"You want me to log our position?" Hollis asked.

"I want you to log theirs. Bearing, elevation, rate of travel. Every hour."

"And ours?"

Vane looked at him. "Ours is not a survey, Mr. Hollis. Ours is an estimate."

* * *

They made camp early, in the lee of a granite shoulder that cut the wind into manageable pieces. Vane's crew numbered six: himself; Hollis; the Kessler brothers, who had done demolition work on three continents and asked questions on none; and Tam Broadfoot, a packer with hands like root vegetables and the discretion of a grave. Good men. Expensive men. Men whose wages were entered in a ledger under a column marked *contingencies*, because that was how Vane thought of everything now — everything between the signing of a contract and the closing of one.

He sat apart from their fire, as he always did, and took out his notebook.

The arithmetic was simple enough to do by firelight. The Harrow Line's charter over the Rivergate corridor reverted at first snow. After that, any survey conducted there belonged to whoever held the next concession — and Harrow would hold it, because Vane had spent eleven months and a considerable sum making sure of it. Permits, clerks, a brittle little man in a provincial office who signed what he was told to sign. All of it arranged. All of it *clean*.

Except for Casper Amberdale, who had walked into Greywater with ninety days of legal permission and his father's ghost in his pocket, and begun doing exactly what his father had done before him: finding things in that mountain that other people had paid good money to bury.

Vane turned to the earlier pages of the notebook. His own handwriting, neater then, from a meeting nearly a year ago in a room above a bank in the capital. A director of the Harrow Line — Vane never wrote names, only initials, a habit he'd learned from men more careful than himself — explaining the situation with the unhurried patience of a man discussing drainage.

*The old collapse. The viaduct. The steel was ours. The inspection was ours. The engineer who found the fault was dealt with, and the fault was buried with him. If the son goes into that mountain and comes out with paper, we are not a company with a problem. We are a company with a trial.*

And beneath that, in the director's voice but Vane's hand: *What is the cost of the alternative?*

The answer had been given to him across a polished table, and it had not been a number. It had been a shrug. *Mountains kill people, Vane. It's why we pay engineers.*

He had gone home that night and slept badly, and then he had slept fine, and he had told himself for eleven months that the second sleep was the honest one.

* * *

In the morning they climbed.

The route Vane had chosen ran along the high ridge parallel to the gorge — close enough to watch, far enough to be unseen, and, though he did not say this aloud even to himself, high enough that anything that happened below would happen without witnesses. The Kessler brothers moved ahead setting the pace. Broadfoot carried the heavy glass. Hollis brought up the rear with the instrument case, and every hour, on the hour, Vane heard the scratch of his pencil logging the figures of the people below.

At midday they gained a shelf of rock overlooking the whole upper gorge, and Vane raised the glasses again and found them immediately. They had reached the ruins. Even at this distance he could see what the ruins were: the stumps of piers, the shattered spine of a viaduct, thirty years of weather gnawing on masonry that had been cheap the day it was poured. His masonry. His company's steel, rusting in a graveyard his salary depended upon nobody measuring.

Amberdale stood among the wreckage, very still, and Vane felt — absurdly, distantly, the way one feels weather coming in a bad tooth — that the man was reading something. Not the stones. The *record*. Somewhere down there was a ledger or a plate or a stamped girder, and somewhere in Vane's own notebook was the meeting where he'd agreed that such things must never meet.

"Mr. Vane." Hollis beside him, breathing hard from the climb, glasses of his own raised. "They're at the old viaduct site. That's — sir, that's the 1897 collapse. That's where the—" He stopped.

"Say it."

"The inspection notes would be down there. If the keeper's station survived, the original records could have survived. Load tests. Steel certifications. Sir, if Amberdale gets certified proof of the steel batch to the provincial office—"

"He won't."

"How can you know that?"

Vane lowered the glasses. Below, the small party was moving again, picking along the broken abutment toward the gorge itself. Toward the crossing they would have to make, because there was no other way, because the mountain allowed exactly one road and charged for it.

"Because I've read the same mountain he has," Vane said. "There's no bridge down there, Mr. Hollis. There's a forty-yard gap with eighty-foot walls and a river at the bottom of it. He can't go around, he can't go back without losing his season, and he can't cross. Whatever he finds at those ruins, he'll have to leave it behind him, because paper doesn't jump."

Hollis was quiet a moment. "And if he builds something?"

"Out of what?"

"Out of the wreckage. He's an engineer. It's what they do."

Vane looked at the young man properly then — the set of his jaw, the way his thumb worried the edge of his notebook — and recognized, with the weary clarity of a man watching weather arrive, that the scrupulousness was not going to train out.

"It's what they do," Vane agreed. "And then the snow comes down early, and the wind comes off the icefield, and a rope span built by four exhausted people in a dying autumn fails at exactly the moment mountains fail things. And a board of inquiry sits in a warm room in the capital and writes *misadventure* in a fair hand, and the Harrow Line takes its charter, and everyone is sad for a week." He closed the notebook. "I am not killing anyone, Mr. Hollis. I am declining to save them. In law, and in every ledger I answer to, those are different entries."

"Are they different entries in yours?"

The question hung there. Below them, so far down that the sound arrived late and thin, the river said what rivers say.

"Log the position," Vane said. "Bearing, elevation, rate of travel."

* * *

That evening the wind turned.

It came down off the icefield in long white gusts, carrying the smell of snow that hadn't fallen yet but was thinking about it, and Vane sat against the granite with his coat buttoned to the throat and watched the fire the Kesslers had built shrink to a red eye. Broadfoot was asleep. The brothers played cards by touch, betting matches. Hollis sat at the edge of the light with his notebook open, not writing, just holding it, like a man holding a door shut.

Vane took out the notebook instead — the private one, the one with initials — and read the last entry he'd made in the capital. The director's final instruction, delivered with that same patient shrug:

*Follow if you must. Watch if you must. But let the mountain do the work, and let it do it alone. No names near it, Vane. No fingerprints. If a single charge of ours is found, if a single wire traces back, you will be the man between me and the gallows, and I want you to consider, calmly, what a man in your position is worth to me then.*

He had considered it calmly. That was the worst of it. He had sat in his rooms with the gas lit and considered it with the same clear head he brought to a load calculation, and he had concluded that he was worth precisely as much as his usefulness, and that his usefulness ended the day the corridor reverted.

So here he was. On a ridge. Watching.

From far below, faint on the wind, came a sound he needed a moment to place: hammering. Rhythmic, patient, unmistakable. Someone down in the dark was driving timber.

Vane found the glasses. The firelight of the lower camp was a smudge in the black throat of the gorge, right at the viaduct ruins, and around it — he couldn't be sure, the light was too poor, but he would have sworn it — scaffolding. Lines rising where no lines should be. The carpenter's rhythm carried up the rock, stroke after stroke after stroke, each one saying the same thing: *we are building, we are building, we are building.*

"He's actually doing it," Hollis said softly. He'd seen it too. "A span. Out of the old wreckage. Four days' work if they're lucky. Five if the weather—"

"Three days," Vane said. "Look at the sky."

Hollis looked. The clouds had come down to sit on the peaks like a lid on a pot.

"So he fails," the young man said. "Or he turns back. Either way, you win. You don't need to be here for that, sir. You could go down to Greywater and wait. Warm room. Telegram office." His voice was carefully level, and Vane heard what was underneath it, the way one hears water under ice. "Why are we still on this ridge?"

Because the director did not trust the mountain alone. Because *let the mountain do the work* had a second clause that had never been written down but was understood by everyone at that polished table: *and be there to see it done*. Because a man who paid for contingencies liked his contingencies confirmed.

But what Vane said was: "Because estimates require observation."

Hollis nodded slowly, and wrote something in his notebook — not bearings, Vane suspected, not elevations — and the scratching of the pencil sounded, in the wind, like a small conscience trying to keep its accounts.

* * *

He woke past midnight to silence, which on a ridge is louder than noise.

The hammering had stopped. Vane crawled to the shelf's edge with the glasses and found the lower camp dark — truly dark, no fire, no lantern moving, nothing. Snow was falling now, fat and slow, flakes big as moths, erasing the scree, the gorge, the wrecked viaduct, the whole argument of the landscape. Somewhere under that whiteness four people were either sleeping, or trapped, or already beyond anyone's help, and the mountain was doing exactly what the director had paid for, invisibly, namelessly, without a fingerprint.

*No names near it,* Vane thought. *No fingerprints.*

Except his own, he realized, were all over it. On the permits he'd greased. On the clerk he'd bought. On the orders he'd relayed and the denials he'd drafted and the eleven months of clean, patient, well-paid arrangement. The mountain might kill them, but the mountain had been *aimed*.

Behind him, a match scraped. Hollis, awake, sitting up with his notebook on his knees.

"I can hear you thinking, sir," the young man said quietly.

"Go back to sleep."

"In the morning, when the snow clears — if we look down there and there's nothing to look at, four sets of tracks ending at a broken bridge — what do we write in the log?"

Vane didn't answer at once. Down in the dark, under the falling snow, he thought he could almost hear the river, and under the river, fainter, the memory of a shrug across a polished table.

"We write what's true," he said finally. "Weather. Distance. Misadventure."

"Yes, sir," Hollis said, and lay back down, and did not sleep, and Vane knew it, because neither did he.

Above them the snow thickened, sealing the ridge, sealing the gorge, sealing — if the mountain had its way — every loose end in the Rivergate country under one clean white sheet. And far below, patient as a heartbeat in the dark, the hammering began again.
