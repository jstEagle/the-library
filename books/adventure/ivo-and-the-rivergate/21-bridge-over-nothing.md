## 21. Bridge Over Nothing

Sefu had been looking at the gorge for an hour before he said anything, because the first thing a carpenter learns is that the wood will tell you what it wants to be if you give it silence long enough.

The gorge did not want to be crossed. That was the honest answer, and Sefu had built his whole second life on honest answers. It ran maybe ninety feet from lip to lip, but the ninety feet were a lie of their own — the near edge crumbled into loose shale that would not hold a footing stake, and the far side rose in a sheer face of blue-grey granite where his father's viaduct had once anchored its final pier. Below, the river ran white and loud over fallen stone, and the wind came up the throat of it with a low moaning note, like a man humming through closed teeth.

He shifted his weight off his bad leg and leaned on the surveyor's tripod Casper had left planted at the edge.

"Talk to me," Brigid said behind him. She'd come up carrying two of everything, as she always did — two coils of rope, two hammers, two tin cups of tea gone cold. "You've got the face."

"What face?"

"The one you make when the answer's no but you're working out how to say yes anyway."

Sefu almost smiled. "My father used to say a bridge is just a lie the ground agrees to tell for a while." He pointed with his chin at the wreckage below. "Problem is, this ground already told its lie once. The viaduct. And it took the truth down with it."

Brigid set the cups on a flat stone and looked out over the drop. The morning was hard and clear, the kind of light that showed every flaw in a thing. Forty feet down, snagged on a shelf of rock above the rapids, lay the bones of the old span — twisted lattice trusses, a tangle of cable, timbers black with forty years of weather but still, here and there, holding their shape. His father's crew had died building this. Somewhere under that wreckage were men whose names Casper had read aloud three nights ago by lamplight, names that had been struck from every ledger Harrow kept.

"You're thinking about the steel," Brigid said.

"I'm thinking about everything." He crouched — carefully, favoring the left knee, the one the scaffold had ruined at Copper Bend eleven years ago — and drew in the shale dust with a stick. "Ninety feet clear. No natural anchor on this side worth trusting. Far side we've got the old pier stub, and if the granite held a viaduct once, it'll hold four eyebolts now. So we don't build from this side. We build from theirs."

"And get across to the far side how?"

Sefu looked at the wreckage again. At the cable.

"With what they left us."

* * *

They spent the rest of that day and half the next hauling the lightest of the old cables up from the shelf, and it was Ivo who solved the problem of getting down to it.

The boy went over the edge like water finding a crack. No rope at first — just hands and bare feet finding holds in rock that offered none, moving with the unhurried certainty of someone who had been climbing this gorge since before he could name it. Sefu watched from above with his heart in his mouth and his hands gripping nothing, and understood suddenly why the others spoke of Ivo the way sailors spoke of weather: not as a person to be reasoned with but as a force to be respected.

"Throw me the line," Ivo called up, standing on the shelf itself, dwarfed by the dead viaduct's ribs. "The small one. I'll rig a haul."

"Boy, you tie yourself off first," Sefu shouted back, "or so help me I'll come down there and—"

Ivo looked up at him with those flat grey eyes that gave away nothing and everything. "You can't climb this," he said. Not cruelly. Just true, the way Ivo's words always were. Then he tied himself to a stanchion with a knot Sefu recognized — a bowline on a bight, properly dressed, the kind of thing no feral child should know — and waited.

So somebody had taught him. Somebody had stood on this rock and put a rope in his hands and shown him the loop and the turn, years ago, before the ledgers stopped. Sefu fed the line down slowly and thought: *the last keeper taught him knots.* He didn't say it aloud. Some things you let a boy keep.

By dusk they had a light haul-line rigged from the far pier stub back across the gap, and by full dark, working by lantern light with Brigid belaying him and Casper hauling, Sefu had walked the first heavy cable hand-over-hand along it — a hundred and twenty feet of inch-and-a-half wire rope, salvaged from the wreck, its galvanizing worn to brown lace in places but its core, when he tested it against a chisel, still sound.

Mostly sound. He marked the doubtful spots with red yarn and told no one yet.

* * *

The design came to him in the night, the way designs did — whole, unasked-for, arriving like a guest who'd been waiting outside the door.

He woke in the shantytown ruins with frost on his blanket and the shape of the thing already built in his head: a suspension footbridge, single-span, catenary sag of six feet, deck of split timber laced between two main cables with rope cross-bracing every four feet. Two handrail cables slung from A-frame timber towers at each end, because a bridge without rails is a dare, and he wasn't building dares. Anchors on the far side into the old pier stub — drilled, wedged, grouted with whatever mortar Brigid could mix from the viaduct's own collapsed masonry. Anchors on the near side — that was the problem, the shale, the crumbling lip —

— solved by burying. Deadman anchors. Timber cribs sunk six feet into the slope behind solid ground, loaded with stone, the cables running through buried channels so the earth itself became the holding. His grandfather had built fences that way in country where every post hole was a fight. You didn't argue with bad ground. You made the good ground do the work.

In the morning he drew it in the dirt for all of them, and Casper studied it the way Casper studied everything — silently, completely, twice.

"The sag's right," Casper said finally. "But the deck timber. Where?"

"Wreckage," Sefu said. "There's dimensional lumber down on that shelf — stringers off the old approach ramp. Black, wet, forty years cold. But it's pitch pine, and pitch pine doesn't rot easy even drowned. We split it, we check every piece, we use only what rings true when you knock it." He rapped his knuckles on a stone. "Sound wood tells you it's sound. Rotten wood lies quiet."

"That's a proverb or a memory?" Brigid asked.

"My father. He was a joiner in Mombasa before the company shipped him here. Everything he made, he'd tap it and listen like a doctor." Sefu looked out at the gorge. "First thing he ever taught me. Last thing too, near enough — the company never wanted men who listened to the material. They wanted men who told the material what to be."

Casper was quiet a moment. "That's why they fired me," he said. "More or less. I listened to a trestle and it told me things the schedule couldn't afford to hear."

"Well then." Sefu rolled up his imaginary sleeves, which was habit, since he wore the same wool shirt every day. "We understand each other."

* * *

Four days. That was the honest count, and Sefu gave it to them straight around the fire that night, drawing in the ash with a stick while the others watched.

Day one: salvage and split the deck timber, drill and set the far anchors. Day two: hang the second main cable, tension both, build the towers. Day three: lace the deck, rail cables, cross-bracing. Day four: test, load, test again, and only then — only then — walk it.

"We don't have four days," Casper said. Not arguing. Counting. Sefu had learned that about him: the man counted everything, snowfall, food, hours, the distance between hope and arithmetic.

"No," Sefu agreed. "We have maybe five before the snowline comes down to meet us, and Vane's people are somewhere behind us eating our margin. So we work in the dark and we work tired and we don't cut a single corner, because a corner cut on a bridge isn't a shortcut, it's a grave with extra steps." He tapped the ash drawing. "Or we rush it, and we find out in the middle of the span which of us the mountain wants."

Nobody argued. That was the thing about this crew, the thing that still surprised him after weeks on the trail together — nobody argued with the truth when it was spoken plainly. Brigid nodded and started listing what she could blast loose from the wreckage without bringing the shelf down. Ivo simply got up and began coiling rope in the dark, because Ivo heard *work* the way other children heard dinner bells.

Casper stayed crouched by the drawing a moment longer. "My father designed a viaduct here," he said quietly. "And they built it cheap, and it fell, and the men who fell with it were blamed for the steel that killed them." He looked up. "Build it true, Sefu. Whatever else happens. Build it so true that when they find it — and they'll find it — nobody can ever say these men cut corners."

Sefu felt something settle in his chest, heavy and warm at once. "Mr. Amberdale," he said, "that's the only way I know how to build."

"Casper."

"Casper," Sefu amended, and smiled, because his leg hurt and his hands were cracked and he was happier than he had been in eleven years of walking away from things.

* * *

The days went the way days go when you spend them against a deadline — simultaneously endless and gone.

He worked the far side mostly, because his leg was better on a ladder than on scree and worse at hauling than at fitting, and because someone had to be the man on the anchor end when the cables came across. Brigid ran the salvage below with an ingenuity that frightened him a little; she used her charges like a surgeon uses a scalpel, quarter-sticks taped to specific members of the wreck, dropping exactly the steel she wanted onto exactly the shelf she wanted, and not one stone more. "Dynamite's honest," she said, when he told her so. "It does precisely what you ask and nothing else. It's the only thing on this mountain that does."

Casper hauled until his palms bled and then wrapped them and hauled more. Ivo went everywhere the ropes couldn't, carrying tools up faces Sefu wouldn't have attempted with wings, and slept, when he slept at all, curled against the warm rock like it was a living thing that might leave him.

On the second evening, splitting deck timber by lantern light, Sefu's knife slipped on a frozen knot and opened his thumb to the bone. Brigid bandaged it without a word, her hands quick and sure, and Ivo appeared beside him holding — of all things — a strip of pitch pine already whittled smooth, a splint, shaped by someone who had watched and understood and acted before being asked.

"Where'd you learn splints?" Sefu asked him.

Ivo shrugged. "Bodies break here," he said. "You learn to fix them or you learn to bury them." And he went back to the fire, and Sefu sat with his splinted thumb and thought about a twelve-year-old alone in a cave with a dead man's ledger, learning medicine from necessity, and had to look at the gorge for a while until his eyes behaved.

* * *

By the fourth dawn the bridge existed.

Not finished — the deck was laced only halfway across, the rail cables hung slack, the towers braced but not yet fully pinned. But the two main cables swept their catenary curve across the ninety feet of nothing, taut and singing faintly in the wind, and when Sefu struck one with the flat of his hammer it rang a clean low note that carried down the gorge and came back changed.

"There," he said, to no one, to everyone. "Hear that? That's a true tension. She's telling us what she is."

"She?" Brigid said.

"Everything I build is a she. My father's rule. You take better care of something you'd call by name."

He spent that day doing the work he loved most and trusted least: testing. He loaded the completed half-span with stone until the cables groaned their note a half-step lower, watched the deflection, measured it against his figures, unloaded, watched it sing back true. He inspected every yard of cable by hand, running it through a rag — the rag catching where wire was broken, sliding clean where it was sound. He found three broken-wire patches on the upstream cable and marked each with red yarn, and calculated, and recalculated, and concluded with a carpenter's stubbornness that the sound core would carry five times their worst load.

Mostly he believed his own conclusion. Carpenters have to. Doubt is a fine tool for inspection and a terrible one for walking on.

That evening the clouds came down from the north like a lid closing, and the wind turned, and everyone stood at the gorge rim watching the first grey smudge of snow eat the high peaks to the west.

"Tomorrow," Casper said. "We finish the deck tomorrow and cross tomorrow night."

"Then tomorrow we finish the deck," Sefu said.

But he lay awake a long time that night, listening to the wind hum through his half-built bridge, and somewhere in the dark hours he got up with his lantern and walked the near anchorage one more time, and the far one, and ran his rag along the cables one more time, and told himself the unease in his gut was just the weather coming.

The rag caught on something new. A single broken wire, high on the upstream cable, thirty feet out from the far tower — right where the wind funneled hardest through the gorge. One wire. Out of a hundred and nineteen. Nothing. He marked it with red yarn, wrote it in his head alongside the others, and told himself: *nothing.*

The wind moaned through the span all night, and the note it made was very slightly wrong, and Sefu, who had spent his whole life listening to materials tell the truth, lay in his blanket and could not decide whether what he heard was the bridge — or the mountain, clearing its throat.
