## 22. Crossing

They crossed at first light, because Sefu said the wind slept in the hour before dawn and woke angry, and because every one of them had spent the night listening to that wrong note and pretending they hadn't.

The bridge was a skeleton of Sefu's making — two cables of braided hemp and salvaged wire strung across the gorge where the viaduct's spine had snapped forty years ago, decked with ladder-sections of viaduct timber lashed crosswise. It spanned perhaps ninety feet of nothing. Below it the river ran white and small, so far down that its sound arrived after its sight, like thunder from a dead storm.

Casper went first, as he'd insisted, with the instrument case slung across his back on a strap Brigid had doubled and stitched herself. The transit, the levels, the chain, the notebooks — four days of Sefu's carpentry and thirty years of his father's ghost, all riding between his shoulder blades.

"Weight forward," Sefu called from the near tower, his bad leg braced against the timber, hands busy on the anchor even now, tightening what needed no tightening. "Don't look down unless you mean it."

"I never mean it," Casper said, and stepped out.

The deck swayed — not violently, but with a slow, oceanic patience, the whole span breathing under him. The gorge wind came up cold off the snowline above them, carrying the smell of stone and coming winter. He kept his eyes on Ivo, who had already crossed twice before sunrise without being asked to, moving along the cable itself like something born in rigging, and who now crouched at the far tower watching Casper with that flat, unblinking attention that was either judgment or affection; after three weeks, Casper still couldn't tell the difference, and had stopped needing to.

Brigid came next, light for all her muscle, chattering the whole way because silence made her think about the drop. "You know what this is? This is a rope bridge built by a man with one good leg out of the corpse of a bridge built by liars. There's a sermon in that. Somebody write it down." Her laugh bounced off the gorge walls and came back thinner.

She was ten feet from the far tower when the upstream cable spoke.

It wasn't a snap. It was worse than a snap — a long, rising *whine*, a wire parting somewhere ahead of her, and then another, and the whole cable jumped like a struck harp string. The red yarn marker, thirty feet out, whipped loose into the air. The deck lurched sideways and dropped a foot.

Casper felt the world tilt. He threw himself flat against the far tower's timber, arms locked around the post, and watched — everything slowed, everything precise, the engineer's curse of seeing exactly what is happening — watched the fraying cable shed its strands one by one, watched the deck swing, watched Brigid drop to her knees and grab for the lower cable as the planks beneath her tilted toward the open air.

Ivo moved before anyone shouted.

Later, Casper could never reconstruct it properly. There was the boy going out along the downstream cable hand over hand, feet hooked in the strands, no hesitation, no calculation — the way water moves, the way things fall. There was Brigid sliding, boot heels skidding on wet timber, one hand on the lower cable and the other clawing at nothing. And there was the moment the upstream cable let go entirely — a sound like the mountain clearing its throat at last — and half the deck swung down and outward over the gorge, and Brigid hung from her one grip with the river a thousand feet below her boots, and Ivo reached her.

He got a fist in her collar. That was all. A child's fist in a grown woman's coat, holding her against the full arithmetic of her weight and the swing of the broken span, while the remaining cable groaned and the gorge wind pushed them both like a hand testing a door.

"Hold," Ivo said. Not loudly. In the voice he used for signals, clipped and absolute. "Hold. Hold."

Brigid held. Casper hauled himself along the tower to the anchor point, found the tail end of the broken cable whipping free, caught it on the second grab, and wrapped it twice around the post while Sefu shouted instructions from the far side that were mostly profanity arranged into engineering. Between them — boy, woman, engineer, carpenter, none of them speaking in sentences anymore, only in tugs and counterweights and names — they brought her up over the rail of planks and onto solid rock at the tower's base, where she lay on her back gasping at the sky and laughing, or crying, or both, in the way of people who have just been handed back to themselves.

It was only when Casper's own breathing slowed that he noticed the absence on his back.

He turned. The strap had torn clean through when the deck dropped — he could see the end of it, frayed white, still looped through the tower's iron ring where he'd clipped it without thinking, some instinct saving the thing that held everything else. But the case itself had swung wide on the release, out over the gorge, and hung there for one impossible moment at the end of its stitching —

— and then the stitching finished its work, and let go.

They all watched it fall. There was nothing else to do. It turned slowly, catching the early light, brass fittings flashing once, twice, and then the gorge took it, and the river swallowed the sound of it landing, and there was nothing below but white water and distance.

Nobody spoke for a long time. The broken cable dangled into the gorge beside them, its severed end ticking softly against the rock.

"The transit," Brigid said finally, sitting up. Her voice was steady, which cost her something; Casper could see it costing her. "The levels. The chain."

"The notebooks?" Sefu called across.

"In the case." Casper heard his own voice come out flat and strange. "All of it. Every measurement since Greywater. Every bearing, every grade, every chain-length." He looked at his empty hands. "Half the survey. The legal half. Without instruments and field notes, what we've charted is a story. A story isn't evidence. I learned that much from Harrow."

Ivo stood apart from them at the tower's edge, looking down into the gorge, and said nothing at all.

* * *

They made camp on the far side because there was no sense going back, and no strength to go forward. Brigid sat wrapped in blankets by the fire, unhurt except for rope burns on her palm and a bruise blooming along her ribs, drinking tea she didn't taste. Sefu had gone silent in the way he did when his mind was working — staring into the flames, rebuilding the crossing in his head, finding the exact wire, the exact load, the exact moment his craft had failed him.

"It wasn't your bridge," Casper told him.

"It was my bridge." Sefu didn't look up. "One wire in a hundred and nineteen. I marked it. I heard it all night and I marked it and I called it nothing." His jaw worked. "Nothing is how bridges kill you. Nothing is how men die on viaducts built from cheap steel that somebody signed off on. I know better. I've known better my whole life."

"You'll build the next one knowing better still," Casper said. "That's the whole trade."

But he said it hollowly, because he was doing his own arithmetic, and the sum was ruin. Ninety days, spent. Permits expired or expiring. Instruments at the bottom of a gorge. And somewhere behind them, Vane's crew moving through country Vane knew, toward a prize that no longer legally existed.

He walked away from the fire as the light failed, out along the cliff edge, and stood where the ground fell away into dark, and let himself feel it — really feel it, for the first time since Greywater. The grief of it. Four days of Sefu's labor, weeks of measurements taken in cold and fear, the whole paper architecture of vindication, gone in four seconds of parted stitching. His father had lost everything to Harrow's lies, and now Casper had lost the means of proving anything at all, and the mountain would close over the corridor with the first real snow, and that would be the end of it: another Amberdale undone by the Rivergate, another name added to the ledger of what the gorge takes.

He was still standing there when Ivo came to stand beside him. The boy didn't ask what was wrong. He never asked; he simply arrived, the way weather arrives, and waited.

"Half our evidence is at the bottom of that gorge," Casper said.

Ivo considered this with the seriousness he gave everything. Then he pointed — not down into the gorge, but back, along the route they'd traveled. Through the dark, Casper could just make out the black seam of the cave mouth high on the opposite slope, the passage they'd come out of, the galleries, the keeper's station, the ledgers, the rails of a gauge no company on record ever used.

"You're counting paper," Ivo said.

"What?"

"Your evidence." He tapped his own chest, then gestured at the mountain, the whole dark mass of it. "It's in there. The rails are in there. The ledger is in there. The gate is in there." He shrugged, a small, fierce motion. "Vane can burn paper. He can't burn a tunnel."

Casper stared at him. And then it came, the realization arriving not as a thought but as a physical event, a pressure behind the sternum, a straightening of the spine — the way understanding always came to him on site, all at once, the drawing assembling itself in the air.

The cave. The cave itself. Forty miles of collapsed line bypassed by a passage carved with rail-gauge notches, floored with experimental rails, documented in a keeper's ledger going back decades — a working structure, standing, measurable, walkable. You couldn't lose it in a gorge. You couldn't forge it, buy it, or bury it under a charter, because it was stone and iron and it was *there*, and every inch of it contradicted everything Harrow had sworn for forty years. They didn't need the transit. They needed witnesses, and testimony, and the wit to bring the right men through the gate before the snow sealed it — or after, if it came to that, because a tunnel through a mountain does not expire the way a permit does.

"We don't have to replace the instruments," Casper said slowly, hearing the plan take shape in his own mouth. "We have to lead people through. Inspectors. Engineers. Anyone with the authority to see it and the honesty to say what they saw. The cave is the evidence. It's been the evidence all along. I've been carrying the proof on my back when it was standing around me the whole time."

Ivo nodded once, satisfied, and turned back toward the fire.

Casper stayed a moment longer at the edge, looking down into the dark where the instruments lay among the stones, giving them their due. Then he went back to the fire, where Brigid looked up at him with red-rimmed eyes and Sefu raised an eyebrow over the flames.

"Change of plan," Casper said. "We're not finishing a survey. We're building a procession."

And it was Sefu — listening, as always, to things farther off than any of them — who stiffened suddenly, head tilting toward the darkness beyond the firelight, toward the ridge above the plateau where the wind carried sounds up from the valley trails.

Because the wind had changed direction. And on it, faint but unmistakable, came the ring of iron on stone. Many boots. Many men. Coming down off the heights behind them, moving fast.

Moving toward the deep gate.
