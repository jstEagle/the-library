## 23. Snowline

The first flake came down like a question.

Ivo saw it before any of them did — a small white turning in the air above the plateau, separate from the ash-grey sky, drifting with the unhurried patience of something that had all the time in the world and intended to use it. He stopped walking. Behind him, Casper's boots crunched on frost-brittle grass, and Brigid said something to Sefu about the rope coils chafing her shoulder, and none of them noticed that the season had just put its hand on their necks.

Ivo held out his palm. The flake landed in the crease of it, six-sided, perfect, and melted against his skin in the space of one heartbeat.

*Two short, one long,* he thought, the way he thought in signals when words felt too big for his mouth. *Line closed ahead.*

"Casper," he said.

Casper came up beside him, following the boy's gaze upward, and Ivo watched the understanding move across the man's face like weather — first confusion, then recognition, then the particular stillness of an engineer doing arithmetic he didn't want to do. Casper turned his hand over and caught the second flake, and the third, and then looked south toward the peaks where the clouds had begun to organize themselves into something with intent.

"That's early," Casper said quietly.

"That's *early*," Ivo agreed.

Behind them, Sefu lowered himself onto a boulder with the careful choreography of a man whose leg had opinions about standing, and Brigid set down the blasting satchel she never let more than arm's length from her body. They were four small figures on a high shelf of stone, with the drowned gallery and the viaduct gorge behind them and nothing ahead but country none of them had mapped.

"Tell me about winter here," Casper said. "You've lived through them."

Ivo crouched and drew in the frost with one finger: the plateau, the ridge line, the dark seam of the range. "Snow comes from there." A slash at the north. "Fills the passes by the feast of lights. After that, the high routes are graves. Men have walked into them thinking they'd walk out." He drew a line along the mountain's flank, curving inward, downward. "But the mountain has insides."

Brigid's head came up. "You mean the gate."

"The upper galleries close too — ice seals the seaward mouths, the wind finds every crack and plugs it. But the deep passages don't freeze. Water runs warm under the rock. The old keeper told me—" Ivo stopped. The old keeper had told him many things, in the years when he still spoke much at all, and most of those things Ivo had folded away like papers too fragile to unfold. He smoothed the frost flat instead. "There are ways through. Winter ways. The river keeps its own road open."

Sefu squinted at the drawn line. "And you've walked these winter ways yourself?"

Ivo was quiet long enough that the silence became its own answer.

"No," he said finally. "Not the deep way. Not past the third door."

"What's at the third door?" Brigid asked.

Ivo looked at the place on the mountain where the ground fell away toward the hidden galleries, though nothing could be seen — the gate kept its face turned inward. "The ledger," he said. "The last ledger. Where the writing stops."

* * *

They made camp early that night, in the lee of a fallen watchtower whose stones the laborers had cut before Ivo was born. Casper built the fire small and shielded, the way men build fires who have learned what light costs, and Brigid rationed the dried fish with the grim fairness of a quartermaster, and Sefu recited from memory the elevations they'd taken since the crossing, his voice going soft and rhythmic until it was almost a lullaby — numbers as prayer, measurement as comfort.

Ivo sat apart, as he always sat apart, but not so far as he once would have. That was new. That was theirs, this distance-that-wasn't-distance, this arrangement the four of them had made without ever discussing it: near enough to be warmed, far enough to remain himself.

He was sharpening the little knife the keeper had left him when Casper came and sat beside him, not asking permission, not apologizing either. That was Casper's way. He simply arrived, the way weather arrived, and waited for you to adjust.

"The deep gate," Casper said. "You've been inside it."

"I've been to the door."

"And no farther."

Ivo turned the knife in his hands. "The keeper went in. Many times. He'd come back with mud on his boots and a look on his face like a man who'd been arguing with someone underground." He hesitated. "The last time, he didn't come back."

"You were a child."

"I was always a child. Then one day I wasn't, and he still hadn't come back." The knife's edge caught firelight. "I went to the third door after. Called his name. Called the signal — three short, the come-home call. Nothing answered except my own voice coming back wrong. Slower. Like the mountain was chewing on it." He looked up at Casper. "I decided the deep gate belonged to whatever took him. I decided I would keep everything else and leave that alone. Eleven years, I've left it alone."

Casper nodded slowly. In the firelight his face was all planes and shadow, and Ivo thought, not for the first time, how much the man looked like the sketches in the back of the old ledgers — the same stubborn jaw, the same eyes that measured things even when they were only looking at a boy.

"My father's final entries end mid-sentence," Casper said. "In the keeper's station. You showed me. 'If the water rises past the third mark, do not—'" He didn't finish it. Neither of them did. The sentence hung between them, unfinished for eleven years, unfinished now.

"Do you know what the third mark is?" Ivo asked.

"No. Do you?"

"No." Ivo fed a twig to the fire. "That's what frightens me. Not the dark. Dark is just a room with the lamp out. What frightens me is that the keeper knew something, wrote half of it, and the other half is sitting in the deep gate waiting for us. Knowledge shouldn't be able to wait. But that knowledge has waited eleven years, patient as tide."

Across the fire, Sefu had gone quiet, listening without pretending otherwise. Brigid lay with her arms behind her head, staring at the sky, where the flakes had thickened from questions into answers.

"So we don't go," Sefu offered. "We winter on the plateau. Ugly, but survivable. I've wintered worse."

"On what food?" Brigid said flatly. "On what fuel? This isn't a wintering spot, it's a waiting spot, and there's a difference. Waiting spots kill you polite."

"Then we go down and around—"

"Through Harrow territory," Casper said. "Past Vane's crews. With no permits worth the paper and no evidence worth the name." He shook his head. "The deep gate is the only road. And maybe—" he paused, and Ivo heard the thing underneath the pause, the hope wearing work clothes, "—maybe it's more than a road. If my father found proof down there, real proof, then the deep gate isn't just how we survive the snow. It's why we should go in at all."

Ivo stared into the fire and felt the fear rearrange itself, which was somehow worse than the fear had been. It was one thing to be afraid of a place. Places you could refuse. But he was beginning to understand that the deep gate was not a place. It was a truth with walls around it, and truths did not stay sealed because a boy decided not to knock.

* * *

It snowed all night, gently, treacherously, the kind of snow that seemed apologetic while it buried things.

Ivo woke before dawn out of habit older than memory and lay listening to the hush. Snow changed the world's voice. It packed every sharp edge in wool, ate echoes whole, and for a boy who navigated by echo the way other people navigated by sight, it was like going deaf by degrees. He hated it with a specific, professional hatred.

By grey morning there were four inches on the watchtower stones and the world had been simplified — black rock, white skin, no middle distances. Their tracks from yesterday already filling. Brigid stood at the camp's edge stamping her boots, breath steaming, looking like a woman being personally insulted by the sky.

"Well," she announced to no one and everyone, "it's started."

Casper spread the map on a flat stone, weighting the corners with pebbles, and Ivo knelt beside him and began, for the first time in front of anyone, to draw the winter routes from memory. His finger traced lines that existed nowhere on paper — the low gallery that ran beneath the frozen falls; the chimney vent that breathed warm air all winter and could be followed by feel; the long descent through strata the keeper had called the ribs, where the rock arched overhead like the skeleton of something enormous and patient.

"And here," Ivo said, and his finger stopped, "the third door."

The others gathered. Even Sefu leaned in, bad leg forgotten, eyes moving over the penciled geometry with the hunger of a craftsman shown another craftsman's work.

"How far to the door from the vent shaft?" Casper asked.

"Half a day. Good going. The passage widens, then narrows, then—" Ivo made a shape in the air with both hands, a narrowing, a threshold. "Then the door. It's not a door like wood and iron. It's a place where the mountain pinches shut, and the keeper had it timbered. Framed it like a portal. There's a bell-pull. A signal wire runs through, old ones, the kind that hummed down the tunnels before telegraph." He swallowed. "Beyond it, the galleries drop toward the river's root. Warm water. Black water. The keeper's last ledgers were found in the station, but his *first* last ledgers — the working books — he kept beyond the door. I've seen the shelf through the gap. Never touched them."

"Why not?" Brigid asked, and her voice had lost its edge; it was almost gentle, which from Brigid was more alarming than shouting.

Ivo looked at the fire pit, at the grey ash, at anything. "Because if I read them," he said, "then it becomes true that he's not coming back. As long as the books stayed shut, some part of me could keep the come-home signal ready. Three short. Every night for two years, I rang the wire at the third door. Three short, and listened." His jaw tightened. "The mountain never once answered."

Nobody spoke. The snow ticked softly against the tarp stretched over the supplies. Somewhere below the plateau, muffled by stone and white, the river went on saying the only word it knew.

"We'll go together," Casper said at last. "All four. Nobody reads anything alone. Whatever's written past that door, we face it the way we crossed the gorge — roped, spaced, watching each other." He met Ivo's eyes. "You don't have to be the one who opens it. But I'm asking you to be the one who leads us to it. Because nobody else alive knows the way, and I trust your knowing more than I trust my own."

Ivo felt the words land somewhere under his ribs, in the place where he kept the things he couldn't say. *Trusted.* Not tested, not this time — trusted. It was a different weight entirely. A heavier one. The kind of weight you carried because you'd agreed to.

"All right," he said. "One condition."

"Name it."

"When we get to the door — before anyone touches the books, before anything — I ring the wire. One last time. Three short." His voice didn't waver, but it wanted to. "So some part of me knows I kept faith. And then we open it, and whatever's true in there, we let it be true."

Casper put out his hand, and Ivo took it, and the grip was brief and hard and said everything the ceremony of it required.

Behind them, Sefu was already re-coiling rope with his quick precise hands, and Brigid had pulled out her charge kit and begun counting fuses with her lips moving, and the camp dissolved into motion the way good camps did — each person becoming useful, the group becoming a machine with a warm heart.

Ivo stood a moment longer, looking north, where the snow was coming down thicker now, fat flakes with serious intentions, erasing the plateau inch by inch. The mountain's white silence was settling over everything, and somewhere beneath it, past the vent and the ribs and the timbered pinch of the third door, the keeper's last words waited in the dark with the patience of water.

*Three short,* he thought. *Come home.*

Eleven years, and no answer. But tomorrow he would ask one more time — and this time, he would not stop at the door.

He shouldered his pack and turned to lead them down into the mountain's throat, toward the truth he had spent his whole life leaving sealed.

The snow followed them in.
