## 26. Marta's Telegram

The telegram office in Greywater was a closet with a window, and the window belonged to Odell Prine.

Marta had known that fact for eleven years — ever since the railway pulled its agent out of Greywater and left the wire to be tended by whoever would sit in the little room beside the chandlery and mind it for a cut of every message sent. Odell had been the only taker. He kept the key on a string around his neck, kept his chair angled so he could see both the door and the sea, and kept, as far as anyone could tell, a private ledger of everything that passed through his hands. Not an official one. Nothing so honest as paper. Just a look he gave certain messages, a small wet gleam of interest, like a man tasting soup to learn what was in the pot.

Marta stood across the lane from the chandlery in the grey light before opening, her breath smoking, and looked at that window the way she'd once looked at a husband who drank too much: knowing exactly what it was capable of, and deciding anyway what she had to do about it.

The inn behind her was quiet. She'd left the fire banked and the coffee pot full for the first fishermen coming off the night boats, and she'd told herself she was stepping out for eggs from the Halloway place, which was even true, or true enough — there were eggs in her basket, under the cloth, under the folded paper she had no business carrying anywhere near Odell Prine's window.

Three days since Vane's men had come through asking their pointed questions. Three days since she'd lied badly — *Amberdale? Never heard the name* — while her hands betrayed her by wiping the same clean glass twice. Three days of watching the Harrow crew come and go at all hours, of counting their lanterns on the cliff path at night, of noticing that they came back from the shore direction wet to the knee when there'd been no rain and no reason to wade. And this morning, one of them — the young one, the junior surveyor with the soft voice and the guilty eyes — had bought bread and salt pork and said, too casually, into the middle of paying, "Your mountain's loud lately. You hear blasting up there?"

There was no blasting on the Rivergate lines. There hadn't been legal blasting on those lines in twenty years. Casper Amberdale had no dynamite; Brigid Doyle did, but Marta had watched her inventory the stuff like a miser counting coins, and if charges had gone off inside that cave, they hadn't come from Brigid's stores. Which meant someone else's powder had found someone else's rock, and the only people in Greywater with fresh explosives were men wearing Harrow coats.

Marta had run an inn on this coast long enough to know that most trouble wanted nothing from her but silence. She'd given silence freely for years — to smugglers, to runaway wives, to a customs man who drank himself stupid every second Thursday. Silence was the local currency, and she was rich in it.

But this wasn't smugglers' silence. This was the kind where men got sealed inside mountains, and she had read enough of old Tom Amberdale's ledger pages — pages Casper had shown her over her own kitchen table, lamplight between them, both of them speaking low as if the walls had ears for Harrow — to know that on this coast, silence had a body count.

She crossed the lane.

* * *

Odell was already in his chair, tea steaming, a half-finished game of solitaire laid out on the counter like an altar. He looked up with the expression he wore for everyone: mild, moist, faintly disappointed, as though the world kept arriving at inconvenient hours.

"Marta Quill. Early for you."

"Early for everyone," she said. "I need to send a wire."

"Business is good." He turned the ledger toward her without enthusiasm. "Rate's fourpence a word to the provincial capital. Five if it's after eight."

"It's not after eight."

"It's not eight yet," Odell agreed, and smiled at his own precision.

She had rehearsed this on the walk down. That was the thing about courage, she thought — it wasn't the absence of fear, it was fear that had done its homework. She knew what she had to say and she knew what she must not say, and the space between those two things was narrow as a plank over a gorge.

"I'll write it myself," she said, taking the blank form from the counter before he could offer to take dictation. Odell loved taking dictation. It put him inside the message, made him a co-author of other people's business.

He shrugged and went back to his cards, which was worse. A watching Odell was a dangerous Odell; a pretending-not-to-watch Odell was an Odell building a story he could sell later.

Marta bent over the form. Her handwriting, usually so sure across a bar tab, came out cramped.

She had spent two nights composing this. The first draft had named Vane outright — *Aldous Vane, Harrow Line surveyor, has sabotaged the Rivergate cave passage with explosive charges* — and she'd burned it in the stove, because it was accusation without proof, and accusations without proof were how small women like her ended up as footnotes in other people's trials. The second draft had named Casper — *Casper Amberdale holds concession permits for the closed corridor, number 4471 through 4476, issued ninety days past by the clerk of works at Marrow Junction* — and she'd burned that too, because naming Casper told Harrow exactly where to point their lawyers, and their guns if it came to that.

What remained, after two nights of burning, was almost nothing. And almost nothing, she had realized somewhere around the third candle, was exactly right. The engineer's office didn't need her opinions. It needed numbers. Numbers were cold, checkable, undeniable — and once a clerk in the capital started checking them, everything else would follow like water finding its level.

She wrote:

*PERMIT SERIES 4471–4476 RIVERGATE CORRIDOR STOP HOLDER REPORTS UNAUTHORIZED DEMOLITION WITHIN CONCESSION BOUNDARIES THIS WEEK STOP REQUEST INSPECTION BEFORE FIRST SNOW PER CLAUSE NINE STOP CONFIRM RECEIPT TO GREYWATER INN*

She read it four times. Every word of it was true. The holder had reported it — to her, at third hand, through the boy Ivo's signals relayed by Sefu's perfect memory, but reported nonetheless. Unauthorized demolition had occurred; the mountain itself had said so. Clause nine — she'd made Casper find the actual clause in the permit papers, and there it was, clause nine, *the Commissioner may order inspection of any works within the concession upon report of irregularity*, dry as dust and solid as bedrock. She wasn't accusing. She was invoking. There was a difference, and the difference was the width of a prison wall.

"Long message," Odell said behind her. "That's better than two shillings' worth."

"My sister's ill," Marta said. "In the capital. I'm wiring the family."

"You haven't got a sister in the capital."

It was said mildly, conversationally, the way a man remarks on weather. But the cards had stopped moving, and Marta felt the whole room tilt slightly toward him, the way a boat tilts when something large passes underneath.

"I've got three sisters," Marta said, turning around slowly, "and one of them owes me money, and none of them are your business, Odell Prine, unless you'd like my custom to move to the chandlery next door, where I hear Mr. Fenn has been asking about getting a wire of his own."

Fenn had asked no such thing. Fenn couldn't spell *chandlery*. But Odell's monopoly was the one soft place on him, and Marta pressed it now with the flat of her thumb.

Odell studied her for a long moment. Then he took the form, read it once — she watched his eyes move and saw the moment they snagged on *RIVERGATE* and *DEMOLITION*, saw the little wet gleam kindle — and began tapping it out on the key with fingers yellowed by forty years of tobacco.

The needle clattered. Each letter went out over the hills and under the sea along the single copper thread that connected Greywater to the rest of the world, and Marta stood in the closet-sized room listening to her courage leave her body one letter at a time, feeling lighter and more exposed with every click, until at last Odell flipped the switch and the line went silent.

"Sent," he said. "Receipt confirmation will come back whenever they bother. Could be an hour. Could be tomorrow." He tore off the carbon copy and held it out — then, at the last moment, drew it back an inch. "You know," he said, "there was another gentleman in here Tuesday. Asked me had anything gone out lately about the Rivergate lines. Offered me a pound a month to let him know."

The room got very cold, or Marta did.

"And what did you tell him?"

"Told him I don't discuss customers' messages." Odell handed her the carbon at last, and his eyes, when they met hers, held something she had never seen in them before — not menace, not greed, but a tired, complicated decency, like a rusted hinge that still turns. "Which is true. What I didn't tell him is that nobody's offered me two pounds."

Marta understood. It landed in her chest like a dropped anchor.

"Two pounds a month," she said.

"Paid quarterly. In advance." Odell picked up his cards and resumed his solitaire as if they were discussing the price of herring. "And in exchange, I hear nothing, see nothing, and if a certain gentleman comes back asking, I tell him the only traffic on this line is fish prices and family illness. Which will also be true, going forward."

"Going forward," Marta repeated.

"A woman can wire her sick sister more than once," Odell said. "If the sister keeps taking a turn."

For a moment Marta wanted to laugh and cry at once, and settled for neither. Eleven years she'd had this man pegged as a weasel, and here he was, selling himself to her side of the street for less than Harrow had offered — not out of principle, she suspected, but out of some deep crabbed resentment of being taken for a cheap man. Fine. The world ran on stranger fuel than virtue. She'd built an inn on it.

"Quarterly, in advance," she said. "First payment tonight, after supper. And Odell —"

"I know." He didn't look up from the cards. "You were never here. I was never offered anything. And if anyone asks, the only Marta Quill business on this wire is a sick sister who won't die and won't get well."

Marta tucked the carbon copy into her basket, under the eggs, and walked back out into the grey morning feeling as though she'd just stepped off a ledge and found, instead of falling, that the air had decided to hold her up.

* * *

The inn was awake when she returned. Two night-boat men sat over coffee, arguing amiably about the price of cod, and young Tam Halloway was sweeping the front step with the grim diligence of a boy doing chores under protest. Marta set the basket down, hung her shawl, and allowed herself one full minute — she counted it against the clock over the bar — of simply standing still.

Then the kitchen door opened and Sefu came through it sideways, favoring his bad leg, carrying an armload of split cedar for the stove, and the minute was over.

He'd been sleeping in the loft for a week now, he and Brigid both, since the collapse of the seaward entrance had made the inn a garrison. They ate at her table, paid in labor and stories, and had become, without anyone announcing it, part of the furniture of the place — the good kind, the kind you'd defend with a broom.

"Any word?" Sefu asked quietly, stacking wood by the stove.

"The wire's gone. Permit numbers, clause nine, request for inspection before snow." She kept her voice under the clatter of the kitchen. "No names. Not mine, not Casper's, not yours."

Sefu nodded slowly. He had the habit, she'd noticed, of receiving news the way a bridge receives weight — testing it, distributing it, letting nothing concentrate in one place. "Then now we wait."

"Now we wait," Marta agreed. "How long does a reply take?"

"From the commissioner's office?" Sefu considered. "If a clerk reads it this morning and it goes up the chain, three days. If it sits in a tray, forever. If—" he paused, choosing honesty over comfort, the way he always did— "if someone at Harrow has a friend in that office, it might never arrive at all, and arrive instead as a question in Aldous Vane's pocket."

"That cheerful, are you."

"I'm accurate," Sefu said. "Cheerful is Brigid's department."

As if summoned, Brigid came down the stairs with her hair tied back and her sleeves rolled, smelling of gunpowder and lye soap, and stopped dead when she saw their faces. "What's happened?"

"Nothing's happened," Marta said. "That's rather the point. I've lit a fuse, and now we stand well back and watch the spark travel."

Brigid looked from her to Sefu and back, and something in her face shifted — the hard professional wariness giving way, briefly, to something younger. "You sent it yourself. Through Prine."

"Through Prine."

"He'd sell his mother for a pint."

"He'd sell his mother for two pounds a quarter," Marta said, "and I've already outbid Harrow. Go put coffee in me, the pair of you, and stop looking at me like I've grown a second head. I've run this inn twenty-two years. I know how to buy a man and how to make a lie hold weight. It's the same skill, mostly."

Brigid laughed — a short, surprised bark — and went to the stove. And Marta stood in the middle of her own kitchen, in the smell of coffee and cedar smoke, and let herself feel it at last: not pride exactly, and not fear either, but the strange suspended sensation of having committed an act whose consequences were now larger than she was and entirely out of her hands.

Somewhere north of the village, up past the tree line, inside the stone, five people were racing a winter that had come early, dragging a dead man's truth toward the light. Marta could do nothing for them — no rope, no bread, no lamp would reach that far. All she could do was what she'd done: pull one small lever in one small room, and trust that the machinery of the world, so often rusted, so often bribed, might this once actually turn.

* * *

The reply came at dusk.

Marta heard the tap-tap-tap of the delivery boy's knuckles on her door and knew before she opened it — knew by the way the boy stood, cap in hand, breathless from running, holding the flimsy yellow slip like it was hot — that the world had begun to move.

She read it standing on her own doorstep, in the failing light, with the sea going dark behind her and the mountain a blacker shape above it all:

*COMMISSIONER'S OFFICE ACKNOWLEDGES RECEIPT PERMIT SERIES 4471 THROUGH 4476 STOP IRREGULARITY REPORTED IS MATTER OF RECORD STOP INSPECTION PARTY BEING FORMED STOP DEPARTURE WEATHER PERMITTING WITHIN SEVEN DAYS STOP ROUTE VIA COASTAL ROAD THEN RIVERGATE APPROACH STOP ADVISE HOLDER OF RECORD REMAIN REACHABLE STOP*

Seven days. An inspection party, formed and moving. The words sat on the page in their flat official ink, and Marta read them three times, and each time they grew larger.

*Advise holder of record remain reachable.* The holder of record was a hundred miles inland, inside a mountain, racing snow with a boy and a blacklist and a bad leg. Reachable. She almost laughed.

Then she looked up at the ridge line, where the last light was dying, and saw — small, distant, unmistakable in the clear cold air — a lantern moving along the high path. One lantern, then a second, then a string of them, descending from the direction of the collapsed portal, heading not toward Greywater but along the coast road. Toward the capital road. Toward the very route an inspection party would have to take.

Vane's men were already moving too.

Marta folded the telegram into her apron, went inside, and began, calmly and thoroughly, to plan how to slow six armed men down for seven days — starting, she decided, with a barrel of cider that would prove mysteriously sour, and horses whose shoes would need urgent attention, and a bridge on the coast road that she suddenly remembered was washed out, and would swear to on any Bible they liked.

Outside, the first wind of the coming storm came off the sea and rattled the windows of the Greywater Inn, and inside, Marta Quill rolled up her sleeves.
