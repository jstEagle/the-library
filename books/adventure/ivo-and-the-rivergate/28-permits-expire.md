## 28. Permits Expire

Casper woke before dawn with the number already in his head, the way a man wakes with a toothache already in his jaw. Ninety days. He had counted them the way other men counted coins, turning each one over in the dark, feeling its weight, spending it carefully — and now the purse was empty. Yesterday, by his reckoning. Perhaps the day before, if the clerk's brittle pen had granted them a grace he'd never mentioned, but Casper had learned long ago not to build hope on clerks' mercies.

He lay in the cold of the snow cave and listened to his crew breathe.

Brigid slept curled around her pack like it was a child, one hand resting on the canvas even in sleep, as if dynamite could be soothed by touch. Sefu had wedged himself against the wall with his bad leg propped on a rolled blanket, and even unconscious his face carried that particular set of a man who had made an agreement with pain years ago and honored it daily since. Ivo slept nearest the entrance, always nearest the entrance, folded into a shape so small and self-contained it seemed impossible the boy contained a whole territory inside him — galleries, ledgers, a dead keeper's cot, thirty years of tide tables memorized in signal code.

Outside, the storm had spent itself sometime in the night. The silence beyond the entrance had that particular density of deep mountain winter, a silence with weight to it, pressing at the mouth of the cave like water against a dam.

Ninety days, gone.

Casper sat up and did the arithmetic again anyway, because he was an engineer and engineers check their work even when they dread the answer. The permits had been issued on a Tuesday — he remembered because the clerk's calendar had been a day behind and Casper had corrected him, and the man had looked at him with the particular resentment of someone who preferred his errors comfortable. From that Tuesday: ninety days. The passage through the gate. The drowned gallery. Four days building Sefu's bridge over nothing. Three days pinned in this very cave while the storm argued with the mountain. And now—

Yesterday. It had lapsed yesterday, somewhere between the last ration of hardtack and Brigid's second story about the foreman who tried to dock her pay for breathing too loud. Somewhere in all that white silence, the concession corridor had quietly reverted to the Harrow Line, and everything they had bled for — the survey notes, the gauge measurements, the charted galleries, the whole careful architecture of legal evidence — had become paper with no standing. Worthless. Not merely worthless but worse than worthless, because a survey conducted on lapsed permits wasn't just invalid, it was trespass. They hadn't lost their case; they had become criminals in the act of making it.

Casper found himself smiling in the dark, and the smile frightened him a little, because it felt less like despair than relief.

* * *

They ate the last of the food without ceremony. There is a particular honesty in the final meal of an expedition — no pretense of plenty, no stretching, just the plain division of what remains. Brigid dealt out the hardtack like cards. Sefu melted the last fat into the tea and called it a feast, and for once nobody laughed at him, because it was true; it was a feast, the only kind left.

"We should talk about what happens now," Casper said.

Ivo looked up from his cup. The boy's face in lamplight was all angles and attention, and Casper had learned to read it the way he'd learned to read gauges — small movements meaning large things. Right now the needle pointed at wariness.

"You're going to say we turn back," Ivo said.

"I'm going to say the truth first, and then we'll see." Casper turned his cup slowly on his knee. "The permits expired yesterday. By my count, and my count's good."

Sefu set down his tea. Brigid went very still, the stillness she got when something detonated quietly inside her.

"Expired," Brigid repeated. "As in—"

"As in the corridor belongs to Harrow now. As in every measurement we've taken since yesterday morning was taken illegally. As in Vane doesn't need to sabotage us anymore. He only needs to report us." Casper heard his own voice and was surprised at how level it came out. "If we walked out right now and put our findings in front of the engineer's office, they wouldn't read them. They'd arrest us for reading them ourselves."

The silence that followed had texture. Snow-silence, cave-silence, the silence of three people recalculating everything.

"So it's done," Sefu said at last. His voice was gentle, which was how Sefu delivered the hardest things — gently, the way you set down something breakable. "All of it. The bridge. The drowned gallery. Marta's telegram, if it ever got out." He shook his head slowly. "Four days I gave that gorge my leg's best remaining years, and it's done just like that."

"It was never going to be enough," Brigid said, and there was fury in it, old fury, the kind she carried from union halls and blacklists. "You don't beat men like Vane with paperwork. Paperwork is their language. You can't win a fight by speaking the enemy's tongue better than he does — he'll just buy the dictionary."

"That's rather my point," Casper said.

They all looked at him.

"I've been thinking about it all night," he said. "Lying here listening to the storm, doing sums. And here's the sum that matters: I have spent my whole life believing that the record would save us. That if a thing was written down properly — inspected properly, signed properly, filed properly — then the truth would out. My father believed it too. That's why he kept ledgers. That's why he planned to testify." He paused. "And where did believing in paperwork get him? Sealed in a gallery by men who understood perfectly well that paper can be bought, buried, burned. Where did it get me? Dismissed in one morning for refusing to sign a lie — and the lie signed anyway, by someone else, and the trestle stood until it fell, and the company paid out settlements cheaper than steel."

Brigid's mouth twitched. It wasn't quite a smile. "You're saying give up?"

"I'm saying stop lying to ourselves about what we're carrying." Casper reached into his coat and drew out the survey folio — oilcloth-wrapped, corner-battered, the thing they had guarded across the gorge and through the flood. He set it on the stone between them. "This is worth nothing now. Legally. It's a diary, not a document. And I find—" he searched for the word and let it come rough, unpolished, "—I find that I feel lighter holding it than I have since Greywater. Because as long as the permits were alive, everything depended on them. Every step was measured against a deadline. Every risk was weighed against a piece of paper some clerk could void with a stroke. We were servants of the paper." He looked around the circle of faces. "The paper's dead. Which means for the first time since I took this contract, we are free to do what's *right* instead of what's *valid*."

Sefu frowned. "Those aren't always different things."

"No," Casper agreed. "But they were, yesterday. Yesterday, the valid thing might have been to cut losses, retreat, save ourselves for a legal fight we'd already lost. Today there's no valid thing. There's only the true one."

* * *

Ivo had not spoken through any of it. Now the boy rose, unhurried, and went to the cave entrance, and stood looking out at the morning.

The storm had left the world remade. The plateau below them ran white to the tree line, and beyond the trees the mountains stepped down toward the coast in long blue shadows, and above everything the sky was doing that thing winter skies do after a great storm — scoured clean, hard, brilliant, indifferent. Casper joined him at the entrance. The cold hit his face like a fact.

"The deep gate," Ivo said.

It wasn't a question, but Casper treated it as one. "You know that ground better than any of us."

"I know *of* it." Ivo's voice had gone flat, controlled, the voice he used when the signals underneath were ones he didn't want transmitting. "I told you. The keeper stopped going there. Near the end. His last entries — they end mid-line. Like your father's map." The boy's jaw worked. "There's a place past the third junction where the air changes. Warmer. The ice on the walls melts and refreezes in shapes like — like hands. I used to think the mountain was reaching for me." He said it without shame, the way children say terrible things, and Casper's chest tightened. "I never went past it. Not once in six years. The keeper told me once, the only time he talked about it: *some doors you keep shut until you're strong enough for what's behind them.*"

"And do you think he meant the door?" Casper asked quietly. "Or what's behind it?"

Ivo didn't answer. But he didn't say no, and from Ivo, that was a map with the route marked.

Behind them, Brigid had come to stand at Casper's shoulder. She was looking at the folio on the stone floor, and her expression had shifted into something Casper hadn't seen on her before — not the defiance, not the black humor, but a kind of fierce clarity.

"My da worked the Connemara lines," she said abruptly. "Before they closed them. He used to say there's two kinds of men who work the rails. The ones who serve the timetable, and the ones who serve the road. Timetable men die comfortable. Road men die useful." She spat, expertly, into the snow outside. "I've been a timetable woman my whole cursed life. Blacklisted for one speech. One! Because I said the men laying track deserved to eat. And every job since, I've kept my head down and my charges neat and my signature clean, waiting for the industry to forgive me." She laughed, short and bitter. "It was never going to forgive me. And now the permits are dead and there's no industry left to forgive or not forgive, and I'll tell you what I feel, Casper Amberdale. I feel *unemployed from my chains*."

Sefu snorted. "That's the most romantic thing you've ever said."

"Don't get used to it."

But Casper saw what moved under her words, the same current moving under his own. Liberation through letting go — he'd have phrased it more carefully once, wrapped it in engineering metaphors, load-bearing truths and such. But the truth didn't need dressing. They had been carrying the weight of legitimacy, and legitimacy had died in the snow, and they were still standing. Which proved the weight had never been holding them up. It had only been holding them down.

"The deep gate," Casper said. "Then. All the way. Not for the permit — there is no permit. Not for vindication — vindication is just another word for being forgiven by people who wronged you, and I'm done applying for it." He picked up the folio and slung it over his shoulder, and it felt lighter than it had any day of the journey. "We go because my father wrote *if I do not return, the gate must stay open*, and nobody has kept that instruction. We go because whatever's behind that warm air and those hand-shaped icicles has been sealed for thirty years by men who needed it sealed. And we go because—" he hesitated, then finished honestly, "because it's the last door, and I'd rather open it and be destroyed by what's inside than live the rest of my life listening to it knock."

* * *

They broke camp with the efficiency of people who had done it too many times to be sentimental. Brigid coiled the rope. Sefu checked his leg bindings with the grim tenderness of a man negotiating with an unreliable colleague. Ivo banked the fire down to nothing, leaving no sign, erasing them from the mountain's memory the way he erased himself from everyone's.

At the entrance, Casper paused and looked back at the little cave — three days of shelter, three days of stories, the place where they had survived by staying still. Then he stepped out into the blue morning, and the snow took his boots to the shin, and the four of them began to move.

The plateau descended in long rollers toward a dark seam in the mountainside that Casper could see from half a mile off — a cleft where the rock showed black through the ice, breathing faintly, impossibly, warm air that steamed against the cold like breath from a sleeping animal. As they drew closer, Casper saw the ice formations at the seam's edge, and understood Ivo's words completely. The melt-freeze had built the walls into columns and curves and reaching shapes, translucent in the morning light, and yes — like hands. Dozens of them, pale and open-palmed, lining the approach to the deep gate as though the mountain itself were pleading, or welcoming, or warning. He couldn't decide which, and suspected the answer changed depending on who was walking toward it.

Ivo had stopped ten yards out. The boy stood rigid, breath smoking, staring at the entrance with an expression Casper had seen once before — on the drowned gallery, at the moment the water reached Sefu's chest. Fear worn openly, because hiding it would cost more strength than showing it.

"Ivo," Casper said.

"They sealed him in there." The boy's voice was very small for such a large statement. "Whatever's in there — it's where they sealed him. Isn't it. That's why the entries stop. That's why the keeper wouldn't go back. He knew."

Casper crouched to bring himself level with the boy. Up close, he could see Ivo trembling, and see also that the boy's feet were planted like driven spikes. Six years old, watching men in Harrow coats do murder in the dark, and surviving by silence, and carrying the silence ever since like a rail too heavy to set down.

"Listen to me," Casper said. "Thirty years ago, my father wrote that the gate must stay open, and then he walked into the dark knowing what it might cost, because some things cost more left undone. I am not asking you to be brave the way he was brave. I'm asking you to walk in with us — with *us*, Ivo. Four of us. Nobody goes anywhere alone anymore. That's the whole point of what we've become."

Ivo looked at him for a long moment. Then at Brigid, who nodded once, soldier-nod. At Sefu, who touched two fingers to his brow in a carpenter's salute.

"Okay," Ivo said. And then, straightening, in the clipped cadence of the signal code he thought in: "Line clear. Proceed."

They crossed the last stretch of snow together, four sets of prints stitching toward the open hands of ice, and as Casper passed beneath them into the warmth of the deep gate, he felt the temperature change on his skin like a threshold — the mountain's held breath, thirty years stale, finally exhaled.

Inside, the dark went down and down, and somewhere far below, water was moving.

And somewhere closer — much closer — in the warm dark ahead of them, something shifted. A sound. A scrape of boot on stone, deliberate, unmistakable.

Ivo's hand found Casper's sleeve and gripped it.

"We're not the first ones here," the boy whispered.
