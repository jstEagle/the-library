## 29. Vane Catches Up

Aldous Vane had climbed mountains before, but never one that fought back.

The ridge above the deep gate was a spine of black rock furred with old snow, and it took his crew the better part of two hours to work down from the saddle where they'd made their cold camp. The wind came off the plateau in long, flat shoves, carrying grit and the smell of iron-cold stone. Vane walked at the front because he always walked at the front — not out of courage but out of arithmetic. A crew followed its leader's back. If his back was steady, they believed the ground under them was steady too. It was the oldest trick in supervision, older than surveying itself: confidence as an instrument, more useful than any transit or chain.

Behind him came nine men. Nine, now. He'd started with fourteen at Greywater, and the mountain had taken its tithe — a turned ankle on the scree, a shoulder wrenched in a fall of ice, two men who had simply sat down one morning and refused to rise again, staring at the white horizon with the flat patience of animals who know weather better than employers. Vane had docked their pay and left them with the pack mule and half the rations, and he told himself the ledger balanced. Men who quit were not losses. They were subtractions.

Only one of those subtractions troubled him, and it wasn't a man who'd sat down. It was Elias Wren, his junior surveyor, twenty-six years old, precise as a draftsman's rule, who had been asking questions for three days now in the quiet way he did everything — not challenging, just measuring, the way he measured everything, angles and distances and, lately, Vane himself.

*Why are we following Amberdale's line instead of cutting our own?*

*If the permits lapsed, whose authority are we operating under?*

*Mr. Vane — what exactly did the company do at Rivergate? The first time.*

Vane had answered none of them directly. He'd answered all of them the same way, with the only sentence that had ever been required in his career: *The Harrow Line pays you to hold the rod, Wren, not to read the map.* It had worked on every man he'd ever supervised. It was not working on Wren, and Vane found this more unsettling than the storm, than the rations running thin, than the fact that he was, by any honest accounting, pursuing four people — one of them a child — across a frozen plateau in order to accomplish what, precisely?

He knew the answer. He simply declined to look at it straight-on, the way he declined to look at the sun.

The orders had come down from the provincial office six weeks ago, couched in the gentle language corporations use when they mean to be understood perfectly: *Harrow's charter interest in the Rivergate corridor must be protected. Any competing survey activity should be discouraged. Discreetly.* Discouraged. Vane had spent thirty years learning what that word weighed. In the low country it meant lawyers and liens. In the high country, in winter, in a place where no telegraph wire ran and no magistrate would come until spring — it meant whatever the mountain allowed, and nothing more.

*Let the mountain do the killing,* his section chief had said, laughing over brandy in a warm office Vane could still see if he closed his eyes. *We're not paying you to be murderers, Aldous. We're paying you to be late. Slow them down. Lose a cache. Take the high route. Winter is very thorough, and it doesn't leave fingerprints.*

Vane had told himself the same story all the way up the plateau. He was not doing anything. He was merely declining to prevent things. There was a moral difference — he was nearly sure there was — and the difference was the width of a signature, which was, after all, his profession. He signed things. He had signed the inspection reports on the old viaduct steel, or rather he had signed the summaries of the summaries, three removes from the truth, each remove small enough to seem like nothing, each nothing stacking up over the years into a career, a house in the provincial capital, a reputation for getting difficult corridors done.

And beneath all of it, like water moving under ice, the thing he did not look at: a bridge had fallen. Men had died building it. And somewhere in the chain of signatures between the cheap steel and the dead men, his own name appeared, in his own younger hand, certifying steel he had never tested.

So when the boy's voice drifted up out of the dark ahead of them — thin, clear, carrying across the snow with the eerie precision of sound in extreme cold — Vane felt something in his chest that was not surprise. Surprise would have meant he hadn't known. He had known. He had known since Greywater, since Marta's lies about the shore road, since the fisherman who looked at his boots and said *nobody goes to the caves, sir, nobody comes back neither.* The Amberdale man had found the gate. Of course he had. It was the only ending the story permitted.

"Light off," Vane said quietly. "All of you. Lamps lit, rifles out, but slung. Nobody points anything unless I say."

They came around the last shoulder of rock and stopped.

The entrance to the deep gate was a wound in the mountainside — a tall fissure where the cliff had split sometime in the world's violent youth, its edges hung with icicles like the teeth of something that had been eating slowly for ten thousand years. Warm air breathed out of it, impossibly, carrying the mineral smell of deep water and old stone. Snow around the mouth was trampled, printed with boot-prints going in. Four sets. And beside the entrance, wedged in a crack of rock, a lantern still burned — left deliberately, a marker, a message.

Vane stood looking at it for a long moment. Then he laughed, short and without humor, his breath smoking in the lamplight.

"He wants us to know," he said. "Amberdale wants us to know he's inside." He shook his head slowly. "That's either courage or a trap, gentlemen, and I confess I can't tell which."

"It's an invitation," said Wren, behind him.

Vane turned. The junior surveyor stood with his lamp raised, and the light caught his face from below, hollowing his cheeks, making him look older than twenty-six. His rifle was slung, as ordered. His hands were bare despite the cold, tucked under his arms, and Vane realized the boy had been standing with his hands hidden so no one could see them shake — or perhaps so he couldn't feel the rifle's stock against his fingers, tempting him toward some use of it.

"An invitation," Vane repeated.

"To talk. To settle it." Wren's voice was steady, which cost him; Vane could hear what the steadiness cost. "You've been driving us across this plateau for days, Mr. Vane, and I've done the sums. We're not here to survey. There's nothing left to survey — the corridor's charted, the permits are dust, and we both know it. So what are we here for?"

"We're here," Vane said, "because the company instructed—"

"The company isn't here." Wren took a step forward, into the ring of lamplight. "The company is four hundred miles away drinking brandy. We're here. Nine men with rifles, chasing a surveyor, a dynamiter, a carpenter, and a child into a hole in a mountain. And I keep thinking about the last time Harrow men went into that mountain, Mr. Vane. I've been reading the old files. The viaduct file. There's a keeper's name in it — a station keeper who filed a complaint about the steel, who was going to testify, and then—" He stopped. Swallowed. "And then didn't."

The wind moved along the ridge. Somewhere above them, a mass of snow let go with a soft whump and slid a hundred feet down the face, settling.

"You've been reading things that aren't your business," Vane said.

"Maybe." Wren's jaw tightened. "Or maybe my business is exactly that, because I'm the one holding the instruments when you tell me what to record. And I want to know, before we go another step, what we're recording. Are we sealing people in a cave tonight, Mr. Vane? Is that the instruction? Because I've heard the men talking. Hollis says we're collapsing the entrance 'for safety.' For safety." He almost smiled, and it was a terrible smile, young and horrified. "Whose safety? Say it plainly. If you want me to point a rifle at a twelve-year-old boy, say it plainly."

For a moment — a long moment, measured in heartbeats and falling temperature — Aldous Vane considered lying. He was good at lying; it was, if he were honest, his core competency, the skill under all the other skills. He could have said *we're securing the site*, could have wrapped the order in the language of engineering and prudence, could have made it sound like the sensible, professional thing, the thing any careful man would do. Half these men would have swallowed it whole. They wanted to swallow it. It was warmer than the truth, and they were very cold.

But Wren was looking at him with those measuring eyes, and Vane thought: *he'll take the measurement anyway. Whatever I say, he'll record it. That's what he is. That's what I made him.* And something in Vane — some splinter of the man he'd been at twenty-six, before the signatures, before the summaries of summaries — flinched from being measured and found wanting.

"Inside that cave," Vane said, and his voice came out lower than he intended, roughened by the wind or by something else, "is evidence. Evidence that the Harrow Line used defective steel on the Rivergate viaduct. Evidence that men died because of it. Evidence that has been buried — literally buried — for thirty years, and that the company has paid a great deal of money to keep buried. If that evidence comes out of that mountain, gentlemen, it does not come out alone. It comes out with names attached. My name is one of them. Perhaps yours will be too, in time — everyone who touched the file, everyone who signed, everyone who looked away. That is what we are here to prevent. Not a survey. A reckoning."

Silence, except the wind.

"So it's true," said one of the others — Kessler, the big tracklayer, a man Vane had counted as solid as ballast. Kessler's rifle was suddenly in his hands, not aimed, just held, the way a man holds a thing he's reconsidering. "The viaduct. My uncle worked that viaduct. He was one of the ones that—" He stopped. Looked at the cave mouth. Looked at Vane. "You knew. All this time, you knew, and you brought us up here to bury it deeper."

"I brought you up here," Vane said, "to do a job."

"No." Kessler shook his head slowly, and around him, subtly, the geometry of the crew changed — men shifting weight, angling, no longer a formation pointed at the cave but a loose ring with no certain center. "No, Mr. Vane. You brought us up here to do your job. There's a difference, and I'm seeing it plain for the first time in fifteen years."

Vane felt the ground move under him — not the mountain, the other thing, the worse thing. He had spent his whole life managing crews, and he knew every sign of a fracture: the dropped eyes, the exchanged glances, the sudden interest men took in their own boots when they no longer wished to be seen agreeing with you. It was happening in real time, in the blue lamplight, at the mouth of a hole in the world, and he could not stop it, because the only tools he had ever possessed were authority and narrative, and both of them were failing at once.

"Wren," he said, and made his voice the supervisor's voice, calm, level, the voice that had signed a thousand clean reports. "Rifle up. We're going to secure the entrance. Nobody gets hurt. We seal them in, we wait for the thaw, the provincial engineers dig them out in spring, everyone lives, and the record stays clean. That's the order. That's the whole of it. *For safety.*"

"And if the tide comes up?" Wren asked softly. "I've read the tide tables too, Mr. Vane. The lower galleries flood. You know that. Everyone who's read the keeper's file knows that. Sealing them in isn't waiting for the thaw. It's drowning them and calling the water weather."

"That," said Vane, "is an interpretation."

"It's arithmetic." Wren unslung his rifle — and Vane's heart seized — but the boy only held it by the barrel, stock down, and drove it butt-first into the snowdrift at his feet, where it stood upright, abandoned, a small absurd monument. "I won't. That's my whole report, sir. I won't. Write me up. Court-martial me in the spring. Dock my pay till I die. But I am not going to stand at this door and help you make widows so your signature stays clean."

One by one — not all, not quickly, but one by one — the rifles went down. Kessler's first, driven into the snow beside Wren's like a fence post. Then the two brothers, Ollie and Tam, who looked at each other and needed no words. Then the others, some angrily, muttering, some with the relief of men setting down a weight they'd carried too far. Two men kept their weapons. Dree, the foreman, and young Pike, who was too cold and too loyal and too frightened to think past the order. They stood apart, rifles half-raised, uncertain, suddenly a minority in their own expedition.

Vane stood in the middle of it, alone in the way he had never been alone in thirty years of command, and understood that the mountain had not killed anyone after all. The mountain had only waited. It had let him climb all this way and deliver himself to this exact spot, this exact moment, where the arithmetic he had spent a lifetime balancing finally refused to balance — where the sum of nine rifles came to less than zero, where the word *safety* stood exposed in the lamplight for what it had always been.

He looked at the burning lantern by the cave mouth. At the warm air breathing out of the dark. Somewhere down there, Casper Amberdale was waiting — had maybe heard all of it, every word, carried up through the stone. Good, Vane thought, with a bitterness that surprised him by how much grief was mixed into it. Let him hear. Let the record show.

"Mr. Vane," Wren said quietly. "It's not too late to walk down there yourself. Knock. Talk. Thirty years is a long time to carry a thing. Maybe heavier to carry it alone."

Vane looked at the boy for a long time. Then he drew his coat tighter, lifted his own lamp, and stepped past the abandoned rifles toward the fissure in the rock — toward the warm breath of the deep gate, and the voices below, and the reckoning.

"Nobody fires," he said, without turning around. "Anyone who fires answers to me. That's the last order I ever give this crew."

And the mountain, patient as a ledger, opened its dark mouth and waited to see what he would write next.

* * *

Below, in the warm dark, Casper Amberdale heard the boots stop. Heard the voices, muffled by stone but rising at the end, the way voices do when a question hangs unanswered. He heard a rifle stock strike snow — twice, three times, a rhythm like someone driving spikes.

Ivo pressed close against the wall, listening with his whole body, head tilted, eyes shining in the lamplight. "They're putting them down," the boy whispered. "Most of them. Not all."

Casper's hand rested on the keeper's ledger, bound in oilcloth against his chest, his father's last entry folded inside it like a letter thirty years late. Beyond the entrance, footsteps crunched closer — unhurried, deliberate, a single set, coming down toward the light.

"They're coming in," Ivo breathed. "Just one of them."

Casper squared his shoulders, lifted the lamp higher, and watched the fissure's mouth, where the shadows began to move.

"Then let's hear what Aldous Vane has come to say for himself."
