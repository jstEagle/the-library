## 30. Standoff at the Deep Gate

The dark in the deep gate was not like other dark. Casper had known the dark of tunnels, of cellars, of a night so overcast on the prairie that a man could lose his own hand before his face. This was different. This dark had weight and grain, a texture like wet felt pressed against the eyes. It was the dark of a place that had been closed for thirty years, breathing slowly through stone.

And into that dark, six rifle barrels caught the last gray light bleeding down from the entrance behind them.

"Set it all down," Vane said. His voice carried strangely in the gallery — flattened by the rock, stripped of its usual silk. "Tools, satchels, the ledger. Everything on the floor. Then you'll step back against that wall where I can see you."

Casper did not move. Beside him he could feel Ivo trembling — not with fear, Casper realized, but with something older and more animal, the vibration of a creature whose territory had been violated. Brigid's hand had drifted, casual as a woman adjusting her shawl, toward the canvas roll at her hip. Sefu stood on his bad leg with his weight settled low, the way he always did when he was calculating whether a structure would hold or fail.

"Mr. Vane," Casper said, and was surprised at how level his own voice came out. "You've walked four days through snow to point rifles at five people in a hole in the ground. That seems like a great deal of effort to spend on a survey you keep telling everyone is worthless."

"The survey is worthless." Vane stepped forward, and his boots rang on old iron — they were standing, Casper understood suddenly, on rail. On his father's rails, the experimental gauge, laid here in the dark and left here when the men who laid them stopped coming home. "The permits lapsed. The concession reverted. Every page you've charted belongs to Harrow now, by law."

"Then why are your men shaking?"

It was true. In the lamplight spilling from Ivo's lantern at Casper's feet, he could see it: the youngest of Vane's crew, a narrow-shouldered man called Pell, had his rifle up but his elbows in, arms folded tight against his body as if the cold came from inside him. The big one behind him — Hollis, they'd called him on the ridge — kept glancing back toward the entrance, toward the storm-light, as if measuring how quickly a man could retrace four days of mountain.

Vane smiled. It was his drawing-room smile, the one from Greywater, the one that said *I expected exactly this*. But this far underground, with no witnesses but enemies, the smile had nothing to stand on. It hung in the air between them like a bridge with no abutments.

"You're a sentimental man, Amberdale," Vane said. "That's what ruined you the first time. A signature is a small thing. You lost your livelihood over one. Don't lose anything else over paper."

"It isn't paper." Casper crouched slowly — keeping his hands wide, keeping them visible — and lifted the keeper's ledger from his satchel. The leather cover was cracked and salt-stained, swollen from decades of cave damp, and it weighed more than a book should weigh, the way some books do. "It's a record."

"There are records enough in this world."

"Not like this one." Casper straightened. He looked past Vane, past the leveled rifles, to the men holding them. "Thirty years ago a crew came into this mountain to build a line. Nine men. They were promised double wages because the work was dangerous, and they were given steel that was sold to Harrow as scrap grade and certified as standard. Your company's steel, Mr. Vane. Certified by your company's inspectors. When the viaduct let go, eleven men died on it — nine below and two above — and the report that was filed blamed the weather."

Pell's rifle dipped an inch. Casper watched it dip and made himself stop watching, made himself look at the man's face instead.

"I have read that report," Casper went on. "I grew up reading it. My father was the engineer on that crew. My father was also the keeper of this gate — did you know that? There's a station back there in the dark with a cot and a stove and ledgers going back twenty years, kept in his hand. And the last entry ends mid-sentence, Mr. Vane. Mid-sentence. Men don't stop writing mid-sentence unless something stops them."

"Amberdale—" Vane began.

"So I'm going to read it aloud." Casper opened the ledger. His hands were steady now, steadier than they had been any morning of his working life, and he wondered distantly at that — that terror should finally leave him precisely when it had the most reason to stay. "Not for you. For them." He nodded at the rifles. "They've been ordered to let the mountain do their killing. I'd like them to hear what the mountain has been holding onto."

* * *

The lantern light moved as Casper turned pages, and the shadows moved with it, huge and slow across the walls where the gauge-notches ran in their long carved rows. Somewhere deeper in the gallery water dripped at the interval of a slow clock.

He began with the early entries, because they were ordinary, and ordinariness was the sharpest blade he had. *March 4th. Steel delivery inspected. Heat numbers 441 through 460. Flange cracks visible to the eye on seven beams. Flagged for rejection.* He read the date, then the next. *March 9th. Rejection overridden by order of H.L. district office. Beams accepted under certificate 88-C. Inspector's name withheld per instruction.*

"Certificate 88-C," Casper said, and looked up. "Does that number mean anything to anyone here?"

Hollis shifted. The sound of it — leather, breath, the small surrender of a body deciding something — was louder than the dripping water.

*April 2nd,* Casper read. *They've brought the cheap heat to the viaduct span despite my protest filed in triplicate. I am told the schedule matters more than the modulus. I have written to the provincial office. I am told the provincial office dines with the district office. If the span fails it will fail in spring loading and there will be no honest report afterward, because the men who would write one will be the men who signed the certificates.*

His voice caught on the next line, and he let it catch. Let them hear that too.

*If I do not return, the gate must stay open.*

He closed the ledger partway and held it against his chest. In the silence, Ivo's hand found the hem of Casper's coat and held it, the way a boy holds a coat strap on a crowded platform — not for comfort, Casper thought, but for anchoring. To keep himself in the present tense.

"My father wrote that," Casper said. "And then he didn't return. For thirty years, every map, every report, every newspaper column in this province has said that Edmund Amberdale's crew vanished — that the mountain took them, that the river took them, that they were careless men undone by careless work. And the company that supplied the steel sent flowers to the widows and closed the file." He raised his eyes to Vane. "Your charter, Mr. Vane. Built on certificate 88-C. Built on a lie with nine names buried under it."

"You have a book," Vane said quietly. "A dead man's book. Courts want living witnesses, Amberdale. They want documents with chains of custody. They want—"

"They want the truth to be inconvenient," Brigid cut in, from the wall. Her voice was flat and hard as a chisel. "Which is why men like you spend so much money making sure it stays buried. I've heard this speech before, Vane. In a hall in Caerlon, before they blacklisted me. Same words. Same smile. Different suit."

"Madam—"

"And here's what your speech doesn't account for." She jerked her chin at the darkness beyond the lantern light. "There's a living witness in this cave. Been living in it since he was six years old. Seen everything. Heard everything. And he's standing right here, and he's not going anywhere, because this is his house."

Every rifle in the gallery shifted — not toward Brigid, but toward Ivo. Casper felt the boy flinch against his side, and then felt the flinch stop, mastered, the way Ivo mastered everything: by standing stiller than the thing that frightened him.

"He's a child," Vane said. "A feral child living in a hole. No court in the province—"

"He's the keeper of this gate," Sefu said. His deep voice rolled along the gallery like ballast settling. "Kept the light. Kept the ledgers after your company stopped sending anyone. Kept the passage open thirty years waiting for somebody to come ask him what happened." Sefu limped half a step forward, and even Vane's men watched him do it, because there are men whose bodies make arguments on their behalf. "I've built bridges on four continents, Mr. Vane. I know load-bearing when I see it. That boy is carrying more weight right now than your whole charter."

* * *

It was Pell who broke first.

Later, Casper would try to reconstruct exactly how it happened — which words, which silence, which drip of water marked the hinge — and he would never be able to. Some failures of discipline are not events but erosions, and you only see the shoreline after the sea has already come in.

Pell lowered his rifle. Not all the way. Just until the muzzle pointed at the floor, and his arms came down, and he made a sound — small, involuntary, almost apologetic — like a man setting down something heavy he'd forgotten he was carrying.

"Pell." Vane's voice cracked like a flag in wind. "Pick it up."

"My brother worked the viaduct contract." Pell's voice was young and scraped raw. "Season of '61. Came home with his hands shook so bad he couldn't hold a fork. Said the steel sang wrong when the trains crossed. Said everybody knew and nobody said." He looked at Casper, not at Vane. "Is that the same steel? The singing kind?"

Casper opened the ledger again, found the page. "*May 14th. Trains crossing the completed spans produce an audible harmonic defect. Crew calls it the singing. I have measured it. It is the sound of steel working past its tolerance. It is the sound of a schedule being paid for in advance.*"

Pell sat down. He simply sat, right there on the old experimental rails, rifle across his knees, and put his face in his hands.

Hollis lasted longer. Hollis was the loyal one, the paid one, the man who had shadowed them across the plateau and told himself it was business, just business, the way men tell themselves things in order to go on telling themselves things. He kept his rifle up and his jaw set, and Casper read on — the names now, entry after entry, his father recording each man of the crew with the tenderness engineers reserve for structures and sometimes, rarely, for people. *J. Marek, rigger, afraid of heights, never once showed it. T. Osei, foreman, kept a whistle for his daughter and blew it at noon. W. Brandt, mason, owed two months' wages, never complained.* Names. Names. Names.

"Hollis," Vane said, low. "We have orders."

"We have orders," Hollis agreed. His voice was strange, distant, a man reading his own thoughts off a card. "Orders to seal five people in a cave in winter. Orders from men who signed certificates." He looked down at Pell sitting on the rails. At Ivo, small and straight-backed beside Casper. At the ledger in Casper's hands. "My mother's pension comes out of a Harrow fund, Mr. Vane. Twenty-two years I've drawn it. Nobody ever told me where the fund started."

Nobody answered him. Nobody needed to. Hollis brought the rifle down the way a man lowers a flag at dusk — formally, deliberately, folding the stock against his shoulder — and leaned it against the tunnel wall, and the sound of iron touching stone was the loudest thing the mountain had said in thirty years.

The last two men looked at each other. Looked at Vane. One of them — Casper would learn his name was Dree, and that he had a farm and a wife and a mortgage held by a Harrow bank — unshouldered his rifle and set it down beside Hollis's. The last man hesitated, rifle wavering, and then Ivo stepped forward.

Out of the shelter of Casper's coat, out into the open ground between the crews, into the space where a stray shot would find him first. He picked up the lantern. He held it high, and the light caught his face — thin, scarred, twelve years old and older than the mountain around him.

"The gate sees you," Ivo said. He used the signal-cant, the clipped rail-speech he fell into when the ordinary words wouldn't carry the weight. "All ways clear. All ways recorded." He touched the ledger without taking it. "Nothing leaves this mountain unwritten."

The last rifle went down.

* * *

Vane stood alone in the middle of the gallery, and Casper watched a man discover the exact dimensions of the thing he had built.

It was not, Casper thought, that Vane loved the company. Casper had met men who loved companies, and they were a simpler species. Vane loved winning — loved the clean geometry of it, the way a well-run scheme closed like a well-made joint. And now he stood in a tunnel at the bottom of the world with four rifles on the ground and his own junior surveyor not meeting his eye, and the geometry had failed, and there was nothing left to be brilliant about.

"This changes nothing," Vane said. But he said it to the wall.

"It changes everything," Casper said. "It changed the moment they lowered the guns. You taught me that, actually — back in Greywater, when you offered to buy my permits. You said a document is only worth what a man will honor. Remember? You meant it as a threat." He lifted the ledger. "Well. Here's a document, and here are five men honoring it, and two more of yours about to swear to what they were ordered to do. That's a chain of custody, Mr. Vane. You built it yourself, link by link, walking us up this mountain at gunpoint."

Vane turned. For one moment — one long, dripping-water moment — Casper saw him calculate. Saw the eyes flick to the entrance, to the storm, to the rifles on the floor, to the detonator satchel at Brigid's hip. Saw the whole swift interior arithmetic of a desperate man, and saw it fail, sum by sum, because there was no line in the equation anymore that ended with Vane walking out ahead of anyone.

Then the mountain spoke.

Not a word — a shudder. A long, subterranean groan rolled through the gallery, deep as a ship's keel finding bottom, and the lantern flame bent sideways though there was no wind, and somewhere above them a crack traveled through the rock like a whip cracking in another room. Fine dust sifted down through the lantern light, silvering the air, silvering the old rails, silvering the upturned faces of nine men and one boy standing very still in the dark.

Ivo's hand closed on Casper's sleeve.

"The deep gate," Ivo whispered. His voice had gone white. "It's waking up. It does this when the freeze comes deep — the stone pulls tight, and what's loose lets go." He looked up at Casper, and Casper saw in the boy's face the thing he had seen in no ledger and no report: the knowledge of someone who had lived his whole life inside this mountain's moods. "Casper. The roof over the last keeper's station. It's coming down. And if it comes down there, it takes the passage with it — all of it, the whole way through. Sealed. Forever."

Brigid was already moving to the wall, reading the rock with her fingertips like a blind woman reading a face. Sefu had his ear pressed to a rib of stone, listening. Vane stood forgotten in the middle of the gallery, and no one — not even his own men — looked at him at all.

"How long?" Casper asked Ivo.

"I don't know." The boy's voice was very small. "Nobody knows. Nobody's ever been down here when it woke up before."

Above them, the mountain groaned again, longer this time, and the dust fell thicker, and Casper Amberdale stood on his father's rails in his father's tunnel with his father's book in his hands, and understood that the choice he had been walking toward since the first morning he refused to sign his name had finally arrived — and that it would not wait for dawn, or for courage, or for him.

Somewhere overhead, a crack ran out like a sentence ending mid-word.
