## 31. The Choice

The mountain had been talking to them all night.

Casper had heard it first as a sound he mistook for the river — a long, grinding exhalation somewhere beneath the floor of the deep gallery, as though the water itself had grown teeth and was chewing on stone. But the river was behind them now, sealed away by miles of rock, and what they were hearing came from inside the mountain. From the gate.

He sat with his back against the keeper's station wall, the ledger open on his knees, and listened. The tremors came in waves of three: a low groan, a shudder that made dust sift from the ceiling beams like gray snow, and then a silence so complete he could hear his own heart working. Three groans. A shudder. Silence. Then again. The mountain was not threatening them. It was counting down.

Across the chamber, Ivo crouched at the mouth of the inner passage with one palm flat against the rock, the way a doctor listens for breath in a dying man's chest. He had been there for an hour without moving. Brigid sat nearby with the detonator case open across her lap, the plunger assembly gleaming dully in the lamplight, wires running off into the dark toward the charge she'd spent the last six hours setting against the sealed bulkhead of the deep gate. Sefu had wedged himself into the corner where two walls met, bad leg stretched out before him, both hands wrapped around a mug of cold tea he hadn't drunk. Nobody had spoken in a long time.

The lamp flame leaned sideways when the next tremor came, and a crack ran across the ceiling above the cot — a thin white line, quick as a thought, traveling from one timber to the next before it stopped.

"That's new," Sefu said quietly.

"It's been new for hours," Brigid said. "It's just getting more honest about it."

Ivo lifted his hand from the rock. In the lamplight his face looked older than twelve years, carved down to essentials. "The lower galleries are settling," he said. "The whole weight of the ridge is moving. It's the snow melt coming early through the shale, and it's the blasting Vane's men did at the portal, and it's—" He stopped. His jaw worked. "It's the gate deciding."

"Deciding what?" Casper asked, though he knew.

"Whether it stays shut forever."

* * *

They had argued about it until argument wore itself out, and then they had sat in the exhausted quiet that comes after, which is its own kind of argument. Casper turned the ledger pages without reading them, feeling the paper's softness under his thumb, thinking about the last entry — *if I do not return, the gate must stay open* — written in a hand that had still believed someone would read it.

Now the choice had narrowed to something brutal and simple.

Leave it shut, and the tremors would do the work themselves. Within days — maybe hours — the deep galleries would collapse under their own settling weight, and everything inside would be buried past any recovery: the ledgers, the rails of the secret gauge, the inspection notes from the viaduct, the physical proof that Harrow Line's cheap steel had killed eleven men including Edmund Amberdale's father and gotten away with it for twenty years. The truth would die in the dark, intact and useless, like a letter never mailed.

Or blast it open. Brigid's charge, set against the old bulkhead where the passage narrowed to the vent shaft, might blow the gate wide enough to walk through — wide enough to carry the evidence out into daylight, wide enough for the inspectors Marta's telegram had surely summoned by now. Or it might bring the whole ridge down on top of them. The rock was already moving. A charge in moving rock was a coin toss with all their lives as the stake.

"There's a third option," Brigid said, for the fourth time. "We walk out the way we came in, we take the ledgers, we let the mountain keep the rest. Paper testimony. My word, your word, the boy's word—"

"The boy's word," Sefu said gently, "against Harrow's lawyers."

"I know what my word is worth," Brigid snapped, and then, immediately, quieter: "I'm sorry. I didn't mean—"

"You meant exactly what you meant," Ivo said. He wasn't looking at her. He was looking at the dark mouth of the inner passage. "A feral child who lives in a cave. They'd be kind to me the way you're kind to a dog that bit someone." He said it without self-pity, the way he reported tide heights and gallery depths, and somehow that made it worse.

Casper closed the ledger. The sound of it — a small, dry clap in the vast stone room — seemed very loud.

* * *

He got up and walked to the inner passage, and Ivo shifted aside to let him put his own hand on the rock. It was cold, colder than stone should have been this deep, and it hummed. Not vibration exactly. More like the memory of vibration, the way a struck bell keeps singing after the ear stops hearing it.

His father had touched this same rock. Had stood here with a lamp, probably, in this same spot, reading the mountain the way Ivo read it. And somewhere beyond this wall — Casper pressed his palm flatter, as if he could reach through — somewhere beyond was the place where the final entries ended mid-sentence, and beyond that, the lower gallery where men in Harrow coats had sealed a living man because he knew too much and loved the truth more than his life.

Twenty years. Twenty years of that wall holding its secret while the world above built railways over the grave.

Another tremor rolled through, longer this time, and somewhere far off in the dark there was a sound like a door closing — deep, resonant, final. Dust rained from the ceiling. The lamp guttered and steadied.

"We're running out of mountain," Sefu observed from his corner. "Every hour it settles, there's less of it left standing."

"And every hour," Brigid said, "the charge sits there getting more likely to go off on its own. Rock shift can pinch a wire. Pinched wire means misfire, or worse, it means the charge takes a squeeze and decides to introduce itself." She looked at Casper. "I can't make this decision for you, Casper. I can tell you the odds, but I've been wrong about odds before, and the last time, people I liked died."

"You weren't wrong about the odds," Casper said. "You were right, and the company sent you anyway."

"That's the thing about odds," Brigid said. "Somebody always pays them."

Casper looked at the three of them — the dynamiter blacklisted for telling the truth, the carpenter discarded for a leg that worked at nine-tenths, the boy the world had never bothered to name — and felt something settle in his chest that wasn't fear, though fear was certainly in there, cold and patient as groundwater. What settled was clarity. This was the moment his whole life had been walking toward: the rotten trestle he wouldn't sign, the map with the gap in it, the lantern with his father's initials, the ledger, the viaduct, the crossing where the cable frayed and Ivo caught Brigid out of the air. Every step had been a question, and the mountain was asking the last one now.

And he understood, suddenly and completely, that it was not his to answer.

* * *

"I've been thinking about who decides this," Casper said. "All night, while you were listening to the rock, I was doing sums. Weight of the ridge. Yield of the charge. Odds of collapse versus odds of burial. I'm an engineer. Numbers are how I pray." He picked up the ledger and held it. "But this isn't a bridge. There's no load calculation for whether a man's name gets to survive him. And there's no surveyor's stamp for whose life is worth the risk."

"Casper," Brigid started.

"Let me finish, because if I stop I'll lose my nerve, and I've only got the one supply of it." He turned to Ivo. "Your father-figure kept this gate. He wrote that it must stay open. Then men sealed him in it, and the gate stayed shut for twenty years, and you're the one who found it, and cleaned it, and kept the ledgers, and learned every gallery, and waited — God knows what you told yourself you were waiting for, but you were waiting. You've lived inside this decision since before you could write. You know this mountain better than any engineer alive, better than my father did, better than I ever will. When you say the galleries are settling, I believe you the way I believe a level bubble."

Ivo had gone very still. In the lamplight his eyes were wide and wet-rimmed, but he didn't blink, didn't look away.

"So I'm not deciding," Casper said. "I'm handing it to you. Blast it open, and we might all die, but the truth walks out into the light and my father's name walks with it. Leave it shut, and we live, and the truth dies in the dark, and Harrow wins forever by default. Those are the weights. You're the keeper. The scale is yours."

The silence afterward was enormous. Somewhere in it, distantly, the mountain groaned its count: one, two, three.

Brigid opened her mouth and closed it. Sefu set down his untouched tea. Even the lamp flame seemed to hold itself straighter.

"Why me?" Ivo said at last, and his voice cracked in the middle of the two words, and he hated it, Casper could see him hating it. "Why does everyone keep giving me things? The keeper gave me his cot and his ledgers and then he stopped coming back. You gave me your name and I had to give it back. Now you give me—" he gestured at the detonator case, at the trembling walls, at all of it— "*this*. Why does the person with nothing always get handed the thing that breaks?"

"Because you're not nothing," Casper said. "That's the whole point. That's what everyone in this room has in common — the industry took our names and our livelihoods and our legs and our reputations, and it wrote us off as nothing. And it was wrong about every single one of us. I'm not handing you the thing that breaks, Ivo. I'm handing it to the only person here who's never lied to this mountain. It trusts you. That's worth more than all my numbers."

Ivo looked down at his hands. They were small hands, scarred, sure. Keeper's hands. He turned them over as if seeing them for the first time.

"If I say leave it," he said quietly, "you'll leave it. All of you. Even though it kills the proof."

"Yes," Casper said. "Even though."

"And if I say blast it, and the ridge comes down, we die in here together and nobody ever knows why."

"Yes."

"Then it's real," Ivo said. "It's actually mine." He breathed out, long and slow, the way he did before diving into the flooded galleries, and Casper recognized it: the boy was steadying himself the way he did before the worst water. "Then I need to go listen one more time. Properly. Alone."

"No," Brigid said instantly.

"Yes," Ivo said. "Because if I decide scared, I'll decide small. And the gate deserves better than small." He was already moving, taking the storm lantern down from its hook, and there was something in the way he moved — that economy, that certainty — that made argument impossible. He paused at the passage mouth and looked back at Casper, and for a moment the lamplight made his eyes look exactly like the sea at Greywater in winter: gray, cold, and utterly alive. "Keep the ledger open," he said. "Whatever happens, someone should be writing."

Then he was gone into the dark, and the three of them stood listening to his footsteps fade, and the mountain counted its threes, and Casper did the only thing left to do. He sat down, opened the ledger to a fresh page, dipped the pen, and wrote:

*Ninety-first day. The gate is deciding. We have given the decision to its keeper.*

* * *

The waiting was the longest thing Casper had ever done, and he had once spent thirty-six hours on a frozen trestle waiting for a flood crest that decided, in the end, not to come.

Sefu talked to fill it, quietly, the way he did — stories about bridges he'd built and one he'd refused to build, about his mother's kitchen in a country Casper couldn't picture, about the exact weight of good timber versus bad. Brigid checked her wires four times and then forced herself to stop checking them, sitting on her hands like a child at a funeral. Casper read the ledger aloud, softly, all the entries he could decipher, so that his father's voice filled the chamber instead of the mountain's — daily tonnages, weather notes, a complaint about the quality of lamp oil, and once, startlingly tender, a note that the keeper's boy had caught three fish and insisted on naming each one before they were eaten.

"He named fish," Sefu murmured. "Before eating them."

"He was seven," Casper said. "Maybe eight."

Another tremor. Longer. Closer. A hairline crack raced along the eastern wall and stopped an arm's length from the doorway. Brigid swore under her breath with real feeling.

And then, faintly, from deep in the passage, came a sound that stopped all their hearts: a rail signal. Two short, one long. *Come.*

They were moving before the echo died, Brigid snatching up the detonator case, Sefu swinging onto his crutch, Casper grabbing ledger and lamp. The passage narrowed, twisted, dropped; the air grew colder and tasted of iron and old water; and then the passage opened into the vaulted throat of the deep gate itself, and there stood Ivo with his lantern raised, lit from below like something out of the oldest kind of story.

Behind him, the ancient bulkhead rose to the ceiling — mortared stone, Harrow-stamped, twenty years of dark sealed behind it. Brigid's charge sat against it like a held breath.

"Well?" Casper said. "What did the mountain tell you?"

Ivo lowered the lantern. When he spoke, his voice was steady, and it carried in the great stone throat the way a bell carries.

"The river doesn't keep things," he said. "It carries them. Everything it takes, it moves. Everything buried, it digs up eventually and gives back changed." He looked at Casper, and then at Brigid, and then at Sefu, and last at the bulkhead, and Casper watched twelve years of hiding, of surviving alone, of keeping a dead man's gate in the dark — watched all of it come to a point, like light through a lens. "My father-figure wanted it open. The mountain wants it settled. And I—" the ghost of the signal-boy's grin crossed his face, fierce and terrified and free— "I want to carry it. Set the wire, Brigid. Everyone behind the second bend. We're opening the gate."

Brigid moved like the professional she was, checking the charge with hands that only shook a little, running the wire back along the wall to the firing point, calling clearances in a voice gone crisp and calm. Sefu braced himself against the rock. Casper took Ivo's shoulder — the boy weighed almost nothing, all sinew and salt — and squeezed it once, because there was no time for anything longer and no words big enough anyway.

They crowded behind the second bend, into the cutaway where the rock thickened. Brigid held the plunger assembly. She looked at Ivo.

"Keeper's privilege," she said. "Your hand or mine?"

Ivo stepped forward and put both his hands on the plunger, and Brigid covered them with hers, and together—

The mountain screamed.

Not a tremor this time — a roar, a full-voice bellow of shifting stone, and dust exploded from the ceiling in curtains, and somewhere above them something enormous let go. Ivo didn't wait. He drove the plunger down.

The world went white, then silent, then upside down. The blast came through the rock as much as through the air, a fist of pressure that threw them all against the far wall, and Casper lost the lamp, lost his footing, lost all sense of direction in a blackness full of falling grit and ringing ears. For one endless second he thought: *it's coming down, we've killed ourselves, we've—*

And then, through the smoke and the thunder's long decay, a draft touched his face.

Cold. Moving. Coming *from beyond the bulkhead* — air that had been sealed for twenty years, pushing through, carrying the smell of snow and pine and open sky.

Casper groped for the fallen lantern, found it, coaxed the flame alive. Brigid was on her feet, bleeding from the eyebrow, laughing — actually laughing. Sefu had Ivo by the arm, holding him upright, and the boy was staring past them all at the bulkhead.

Where a ragged hole gaped, tall as a rail car and wider, choked with rubble at its base but *open* — open, and beyond it, impossibly, blessedly, a sliver of gray daylight, and through that sliver, drifting down like the mountain's own surrender, the first flakes of the season's true snow.

The ridge groaned overhead, a long settling sigh — and held.

Ivo found his voice first. It came out cracked and wondering.

"It held. Casper — it *held* —" He turned, and in the lantern light his face was streaked with dust and tears and something that had no name yet. "The gate's open. We have to move *now*, before it changes its mind — and Casper —" he grabbed the ledger from Casper's pack, hugged it to his chest like a living thing— "we carry it out. All of it. Just like the river."

Casper looked at the sliver of daylight, at the snow sifting through it, at the three people he would have called strangers ninety-one days ago, and felt the last weight of the old question lift off him entirely.

"Then we run," he said. "Keeper first."

And behind them, as they scrambled for the light, the deep gate began — softly, patiently, like a man closing a door behind departing guests — to come down.
