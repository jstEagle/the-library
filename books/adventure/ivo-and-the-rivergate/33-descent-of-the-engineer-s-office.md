## 33. Descent of the Engineer's Office

Sefu heard the train before he saw it — not a locomotive, but something smaller and stranger: the sound of many boots on old rails, ringing down the opened gate like a hammer striking a bell that had waited thirty years to be struck.

He was sitting on a fallen timber at the mouth of the deep passage, his bad leg stretched out before him like a piece of equipment someone had forgotten to stow properly. Above him, the new hole in the mountain breathed cold air and drifting snow-smell, and the light that came through it was the flat grey-white of a sky deciding whether to snow again. His hands still smelled of rope pitch and pine resin from the crossing bridge. He found he could not stop looking at them.

Four days they had spent building a span over nothing. Four days of Sefu's arithmetic and Brigid's powder and Casper's stubborn faith, and Ivo moving along the cables like the gorge itself had grown a boy. And then the cable had frayed, and Brigid had nearly gone into the dark, and the instruments — the theodolite, the chain, the levels, half their legal evidence — had dropped away into the white noise below. Sefu had watched them fall with the particular horror of a craftsman watching his own work destroy itself.

And now this. Boots on rails. Many boots.

"Brigid," he said quietly. "Company."

She was already awake. She was always already awake; Sefu suspected she slept the way dynamite slept, entirely and instantly and ready. She came up beside him with her rifle slung loose and her eyes narrowed at the sound.

"That's not Vane's crew," she said. "Too many. Too orderly."

"It's not Harrow at all." Sefu tilted his head, listening the way he listened to a bridge under load — for the note that told you what it truly was. "That's a government step. I know it. I've been inspected enough times to know it. They walk like men who've never once had to hurry."

The first lanterns came around the bend in the deep gallery, and Sefu stood up despite his leg, because some things you meet standing.

* * *

They came through the gate in single file, eight of them, in long wool coats dusted with the plateau's snow, led by a broad woman with a grey braid coiled at her neck and a leather document case strapped across her chest like a bandolier. Behind her walked two clerks, an assistant engineer whose spectacles kept fogging in the cave air, and four provincial constables who looked profoundly unhappy about the geography.

The woman stopped when she saw them — three adults and a child, gaunt, snow-burned, standing among the wreckage of a standoff that had resolved itself only hours before. Her eyes went from Sefu's face to Brigid's rifle to Ivo, who stood slightly apart as always, near the wall, near the dark, one hand resting on the old keeper's ledger where it lay wrapped in oilcloth against his chest.

"I am Chief Inspector Adaeze Okonkwo," the woman said. Her voice carried in the stone without effort, the voice of a person accustomed to being heard in large rooms. "Provincial Engineer's Office. We received a telegram from Greywater eleven days ago regarding sabotage of a concession survey and the possible death of persons inside the Rivergate corridor." She looked around at the soot marks, the scattered tools, the trampled snow blown in through the blast-hole above. "It appears we were late."

"You were early," Sefu said, and heard his own voice come out rougher than he intended. He cleared his throat. "Begging your pardon, ma'am. Most people who come to this mountain come too late for somebody."

Something moved behind the inspector's eyes — recognition, perhaps, or the professional's habit of cataloguing grief. "You are?"

"Sefu Mensah. Bridge carpenter. Late of the Coastal & Northern, dismissed for a leg that doesn't work and a memory that does." He gestured. "Brigid Callahan, blasting expert, blacklisted for talking out loud. Casper Amberdale, engineer, holder of the concession permits — such as they are. And this is Ivo." He hesitated, then did what felt right rather than what felt official. "Ivo of the Rivergate. The keeper."

At the word *keeper*, the inspector's gaze sharpened. Sefu watched her take in the boy — the feral stillness, the lamp hanging at his hip, the way he stood in this cavern the way other boys stood in kitchens — and watched her revise whatever she had planned to say.

"The keeper," she repeated. "Of the gate."

"He'll show you," Sefu said. "Better than any of us could."

* * *

They went down into the lower galleries in a procession, constables' boots echoing off walls that had known only echo for thirty years. Sefu walked near the back, beside the assistant engineer, whose name was Pell and who kept stopping to run his fingers along the rail-gauge notches cut into the rock, muttering measurements under his breath like prayers.

"This gauge," Pell said at last, unable to help himself. "This isn't standard. This isn't anything on record."

"No," Sefu agreed. "It's better."

The drowned gallery had receded with the tide cycle, leaving the lower passage slick and gleaming, and by lamplight the steel showed everywhere now — stubs of rail emerging from mineral crust, tie-plates green with age, and here and there, where Ivo lifted his lamp high, whole lengths of track running straight into the mountain's heart, laid with a precision that made Sefu's craftsman's heart ache. His father had worked the Coastal yards forty years and never seen iron set so true in so hostile a place.

"Who built this?" Inspector Okonkwo asked. Not accusingly. Wonderingly.

"A crew that came here thirty years ago," Casper said. His voice was steady, but Sefu, walking behind him, could see what the steadiness cost. "Led by a man named Elias Amberdale. My father. The province has him listed as vanished. The truth is in the keeper's ledger."

Ivo unwrapped the oilcloth with both hands, careful as a priest, and gave the ledger to the inspector. She read standing, by lamplight, in a cave under a mountain, and Sefu watched her face do what faces did when paper contradicted everything an institution believed about itself. First confusion. Then attention. Then a stillness that was worse than either.

"These entries reference steel shipments," she said slowly. "Heat numbers. Mill certificates."

"Harrow Line steel," Casper said. "Substandard, re-certified with forged stamps, used on the Rivergate viaduct against the engineer's written objection. When the viaduct collapsed, the objection disappeared. So did the engineer." He paused. "My father intended to testify. The last entry says so. It also says — if he did not return — *the gate must stay open.*"

The words fell into the cavern and rang there. Sefu had heard them read aloud once before, the night before the storm, and they had not lost any weight since.

Inspector Okonkwo closed the ledger with exaggerated gentleness, as though it might bruise. "Where is Aldous Vane?" she asked.

* * *

Vane was where they had left him: seated on a crate at the edge of the camp, wrists bound with his own survey twine — Brigid's suggestion, delivered with a smile that Sefu had decided never to ask about — guarded loosely by the two members of his own crew who had refused his final order. The junior surveyor, a young man named Wren with frostbitten ears and an expression like a man stepping off a cliff on purpose, sat beside his former employer and would not look at him.

Vane himself looked, if anything, rested. That was the thing about him that Sefu had understood from the first day in Greywater, watching him ask Marta his soft, pointed questions: Vane was a man who had already survived the worst thing that could happen to him, which was discovering exactly what he was willing to do. Everything after that was bookkeeping.

"Chief Inspector," Vane said pleasantly, as she approached. "I wondered when the office would rouse itself. I trust you had a difficult climb."

"Mr. Vane." She did not return the pleasantry. "You are relieved of your charter authority as of this hour. You will accompany my constables down the mountain. There will be a formal inquiry in Greywater."

"Regarding?"

"The collapse of a seaward cave entrance using charges matching the signature pattern of a blaster employed by your rival. The disappearance of survey evidence. The deaths—" she glanced at Casper, and Sefu saw the flicker pass between them, the thing they had not yet told the inspectors, the thing about the keeper sealed alive in the lower gallery all those years ago, the confession Ivo had given them in the dark with his voice breaking like ice on a river — "of persons connected to the original Rivergate construction. Among other matters."

"Allegations," Vane said. "Mountains breed them. Thin air, thin evidence."

"Mill certificates," said Inspector Okonkwo, holding up the ledger, "are not thin air, Mr. Vane. Heat numbers can be traced. Forged stamps can be compared. And a living witness," she added, and here her voice changed, softened into something almost dangerous in its care, "has already given a preliminary statement."

Every head turned toward Ivo.

The boy stood at the edge of the lamplight, half in shadow, the way he liked to be. But he didn't retreat. Sefu watched him plant his feet — actually plant them, toes gripping the stone through worn boots — and lift his chin.

"I saw them," Ivo said. His voice was small in the great cavern and absolutely clear. "Years ago. Men in Harrow coats. They put the keeper in the lower gallery and set the charge, and I hid, and I lived, and I never told anyone because I thought—" He stopped. Swallowed. Started again, and Sefu felt his own throat close, because he knew this part, knew it from the fire on the plateau, knew it from the inside. "Because surviving felt like the same thing as helping. It isn't. I know that now. The keeper taught me the signals. He called me the river's boy. His name was Elias Amberdale, and he is still in there, and I will say all of it wherever you want me to say it. In Greywater. In the capital. Anywhere."

The silence afterward was the kind Sefu had only heard twice in his life: once at a union meeting just before the vote, and once on the rope bridge, just after the cable frayed. The silence of a thing becoming irreversible.

Casper crossed the space between them and put his hand on the boy's shoulder. He didn't say anything. He didn't need to. Sefu looked away, suddenly interested in the ceiling of the cavern, because some moments you honor by not witnessing them too closely.

* * *

They brought Vane down through the opened gate in the grey afternoon light, and Sefu walked at the rear of the column, because his leg was his leg and because he wanted to see everything.

The constables handled Vane correctly — firmly, impersonally, the way you handle cargo you don't trust. What struck Sefu was Vane's own crew. Wren walked immediately behind the prisoner, upright, pale, and when Vane turned once to speak to him — Sefu couldn't hear the words, only the tone, smooth and reasonable as ever — Wren looked straight ahead and did not answer, and something in his jaw said he never would again. Two more of the Harrow men followed at a distance, and they did not look at their former chief at all. They looked at the rails, the notches, the impossible passage through the mountain, with the expressions of men seeing the true shape of the thing they'd been hired to bury.

The mountain, Sefu thought, keeps its accounts longer than any company keeps its books.

At the seaward mouth — the new mouth, the one Ivo's charge had torn open, ragged-edged and breathing — the light of the outside world came flooding in, and beyond it lay the shore, the grey sea, the cliffs of Greywater with its smoke rising thin and domestic from Marta's chimneys. Somewhere down there, an innkeeper who lied badly and worried worse had picked up a telegraph key and changed the course of everything. Sefu made a private note to buy that woman dinner if it took every coin he earned for a year.

Inspector Okonkwo paused at the threshold and looked back into the mountain — at the rails running into the dark, at the boy standing at his keeper's post with his lamp lit, at the engineer reading his father's ledger one more time by daylight.

"This corridor," she said, mostly to herself, "was supposed to be dead."

"It was only waiting," said Ivo.

* * *

They camped that night at the tide line, the whole strange column of them — inspectors and constables and prisoners and outcasts — because the descent to Greywater was a full day even for unbroken legs, and Sefu's leg had opinions about full days.

He sat by the fire next to Brigid, who was cleaning her rifle with the contentment of a woman whose professional signature had been discussed by a government inquiry and found to be, in context, exculpatory.

"They'll want statements tomorrow," she said. "Pages of them. Names, dates, heat numbers."

"I remember the dates," Sefu said. "All of them. I remember the shipment manifests I checked at Coastal & Northern that matched the ones in the ledger — I checked them years ago and thought I'd imagined the discrepancy. I didn't imagine it. I just didn't have anyone to tell."

"You have us now," Brigid said simply, and went back to her rifle.

Across the fire, Casper sat with Ivo asleep against his shoulder — actually asleep, deeply, the boneless sleep of a child who has finally set down something enormous — and the inspector sat opposite them, writing by lamplight, occasionally glancing up at the pair of them with an expression Sefu could not read and did not try to.

The sea breathed against the shingle. High above, barely visible in the starlight, the opened gate showed as a seam of darkness in the cliff, exhaling the mountain's cold breath, staying open the way a certain dead man had asked it to.

Sefu pulled his coat tighter and let his leg ache and felt, against all reason, completely at peace. Tomorrow there would be testimony, and lawyers, and the grinding machinery of institutions catching up to the truth at their usual glacier's pace. Tomorrow he would stand in an overheated hall in Greywater and recite heat numbers from thirty years ago in a clear voice while men in good coats tried not to hear him.

But tonight — tonight the fire was warm, the boy was sleeping, the gate was open, and the proof ran through the mountain in iron lines no one could forge or falsify, laid true by a dead engineer's hands, waiting all this time for someone to come and read it.

"Hey, Casper," he said quietly, across the flames.

Casper looked up.

"When they ask you tomorrow — at the inquiry — what you're going to ask for?" Brigid had wondered the same thing all evening; Sefu could feel her pretending not to listen. "Your job back? Your name cleared?"

Casper was quiet for a moment. He looked down at Ivo, and then past him, up at the dark seam in the cliffs where the river of his father's unfinished work finally reached the sea.

"Neither," he said. "I'm going to ask them to charter the line."

And far up the coast, carried on the wind off the water, so faint it might have been imagination, came the whistle of the morning boat out of Greywater — bringing, though none of them could know it yet, the first word of what the world beyond the mountain intended to do with the truth it had been handed.
