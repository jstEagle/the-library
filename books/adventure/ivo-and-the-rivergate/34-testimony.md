## 34. Testimony

The hall at Greywater had never been built for what it was asked to hold that morning.

It was a fish-curing shed in its first life, a dance floor in its second, and for the last twenty years it had served as the village's meeting house — a long room of salt-bleached timber with a stove at one end that had opinions about how much wood it deserved and a roof that leaked in exactly two places, both of them over the benches where the important people sat. Marta had spent three days preparing it. She'd scrubbed the floors until the grain stood up like gooseflesh, hung oil lamps on every nail that would bear one, and set the stove to roaring so early that by the time the first witnesses arrived the room was hot as a kiln and smelled of pitch smoke, wet wool, and the sea.

Casper stood at the front of it with his hands folded behind his back, waiting for the room to fill, and watched the faces come in.

They came in slowly, the way villagers do when they are deciding something collectively without ever saying so aloud. The fishermen first, because they had finished their morning haul and because curiosity was a kind of cargo they carried well. Then the shopkeepers. Then the women who ran the drying racks and the children who were supposed to be in school and were not, pressed against the back wall under the stern but non-enforcing eye of the schoolmistress. Old Sera came in leaning on her grandson's arm and took the bench nearest the stove, as was her right by seniority and by the fact that she had been the one to tell Casper about the tide three months ago, low enough to show what wants showing, and everything since had grown out of that sentence like a tree from a seed.

And near the back, flanked by two provincial constables who did not look comfortable in a room this warm, sat Aldous Vane.

He had shaved. Someone had found him a clean shirt. He sat with his hands flat on his knees and his chin level and his eyes forward, and he looked, Casper thought, like a man performing composure the way a bad actor performs grief — technically correct, entirely hollow. His crew was not with him. His crew had walked down off the mountain two days behind the inspectors, and somewhere along the way it had stopped being his crew. Half of them had already given statements. The other half were sitting scattered through the hall like men trying to be invisible in a room where everyone knew their names.

The junior surveyor sat apart from all of them, on the end of a bench near the door, as if he hadn't quite decided whether he belonged to any group in the room. He was young — younger than Casper had realized on the mountain, where fear and lamplight had aged everyone equally. His name was Elias Wren. He had refused Vane's order at the deep gate with a rifle still in his hands and snow melting down his collar, and that single act of refusal had cracked Vane's authority open like river ice in spring. He had barely slept since. It showed in his face. So did something else — something that looked, from a distance, like a man setting down a weight he had carried so long he'd forgotten it wasn't part of his body.

At the head table sat the inspectors: three men from the provincial engineer's office, arrived two days ago through the opened gate with mud on their boots and ledgers under their arms, led by a broad, gray-whiskered man named Corliss who had shaken Casper's hand at the cave mouth and said only, "You'll testify Thursday. Bring everything." Everything had turned out to mean four ledgers, a rusted lantern, a length of Harrow steel stamped with a heat number that matched no shipment Harrow would admit to, and a map drawn in a dead man's hand.

The stove ticked. The lamps hissed. Corliss rapped a knuckle on the table, and the hall went quiet the way water goes still — reluctantly, in rings.

"We're here," Corliss said, "to establish facts. Not grievances. Not reputations. Facts." He said it while looking, briefly and deliberately, at no one in particular, which everyone understood to mean at everyone. "Mr. Amberdale. You'll begin."

* * *

Casper had prepared notes. He had spent half the night in Marta's kitchen arranging them in order, and Marta had listened from the doorway with her arms crossed and finally said, "You'll throw those away inside ten minutes," and she had been right. He got perhaps four sentences into his opening — his dismissal from the Coastal & Northern, the trestle at Millbrook, the signature he would not put to rotten timber — before he understood that the notes were a wall between himself and the room, and that the room deserved better than a wall.

He set them down.

"I was fired for telling the truth," he said. "That's the beginning of this, though it isn't the most important part of it. I want to be careful here, because I've had three months to think about what happens when a man treats truth like a weapon instead of a foundation. I don't intend to make that mistake twice."

He told them about the Rivergate contract. About the ninety-day permits and the clerk's brittle warning — after first snow, the corridor reverts to the Harrow Line — and about arriving in Greywater knowing nothing except that his father's old map ended mid-line at the gorge, as if the man who drew it had stopped believing in it. He told them about the tide. Heads turned toward Sera's bench when he did, and the old woman received the attention the way a lighthouse receives weather.

"I'm going to say plainly what happened in that cave," Casper said, "because there are versions of it already moving around this valley, and versions are how men like Aldous Vane survive. Here is the version I know."

He told them about the gauge-notches cut into the cavern walls. About the keeper's station and the ledgers maintained in a dead man's hand for years after his death. About Ivo — and here he paused, because Ivo was sitting in the front row beside Brigid and Sefu, and Casper had promised him he could leave whenever he wanted, and Ivo had said, in his flat signalman's cadence, *line clear, proceed*, which was the boy's way of saying he would stay. "Ivo is twelve years old," Casper said. "He has lived in that gate since the last keeper stopped coming back. The last keeper was my father. I did not know that when I entered the cave. I learned it from a ledger."

He told them about the experimental gauge. About the viaduct. About the inspection notes in his father's hand proving the steel that collapsed had been supplied by Harrow at a price no honest mill could match, and the correspondence showing the finding was buried, and the final entry — *if I do not return, the gate must stay open* — read aloud in a dark tunnel while armed men held rifles on the people listening.

The hall made a sound at that. Not a gasp; Greywater was a fishing village, and fishing villages do not gasp. It was lower than a gasp — a collective exhale, the sound of forty people breathing out a thing they had half-known for thirty years. Because that was the truth of it, Casper realized as he spoke: the village had always known the keeper hadn't simply vanished. You do not forget a man who walks into a mountain and never walks out. You just stop talking about him, because talking costs more than silence, until someone comes from outside and makes silence more expensive than speech.

He told them about the sabotage — Brigid standing in the wrecked portal entrance, reading the cut of the charges like handwriting, saying *that's my pattern, somebody studied me*. He told them about the drowned gallery, and Sefu's leg, and the hundred yards he had carried a man he'd known for six weeks. He told them about the crossing, and the frayed cable, and the instruments going down into the gorge — and here Corliss interrupted for the first time.

"The instruments," Corliss said. "Your measurements. Your legal evidence of the route's condition. All of it lost?"

"All of it," Casper said. "Which is why I stand here with no survey numbers to offer you. What I offer instead is the cave itself. It's still there. It's open. Every gauge-notch, every rail section, every ledger page is exactly where we left it, and your own inspectors have walked it. I can't prove anything from this table. The mountain can prove everything, and it already has."

Corliss wrote something down. Beside him, the second inspector — a thin woman named Okonjo who had spent the previous day crawling through the lower galleries with a lamp and a notebook — nodded once, slowly, like a judge confirming her own verdict.

"Then let's hear from the mountain's other witness," Corliss said. "Mr. Wren. You were employed by the Harrow Line as junior surveyor on Mr. Vane's crew. You are not obliged to speak. You should understand that anything you say may expose you to charges alongside your former superior. Do you understand that?"

"Yes, sir," said Elias Wren, and stood up.

His voice shook at first. Casper watched him plant his feet the way Sefu had taught the whole crew to plant feet on bad ground — weight low, knees soft — and the shaking settled.

"Mr. Vane hired me eight months ago," Wren said. "I want to start with that, because I've heard people say he must have been corrupt from the start, and I don't believe that's true, and I think it matters that it isn't. He was a good surveyor. He taught me things I'll use the rest of my life. What happened on that mountain didn't begin with a wicked man. It began with a permit deadline and a company that treated the deadline as worth more than the mountain."

He described it in order, and his voice steadied as he went, the way a voice does when a man discovers that the truth, spoken fully, holds him up rather than dragging him down. The orders to shadow Amberdale's crew. The instruction to report their progress daily. The night Vane came back from Greywater having offered to buy the permits and been refused, and stood looking at the fire for a long time before saying, quietly, to no one, *"He could have just sold."*

"He didn't order the sabotage directly," Wren said. "I want to be accurate about that, because accuracy is the only thing I have left to give. He ordered us to 'delay the Amberdale party by any means consistent with safety.' Two of the men interpreted that with blasting powder. They used a charge pattern copied from Brigid O'Leary's published work — Mr. Vane provided the diagrams. When I asked him afterward whether he understood what he'd done, he said—" Wren stopped. Swallowed. "He said, 'The mountain was going to close that entrance eventually. We just helped the schedule.' That was the moment I started keeping my own notes."

He produced them. A small waterproof notebook, swollen with damp, filled with a neat surveyor's hand: dates, orders, quantities of powder drawn from stores, the words Vane had used and the ones he had carefully not used. Corliss received it the way a priest receives a confession — with both hands and a bowed head.

"And at the deep gate," Corliss said gently. "Tell the hall about the deep gate."

Wren's eyes went, for one unguarded second, to the back of the room — to the place where Ivo sat — and then away again, fast, like a man touching a hot rail.

"There's a boy," he said. "Twelve years old. And Mr. Vane ordered us to seal the entrance with the Amberdale party inside it. For safety, he called it. And I looked at that boy, and I looked at my rifle, and I understood that one of those things was going to define the rest of my life, and I put the rifle down." His voice cracked on the last word and he let it. "I put it down and I stepped aside, and Hollis put his down, and then Reyes, and Mr. Vane was left holding an order that nobody would follow, and that's the whole of what I know about power, sir. It lasts exactly as long as people agree to carry it."

The stove ticked into the silence. Somewhere in the back a child whispered a question and was shushed.

Vane had not moved through any of it. He sat with his hands flat on his knees, and when Corliss finally said, "Mr. Vane, you'll have your opportunity to respond tomorrow," the man inclined his head a precise half-inch, rose between his constables, and walked out with the measured tread of a man who had rehearsed dignity in a cell mirror. Only at the door did he pause — one heartbeat, his shoulders rigid under the borrowed clean shirt — and then the door closed behind him and the hall exhaled again.

* * *

They broke at noon for bread and cheese and the fish stew Marta had been simmering since dawn, eaten on benches in the yard because the hall was too full and too hot to hold both testimony and lunch. Casper ate standing against the fence rail, and Ivo appeared beside him the way Ivo appeared everywhere — silently, suddenly, already eating.

"You talked a long time," Ivo said.

"I had a lot to say."

"You said the gate must stay open. From the book." Ivo chewed. Considered. "You said it like it was yours to say."

Casper looked down at him. The boy's face was unreadable in the way of weather — something happening behind it, large and slow.

"It wasn't mine," Casper said. "It was his. But he gave it to whoever would keep it. That's what a keeper does. He doesn't own the gate. He keeps it for the next one."

Ivo absorbed this with the gravity of a customs officer inspecting a suspicious declaration. Then: "Tomorrow they decide about the line."

"Tomorrow they hear Vane. The day after, they decide."

"If they charter it wrong," Ivo said, "it becomes another Harrow. Another company drawing lines through a mountain that belongs to the river." He said it without anger, which somehow made it heavier. "The river doesn't keep things. It carries them. Companies keep things."

Casper crouched down to the boy's level, stew bowl forgotten on the fence post. "Then tomorrow, when they ask me what I want — and they will ask, I've been warned — I'm going to tell them exactly that. Not my father's name cleared onto a plaque. Not my old job back. The corridor, chartered honestly, with the gate kept by someone who knows it." He held the boy's eyes. "Someone the river already trusts."

Ivo studied him for a long moment. Then he finished his stew, handed Casper the empty bowl, and said, "Line clear," and walked off toward the shore, because some boys, even after a winter of found family, still needed the sound of the water to think.

* * *

The afternoon session was shorter. Brigid testified about the sabotage pattern with a dynamite expert's precision, drawing the charge layout in chalk on the hall floor while the stenographer scrambled. Sefu testified about the drowned gallery and recited, from perfect memory, every measurement the lost instruments had taken — dates, chainages, grades, the whole survey rebuilt from inside his skull, and Corliss actually laughed once, in disbelief and delight, and said, "Man, you're worth more than the instruments." Sefu allowed as how his leg disagreed.

Marta testified last. She told them about the telegram — her hands shaking at the wire office, the operator reading the permit numbers back twice, the long terrible silence afterward in which she had imagined every consequence and chosen the truth anyway. She cried a little, angrily, and dared anyone in the hall to mention it, and no one did.

Then Corliss turned to Casper for the closing statement of the day, and the hall, overheated and exhausted, leaned in.

"Mr. Amberdale," Corliss said. "Before we adjourn. There's a matter I'm instructed to raise. The Coastal & Northern has communicated through counsel that, in light of today's evidence regarding the Millbrook trestle, they are prepared to reinstate you. Full restoration of position, back wages, a formal apology entered into the record." He let the murmur run through the benches and die. "The province is also prepared to discuss your appointment as chief engineer on whatever corridor we ultimately charter. Both offers are substantial. The hall would like to know your answer."

Casper had known this question was coming for two days, and he had expected it to feel like triumph, and it did not. It felt like standing at a junction with two rails running out ahead of him, both of them laid by other men.

He stood, and the room went very quiet, and he thought of a rotten trestle and a pen he had refused to pick up, and of a ledger whose last entry was an instruction rather than a lament, and of a boy on the shore learning the sound of the tide.

"No," he said.

The word landed in the hall like a dropped tool.

"Coastal & Northern fired me for being right, and now they want to hire me back for the same reason, and if I take it, the lesson they learn is that being right eventually pays, which means the next man should hold out for payment instead of holding the line. I won't teach that lesson." He turned slightly, addressing the whole room now, the fishermen and the shopkeepers and the children and the inspectors. "And I won't be anybody's chief engineer of a corridor I haven't helped build from the first spike. What I ask is smaller and harder. Charter the Rivergate honestly. Build it to the gauge my father designed — the gauge that fits the mountain instead of fighting it. Preserve the gate as a working passage, not a sealed curiosity. Put the crew that opened it on the payroll to build it — O'Leary, Mekonnen, and myself, if we'll have each other, which I believe we will." He paused. "And appoint a keeper for the gate. Not a caretaker. A keeper. Someone who lives with it, knows its moods, keeps its ledger. The position has been vacant thirty years, and it hasn't gone unfilled — it's been filled illegally, faithfully, by a twelve-year-old who deserves to hold it in daylight."

Every head in the hall turned toward the front row. Ivo, who had slipped back in during the afternoon session, sat very straight beside Brigid, his face composed into the particular stillness of a boy refusing to hope in public.

Corliss looked at him for a long moment, then down at his notes, then at the thin inspector beside him, and something passed between the three of them that needed no words.

"This commission will deliberate tonight," Corliss said. "We'll reconvene at nine tomorrow to hear Mr. Vane's response, and after that—" he gathered his papers, and the lamplight caught the gray in his whiskers, and for just a moment he looked less like an inspector and more like a man about to make history and enjoying it enormously— "after that, this commission intends to render its decision on the charter. Get some sleep, all of you. Tomorrow the mountain gets its answer."

The hall emptied slowly into the cold blue evening, and Casper stood at the window watching the lamps of Greywater come alight one by one along the shore, beyond them the black line of the cliffs, and beneath the cliffs, behind the stone, the gate — open, for the first time in thirty years, waiting to learn what it would become.

Behind him, small footsteps on the boards. Ivo stopped at his shoulder and looked out at the same dark water.

"They'll ask me tomorrow," the boy said. "In front of everyone. Whether I'll take it."

"What will you say?"

Ivo was quiet a long time. Out past the harbor bar, the tide was turning, and the sound of it came faintly through the walls — the river carrying everything, keeping nothing.

"I don't know yet," he said. "But I think I know what my name will be."
