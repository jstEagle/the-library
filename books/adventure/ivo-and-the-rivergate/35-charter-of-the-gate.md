## 35. Charter of the Gate

Brigid O'Rourke had spent eleven years learning to read the moods of powerful men, and she had learned it the hard way — through foremen's silences, through the particular way a company man's smile failed to reach his eyes when he decided you were trouble. She had learned to hear the word *no* in a hundred dialects: the polite no, the legal no, the no that came wrapped in a promotion to some distant siding where she could do no harm. So when the letter arrived in Greywater bearing the provincial seal, she read it three times before she let herself believe it, because her whole life had trained her to expect the trap inside the good news.

There was no trap.

She stood in Marta's kitchen with the paper flat on the scrubbed pine table, and Sefu leaned over her shoulder reading aloud in his slow, careful way, each word tested like a plank before he put his weight on it. Casper sat across the table with his hands wrapped around a cup of tea gone cold, and Ivo perched on the windowsill with his boots swinging, pretending not to listen, which was how Ivo listened to everything.

"*By order of the Provincial Engineer's Office,*" Sefu read, "*the Rivergate corridor is hereby chartered for construction of a public railway, to be laid to the gauge of the original experimental line, the cave passage known as the Tollgate to be preserved in perpetuity as a working section thereof.*" He stopped. He read the next part silently, his lips moving, and then he looked up at Brigid with an expression she had never seen on his face before — the look of a man who has checked his own arithmetic four times and still can't make the sum come out wrong. "*The undersigned are hereby engaged as chief of construction, master carpenter, and blasting engineer, respectively.*"

"Respectively," Brigid said. Her voice came out strange. She cleared her throat. "That's you, me, and Casper. In order."

"I know what respectively means," Sefu said mildly. "I wanted to hear it twice."

Marta, who had been standing at her stove with her back to them for a full minute, doing something to a pot that needed no doing, finally turned around. Her eyes were red. "Well," she said, in the voice of a woman who had lied badly to Harrow men and worried worse and was now, for once in her life, allowed to stop worrying. "Well. Somebody put that in writing, did they? The whole province? In writing?"

"In writing," Brigid said. "Sealed. Stamped. Signed by a man named Corrigan who I'm told has a reputation for meaning what he says."

"Imagine that," Marta said, and went back to her pot, and Brigid heard her sniff once, sharply, and pretended not to.

Ivo slid off the windowsill and came to the table. He didn't touch the letter. He stood looking down at it the way he looked at things that mattered — completely still, head tilted, the way he'd once stood at the edge of the drowned gallery listening to the echo tell him how deep the water ran. "Does it say the gate?" he asked.

Casper turned the letter around and put his finger on the line. "Preserved in perpetuity," he said. "As a working passage. Nobody seals it, Ivo. Nobody ever seals it. It's law now."

Ivo considered this. Then he nodded, once, the small precise nod he used for things he had decided to accept, and went back to the window, and Brigid understood that inside that narrow chest something enormous had just been set down — the fear he had carried since he was small enough to fit inside a keeper's cot, the fear that the world's habit was to close things, to wall things up, to make people and places disappear. The province had put it in writing. The gate stayed open. The river's boy had a document.

Brigid looked down at the letter again, at the line with her own name in it. *Blasting engineer.* Not *the woman who gave the union speech.* Not *the dynamiter who was blacklisted from every line between here and the coast.* Her name, her trade, her signature pattern — the same pattern Vane's men had stolen to frame her crew — written into the charter of a railway that was going to be built honestly or not built at all.

She had to sit down. She did it casually, pulling out a chair, but Sefu saw, and put his big warm hand briefly on her shoulder, and said nothing, which was the kindest thing he could have done.

* * *

The ceremony, when it came, was held in Greywater's overheated hall, the same hall where Casper had testified, and Brigid spent the first half hour of it fighting the feeling that she was going to be arrested.

That was the thing about vindication nobody warned you about: it arrived wearing the same clothes as the trap. The hall was full. The whole village had turned out, plus the government men in their good coats, plus a newspaper man from the provincial capital who kept writing things down, and Brigid stood at the front with her hands at her sides and felt every eye on her like a lamp too close to a fuse. Eleven years of being the woman companies hired quietly and fired loudly. Eleven years of sitting at the edge of camps, of eating second, of learning which foremen's word was worth the spit to seal it.

Commissioner Corrigan was a broad, gray, unhurried man who read the charter aloud from the steps of the hall's little stage, and he read all of it, every clause, in a voice like a man laying rail — steady, even, each section seated firmly on the last. The corridor. The gauge. The preservation of the Tollgate passage. The findings of the inquiry into the viaduct collapse, *and the exoneration of the engineering staff of the late Rivergate survey, including the posthumous clearing of the name of Edmund Amberdale, keeper.*

Brigid heard the hall react to that — the rustle, the murmur, old women crossing themselves — and she heard Casper beside her not react at all, which was its own kind of sound. She glanced at him. He was watching Corrigan with his jaw set and his eyes very bright, and his hands were perfectly still on the rail in front of him, and she thought: *there's a man holding a bridge up from underneath.* She knew the posture. She'd held it herself, plenty of times, in rooms where the roof was coming down and the only thing to do was stand in the doorway and be the last true thing standing.

Then Corrigan came to the appointments.

"*For chief of construction, Casper Amberdale.*" A pause. "*Who has asked that the record also reflect his resignation, effective immediately, from any position with the Harrow Line, past or future, and who will serve the province under his own name and no company's.*" Corrigan looked up over his spectacles at Casper, and something passed between them — Brigid never learned what, some private thing from the testimony days — and Corrigan said, "*Accepted and so recorded.*"

"*For master carpenter, Sefu Mensah, whose span across the gorge the inquiry's engineers have examined and described in their report as* — *" Corrigan paused, and Brigid would have sworn the dry old man was enjoying himself, "*— *'sound beyond any requirement of law, and an embarrassment to the profession.'*"

The hall laughed, warm and loud, and Sefu, standing straight as a trestle in his good coat with his bad leg braced, inclined his head like a man accepting a compliment he intended to live up to.

"*For blasting engineer, Brigid O'Rourke,*" Corrigan said, and here he did stop, and looked directly at her, and the hall went quiet in the way a hall goes quiet when it knows what's coming. "*Whose signature patterns were misappropriated in the commission of crimes against this survey, whose testimony was material to the conviction of Aldous Vane, and whose record, as examined by this office, contains not a single charge that survives honest scrutiny.*" He set the paper down. "*The province is fortunate, madam, to get you at any price. It is a disgrace that it gets you at this one.*"

Brigid had prepared, in the nights before, a short speech. Something dignified. Something about second chances. She opened her mouth to deliver it and what came out instead was: "You'll have the best-blasted mountain in the dominion, Commissioner, or you can dock my wages," and the hall roared, and Corrigan's gray face cracked into an actual smile, and Brigid O'Rourke stood in the light of a hundred lamps in a hall full of people who knew her name and knew it clean, and let it happen to her.

Afterward there was food, because Marta had commandeered the hall kitchen three days in advance and would have fed a regiment. Brigid found herself at a long table with Sefu on one side and the newspaper man on the other, and the newspaper man wanted to know about the cave, about the flood, about the crossing, and she gave him the parts that were hers to give and deflected the parts that belonged to others, and somewhere in the middle of it she realized she was telling the stories like stories — with the shape of them, the fear and the laugh in the right places — instead of like testimony. That was new. That was what it felt like, she supposed, when a thing stopped being a wound and started being a scar you could put your hand on in winter.

Ivo did not come to the ceremony. Brigid found him afterward, at dusk, up at the keeper's station — she'd hiked the lower galleries with Casper enough times now to know the way — sitting at the old desk with the great ledger open, the lamp trimmed and burning, doing what Ivo did, which was keeping. She stood in the entrance a while before he acknowledged her.

"They gave it a number," Ivo said. He was looking at a paper on the desk, an official copy of the charter, its margins dense with the small print of law. "The gate. Section seven, subsection four. The Tollgate passage is *section seven, subsection four* now." He said it without anger, which somehow made it worse. "It had a better name before."

"It had a name one boy used," Brigid said, coming to lean against the desk. "Now it has one the whole province uses. Both of them are true. That's more than most things get."

Ivo considered this with the grave, total attention he gave everything. Then he turned the ledger around so she could see the page. The old entries — his father-figure's entries, the keeper's entries, the ones that ended mid-sentence in the deep gate — were on the earlier pages, and Ivo had begun, in his careful hand, a new sequence. Dates. Water levels. Air readings. The small daily facts of a living passage. And at the top of the newest page, written larger, as if it were itself a reading to be recorded: *CHARTERED. OPEN BY LAW.*

"Section seven, subsection four," Ivo said. "But in here it's the gate." He closed the ledger gently, the way you'd close a door on a sleeping child. "The law can visit anytime it likes."

* * *

They went up to the plateau the next morning to walk the line.

The charter gave them the whole corridor, and the whole corridor was forty miles of ruined grade, collapsed tunnel, the gorge with Sefu's famous span still strung across it like a spider's web that had outlasted the spider, and the viaduct — the viaduct they had not yet decided what to do with, and Brigid caught Casper looking at it the whole time they were up there, the way a man looks at a grave he intends to build a monument over.

But the walking was good. That was the thing Brigid kept being surprised by: how good it felt to walk a line you were going to build, with the paper in your pocket that said it was yours to build. She had spent her career blowing things open for other men's routes, other men's profits, and then blowing things shut again when the profits moved on. Now she walked the grade with a surveyor's chain over her shoulder and her own name on the contract, and every rock she looked at, she looked at as a thing that would still be there in fifty years, carrying trains, because she had decided where it would sit.

Sefu walked the gorge rim for an hour without speaking, measuring with his eyes, and finally said, "Timber. There's enough sound wreckage below to frame the approaches, and the province can bring the rest up the old switchback if we clear it. Six months for a permanent span. Four, if the weather lies to us kindly."

"Four months," Brigid said. "You said four days for the rope one."

"The rope one was to keep us alive." Sefu looked out over the gorge, where the river ran small and bright a thousand feet down. "This one is to outlive us. It's a different arithmetic."

They stood there the three of them — Casper had gone ahead along the rim, following the old grade — and Brigid found herself doing something she never did: talking about the future as if she believed in it. "I'm thinking about the charges for the tunnel mouths," she said. "Low-shatter patterns. I've got a new cut sequence I've been carrying in my head for six years with nowhere to use it, because every company wanted it fast and cheap and didn't care what the rock looked like after. This one, I want the faces clean. I want a man to ride through that mountain in fifty years and see stone that was cut by somebody who gave a damn."

Sefu nodded slowly. "And I'll build him trestles he can trust in the dark," he said. "And Casper will grade the roadbed so the water goes where the water should."

"And Ivo will keep the gate," Brigid said, and they both smiled, because it had already stopped being a question.

They caught up to Casper at the head of the old switchback, where the grade began its long descent toward Greywater, and he was standing very still, looking down the mountain, and Brigid followed his gaze and understood. Below them, far down where the valley opened, the Harrow main line ran — the great steel river of the company that had killed his father's crew, stolen the gate's secret, bought the silence, and lost. And along it, small with distance, a work train was moving, and Brigid realized with a strange lurch that it was moving *toward* them, or toward the valley junction below Greywater, and it was carrying — she could see them even at that distance, stacked like cordwood — rails. New rails. Rails of a gauge that matched no company on record.

"They're delivering early," Casper said. His voice was quiet and steady and did not quite cover what was under it. "Corrigan said spring. That's the first shipment." He took out his watch, an old habit, and looked at it, and Brigid knew he wasn't reading the time. He was reading the season. The light was already going long and gold at the edges; the plateau grass had gone to rust; and up here, in the country Ivo knew, first snow was a matter of weeks, not months.

"Four months, you said," Brigid said to Sefu.

"Four months, if the weather lies to us kindly," Sefu said again.

They stood a moment in the wind, the three of them, with the whole impossible corridor spread out below and above and through the mountain, and then Casper put his watch away and turned back up the grade with the set to his shoulders that Brigid had learned to read as *we begin now.*

"Then we'd better go lie to it," he said, and started walking, and they went with him.

* * *

That evening, back in Greywater, the four of them sat in Marta's kitchen around the charter itself, which Marta insisted on framing and hanging over her stove, and which Ivo had been allowed, after solemn negotiation, to touch once. Marta fed them until they surrendered. Outside, the tide was coming in over the flats, and the light through the window went from gold to violet to the deep blue that meant the season was turning.

Brigid sat back with her tea — hot this time — and looked around the table at the crew. Not the crew, she corrected herself. She'd been using the word *crew* for eleven years for collections of strangers thrown together on other men's projects, and this was not that. This was the thing she'd stopped believing in somewhere around the third blacklisting and had never expected to see again from the inside: people who had chosen each other, in a cave, in a flood, in a snowstorm, in a standoff with rifles in the dark, and kept choosing each other since.

Sefu was sketching trestle bents on the back of a delivery manifest, his pencil moving with the calm of a man whose arithmetic never lied. Casper was reading the gauge specifications, his lips moving, his father's old map open beside the new provincial drawings, the dead man's ink and the living government's ink lying together on the same table without contradiction. Ivo sat by the fire with the ledger on his knees, writing the day's entry, and Brigid watched him write and thought about what the boy had said at the registry, or would say, or had already decided to say — she'd caught him practicing the letters on scrap paper for a week now, over and over, a name he was building with his own hand.

"So," she said, into the comfortable noise of the fire and the pencil and the turning pages. "Four months. Forty miles. A mountain, a gorge, a viaduct, and a cave the law says nobody's allowed to close." She raised her teacup. "To the best-blasted mountain in the dominion."

Sefu raised his pencil. Casper raised the gauge specification. Ivo looked up from the ledger, considered the three of them, and raised the lamp — the keeper's lamp, the one with the old stamped initials on the base, burning in the window where the whole valley could see it.

"To the gate," Ivo said. "Open by law."

And it was later — much later, when Sefu had gone up to his loft and Casper had gone out to check the tide against the tables, because Casper still walked the shore every low tide — that the knock came at Marta's door. Brigid answered it, still half in the warm haze of the evening, and found on the step a mud-splattered boy with a telegraph office cap and a slip of paper, and behind him, coming up the dark street from the direction of the valley junction, the sound Brigid had spent eleven years learning to fear and was only now learning to hear plainly: the sound of a work train whistling for the crossing, coming up out of the night toward Greywater, three weeks ahead of any schedule anyone had promised.

She looked at the telegram. She read it twice. Then she turned back into the kitchen, where Marta was banking the fire and Ivo was blowing out the lamp, and said, "Get Casper. The first shipment's here tonight — and the telegram says there's a man on it from the Engineer's Office who wants to inspect the gate at first light." She paused, feeling the words settle into place like a charge seated in its hole. "He's asking for the keeper."
