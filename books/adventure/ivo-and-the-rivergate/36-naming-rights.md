## 36. Naming Rights

The registry office in Greywater smelled of lamp oil and old paper and the particular staleness of rooms where men wrote down other people's lives without ever having lived them.

Ivo stood in the doorway a long moment before entering. He had been in buildings before — the keeper's station was a building of sorts, though he thought of it more as a den, a hollow, a place that belonged to the mountain with the mountain's permission — but this was different. This room had straight edges. Straight-edged windows, straight-edged counter, straight-edged man behind the counter who looked up at Ivo the way people looked at weather they hadn't planned for.

"You'll be the boy," the man said. Not a question. Word traveled in Greywater faster than the tide came in; Marta's inn was a bell tower of gossip, and everything that had happened on the mountain — the standoff, the inspectors, Vane being walked down through the opened gate between two government men — had rung through it for three days straight.

"I'm the keeper," Ivo said.

The clerk's pen paused above his ledger. "The *appointed* keeper," he corrected gently, in the tone of a man laying a ruler across a crooked line. "Pending paperwork. Which is why you're here." He gestured at the chair on the visitor's side of the counter. It was too tall for Ivo; his feet wouldn't reach the floor. He sat anyway, because Casper had told him this morning that there were rooms where sitting was part of the work, and this, apparently, was one of them.

Casper stood behind him. He'd been standing behind Ivo all week — at the inquiry in Marta's overheated hall, on the walk down from the plateau, even in the cave when the inspectors' lamps had swept the galleries where Ivo had spent his whole life. He never said he was standing guard. He just arranged himself there, the way a breakwater arranges itself, and let the words go over both their heads.

Brigid was there too, leaning against the doorframe with her arms crossed, radiating the particular patience of a woman who had once threatened to blow up a foreman's outhouse and considered it a reasonable act of workplace communication. Sefu waited outside, because his leg hated stairs and he refused to let anyone see him favor it more than necessary.

"Name," the clerk said, pen poised.

"Ivo."

"Full name, boy. Christian name and surname. The appointment has to be entered properly or it won't hold against challenge. And there *will* be challenges." The clerk's eyes flicked toward Casper, then away. Everyone in Greywater knew whose steel had held up whose viaduct now. Everyone knew what Harrow had buried under forty years of quiet. But knowing a thing and trusting it were different animals, and the clerk trusted paper the way Ivo trusted the river — completely, and only after testing it many times.

Ivo looked at the ledger. It was a fat book bound in green cloth gone grey at the corners, its pages ruled into columns: name, trade, location, date. Hundreds of names. Thousands. Every one of them written by someone else about someone else. He wondered how many of those people had chosen what went beside their names. How many had simply inherited it, the way you inherit a coat that doesn't fit, and worn it your whole life because taking it off wasn't an option anyone offered.

He knew about names. He knew what it was to not have one.

The last keeper — Casper's father, though Ivo was still learning how to hold that fact without dropping it — had called him *the river's boy*. That had been enough, then. A river doesn't need your surname to know you. It knows you by your weight, your step, the way you read its moods at dawn. For six years the river had been the only witness to his life, and it had never once asked him to spell anything.

But the river didn't write ledgers. Men did. And men, it turned out, needed two names minimum before they'd hand you a lamp and a post and call it yours.

"He hasn't got a surname," Brigid said from the doorframe. Her voice was casual in the way a lit fuse is casual. "Registry never registered him. Nobody did. That's rather the point of everything that's happened, if you've been following along."

The clerk flushed. "There are provisions. Guardianship filings, ward-of-the-province designations—"

"No," Ivo said.

It came out quieter than he meant it to, but it came out clean, the way a signal flag goes up: one word, no wind to tangle it. The clerk stopped talking. Casper shifted behind him, and Ivo felt the shift — not a correction, not a hand on his shoulder telling him to be reasonable. Just attention. Just a man making sure he heard whatever came next.

"There are provisions," Ivo repeated, tasting the clerk's own phrase. "I want to use them my way."

The clerk folded his hands. "Go on."

Ivo had thought about this for three days. He had thought about it in the bath Marta had insisted on, in the bed that was too soft, in the hall during the testimony when Casper had stood up in front of the whole province's man and said *I don't want my old job back, I want the corridor chartered honestly*. He had thought about it while watching Casper say that — watching a man set down a name like setting down a heavy pack, and stand straighter without it.

Casper Amberdale had been given his name the way rails are given their gauge: by other people, long before he could object, and he had spent his whole life being measured against it. His father's name. His father's disgrace. His father's ghost walking ahead of him through every tunnel. In the deep gate, reading the last ledger entry by lamplight, Casper had wept — Ivo had seen it, had pretended not to, which was its own kind of keeping — and afterward he had said, *he wasn't a coward, they made him a story instead of a man*, and Ivo had understood something then that he was only now ready to say out loud:

A name can be a cage or a door. Most people never get to choose which.

But he could. Right now, with a pen waiting, he could.

"The keeper called me the river's boy," Ivo said. "Because the river gave me over to him, near enough. Flood year. You can look it up if your books go back far enough — probably they don't, nothing about Greywater gets written down unless it's taxes." The clerk had the grace to look mildly ashamed. "He never gave me his name. I used to think that meant I wasn't really his. Took me years to understand it was the opposite. He was saying: you're not mine to give. You're your own thing the river sent. He was leaving the space empty on purpose."

The room had gone very still. Even the scratch of some other clerk's pen in the back had stopped, the way birdsong stops when a hawk crosses the yard.

"So I'm not going to fill the space with somebody else's name," Ivo said. "Not his. Not—" he glanced back at Casper, quick, and Casper met it steady, no flinch, no hurt, only that same breakwater patience — "not anybody's. I'm going to write what I am."

He took the pen. It was heavier than it looked, a black stick with a cold metal nib, and the inkwell was one of those glass ones with a lip like a bird's beak. His hand, which could tie a snare in the dark and read echo off wet stone, hesitated over the ruled line. Writing was still new to him. The keeper had taught him letters from the old ledgers, sounding out entries like songs, but Ivo had mostly learned by tracing, and his letters came out tall and uneven, a fence line built by someone who respected fences but had never lived inside one.

He wrote: **Ivo**.

Then he paused, and the clerk leaned forward helpfully, mouth opening to suggest, and Brigid made a small sound from the doorway that closed it again.

Then Ivo wrote: **of the Rivergate**.

His letters wobbled. The *g*'s descended below the line like things diving. He didn't care. When he finished he sat back and looked at it, and something settled in his chest that he had no signal flag for — a feeling like the moment after a storm passes and you go outside and check every line and every stone and find it all still standing, *your* lines, *your* stones, held.

*Ivo of the Rivergate.*

Not inherited. Not assigned. Made. The way Sefu had made a bridge out of wreckage and rope. The way Brigid had made a family out of three strangers and a feral child around a fire in a dead shantytown. The way Casper had made a new self out of the ruins of the old one, right there in the dark with the avalanche rumbling, handing the detonator to a twelve-year-old because he believed the Gate should decide its own gate.

"That's not a surname," the clerk said, but weakly, already reaching for the regulation book.

"It's better than one," Ivo said. "A surname tells people where you're *from*. Mine says where I *keep*." He tapped the page. "Put it in. The province wants a keeper. There's the keeper. All of him, spelled out."

* * *

It took an hour. Of course it took an hour. The clerk consulted his regulation book twice, sent a runner once, and at one point disappeared into a back room for so long that Brigid started humming a blasting cadence under her breath in a way that made the junior clerks nervous. But the provisions existed — the clerk found them, half-relieved, as though the book itself had taken Ivo's side — a clause about persons of established local standing whose parentage was unrecorded, permitting registration under a descriptive appellation of the applicant's own devising, witnessed.

Witnessed. That was the word that mattered. Two witnesses of good standing, signatures beside the applicant's, swearing the name was true.

Casper signed first. He wrote *Casper Amberdale* in a firm engineer's hand, and then, after a pause Ivo watched him take, added beneath it, unasked and unofficial, in smaller letters: *proudly.* The clerk pretended not to see it. The clerk was learning.

Sefu came up the stairs for his turn, refusing Casper's arm with a look that ended the discussion before it began, and signed *Sefu Okonkwo-Bell* with the careful precision of a man who remembered every measurement he'd ever taken and would not misplace a single letter either. Then Brigid, who signed *Bríd Ní Riain* and then crossed out the accent marks because, she said, "the provincial pen nibs can't handle the fada, poor dears," and signed again.

Four names on one line. Well — three names and one description that was truer than any name.

When it was done, the clerk blotted the page, blew on it, and turned the ledger around so Ivo could see the entry rendered official: *Ivo of the Rivergate. Keeper, Rivergate Corridor. Appointed by order of the Provincial Engineer's Office.* Below it, the date. Below that, the four signatures like fence posts holding up a claim.

"There," the clerk said, and for the first time he smiled, and it transformed his face from a filing cabinet into something almost human. "You know, in thirty-one years at this desk, that's the first time anyone's asked me for a blank space instead of complaining about one." He closed the book gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping child. "Congratulations, Keeper."

Ivo slid off the too-tall chair. His feet hit the floorboards and the floor held him, solid, ordinary, and he found he was grinning and couldn't stop.

Outside, the afternoon light lay gold on Greywater's crooked street. The tide was coming in — he could feel it in his teeth, in the pressure behind his ears, the old clockwork of the water turning over — and somewhere beyond the cliffs the Rivergate was swallowing the sea by inches, patient as it had always been, waiting for its keeper to come home.

Marta was waiting on the inn steps with bread and a face she was failing to keep neutral. Sefu lowered himself onto the step beside her. Brigid came out last, squinting at the light, and clapped Ivo on the shoulder hard enough to stagger him, which was how she hugged.

"Well, *Keeper of the Rivergate*," she said, savoring it. "First official act?"

Ivo considered. There would be papers to carry back to the mountain, and the inspectors wanted a joint walk-through of the lower galleries, and Casper had a meeting about the charter survey that would run late into the evening, and there was a hundred small machinery-pieces of the future all demanding oiling at once.

But there was one thing first. One thing that belonged to him alone to do, and he had known it since the pen left his hand.

"Ledger," he said. "New page."

They looked at him, all of them, and understanding moved through the group like warmth through shared blankets. The keeper's ledger — the real one, the old one, the one whose final entry ended mid-sentence in his father's— in *the* keeper's hand thirty years ago — was back in the keeper's station where it had always lived, waiting on its shelf between the lamp oil and the tide tables.

"Tonight," Casper said quietly. "We'll go up together. First entry's yours."

Ivo nodded. Then he stopped. Because down the street, past Marta's sign and the fish racks and the church with its leaning spire, a figure was coming up from the harbor road — travel-worn, dust-grey, walking with the deliberate pace of someone who had come a very long way and rehearsed the journey's ending many times.

A woman. Older. Casper's coloring, Casper's stubborn set of shoulders, a face Ivo had only ever seen in a creased photograph tucked inside a map of closed lines.

She stopped at the foot of the inn steps. She looked at Casper for a long, long moment, and the whole street seemed to lean in.

"Casper," she said. "I got a telegram. I came as fast as the trains allowed." Her voice shook on the last word. "Tell me it's true. Tell me about Edmund."

And Ivo felt the ledger waiting, and the blank page waiting, and understood that some entries you make with ink, and some you make by opening a door.
