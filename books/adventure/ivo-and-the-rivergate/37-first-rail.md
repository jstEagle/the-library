## 37. First Rail

The thaw came to the Rivergate the way forgiveness comes — slowly, unevenly, and all at once in the end.

For weeks the mountain had shrugged off winter in stages: first the plateau, where the snow pulled back from the ruins of the old shantytown like a sheet drawn from a sleeping face; then the high galleries of the gate, where meltwater began its ancient work again, threading down the carved walls and pooling in the gauge-notches until they gleamed like silver inlay; and last of all the shore itself, where Greywater's harbor ice broke apart one March morning with a sound Casper heard from his window at Marta's inn — a long groan, then a crack, then nothing but open water breathing against the stones.

He had been awake when it happened. He was awake most nights now, though not from worry. It was something closer to anticipation, an ache he carried under his ribs like a second pulse. In four days, if the weather held and the timber arrived on the morning boat, they would lay the first rail of the Rivergate line.

His father had never laid it.

Casper had thought about that sentence every day since the charter came through — turned it over the way a man turns a stone in his pocket, feeling for edges. Elias Amberdale had surveyed these lines, had cut the experimental gauge into the mountain's heart, had kept his ledger by lamplight in a cave that smelled of salt and iron, and had died in the dark of the lower gallery with his final entry unfinished. He had never driven a spike here. He had never seen a locomotive take the curve at the drowned gallery or heard a whistle come back off the walls multiplied into a choir. Everything he had built, he had built for a future that men in Harrow coats stole from him before it could arrive.

Now Casper stood to inherit that future, and the weight of it sat strangely on him. Not as guilt — Ivo had taught him, in his wordless way, that guilt was a burden the dead never asked to be carried. As responsibility, maybe. Or as inheritance, which is only responsibility wearing its Sunday clothes.

* * *

The morning the timber came, Brigid was on the dock before the boat finished warping in, arguing cheerfully with the harbor master about sling weights while Sefu checked each stick against the manifest from memory — he had read the order once, three months ago, and had not needed to look at it since.

"Forty-foot rail timbers, creosoted, sixty-two pieces," Sefu announced, tapping the seventh baulk. "This one's forty-one feet and green at the heart. Send it back."

The harbor master squinted at him. "You measured that by eye?"

"I measured it by looking," Sefu said, which settled the matter in the way his pronouncements always did — not with force, but with the quiet authority of a man who had spent thirty years learning what wood could be trusted with a man's life.

They were a crew now, whatever the provincial payrolls said. The registry listed them as employees of the Rivergate Charter Company: C. Amberdale, chief engineer; B. Doyle, blasting and works; S. Okonkwo, master carpenter; I. of the Rivergate, keeper of the passage. The clerk who entered the last title had hesitated, pen hovering, and Casper had watched Ivo watch him — the boy perfectly still, hands folded, waiting without apology for the world to catch up to what he already knew himself to be. The clerk wrote it down. The mountain had a keeper again, and this time the appointment was signed.

Casper came down to the dock as the first slings swung ashore, and Brigid spotted him and raised both arms over her head like a signalman giving the road clear.

"She's all here!" she shouted across the water. "Every stick, every spike, every pound of your precious experimental-gauge chair plates! And not one union speech made on government property!" She grinned. "Yet."

It still astonished him, some mornings, how much noise joy could make. A year ago he had been a dismissed engineer eating cold supper alone in a rented room, folding his father's map along creases worn soft as cloth. Now there were people shouting his name across a harbor because the timber had come in true.

* * *

They spent two days staging materials inside the gate before anyone spoke aloud about the spike.

It was Ivo who brought it up, in his fashion — which was to say sideways, by lamplight, in the keeper's station where the old ledgers still lived on their shelf. Casper had come down to check the drainage channels above the track bed, and found the boy sitting cross-legged on the cot with the final ledger open on his knees, the page where Elias Amberdale's handwriting stopped mid-sentence.

"You'll drive it," Ivo said. Not a question. A statement delivered with the flat certainty he reserved for things he had decided on someone else's behalf — tide times, wrong turns, the difference between a safe gallery and a drowning one.

"Drive what?"

"The first spike." Ivo looked up, and the lamp caught the scar along his jaw, the one he'd gotten years ago and never explained and never needed to. "At the portal end. Where the new line meets the old bed. Everyone knows it's supposed to be you. Even Vane's men knew it, before." He paused, choosing words the way he chose footholds. "Your father kept this place. He never got to finish it. So you finish it. That's how keeping works."

Casper sat down on the keg beside the cot. Down the passage, faint as a heartbeat, he could hear the meltwater running in its channels — the same water, he thought, that had run the day his father sat on this same cot and wrote himself toward a death the ledger couldn't record.

"There's a part of me," Casper said quietly, "that wanted to give it to you. You're the keeper. It should be yours."

"It is mine." Ivo closed the ledger with care, aligning its edges. "All of it. Every day. That's why you drive the spike. I keep the gate. You build the line. If I did everything, there'd be no line, and if you did everything, there'd be no gate." He slid off the cot, small and certain, and stood facing Casper with the lamp between them. "My father-figure—" he stopped, corrected himself with the precision he applied to signals, "—the last keeper used to say the river doesn't keep things. It carries them. Your father is carried now. What's left is the work. Do the work."

Casper laughed — a short, wet sound he hadn't planned — and put his hand briefly on the boy's shoulder. Ivo permitted it for exactly two seconds, which from Ivo was an embrace.

* * *

The day itself came in clear and cold-bright, the kind of spring morning that makes a man believe the year intends to keep its promises.

They laid the first length of track at the seaward portal, on the rebuilt approach where the sabotage collapse had been cleared through the winter — Brigid's charges had opened it again in January, cut to her own signature pattern deliberately, so that any man who studied the rubble would know the Harrow blast and hers were different animals entirely. Now the portal stood framed in fresh timber, and beyond it the old approach bed ran straight and graded toward the light.

Half of Greywater came to watch. Marta arrived with a basket of rolls and a thermos of coffee strong enough to strip paint, and planted herself on a rock with the air of a woman attending a coronation. Old Tamsin, who had told Casper about the tide that would show what wanted showing, came wrapped in two shawls and stood closest of anyone to the cliff face, as if the mountain were an old friend she'd quarreled with and missed. Even a few of Vane's former crew appeared at the edge of the crowd — the junior surveyor, Wren, among them, hat in hand, watching with the careful neutrality of a man testing whether he was welcome. Casper caught his eye and nodded once. Wren nodded back. Some accounts took longer to settle than others, but the interest was accruing in everyone's favor now.

Sefu directed the setting of the first tie with the tenderness other men reserved for infants. Brigid supervised the ballast tamping and narrated it loudly enough for the crowd, explaining to a ring of fishermen's children why gravel was laid in layers, "like making a very large, very serious cake." Ivo stood apart on the ledge above the portal, at the old keeper's lookout, holding the lit lantern — the rusted one, Elias's initials still legible beneath a century of corrosion and a winter of Ivo's polishing. He had cleaned it until the brass shone through. He held it now the way a lighthouse holds its flame: steadily, and for other people.

And then there was nothing left to do but the thing itself.

Casper knelt at the joint where the first rail met the prepared bed. The rail was new steel, rolled to the experimental gauge his father had designed — the gauge no company on record had ever used, the gauge that had started as ambition and become evidence and become, now, simply the way the line was built. He set the spike in its hole with his fingers. He took up the hammer.

It was a plain thing, the hammer. Forty ounces of steel on a hickory handle, bought from the Greywater general store for two dollars ten. But his hands remembered another hammer — the one in the photograph his mother had sent, the one in his father's grip outside a trestle camp twenty-six years ago, a man grinning at the camera with his sleeves rolled up, certain the future lay ahead of him in straight lines and honest steel. His mother's letter had come with the photograph, back in Greywater's first autumn: *Your father believed rails were promises. Drive them well.*

Casper drew breath. The crowd had gone quiet — even Brigid, even the children. Above him, Ivo raised the lantern higher, and its light fell full on the spike head.

He swung.

The first blow rang out and went up the cliff face and into the cave behind it, and the mountain answered — a long, braided echo rolling through the galleries, gathering harmonics off the carved walls, returning to them doubled and doubled again until it seemed the whole range had taken up the stroke. The second blow set the spike. The third seated it home. On the fourth, Casper rested his palm flat on the rail head, and the steel was warm from the sun and vibrating faintly with the memory of being struck, and he understood — finally, fully, in the place where grief had lived so long it had grown its own furniture — that this was what his father had wanted. Not vindication. Not his name restored in some office ledger. This: a rail laid true, a promise driven into the earth, a line that would carry people safely through the mountain for a hundred years after everyone standing on this bed today was gone.

Grief didn't leave him. He had learned better than to expect that. But it changed state, the way ice changes to water — the same substance, suddenly moving, suddenly able to flow somewhere useful. He knelt there a moment longer with his hand on the rail, and let it flow.

Marta cried openly. Tamsin said something to the cliff in her low voice, too quiet to catch, and touched the stone with two fingers. Brigid blew her nose on her sleeve and pretended she hadn't. Sefu put a hand on Casper's shoulder, heavy as a tie-plate, and said only, "Good seat. He'd have approved of your swing."

Ivo climbed down from the lookout and walked the length of the first rail, end to end, inspecting it with the gravity of a bishop consecrating ground. At the far end he stopped, turned, and gave Casper the signal — two short waves of the lantern, arm crisp, meaning unmistakable to anyone who had learned his language:

*Line clear. Proceed.*

* * *

They worked the rest of the day and half the next, and the line grew into the mountain a rail-length at a time.

It was strange and wonderful work, stranger than anything Casper had done in fifteen years of engineering. The gauge was his father's, but the methods were theirs — Sefu's timber cribs fitted to the cave's irregular floor, Brigid's controlled shots easing the tight curve at the second gallery, Ivo calling out drainage hazards from memory of every flood season he had survived alone. The cave was becoming a railway, and the railway was becoming part of the cave, and neither surrendered to the other. Where the drowned gallery flooded at high spring tides, they built the bed up on Sefu's arched cribbing, high enough that the water passed beneath like a dark river under a bridge — "bridge over nothing," Sefu called it, and grinned every time, because he had named the technique himself and no one could take that from him.

On the third evening, Casper walked the completed trackage alone, from portal to the deep gate and back, pacing it out at surveyor's stride. Two miles and a quarter of finished line inside a mountain that had swallowed his father. Lamplighters — Brigid's design, little reflector shields to protect the bats — glowed along the walls at intervals. The meltwater sang in its channels. Somewhere ahead, the deep gate stood open, as Ivo's charge had left it, its shattered seal now framed in commemorative timber with a small brass plate: *Opened by decision of the Keeper, in the year of the great snow.*

Casper stopped at the plate and read it twice. Then he took out his notebook — the same battered one that had survived the gorge crossing, water-stained, half its pages warped — and wrote beneath his last survey entry:

*Track complete through the gate. Gauge true. Bed solid. My father's line will carry its first train in six days.*

He underlined the last three words. Six days. The provincial inspector had confirmed the date by telegram that morning, and the engine — a small mountain-class locomotive borrowed from the coastal division, painted fresh coal-black with the Rivergate charter number on her flank — was already aboard a barge, rounding the headland.

A first train. Through the gate. His chest tightened around the thought the way a fist closes around a spike.

That night, back at the keeper's station, Ivo was oiling the lantern when Casper told him. The boy went still — that total stillness, the one he'd learned from caves, where movement meant danger and stillness meant listening. Then he set down the oil rag, crossed to the ledger shelf, and took down the brand-new book they had ordered from the mainland: blank pages, cloth binding, the words RIVERGATE — KEEPER'S LEDGER embossed in gold on the spine.

He opened it to the first page and smoothed it flat with both palms.

"When she comes through," Ivo said, "I'll stand at the post. Lamp lit. Like a keeper should." He looked up, and in the lamplight his eyes were bright and absolutely unafraid. "But you'll ride in the cab. First engineer through the gate. That's how it has to be."

Casper started to object — he wasn't an engine driver, he was a surveyor, the honor belonged to—

"That's how it has to be," Ivo repeated, in the voice that ended arguments, and turned back to his ledger, and uncapped his pen, and began to write the entry that would wait, ink drying in the lamplight, for the sound of a whistle coming home through the mountain.

Down the passage, faint and patient, the meltwater ran on toward the sea — carrying everything, keeping nothing, ready for whatever came next.
