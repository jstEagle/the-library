## 38. The Train Comes Through

The lamp had to be trimmed just so.

Ivo had learned that from the ledger — from the last keeper's careful hand, which wrote *trim wick to a thumb's width, no more; a keeper's light is not for seeing by but for being seen by* — and he had practiced it every evening since the snows went out of the mountains and the river ran loud again with meltwater. A thumb's width. No more. The flame stood steady in its glass house, throwing a warm circle on the stone shelf where the ledgers lived, and beyond that circle the great gallery opened into dark the way it always had: patient, enormous, listening.

Tonight the gallery was going to be full of whistle.

Ivo could feel it coming the way he felt tides coming, in his back teeth and the soles of his feet, a pressure building somewhere past the seaward arch where the new rails ran down through the cut Brigid's charges had opened two seasons ago. The rails themselves were Sefu's pride — laid true to the experimental gauge, the gauge nobody owned until now, each joint checked twice against the memory in his head because he trusted his memory more than any chainman's tape. Casper had driven the first spike in spring, and Ivo had held the rail steady while he swung, and the sound of iron biting home had gone through the whole mountain like a word spoken in a language the stone understood.

But rails were one thing. Rails were quiet. Rails were promise.

A train was a train.

He climbed to the keeper's post with the lamp swinging from its hook at his hip, up the worn steps the last keeper had cut into the gallery wall, and settled himself on the ledge where generations of keepers had sat before him — though how many generations, the ledgers only guessed, the earliest pages written in ink gone brown as old blood. From here he could see the length of the middle gallery, the one he had guarded alone through all those years when the gate was a secret and he was its only voice. He knew this gallery better than he knew his own hands. He knew where the water hung from the ceiling in stone fingers. He knew which echo came back flat and which came back singing. He knew the exact spot, forty paces down and left of the post, where he had once hidden a stolen biscuit from gulls and found it months later gone to dust, and how foolish and enormous his loneliness had felt even then, at eight, at nine, at ten, guarding a door no one wanted open.

Now they wanted it open. Now there was a charter with the province's seal on it, framed in Greywater at Marta's inn where everyone who passed could read it, and the corridor had a name again — the Rivergate Line — and men came with mules and timber and shouted cheerfully in places where shouting had been forbidden by nothing but silence for thirty years.

Ivo still flinched at some of the shouting. He was working on it.

Below, far off toward the sea-arch, a red signal lamp winked three times. Brigid's lamp. Three meant *clear and holding*. She was stationed at the arch mouth with her watch out and her jaw set, because Brigid did not trust tunnels on principle and said so loudly, and because someone with experience of things that blew up ought to stand nearest the thing most likely to make noise. Sefu was with her, perched on a timber stack with his bad leg stretched out, having lost the argument about whether he should climb to the upper galleries at all. Casper was walking the line itself, Ivo knew without looking, doing a last inspection with his hand on the rail the way other men touched a horse's neck — feeling for flaws, for lies in the metal. Casper touched everything like that. It was how you could tell he had been raised by bridges.

And down the valley, past the cut, past the trestles Sefu had designed and the grades Casper had walked a hundred times, the train was coming.

Ivo heard it before anyone on the crew could have — a tremor in the rock, faint as a heartbeat under a pillow. He had spent his whole life reading this mountain's body. He knew the shiver of tide against the sea-arch, the groan of frost leaving the high galleries, the small dry tick of stone settling after rain. This was none of those. This was rhythm. This was weight on steel, arriving in beats, growing.

His chest did something complicated. He pressed his palm flat against the ledge, against stone that had been his whole world, and waited.

* * *

The whistle came at last as the train entered the cut, and it went through the cave like weather.

It was not a sound so much as a change in the air itself — a long, rising cry that filled the lower gallery wall to wall, that poured up the vent shafts and rolled through the upper passages where Ivo had once played echo-games with nobody, that came back off the far walls changed and multiplied until it seemed a dozen trains were crying at once, a whole railway of ghosts answering their living brother. Dust sifted golden through the lamplight. Water trembled in the stone fingers overhead. Somewhere below, Ivo knew, Brigid was swearing with admiration and Sefu was laughing, and Casper had stopped walking and taken off his hat.

Ivo stood up on the keeper's ledge.

The light grew in the seaward arch — a single eye at first, then a blaze, the firebox's glow painting the arch stones orange, and then the engine itself came through, huge and black and breathing steam, its cowcatcher parting the shadows like a plow parting earth. It moved slow — Casper had ordered walking pace through the gate, no exceptions, ever — but slow for a train was still faster than anything this cave had ever held. The ground sang. The rails sang. The mountain, which had swallowed a locomotive's worth of secrets and kept them thirty years, now held one in the open light and did not object.

Ivo lifted the lamp.

That was the keeper's work. That was the whole of it, tonight: stand at the post, lamp lit, trimmed to a thumb's width, and show the way through. His arm shook and he let it shake. The flame wavered and steadied. Down on the footplate the driver saw him — Ivo watched the man's face turn, watch the man lift his own hand, small and formal, the way crews saluted stationmasters and bridge tenders and all the quiet officials of the iron road — and something broke loose in Ivo's chest that he would not have a name for until much later, writing about it by lamplight, and even then he would only call it *the door opening from the inside.*

The engine passed beneath him. He felt its heat rise and wash over the ledge, smelled coal smoke and hot iron where there had only ever been salt and wet stone, and then the cars came through one behind another, windows lit, faces at the glass — engineers from the provincial office, workmen, a woman with a camera on a tripod who pressed her palm to the window as she passed, as if greeting the mountain itself. The gallery that had known only Ivo's footsteps and the drip of water now rang with the rolling thunder of a train, and the echoes that came back were not lonely ones.

He waved the lamp through. Car after car after car. His arm never tired, or if it tired he never noticed, because he was busy memorizing everything — the exact note of the wheels on the experimental gauge, the way the firelight strobed across the ceiling, the moment the caboose's red lamp swung past the arch and the great sound began to fold itself away into the inner distance, diminishing, softening, going on ahead into the mountain toward the plateau portal where more people waited to see it come out the other side.

Through. All the way through. Forty miles of collapsed line bypassed by a passage the companies had sworn did not exist, carrying a train the province had sworn would never run, on a gauge drawn by a dead man whose name was about to be spoken in halls where names get carved into things.

Ivo sat back down on the ledge, quite suddenly, because his knees had opinions.

* * *

They came up to him afterward, of course. They always came up to him afterward, and he had stopped pretending to mind.

Casper arrived first, hat in hand, coal dust on his cheek, and stood at the bottom of the keeper's steps looking up the way he always did — as if the ledge were a pulpit and Ivo's presence on it a sermon he hadn't earned. "Well?" he said. "Keeper. Report."

"Gate held," Ivo said. "Walls held. Echoes held." He paused, savoring it. "One stone finger dripped on the second car. The woman laughed."

"A full report," Casper said gravely. "The office will be pleased."

Brigid came clanking up behind him, still in her inspection harness, grinning like a lit fuse. "Did you hear it? In the upper galleries? I stood at the arch and I felt it in my fillings, and I thought, well, there's a sound worth getting blown up for, and then I remembered I'm the one does the blowing." She thumped the stone. "This old girl sang."

"She did," Ivo said. "She remembers trains. Her bones remember. The gauge fits her like a hand fits a glove."

Sefu came last, leaning on his stick, and did not say anything at all for a while, which from Sefu was the highest praise there was. He looked down the line where the tail-lamp had vanished, and then up at the ceiling, and then at the rails, and Ivo watched him checking joints with his eyes alone, out of habit, finding them true, and finally nodding once, the way a man nods at a finished thing.

"Your father drew this gauge," Sefu said to Casper, quietly. "But it took all of us to give it a train."

"It took one more than that," Casper said, and turned, and looked up at Ivo on his ledge with the lamp burning beside him, and did not finish the sentence, because he didn't need to. That was the thing about the crew — the family, the word Brigid used and Sefu pretended to find sentimental and Casper defended with statistics about loyalty — they had gotten very good, all of them, at saying the ends of sentences with their eyes.

But Ivo said it anyway, because tonight was a night for saying things.

"The gate kept me," he said. "Now I keep it open. That's the trade. That's the whole job."

* * *

Long after the others had gone down to Greywater for the celebration Marta had threatened to hold whether anyone attended or not, Ivo stayed at the post.

This was not avoidance, exactly. Or it was, a little. But mostly it was that a keeper stays at his post on the night of the first passage, because the ledger says so — *the keeper witnesses*, the old hand had written, in an entry Ivo had copied out three times just to feel the words move through his fingers — and because some part of him needed to sit alone in the gallery now that alone was a choice instead of a sentence, and learn what it felt like.

It felt different. That was the discovery. Loneliness in a room you cannot leave is a wall. Loneliness in a room you can leave is just quiet, and quiet, it turned out, was where Ivo did his best thinking.

He opened the ledger — the old one, the brown-paged one, the last keeper's book — and read the final entry again, though he knew it by heart, though Casper had read it aloud in the dark to armed men and broken them with it. *If I do not return, the gate must stay open.* Below it, in a newer hand, Casper's hand: the testimony, the date, the names cleared. And below that, in Ivo's own careful letters, learned from the keeper's copybook: *gate open. train passed. all safe.*

Three lines. Thirty years between the first and the last.

He trimmed the lamp to a thumb's width and listened to the mountain settle around the memory of the train, the way a house settles around the memory of laughter. Far off, muffled by miles of stone, he could hear the celebration starting in Greywater — a fiddle, maybe, or just the wind in the cut. It didn't matter which. Sound carried here. Everything carried here. That was the whole nature of the gate: things went in one side and came out the other, changed by the passing, and the keeper's job was to make sure nothing got lost on the way.

Down at the seaward arch, the tide was turning. He could feel it in his feet. Tomorrow the low water would show the cave mouth again, the way it had shown it to a stranger with his father's map, all those months and one whole life ago. Tomorrow there would be survey parties and supply wagons and the woman with the camera wanting the light at dawn. Tomorrow the gate would do its work in daylight, honestly, on a charter with a seal.

But tonight the lamp burned at a thumb's width, and the gallery was quiet, and Ivo of the Rivergate sat at his post with the old ledger open on his knees, and began, slowly, to write the next entry — pausing once, pen lifted, to listen to the river running strong and free through the mountain's heart, carrying everything, keeping nothing.

The first word he set down was *home.*
