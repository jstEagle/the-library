## 39. Low Tide Again

The tide tables had promised it for a month, and the tide tables had never once lied to him. Casper Amberdale had learned that much in the year since he'd first spread a paper chart across a table in Marta's inn and traced the moon's arithmetic down to a single morning in late autumn — the morning the sea would pull back its skirt and show the bones of the coast.

He woke before dawn in the keeper's cottage above Greywater, dressed in the dark without striking a lamp, and stood a while at the window watching the last stars burn off over the water. The cottage had been the old tide-watcher's once, back when the village had bothered to watch for anything more dangerous than weather. It had a crooked chimney and a door that swelled every wet season and a view of the shore road that Casper had come to think of as his own the way a man comes to think of his own hands — not because he'd chosen them, but because they were what he'd been given to work with.

On the table by the door, where he'd left them the night before, lay the flowers.

They weren't much to look at. Sea thrift, mostly, the pink-headed cushions that grew out of the cliff grass where nothing else bothered to live, and a few late asters Brigid had pressed on him at breakfast the previous evening with a gruffness that fooled no one. *For the old man,* she'd said, and then had turned back to her tea as if she hadn't said anything at all. Brigid did that. She gave things and then pretended she hadn't, the way a dynamite expert might light a fuse and walk away before the boom, so as not to have to watch what she'd caused.

Casper wrapped the stems in a scrap of oilcloth and went out into the blue hour.

* * *

The village was not awake, exactly, but it was never fully asleep either. Greywater kept the hours of the sea, and the sea was already turning over in its bed. Smoke stood up straight from a dozen chimneys. Somewhere a dog gave one considered bark and thought better of it. The fish-gutting shed down by the slip was lit, and through its fogged window Casper could see old Tam Yelland's silhouette bent over his knife, working by lamplight the way he'd worked by lamplight for fifty years and would work by lamplight until the lamp or the man gave out.

Tam looked up when Casper passed. He didn't wave. He never waved. But he lifted his chin a half-inch, and Casper lifted his back, and that was the whole of the conversation, and it was enough. A year ago, the village had watched him the way gulls watch a man carrying fish — with calculation, with appetite, with the patience of creatures who have learned that everything washes ashore eventually. Now they watched him the way they watched the lighthouse. Not with warmth, exactly. With reliance. It was a Greywater kind of love, and Casper had stopped needing anything more from it.

The shore road ran out along the cliff top and then gave up, as roads did here, and became a path, and the path became a suggestion, and the suggestion became the kind of scrambling descent that Casper could now make in the dark without thinking about it, which would have horrified the surveyor he'd been two years ago. That man — the one who'd refused to sign a rotten trestle and lost everything for it — that man had believed that a life was a thing you built on paper. Certifications. Signatures. The clean geometry of a line drawn true. He had believed that if you were right, the world would eventually have to admit it.

The world had never admitted it. That was the thing the wilderness had taught him, and it did not teach it gently. The mountain had not apologized for the viaduct. The sea had not apologized for the keeper it had drowned, or for the men in Harrow coats it had let walk away. The wilderness forgave nothing. It didn't hold grudges either — that was the strange comfort of it. It simply went on being what it was, indifferent to every human ledger brought against it, and if you wanted forgiveness you had to get it from the only creatures capable of giving it, which was to say, from each other.

Casper's boots found the last of the scree and then the wet sand, and the shore opened before him, and he stopped, because it always stopped him, even now.

* * *

At low water ordinary, this stretch of coast was a gray crescent of shingle and weed, unremarkable, the sort of beach a surveyor's eye would pass over in a single line of notation. But at extreme low tide — at the tide the old woman had once told him would run *low enough to show what wants showing* — the sea withdrew nearly a quarter mile, and the world underneath the world came up gasping into the air: ledges of black rock furred with kelp, pools like spilled ink holding their own private skies, and there, at the base of the cliffs where no cliff base should be reachable, the mouth of the cavern the villagers had called the Tollgate for longer than any of them could say why.

It was open now. It was always open now. That had been the decision that had remade everything — Ivo's decision, made with a detonator in his small scarred hands and the whole buried weight of the mountain groaning above them — *the river doesn't keep things, it carries things* — and the blast had roared and the passage had held, barely, and the government men had come down through the opened gate like water finding a channel, and the world had changed shape in a single week.

But even a year on, with the new line graded and the first rail laid and the gate chartered and lit, the cave mouth at low tide still looked to Casper less like an engineering achievement than like a wound that had been allowed, at last, to heal open. The masons had dressed the entrance in cut granite and set the iron arch over it, and the arch bore the new charter's stamp, and there was a proper road down to it now for the maintenance crews. But the sea still owned the approach twice a day, and the rock around the dressed stone still wore its ten thousand years of barnacle and storm, and the two truths — the ancient one and the new one — sat together without reconciling, the way Casper had learned to sit with his own.

He walked out across the wet sand with the flowers in his hand and the tide's breath all around him, that vast slow exhalation of the retreating water, and he did not hurry. There was no need to hurry anymore. That was the strangest inheritance of the year: time, which had once run through him like current through a wire, now moved at the pace of the tide itself. The permits had expired. The race was over. He had lost the race, in the strict legal sense — the concession had lapsed a full day before the crew had stood at the deep gate with Vane's rifles behind them — and it had turned out that losing was the only way anyone had won.

His father had understood that, Casper thought. Or had begun to understand it, at the end. *If I do not return, the gate must stay open.* Not: *avenge me.* Not: *clear my name.* The gate must stay open. The old keeper had spent his last months in the dark learning what his son had needed a whole year and a mountain to learn — that the keeping of a thing was never the same as the owning of it.

* * *

The flowers went at the base of the iron arch, on the seaward side, where the dressed granite met the wild rock. Casper knelt on the wet shingle and worked the sea thrift into a crevice with his fingers, packing sand around the roots the way Brigid had shown him — *they're cliff flowers, they want to be treated rough* — and set the asters beside them in a jar half-sunk into the sand, because Brigid had insisted flowers needed water even flowers that grew out of rock, and Casper had learned not to argue with Brigid about the care of living things.

He stayed kneeling a while.

He had never found the body. He had found everything else — the ledger, the lantern with its stamped initials, the testimony, the names, the whole buried architecture of the truth — but the mountain had kept the man himself, somewhere in the lower galleries that the new line's engineers had voted to leave sealed, out of respect and out of sense. There was a marker now, though. A small brass plate inside the gate, at the keeper's station, with a name and two dates and a line from the last ledger page, and Ivo had polished it every Sunday for a year without anyone asking him to, and had once told Casper, in his flat signal-code way, that a keeper keeps the names as carefully as the lamps.

*I'm here,* Casper said. He didn't say it aloud. He had tried, the first few times, in the early months, standing here with the wind taking his words apart, and it had felt like falsifying an inspection — saying a thing that was true in substance and false in form. What he meant was not *I'm here* but *I stayed.* What he meant was *I didn't seal it. I didn't take the job they offered me in the city, the one with the office and the title and the quiet. I didn't go back to being an Amberdale, whatever that ever was. I'm the keeper of your gate, and the gate is open, and the first train came through in the spring, and Ivo stood at your post with your lamp and waved it through, and I think — I think that's the report you wanted filed.*

The tide pools caught the first real light. The sun was coming up over the headland the way it did this time of year, late and low and gold, and the whole exposed floor of the sea lit up like the inside of a struck bell. Casper had seen a great many things in his life that other men had called beautiful — mountain vistas from the cab of a locomotive, the blue glare of a glacier, the geometry of a bridge rising over water — and he had appreciated them the way an engineer appreciates, professionally, with one eye always on the load. But this — this wet plain of rock and light, with the gate standing open at its heart and a fistful of pink thrift blooming against black stone — this he appreciated with both eyes, and no notes taken, and nothing to be filed.

*You were right,* he said, in the silence where no words were. *Not about the survey. About the gate.*

* * *

He was still kneeling there when the footsteps came up behind him on the shingle — light, quick, deliberate, the tread of someone who had learned to walk loudly enough to be heard and quietly enough to be safe, both at once, because the cave had taught him both. Casper didn't turn. There was only one person in the world who walked like that, and he'd have known it in his sleep.

Ivo came and stood beside him, hands in the pockets of the coat that had been Sefu's and was too big and would be Ivo's for years yet, the way good coats and good names were passed down in this crew. The boy looked at the flowers. He looked at the arch. He looked at the light coming up the exposed seabed, and his face did the thing it did when he was reading a signal — total attention, total silence, the whole body become an ear.

"Lowest tide till next year," Ivo said. "Tam checked the tables twice."

"I know. I checked them three times."

"Brigid says you check things four times when you're nervous."

"Brigid talks too much for a woman who communicates primarily in explosions."

Ivo made the sound that served him for a laugh, a short exhale through the nose, and sat down on the shingle beside Casper with the unselfconscious ease of a boy who had been sitting on cold rock since before he could remember why anyone wouldn't. They watched the water together. Far out, the sea was already beginning to think about coming back — a long low line of white where the swell found the first of the hidden ledges.

"I brought the ledger," Ivo said, after a while.

Casper turned. The boy had a oilcloth bundle under one arm, and inside it, wrapped with the care Ivo gave to lamps and names, was the new ledger — the one with the fresh pages, the one Ivo had begun a year ago in his own careful hand, the first entry reading *names of the crew* and then all four of them, and *the date,* and then two words: *gate open.*

"You brought it to the beach?"

"It wanted bringing." Ivo said things like that, in that flat declarative way, and a year of practice had taught Casper not to ask what he meant but to wait, because the meaning always arrived, the way the tide always arrived, on its own schedule and fully. "The old keeper wrote his last entry down there." Ivo nodded toward the cave mouth, toward the dark beyond the iron arch where the lamplight of the keeper's station burned always now, a permanent flame by charter and by choice. "Mid-sentence. In the dark. Nobody ever finished it for him." He looked at Casper. "Somebody should finish it. On the page. Where the tide can't get at it."

Casper felt something move in his chest, slow and enormous, like the groundswell out beyond the ledges.

"What did you want to write?"

Ivo opened the ledger across his knees. The pages were good paper, bought in the city by Sefu on one of his supply runs, and the handwriting was Ivo's own — the letters he'd taught himself from the old ledgers and the signal manuals, upright and exact, each one set down like a rail spike driven true. He turned to the newest page, and there, already written, was the date. And beneath the date, in that same exact hand, the boy had written:

*One year. Gate open. Tide low. Keeper Casper walked out to the mouth and put flowers on the stone for the old keeper, his father. The flowers are sea thrift because they grow where nothing else will. Brigid grew them. She said not to tell him that.*

Casper read it twice. The second time, the words went slightly blurred, and he let them.

"You wrote *keeper Casper,*" he said, when he could trust his voice.

"You signed the keeper's book. That's what it says in the book." Ivo shrugged, the coat shrugging with him. "The book doesn't lie. That's the whole point of the book. That's what the old keeper — " He stopped. Started again, in the way he had, rebuilding the sentence from sounder timber. "That's what he kept it for. So that what happened would be written down by someone who saw it, in their own hand, and nobody could come along later and say it went another way." He looked out at the sea. "Vane said it went another way. For thirty years, everybody said it went another way. The book says how it went."

The tide had turned. Casper could feel it before he could see it — a change in the air, a gathering, the sea drawing its breath — and out past the ledges the white line was coming on, patient and total, the way all the true things came: not fast, not negotiable, but arriving.

"I never thanked you," Casper said.

"For what?"

"For the flowers growing where nothing else will. That's — " He stopped. He was a surveyor; he believed in saying the line exactly. "A year ago I came out here with a contract and a dead man's map and nothing else. No name worth having. No trade that would have me. And the gate took me in, and the gate was you. So. Thank you."

Ivo considered this with the gravity he gave to everything, and then he reached out and took the pencil from the ledger's spine and held it out to Casper, handle first, the way you hand a man a tool you trust him to use.

"Then write it in the book," Ivo said. "That's what it's for. So it's true even after the tide comes back."

Casper took the pencil. The sea came on across the exposed seabed, filling the pools, finding the ledges, erasing the morning's whole revealed world without malice and without mercy, exactly as it had erased every world before it and would erase every world after. The flowers stood in their crevice above the waterline, rough-treated and blooming. The lamp in the keeper's station burned on behind the iron arch. And Casper Amberdale — no, not Amberdale, not anymore, only the keeper, only Casper of the Rivergate, the name he had chosen the way Ivo had chosen his — bent over the open page in the rising light and began to write.

Behind them, up the cliff path, small and clear on the morning air, came the sound of the new line's first whistle of the day — the morning train, coming down through the mountain, carrying its handful of passengers through the galleries Ivo had once guarded alone. Ivo stood up to wave the lamp, because the keeper always waved the lamp. And Casper kept writing, and the tide kept coming, and the gate stood open.
