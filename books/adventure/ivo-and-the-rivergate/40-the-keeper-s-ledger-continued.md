## 40. The Keeper's Ledger, Continued

The lamp on the keeper's desk had a new wick. Ivo had cut it himself that morning from a strip of cotton shirt, the way the old keeper had taught him before he was the old keeper, back when he was only the man who came and went and always returned, until the day he didn't. *Trim it close,* he'd said, his big fingers folding Ivo's small ones around the scissors. *A wick trimmed true burns steady. A wick left long smokes and gutters and lies about how much light there is.* Ivo had thought then that everything a keeper said was about lamps. He knew better now. Almost nothing a keeper said was about lamps.

He sat at the desk with the ledger open in front of him, and outside the gate the river ran loud with spring.

It had been a year — nearly to the day, though Ivo did not count days the way Casper did, in columns and tallies and small anxious arithmetic. Ivo counted them the way the tide counted them: by what came in and what went out and what was carried past the stone. And by that counting, this was the season when the water pulled farthest back from the cliffs and showed the cave mouth like an open hand. The season when a man with a leather satchel and his father's map had walked up out of Greywater against every warning the village could give him, and stood at the Tollgate with his hat in his hands like a man arriving at a church he wasn't sure would have him.

Ivo dipped the pen.

His hand had changed over the year. It was still small — he was still small, though Brigid swore he'd grown two inches since the snowline and Sefu swore she was measuring him wrong out of hope — but the hand itself had steadied. A year ago it had known how to hold a snare line, a signal lantern, a fish hook, a knife for cutting kindling. Now it knew how to hold a pen, and the knowing had come slowly, the way all of Casper's lessons came slowly, without impatience, as if knowledge were a load that could be shifted onto a boy's shoulders one plank at a time until one day he looked down and found himself carrying a bridge.

Above the fresh page, the old entries went back and back. He did not read them tonight. He knew them the way he knew the galleries — not word by word but shape by shape, the way the handwriting grew cramped in winter and loose in summer, the way the ink changed color when the keeper switched bottles, the way the final entry stopped mid-sentence with the pen pressed so hard into the paper it had torn through, and below that torn place nothing at all except the blankness Ivo had once believed would last forever.

*If I do not return, the gate must stay open.*

Casper had read those words aloud in the dark with rifles pointed at them, and the words had done what no bullet could do. They had walked down the barrels of four guns and come out the other side as men lowering their arms. Ivo had watched it happen. He had understood, in the way of a boy who has learned everything important by watching, that paper could be a kind of light, and that a keeper's truest work was not trimming wicks at all. It was keeping the writing safe until someone came who needed to read it.

Now it was his turn to write.

He wrote the date first, because the keeper had always written the date first, and because Casper had drilled him gently all winter on the calendar until Ivo could find any day on it the way he could find any gallery in the mountain — by its relationship to the things around it. Then he sat for a long moment with the pen lifted, listening.

The river was doing its spring voice, deep and full, carrying snowmelt down from heights that were already browning green. Through the stone he could hear the other sounds layered under it: the tick of the lamp, the settle of timber somewhere in the upper works where the construction crews had been shoring the old galleries, and — faint, from the direction of the portal — voices. Marta's inn had emptied half of Greywater up the cliff path today. There was always someone coming now. That was the strangest weather of all, this human weather, this constant arrival. For nine years the gate had been a secret kept by one boy and one dead man. Now it was a thoroughfare, and Ivo had discovered that he did not mind. He had expected to mind. He had braced himself for the feeling of being overrun, the way the lower galleries flooded, and instead what he felt most evenings was something he had no rail-signal name for: the sound of people choosing to be here, going home along a passage he kept.

He put the pen down and wrote the first name.

*Casper Amberdale.*

No — he stopped, pen hovering, and looked at the name a long time. Then he crossed it out. Not struck through, not erased; he drew a single careful line through it, the way a surveyor marks a correction, honest about the change rather than ashamed of it. Below it he wrote:

*Casper of the Rivergate.*

Because that was what the registry had made official last month, in a hall that smelled of stove heat and ink, when the clerk had asked him — asked *him*, Ivo, standing witness — whether the engineer wished to be recorded under his former name or the name of his appointment, and Casper had looked at Ivo first, before answering, as if the question belonged to both of them. *Keeper of the Rivergate corridor,* Casper had said. *And my name goes with the post.* The clerk had blinked. Brigid had gripped Sefu's arm hard enough to bruise. And Ivo had felt something move through his chest that was too large to be pride and too quiet to be joy — the feeling, he decided later, of watching a bridge take its first load and hold.

They had refused their names together, in a way. That was the family they had become: people who had each set down a name the world had handed them and picked up one they had built themselves.

He wrote the rest.

*Brigid O'Faolain, powder chief.* She had argued for *demolitionist*, said it sounded more respectable, and Casper had said that nothing about her was respectable and that was the entire point, and she had laughed the laugh that could be heard three galleries away. In the end the charter said *powder chief* because Ivo had suggested it and nobody remembered afterward whose idea anything was anymore, which was, Ivo had come to understand, what a crew was: a machine for forgetting who deserved credit.

*Sefu Mensah, master carpenter.* His leg was better this spring. Not good — it would never be good, the mountain had taken its toll and never gave change — but the new span across the gorge had steps built into it at exactly the intervals Sefu had measured with his own stride, so that crossing it, he said, was the first walk in eleven years that didn't argue with him. He was teaching Ivo joinery on Sundays. Ivo's hands were good at it. Snare-work and joinery turned out to be the same knowledge wearing different clothes: know where the strain falls, and build so the strain is carried by the whole, never by one point.

*Marta Calloway, provisioner, Greywater.* She had refused any title grander than that, and had cried anyway when the charter named her, and had pretended she hadn't, and had fed everyone until they begged her to stop. Her telegram lay framed now above the bar of the inn, behind glass, the little strip of paper that had moved a government. Ivo had seen it. It was very small. Most enormous things were, he was learning. His father's confession had fit inside a boy's silence for nine years. The truth had fit inside a ledger page.

He paused again, pen dripping a small dark bead onto the blotter, and wrote the fourth name, and this one took the longest, not because the letters were hard but because he made himself write them slowly, the whole name, every part of it, out loud in his head:

*Ivo of the Rivergate, keeper.*

There. In his own hand. On the line where names go.

Brigid had been the one who started it, a year ago on the plateau, around a fire in a ruined shantytown, when she'd asked him his surname the way she asked everyone everything — directly, warmly, like a crow after something shiny. *I don't have one,* he'd said. *The keeper called me the river's boy.* And Casper had offered his own name, freely, the way you offer a coat in cold weather, and Ivo had refused it — not because he didn't want to belong to Casper, but because belonging to Casper was not the same as being himself, and even at twelve he had known the difference the way he knew the difference between the river and the sea: connected, always connected, but each running its own course. *I'm not an Amberdale,* he'd told him. *I'm the Gate.* And Casper had nodded, slow and serious, the way he nodded at a reading on an instrument that confirmed what he'd hoped and feared, and that nod had been the second-best gift anyone ever gave Ivo.

The best gift was harder to write. It was this: that no one had ever tried to give him a name again. They let him grow into his own the way you let a tree stand. Even the officials at the registry, even the provincial engineer himself, had waited while a twelve-year-old boy bent over the form and printed, letter by careful letter, the name he had made, and then stamped it official, and that was that. Identity, Casper said, was authored, not assigned. Ivo had looked the word up. *Author.* One who causes something to exist. He liked that the word did not say the thing had to be made well, or finished, or even true yet. Only caused. Begun. A beginning was enough to make you the author of it.

He looked at the four names on the page. Four lines of wet ink catching lamplight. It seemed too few. It was not too few — the ledger was not a census, the keeper had written once, it is a record of trust — but his pen wanted more, so he added beneath them, smaller:

*And all who pass, carried.*

That was the old formula. Every keeper's entry for sixty years had ended with some version of it, and Ivo had found the variations in the earliest volumes, yellowed and brittle: *all who pass, counted; all who pass, remembered; all who pass, kept.* The last keeper had shortened it. Ivo understood why now. *Kept* sounded like possession, and the river had taught him better. The river doesn't keep things. It carries them. You could hold nothing in moving water — not a boat, not a body, not a grief, not a name — you could only help it along its course and trust the course.

He blew gently on the page until the ink dulled to dryness, then closed the ledger, and went out to see the evening in.

* * *

The portal at low tide was the best place in the world, and Ivo had earned the right to say so, having surveyed, in his own informal way, every place within three days' walk.

He came down the switchback path as the sun went copper behind the range, and the sea was performing its great annual withdrawal, dragging back from the cliffs in long silver sheets, exposing the barnacled teeth of rock and, between them, the cavern mouth — the Tollgate, the Gate, the door under the world — standing open and dripping, tall enough now to drive a locomotive through, which, in ten weeks, they intended to do.

Work lights burned inside. The new rails ran back into the dark on the experimental gauge, his father's gauge, the gauge no company on record had ever used because no company on record had ever been honest enough to admit the line existed. Casper had fought the province for a year over that gauge. Everyone sensible had told him to standardize, to save money, to bury the anomaly with everything else that had been buried. Casper had stood in the overheated hall in Greywater with his father's inspection notes in his hand and said, in the flat voice Ivo had learned meant the opposite of calm, that the gauge was the one thing about the Rivergate that had never lied, and that they would build to it or he would build it alone. The province, worn down by testimony and telegrams and the sheer geological stubbornness of the man, had yielded. The first rails had gone down in April. Casper had driven the first spike — his father's spike, saved from the viaduct wreckage, straightened by Sefu's hands — and Ivo had held the rail steady and felt the blow come up through the steel like a heartbeat.

Voices echoed out of the mouth now. Sefu's laugh, deep as a cistern. Brigid giving somebody hell about a miscounted crate. And under it, threading through, the whistle-note of the little construction engine they kept inland, testing brakes, a sound Ivo had hated once and now heard the way you hear a friend calling from down the road.

He stood at the threshold and let the tide-air move over him — salt and stone and creosote and, faintly, flowers.

Flowers. Someone had left flowers at the cave mouth again. A twist of paper holding gorse and sea-pink, tucked into a crack in the rock at the height of a grown man's hand. Ivo knew whose they were without looking for the sign. Once a year, at extreme low tide, Casper walked down alone to this exact spot and stood for a long time saying nothing, and left flowers for a father who had come into this cave thirty years ago with a lantern and a conscience and had never walked out. The wilderness forgave nothing — Casper said that too, another of his sayings that weren't about what they were about — but people did. People forgave, and planted rails in the forgiveness, and laid flowers on the stone, and came back the next year to do it again. That, Ivo had decided, was what keeping was. Not holding. Returning.

He touched the flowers once, lightly, the way you touch a rail to feel whether a train is coming. Then he went inside, down the passage, past the galleries he had guarded alone through nine winters, past the drowned gallery where Sefu's leg had nearly failed and Casper had carried him the last hundred yards with Ivo calling the turns by echo — strangers becoming a crew in waist-deep black water, one armful of carpenter at a time. Past the vent shaft they'd climbed out of with the first snow falling above. Past the place where the rope bridge had crossed nothing, built by a man with a bad leg out of pure defiance, because making something true was the only answer a craftsman could give to a landscape built on lies.

At the deep gate he stopped, as he always stopped.

The charge scars were still visible in the rock, fanned white like frost ferns, where Ivo's blast had opened the sealed gallery a year ago. He had set that charge himself. Vane had stood there with drawn men and a broken crew, and the tremors had been running through the mountain like a fever, and Casper — Casper, who wanted his father's proof more than he had ever wanted anything, who had lost his name and his trade and nearly his life for it — had handed the detonator to a boy. *You're the keeper,* he'd said. *Not me. Not yet. Maybe not ever. It's yours.* And Ivo had stood in the shaking dark with everyone's lives humming in the wires at his feet, and had thought about the river, and had said the sentence that had since been carved — Brigid's doing, over his mild objection — into a brass plate beside the scars:

*The river doesn't keep things. It carries them.*

Then he had thrown the switch, and the mountain had roared, and the passage had held, barely, dust and glory together, and the men who came down through the smoke a week later — inspectors with provincial seals, summoned by a barmaid's telegram and a boy's living testimony — had found the gate open and the truth sitting in it like a lamp.

Inside the deep gallery, the last keeper's station stood preserved now behind a rope, the cot made up, the tools hung, the ledgers in their case. Tourists came sometimes on summer Saturdays and looked at it all with their hats off, not quite knowing why. Ivo came every evening. He checked the lamp oil. He checked the rope. He read a page at random, keeping the dead man's handwriting alive in his own mouth. And he stood a moment at the case where the final volume lay open to the torn page, mid-sentence forever, and he finished the sentence silently, as he did every night, with whatever the day had given him. Tonight the finishing went like this: *if I do not return, the gate must stay open — and it has. Look. Look what came through.*

* * *

Full dark now, and the work crews gone down to Greywater, and the mountain to himself the way he liked it best: not empty, but quiet, inhabited by sound. Water in its courses. Timber settling. The lamp ticking. He climbed to the keeper's station in the upper gallery — his station now, the post with the view of the portal, where the signal lantern hung ready and the fresh ledger waited on the desk.

He lit the lamp. Trimmed wick, steady flame. Outside the watch-window the stars were out over the range, and far below, strung along the shore road, the lights of Greywater were coming on one by one, and beyond them the sea worked at its endless honest labor of arriving and withdrawing.

Ivo opened the ledger to the page he had written at dusk and read his own four names by lamplight, checking them the way Sefu checked a joint — for fit, for trueness, for the places strain might fall. Family, recorded. Not inherited; chosen, and chosen daily, the way you choose to keep a lamp lit. Somewhere down the coast Casper was walking home from the flower-stones, and Brigid was probably winning an argument at Marta's bar, and Sefu was asleep with his leg propped and his measure-lines drawn on tomorrow's timber, and every one of them would pass through this gate tomorrow and the day after, carried, and be glad of the carrying.

Ivo took up the pen and, beneath the names, added the last line of the entry. He wrote it slowly, pressing just hard enough — not so hard the paper tore, not so soft the words faded — a wick trimmed true:

*Gate open. All who pass, carried. — Ivo of the Rivergate, keeper.*

He blotted the page, closed the book, and set the lamp high in the window, where the light would reach the water.

Far out on the dark sea, right on schedule, the night boat from the capital was rounding the point with her riding lamp burning — the fortnight packet, bringing pay-chests and letters and, according to Marta's wire that morning, a provincial engineer's assistant with questions about extending the corridor east. New country. Unsurveyed ground. A gap on the map where something ought to be.

Ivo watched the light come on across the water, and smiled, and reached for the next blank page.
