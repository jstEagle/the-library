## 1. The Blacklist

The bell over the door of Halloway & Sons Outfitters had a cracked note in it, a flat half-tone that Rosalind had been meaning to fix for three years and never had. She liked it that way, if she was honest — a small imperfection in an otherwise carefully unremarkable life. Customers heard it and thought *old shop*, thought *honest*, and never once looked at the woman behind the counter long enough to wonder why she flinched when the wind came down off the Karst Range and set the bell swinging on its nail.

"Morning, Vera," said old Tam Pell, shuffling in with his usual parcel of excuses about his boots. "You got those resoled?"

"Done yesterday." She lifted them onto the counter, heels first, the way he liked. "Re-stitched the welt too. You've been walking on the outside edge again. Your left hip's worse."

Tam squinted at her over his spectacles. "Witchcraft, is what that is."

"Observation." She rang him up under the name she'd worn so long it sometimes felt like a coat grown into the skin: Vera Lask. It was a good name, plain and forgettable, the kind of name that belonged to a woman who resoled boots and ordered stock and went home to a rented room above a bakery that smelled of yeast until ten each night. Nobody named Vera Lask had ever led a client up a mountain. Nobody named Vera Lask had ever come down without one.

She watched Tam go, the bell giving its broken note behind him, and stood for a moment with her hands flat on the counter, feeling the morning settle.

The shop sat low in the valley town of Merrick's Ford, where the river came out of the mountains already tired, grey-green and heavy with glacial flour even in autumn. From the loading dock out back you could see the Karst Range on clear days — a wall of white and black stone forty miles north, its peaks like teeth broken at different heights, the glacier between them holding the light long after the valley had gone to shadow. Rosalind had not looked at it directly in six years. She was aware of it the way a man is aware of a scar: by feel, by weather, by the ache that arrived before storms.

She was aware of it now, though the sky was clean.

* * *

The radio behind the counter was tuned, as always, to the regional service, playing low enough that only the headlines surfaced through the clatter of the workshop. Rosalind was cutting leather for a rifle sling — a commission from a farmer who'd never hunt anything larger than a fox but wanted to look like a man who might — when the announcer's voice changed register, dropping into the careful flatness they used for bad news.

"—confirming this morning that contact has been lost with a six-member government survey team operating in the upper Karst drainage. The team, contracted through the Ministry of Lands and Water, was conducting boundary and resource assessments near the glacier source. A ministry spokesperson described the loss of contact as 'logistical' and said a search would be 'proportionate.' No further details were—"

Rosalind's knife stopped in the leather.

*Proportionate.* She knew that word. It was the word bureaucrats used when they wanted a thing small. Six people did not go missing in the Karst Range and get a *proportionate* response; six people went missing in the Karst Range and got helicopters, got every warden in two districts pulled off their beats, got the kind of mobilization that made the newspapers for a week. Unless someone in authority preferred them missing.

She set the knife down. Her hands were steady, which surprised her. Underneath the steadiness her pulse was doing something old and familiar, a drumbeat she recognized from the worst year of her life.

The announcer moved on to grain prices. The workshop noise resumed around her — the rasp of the belt sander, young Dov swearing at a jammed stitching machine — and Rosalind stood in the middle of it all, listening to the silence inside herself where the fear used to live, and finding that it wasn't silent after all. It had just been waiting.

*Upper Karst drainage. Near the glacier source.*

That was her country. Not the lowland trails she'd walked a thousand times since, not the tame foothills where tourists paid to feel adventurous for an afternoon — the real country, above the treeline, where the river ran cold enough to kill a swimmer in four minutes and the rock held no memory of footprints longer than a day. She had known it better than anyone alive. That had been the problem. That had always been the problem.

"Vera?" Dov had come to the doorway, wiping his hands. "You want lunch?"

"I'm not hungry."

He gave her the look he gave her whenever she went quiet like this — a boy's puzzled kindness, sixteen and unable to imagine that a woman who smelled of saddle soap and boot wax could have anything behind her eyes but inventory. "You okay?"

"Fine. Mind the counter till one."

She went out the back and stood on the loading dock with her arms folded against the cool air, and there it was: the Karst Range, white on black, patient as a creditor. Somewhere up in that whiteness were six people who had stopped answering their radio, and somewhere below them, in offices with good light and framed certificates, were men saying the word *proportionate* as though it were a prayer.

Rosalind Fairweather — a name she hadn't spoken aloud in six years, a name that lived now only in court records and redacted files and the memories of people who mostly wished they could forget it — turned her back on the mountains and went inside.

* * *

It took four days for the story to grow teeth.

On the second day the regional paper ran a piece on page seven: *Survey Team Overdue in Karst Range; Search Underway*. The photograph showed five faces and a caption apologizing for the sixth being unavailable. Rosalind read it twice in the bakery downstairs before opening, standing in the smell of warm bread with the paper trembling very slightly in her hands, and told herself the trembling was the cold.

They were young. That was what struck her. Three of the five faces were barely more than children — graduate students, probably, or junior contract staff, the kind of eager, competent kids the Ministry burned through by the dozen, sending them into country they didn't understand with instruments they didn't know how to trust. One of the photographs was a girl with a wide smile and a smudge of dirt across her nose, the kind of girl who wore her competence like a badge and her optimism like armor. *Juniper Hale, 24, of Merrick's Ford,* said the caption.

Merrick's Ford. Local girl. Rosalind stared at the face until the bread smell turned cloying and the baker called up the stairs to ask if she wanted her usual, and she found she couldn't answer.

On the third day, the search was scaled back. Weather, the Ministry said. Approaching calving season, they said. Standard seasonal protocol. On the fourth day, a man in a good coat came into Halloway & Sons and asked, politely, whether they stocked expedition-grade rope, and Rosalind sold him static line and asked no questions, and hated herself for asking no questions, because the man's boots were wrong — too clean, too new, city boots — and his accent belonged to the capital, and men like him did not buy rope to climb.

That night she climbed the stairs to her room above the bakery and took down the tin box from the top of the wardrobe.

She hadn't opened it in over a year. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth, lay the things she'd kept of the person she used to be: a brass compass with her initials scratched into the case — R.F., letters she'd once traced with her thumbnail on cold mornings until they felt like a talisman and then, later, like an accusation. A folded topographic map of the upper Karst, soft as cloth from handling, annotated in her own hand with notes no printed map carried: *rockfall zone, unstable since '09. Serac field — do NOT cross after noon. Cave mouth below the east moraine, dry in autumn, floods in spring.* And beneath the map, a newspaper clipping gone brown at the folds, and beneath that, the letter.

She didn't take out the letter. She never took out the letter. She took out the map instead and spread it on the narrow bed under the lamp, and her fingers found the glacier source the way a tongue finds a broken tooth.

Six years ago she had come down out of that country with a dead client on a sledge and a story nobody believed, and the machinery of reputation — which is to say, the machinery of money and influence, which is to say, the machinery of a handful of men whose names she had learned only afterward, in fragments, in the margins of hearings she was permitted to attend but not address — had ground her flat. *Negligence. Poor judgment. Route deviation.* The words had been repeated until they wore grooves, until even she sometimes woke at night wondering if they were true, though she had stood on that slope, she had read that snowpack, she had made the call any guide with a spine would have made, and the mountain had simply done what mountains do. The client's family had needed someone to blame. The family had friends. The friends had power. And Rosalind Fairweather had become a cautionary tale told in guide certification courses across two countries, a name spoken with a shake of the head, banned from the range by administrative order, erased so thoroughly that within eighteen months the outfitter who hired "Vera Lask" had no idea he'd employed the most notorious mountain guide of the decade.

She had built a life in the shape of that erasure. Small, quiet, useful. Boots resoled. Ropes coiled. Advice given freely to hunters and fishermen who never suspected that the plain woman behind the counter had once read avalanche terrain the way priests read scripture.

And now six government surveyors had vanished in her country, near the source, in the season when the ice begins to move, and the men in the good coats were calling the search *proportionate*, and a local girl with dirt on her nose was somewhere in the white silence with her radio dead.

Rosalind sat on the bed with the map across her knees until the lamp burned low and the bakery below went dark, and she did not sleep.

* * *

On the fifth day, Tam Pell came in for his boots a second time, which was how she knew something was wrong, because Tam Pell had never in three years collected anything early.

"They're saying it's weather," he said, not looking at her, looking at the rack of fishing rods as though it required his opinion. "Scaled the search back. My nephew's a dispatcher — he says the wardens got pulled off it Tuesday. Told to file and forget."

"File and forget," Rosalind repeated.

"That's what he said. Word for word." Tam turned, and his old eyes were wet-rimmed and furious. "Juniper Hale is my granddaughter's friend. Grew up two doors down. That girl taught my Ruthie to swim in the mill pond, and now some ministry man says the weather's turning and we should let the mountain keep her." His voice cracked on the last word and he mastered it, jaw working. "Somebody ought to go up. Somebody who knows that country. But everybody who knows that country—" He stopped. He looked at her then, really looked, and Rosalind saw the thought move behind his eyes like a fish under ice, the thought that everyone in town had quietly had at one time or another without ever saying it aloud: *there's something about Vera Lask. There's something about the way she reads the sky.*

"Everybody who knows that country what?" Rosalind said quietly.

"Nothing." Tam shook his head, paid for nothing, took his boots, and left, and the bell gave its broken note, and Rosalind stood alone in the shop with her heart going like a trapped bird.

Because she knew what he'd almost said. Everybody who knows that country is either bought or banned. And the one person in this valley who knew the upper Karst better than the wardens, better than the Ministry's own surveyors, better than anyone living — that person was a ghost wearing another woman's name, with everything to lose and nothing, officially, to give.

*Proportionate,* she thought. Six lives, weighed against somebody's convenience, and found to be worth a file number and a shrug.

She worked the afternoon with her hands on autopilot — stitched, cut, priced, swept — while something long dormant turned over in her chest and opened one eye. She caught herself reading the barometer twice an hour. She caught herself standing at the back window at dusk, watching the last light die on the glacier's high shoulder, measuring, the way she used to measure, the color of the ice, the direction of the wind, the mood of a country that forgave nothing and forgot less.

That night, closing up, she took the brass compass from the tin box and put it in her coat pocket. Just that. Just to feel its weight. She told herself it meant nothing, that she was only keeping it close the way a man keeps a stone from a beach he'll never see again.

The bell didn't ring, but the door rattled once in the wind off the north, and Rosalind stood in the dark shop, hand closed around the compass, listening to the mountains breathe from forty miles away.

Three days later, well past midnight, she would find a notebook on her doorstep — a surveyor's notebook, water-spotted, dog-eared, with a name inked inside the cover in a young, determined hand: *J. Hale.*

But that was still to come. For now there was only the weight in her pocket, the ache in the old scar, and the knowledge settling over her like the first snow of the season: that the past had finished circling, and had landed.
