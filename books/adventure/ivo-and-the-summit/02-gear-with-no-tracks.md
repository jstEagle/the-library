## 2. Gear With No Tracks

The helicopter set Ivo Voss down on the shelf of gravel above the survey camp at seven minutes past nine in the morning, and by nine-fifteen he had already counted four things that were wrong.

He did this the way he did everything — methodically, without hurry, kneeling first at the edge of the site and simply looking. Twenty years in the Karst Range had taught him that a camp told you its story in the first five minutes if you were willing to shut up and listen to it. Most people walked straight in and started touching things, and by doing so they wiped the story clean like a thumb across wet ink. Ivo stayed on the perimeter. He let the camp speak.

It was a government survey site, six tents arranged in the regulation crescent with the cook fly at the open end facing the river — textbook layout, the kind you'd find illustrated in the Field Operations Manual, page forty-one. The canvas was new. The guy-lines were taut and evenly tensioned, each one dressed with a tidy trucker's hitch. The tent pegs had been driven square and deep into ground that was half-frozen, which meant someone had taken their time about it, working each peg in with care rather than hammering them home in a rush before dark.

That was the first wrong thing. Nobody who expected to leave this camp in a hurry pitched it like a chapel.

He stood, brushed grit from his knee, and walked the perimeter slowly, his boots crunching on frost-stiffened scree. The morning was clear and hard-edged, the kind of cold that made the air feel like glass. Upvalley, the Karst Range stood in its usual silence — grey limestone walls streaked with snowmelt, and above them, hidden behind a shoulder of rock, the glacier that fed the river running silver-bright past the camp. The water was loud here. It was always loud. He'd have heard a gunshot from two kilometres off.

Wrong thing number two: the food.

The cook fly sheltered a folding table, and on the table sat breakfast. Porridge in pots, gone to concrete. A loaf of bread, sliced, three slices missing, the rest furred green. A tin of butter with the lid off. Six enamel mugs in a row, and when he lifted the nearest one and sniffed it, the ghost of tea still clung to the bottom — old tea, weeks old, but tea nonetheless. He turned the mug over. No dust inside. No insects. Nothing had gotten into the food. Nothing had disturbed it.

Six mugs. He checked the manifest in his breast pocket. Survey team of five: team leader Aldous Renk, geologist; two technicians, male; a field medic, female; and a junior cartographer, twenty-three years old, named Juniper Hale. Five people. Six mugs.

He set the sixth mug back exactly where it had been, handle angled the same way, and photographed it from three positions.

* * *

By eleven he had catalogued the camp in his field book — fourteen pages of it, small neat print, because Ivo Voss's field books were famous throughout the service for being small and neat and complete, and because he had learned long ago that the details nobody wanted were always the ones that mattered later.

The personal gear was all present. Sleeping bags rolled or unrolled according to no pattern he could find, but all there. Boots — eleven pairs, which was itself an arithmetic problem, since five people ought to own ten pairs between them unless someone had brought spares. Rucksacks stacked under the cook fly, weighted down with stones against wind, as if their owners had stepped out expecting to come back before the weather turned. A satellite phone sat in its charging cradle, battery dead, cable neatly coiled. A second phone, the team's emergency unit, lay in the leader's tent beneath a folded map — also dead, also undamaged.

No signs of struggle. No blood. No scavenger sign worth the name — a few fox tracks through the cook area, days old, uninterested. No note. No journal left behind except the leader's logbook, which ended mid-sentence on the eleventh day of the survey:

*Grid anomaly at the source confirmed. Returning tomorrow with full kit to —*

And nothing after that. The pen had been capped and laid across the page. Capped. Laid across the page, deliberately, like a man setting down his work for the night.

Ivo crouched over that logbook for a long time. Around him the river roared its one-note sermon, and the light moved slowly down the western wall of the valley, and he read the sentence perhaps thirty times.

*Returning tomorrow with full kit to —*

To what? To what?

He photographed every page. Then he sat back on his heels and looked at the camp again, the whole of it, and let the dread come, because it wanted to come and he had learned not to fight it. Dread was data. Dread was the body reading patterns faster than the mind could. His body was telling him something, and he made himself listen.

Here is what the camp said, if you listened: five — no, six — people had finished their breakfast on a morning some nineteen days ago. They had capped the pen, weighted the rucksacks, left the food out under the fly because they intended to return within hours. And then they had walked away from everything a human being needs to stay alive in the Karst Range — boots, bags, phones, food — and they had not come back.

And here is what the camp did not say, which was worse: there were no footprints leading out.

* * *

He spent the afternoon on the ground, literally on it, crawling the perimeter on hands and knees with a hand lens, the way he'd been taught by a ranger so old she remembered the range before the road. The soil around the camp was a mixture of glacial silt and gravel — fine-grained near the riverbank, coarser upslope — and after rain or melt it held prints the way wax holds a seal. There had been no rain in nineteen days, but there had been melt, every afternoon, brief and bright.

Inside the perimeter: prints everywhere, boot prints overlapping boot prints, the ordinary churn of a lived-in site. He could distinguish at least five different sole patterns, maybe six. One set was small — women's size, narrow heel, a distinctive diagonal wear bar across the ball of the foot. He measured it, photographed it, drew it in his book. Juniper Hale, he thought. The junior cartographer. Twenty-three years old. Somebody's granddaughter, probably. Somebody's whole world, certainly.

Outside the perimeter: nothing.

Not few prints. Not faded prints. Not prints obscured by wind or meltwater. Nothing. The silt lay smooth as poured cream in a band two metres wide all around the camp, broken only by fox tracks and one set of chamois spoor high on the talus slope. It was as though six people had eaten breakfast, stood up, and evaporated.

There were only three explanations, and Ivo wrote them down in order of plausibility.

One: heavy snowfall had fallen after their departure and obliterated the trail. But the tents showed no snow-loading damage, the guy-lines bore no ice scars, and the porridge pots would have been filled with drift. Rejected.

Two: the team had walked out on bare rock — the talus fan to the east, where prints wouldn't hold. Possible. But the talus fan began forty metres from the nearest tent, across forty metres of perfect recording surface, and not one of those six pairs of boots had crossed it. Unless they had flown. Rejected, mostly.

Three: someone else had erased the trail.

He sat with that third explanation while the sun dropped behind the ridge and the temperature fell like a stone down a well. Someone had come into this camp — or been in it — and swept the ground clean. Not ploughed it, not trampled it. Swept it. He found the evidence at dusk, in the last usable light: along the eastern edge of the perimeter, in the lee of a boulder where a sweeping motion would naturally end, a faint arc in the silt. A single curved stroke, like the tail of a comma, where the broom — or branch, or boot dragged sideways — had lifted a hair too early.

Somebody had been thorough. Thorough people made mistakes only at the edges.

He marked the arc with a flagged stake from his pack, photographed it with scale bars, and then sat outside the leader's tent eating cold sausage and hard bread, watching the glacier shoulder glow faintly in the starlight, and thought about the sixth mug.

Five-person team. Six mugs. Eleven pairs of boots. A visitor, then — someone who ate with them, drank tea with them, someone familiar enough to sit down at the table. And then everyone vanished, and the ground forgot they had ever walked.

* * *

The flight back took two hours. He spent them writing his report in his head, and it was a good report, a report that asked questions instead of closing them. When he landed at the warden station at Upper Ferrow, the light was on in the duty office and Superintendent Brack was still at his desk, which was unusual enough that Ivo went straight up instead of waiting for morning.

Brack was sixty, thick through the shoulders, a man who had come up through enforcement and never quite lost the habit of looking at everyone as a suspect. He listened to Ivo for eleven minutes — Ivo timed it without meaning to — and then pushed his chair back from the desk with the particular scrape that meant the meeting was over.

"Tragic business," Brack said. "Exposure, most likely. They went up onto the glacier without proper gear, got caught in weather, wandered. Happens every year somewhere in these ranges."

"They left their gear," Ivo said. "All of it. Their boots, Superintendent. You don't wander a glacier in your socks."

"People panic. They do strange things."

"Not six people at once, all in the same direction, leaving their boots." Ivo kept his voice level. He had learned that from the mountains too: volume changed nothing up there; only patience changed things. "There's a swept perimeter. Someone cleaned the ground around that camp."

Brack's face did something small and closed, like a door latching. "Swept it."

"The silt holds prints. There aren't any. Except one arc where they missed."

"You've been crawling around on your knees in the dirt for two days, Voss, and you've found a mark in the mud." Brack gathered papers that didn't need gathering. "The ministry wants this file handled quietly. Families are grieving. There's no sense stirring up theories about — what did you say? Murder?" He said the word the way a man says a foreign word he doesn't trust himself to pronounce.

"I said someone erased the trail. I didn't say murder."

"What would you say, then?"

Ivo looked at him. Outside the office window the valley was black, and somewhere up in that blackness the glacier sat doing whatever glaciers do in the dark — growing, shifting, keeping its own records. "I'd say we don't know, sir. That's my professional finding. We don't know, and the site should be preserved until we do."

"The site," Brack said, "is being released tomorrow. Survey equipment gets inventoried and returned. The file closes as death by misadventure pending recovery of remains, and recovery efforts stand down at the end of the month when the weather turns." He met Ivo's eyes then, and for just a moment something moved behind his face — not guilt exactly, Ivo thought. Something more tired than guilt. Something more frightened. "You wrote a good report, Voss. I'm sure you did. You always do. File it."

* * *

He filed it. Of course he filed it. He was a warden; the service was the service; and a man who couldn't follow procedure had no business wearing the badge. He typed the report cleanly, attached his photographs, logged his field book, and signed the chain-of-custody form for the leader's logbook, which went into an evidence locker in the basement of the Upper Ferrow station and presumably into a drawer and presumably into oblivion.

But Ivo Voss had been raised by a mother who kept duplicates of everything, and he had inherited the habit like a crooked finger. Before he filed the originals, he copied the photographs to a memory card and put the card in the tin box where he kept the things that mattered — his father's compass, a photograph of a woman he no longer spoke to, a brass whistle that had once belonged to a client who hadn't come home either. And in the copy of his report, before submission, he made one small change that no one would ever notice and that he would never be able to explain to Brack: in the section listing recovered personal effects, he recorded the sole pattern of the small boot print in full forensic detail — size, wear bar, heel width — the kind of entry that existed for one purpose only.

So that someday, somewhere, if a boot matching that description turned up on a living person, he would know.

He drove home along the reservoir road in the dark, past the outfitter shops shuttered for the season, past the lowland towns where the guides who still had names were wintering. At a red light he caught himself thinking about her — Rosalind Fairweather, banned from these ranges eight years now, her name struck from every register, the best mountain guide he had ever shared a rope with and the last person he'd ever want to see again, and the only person alive whose eye he trusted more than his own.

He gripped the wheel and put the thought down the way you put down a load that's too heavy to carry and too useful to abandon.

Because here was the thing he hadn't written in any report, hadn't said to Brack, barely admitted to himself: the sweep marks in the silt had been made by someone who knew how ground behaved. Someone trained. Someone patient. And the camp itself — the textbook crescent, the dressed hitches, the pegs driven square into frozen ground — that camp had been pitched by someone who cared about craft, and there weren't many of those left in the Karst Range, and most of the ones there had ever been had one name, and the service had spent eight years pretending the name had never existed.

The light turned green. Ivo drove on, and behind him the mountains kept their silence, and in his breast pocket, against his heart, the field book rode home with its fourteen pages of questions that nobody upstairs wanted answered.
