## 3. An Old Debt

The rain came in off the lowlands that October like something with a grudge, and Rosalind Fairweather spent the morning of it mending tents she no longer cared about.

She worked in the back room of Halvorsen's Outfitting, under the one window that didn't leak, her hands moving through the old rituals — seam grip along a split ridge seam, a new shock-cord threaded through the pole channels of a dome tent some weekend family had dragged over scree until it screamed. The work was beneath her. Everyone in the valley knew it, though nobody said so to her face. The woman who had guided the Karst Range before most of them could name its peaks, now patching rental gear for tourists who thought altitude was a kind of weather.

She had gotten good at not caring. That was the trick of the last six years: you built a small life the way you built a snow cave, deliberately, low to the ground, with walls thick enough that the wind forgot you were there. *Maren Ostergaard*, said the name on her work roster and the lease on her rented room above the feed store. Maren Ostergaard mended tents. Maren Ostergaard drank one beer at the Kettle & Spoke on Fridays and left before the music started. Maren Ostergaard did not look up when men in government jackets came through asking questions, because looking up was how people learned your face.

But her hands still knew the mountains. That was the cruelty of it. Some nights she'd wake with her fingers curled around a phantom ice axe, her body remembering a traverse her mind had sworn off forever, and she would lie in the dark listening to the rain and feel the Karst Range out there to the north like a hand pressed flat against the back of her neck.

The bell over the shop door rang at half past two, and she knew before she turned who it was by the sound of the cane — that particular arrhythmic knock, wood then boot, boot then wood, like a man walking slightly out of time with himself.

"Shop's open," she called, not turning around. "Rentals are half price in the shoulder season."

"And the mending's free if you bring the mender a bottle of the good rye." Old Tam Vossen lowered himself onto the stool by the stove with the care of a man negotiating with his own skeleton. He was seventy-eight this winter, or maybe eighty; he'd stopped being precise about it years ago, the way he'd stopped being precise about most things except rope and weather. His face had gone the color and texture of old saddle leather, but his eyes were still quick, still pale grey, still missing nothing. "You're letting the tent seams go crooked, Maren. A man can tell a lot about a woman by her seams."

"My seams are fine, Tam."

"Your seams are crooked because your head's been north all week." He set the promised bottle on the counter between them, unopened, deliberate as a chess move. "Everyone's talking about it. The survey team. You've heard."

Rosalind threaded the needle through canvas and pulled it tight before she answered. "I mend tents, Tam. I don't follow the news."

"Six people," Tam said, as if she hadn't spoken. "Government hydrological survey, out of the capital. Went up the Vesper River three weeks ago to map the meltwater at the source glacier. Supposed to be down nine days ago." He leaned forward, and the stove light caught the wet shine in his eyes. "Radio silence since the fourteenth. They're calling it a delay. Weather window, they said on the wireless. But it's been eleven days, girl, and no weather in the Karst holds eleven days unless it wants to."

The needle slipped. Rosalind looked down at the bead of blood welling on her thumb and watched it with a strange detachment, as though it belonged to someone else, someone whose hands were allowed to shake.

"They'll send a search party," she said.

"They sent a warden. One warden, to have a look at the lower camp." Tam's laugh was dry as old rope. "One. For six souls in the highest range in the country. When your father ran parties up there, we'd have mustered thirty men and a helicopter inside a week."

"Your father's been dead twenty years, Tam."

"Aye, and the mountains have gone to strangers since." He shifted on the stool, and she heard the pain in it, the deep grinding ache of a body that had carried too many loads down too many scree slopes. "There's a girl on that team. Junior surveyor. Nineteen years old."

Rosalind kept her eyes on the tent. "I'm sorry to hear it."

"She's my granddaughter, Rosalind."

The name landed in the quiet room like a dropped carabiner. Not *Maren*. Not the false name she wore like a shell. Her own name, in Tam Vossen's cracked voice, after six years of careful mutual pretending — because of course he had always known, they had all known, the whole river valley had known and kept its mouth shut out of some mixture of loyalty and pity that she had never decided whether to be grateful for.

She set down the needle. "Don't."

"Juniper," Tam went on, relentless now, a man past caring about the fall. "Juniper Vossen. My son's girl. Clever as a corvid, stubborn as her grandfather. She wrote me in August — she had the posting, she was so proud, first real fieldwork, mapping the source glacier." His breath caught, wheezed, steadied. "She asked me about the approach up the Vesper gorge. I told her what I told every green kid: trust the river, respect the ice, and if you ever get the chance, ask Rosalind Fairweather to check your knots."

"Tam—"

"I couldn't tell her why I said that instead of just saying *ask Rosalind*." His voice cracked clean through. "I couldn't tell her the best guide this valley ever produced is banned from the very ground she'd be standing on. Banned for a death she didn't cause, hounded out by men who needed someone to blame, and I sat here in my chair and let it happen because I was tired, Rosalind. I was old and tired and I kept my head down same as everyone."

Rosalind stood. Her legs felt distant from her, like borrowed equipment. "You don't know what happened up there. Nobody knows what happened up there."

"I know you." Tam gripped the edge of the counter and hauled himself half upright, and for a moment the dying man vanished and the old expedition leader stood in his place, the one who had taught her to read a cornice from a hundred meters, who had trusted her at twenty-two with clients' lives when older guides wouldn't. "I've watched you tie off a load in a sixty-knot gust. I've seen you turn a party back at the moraine when every other guide in the valley would have pushed for the summit and the fee. You do not leave people on a mountain, girl. It's the one thing about you I have never once doubted."

"The blacklist stands. If I go up that river, I'm arrested on sight. Every warden in the district has my description."

"Then don't get described."

"It's not—" She pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. Behind them, unbidden, the mountain rose: the white throat of the source glacier, the wind singing off the seracs, and underneath it, always, the other memory — the one with blood in it, the one that had ended everything. "Tam, listen to me. Six years ago I walked out of that range carrying a dead client on my back and a lie in my mouth, and I have paid for both every day since. If I go back, I lose the only thing I have left, which is being no one. And for what? A search I'm not qualified to join anymore? I haven't been above the treeline in six years. My gear's sold. My maps are burned. I mend tents, Tam. That's what I am now."

"That's what you're hiding as." He sank back onto the stool, and the strength went out of him all at once, like air out of a punctured raft, and she saw again how ill he was — the yellow tinge under the leather of his skin, the way his wrists stuck out of his sleeves. The doctors had given him till spring. Everyone knew. "I'm not asking you to save six people, Rosalind. Eleven days is eleven days; I know the arithmetic as well as you do. I'm asking you to find my Juniper. Find her, or find where she lies, and bring an old man the truth before he goes into the ground not knowing. That's all. That's the whole of it."

The stove ticked. Rain ran down the window in long silver threads. Somewhere out in that grey wash, north beyond the hills, the Karst Range stood under its first snows of the season, holding whatever it held.

"I can't," Rosalind said quietly.

Tam looked at her for a long moment. Then he nodded, slow, and took up his cane, and gathered his coat around his shoulders, and she understood that he had known before he walked in what her answer would be — had perhaps come anyway because a man owed it to his granddaughter to spend his last coin, even knowing the purse was empty.

"No," he said. "I suppose you can't. You never could do anything but exactly what survival required. That was always your gift." At the door he paused, one hand on the frame, and did not turn around. "Your father would be ashamed of neither of us, girl. That's the only comfort I've got left to give you."

The bell rang. The door closed. Rosalind stood alone in the back room with the unfinished tent and the untouched bottle of rye, and outside the rain went on falling, patient as grief.

* * *

She did not sleep that night.

She lay in her narrow bed above the feed store and listened to the gutters overflow and tried to make her mind lie still, and it would not. It kept producing images the way an injured man produces blood: Juniper Vossen at nineteen, writing to her grandfather about knot checks. Six tents — no, she'd have checked the manifest by now, she realized, she'd already done it without deciding to, six names, radio silence since the fourteenth, one warden sent to look. One warden. In her day the service had mustered companies. Either the government had grown cheap, or it had grown indifferent, or —

Or it didn't want a large search, said a voice in her head that sounded unpleasantly like her own, six years younger, standing in a warden's office watching a report get filed and forgotten. Or somebody preferred that the Karst keep its secrets.

She got up at three and dug the old topographic sheets out of the false bottom of her trunk — she had told Tam she'd burned them, and it had even been nearly true; these were copies, and she had never quite managed the fire. She spread them on the floor by candlelight and put her finger on the Vesper River and followed it up, up, past the gorge, past the moraines, to the blue-white fist of the source glacier, and her finger stopped there and would not move, and neither would she, for a long time, kneeling on cold floorboards in the dark like a woman at prayer to a god she hated.

At dawn she made herself coffee and told herself the day would be ordinary. It almost believed her.

Until she opened the door to go down to the shop, and looked at the mat.

The package sat square in the center of it, wrapped in waxed canvas and tied with climbing cord — tied with a bowline on a bight, finished with a fisherman's backup, the exact knot Tam Vossen had taught her forty years ago on a sunlit moraine when she was small enough that the rope reached her waist. There was no note on the outside. No footprints in the mud of the landing, which meant it had been left in the night, in the rain, by someone careful, or someone who knew she would look.

Her heart was already pounding as she crouched and untied it — her fingers remembering the cordage, hating themselves for remembering — and folded back the waxed canvas.

A notebook. Small, waterproof stock, spiral-bound, the cover scuffed and mud-stained. On the flyleaf, in a round, urgent, nineteen-year-old hand:

*J. Vossen — Hydro Survey '24 — Property of the Crown (but mostly mine).*

And tucked inside the front cover, a single sheet torn from the same book, written in the same hand but faster, the letters leaning like trees in wind:

*Mrs. Ostergaard — Grandda says you'll know what this is. Grandda says you're the only one who reads country the way I need it read. I wasn't supposed to write any of this down but I did, because somebody has to, and if I'm wrong about why our radios stopped working then tear it up and forgive me. But if I'm right — please. Please come and find us.*

*We found something at the source that isn't on any map.*

Rosalind read it twice. Three times. The rain had stopped; the light coming up over the eastern hills was thin and colourless, and it fell across the page and made the pencil lines seem carved rather than written.

Then she turned to the entries themselves, and the world narrowed to the size of a notebook page.

Dates in September. Elevations logged with a surveyor's precision. Sketches — good ones, better than good, the confident linework of someone who saw terrain the way Rosalind once had — of the upper Vesper gorge, the terminal moraine, the blue ice of the source glacier. And there, on a page dated the eleventh, a drawing that stopped her breath: the glacier's flank rendered in careful cross-section, and beneath the ice, hatched in hard pencil strokes, a shape no glacier ever grew.

Straight lines. Right angles. The unmistakable geometry of blasting scars, of tunnels, of workings.

Beneath it, in smaller writing, as if the hand had grown afraid of its own size:

*Ore samples. Fresh. Days old, not decades. Someone is mining under the source ice, and it isn't on any permit I can find. T. says leave it. R. says report it. We voted to finish the survey first. God, I hope that's right.*

The last dated entry was the thirteenth. After that, blank pages.

Rosalind knelt on her doorstep with the notebook open in her lap while the valley woke below her — smoke rising from chimneys, the shop bell ringing faintly down the hill, the ordinary machinery of the small life she had built so carefully, wall by wall, to keep out exactly this. Whoever had left it had known precisely which door. Had known the false name. Had known that the woman inside it was not Maren Ostergaard and had never been, and had gambled everything on the person underneath the shell.

They had gambled right. That was the terrible thing. She could feel it happening already, the guilt and the resolve shifting inside her like plates under strain, the small buried life cracking along seams she had sewn crooked on purpose.

She looked north, past the rooftops, to where the clouds broke briefly over the high country and showed her a single white summit, sharp as a broken tooth, before the weather closed over it again.

Eleven days. Maybe twelve. And somewhere under that ice, a nineteen-year-old girl who tied her knots the way her grandfather taught her, waiting for the only guide in the valley who would come.

Rosalind Fairweather closed the notebook, went inside, and began, quietly and without ceremony, to take down the small life she had built — starting with the false bottom of the trunk, and the maps she had sworn were burned.

By nightfall she would be on the river.
