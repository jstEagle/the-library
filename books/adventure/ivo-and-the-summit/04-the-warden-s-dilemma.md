## 4. The Warden's Dilemma

The order came down on a Tuesday, typed on district letterhead and signed by a man Ivo Voss had met exactly twice, both times at functions where the wine was better than the conversation.

*By authority of the Regional Directorate, the upper Karst drainage, including all tributaries above the confluence at Marker 14, is hereby declared closed to all public entry pending conclusion of administrative review. Post notices. Log compliance. File by Friday.*

Ivo read it three times at his desk in the warden station, a converted fire lookout with a woodstove, a wall of topographic maps, and a window that looked out on the lower shoulder of the Karst Range, where the peaks stood in their usual November haze like a jury that had already decided.

Closed pending review. It had a clean sound to it, bureaucratic and final, like a drawer sliding shut. But Ivo had spent eleven years learning what mountains did with bodies, and he knew that a closed file was not the same as a closed case. The survey team — six people, government contract, last radio contact nineteen days ago — was still up there somewhere, or the pieces of them were. And the order in his hands wasn't asking him to find them. It was asking him to stop looking.

He read the signature again. Deputy Director Halloran. A name that belonged to offices, not to altitude. Halloran had never in his career signed anything that required boots.

Ivo fed the woodstove another split of birch and pulled the incident file toward him across the desk. It was thin. That was the first thing wrong with it. Six missing persons should have generated an inch-thick folder by now: interview transcripts, flight plans, equipment manifests, family statements. What he had was a single-page mission summary, a list of names so abbreviated it might have been written on a napkin, and a satellite photograph so degraded it could have been of any white place on earth.

Six names. He'd memorized them the first night. Okafor, team lead. Brandt, geologist. Lindqvist, hydrologist. Mercer, technician. Two more whose surnames had been truncated in transmission — *Fenwick, J.* and *Osei, D.* — junior staff, presumably, the kind of people who carried the packs and asked the questions nobody senior wanted to ask.

*Fenwick, J.* Juniper Fenwick. He'd said the name aloud once, alone in the station, and the room had felt colder afterward.

He had walked the abandoned camp himself, nine days ago, before the order came. He could still see it when he closed his eyes, which he did too often lately. Three dome tents staked with the neatness of people who expected to come back. A tarp strung between two boulders over the cook area. A stove, cold. Food in the bear barrels — not scattered, not torn open, just *left*, sealed and stacked, enough for another six days in the field. A radio on its charging cradle with a full battery indicator glowing green, patient as a held breath.

And around it all, nothing. No tracks leaving. No drag marks. No blood. The ground had been soft from rain two days before the team's last check-in, soft enough to hold every boot print they'd made coming in, and those prints simply stopped at the edge of the kitchen tarp, as if six adults had stepped out of existence over breakfast.

His report had used the word *anomalous*. His supervisor's response had used the word *inconclusive*. Then the order came, and the word became *closed*, and Ivo understood that he was being handed a shovel and told to bury something while calling it gardening.

He should have posted the closure notices that afternoon. That was the job. That was the oath, even — protect the resource, enforce the regulations, keep people out of places that would kill them. Eleven years of doing exactly that, competently, without drama, and he had been good at it in the way that quiet men are good at things: thoroughly, invisibly, without ever once being asked what he thought.

Instead he sat at his desk until the light went blue in the window, and then he did the thing he would later understand was the first crack in the ice beneath him.

He wrote a records request.

Not for the current file — that was above his clearance anyway, and the current file was a fiction with a cover page. He requested the *historical* files. Search and rescue archives, Karst Range, all operations going back twenty years. He justified it in the request form with language carefully chosen to bore anyone who read it: *comparative terrain analysis for seasonal hazard assessment; historical incident patterns relevant to proposed closure signage placement.*

It was true, in the way a locked door is true. Nobody signs off on hazard assessments for a valley they intend to forget. If the Directorate genuinely believed the survey team had wandered off and died — the official theory, repeated in memos with the confidence of men reciting scripture — then the history of how people died in the Karst was exactly what a thorough warden would want.

If they believed anything else, the request itself would tell him. He stamped it, dated it, and put it in the courier pouch with his heart going harder than the paperwork warranted.

* * *

The files arrived eleven days later, in two gray archive boxes tied with cotton string, delivered by a supply flight that also brought diesel, mail, and a memo reminding him that the closure notices were overdue.

He waited until dark to open them. There was no reason to wait until dark. The station was his; no one reviewed how a warden spent his evenings. But some instinct older than his training told him that what he was about to read was best done alone, with the door barred and the stove hot, the way you handle something that might still be alive.

The boxes smelled of dust and basement. Inside, folders in chronological order, each one tagged with a case number and a color-coded tab — green for resolved, yellow for partial, red for unrecovered. The Karst Range had gone red more often than any drainage he'd ever worked. He'd known that abstractly. Everyone in the service knew the Karst ate people. What he hadn't known was how systematically the eating had been recorded, and then filed, and then forgotten.

He started at the beginning and read until his tea went cold.

A climber lost in a whiteout, twelve years back — recovered, green tab. A pair of prospectors who ignored warnings, eight years back — one body found at the moraine, one never, yellow. A scientific expedition, ten years ago, that had reported "unusual activity" near the source glacier and then requested an early extraction; the extraction log showed the aircraft turned back by weather, but the weather record for that date showed clear skies across the entire range. Red tab. The expedition leader's follow-up letters — there were three, increasingly sharp — were paper-clipped to the file and marked *no action required.*

Ivo sat with that phrase for a long time. *No action required.* Written in a hand that wasn't the expedition leader's, on a letter that was addressed not to the Directorate but to someone above it.

He kept reading. The pattern assembled itself slowly, the way a landscape assembles out of fog — not revealed all at once but accumulating, ridge by ridge, until suddenly you saw the whole shape of it and couldn't unsee it. Incidents in the high Karst clustered around the source glacier the way iron filings cluster around a magnet. Survey teams, water-quality testers, a seismic crew whose permit application existed in the file but whose completion report did not. Each time: an anomaly reported, an investigation opened, an investigation closed, a red tab, a silence.

And threaded through nearly every file, appearing and disappearing like a figure crossing a trail ahead of you — a guide's name.

He found her first in the prospector file. *Party guided by R. Fairweather, licensed, declined comment.* Then in the seismic crew file, in a witness statement: *Ms. Fairweather advised against the route. Her advice was overruled.* Then, five years back, the file that had a weight to it he could feel through the paper before he even opened it — the client death. The one everyone in the lowlands still told stories about, the one that had ended a famous guide's career with a single word that followed her like a brand: *negligent.*

The inquiry summary was four pages. Four pages for a death in technical terrain, with a client, in weather. Ivo had sat on review boards; he knew what a real inquiry produced. Real inquiries ran forty pages minimum. They interviewed every member of the party. They reconstructed the route hour by hour. They subpoenaed weather data and equipment records and the guide's planning documents.

This one had none of it. It had a narrative — brief, confident, damning — and a conclusion, and a signature block, and almost nothing else. The narrative described a fixed-line traverse above a cornice. It described the client falling. It described the guide's failure to protect.

What it did not describe was why the client had been on the traverse at all, against the documented advice of the guide — advice that appeared nowhere in the summary, though Ivo found it referenced in a separate statement, buried in an appendix, from a second client on the trip who had since left the country and could not be reached for confirmation.

And everywhere in those four pages, black bars.

Redaction in a search-and-rescue file was normal. Names of minors, medical details, informants — those got blacked out. But this was different. This was systematic. The guide's name was redacted in the header. Redacted in the narrative. Redacted in the conclusion, where the finding of negligence was assigned to a person whose identity the reader was apparently not permitted to know, though the finding itself was public knowledge, famous, ruinous. Everyone in the climbing world knew who had been blamed. The document simply refused, formally, officially, to say so.

Ivo laid the pages side by side under the desk lamp and understood, with the slow cold certainty of a man watching a cornice begin to creep, that the blacklist had not been a judgment. It had been a *product*. Manufactured. Assembled from a thin file, a suppressed statement, and a redaction heavy enough to hide whatever the original report had actually said.

Someone had needed Rosalind Fairweather out of the Karst Range. Not discredited — she'd have been discredited anyway, by any honest inquiry into a client death. Removed. Banned from the very terrain where, year after year, incident after incident, her name kept surfacing as the one voice advising caution, the one professional flagging anomalies, the one guide whose parties came back.

He thought of her then — really let himself think of her, which he had not permitted in a long time — standing in the gravel bar light two summers ago, or rather not standing anywhere, because he had made sure not to see her, had routed his patrols away from the outfitter town, had built his life into a shape with her absence pre-cut into it. She had never told him the details of the inquiry. She had only ever said, in the flat voice she used for things that cost her: *the file isn't what happened.* And he had believed her and done nothing, because believing her required believing that the service he served had lied, and he hadn't been ready to live in that country.

He was living in it now. The border had crossed him while he slept.

The stove ticked as it cooled. Outside, wind moved down from the Karst with the smell of snow in it, and somewhere up in that darkness were six people, or the truth of six people, and an order on his desk declaring the whole question closed.

Ivo looked at the closure notice, still unposted, still sitting square on the corner of his desk where he'd been unable to make himself touch it. Then he looked at the red-tabbed file, and at the black bar where her name should have been, and he took out a fresh sheet of paper and began, in the small precise handwriting of a man writing something he cannot take back, a private memorandum to himself: dates, case numbers, discrepancies, signatures. A map of the lie.

When he finished, near dawn, he added one line at the bottom, unsigned:

*They didn't close the valley because it's dangerous. They closed it because someone knows what's up there.*

Then he packed the red file deep in his rucksack, next to his warrant card, where he kept the things he'd die before surrendering — and began, methodically, to plan a patrol that would take him upriver, alone, past Marker 14, into country his own signature had forbidden him to enter.
