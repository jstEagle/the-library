## 5. Upstream

The river did not care that she was banned from it.

That was the first thought Rosalind Fairweather had as she slid the old wooden-framed packraft off the roof of her truck in the blue hour before dawn, and it was the thought she kept returning to as she worked — checking the lashings, testing the valve, weighing the dry bag with Juniper's notebook inside against the weight of everything else she was carrying. The Karst River ran out of the high country the way it had run for ten thousand years, indifferent to permits and blacklists and the signatures of men who had never stood on its banks. It would carry her or it would drown her, and either way it would not ask her name.

She'd told herself she wouldn't do this. She had said the words aloud, kneeling at Halvorsen's bedside three nights ago, his granddaughter's notebook a cold weight in her coat pocket: *I'm not going back up there, old man. You know what they'll do.* And he had looked at her with eyes gone yellow as old snow and said nothing at all, which was worse than any argument, because Halvorsen had never once in thirty years of friendship let silence stand where he could put a blade.

Then the notebook had appeared at her door. No note. No footprints in the frost that she could find, though she'd circled the building twice with a flashlight, feeling like a fool and a criminal both. Just the notebook, wrapped in oilcloth, set square in the center of her mat like an offering left for something dangerous.

She had read it through twice by lamplight, and by the second reading she had stopped pretending she wasn't going.

* * *

The water took her just as the light came up.

It came slowly to this valley, filtered through cloud the color of unpolished pewter, so that the mountains seemed to assemble themselves out of darkness piece by piece — first the black teeth of the lower ridgeline, then the pale shoulders of the middle peaks, and last, when the light finally climbed high enough, the Karst Range itself, white and enormous and impossibly far away, the glacier at its heart catching the first sun like a blade laid flat on a table.

Rosalind paddled with the current doing most of the work, letting the raft drift wide through the bends, her paddle dipping quiet as a heron's beak. The valley narrowed around her. Alder scrub gave way to spruce, spruce to bare grey stone streaked with the old high-water lines of spring floods. The air smelled of cold water and crushed mineral and, faintly, of snowmelt — a smell that went into her chest like a key turning in a lock she'd hoped was rusted shut.

Six years. Six years since she'd been on moving water above the lowland bridges. Six years since anyone had trusted her with a rope, a route, a life. She flexed her hands on the paddle shaft and found them steady, and was surprised by that, and then surprised by her surprise. Her body remembered what her reputation had been made to forget.

*You're a good guide, Rosa. That's what makes it unbearable.*

Halvorsen's voice again. He'd said it the day the inquiry report came down, standing in the doorway of her rented room with his hat in his hands like a man at a funeral he didn't believe in. She had been twenty-nine. The word *blacklisted* hadn't even been used yet — that came later, soft-spoken, in phone calls that ended quickly, in outfitters who suddenly had no trips, in the slow polite closing of every door between her and the high country.

*They need someone to blame, and you were the one who walked down.*

* * *

His name had been Daniel Ostrer. Thirty-four years old, a geologist with more money than sense and a smile that made junior guides roll their eyes. He'd booked the Karst source approach through Halvorsen's outfit at the very end of the season, when the ice was already talking to itself at night, and he'd insisted on the full traverse — the serac field, the ice caves, the standing stones at the headwall where the glacier calved into a lake the color of skimmed milk.

Rosalind had told him no. Twice. Then the weather window opened, four days of hard clear cold, and Daniel Ostrer had looked at her across the gear table with that smile and said, *You're the only one who says I can't, and everyone else says I can,* and she had been young enough, and proud enough, and broke enough, to say yes.

She remembered the third day with a clarity that never dulled, no matter how many winters poured over it. The traverse below the east seracs, the two of them roped at forty feet, the morning so still she could hear the glacier digesting itself somewhere beneath their boots. Daniel ahead of her, moving well, better than she'd expected. And then — nothing dramatic. No roar, no warning bell. Just a crack like a knuckle popping somewhere overhead, and a shadow sliding across the snow, and her shouting his name in a voice she would hear for the rest of her life.

The serac came down the size of a house. It missed her by the width of the rope.

What it did to Daniel, the report called *injuries consistent with catastrophic blunt force*. What it did to her was simpler: it made her the survivor. The one who walked down. The one whose route plan was in the file, whose signature was on the waiver, whose judgment had placed a client beneath unstable ice at the tail end of calving season.

The inquiry had cleared her. That was the bitter joke of it — the official finding, after eleven days of testimony, was *no guide error identified; event classified as unforeseeable natural hazard.* Cleared. And then the blacklist had come anyway, quiet and unofficial, passed hand to hand among the outfitters and the licensing board and, she understood much later, certain offices in the regional survey authority whose reasons she had never learned. Nobody ever told her she couldn't work the Karst again. They simply made sure there was never work to be had.

For years she'd believed it was grief that followed her, some stain of the accident itself. It was only in the last few days, reading Juniper's careful pencil notes about access restrictions and closed files and a mining claim that shouldn't exist, that another shape had begun to emerge from the fog — the possibility that the door had been closed on her not because of what happened under the seracs, but because of where it happened. Because of what might be seen by anyone who went looking too closely at the source of the Karst.

She pushed the thought down. Speculation was a luxury. Distance was the task.

* * *

By midmorning the valley walls rose sheer on either side, banded limestone folded like wet paper, and the river quickened. She ran two rapids that she remembered from six years ago and didn't remember being this pushy, and portaged a third around a fallen block the size of a chapel, hauling the raft and her sixty pounds of kit over scree that shifted and sang under her boots. Her shoulders burned. Her breath smoked. Somewhere behind her, far downstream, was the life she had built out of smallness — the false name, the lowland outfitting shop, the careful ordinariness of a woman who fixed other people's tents and never slept above the treeline.

Ahead of her was everything else.

There was a freedom in it that frightened her, and she examined that fear the way she'd once taught clients to examine a snowpack: cut a block, look at the layers. Underneath the fear of capture — Ivo's face surfaced there, unbidden, and she shoved it down harder — underneath that was something older and stranger. For six years she had been a woman defined by a single afternoon, a woman who existed in the negative space of a ban. Out here, alone on the water with the banned mountains rising around her, she felt the strange vertigo of becoming undefined again. Whatever happened now, she had chosen it. The blacklist could take her livelihood. It could not take her off this river.

*Grim freedom*, Halvorsen would have called it, and spat, and told her freedom that cost this much was usually bought with someone else's blood. But Halvorsen was dying in a room that smelled of camphor, and his granddaughter was somewhere in the high country, alive or frozen or buried under whatever had taken the rest of the survey team, and Rosalind had stopped pretending those facts belonged to someone braver than her.

She ate cold oats drifting through a long calm stretch, watching the current braid and re-braid itself around gravel bars, and let herself think about Juniper properly for the first time since the shore. Twenty-three years old, according to Halvorsen. Sharp, stubborn, wrote everything down. A girl who had grown up on stories of the Karst Range told by a grandfather who'd guided it before the roads, who must have fought for her place on that survey team the way young people fight for anything — by refusing to hear no.

And now the team was missing, and the file was being closed, and the only person in the province willing to go upriver was a woman with no permit, no partner, and no name worth putting on a form.

*I'm coming*, Rosalind said silently, to the girl, to the mountains, to no one. *Whoever you are. Whoever did this. I'm coming.*

* * *

The light changed in the early afternoon, flattening, and she felt the pressure drop in her ears before she saw the cloud — a grey wall building over the western passes, dragging its skirt of snow down the valleys. Weather. Not the storm yet, but the promise of it, and the promise was a clock. Every hour of fair travel now was an hour stolen from the storm's account, and beyond the storm waited the thing she was racing outright: calving season, when the glacier at the source began shedding its winter load and the meltwater routes through the high country swelled from creeks into torrents. In ten days, maybe twelve, the upper gorge would become impassable. The source would seal itself behind falling water and shifting ice until autumn.

She calculated as she paddled. Two days to the confluence if the water held. Another day through the narrows. Then the high route on foot, past the old survey cache, up onto the glacier's flank. Four days out, five with bad luck. It fit inside the window the way a foot fits inside a boot half a size too small — technically, and painfully, and only if nothing swelled.

Nothing ever failed to swell.

She made camp that night in a hollow above a shingle bar, raft hauled high and turned against the wind, stove hissing behind a windbreak of stacked stone. The temperature fell fast once the sun left the valley. She sat wrapped in her quilt eating rice and jerky and watched the stars come out over the divide, hard and close, and for a long time she let herself feel the thing she had not let herself feel in six years: that she was good at this. That the mountains had not forgotten her, and she had not forgotten them, and every knot her hands tied without looking was a sentence in a language she still spoke fluently.

Then, near midnight, a sound carried down the valley and broke the spell.

An engine. Faint, irregular, somewhere far downstream — not the steady drone of a boat motor but the stuttering rise and fall of something working against the current, stopping, starting again. She killed her stove and lay flat on the shingle and listened until her ears ached, but the sound did not come again, and after a while the river filled the silence the way it always did, and she told herself it had been a log jam settling, or an owl, or the wind playing tricks in the narrows.

She did not believe it. She had spent six years learning to read the lowlands, and the lowlands had taught her exactly one lesson worth carrying: when powerful men want something kept quiet, they send someone to keep it quiet.

Rosalind banked her fire early and lay awake in the dark with the notebook zipped inside her jacket, listening to the river run toward the source, and toward whatever was waiting there. Somewhere upstream, a girl had written *they know we know* and then stopped writing forever. Somewhere downstream, an engine was climbing against the current in the dark.

Dawn was still two hours off when she broke camp and pushed back onto the water, paddling hard, alone, into the narrowing throat of the valley.
