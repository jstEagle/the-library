## 6. Two Banners

The order came down at six in the morning, and it was wrong in three ways before Ivo finished reading it.

First: the river corridor above the Gorge of Whispers had been closed to all travel for eleven days. Nobody with a permit could be on it. Second: the dispatch described the unauthorized party as *a single individual, unidentified, traveling by canoe toward the source*, which meant someone had seen her — or seen someone — and reported it fast enough to reach the district office overnight. Third, and worst: the order instructed him to intercept and detain, but gave no call sign for air support, no backup designation, no mention of coordinating with the provincial police who technically held jurisdiction below the moraine line.

It was an order written by people who wanted something handled quietly, and Ivo Voss had been a warden long enough to know that quiet orders were where careers went to die — one way or another.

He read it twice in the station kitchen while his coffee went cold, then folded it into his breast pocket, where it sat over his heart like a stone he'd chosen to carry himself.

"Voss." Hendricks was in the doorway, still in yesterday's shirt, eyes rimmed red from the night shift. "You're taking this?"

"I'm taking the boat."

"You know what I mean." Hendricks lowered his voice, though the station was empty except for the two of them and the radio's dry static. "Single party, upper corridor. You really think some tourist paddled past three closure signs and a locked gate? Whoever this is knows the water."

Ivo rinsed his mug and set it upside down on the rack. "Then they'll be easy to find."

"That's not what I asked."

It wasn't, and they both knew it. Hendricks had been at Ivo's shoulder through the survey-camp inspection, had watched him photograph footprints that didn't exist and food that hadn't been touched, had stood beside him when the district superintendent closed the file over both their objections. Hendricks owed him nothing and liked him anyway, which made him the only man in the service who could ask the question straight.

"They want this person stopped," Ivo said. "That's what I'm going to do."

"And if you catch them? Detain them how, exactly? You're carrying a warrant card and a first-aid kit. The order says *detain*. That word has weight, Voss. Somebody wrote it on purpose."

"Somebody writes everything on purpose lately."

Hendricks studied him for a long moment, and whatever he saw — Ivo never learned what — made him step aside from the door. "Radio check every four hours. If you miss two, I'm coming up after you, and I won't be polite about it."

"Wouldn't expect you to be."

The river took him at half past seven, gray-green under a sky the color of old tin, running high with snowmelt and full of the small treacherous debris that early season always carried — birch limbs spinning end over end, foam lines curling off submerged boulders, the whole current shrugging against the hull like a living thing that hadn't decided yet whether to let him pass.

He knew these waters the way he knew the back of his own hands: every eddy behind every point bar, every shallow where the gravel shifted each spring, every place where the canyon walls pinched in and the current doubled its temper. He'd run them as a young ranger, cocky and quick, and later he'd run them with Rosalind Fairweather sitting in the stern behind him, calling lines in that low, unhurried voice of hers, reading water the way other people read print.

*Take the left tongue, Ivo. The right one lies.*

He hadn't thought about that voice in months. Now it filled the whole canyon.

He told himself, pushing upstream against the easier channels along the bank, that this was procedure. An unauthorized traveler on a closed river was a legitimate enforcement matter; the fact that the traveler might be Rosalind changed nothing about his duty. If anything it simplified things. She was the only person alive who could navigate that corridor alone in early melt, which meant she was the only person who could tell him what she knew about the missing surveyors, which meant she was either a witness or a suspect, and either way she belonged in front of him, in front of the service, answering questions under proper authority.

That was the story he built, stroke by stroke, all morning. It was a good story. It had the shape of duty, the clean lines of procedure, and it fit inside the warrant card in his pocket like a knife fits inside a sheath.

It did not explain why he'd left the handcuffs in the truck.

* * *

He found her first campsite at noon, or rather he found the absence of one.

She'd pulled out on a gravel shelf below the second rapids — he could read the scuffed line where a hull had been dragged, the faint impression of a knee in the wet sand already softening at the edges — and she'd camped there long enough to eat, because there was a fire ring built from river stones, cold now, ash tamped flat beneath a cairn she'd rebuilt to look like flood scatter. But it was the small things that undid the disguise. The stones of the ring had been placed clockwise, tight as masonry, because she'd been taught by an old Ktunaxa packer thirty years ago and never unlearned it. The ash was tamped, not scattered, because open ash drew birds and birds drew eyes.

And beside the fire ring, pressed into the sand where she'd wanted it found or hadn't cared enough to hide it, a single boot print. Narrow heel. Deep toe. The print of a woman walking uphill under load, unhurried, certain of her footing.

Ivo crouched over it for a long time.

He should have photographed it. Should have logged coordinates, bagged what trace evidence the wind hadn't taken, radioed a description downstream. That was the procedure, and the procedure was the banner he'd sworn under, the thing that separated a warden from a man playing policeman in the wilderness.

Instead he put his own boot beside hers in the sand — not touching, just beside — and measured the difference. Hers was smaller. Her stride was longer. Even in a print, even reduced to pressure and displacement, she moved like someone who trusted the ground to hold her, and God help him, some traitor part of him relaxed at the sight of it, the way you relax when you hear a familiar voice in a dark house.

*Alive*, said the traitor part. *Moving well. Eating. Sleeping enough to build careful fires.*

He stood up and radioed Hendricks at the four-hour mark, voice level, report minimal. *No contact yet. Tracking sign of one party, solo, competent. Continuing upstream.* He did not say the name. He noticed that he didn't, and filed the noticing away in the same drawer where he kept the missing handcuffs and the folded order, the drawer that was getting fuller by the hour.

The afternoon he gained on her. He could feel it in the freshness of the sign — the drag marks sharper, the knee prints still holding crisp edges — and by late light he was reading water ahead of him instead of tracks behind, because he knew where she'd have to stop. The gorge country began above the fifth rapids, where the valley walls leaned together and the river dropped into a series of chutes that no sane paddler would run alone in high water. She'd have to portage, and portaging took time, and time was the one thing he had more of than she did.

Unless she didn't stop. Unless she ran them.

He stood on a granite bench above the fifth chute as the light went copper, watching the water tear itself white below him, and tried to imagine her lining a canoe down the bank in the dark rather than risk a camp within his reach. He could imagine it. That was the problem. He could imagine every hard, brilliant, self-destructive thing she might do, because he'd spent eight years loving a woman whose answer to a locked door was to find the window, and then the roof, and then the reason the door was locked in the first place.

The client death. The inquiry. The blacklist. He'd been in the courtroom gallery the day they stripped her certification, watching her stand straight-backed in a borrowed jacket while men who'd never carried a pack above the tree line explained to her what she'd done wrong on a mountain they couldn't have found on a map. He'd believed the verdict then. He'd needed to believe it, because the alternative was believing that the service he'd given his life to had eaten one of its own to protect its own, and he hadn't been ready for that.

He was being made ready now. The redacted files had seen to that — her name cut out of search reports like a tumor excised, pages numbered out of sequence, a whole history of competence erased so thoroughly that only someone looking for the erasure would ever find it. Someone had built her blacklist the way you build a dam: deliberately, with engineering, for a purpose downstream.

And now she was paddling toward that purpose, alone, and his service had handed him a quiet order and sent him after her with no backup and no air support, and somewhere beneath his ribs the two truths were grinding against each other like millstones.

*Duty*, said one. *Love*, said the other. And between them, the grain of his life being crushed fine.

* * *

He caught her at dusk on a gravel bar below the mouth of the gorge, and afterward he would understand that she had let him.

That was the humiliation of it, and the gift. He came around the last bend running the shallows hard, bow wave hissing, and there she was — canoe pulled up on the bar, fire laid but unlit, herself standing at the waterline with her paddle planted like a staff, watching him come. Not surprised. Not scrambling. Waiting, the way you wait for weather you've been reading all day.

He grounded the boat ten feet from hers and stepped out into shin-deep water, and for a moment neither of them moved. The river spoke between them, patient and enormous. Behind her the canyon walls went up and up into shadow, and above them the first stars were pricking through, cold and indifferent.

"Rosalind."

"Warden Voss." Her voice was exactly as he remembered it, low and dry, and the use of his title was a blade turned flat — showing him the edge without cutting. "You're a long way from your jurisdiction."

"The corridor's closed. You're on it."

"I'm aware." She tilted her head at the folded paper she could surely see in his pocket, or simply knew it was there. "Read me the order. Word for word. I want to hear which words they chose."

He didn't move. "You know I have to take you in."

"Do I?" She took one step closer, boots grinding on the gravel, and up close he could see what eight years and a blacklist had done to her face — the same bones, the same grey eyes, but carved harder now, leaner, lit from inside by something that looked less like hope than like fuel. "Look at this river, Ivo. Look at where we're standing. There are two boats on this bar and one of them belongs to a woman the government erased from its own files, and the other belongs to a man sent upriver alone, at speed, with no backup, to *detain* her. You've read enough incident reports to know what that arithmetic means. So before you touch that warrant card, you're going to tell me why your superiors are so afraid of one solo paddler that they can't say her name on the radio."

"I don't work for your theories."

"No," she said softly. "You work for mine. You just haven't admitted it yet."

The current pulled at his legs, insistent, and he became aware that he was standing in the river between two boats like a man standing between two countries, and that the border ran directly through his chest. Everything he'd built the argument for on the water — procedure, authority, the clean machinery of the law — was right there in his pocket, four ounces of laminated cardstock. All he had to do was say the words. *Rosalind Fairweather, you're detained pending investigation.* All he had to do was become, for one moment, the instrument of the people who had redacted her out of existence, and trust that the system would sort it out later, the way it had sorted out the client death, the way it had sorted out the survey team, the way it sorted out everything — quietly, downstream, in files nobody read.

"You were at the camp," he said instead. "The survey camp. Before me, or after."

Something flickered in her face — not guilt. Calculation, and beneath it, briefly, grief. "After. I found a notebook."

"A notebook."

"Pages of one. Juniper Hale's. She was on that team, Ivo. Nineteen years old. Her grandfather is dying in a room above my outfitter's shop, and he asked me—" She stopped herself, jaw tightening, and he watched her decide how much truth the river could be trusted with. "There are people in these mountains who executed that survey team. Not lost them. *Executed* them. And the order in your pocket didn't send you up here to find them. It sent you up here to find me."

"You can't know that."

"I can count," she said. "So can you. That's why you left the cuffs in the truck."

The words landed like a hand on the back of his neck. He hadn't told anyone about the handcuffs. He hadn't let himself think about the handcuffs.

For a long moment the only sound was the river, and the small wind coming down the gorge carrying the smell of ice — that mineral, ancient smell, the smell of the source, of whatever was buried under the glacier waiting for all of them. Rosalind watched him across ten feet of moving water with the patience of a woman who had already lost everything that could be taken from her, and Ivo felt the two banners he served pull taut between his hands until the rope hummed.

If he reached for the card, he lost her — maybe to a cell, maybe to whoever had written the order, maybe to the river itself, because she would run the chutes in the dark before she'd let him walk her out. If he stepped aside, he was finished — a warden who'd had his suspect in arm's reach and let her go, and there was no version of that story that ended with his badge intact.

"Every four hours," he heard himself say, "I radio a position report. My partner comes looking if I miss two."

"I know how the leash works."

"So do I." He looked at her — really looked, letting eight years of silence burn off like morning fog — and saw that she understood, and hated needing to understand, and understood anyway. "I can't not report you, Rosalind. If I go silent, they send people. Armed ones, next time, and not wardens."

"Then report me," she said. "Report a lone paddler, unidentified, evading contact. Report that you lost the sign in the gorge, where sign goes to die anyway." A ghost of the old smile, there and gone. "You taught me that trick yourself, the winter we ran the Kettle River. Lie with the truth. It's the only lie that holds."

"It's not a trick. It's a discipline."

"It's a marriage," she said, and the word went through him like a spear of cold water, because she'd said it without flinching, without softness, as simple fact — the thing they had been to each other, dissolved on paper but nowhere else, never anywhere else.

He stepped back. Out of the water. To the side — not toward his boat, not toward hers, onto the neutral gravel between them, a man standing down from a post only he could see.

"I need forty minutes' start," she said. "Then you follow. Loudly. Radio chatter, engine noise, the whole performance. Anyone watching needs to believe the warden's doing his job."

"And where am I following you to?"

She looked up the gorge, into the dark where the river went underground and the glacier stood over its secret, and when she answered, her voice was quiet enough that the river almost took it.

"To the source. Where the truth's been buried since before either of us got our certifications. Bring your warrant card, Ivo." She was already turning to her canoe, already moving with that unhurried certainty he'd measured in a bootprint hours ago. "You're going to need something to surrender."

He stood on the gravel bar as she pushed off, paddle biting, hull sliding into the current that ran toward the forbidden water, and he did not reach for his radio, and he did not reach for his boat, and for the space of ten full breaths he stood perfectly still between the two banners while the stars came out over the Karst Range like witnesses.

Then he checked his watch, counted forty minutes, and started after her — telling himself, all the way up the darkening gorge, that he was only gathering information.

The lie held for nine miles. At the tenth, where the river vanished under the mountain with a sound like a held breath, he stopped lying to himself, and the silence that followed was the freest and most frightening thing he had ever known.
