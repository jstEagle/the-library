## 7. The Gorge of Whispers

**Rosalind**

The storm came down the valley like something with intent.

Rosalind had watched weather her whole life — had read cloud and wind the way other people read letters from home — and she had never seen a front move with this kind of purpose. It came off the high ice in a grey wall, swallowing the peaks one by one, and by the time she had the tarp strung between two alders it was already on her.

She crouched under the canvas through the worst of it, listening to the river change. That was the thing about mountain water: it spoke. In the evening it murmured. In the night it argued. By morning, when she crawled out into a world rinsed silver and dripping, it was screaming.

The lower trail — the one that hugged the true left bank, the one every map showed as the sensible route to the source — was gone. Not damaged. *Gone.* Where the path had run along a shelf of gravel and pine root, there was now a brown torrent moving whole trees sideways, boiling up against the cut bank, tearing it away in slabs the size of tables. The storm had found some hanging valley above and emptied it all at once, and the river had answered by doubling its width and tripling its voice.

She stood at the edge of it with her hands in her pockets and did the arithmetic she didn't want to do. Calving season would open the high route within days — a week if she was lucky — and after that the glacier's face would be shedding ice into the river like a dog shedding in summer, and nothing would go up or down that water until autumn. If she waited for the trail to dry, she might as well turn around now. If she turned around now, Juniper Ashcombe stayed wherever she was, which was very possibly nowhere at all.

Which left the gorge.

Everyone who worked this range knew about the Gorge of Whispers and most of them made a career of never going near it. The river dropped into a slot of limestone not much wider than a wagon and ran underground for the better part of four kilometers, and the old route — the poacher's route, the smuggler's route, the route that had never been on any government map because no government wanted the liability of admitting it existed — threaded the rim of that slot on ledges barely wide enough for a loaded pack frame. Her father had taken her through it when she was eleven. She remembered his hand on her shoulder blade, steady as a rail, and his voice saying: *the rock doesn't care that you're scared, so stop spending breath on it.*

She was thirty-four now and there was no hand on her shoulder blade, and she was going to walk that ledge alone, and she was fine with that. She told herself she was fine with that.

She was breaking camp when she heard the footstep.

Not a footstep, exactly. A weight shifting on gravel, one ridge over, deliberate in the way that trained people are deliberate. Rosalind went still with a tent peg half-driven and let her eyes do the work while her body pretended to be busy.

He came around the alders twenty minutes later, because he wanted her to know he'd given her time to run, and she hadn't taken it, and they both understood what that meant.

Ivo Voss looked worse than the last time she'd seen him — three years ago, across a hearing room table, while men in better coats than either of them owned took turns explaining what she was. His rain gear was soaked through at the shoulders. He carried his pack like it was full of rocks, which meant he'd packed for a long patrol, which meant he'd known before he left the station that he wasn't coming back today.

"You're on my river," she said.

"Your river." He stopped ten paces out, close enough to talk, far enough to reach for nothing. "Last I checked, rivers don't belong to blacklisted guides."

"Last I checked, wardens don't hunt solo women upstream of the closed line." She drove the peg home and stood. "So let's not pretend we're both here by accident."

"I'm here because an unauthorized party is on the river."

"One party. Singular." She shouldered her pack. "You've been tracking me since the gravel bar below the falls, Ivo. You crossed my campsite twice in the night to check my fire. You could have called it in. One radio call — *subject located, moving to intercept* — and you'd have a helicopter here by noon and your file closed by Friday." She looked at him straight. "You haven't called it in."

The wind moved through the wet alders. Somewhere above them, loosened by the storm, a chunk of the slope let go and rattled down into the flood.

"The trail's gone," he said finally.

"I noticed."

"It'll be a week before—"

"Ten days," she said. "And calving opens in six. Do your own arithmetic, warden. You were good at arithmetic once."

He flinched at that — small, but she'd spent years learning to read him the way she read water, and she caught it. He looked past her, upriver, toward the dark seam in the cliffs where the gorge began, and she watched him arrive at the same place she'd arrived an hour ago, and she watched him hate it.

"If we take the rim route," he said slowly, "we travel together."

"*We* don't travel anywhere. *I* travel. You can follow at whatever distance keeps your conscience comfortable."

"Rosalind." Just her name, and the way he said it undid three years of careful distance in a single syllable, and she hated him for it. "There's a section past the second narrows where the ledge goes to eighteen inches and the drop is eighty meters of white water. Alone, with a full pack, in post-storm wet? You'd be gambling a life that isn't only yours anymore. There's a girl missing up that river. If anyone's going to find her, it needs to be two of us, roped, watching each other's feet."

"And when we find her? Or find what's left?" Rosalind adjusted a strap that didn't need adjusting. "You going to arrest me at the glacier's foot? Because that's the end of this road, Ivo. You know it and I know it. Sooner or later you have to choose what you actually came up here to do."

He didn't answer. He uncoiled his rope instead, and checked it with the quick, blind-handed competence she'd fallen in love with a decade ago, and said, "We'll cross that bridge when the river lets us build it."

It wasn't an answer. But it was a truce, and truces on mountains are worth more than answers.

They walked into the gorge together, ten paces apart, saying nothing, the river roaring somewhere beneath their feet like a crowd in another room.

**Ivo**

The gorge earned its name within the first hour.

The limestone walls funneled the wind into channels and gave it voices — low moans through the fissures, a thin whistling where it split over a chockstone, and underneath everything the muffled thunder of the buried river, so that walking the rim felt like crossing the roof of a shouting building. Ivo kept his eyes on his feet and his peripheral vision on Rosalind ahead of him, and tried very hard to think like a warden and not like a man.

It wasn't working.

He knew her rhythm. Ten years of shared rope will teach you a person's body the way a language teaches you its alphabet, and hers hadn't changed: the little pause before committing weight to a slick ledge, the habit of testing holds with her palm flat rather than her fingers hooked, the way she saved her breath on the climbs and spent it on the traverses. He matched her without meaning to, the way you fall into step with a drumbeat, and twice she glanced back as if she'd felt him doing it, and twice he pretended to be checking the terrain.

The storm had left the rock weeping. Water sheeted down the walls in places, and the ledges wore a skin of green slime that his boots reported to him honestly and unpleasantly. At the second narrows — the section she'd named back at camp, the eighteen-inch shelf above the eighty-meter drop — they roped up without discussing it. She simply stopped, coiled out forty feet of rope, and held one end out behind her without turning around, and he clipped in, and that was the whole conversation.

Halfway across the narrows, the ledge pinched to almost nothing where a block had spalled off the wall sometime in the last century. Rosalind went through it facing the rock, arms spread, moving like a climber on a traverse, and Ivo followed with his heart going harder than the altitude justified, watching her boots place themselves with surgical calm and thinking: *she has done this alone. She has done all of this alone, for years, because of us.*

Because of him, if he was honest, which on a rope he always was. He'd sat in that hearing room too. He'd been asked directly whether he believed her account of the descent, and he'd said the case was outside his knowledge, which was technically true and morally a lie, because he *did* know — he'd been on the ridge that day, he'd seen the cornice conditions, he'd known the client had climbed past the turnaround time against Rosalind's explicit order. He'd said nothing, because saying something would have ended his career, and he'd told himself for three years that staying silent was prudence. Walking this ledge, it felt like what it was.

"Anchor," Rosalind called back, her voice flattened by the wind-voices. "Belay through."

He belayed her across the exposed slab beyond the pinch, and then she anchored and brought him across, and then they were through the narrows and the ledge widened into a sloping gallery of broken limestone, and the tension in his shoulders dropped half an inch.

That was when he saw the rebar.

It shouldn't have been there. That was the first thought, clean and cold, arriving before any other. Old smugglers' routes didn't have rebar. Government trails didn't have rebar. Rebar meant concrete, and concrete meant money, and money meant somebody had built something in this gorge that appeared on no map he'd ever signed.

A length of it jutted from a drill hole in the wall, rusted orange at the exposed end, bent slightly outward — and snagged on it, crumpled like a shed skin, was a glove.

Grey synthetic shell, survey-grade, the kind issued by the ministry's mapping division. Ivo had signed for dozens of pairs. This one was torn open across the back of the hand — no. Not torn. He got closer, closer than he wanted to be to the drop, and looked, and his stomach went quiet and professional.

Cut. Sliced cleanly across the knuckles, one stroke, sharp blade, from inside the glove outward. And below the cut, stiffened into the fabric, a stain that had once been red.

"Rosalind."

She was already coming back along the ledge. She crouched beside him, and he watched her see exactly what he'd seen, in the same order: the glove, the cut, the blood. Her face did something complicated and then became very still.

"That's ministry issue," she said.

"Survey team issue." He worked the glove free of the rebar carefully, keeping the fabric intact, and turned it over. Inside the cuff, in faded marker: a kit number. He knew the numbering sequence. He'd requisitioned against it. "This is Ashcombe's size. Junior staff get the small shells."

"Juniper's."

"Maybe. Kit numbers get reassigned." He said it because it was procedurally true, and hated himself for saying it, because they both knew what the glove actually was. A glove doesn't tear itself off a hand. A glove gets cut off — quickly, urgently, by someone else, with a knife, when a hand is bleeding and needs treating or when a hand is beyond treating and someone is in a hurry.

And the rebar meant the hurry had happened *here*, in a gorge that was supposed to be empty, on a route that was supposed to be unknown.

"They didn't wander," Rosalind said quietly. Above them the wind moaned through the fissures; below them the hidden river thundered; and between those two voices her words landed like a stone dropped into still water. "The official story is they got lost above the moraine. Exposure. Wandering deaths." She looked up at him, and her eyes in the grey light were the flattest he'd ever seen them, which for Rosalind Fairweather meant fury with nowhere to go. "You don't wander into a slot gorge four kilometers from your camp and lose a glove to a knife."

"No," Ivo said. "You don't."

He put the glove inside his jacket, against his chest, where the rain couldn't reach it. Evidence, his training said. Bag it, log it, chain of custody. But evidence belonged to a system, and three days ago his superiors had closed the file on six missing people over his written objection, and he no longer trusted the system to hold anything gently.

"We keep moving," Rosalind said. She was already standing, already facing upriver, already becoming the guide again — but she paused, one hand on the wet rock, and didn't look back at him when she said the next part. "Whatever we find up there, Ivo — whoever did this — I need to know, right now, on this ledge, whether you're taking notes for your report or taking notes for the truth. Because I will walk differently depending on the answer."

The gorge breathed around them. Somewhere deep under the limestone, the river found a wider chamber and its thunder softened into something almost like a voice.

Ivo touched the glove through his jacket and heard himself say, "For the truth. Whatever it costs."

She held his eyes one second longer than comfort allowed — checking, the way she'd check an anchor — and then nodded once, and turned, and led on up the ledge into the whispering dark.

Behind them, far downstream, faint under the river's roar, came a sound Ivo needed three full seconds to identify, and when he identified it his blood went cold.

An engine. Small, high-revving, mechanical — the sound of a chainsaw, or a generator, or a pump.

Working. Upstream. Ahead of them.
