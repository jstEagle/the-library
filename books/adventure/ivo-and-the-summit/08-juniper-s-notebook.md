## 8. Juniper's Notebook

The pages were damp at the edges, warped where the river had gotten to them, but the ink had held. Rosalind spread them across the flat stone beside her fire with the care of a surgeon laying out instruments, weighting each corner with pebbles from the gravel bar, and then she sat back on her heels and did not touch them for a long moment.

She had dried them first. That was the discipline of it — the old discipline, the one that had lived in her hands through seven years of false names and lowland work: dry the paper before you read it, or you'll tear it and lose what it says forever. She had learned that from a drowned man's logbook once, in another life, on another mountain, back when her name was still worth printing on a permit.

Now she let the fire warm her back and looked at the handwriting.

It was Juniper's. She had never seen the girl write before — had only met her twice, three years ago, when Halvorsen brought his granddaughter round to the outfitter's to be fitted for boots — but there was no mistaking it. The same careful, slightly left-leaning script that had filled out the rental forms, each letter formed like something set down deliberately, by someone who wanted to be understood. A surveyor's hand. Precise even when the letters wobbled, which they did increasingly as the pages went on.

*Day one,* the notebook began, in its neat way. *Approach up the Karst River. Weather clear. Moraine crossing slow but uneventful.*

Rosalind read it twice, slowly, the fire cracking behind her, the gorge breathing its underground whisper somewhere below the dark. Then she began again at the beginning, and this time she read it like a guide.

* * *

The team had gone up the same route she'd taken. Of course they had; there was only one route, not counting the ones that killed you. But they'd gone as six, with government weight behind them and a radio schedule to keep, and reading their progress was like watching a familiar film of her own climb played by other hands. *Crossed the scree field below the second moraine. P. complains about the freeze-dried eggs.* Small human things. A joke about Wren's hat. A sketch of a marmot that had stolen a spoon.

Then day four, and the handwriting changed.

Not dramatically. That was the terrible thing about it, Rosalind thought — how gradually the precision frayed. The letters stayed upright, stayed legible, but they got smaller, tighter, as though Juniper were trying to fit more into less space, or as though the page itself were becoming dangerous to fill.

*Reached the head of the valley. The glacier terminus is wrong.*

Rosalind stopped.

She knew the Karst Glacier. She had known it better than anyone alive, once — had guided under its flanks for nine seasons, had memorized its moods the way sailors memorize weather. And she knew what Juniper meant, because she had seen it herself two days ago from the ridge above the gorge: the snout of the ice sitting lower and grayer than any photograph or memory of it, its face crumbled into rubble where it should have shown clean blue.

*The terminus is wrong,* Juniper had written. *Retreated further than the last survey, which is expected, but the debris cover is new. Fresh rockfall where there shouldn't be rockfall. The moraine has been disturbed. Dug down half a meter at three points. Found blasting cord fragments. Modern.*

There was a pressed space after that line, a small deliberate gap, as if the girl had needed to make herself stop writing for a moment.

*P. says it could be old seismic work. Nobody does seismic work here. There's no road, no claim, nothing. This whole drainage is closed. He says someone should ask why we're the first official party up here in eleven years. Nobody answers him.*

Rosalind turned a page. The pebble holding its corner slipped and she caught it, reset it, her fingers steadier than she felt.

* * *

The next entries came faster, shorter, the surveyor's discipline compressing itself into bulletins:

*Sampled the meltwater. Turbidity high for the season. Fine gray sediment, not glacial flour — wrong color, wrong grain. Took samples.*

*Wren found a drilling casing half-buried in the lateral moraine. Cut and abandoned. Recent corrosion only. Photographed it.*

*Heard machinery today. Or thought I did. Through the ice, maybe. P. heard it too. Sounded like it was coming from underneath us.*

Rosalind looked up at the dark mouth of the gorge, where the river went under and did not come out for two kilometers. The whispering sound rose faintly from it still, that hushed subterranean voice she'd been telling herself was water all along.

Water made many sounds. She knew them all. This was not entirely water.

* * *

Then came the entry that stopped her breath.

It was longer. The handwriting had loosened, the leftward lean exaggerated, letters running downhill toward the margin. Whatever composure Juniper had been maintaining broke here, mid-page, and what came through was not panic exactly — it was worse. It was a professional woman trying very hard to keep being professional while the ground opened under her.

*We went onto the ice today. Found an entrance in the ablation zone where the melt has undercut the glacier — a cave, big enough to walk. We went in because that's what we're here for, to map what's mapped wrong.*

*The cave goes back further than it should. The walls are smooth in places. Too smooth. Ice doesn't carve itself into corridors.*

*At the back, where the glacier meets the bedrock, there are scars in the rock face. Blasting scars. Fresh ones — the rock dust was still on the ledges, yellow-gray, and there were drill marks in a grid pattern. Someone has been cutting tunnels into the mountain from beneath the ice. From inside the glacier.*

*P. took ore samples. Galena, mostly. Some of the gray mineral we couldn't ID — heavy, soft, leaves a mark. He wrapped it in his spare shirt and wouldn't stop looking at it. He kept saying "this grade" over and over, like a man counting money he was afraid of.*

*This grade. In a closed drainage. Under a protected glacier. With no claim filed, no permit, no road, and no explanation for how equipment got here except that it came up the river the way we did, in pieces, quietly, over years.*

*Someone is mining the source of the Karst.*

*I keep thinking about who can move that much material without anyone seeing. Not smugglers. Smugglers hide from governments. This hides inside one.*

Rosalind sat motionless. The fire popped. Somewhere above, on the glacier she could not see in the dark, the ice ticked and settled, the sound carrying down the valley like knuckles rapping on a door.

She thought about the redacted files. She thought about Ivo's face on the gravel bar, the warrant card, the word *closed* in the mouths of men who had never stood at the source. She thought about a client dead at the bottom of an icefall seven years ago, and an inquiry that had needed someone to blame, and how neatly she had been fitted into the shape of the blame — and she felt, for the first time, not the old sour grief but something colder and cleaner rising underneath it.

Understanding.

She hadn't been blacklisted despite the truth. She had been blacklisted because of it. Because she had been the best guide on that mountain, the one who knew the drainage, the one whose testimony about conditions that day had never matched the official story — and a mountain full of secret tunnels needed the one witness with credibility pushed so far out of the profession that no one would ever call her back to stand on the ice and say otherwise.

*They didn't ruin me,* she thought. *They fenced me off. Like a hazard zone.*

Her hands had gone cold. She fed the fire without looking at it.

* * *

The last pages were different. Folded separately, torn free of the binding, tucked between the others like something Juniper had meant to survive even if the notebook didn't. The handwriting on these was fast, slanting hard, written perhaps against a knee, perhaps in the dark.

*Radio schedule missed yesterday. Base acknowledged late. Voice on the schedule check wasn't Chen. Said it was. Wasn't.*

*Two men came up-valley this morning. Not ours. Company radios — good ones, the kind you buy with a budget, not the kind you steal. They said they were a resupply. Had no resupply.*

*P. hid the samples. Split everything. If anything happens to us, the record is split too.*

*We're moving tonight. Downriver. If we can reach the gorge we can reach people.*

*They know we know.*

That was the last line. Four words, pressed deep into the paper, the pen having torn slightly through at the final stroke. Below it, nothing. No date, no signature, no closing. Just the white of the page, and the small ragged tear where the pen had punctured it, as if the girl's hand had finally done what her words had been refusing to do all along.

Rosalind read the four words until she had lost count of the readings.

*They know we know.*

And then, beneath it, almost invisible unless you tilted the page to the firelight — a smudge, a pressure mark where something had been written and erased, or written on a page above and impressed down. She angled the paper carefully, turning it against the light, and made out the ghost of numbers. Coordinates. Grid references, in the survey format she recognized from her own old permits.

Juniper had hidden the samples and the record. Split everything, she'd written. Which meant somewhere ahead — upstream, toward the source, toward the caves under the ice — there was a cache with her name on it, waiting for whoever was clever enough or desperate enough to come find it.

Or waiting for the men with the company radios, if they found the notebook first.

Rosalind looked at the fire for a long time. The gorge whispered. The glacier ticked. Above her, invisible in the dark, calving season gathered itself like a held breath, and every hour she sat here was an hour off the clock that would seal the high route and drown the evidence and the girl both, if the girl was still alive to be drowned.

Seven years she had kept her head down. Seven years of false names and rented boots and other people's easy mountains, and it had bought her nothing but survival, and survival, she saw now, had been the trap. They had built her a life too small to matter in, and called it mercy.

She folded the pages along their existing creases, wrapped them in oilcloth, and stowed them against her spine where the river could never reach them.

Then she kicked dirt over the fire, shouldered her pack, and stood facing upvalley, into the wind coming down off the ice — the wind that smelled of stone dust and cold and, faintly, unmistakably, of blasted rock.

"They know you know," she said aloud to the dark, to the girl somewhere ahead of her, alive or not. "So now they're going to know I do too."

She took the first step upstream.
