## 9. The Man Below

The glacier's mouth opened before them like the throat of something ancient and indifferent.

Ivo had seen ice caves before — the small ones, the honest ones, where meltwater carved a summer passage and abandoned it again by autumn. This was nothing of that kind. The entrance yawned at the base of the source glacier where it shouldered against black rock, a vaulted darkness forty feet across, its rim hung with icicles as long as a man's forearm. The air that breathed out of it was cold in a way that felt deliberate, curated, like the interior of a church. It smelled of stone and deep time and, faintly, unmistakably, of woodsmoke.

Someone was living in there.

Rosalind stopped at the threshold and stood very still, her breath pluming silver. Ivo watched her read the ground the way she read everything — with her whole body, head tilted, eyes moving in quick economical sweeps. She'd been doing it for three days now, ever since they'd left the gorge behind and begun the last brutal climb toward the source, and he had learned to trust her silences more than most men's speeches.

"Fire," she said quietly. "Recent. Within hours."

"Could be meltwater channels funneling sound," Ivo said, though he didn't believe it and she knew he didn't believe it. "We're a day from where the notebook said the team made camp."

"We're not following the notebook anymore." She crouched and touched the snow at the cave's lip, then lifted her fingers to show him: a grey smudge of ash, fine as flour. "This came out on the draft. Whatever's burning in there is being fed." She looked up at him, and in the flat white light her eyes were the color of the glacier's deepest crevasses. "Juniper's notebook said *they know we know*. Whoever wrote that entry wasn't writing about us."

Ivo's hand went to the radio on his belt without his deciding it would. He hadn't transmitted since the gorge — couldn't risk it, not after what they'd found snagged on that rebar, not after Rosalind had laid the cut glove in his palm and said, *surveyors don't tear their gloves on rock, Ivo, they tear them on wire* — but the habit of the thing, the weight of it, the twelve years of service coiled inside the plastic casing, all of it pulled at him still.

He let go of it. The gesture didn't escape her. Nothing escaped her.

"If there's someone in there," he said, "they've spent however many days listening for boots on the moraine. We walk in loud or we don't walk in at all."

"Loud," Rosalind agreed. "And hands visible. And you first."

"Why me?"

"Because if they're one of the surveyors, they'll have heard of the warden service. A badge is a language everyone up here speaks." She paused, and something moved behind her eyes that might have been bitterness or might have been grief. "Whereas my name, in certain company, means *run*."

Ivo wanted to argue with that. He wanted to tell her that the woman who had dragged him through the flooded gorge, who had read the glacier's moods like a shepherd reads weather, whose competence had kept them both alive through four days of country that killed experienced men every season — that this woman was not the name on the blacklist. But the words caught where such words always caught between them, in the scar tissue of six years' silence, and so he only nodded and unclipped his warrant card from the inner pocket of his jacket and held it loose in his left hand where it could be seen.

They stepped into the cold together.

* * *

The blue light inside the cave was unlike any light Ivo had known. It came from everywhere and nowhere — refracted through walls of ice ten feet thick, filtered of every color but the far end of the spectrum, so that the whole passage glowed like the inside of a bottle of deep water. Their shadows were soft-edged things, barely theirs. Sound behaved strangely too; each footstep arrived twice, once as crunch and once as echo off surfaces too smooth to absorb anything.

The passage narrowed, widened, narrowed again. Meltwater chuckled somewhere below them, invisible, running through channels worn glass-smooth. And always the smell of smoke, growing stronger, until around a shoulder of blue ice they saw it: a fire built small and disciplined in a hollow of stone, feeding on broken survey crates and a surveyor's field manual, page by page. Beside the fire, arranged with obsessive neatness, lay the wreckage of a life: two space blankets, an empty ration tin used as a cup, a boot with its sole flapping, and a satchel — olive drab, government-stamped, strapped shut with wire twisted tight.

And above the satchel, rising out of the dark beyond the firelight like something surfacing from deep water, a man.

He was gaunt past gauntness — the word that came to Ivo's mind was *rendered*, as if the days since the ambush had boiled him down to essentials. His beard was black frost. His left hand was a ruin, the fingers swollen and darkened past the second knuckle, cradled against his chest in a sling improvised from a shirt sleeve. His right hand held a knife — a surveyor's cutting tool, short-bladed, meant for sample bags and rope, held now with the desperate competence of a man who had already decided how he was going to die and intended to make it expensive.

"Don't." His voice was a rasp, almost gone. "Don't come closer. I'll do it. I swear to God I'll do it."

Ivo raised both hands slowly, warrant card pinched between two fingers of his left. "Warden Service," he said, keeping his voice low and even, the voice he'd used on panicked hikers and cornered poachers for twelve years. "Ivo Voss. Badge number 4471. I'm not here for you."

The man laughed — a terrible sound, dry as snapping ice. "Nobody's here for me. That's the whole point. Nobody's *supposed* to find me." The knife wavered, then steadied. "You're one of theirs. Company man. They sent you to finish it."

"I'm not—"

"They had radios." The words tumbled out of him now, a dam failing, frost-black lips working fast. "Company sets, encrypted bands, the kind you need a license for. They knew our route. They knew our schedule. They were waiting at the col when we came down off the icefield, and they—" His breath hitched. His eyes, fever-bright, fixed on Rosalind, half-hidden behind Ivo's shoulder, and the knife swung toward her. "Who is she? Why won't she show her face?"

Rosalind stepped forward.

It was the last thing Ivo expected and probably the last thing the man expected too. She walked into the knife's arc without hurry, hands open at her sides, and crouched down to the man's level by the fire, close enough that the blade could reach her throat if he chose.

"My name is Rosalind Fairweather," she said. "I was a guide in this range for eleven years. I knew Tomas Brandt — your team lead — from when he was a graduate student mapping the medial moraines. He carried my spare rope once, coming down off the north ridge, when his own froze solid. He never let me forget it." Her voice didn't waver. "I'm here because his granddaughter asked someone to come, and no one else would. And because I owe this mountain a debt I've been running from for six years."

The man stared at her. The knife trembled.

"You're the one they blacklisted," he whispered. "Brandt talked about you. The client death. He said—" He stopped, swallowed. "He said the file didn't add up. He said it never added up."

"No," Rosalind said softly. "It didn't."

Something went out of the man's shoulders then — not surrender, exactly, but the exhaustion of a sentry who has finally been relieved and cannot quite believe it. The knife lowered by degrees, though it did not leave his hand. Ivo understood; it wouldn't leave his hand for a long time, maybe ever. Some nights it would be the last thing he gripped before sleep.

"I'm Emrys," the man said. "Emrys Cadwallader. Junior hydrologist." He looked at his ruined left hand without seeing it. "There were nine of us."

* * *

They worked on him in shifts while the fire ate the last pages of the field manual.

Frostbite was a slow argument between flesh and cold, and the cold had been winning for eight days. Ivo had field-medic certification that was due for renewal and a kit that Rosalind had bullied him into repacking at the outfitter's, and between them they did what could be done: warmed him gradually, because fast heat kills frozen tissue faster than cold does; got sugar and water into him in sips; wrapped the left hand in dry dressing and splinted it against a tent stake. Emrys bore it with a silence that Ivo recognized — the silence of a man conserving everything, pain included, because he had learned these past days that everything might be needed later.

The satchel stayed within reach of his good hand the entire time. When Rosalind moved it six inches to make room for the dressing, his fingers closed on the strap before she'd finished the motion, reflexive as a heartbeat.

"It stays with me," he said.

"I wasn't taking it."

"You were moving it." His eyes were clear now, fever receding, and they were hard little stones. "Everything that matters is in this bag. Nine people died so it could exist. You don't move it. You don't touch it. You don't even look at it sideways unless I say so." He drew a breath that shuddered on the way in. "I know how that sounds. I've had eight days alone in the dark to decide how paranoid to be, and I've decided: extremely."

Ivo crouched across the fire from him. "Then hear my offer," he said, and took out his warrant card again, and laid it on the stone between them, face up. "Ivo Voss. Twelve years, Warden Service. That badge has been my whole life since I was twenty-three. If I put it in that fire, it doesn't come back. There's no reissue for burned warrants. There's a tribunal, and questions, and the end of everything I am professionally." He turned the card slightly so the firelight caught the laminate. "So when I tell you what I'm about to tell you, understand what it's backed by."

Emrys looked at the card. Looked at him.

"Four days ago I was ordered to declare this drainage closed and file a report stating the survey team's disappearance was consistent with a wandering fatality," Ivo said. "I filed a different report. I requested the old search files under my own authority, and found names redacted that had no business being redacted. Two days ago I found a surveyor's glove cut through with a blade, hung on rebar in a slot gorge where rebar has no reason to exist. Yesterday I chose not to transmit on my service radio, because I no longer believe my service is speaking only to itself." He picked the card back up and held it out flat on his palm. "I can't prove any of that to you. All I can give you is this, and the fact that I'm standing here instead of filing paperwork that would have sealed this valley with you inside it."

The silence stretched. The fire popped. Somewhere deep in the glacier, with a sound like a distant door closing, the ice settled on itself.

Emrys reached out with his ruined left hand — the good one still holding the knife — and touched the warrant card, just briefly, the way a man touches a talisman.

"Voss," he said. "All right. Voss, I believe you." Then his eyes went to Rosalind, and softened by one degree, which from him was a landslide. "Fairweather. Brandt said if anyone ever came for us, pray it's someone the mountain already tried to break. Because the mountain only breaks what's real." He almost smiled; it cost him visibly. "That's the closest thing to a compliment he ever gave anybody."

Rosalind's face did something complicated and quickly put away. "What's in the bag, Emrys?"

For a moment he didn't answer. Then he set the knife down — actually down, on the stone, within reach but not in hand — and began unwinding the wire from the satchel's buckle with his teeth and his one working hand, clumsy and patient.

"Ore samples. Assays done by our own chemist, dated nine days before we were hit. Blasting scars under the glacier tongue, photographed, with charge casings stamped by a company that officially has no presence in this country. A route map of the adits — the tunnels — marked with volumes." He drew out a waterproof document wallet and held it against his chest like a shield. "And the correspondence. Copies. Someone inside the ministry was feeding the operation schedules, permits, our own survey authorization — they used *our government mandate* to get access to terrain they'd already secretly claimed. The signatures go high. Both sides of the border. Names I've only ever seen on news broadcasts." He looked up, and in the blue light his face was skull-like and utterly calm. "Nine people are dead because they mapped a hole in the ground. So yes. The bag stays with me."

"Not anymore," Ivo said quietly.

Emrys went rigid. The knife was back in his hand so fast it seemed to teleport.

"Listen to me." Ivo kept his voice level, his palms open. "Your left hand is dead tissue going septic. In two days, maybe three, you won't be able to hold that bag, let alone fight for it. Calving season opens soon — days, not weeks — and when it does, the river route downstream closes until next summer. There is no version of this where you carry it out alone. There is only a version where you freeze to death in a beautiful blue room with the truth in your lap, and next spring some warden finds you, and the people whose signatures are in that wallet spend the winter shredding everything." He leaned forward into the firelight. "I'm not asking you to give it up. I'm asking you to stop dying for it and start *delivering* it."

The knife didn't lower. Emrys breathed — three long, deliberate breaths, the breathing of a man doing arithmetic with his own corpse.

"And you?" he said finally, to Rosalind. "The blacklist. Your name in the files. If this comes out, there's an inquiry, and inquiries pull every thread. Yours included. You could walk away right now, and nobody in the world would ever connect Rosalind Fairweather to any of this. That's worth something. That might be worth everything, to a person who's spent six years being no one." His fever-bright eyes searched her face. "Why are you here?"

Rosalind was quiet for a long moment. Outside, far off, the glacier spoke again — a groan, a crack, the sound of a mass the size of a cathedral shifting in its sleep.

"Because I stayed silent once," she said at last, "to survive. And it worked. I survived. And I have been dead ever since." She reached out and closed Emrys's fingers back over the document wallet herself, gently, her hands steady. "Keep the bag. Keep the knife. But we move tomorrow at first light, all three of us, and we deliver it together — or none of us delivers anything."

Emrys looked from her to Ivo and back again, and whatever he saw passing between them — six years of grievance and love braided into something neither of them had named yet — made him exhale, long and slow, the breath of a man setting down a weight he'd carried too far alone.

"All right," he said. "Together." He fed the last of the manual to the fire, and the flames took the paper hungrily, and in the sudden flare of light his eyes went wide and fixed on the cave mouth behind them, on the blue distance beyond.

"But listen," he whispered. "Listen."

Beneath the wind, beneath the mutter of meltwater, faint but unmistakable, drifting down the glacier from the direction of the col:

Engines.
