## 10. What the Ice Remembers

The cold had stopped being an enemy days ago. That was the first thing Emrys understood about dying in the Karst Range: the cold did not kill you the way a man kills, quickly and with intent. It negotiated. It took your fingers one at a time, then your feet, then your opinions, until the last thing it asked for was your name and you gave it gladly, just to have something warm to trade.

He sat with his back against the blue wall of the ice cave, the satchel strapped across his chest like a child he refused to put down, and he listened to the two strangers argue in whispers near the entrance. The woman — Fairweather, though she hadn't offered the name; he'd read it off her gear, off the way she moved on ice like water moving over stone — wanted to know how many men had come up the glacier behind them. The warden wanted to know why Emrys had put a knife to her throat before letting either of them within ten feet.

Both good questions. Neither was the question.

The question was: *why are you still alive?* And Emrys had been turning that one over for eleven days, alone in the dark with his dead colleagues' names, and he still did not have an answer he liked.

* * *

It had started, as these things always did, with paperwork.

Juniper had found the blasting scars on the fourth day of the survey, beneath the tongue of the glacier where the meltwater cut its summer throat. She'd come back to camp with her notebook full of measurements and her eyes full of something brighter than excitement — the particular light of a junior surveyor who has just discovered that the map is wrong. Not incomplete. Wrong.

"There's a road under there," she'd said, crouched over the stove while the rest of them thawed their boots. "Not a road. A haulage tunnel. It's been driven through the bedrock below the ice line, and it's *new*, Da — Mr. Kessler — the shot holes are weeks old, maybe less. Somebody's mining the source."

Kessler had told her to log it and say nothing on the radio. Standard procedure for anomalies. But Emrys had watched the old man's face while Juniper talked, and what he saw there wasn't procedure. It was arithmetic. Kessler had spent thirty years in government surveys and he knew, the way old soldiers know incoming fire, when a discovery stops being science and starts being danger.

They took ore samples anyway. That was the job. Galena and something darker, something Kessler held up to his headlamp for a long time without speaking, and then wrapped in three layers of cloth and put in the document case with the seals. The samples went into the satchel along with the photographs Juniper had taken of the tunnel mouth, the chain-of-custody forms, the radio logs showing that someone on a company band had been broadcasting coordinates from inside the restricted zone for months — coordinates no licensed operation would need to hide.

"We'll carry it out ourselves," Kessler said. "No radio traffic. We walk it down to the valley office and hand it to Halvard personally."

Halvard. Emrys turned the name over now, in the cold, and hated himself a little more each time for not hearing it sooner. Halvard, the regional director who signed their permits. Halvard, who had personally approved the route that kept them on the glacier an extra four days. Halvard, whose signature would appear, in triplicate, in the satchel Emrys now guarded with a knife and nine frostbitten fingers.

They never made the valley. They made it as far as the flat ice below the serac field, the place the maps called the Tableland because it was the only kind ground in the whole upper range.

* * *

The men came at dusk, which was wrong twice over. Nobody traveled the glacier at dusk — the light went flat, crevasses vanished, the temperature fell like a dropped stone. Nobody came up-valley at all, not this late in the season, not toward the source. Emrys had been on watch and he'd seen them first: six figures in matching white shells, roped in pairs, moving with the unhurried competence of people who had walked this exact route before. He'd raised his binoculars and felt relief before he felt anything else, because they wore the high-visibility panels of a sanctioned party, and he'd already keyed the radio to hail them when he heard the voices.

Company band. Clean, encrypted, disciplined. Not smugglers' radios, patched together out of stolen parts and static. These were the short-range sets issued by the Northern Consolidated field offices, the ones with the serial numbers stamped under the battery plates, and Emrys knew that sound the way a man knows his own doorbell, because he'd carried one for six years before the survey service hired him away.

*"Tableland party, hold position. This is a compliance inspection."*

Compliance inspection. He would think about those two words for the rest of his life, however long the ice allowed it to be. Not *drop your weapons*. Not *you're trespassing*. Compliance. As if the murder that followed were a formality, a clause in a contract, something that could be filed.

Kessler stepped out of the tent with his hands visible and his voice level, the voice of a man who had spent decades believing that institutions ultimately protected the people who served them. "We're a chartered government survey. Our warrant is in the—"

The shot took him mid-sentence. Emrys never even saw which of the six fired. There was a sound like the glacier itself cracking, which is perhaps why his memory insisted afterward that the mountain had done it, that no man could make so casual a noise and mean so final a thing. Kessler went down without finishing the word *case*, and the snow around him bloomed dark, and the six white figures did not so much as change their rope spacing.

What happened after that happened fast, and Emrys's mind had spent eleven days refusing to play it at speed. He made it play slow instead, frame by frame, because slow was the only way he could carry it. Okafor reaching for the flare gun and not reaching it. Priya turning to shield Juniper — turning *away* from the shooters, toward the girl, an act of pure instinct that cost her the half-second she needed. Juniper running. Thank God, thank whatever runs the arithmetic of luck, Juniper running, because Emrys had grabbed her pack strap and hauled and she'd run, down into the blue shadow of the pressure ridge, while behind them the shots came in pairs, deliberate, spaced like a man counting.

Then the other sound. Worse than the shooting. The sound of gear being scattered — tents slashed, food tins rolled across the ice, boots thrown wide of camp, a compass hurled into a crevasse. He'd understood it only later, crouched in the dark with his hand clamped over Juniper's mouth: they were building a story. A wandering death. An inexperienced team that strayed off-route, lost its camp, scattered itself across the glacier. The kind of accident the range produced every few years and everyone sighed over and no one investigated, because the Karst Range ate the careless and that was simply what it was for.

They weren't hiding a crime. They were *authoring* one.

And Juniper — Juniper had broken from him. He would carry that too, the tearing of her sleeve out of his grip, her voice pitched thin with a fury he'd had no right to call foolishness: *"I'm going back for the satchel."* Because Kessler had drilled it into all of them — the documents go out or nobody goes out — and she was twenty-three years old and she believed it the way you believe things at twenty-three, absolutely, with your whole burning body. She'd gone back toward the camp and the white figures and the counting shots, and Emrys had gone the other way, down into the ice caves, because his legs had made the decision his heart refused to ratify.

He had not seen her die. He told himself that every hour, in the dark, like a prayer with no god attached. He had not seen it. Which meant some animal corner of him could keep believing she'd made it, that she'd slipped away the way he had, that somewhere between the Tableland and the lowlands a girl with a burned fury in her chest was walking out with the truth.

Eleven days had thinned that belief to a filament. But it held. It held because it had to.

* * *

The strangers were still talking at the cave mouth. The warden — Voss, the warrant card said, and Emrys had studied that card for a long time before lowering the knife, because a warrant card was exactly the kind of prop clever men would counterfeit — the warden was saying something about calving season, about routes closing, about days. The woman was saying something about the helicopter they'd both seen sweeping the glacier, and about how rescue helicopters did not usually fly armed search patterns in grid formation.

Emrys let them talk. He was watching the satchel.

It sat against his ribs, stiff with cold, holding everything. The ore samples in their triple-wrapped cloth. Juniper's photographs of the tunnel mouth, printed from her camera before the batteries died. The radio logs. The chain-of-custody pages in Kessler's small, furious handwriting. And the ledger — God, the ledger — the shipping manifest Juniper had photographed in the tunnel office while the others sampled the face, a manifest with receiving signatures from customs officers in two countries, mineral-rights filings bearing the countersignature of a deputy minister, and on the cover page, typed in the bland font of legitimate enterprise, the name of the man who had signed their permit to be here: **A. Halvard, Regional Director, Survey & Lands.**

Two countries. That was the part Emrys kept returning to, the part that made the whole thing feel less like a crime than a weather system. The signatures crossed the border like the river did, unbothered, finding the low ground. A deputy minister here. A director of concessions there. A police commissioner whose name appeared on a fuel requisition for a haulage tunnel that officially did not exist. You could not take a thing like that to a local constable. You could barely take it to a court. It had to go to someone above the reach of the signatures, and there were perhaps four such people in either country, and Emrys knew the name of exactly one of them: a federal prosecutor named Brandt, whom Kessler had once described, over cheap whisky in a valley bar, as *the only honest man in the building, which is why they keep him in the basement.*

Kessler had said it laughing. Kessler had also written the name in the front of the document case, in pencil, underlined twice.

So Emrys sat in the ice with a nation's worth of rot strapped to his chest and frostbite climbing his wrists, and he performed the only act left to him. He testified. He laid it out for the two strangers in a voice gone rusty from disuse — the ambush, the company radios, the staged camp, the names — and he watched their faces the whole time, because testimony is a transaction and he needed to see whether they could carry the weight.

The warden, Voss, went pale in a way that had nothing to do with the cold. Emrys recognized the look. It was the look of a man discovering that the institution on his shoulder was not a shield but a leash, and that the hand holding the leash belonged to the men in the white shells. The woman, Fairweather, listened differently. She listened like someone cataloguing exits. When he finished, she asked one question:

"The prosecutor. Brandt. Can you get the satchel to him yourself?"

"No," Emrys said. It was the truest word he'd spoken in eleven days. "My hands don't work anymore. Look at them." He held them up, the blackening fingertips, the swelling. "In four days I can't grip a rope. In six I'm a body you have to drag. Whatever happens to these papers next, it doesn't happen through me."

Silence, except for the glacier. The glacier was talking again — that deep, structural groan it made when the temperature swung, the sound of a thing the size of a country shifting its weight. Calving season. The woman and the warden exchanged a glance that contained an entire argument, conducted and concluded in the space of a breath.

Then Voss did something Emrys would remember longer than the gunfire. He took out his warrant card again — the real one, the brass-and-paper weight of the state — and set it down on the ice between them, face up, and left it there.

"Then it goes through us," he said.

Emrys looked at the card gleaming in the blue light, at the emblem of the service that had redacted his teammates' deaths before their bodies were cold, and he thought: *that is either the bravest thing I've ever seen or the last mistake any of us will make.*

Outside, far down the glacier, a sound rolled through the gorge like thunder with nowhere to go — the first great shelf of the season letting go, ice becoming sea, the mountain beginning to close its accounts.

Three days, maybe four. Emrys pulled the satchel tighter against his chest and started telling them where the documents would need to go, and who, and by which route — because if they were going to carry his testimony down off this ice, they were going to carry all of it, every name, every signature, straight into the teeth of the men who wrote them.

And the first name out of his mouth was Halvard's.
