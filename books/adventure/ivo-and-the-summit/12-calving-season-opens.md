## 12. Calving Season Opens

The sound woke him before the cold did.

It came through the ice like a word spoken in another room — a deep, groaning exhalation that started somewhere beneath the glacier's spine and rolled outward, gathering weight as it traveled, until the walls of the cave themselves seemed to shiver. Ivo lay still on his foam pad, one hand already finding the knife at his hip out of pure habit, and listened.

Then it came again. Louder this time. A crack — not a whisper now but a rifle shot of a crack, sharp and clean and enormous — followed by a long descending rumble that he felt in his sternum more than heard.

*Calving.*

He'd known it was coming. Every guide on the river knew the rhythm of the Karst glaciers the way farmers knew frost dates: the summer meltwater working its way down through the crevasse fields, lubricating the ice against bedrock, and then the terminus — that great grey-white wall at the glacier's snout — beginning to shed itself into the proglacial lake in slabs the size of churches. Calving season. The window that opened the high route for six weeks each year and then slammed it shut like a door with a broken hinge.

Except this year they were on the wrong side of the door when it began to swing.

Ivo sat up. His breath ghosted in the lamplight, and beside him Emrys stirred, or tried to. The surveyor had been sleeping badly for two days now, curled around his right foot the way a man curls around an injury he no longer has words for. The frostbite had gone from white to mottled grey-blue overnight, and this morning, when Rosalind had unwrapped the dressing, neither of them had said anything for a long moment. The toes were beyond saving. The question was how far up the foot the damage ran, and what that would mean for a man who still had to walk out of a mountain range whose exits were closing one by one.

"You heard it too," Emrys said. Not a question. His voice had developed a flatness over the past two days, a kind of economized calm that Ivo recognized from men who had decided fear was a luxury their bodies could no longer afford to fund.

"I heard it."

"That's the third one tonight." Emrys pushed himself up against the cave wall, wincing as his boot dragged. "First one was maybe two hours ago. They're getting closer together."

Ivo didn't answer immediately. He crawled to the mouth of the cave instead, where the ice curved away and opened onto the night, and looked out across the glacier.

The moon was three-quarters full and high enough to pour real light across the ice field, and what he saw made the hair rise along his forearms even before his mind finished processing it. The surface of the glacier, which had been a smooth silver plain when they'd first come up from the gorge, was now fractured everywhere — new crevasses opening like mouths, old ones yawning wider, meltwater channels running black and fast in the moonlight where two days ago there had been only shallow rills. And down-valley, perhaps four kilometers distant, the terminus wall stood pale against the dark lake, and even from here he could see the dust hanging above it, the fine glittering cloud of pulverized ice that hung in the air after a major calving event.

As he watched, a section of the wall let go.

He saw it happen the way you see lightning — first the flash of white as the face split, then the sound arriving seconds later, a thunderclap that echoed back and forth between the valley walls and went on echoing, and then the swell rising off the lake, catching the moonlight, a slow silver wave rolling out from the base of the glacier like something breathing.

"Rosalind," he called softly, without turning. "You need to see this."

She was already awake. He'd learned that about her again over these past days — that she slept the way she did everything else, lightly and completely, surfacing the instant the world changed pitch. She came to stand beside him at the cave mouth, close enough that her shoulder nearly touched his, and watched the wave flatten itself against the far shore.

"How many years since you've seen it start this early?" she asked.

"Never." He kept his voice level. "Melt season's been running hot for a month. The whole ablation zone is waterlogged. It's not just the terminus." He pointed up-glacier, toward the massif where the source lay hidden, where the illegal cuts scarred the ice below the summit ridge. "The whole upper glacier is going to start moving. Serac zones especially. Anything standing on ice that steep is going to be rearranged."

Rosalind was quiet for a moment. In the moonlight her face was all planes and shadow, and Ivo found himself cataloguing it the way he always did when he was afraid — the line of her jaw, the small scar above her eyebrow from a fall on the Vantner col eight years ago, the way her eyes moved across terrain the way other people read text.

"The route we came up," she said. "Through the lower icefall. How long?"

"Gone already, probably. Or going." He'd been turning the problem over in his head since midnight, and every answer was bad. "We came up the east lateral moraine while it was still frozen solid at night. That's done. Once the daily thaw starts, those moraine slopes shed rock all afternoon. And the serac field below the col—" He stopped.

"Say it."

"The serac field is the only way down that doesn't put us back past the checkpoint on the valley road. And serac fields during active calving are—" He searched for the honest word and couldn't find a softer one. "A lottery. You cross between collapses. Sometimes you win."

* * *

They held council by lamplight while the glacier spoke outside.

Emrys sat propped against his pack with the satchel of documents strapped across his chest — he hadn't taken it off in three days, and Ivo had stopped suggesting it. The man had watched eleven colleagues die and had spent weeks alone in the ice keeping those papers dry and hidden, and if strapping them to his body was what let him sleep, then the satchel could stay where it was.

Rosalind spread the survey map on the ground between them and weighted the corners with stones. Her hands were steady. Ivo noticed that her hands were always steady now, and remembered a time, years ago, when they hadn't been, and understood what it had cost her to make them so.

"Options," she said. "One: we wait here and hope the route stabilizes. Two: we go down the east side and take our chances with whatever patrol is watching the valley road. Three: the serac field, tonight, before the daily thaw opens the collapse windows."

"And option four," Emrys said quietly, "we stay, and the glacier closes over us, and whoever buried my team gets exactly what they wanted. No witnesses. No documents. A nice tidy story about a lost survey party, which is the story they wrote in the first place." He looked up, and in the lamplight his eyes were fever-bright. "I know which option I'm choosing."

Ivo studied him. The frostbite had climbed past the ankle; Rosalind had marked the edge of discoloration with a pen that morning, and when she checked it an hour ago the line had moved. The man needed a hospital, real warmth, real surgery. What he had was a cave, a camp stove, and two people with every reason to distrust each other except that they had run out of alternatives.

"Your foot," Ivo said. "Can you walk the serac field? Honestly. If the answer's no, we plan around a litter, and a litter changes everything."

Emrys looked at his own boot as if it belonged to someone else. "Honestly? I can walk today. Tomorrow I can't promise. The day after, you'll be carrying me." He lifted his chin. "So ask me again tomorrow, and the day after that, and hope you don't like the answers."

Rosalind's finger traced the map. "The serac field sits below the western col. From there it's the lateral moraine, then the river gorge below the resurgence. Four hours if the ice holds. Eight if we're carrying someone." She glanced up at Ivo. "What's the collapse pattern look like?"

"Cold nights hold it together. First sun starts the shedding, mid-morning is worst, and it doesn't settle again until after dark." He checked his watch. "It's 3:40. We have maybe five hours of stable ice. Six if we're lucky."

"Then we move at first light and we're through the field before the sun clears the eastern ridge."

"It's not that simple." Ivo hated saying it, hated the way both of them turned to him with the particular attention people give the person carrying the bad news. "Stable ice isn't safe ice. The whole field is under tension — you can hear it ticking. We go fast, roped, spaced wide, and nobody stops for anything short of a crevasse. And Emrys—" He met the surveyor's eyes. "If you go into a slot, the rope team has maybe thirty seconds to arrest you before your weight cuts the rope against the lip. Thirty seconds, and then we're choosing between you and ourselves."

"I understand," Emrys said. "I've read the accident reports too." A ghost of something crossed his face — not quite humor, but adjacent to it. "I intend to be very inconvenient to lose."

Outside, the glacier boomed again, closer this time, and all three of them flinched in the same instant. The lamp flame guttered. Somewhere above them, a mass of ice the size of a building had shrugged loose from whatever had held it for ten thousand years, and the mountain was still deciding where to put it.

* * *

They spent the remaining darkness preparing, and Ivo discovered that preparation was its own strange mercy — that when the clock becomes the antagonist, the work of moving is the only prayer available.

Rosalind re-roped the packs, redistributing weight toward the front of the carry order because Emrys would set the pace and the pace would be slow. She worked in silence, her movements quick and certain, and Ivo watched her hands and thought about the years he had spent watching them across campsites and kitchens and, once, across a courtroom gallery, and how he had never once managed to stop.

He checked the ropes himself, running every meter through his gloved fingers, feeling for the soft spots and core shots. Old habit. Old life. There had been a time when this — knots and anchors and the arithmetic of load-sharing — had been the whole of his moral universe, simple and clean, before a badge complicated it and a woman complicated it further. Now the badge was likely forfeit either way; he'd understood that since the night he'd shown Emrys his warrant card and chosen, deliberately, irrevocably, to become a witness rather than an officer. Whatever happened on this glacier, he would descend it as a man with no institution behind him. The strangeness of it wasn't grief, exactly. It was more like the feeling of taking off a pack after a long carry — the sudden lightness, and the rawness underneath where the straps had been.

Emrys packed the documents last, wrapping the satchel in his spare layer and cinching it tight. Then he paused, holding the bundle, and looked at the two of them across the lamplight.

"If I go into a crevasse," he said, "the satchel doesn't come with me. Rope cuts. Satchel goes to one of you. Promise me."

"We'll get you out with the satchel," Rosalind said.

"Promise me anyway."

Ivo met his eyes. "Promised. But it won't matter, because you're going to walk out of here."

Emrys held his gaze a moment longer, then nodded once, and Ivo understood that the nod was worth more than agreement — it was trust, extended by a man who had no surplus of it left, banked against a promise he might need to collect.

At 5:10, the sky behind the eastern ridge went from black to iron to a thin, cold rose. Rosalind killed the lamp. They stood at the cave mouth and looked out at the glacier, and the glacier looked back at them — vast, luminous, groaning, alive in the way that geological things are alive, which is slowly and without any interest whatsoever in human plans.

"One more thing," Rosalind said.

Ivo turned. She was looking at him with the expression he'd learned to dread and love in equal measure, the one that meant she was about to say something true.

"When we're on the ice," she said, "you give the orders. Not me. Your warrant card bought us Emrys's trust, but it's your rope craft that gets us down this mountain, and a rope team with two voices is half a team." She almost smiled. "Consider it a professional courtesy. From one blacklisted guide to a warden with no one left to report to."

"I haven't resigned yet."

"You will," she said gently. "Or they'll take it from you. Either way — today, on this ice, you're just the best mountaineer in the party. So lead."

The words settled into him strangely, part burden and part absolution. He looked out at the serac field below the western col — a jumbled mile of leaning ice towers, blue-shadowed in the dawn, ticking and settling as the night's cold began its losing argument with the sun — and felt the old clarity rise in him, the narrow bright channel of attention that terrain always demanded and sometimes granted.

"All right," he said. "Rope's twenty meters between us. Emrys in the middle, Rosalind on anchor, me on point. We move on my count, we stop on my fist, and nobody — *nobody* — stands under anything that leans." He shouldered his pack and clipped into the line. "We have five hours before the sun turns that field into a demolition site. Let's spend them well."

They stepped out onto the glacier together, three small figures on a mile of groaning ice, and behind them the terminus calved again — a sound like the world clearing its throat — and ahead, at the mouth of the serac field, the first tower of the morning leaned, sighed, and began to fall.
