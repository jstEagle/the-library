## 13. The Serac Field

The glacier gave them one clear hour of light, and then it began to kill them.

Rosalind saw it first as a sound — a deep, tectonic groan rolling up from beneath the ice, the kind of noise that made her back teeth ache and her hands remember things they had no business remembering. She stopped at the lip of the moraine and looked up into the serac field, and her stomach dropped through the soles of her boots.

It was worse than she remembered. Worse than anything the old guidebooks described, because guidebooks were written by people who came back. The ice towers stood above them in ranks, fifty, eighty feet tall, leaning against each other like drunks holding each other upright, blue-green in the early light with meltwater streaming down their flanks in bright silver threads. Between them ran a maze of crevasses, some bridged with snow, some not bridged at all, just black slits breathing cold air upward. And everywhere, everywhere, the sound of water moving under pressure, under the ice, inside it — the glacier's own pulse, quickening.

"Calving season," Emrys said behind her. His voice was thin. Three days of frostbite and fever had hollowed him out until he looked like something carved from driftwood, but his eyes were still sharp, still counting. "It's starting."

"It started yesterday," Rosalind said. "We're late to the funeral." She turned to look at Ivo. He stood slightly downhill of her, pack straps creaking, face turned up toward the towers with the expression she knew too well — the warden's expression, cataloguing hazard, measuring risk, pretending the numbers came out in their favor. "There's no way around. The lateral moraine ends at that headwall. We go through or we go home, and home is a hundred miles of river we can't take with Emrys in this state."

"I know," Ivo said.

"I wasn't asking permission."

"I know that too." Something moved at the corner of his mouth — not quite a smile, but the ghost of where one used to live. "You never did."

She should have hated him for that. For years she had hated him for exactly that, for the way he could stand in front of her with all his careful competence and his quiet certainty and make her feel like a girl playing at mountains. But standing here now, with the ice groaning overhead and the satchel of documents pressing against Emrys's chest like a second heart, all she felt was the old familiar thing underneath the hate, the thing that had never actually died no matter how hard they'd both tried to bury it under grievance and distance and six years of silence.

Love doesn't drown that easy. It just holds its breath.

"We rope up," she said, and heard her voice go flat and professional, the guide's voice, the voice that had kept clients alive on four continents before the Karst Range took it away from her. "Thirty meters between us. Emrys in the middle. If anyone goes in a slot, everyone drops and holds — nobody runs toward the hole, nobody looks down. You hold and you wait and you pray the bridge holds long enough for me to set an anchor." She looked at Ivo. "You remember crevasse rescue?"

"I remember everything," he said quietly, and she couldn't tell if he meant the technique or the years, and decided it didn't matter.

* * *

The first hour was almost kind.

They moved through the outer towers where the ice was older, more settled, following a line Rosalind picked by instinct and old habit — always along the concave faces, never beneath overhangs, testing every snow bridge with the probe before trusting it. The sun climbed and the meltwater threads grew fatter, trickling into the crevasses with a sound like whispering, and Rosalind thought: *the Gorge of Whispers, again, the whole mountain whispers when it's dying.* She put the thought away. Fear was a tool; you used it or it used you.

Emrys moved badly. His left foot was a ruin inside its wrapping, and twice he stumbled on ground that posed no difficulty at all, catching himself on his pole with a grunt. Each time, Rosalind shortened his section of rope without comment, and each time she caught Ivo watching her do it with an expression she refused to interpret.

The trouble came in the third hour, in the deep middle of the field, where the towers grew taller and leaned closer and the light turned submarine, green-blue, filtered through forty feet of ice overhead.

The snow bridge looked solid. It looked like every other snow bridge, a smooth white lens draped across a gap maybe four feet wide. Rosalind probed it twice, three times, felt the firm resistance of packed snow, and stepped onto it with her weight forward, the way you're supposed to, the way she'd taught a hundred clients.

The world opened beneath her.

There was no dramatic crack, no warning roar. The bridge simply ceased — one instant solid, the next nothing, and she was falling into cold so total it felt like falling into iron. Her right arm shot out on pure animal reflex and her axe bit something, a lip of blue ice, and held, and suddenly she was hanging in the throat of a crevasse with her boots swinging over a darkness that went down further than her headlamp could reach, and the rope at her waist snapped tight as Ivo threw himself flat.

"Hold!" she shouted, hearing her voice bounce off the ice walls below her. "I'm on the axe — hold!"

Above her, distorted by the narrow slot of sky, Ivo's face appeared at the lip. She saw him brace his boots against the edge, saw the rope run through his gloved hands, saw him lean back with his whole body and take her weight the way he'd taken it a thousand times on a thousand smaller mountains, back when they were young and stupid and invincible.

"I've got you," he said. His voice was steady. Of course it was steady. Ivo Voss's voice had been steady through avalanches and courtrooms and the worst night of both their lives. "Don't move your feet. Let the axe do the work. I'm going to walk it back, slow, and you're going to climb while I take the slack. Can you kick?"

She looked down. Below her boots the crevasse narrowed into a blue-black funnel, and from somewhere deep in it came the sound of running water, patient and enormous.

"I can kick," she said.

"Then kick."

She climbed. It was ugly and it was slow and her arms burned and somewhere in the middle of it her boot punched through a rotten shelf and she swung free with nothing but the axe and the rope and Ivo's voice coming down to her, level and close, *left foot higher, there, now weight it, good, again* — the same words, the exact same cadence, he'd used on the north face of Mount Ceren six years ago, back before everything, and she found herself climbing toward the sound of his voice the way a drowning woman climbs toward light.

Her chest came over the lip. Her knee. Her whole self, sprawled onto the snow, shaking, alive.

For a moment neither of them moved. He knelt beside her, one hand still locked on the rope, breathing hard, and she lay with her cheek against the snow and looked at him from a distance of eighteen inches and saw that his hands were trembling. Not his voice. His hands.

"You cut the rope loose fast," she said. Meaningless words. Filler. Her heart was slamming so hard she could hear it in her ears.

"You taught me the anchor drill," he said. "Summer of '19. You said someday someone's life would hang on whether I did it boring and perfect." He swallowed. "I did it boring and perfect."

Something cracked in her chest that had nothing to do with broken ribs. She wanted to say a dozen things — about the client, about the inquiry, about the six years of silence in which she'd composed a hundred letters and sent none of them — but Emrys was watching them from ten meters back with the careful blankness of a man who has seen enough death to recognize love when it limps past him, and the mountain was not finished with them.

"Move," Rosalind said, and got up.

* * *

An hour later, she paid the debt back.

It happened fast, the way these things do — no ceremony, no warning, just the mountain reaching out with one casual finger. They were traversing beneath a leaning tower the size of a church, Emrys in the middle of the rope team, when the tower spoke. One word, one syllable, a *crack* like the sky breaking, and Rosalind looked up and saw the serac shrug off a slab the size of a truck.

"MOVE!"

She didn't think. Later she would reconstruct it — the slab's fall line, Emrys's position, the two seconds available — but in the moment there was only her body deciding without her. She hit him at the waist, a full tackle, driving them both sideways off the trail and down the slope as the ice came down where he'd been standing. It missed them by less than a meter. The shockwave slapped her back like a physical hand, and shrapnel — chunks of ice hard as masonry — stung her neck and shoulders through her jacket, and then it was over, the dust settling, the mountain already indifferent again.

Emrys lay beneath her, staring up, white-faced, his breath smoking in ragged bursts.

"You pushed me," he said finally.

"You were in the way."

He laughed — a cracked, disbelieving sound that turned into coughing. When it passed, he looked at her with those sharp counting eyes, and whatever arithmetic he'd been doing since the ice cave, it came out different now. "Ivo told me what you gave up to come find us. What they took from you." His hand closed briefly around her wrist, frostbitten fingers surprisingly strong. "Nobody pays that price for strangers."

"You're not strangers," Rosalind said. "You're the job." She hauled him up. "And the job isn't done."

But when she turned, she saw Ivo looking at her across the settling dust, and the look on his face undid her more than the fall had. It was the look of a man watching something he'd believed buried stand up out of its grave. Six years of case files and redacted reports and the careful professional distance he'd wrapped around her memory like gauze — all of it stripped away in two seconds of falling ice, and what remained was just Ivo, twenty-nine years old again on the Ceren glacier, looking at her like she was the truest thing he knew.

"You didn't hesitate," he said.

"There wasn't time to hesitate."

"There never is." He crossed to her, and his glove touched her jaw, brief and rough and unbearably gentle, brushing ice shrapnel from her skin. "That's the whole secret, isn't it? There never is."

She leaned into his hand for one second. One. Then the mountain groaned again, deeper, and she stepped back into being the guide, because the guide was the only version of herself that could get them all off this ice alive.

* * *

The backtrack route died at four in the afternoon.

They heard it before they understood it — a rumble behind them, long and rolling, and Rosalind turned in time to see the far side of the serac field rearrange itself. A whole rank of towers, the ones they'd threaded through that morning, the ones that formed the only line back toward the ice caves, went down together in a slow-motion avalanche of blue-white ruin. The sound arrived seconds later, a thunderclap that she felt in her sternum, and the dust cloud rose five hundred feet into the windless air and hung there like a curtain falling on a stage.

Nobody said anything for a long time. The message didn't need saying. Behind them was now a plain of shattered blocks and open crevasses that no human would cross in a lifetime of trying. The route back to shelter, back to the caves, back to any possibility of waiting out the season — gone. Erased. The glacier had sealed its own history under a hundred tons of fresh collapse.

"Forward's the only door left," Ivo said at last.

"Forward was always the only door," Rosalind said. "That's how mountains work. That's how everything works." She checked the light — two hours, maybe three, before the temperature dropped and the ice turned to glass underfoot. Ahead, past the last of the towers, the glacier bent eastward toward the descent col, and beyond the col, faint in the haze, the dark line of the lower moraine where the going got easier and the going got dangerous in entirely new ways.

Emrys sank onto a boulder of ice, breathing hard, and pulled the satchel around to check it — the gesture had become reflexive, a man checking his own heartbeat. "How far to the tree line?"

"Two days. Three, the way we're moving." Rosalind crouched beside him, unshouldering her water bottle. "And there's something you both need to hear before we're down there." She looked from Emrys's ruined face to Ivo's set one, and made herself say it plainly, because they deserved plain speech after today. "The moraine funnels into the valley mouth. One road, one trail, one way out — and anyone who wants us won't need to search anymore. They'll just have to wait."

Ivo nodded slowly. "A bottleneck."

"A killing jar," Rosalind said. "So tonight we rest, tomorrow we cross the col, and the day after—" She stopped. Down-valley, tiny with distance, something flickered at the edge of vision. She raised her binoculars, braced her elbows on her knees, and swept the lower slopes.

The moraine. A figure on the moraine, small and dark and deliberate, walking a straight line upslope. Then a second figure. Then, glinting in the low sun, a brief orange flash that might have been glass, might have been a lens, might have been—

Might have been a scope.

"They're not waiting at the bottom," Rosalind said quietly, passing the binoculars to Ivo. "They're coming up to meet us."

Ivo took the glasses. She watched his shoulders change as he found them, watched three days of warden's caution crystallize into something harder. Beside them, Emrys went very still, his hand flattening protectively over the satchel.

"How long?" Ivo asked.

"At their pace, with our lead?" Rosalind looked west, where the sun was already bleeding into the col. "We have until dark to become very difficult to find."
