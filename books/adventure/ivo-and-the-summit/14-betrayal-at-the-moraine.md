## 14. Betrayal at the Moraine

Emrys saw the man's face before he saw the uniform.

That was the thing about nearly dying — it burned certain images into you at a depth no frost could reach. He had spent eleven days in the ice caves rehearsing his own death, and in all that rehearsal he had never once imagined that the face of Corporal Dane Halloway would come back to him on a grey moraine under a sky the color of dishwater, attached to a man wearing a ranger's badge and a rescue-orange armband, waving cheerfully.

"Hello the party!" the voice carried up the scree. "Park service! You're found!"

* * *

They had been moving since first light, three figures strung along the lateral moraine like beads on a frayed wire. Ivo in front, favoring nothing yet, though Emrys had watched him press a hand to his ribs every time they stopped and had said nothing about it. Rosalind behind him with the satchel strapped across her chest like a bandolier, her eyes doing that constant sweep of the high ground that Emrys was beginning to understand was not paranoia but literacy — she read mountains the way he read ledgers, for what was written between the lines.

And Emrys last, because Rosalind had insisted, because the documents rode against her body and his job now was simply to stay upright and stay alive long enough to testify. His left hand was wrapped to the wrist in Ivo's spare sock and two meters of climbing tape, the frostbite having turned his fingers into something he preferred not to look at. The pain came and went in tides. Mostly it had settled into a deep, throbbing absence, which the warden had told him grimly was worse than pain.

The moraine was a highway of broken stone, a ridge of glacier-vomit running parallel to the ice below — grey rock ground to gravel, house-sized boulders balanced on beds of smaller ones, everything loose, everything talking to itself as they walked. Above them the serac field they'd crossed yesterday hung like a row of rotten teeth. Below, the glacier's surface was a chaos of pressure ridges and melt channels, blue-shadowed, groaning at intervals like an old ship.

They were maybe four hours from the tree line. Four hours from cover, from water that didn't taste of rock flour, from the long descent where the radio might reach a tower that wasn't owned by the wrong people.

Then the hail came, and Emrys's blood went to ice that had nothing to do with altitude.

* * *

He knew the voice first. That was the terrible part — before the face, before the orange armband, he knew the *voice*. A particular flat drawl, unhurried, the voice of a man who had given orders beside a burning tent while surveyors' equipment was scattered across the snow to fake a wandering death. Emrys had lain in a melt channel not six meters away, submerged to his chin in slush water, listening to that voice discuss logistics. *Dump the packs here. Take the sat phone. Leave one tent standing, it reads better.* And then, closer, almost conversational, as boots crunched past his hiding place: *Check the channel anyway. Thorough is thorough.*

He had watched Halloway's shadow fall across the water. He had held his breath until his vision went to sparks.

Now that same shadow came up the moraine trail with four men behind it, dressed in park service grey, rifles slung, smiling.

"They're rangers," Ivo said, lifting a hand in answer to the wave. Relief cracked his voice open. "Thank God. That's Callum Reyes leading — no, wait." He squinted. "I don't know the lead man."

"I do," Emrys heard himself say.

The words came out of him small and strange, as if someone else had spoken through his mouth. Everything happened very fast after that. Later he would try to reconstruct it and find only fragments: the way Rosalind's head snapped toward him; the way the lead figure's smile did not waver but his stride did, a half-beat hitch, a man recalculating; the way Emrys's legs made the decision before his mind caught up, turning him downslope toward the boulder field with the blind momentum of an animal.

"It's him," he gasped. "The drawl — the one who ran the ambush — he's wearing their—"

"Down!" Rosalind shouted, and the world went to noise.

* * *

The first shot hit the boulder above Ivo's head and spat stone fragments across his neck like thrown gravel. He dropped behind a slab of gneiss the size of a truck cab, heart slamming, and understood with a clarity that felt like falling: the man in the ranger jacket had fired first. Had fired at *him*, a warden, at a party waving back, without one word of challenge. Every protocol he had sworn by, inverted in a single muzzle flash.

"Emrys!" Rosalind was dragging the surveyor down behind a fallen block, her body curved over his like a shield. "Stay down and stay *down*."

Ivo risked the edge of cover. Five figures among the rocks below, spreading out with a competence that no seasonal ranger patrol possessed — bounding overwatch, covering arcs, weapons up. The lead man had shed the friendly wave entirely. He was gesturing with two fingers, sending his people wide to flank.

"Rangers don't shoot first!" Ivo roared across the moraine. "This is Warden Voss! Badge 4471! Stand down and identify!"

The answer was a burst of rounds that chewed the top edge of his cover into powder. Not warning shots. Not panic fire. Deliberate, aimed suppression, the kind of shooting that intended to keep heads down until the flanking men arrived to finish the work at close range.

*I know these tactics,* he thought, sick. *I taught them.*

* * *

Crouched in the lee of the boulder, Emrys shook so hard his teeth chattered, and it wasn't the cold. He had done this. His bolt, his recognition, his refusal to trust the friendly faces — if he had been wrong, if these were real rangers, then he had just murdered three innocent people's chance at rescue. But the firing didn't sound like confusion. It sounded like procedure. And procedure meant Halloway had reported them already — *hostiles on the moraine, armed, resist arrest* — had written the story before the first shot, so that whatever happened here would arrive in the world pre-explained. Dead fugitives who had fired on park personnel. Self-defense. Case closed twice over.

"They'll flank left," Rosalind said beside him, low and rapid, loading as she spoke. Her hands were steady. He marveled at it the way you marvel at anything impossible. "There's a gully behind us, runs up to the crest of the moraine. We go over the top and down the other side onto the glacier margin. They won't follow fast — too much loose rock, they'll have to pick their line."

"And you know this how?" Ivo called from his position, swapping magazines.

"Because I poached this range for six years and never got caught." No fear in it. Almost a smile. "Emrys. Look at me." He looked. Her eyes were dark and absolutely calm. "When we move, you go second. I go last. Whatever you hear behind you, you do not stop, you do not turn around. The satchel goes over that crest or none of this mattered. Understood?"

"The documents—" he started.

"Are coming with us, yes. That's the whole point of you." Now there was the ghost of a grin, fierce and brief. "No offense."

"None taken," Emrys whispered, and discovered he was grinning back, madly, terribly, because terror at this altitude curdles quickly into laughter.

* * *

Ivo went first anyway. Of course he did. He broke from cover on Rosalind's signal and laid three careful shots into the rocks above the flanking pair — not to hit, just to bend their line, buy seconds — then sprinted a low, crouched forty meters through the boulder field with rounds snapping past him like angry birds. He reached the gully mouth and waved them on.

Emrys ran. His lungs were knives, his taped hand useless for balance, and twice he went down on the sliding scree and was hauled up by Rosalind's grip on his collar without her ever quite stopping. Behind them the gunfire continued in disciplined pairs — suppress, move, suppress — and once he heard a man shout, clear across the wind: *"Warden down! Suspect fire continues!"*

*Liar,* Emrys thought, scrambling. *Liar, liar, writing your report while you shoot.*

Then Ivo staggered.

It happened at the crest, in the open, silhouetted against the sky exactly the way no one trained as he was should ever be. Later Emrys understood: Ivo had turned back. Had turned back to make sure the slowest of them — the half-frozen clerk with the ruined hand — had made the gully, and in that half-second of turned attention a round had found him. High in the back, near the shoulder blade, punching through with a sound like a heavy book slapped shut.

Ivo went down onto the crest rocks and Emrys, against every instruction, against the screaming logic of survival, stopped.

"No!" Rosalind's voice tore across the moraine. She reached Ivo first, dragged him by both arms over the lip of the crest as another round sparked off stone where his head had been. Emrys grabbed the warden's harness and pulled, and together they hauled him down the far side, behind the shoulder of the moraine, out of the line of fire, into the sudden windless quiet of the glacier margin.

* * *

He was conscious. That was the first mercy. He lay on his side in the rock dust, breathing in short hard pulls, and when Rosalind cut away the jacket the wound was a ragged furrow high on his back, ugly and bleeding freely but — she pressed with both thumbs, expert, merciless, and Ivo made a sound he clearly hated making — not deep. Through-and-through's edge. Muscle and misery, not lung, not spine.

"Lucky," she said, and her voice cracked on the word, the first crack Emrys had ever seen in her. "You stupid, lucky man."

"Felt it," Ivo managed, "in my professional opinion... as a lucky stupid man." Blood frothed faintly at the corner of his mouth when he laughed, and Rosalind's face went white until he coughed and said, "Bit my tongue. Just the tongue."

Above them, on the crest, voices. The patrol had reached the top and did not follow — Emrys could hear why, the constant mutter and slide of the unstable slope, and beneath it the deeper argument of the seracs. But he could also hear the radio traffic, thin and metallic on the wind, and he made himself listen, because listening was the one weapon he had left.

"—suspects armed, multiple shooters, refused to identify—"

"—warden wounded by suspect fire, repeat, suspect fire—"

"—requesting intercept teams at the valley mouth, treat as hostile—"

Hostile. The word settled over Emrys like snowfall. In one afternoon, on paper, in the record, they had become the thing hunting them. Three survivors of a massacre, transformed by a radio call into the killers. He thought of Juniper's notebook in the satchel, of the last entry — *they know we know* — and understood finally, completely, what those five words had cost. This was how it worked. Not silence. Narrative. You didn't disappear the truth; you buried it under a better story, and let the mountain do the rest.

Halloway's voice came last, carrying oddly down the wind, unhurried, flat, familiar as a scar: "Break off. They're going for the river. We'll be waiting at the bottom."

* * *

They moved within the hour, because staying still was dying slowly. Rosalind rigged a sling from rope and foam pad and Ivo, gray-faced, furious, allowed himself exactly ten minutes of being carried before insisting on walking with Emrys's shoulder under his good arm. So they went down the glacier margin as a three-legged creature, warden and witness lashed together, and Rosalind ahead with the satchel and the rifle and the weight of all of them, cutting a line toward the dark smear of the tree line.

The sun was going down behind the Karst spires, filling the icefield with a light like struck brass, beautiful and indifferent. Emrys kept glancing back at the moraine crest, empty now, and hearing again the easy drawl: *We'll be waiting at the bottom.*

Every route down ended at the river. Every road, every trail, every valley mouth — and the valley mouths would be sealed by nightfall, by men with badges and a story already written.

"We can't go down any of the ways they expect," he said.

Rosalind didn't break stride. She was looking at the river far below, a silver thread going dark with evening, and Emrys watched her arrive at the same conclusion he was only circling.

"No," she said quietly. "We can't. Which means we go where the river goes when it disappears."

Ivo, half-conscious against Emrys's shoulder, lifted his head. "The gorge," he murmured. "The underground section. Nobody in their right mind—"

"Exactly," said Rosalind Fairweather, and turned downslope toward the dark.
