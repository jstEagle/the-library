## 15. The Long Dark

The coal seam opened in the hillside like a wound that had never been stitched shut — a black mouth three meters wide, breathing cold air outward, and Rosalind found it the way she'd found everything worth finding in the Karst: by listening. The wind changed note against the rock above them, a hollow tone where there should have been stone, and she pressed her palm to the slope and felt the faint exhalation of an underground passage running back into the mountain's ribs.

"Here," she said, and Ivo, who had stopped arguing with her somewhere on the moraine, simply nodded and kept walking.

He was worse. She had known it for two hours without saying it, the way you know weather is turning before the first drop falls. He'd stopped complaining about the cold, which was the worst sign of all. His left side rode high, favoring the flank where the patrol's bullet had torn through below his ribs — a through-and-through, she'd told herself on the ridge, clean entry, clean exit, nothing vital touched. But the exit wound had wept all afternoon, soaking through the field dressing she'd slapped over it at the treeline, and now the skin around it had gone a color she didn't like, a flushed, feverish pink climbing toward his armpit along the line of his lymph nodes.

Emrys went into the seam first with the headlamp, sweeping the beam across walls of glossy black rock veined with pyrite that threw back sparks of fool's gold. The tunnel ran twenty meters deep before narrowing to rubble — old workings, abandoned so long the timber props had rotted to soft brown skeletons. But the first ten meters were dry, floored with fine coal dust that swallowed their bootprints, and the entrance could be blocked from inside with two good stones.

"It'll do," Emrys said, and his voice cracked on the second word, and Rosalind realized he hadn't spoken aloud since the firefight except to count their paces.

They built the wall. They dragged Ivo's pack inside. And then the light came down over the mountains outside, that brutal alpine dusk that gives you fifteen minutes between day and dark, and Rosalind stood at the seam's mouth watching the last sun gild the serac field they'd crossed — watching the place where Ivo had caught her wrist as the cornice let go beneath her, where she had hauled Emrys out of the crevasse by his collar while the ice groaned around them like something dreaming — and she thought: *we are not getting off this mountain by being strong.*

Then she turned around and went in to do the work.

* * *

The kit came down to this: half a roll of gauze, one pressure bandage already used, a bottle of iodine gone the color of strong tea, four tablets of broad-spectrum antibiotic she'd carried for years against exactly this kind of arithmetic, a sewing needle sterilized over flame, and a strip of moleskin. She laid it all out on Ivo's open pack like instruments on a tray, and the smallness of it made her throat tighten. Twenty years ago she would have had a proper med kit, a satellite phone, a helicopter on call. Twenty years ago she'd been the best guide in the range, and everyone had said so, right up until the day they said other things.

"Fever's what, thirty-eight-five?" Ivo asked from where he lay propped against the coal face, jacket rolled under his head.

"Thirty-eight-nine if you're lucky."

"That's not lucky."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't."

She cut his shirt away with her knife, careful of the skin, and got her first real look at the wound by lamplight. The entry was a neat gray pit below the tenth rib. The exit was uglier — ragged, gaping wider than it should have, the flesh around it swollen and hot and weeping a thin yellow fluid that smelled wrong, sweetish, the smell of tissue giving up its argument. Infection had gotten in during those hours on the moraine when they'd run and crawled and run again, sweat and coal dust and whatever lived in the wounds of old mines working its way deep.

"You need to drain it," Ivo said. He'd seen enough mountain medicine to know. He said it evenly, the way he reported conditions on his incident forms, and she wanted to hit him for it, for being brave at her in that flat warden's voice, as if courage were just another form to file.

"I know."

"And then the antibiotics."

"Yes."

"Rosalind." He caught her hand before she could pick up the iodine. His fingers were dry and burning. "Say it plain. What are my odds?"

She looked at him. Sixteen years of looking away from this man, sixteen years of a whole geography of avoidance mapped between them, and here in a hole in a mountain with the light failing she couldn't find a single direction left to look but at him.

"If we get you down to the resurgence by tomorrow night, and the antibiotics hold, you'll be sick and miserable and alive." Her voice held. She made it hold. "If the infection goes systemic before then, the odds stop being mine to give."

He nodded slowly. "All right," he said. "Do what you have to."

So she did. She drained the abscess with the needle and the heel of her hand, pressing the edges of the wound until the trapped fluid ran clear-then-cloudy onto the gauze, and Ivo made no sound at all except once, low in his chest, a sound like a door being closed very carefully. She flooded the wound with iodine and packed it and wrapped it, and gave him two tablets with melted snow, and by the end her hands were shaking and his were steady, which seemed entirely the wrong way round.

Emrys kept watch at the seam's mouth, a silhouette against the starlight, the satchel of documents strapped across his body even sitting down. He didn't turn around. He gave them the darkness the way you give strangers a room.

* * *

The fever climbed through the first watch of the night. Rosalind sat with her back against the coal face and Ivo's head in her lap because there was nowhere else to put him, and she monitored him the old way, with knuckles on his sternum and lips near his temple, counting breaths, reading heat. Outside, the glacier spoke in its sleep — long compressions and reports, the calving season opening its throat somewhere upvalley — and each time the ice boomed, Emrys flinched at his post, and each time, Rosalind's hand found Ivo's shoulder and pressed, grounding herself on the fact of him.

Around midnight by her watch, he began to talk.

Not delirium, quite. A borderland talk, lucid and unguarded, the way men talk when pain has burned away everything decorative.

"The file," he said. "Your file. When I pulled the search records, your name wasn't redacted. It was *removed*. Someone went through with a blade, physically. You could see the knife marks in the paper." His eyes were half-open, fixed on the lamplight. "I kept thinking — who has the power to unwrite a person? Not to accuse her. To erase her. And then Emrys showed us those documents, and I understood. Same pen. Same hand. They wrote you out sixteen years ago because you were standing too close to something, and they've been writing people out ever since."

"Ivo—"

"I'm not asking." His hand found hers in the dark, clumsy with fever. "I'm telling you what I know. You don't owe me the story. But I want you to know I already believe you."

And that — that undid her more than the wound had undone him. Because she had rehearsed confessions for sixteen years, in showers, on rope lengths, on the long gray ferry crossings of her exile, and every rehearsal had ended with the listener's face changing, hardening, the small recalculation of a person deciding what you were. She had built her whole life on never having to see that face again.

But he was burning, and the night was long, and the truth was the only medicine she had left that wasn't in the kit.

"His name was Teodor Vasch," she said.

Emrys's silhouette went very still at the mouth of the seam.

"He was fifty-one. A geologist. Hired me privately, cash, no outfitter middleman — which should have been my first warning, and wasn't, because the money was good and my mother's roof needed replacing and I was twenty-six and stupid in the specific way that competent people are stupid, which is to say I thought competence covered everything." She heard her own voice go flat, reportorial, and let it. Flat was how you carried it. "He wanted the upper Karst, past the survey markers, into the closed sector. Said he had a research permit. He did have a permit — I checked later, after, when it mattered — but it was for the lower valley. Forged. Or rather, not forged. *Amended.* By someone with access to the registry."

Ivo's breathing had slowed, sharpened. Listening through the fever.

"We camped at the source on the third night. And in the morning there were other men at the glacier. Four of them, company radios, matching gear, no permits displayed. Teodor went out to meet them alone — told me to stay with the tents, and God help me, I stayed, because he was the client and I was the guide and that's the catechism they teach you. I heard voices. Then I heard one shot." Her hand had stopped moving on his hair; she made it move again. "One. Not a fight. An execution is quiet, Rosalind. Nobody tells you that. It's quieter than anything."

"What did you do?" Ivo asked softly.

"What any sensible person does when four armed men are between her and the only route down." Bitterness, then, at her own younger self. "Nothing. I broke camp at dusk, I went down the east flank alone, and I told myself I'd bring back the police. And when I reached the valley — the police were already looking for me. Not for Teodor. For me. The story was already written: guide takes client into closed ground, client dies, guide flees. My permit was invalid, they said. My account was self-serving, they said. And the men who'd been at the glacier were never in any report at all, because the officer who took my statement worked for the same district office that had amended Teodor's permit, and I understood — standing there in that little room with the tape recorder running — that I was being given a choice they thought was generous. Sign the statement as written, accept the ban, disappear quietly. Or keep insisting on four men with company radios, and become a liar officially, and maybe worse than a liar."

"You signed," Emrys said from the dark. Not a question. His voice had a scraped, hollow quality, like wind through the seam itself.

"I signed." The word cost her; she paid it. "Sixteen years I've told myself it was survival, and it was, and it was also cowardice, and both things are true at once and neither one cancels the other. Teodor has a daughter in Vellmark who believes her father died lost on the ice because of me. I let her believe it. I let *everyone* believe it, because the alternative was a grave next to his and a story no one would print." She looked down at Ivo's face, fever-bright, watching her. "That's the confession. Not that I ran. That I stayed silent. That I chose my own skin for sixteen years and called it wisdom."

The glacier boomed, far off, a sound like the mountain clearing its throat.

Ivo was silent a long moment. When he spoke, his voice was thick but level. "You know what I did, the week after they blacklisted you? I filed a supplementary report. Full detail. Four men, company radios, the amended permit. I was twenty-four and I knew everything." A breath that might have been a laugh. "It went into a drawer. I checked, years later. The drawer was empty. So don't stand there being the only coward on this mountain, Rosalind, because the badge I've been hiding behind is made of the same silence."

"That's not the same."

"It's exactly the same. I just got paid better for mine." His hand tightened on hers. "Here's what I actually think, and you can throw me out of your lap for saying it. You survived. That's not a crime. You carried it, alone, for sixteen years, and you're still carrying it up a glacier toward the men who did it, instead of running the other direction like any rational person. If that's cowardice, then I've never met courage and wouldn't recognize it."

She hadn't known she was crying until the tear fell on his forehead and he didn't wipe it away, just closed his eyes, accepting it like rain.

"Forgive me," she said. "Not for the signing. For the years. For letting you think—" Her voice failed and restarted. "For letting you think you'd loved someone who killed a man."

"I never thought that." Simple. Final. "Not one day. Ask anyone. I was insufferable about it."

A sound escaped her that was half laugh, half sob, and Emrys turned his head slightly at it, and turned back to his watch, and pretended — courteously, completely — that he had heard nothing at all.

* * *

Toward morning the fever broke. She felt it happen under her hands, the heat sliding out of him like a tide going out, leaving damp and chill and the particular gray exhaustion of a body that has fought something to a draw. She got broth into him from the melting snow and the last of the soup cubes, and he drank it obediently, and slept — real sleep, not fever sleep — for two hours with the dawn graying the seam's mouth behind Emrys's shoulders.

Rosalind didn't sleep. She sat in the coal dark and watched the light come, and did the arithmetic she'd been avoiding. One dose of antibiotics left after today. Two days' travel to the resurgence, if Ivo could walk, and he couldn't, not yet, not fast. The route downriver would close within days — maybe hours — when the calving reached the gorge, and the 'rescue' teams would sweep again, and Emrys's frostbitten fingers were going black at the tips no matter how bravely he curled them into his palms.

And somewhere below them, at the valley mouth, the men with company radios were waiting for the mountain to hand them three bodies and a satchel.

Ivo woke as the light strengthened, and she saw him arrive at the same sum, because his eyes went straight to hers with the question in them before he'd fully opened them.

"How do you feel?" she asked.

"Like I was shot." He flexed his bandaged side, winced, tested it the way he tested river ice — weight, then more weight. "But it's holding. The packing held." He pushed himself up on one elbow, and though the motion cost him visibly, he completed it, and held it. "Rosalind. The river comes out of the mountain at the resurgence, below the gorge. Every map agrees on that. But Emrys's survey sheets show something the maps don't — an old mine adit on the north bank, upstream of the narrows, that the surveyors used as a cache point. It connects to these seams. These workings run all through the north flank."

She stared at him, understanding arriving like cold water. "You want to go *through* the mountain."

"I want to come out of it behind them," Ivo said. "Below the checkpoint. Where nobody's watching, because nobody knows the way exists — except dead men and a dying surveyor." He looked past her to Emrys, who had risen silently and was holding out the survey map, unfolded, his ruined fingers surprisingly gentle with it.

"There's a pencil mark," Emrys said. "Juniper's. She marked it *way through?* with a question mark." His voice steadied. "Time we answered it."

Rosalind folded the map along Juniper's crease and looked once more at the gray light at the seam's mouth, at the glacier booming its slow countdown beyond. Then she shouldered the kit.

"All right," she said. "We go under."
