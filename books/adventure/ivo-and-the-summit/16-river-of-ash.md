## 16. River of Ash

The fever came in waves, and Ivo learned to read them the way he'd once read weather over the Karst: a pressure behind the eyes first, then the cold that no amount of wool could touch, then the burning that made the coal seam walls ripple like water. He lay in the dark with his thigh packed in Rosalind's last clean bandage and counted his heartbeats because counting was something he could still do correctly.

Forty-one minutes between waves this time. The last interval had been fifty.

"You're getting worse," Rosalind said. She wasn't looking at him. She was looking at his leg, at the angry red lines crawling up from the wound like roots seeking soil. "The infection's tracking."

"It's fine."

"Say that again and I'll leave you here for the wolverines."

He almost smiled. It cost too much. He turned his head against the pack roll and watched her work — her hands steady, her face lit by the stub of candle she'd been rationing like a miser. Three days in the seam now. Three days of melted snow and half a tin of pemmican each and Emrys's frostbitten fingers wrapped in strips of shirt, and the wound in Ivo's thigh had gone from a nuisance to a verdict.

"The roads are out," Rosalind said. "You know that. Every track out of this range funnels through the valley stations, and every station has men on it who've already shot at us once."

"I know what I said." His voice came out gravelly, thinner than he intended. "I said we follow the river."

"There is no river down here. There's a coal seam and a mountain sitting on it."

"There's a resurgence." He closed his eyes and made himself see it — the old survey maps he'd memorized during his first year as a warden, back when he'd believed the paperwork meant something. "Karst country. The main channel goes underground below the gorge — you saw that yourself. It comes back up. Fourteen kilometers south-southwest, where the limestone meets the shale. There's a spring line. Big one. The old trappers called it the Weeping Wall. Water out of solid rock, cold enough to kill a man in ten minutes, and a river running out of it through country too steep for vehicles and too worthless for anyone to bother watching."

Emrys looked up from the survey map spread across his knees. Even in the candlelight Ivo could see how bad the man's hands were — swollen, mottled black at the fingertips. "He's right. Here." He tapped a point with a pencil held awkwardly between two bandaged knuckles. "Contour lines crowd together like teeth. No trail marked. No road. Which means nobody's waiting there."

"Nobody's *anything* there," Rosalind said. "It means fourteen kilometers of ground none of us can stand up in, carrying a man who can't walk, with three days of food left and a patient whose blood is trying to boil him alive."

"I can walk."

"You stood up an hour ago and fell into the wall."

"I'll walk."

She set down the bandage roll and looked at him then, really looked, and Ivo understood that whatever she saw in his face decided it more than his argument did. Because they both knew what staying meant. The fever didn't negotiate. And they both knew what the roads meant. The men on the moraine hadn't been rangers; rangers didn't shoot first and report you as hostiles after. Somewhere above them, someone was still being paid to make sure three people and a satchel of documents never reached daylight.

"The resurgence," she said finally. "Fine. But if you collapse on me, Voss, I'm not carrying you."

"Liar," Emrys said mildly, not looking up from the map.

* * *

They moved at first light, or what passed for it — a gray seeping through the seam mouth like ink through cloth. Ivo got himself upright by stages, jaw clenched, using his ice axe as a crutch, and managed eleven steps before the world tilted sideways and Rosalind's shoulder came under his arm without ceremony, without comment, the way she did everything.

She was stronger than she looked. He'd known that for years, in the abstract way a man knows things about a woman he's loved and lost and never stopped watching from across a valley. But knowing it and leaning his whole weight into it were different animals. Her frame was wiry, all tendon and gristle earned from twenty years of hauling loads up mountains men twice her size wouldn't attempt, and she took his weight the way she took everything — as a problem to be solved, not a burden to be resented.

"Left foot," she said. "There's a ledge. Trust me."

"I always trusted you."

"No, you didn't. That was our whole problem." But her grip tightened on his ribs when she said it, and he chose to believe that meant something.

The terrain off the seam was everything the map promised and worse. Scree slopes that ran like water under their boots. Gullies choked with avalanche debris from springs past. Twice they crossed avalanche paths still scoured white and bare, and Emrys picked their line through them with the survey map held inside his coat to keep it from the wind, reading contours the way other men read scripture.

Ivo walked when he could walk. When he couldn't, he leaned. And when he couldn't lean anymore — near midday, on a slope of rotten shale above a drop that went down and down into gray nothing — his leg simply declined to participate, folded like wet cardboard, and he went down sliding before he could get the axe planted.

Rosalind caught his collar. Held him. The drop yawned. Then she hauled him back up the slope hand over hand, cursing steadily and creatively, until they were both sprawled on stable ground and his heart was slamming hard enough to hurt.

"That's it," she said. "No more walking."

"I can—"

"Shut up." She shrugged off her pack, dug out the rope, and began rigging a carry harness with quick, furious competence — loops around his thighs, a chest sling, the whole load configured to ride against her back with his arms over her shoulders. "Emrys, take his pack weight. Take your own too if your hands allow it. We move slow, we move smart, and we don't stop again until the spring line."

"You can't carry me fourteen kilometers."

"I carried a client with a broken femur off the north ridge of Mount Cader in a whiteout when I was twenty-three," she said, cinching the sling tight enough to drive the breath out of him. "Nine hours. He weighed more than you and he screamed the entire way. You're lighter and quieter. Practically a holiday."

He wanted to argue. The fever argued for him instead, dragging him down into a red murk where the shale rippled and the wind spoke in voices. He felt her rise beneath him, felt the stagger and recover, felt her boots find purchase on ground he couldn't see. Somewhere in the murk he understood that he was being carried out of the mountains by the woman he'd sworn to arrest, and that his badge was probably still in his breast pocket, and that he couldn't remember anymore which of those facts was supposed to matter.

"Ivo." Her voice, close, vibrating through her back into his chest. "Stay with me. Talk to me."

"What do you want me to say?"

"Anything. Tell me about the first case you ever worked. Tell me something true."

He thought about it through the fog. "A poacher," he managed. "Up the Little Sable. Old man, snaring ibex out of season because his daughter was sick and the meat sold well. I arrested him. Wrote it up clean. My supervisor tore the report up and told me to write it again with the snares found abandoned." A pause; the wind; her breathing, measured and strong beneath him. "I rewrote it. That was the day I learned what the uniform was actually for. Took me another ten years to admit it out loud."

"And now?"

"Now I'm being carried down a mountain by a blacklisted guide while the only honest thing I've done in fifteen years rides in a satchel on a frostbitten surveyor's back." He laughed, and it turned into a cough. "Seems about right."

Her stride didn't break. But after a moment she said, very quietly, "For what it's worth — the ibex poacher's daughter lived. I checked, years ago. She runs a tea house in Ferndale now."

He hadn't known that. He filed it away in the place where he kept the small warm things, next to the memory of her laugh in a tent on the Col du Verre, and let the fever take him again.

* * *

He surfaced properly near dusk, to the sound of water.

Not the wind. Not the drip of meltwater. Running water, deep and constant, the voice of a river that had been traveling in the dark for kilometers and had finally, violently, come home to the light.

Rosalind set him down on a slab of limestone at the lip of a bowl in the mountainside, and Ivo looked at the Weeping Wall and understood why the old trappers had given it that name. The cliff face wept everywhere — a hundred threads of water silvering down gray rock, feeding moss the color of emeralds, converging at the base into a single churning pool that spilled over broken stone and became, within fifty meters, a river wide enough to float timber. Cold rolled off it like a presence. Mist hung in the air, catching the last light.

Beyond the river, the country opened. Steep, yes — walls rising on either side — but a corridor, a way through, trending down and south toward the lowlands. No road. No track. No watchtower. Just water going where water goes, which is to say: out.

"We're through the wall," Emrys said. He was crouched at the pool's edge, filling bottles, holding his ruined hands in the flow because Rosalind had ordered him to despite the pain. "Map says the gorge opens into farmland inside two days' walking. There's a footbridge marked at the confluence — old shepherd route. After that, the valley."

"After that," Rosalind said quietly, "is the checkpoint."

Ivo turned his head. She was standing at the waterline, looking downstream, and her face had gone to the flat, unreadable mask he knew too well — the one she wore when she was doing arithmetic she didn't like.

"You've heard something."

"Radio, an hour ago, while you were sleeping." She didn't look at him. "Band scan. Valley police frequency, except it isn't police traffic. Checkpoint established at the valley mouth. 'All persons entering or leaving the range to be processed.' They used the word *processed*, Ivo. Police don't talk like that. Companies talk like that."

Emrys had gone very still by the water. "They know we're coming down."

"They know *someone's* coming down. They've known since the moraine." She crouched beside Ivo, and for the first time since the seam she let him see the exhaustion underneath — the hollows of her eyes, the tremor in her hands that she stilled by pressing them flat on her knees. "Two days' walk puts us at that bridge the morning after tomorrow. If we go down the valley road, we walk straight into their hands with everything we have. If we try to climb out cross-country, we lose two days we don't have, and you die on the way."

"So we don't use the road," Ivo said. "And we don't climb."

She looked at him.

"The river," he said. "It's high with melt. Fast through the gorge. But it's *going* where we're going, and nobody sets a checkpoint on whitewater." He pushed himself up on one elbow, and the fever was still in him but his mind had gone strangely clear, the way it sometimes did at the edge of exhaustion — every fact sharp-edged, every option laid out like tools on a bench. "There'll be boats somewhere along this water. Fishermen, shepherds, an outfitter's cache — there's always a cache. We find one, we run the gorge at dawn, we come out below their net before they understand what happened."

"Running whitewater with a fevered man, a wounded—"

"With *me*. I grew up on this water, Rosalind. Before I ever wore a badge, I ran the Sable gorge every spring for six years. I know every rock in it by its first name." He held her gaze. "It's the one thing left I'm good for. Let me be good for it."

For a long moment she said nothing. The river filled the silence, talking its endless cold talk. Then Emrys, wringing out his bandaged hands, said without turning around:

"She won't say yes. But she's already planning it. Watch her eyes. She does that thing with her eyes when she's already three moves ahead."

Rosalind exhaled through her nose — the ghost of a laugh, or a surrender. "God help me, both of you." She pulled the radio from her jacket and turned it over in her hands, and Ivo saw the plan assembling behind her eyes, move by move, exactly as Emrys had predicted. "Then we do it properly. Tomorrow we walk the bank and find our boat. And tomorrow night—" she paused, and her expression shifted into something harder, something that made the hair on his arms rise despite the fever, "—tomorrow night, somebody's going to give that checkpoint something else to look at."

"What do you mean?" Emrys asked.

She looked down at the satchel, then at the river, then at Ivo — and whatever she was weighing behind those gray eyes, Ivo realized with a cold that had nothing to do with the water, it was the kind of arithmetic where one of the three of them might end up on the wrong side of the equals sign.

"I mean," Rosalind Fairweather said, "that if they want to stop a courier, we should give them a courier to stop."
