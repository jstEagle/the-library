## 17. The Checkpoint

Emrys felt the valley change before he saw why.

For three days they had come down out of the high country in a kind of trance — Ivo feverish and gray-lipped, leaning on Rosalind's shoulder at every rest stop; Emrys himself navigating by Juniper's survey map, his frostbitten fingers clumsy as claws on the paper. The river had carried them through country that grew softer by the mile: glacier gave way to scree, scree to larch forest, forest to the first green meadows of the lower valley, where the water ran clear instead of white with rock flour and the air smelled of pine resin and warm stone instead of cold and iron.

It was too easy. That was what Emrys kept telling himself, over and over, like a man counting knots on a rope. It's too easy.

He had been a junior cartographer for eleven years. He knew how to read terrain the way other men read faces, and the terrain was lying to him. The game trails ran empty. No woodsmoke in the mornings, though this was shepherd country and it should have been thick with it. Once, near dawn, he'd heard an engine idling somewhere beyond a ridge — a clean, even idle, nothing like the coughing two-strokes the valley farmers used — and then silence, deliberate silence, the way a man stops breathing when he hears footsteps overhead.

Now, from the shoulder of the last ridge before the valley mouth, he saw it, and his blood went thin and cold.

The road that came up the valley ended at a checkpoint. Two vehicles with light bars but no markings. A folding table. Four men in dark windbreakers, one of them wearing a police vest over civilian clothes — the vest new, the boots wrong, mountain boots worn by men who stood beside vehicles all day. There was a fifth man apart from the others, up on the rocks above the road with binoculars, sweeping them across the slopes in slow, patient arcs.

Not searching. Watching. They weren't looking for survivors of a lost survey team. You didn't set a checkpoint with rifles under windbreakers to *rescue* anyone.

"They're not police," Emrys said. His voice came out flat, which surprised him. He had expected it to shake.

Rosalind lay beside him in the heather, her field glasses steady. "No."

"The vests—"

"Costume." She passed him the glasses. "Look at their hands."

He looked. Three of the four wore gloves. The fourth had hands soft as a clerk's, and a radio clipped to his belt with a coiled cord — a company pattern, heavy-duty, the kind used on drilling sites where static killed cheap sets. Emrys had seen a hundred of them. He had catalogued their frequencies once, in another life, before he understood what the company was mapping beneath the ice.

"That's Halloway," he said quietly. The man with the soft hands. "Site security coordinator. He signed the requisitions for the blasting permits we found copies of. He was at the source camp when—" He stopped. Swallowed. "He was there."

Ivo sat propped against a boulder behind them, his wounded arm bound tight against his chest, his face shining with sweat in the cool morning air. The wound had gone bad despite everything Rosalind could do; the skin around the dressing was hot and angry, and the fever made his eyes glassy, but his voice, when he spoke, was still level. Still a warden's voice.

"How many more between here and the main road?"

"At least a second post," Emrys said. "Maybe mobile units. They'll have the river watched downstream too — any raft, any crossing." He lowered the glasses. His hands were trembling now, and he let them. There was no one left to perform steadiness for. "They know we're coming down this valley. It's the only way out that doesn't cross the pass, and the pass is snowed in. We've been funneled. Like animals above a drive."

Nobody answered that, because nobody could.

* * *

They held council in the hollow below the ridge, among boulders furred with moss, with the stream talking loudly enough to cover their voices. Rosalind spread the map on a flat stone and weighted its corners. Emrys watched her hands — capable, unhurried, the hands of a woman who had spent years learning to move through country without leaving a mark on it or being marked by it. He had known her three weeks and trusted her with his life, which frightened him less than it should have, because the alternative was trusting no one, and no one would carry the satchel down.

"So," she said. "Options."

"We go around," Ivo said. "West wall, above the tree line, drop into the next valley."

"Three days over broken ground with you running a fever and Emrys barely able to grip a rope." She said it without cruelty, just arithmetic. "And they'll have watchers on the west wall. That's the obvious alternative, so it won't be unwatched."

"We wait for dark and walk through," Emrys offered. "They can't stop everyone. Farmers come down this road."

"Farmers who live here," Rosalind said. "We don't. And they've got a list. Warden Voss is a public figure — his face will be on every clipboard. Mine may be. Yours definitely is, if they think anyone survived." She looked at the map for a long moment. When she spoke again, her voice had changed — quieter, more settled, the voice of someone arriving at a place she had already been circling for days. "So we don't all go through."

Emrys felt it coming the way you feel weather coming, a pressure behind the ears.

"There are two satchels," Rosalind said. "The real documents, and the decoy I packed at the coal seam — old files, my own papers from years back, enough weight and bulk to feel right. Anyone who grabs it and runs will think they've got the prize until they open it, and by then—" She stopped herself. Emrys saw her decide not to finish the sentence, saw the thought move behind her eyes and get shut away: *by then it won't matter to me.*

"No," Ivo said.

She hadn't finished laying it out and he said it anyway, flat and immediate, the word coming out of him like a reflex.

"You don't even know what I'm going to propose."

"I know exactly what you're going to propose." Ivo pushed himself straighter against the boulder, and Emrys saw the effort it cost him, the sweat standing out at his hairline. "You take the decoy satchel and walk down the road in plain sight. You give them something to chase. You lead Halloway and his friends away from the gorge while Emrys and I take the real documents through. And then you—" His voice caught, not from the fever. From the arithmetic, the same arithmetic she had done to him a moment ago, done honestly this time. "Rosalind. They shot at us on the moraine. These aren't men who take prisoners and fill out paperwork."

"Some of them do. Some of them need me alive, actually. Think about it." Her eyes were bright and hard and terribly calm. "I'm the loose end they've spent years tying off. If they catch me, they'll want to know who I've talked to, what I've kept, where my copies are. Dead, I'm a body they have to explain. Alive, I'm a question they can interrogate. That buys hours. Hours is all you need."

"And after the hours?" Emrys asked softly.

She didn't look at him. "After the hours, you two are through the gorge and on the main road, and the documents are in front of a prosecutor, and none of what happens to me matters anymore."

"It matters," Ivo said.

"To whom?" For the first time her composure cracked, just a hairline, just enough for Emrys to see the enormity of what lived underneath it — years of erasure, of false names, of a woman who had trained herself to believe her own survival was the cheapest currency on the mountain. "Ivo. I have been nobody for six years. Nobody has a use. This is the first time in my life being nobody is worth something to somebody else."

"That's not—" He stopped. Started again, slower, and Emrys looked away, feeling like a trespasser in a room he couldn't leave. "That's not what you are. Not to me. Not anymore. Six years ago I chose the badge over you because I told myself the badge was bigger than one person. I was wrong then. I'm not doing it twice. If you walk down that road, I walk down it beside you, and the documents go wherever chance takes them, and everything those people died for—" his hand moved toward the satchel, toward the names inside it, Juniper's name among them, "—goes into a drawer in some company office and stays there."

Silence, except the stream.

It was Emrys who broke it, because he was the one who owed the most and had the least left to lose, and because he had spent nine days in the ice listening to his teammates die and had promised himself, in the long blue dark, that if he ever got out he would not be a coward about anything again.

"There's a third way," he said.

They both turned to him.

"Halloway knows my face, yes. But he knows it from the source camp, from before. He last saw me in a red survey jacket, clean-shaven, forty pounds heavier." Emrys touched his own cheek, the beard he hadn't been able to shave, the bones the hunger had carved. "I'm not that man anymore. And there's a shepherd track on the east side — I mapped it myself, three summers ago, for the grazing study. It comes down behind the checkpoint through the alder, within fifty meters of their table. Close enough to hear them talk. Close enough to see how many radios, how many rifles, whether there's a second post called in ahead."

"You're not walking past them either," Ivo said.

"I'm not offering to walk past them." Emrys pulled the map closer, and his frostbitten fingers, clumsy as they were, found the lines without trouble, because he had drawn them. "I'm offering reconnaissance. One hour. Then we decide with facts instead of fear. But while I'm gone, the two of you settle this, because I can tell you what I've seen out here, and what I've seen is that neither of you will leave the other, and if that's true, then the plan where one of you plays bait is dead on arrival, and we'd better find one that isn't."

* * *

The shepherd track was real, and it held, and it brought Emrys down through the alder thicket with the checkpoint below him close enough to count the cartridges in the magazine lying on the folding table beside a thermos. He lay in the green shade with ants crossing his wrists and listened.

Halloway talked on the radio constantly, and carelessly, because he believed the valley was already his. Emrys learned that there was indeed a second post at the bridge, eight kilometers down. He learned that a helicopter was on standby at the mine strip, thirty minutes out. He learned the phrase that mattered most, spoken by Halloway to someone senior-sounding on the far end, in the bored tone of a man confirming logistics:

*"If it's the woman, take her breathing. The old man wants to ask her questions himself. The other two — circumstances permitting."*

*Circumstances permitting.* Emrys wrote the phrase on the inside of his mind, where he kept everything he intended to say aloud someday, under oath, in a room with lights.

He also saw something Halloway hadn't meant to show anyone: the fifth watcher on the rocks changed position every twenty minutes, precisely, like a sentry following orders written by someone who had never climbed a rock in his life. The rotation had a gap — ninety seconds, maybe less, when the eastern slope above the road was blind.

Ninety seconds. Emrys climbed back up through the alder with his heart hammering and his mind, for the first time since the ambush, working fast and clean.

* * *

"One hour," Rosalind said, when he slid back into the hollow. "You were gone two."

"It took an hour to get there. The rest was listening." He crouched by the map, breathless, and told them all of it — the second post, the helicopter, the rotation gap, and last, carefully, watching both their faces, the order about the woman taken breathing.

Something moved through Rosalind's expression when he said it. Emrys had expected fear. What he saw instead was calculation catching fire — the look of a guide reading a route that everyone else had called impossible and finding, against expectation, that it went.

"They want me alive," she said slowly. "That changes things. That means if I'm taken, I'm not dead. It means I have time. And it means—" she glanced at Ivo, and her voice gentled into something almost apologetic, "—it means the bait plan works better than I hoped, because they won't shoot me on sight. They'll chase me. And chasing me pulls every rifle in this valley away from the gorge."

"No," Ivo said again. But this time the word came out ragged, and Emrys heard what was underneath it: not refusal. Terror.

"Ivo." She knelt in front of him, took his good hand in both of hers. "Listen to me. Nine days ago I went up that glacier ready to die anonymously for a girl I'd never met. You came up after me knowing your career would end for it. We've already spent everything we were afraid of losing. What's left to protect except each other, and them?" She nodded at the satchel. At Juniper's notebook inside it, at the names of the dead.

Ivo closed his eyes. When he opened them, the fever burned in them, but so did something else, something that had been fighting the fever to a standstill for days.

"Not alone," he said. "You want to be the bait? Fine. But not alone, and not blind. Emrys goes partway with you along the road — far enough to see you taken, to confirm they buy it. Then he circles back to me and we run the gorge. And you—" his grip tightened on her hand, "—you give them the decoy, you play helpless, you keep them talking, and the moment that helicopter lifts off with Halloway aboard thinking he's won, you find water and you go to ground and you come downriver. Because I am not delivering these documents to anyone until I know you're alive to hear about it. That's my price. That's the renegotiation. Take it or I burn the satchel right now and we all walk into that checkpoint together and let God sort it out."

Emrys held his breath.

Rosalind looked at Ivo Voss for a long, long moment — and Emrys, watching, understood that he was seeing something being rebuilt that had been broken for six years, plank by plank, in the dark, without either of them admitting it.

"All right," she said finally. "All right. Together as far as the bend, then I'm on my own, and you'd better be fast in that gorge, warden, because I intend to be insufferable about rescuing you for the rest of our lives."

Below them, faint on the wind, a radio crackled, and Halloway's voice drifted up through the alder, calling in the hour, calling in the watch, certain that the valley was sealed and the day was already his.

Rosalind shouldered the decoy satchel, checked the angle of the sun, and started down toward the road.
