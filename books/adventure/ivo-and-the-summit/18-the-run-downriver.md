## 18. The Run Downriver

The decoy satchel rode in the bow of the raft like a passenger, strapped upright against the frame with the same care Rosalind would have given a living thing. Inside it: her old files, her old name, the paper ghost of a woman who had drowned once already and learned to swim in the shape of someone else. Let them read it. Let them choke on it.

She cinched the last strap, then looked up at Ivo.

He stood at the water's edge where the resurgence boiled out of the gorge mouth, gray-green and furious even in the early light, and he was doing the thing with his jaw that meant he was losing an argument with himself. He'd been losing it since midnight. She could hear the words he wasn't saying — *let me take the decoy, let me be the bait* — stacked behind his teeth like stones.

"Don't," she said.

"You haven't heard what I was going to say."

"I've been hearing it for three days." She tested the oarlock, worked it back and forth. The bronze pin was worn but true; she'd greased it at dawn with the last of the tallow from their stores. "You can barely stand. You're gray around the mouth. If you run this river today you'll faint in the first hole and drown before Emrys and I finish arguing about whose fault it is."

"I'm not asking to run it."

"No. You're asking to trade places. Same argument, worse arithmetic." She straightened and made herself look at him properly — the fever-bright eyes, the bandage under his shirt gone stiff and dark at the edge, the way he held his left arm close to his ribs like something borrowed. "Ivo. Listen to me once without planning your rebuttal. They know your face. Your warrant card is in that satchel with my files, and when they open it they'll know exactly who they caught, and they will not take him alive to ask questions. But me—" she spread her hands, "—I'm nobody. I'm a rumor in a rain shell. I've spent six years being nobody. It's the one skill I have left."

"That's not—"

"It's exactly what it is." Her voice came out harder than she intended, and she softened it the only way she knew how, by telling the truth sideways. "Six years ago you asked me to stay. I stayed. Now I'm asking you to let me go. We're even."

Emrys appeared out of the willow scrub with the real satchel slung across his chest and the survey map folded into a tube lashed to his pack frame. His frostbitten hand was wrapped in wool now, three fingers lost to feeling if not yet to flesh, and he'd stopped complaining about it, which frightened her more than the complaining had. He looked at the two of them standing apart by a hand's width of cold air and said nothing about it, which was its own kindness.

"Water's up another foot since yesterday," he said. "Snowmelt plus the dam they built upstream for the sluicing. The gauge marks are all underwater." He crouched, touched the current, pulled his fingers back like he'd touched a nerve. "First rapid's a mile down. After that it doesn't stop until the narrows."

"Then we don't stop either." Rosalind swung the radio off her shoulder — the company set they'd taken from the ambush patrol's dead man, charged, tuned, and loaded with everything she needed to lie beautifully. She turned it over in her hands. "I'll call in positions as I go. Keep them chasing a ghost downstream while you two cut through the gorge on foot. By the time they figure out the coordinates don't hold water, you'll be at the valley road and this will be a prosecutor's problem instead of ours."

"And when they catch you?" Ivo said quietly.

She smiled, and it cost her something to make it reach her eyes. "They won't."

---

The river took her the way it took everything — without negotiation.

The raft was a survey boat, aluminum-framed, patched twice with tent fabric and pitch, and it handled like a drunk ox, but it floated, and floating was the whole of her plan. She ran the first rapid standing, reading the water the way she'd taught Ivo to read it years ago on a gentler river in a gentler life: the V of smooth water marking the channel, the downstream-tilted tongues where the current dove, the black smiles of holes that would flip her and keep her. She threaded them. Spray came over the bow in sheets and froze on her forearms, and she laughed once, alone, a sound torn out of her rather than chosen, because there was no one left to hear it and no reason left to pretend she wasn't afraid.

At the eddy below the second drop she keyed the radio.

"Patrol Two, Patrol Two, this is Fairweather party." She'd decided to use her own name on the air. It was the last piece of bait, and the sweetest. "I'm on the river, mile marker eleven by the old survey gauge. Heading for the valley mouth. I have the documents. Come and get them."

Static. Then a voice, clipped and male and trying very hard to sound like a policeman instead of what it was. "Copy, Fairweather party. Maintain course and speed. Units are moving to intercept at the lower bridge."

"Negative on the bridge," she said, and fed them the first lie, smooth as rope through a block. "There's a logjam below the confluence. I'm portaging right bank, will re-launch above the falls. Meet me at the falls."

A pause long enough to taste. "Confirmed. Falls, right bank."

She stowed the radio and pushed back into the current, grinning into the wind with her teeth bared. There were no falls on this stretch. There hadn't been falls here since before the mining company had rerouted half the river for their sluice boxes. Every minute they spent driving toward a waterfall that existed only in her voice was a minute Ivo and Emrys spent walking south with the truth in a canvas bag.

*Let them come to the falls,* she thought. *I'll leave them a note.*

---

The gorge narrowed the way a throat does.

Walls of limestone rose on either side, streaked white with bird lime, close enough in places that she could have touched both banks with the oars. The river compressed between them and went from furious to insane, stacking itself into standing waves taller than the raft, and the noise was total — a roar that lived in her sternum and her molars and made thought a luxury. She ran it blind in the troughs and by instinct in the crests, and somewhere in the middle of it she stopped performing bravery and simply performed competence, stroke after stroke after stroke, because competence was the only prayer she'd ever believed in.

Halfway down, she saw them.

Two figures on the left rim, small against the sky, and the sun flashing off glass — binoculars or a rifle scope, impossible to tell, irrelevant to tell. They'd found her. Of course they'd found her; she'd invited them. What mattered was whether they had boats below or only boots, and the answer came a minute later when the gorge opened into the long boulder garden and she saw, pinned against the far bank, a gray inflatable with an outboard and four men in it, holding in the eddy, waiting for the river to deliver her.

Her radio crackled. "Fairweather party. We have visual. Cut your motor and drift in."

She keyed it, breath ragged, playing the exhausted fugitive because it cost nothing and bought everything. "Can't — motor's flooded — I'll run the right channel, meet you below the garden—"

"There is no right channel," the voice said, almost gently. "Drift in. Last warning."

She looked. He was right. The right channel was a lie she'd invented on the spot, and the river had already erased it — the whole right side was a single collapsing wave train feeding into a sieve of boulders that would eat the raft and everything attached to it.

Which meant the choice had already been made for her, hours ago, by a woman who'd planned this run in the dark with a pencil stub and a stolen map. The left channel went through. Barely. At this water level. Once.

"Coming in," she said, and threw the radio into the river.

Then she dug for the left side with everything the broken river would give her.

---

The boulder garden tried to kill her nine ways and settled for seven.

The first hole hit her abeam and stood the raft on its starboard tubes; she threw her weight left and it came down wallowing, shipping half the river. A boulder the size of a chapel rose dead ahead and she ferried hard, the current grabbing the hull like a hand closing, and she felt the frame flex, felt one of the patched cross-tubes begin to tear free with a sound like a rib going. The decoy satchel strained at its straps. Behind her — she didn't look, she *heard* — the inflatable entered the garden after her, engine screaming, men shouting, because whoever commanded them wanted the satchel more than they feared the water, and that was the whole trap working exactly as designed.

Through the slot. Through the haystack beyond it. A lateral wave swatted the stern and spun her and for three full seconds she was surfing backward down a river that hated her, watching her own wake and the gray boat plunging after her, closer now, close enough to see faces, and one of them she recognized — the ranger from the moraine, the friendly one, the one Emrys had bolted from — and he was pointing something at her that was not binoculars.

The shot cracked flat across the water and hit nothing, or hit the raft, she couldn't tell. The second shot took the oar out of her right hand at the grip, splintering it, and the river did the rest.

Without the right oar she couldn't hold the line. The current took the bow and swung it into the last boulder field, the one the map called the Teeth, and the raft struck the first tooth with a bang she felt in her spine. The frame buckled. The decoy satchel tore loose and cartwheeled into the water ahead of her, red canvas bright as a wound, and some distant part of her mind noted with professional calm that it floated, that it was carrying on downstream without her, that the plan had accounted for this too.

The second tooth hit the hull amidships and opened it like a letter.

Cold took her whole.

It was a violence beyond description, a fist of ice closing on her heart, and she'd known it would be, she'd grown up on this water, and knowing was worth nothing. She was under, tumbling, the world a green-gray blur of bubbles and rock-shadow, and her boots found bottom and pushed and she rose into light and air and took one glorious breath before the next wave put her under again. Somewhere above, muffled by tons of moving water, the inflatable's engine died against stone. She heard men shouting. She heard one word carry clear across the roar —

*"Satchel!"*

Good, she thought, from inside the drowning. *Take it. Open it. Read your own names.*

The river rolled her. Rock kissed her shoulder blade, her hip, her scalp — glancing blows, the river taking its tax. She curled as she'd teach clients to curl, feet up, arms wrapped, a ball of meat and bone surrendering to the current so the current wouldn't crush her, and she let it take her, down and down the boulder garden, past the narrows, past the place where the walls fell away and the valley opened its gray throat to the sky.

She surfaced once, gasping, and saw the red satchel snagged on a midstream boulder fifty yards ahead, flapping like a shot bird.

She saw the eddy behind it. The one Emrys had marked on the map in pencil, the one she'd promised herself she'd reach.

She reached for it.

The river reached first.

---

Cold is a strange country. It has its own clock.

Rosalind hung in the tail of the eddy with one arm hooked over a submerged root and watched the light change on the water and understood, with a clarity that felt almost peaceful, that she was not going to climb out. Her legs weren't answering. Her right arm answered slowly, badly, like a subordinate who'd stopped believing in the mission. The cold had gotten into the deep rooms of her and was turning down the lamps one by one, and each lamp that went out felt less like dying and more like finishing — like a chapter ending the way chapters do, not because anything was resolved but because the writer had run out of reasons to continue.

*Ivo,* she thought, and the thought had no panic in it at all, only weight. He'd be at the valley road by now, or near it, feverish and furious and alive, with Emrys limping beside him and the truth in the bag between them. That was the arithmetic she'd traded with. One life spent against two saved and a hundred names dragged into the light. Any warden worth his badge would call it a good exchange. Any guide worth hers.

She'd never told him. In the coal seam, with the fever talking, she'd told him everything else — the client, the cornice, the silence she'd bought with her exile — but she'd never said the plain sentence underneath all of it, the one with no mountains in it at all. She'd saved it, the way you save dry wood, for a fire you kept meaning to build.

Well. Some fires other people have to light for you.

The light on the water went gold, then copper. Downstream, faint on the wind, she heard engines — vehicles, many, at the valley mouth — and then, cutting through everything, a sound she knew from her other life, her first life: a helicopter, beating upriver toward the falls where her lies had gathered her hunters like flies. Toward the decoy. Away from the road.

Away from him.

She laughed, or the river laughed for her, and her grip on the root was a fact she could no longer verify. The last lamp dimmed. The cold country opened its door, and it was, she noticed with the last of her attention, very quiet, and very much like sleep, and it smelled — impossibly, absurdly — like pine smoke and old canvas and the inside of a tent on a morning twenty years gone, when a young warden had handed her coffee and said her name like it was a place he wanted to live.

*Rosalind.*

Not the river. Not memory. The voice was coming from the bank, upstream, hoarse and cracking and absolutely furious, and it was saying her name again, louder, the way you say a name when you are dragging yourself along a shoreline on legs that should not be holding you, when you are scanning brown water for a red jacket that isn't there, when the whole gray evening depends on the answer.

The eddy turned her slowly, gently, like a hand.

And the last thing the cold country showed her, before it closed its door, was a figure stumbling along the gravel bar with the sunset behind him — and then the water took even that.
