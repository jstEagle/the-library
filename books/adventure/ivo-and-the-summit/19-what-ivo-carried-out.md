## 19. What Ivo Carried Out

The river gave him nothing back.

Ivo had stood on the gravel bar below the wreck for an hour, calling her name until his voice cracked into the roar of the water, until Emrys — frostbitten, feverish, half-carried himself — had put a hand on his shoulder and said, quietly, *she told us to go.* And that was the worst of it. She had told them to go. She had planned it that way, planned it with that crooked half-smile she used when she was about to do something reckless and wanted no one to argue, and Ivo had let her, because there had been no other way, because the documents had to come out, because love in the mountains is just arithmetic with a knife's edge.

He had not seen her go under. That was the mercy or the cruelty of it; he could never decide which. One moment her raft was a yellow fleck threading the white water ahead of the security boats, radio crackling false coordinates in her lowland accent, and then the gorge turned, and the fleck was gone, and the river closed over the place where she had been like a mouth.

*She's a better boatman than anyone I've ever known,* he told himself, walking. *She ran that gorge twice before either of us could legally drive.*

But the water was high with early melt, and the security men had been shooting, and when they'd fished the shattered raft from an eddy two miles down, there had been blood on the gunwale. Not much. Enough.

* * *

They came out of the range at dusk on the third day, through the resurgence where the river was born again out of the mountain's dark throat, cold as confession. Emrys navigated by the survey map even now, though he knew this last stretch by heart — knew it the way a man knows the corridor between his cell and the interrogation room. Ivo carried him the final mile on his back, the infected wound in his own side screaming with every step, and thought, absurdly, of all the times Rosalind had carried him — gear, moods, griefs — and how he had never once said thank you properly, only *put me down, I can walk,* and she would say *I know you can,* and keep walking.

That was the whole marriage, he thought. That was the whole thing, in miniature. He kept trying to walk and she kept carrying him anyway.

The prosecutor's name was Halloran. Emrys spoke it like a password, like a prayer he wasn't sure was still valid: *Vera Halloran, federal building, Meridian Street, tell her the Karst survey sent her ice samples in March and ask her why she never opened the box.* There was a story in that, Emrys said, told once, briefly, in the coal seam while Ivo's fever burned: a junior prosecutor who had requested evidence two years ago and been reassigned within the month, who had spent the time since being very careful and very patient and keeping one drawer of her desk locked.

Meridian Street was four days' travel if the buses ran, and the buses did not run in the valley mouth anymore, not since the checkpoint went up. So they walked the long way, along the irrigation roads, Ivo's wound opening and closing like a bad bargain, Emrys fading and rallying by turns, both of them living off a hunter's cache Emrys remembered from a life that felt like it belonged to someone else. At night Ivo lay awake listening for her voice in the river sound. Every river sounded like her now. Every river said *I know you can.*

On the fifth morning a grain truck took them as far as the county line, and on the sixth they stood outside the federal building in Meridian City, two ruined men in borrowed coats, and Ivo understood suddenly that he was carrying the most dangerous paper in the country in a satchel slung across a wound that had soaked through his shirt twice since dawn.

"Tell me again," he said. "Exactly what she gave you."

Emrys looked at him with those eyes that had gone flat and watchful in the ice caves and had never quite come back. "She made the swap herself. Night before the checkpoint. Said if she didn't make it out, the papers still had to walk." He hesitated. "She wouldn't tell me what was in it. Just said, *the truth travels better in disguise.*"

* * *

Vera Halloran was smaller than Ivo expected, and younger, with the particular stillness of a person who has spent years pretending to be harmless. She listened to Emrys say *the Karst survey sent you ice samples in March* without moving a muscle in her face, and then she closed her office door, drew the blind, and unlocked the bottom-left drawer of her desk with a key she wore on a chain around her neck.

"Sit down," she said. "Both of you. You look like you're about to fall over, and I need you conscious for this."

The satchel came off Ivo's shoulder with a reluctance that surprised him — his hands actually shook, and not entirely from the fever. Two weeks it had lived against his body. Two weeks of guarding it with Rosalind's voice in his ears: *whatever happens, it goes to Halloran, not to any badge, not to any station, do you hear me, Ivo? Not to your own service. Especially not to your own service.*

Halloran spread the contents across her desk under the desk lamp, and Ivo watched her face as she read, and her face was a lesson in controlled catastrophe. Survey maps annotated in three hands. Photographs of blasting scars beneath the glacier tongue, ore cores sealed in waxed cloth, assay results stamped with a company logo Ivo recognized from the equipment at the checkpoint. Then letters — copies, but good ones — on ministry letterhead, signatures he didn't know and two he did, names that had signed off on his own search-and-rescue budgets, names that had ordered the Karst declared closed.

"Where," Halloran said softly, "did this come from?"

"A man named Emrys Llewellyn," Ivo said. "Junior surveyor. The one they didn't find."

"I know who he is." She was already moving, pulling a camera from the drawer, a document sleeve, a phone with a scrambled casing. "I've been waiting two years for someone to bring me this. I want you to understand something, Mr. Voss." She looked up, and for the first time her stillness cracked and showed the furnace underneath. "The last person who tried to hand me evidence in this case died in a car accident on a dry road. So we're going to do this properly, tonight, before anyone knows you're in the city."

It was while she photographed the third letter that Ivo noticed the folder at the bottom of the stack. Thinner than the rest. Older. The paper gone soft at the edges, handled many times, corner creased the way paper creases when someone has folded and unfolded it through long nights.

A label, typed years ago: **FAIRWEATHER, R. — INCIDENT FILE 114-C.**

His heart stopped and then restarted wrong.

"That's not survey material," he said.

"No," Halloran said, frowning, turning it over. "This looks like a service internal file. Restricted classification." She glanced at him. "You'd know more about that than I would."

He would. He knew the format, the punch-hole pattern, the archive stamp — he'd requisitioned files from the same registry, including, six months ago, the old search records with Rosalind's name redacted so thoroughly it was less redaction than erasure. He reached out and touched the folder and his fingers were steady now, steadier than they'd been in days, because some part of him had already understood, the way you understand a slope is going to slide before the first stone moves.

"She swapped them," he said. "At the checkpoint. The decoy satchel — the one she took downstream, the one she—" His voice failed and reassembled itself. "Open it."

Halloran opened it.

Photographs first. The cornice above Camp Four, taken from angles only a climber could have reached. The rope ends — cut, sheared clean, not frayed, and beside them comparison photographs of abraded rope, torn rope, rope that fails honestly. A client's harness, recovered, with a carabiner gate that had been filed. Lab reports, unsigned, dated after the official inquiry closed. Witness statements that contradicted each other in ways no honest investigation leaves behind. And a covering analysis in handwriting Ivo knew better than his own — tight, slanted, unsentimental, every conclusion numbered — demonstrating that Daniel Voss's death on the Karst glacier seven years ago was not guide error but sabotage, and that the inquiry that blacklisted Rosalind Fairweather had been steered by the same company whose logo was stamped on the ore cores lying three inches away on the desk.

Daniel Voss. His brother. The client whose death had ended her career, her name, her life.

Ivo sat down slowly, because his legs had decided the matter without consulting him.

"She found this," he said. "Years ago. Before the inquiry even—"

"Before, during, after — I can't date it yet," Halloran said, but her voice had changed, gone careful, the way voices change around the wounded. "Mr. Voss—"

"She carried it for seven years." He was looking at the file and not seeing the office, seeing instead a gravel bar in a gorge, a woman standing in the spray with a satchel strap across her chest, saying *the truth travels better in disguise,* and smiling that smile, and he had thought — God help him, he had thought she was sending the survey evidence out under guard while she drew the fire with an empty bag. A decoy. Blank paper. Weight and nothing else.

Not empty. Never empty. She had sent her own vindication into the gunfire so that the real cargo would get through, and she had never said a word, because saying a word would have made it about her, and Rosalind Fairweather would rather drown than make it about her.

"The decoy satchel," Halloran said quietly. "The one lost on the river. It contained this?"

"It contained the only proof she ever gathered that my brother was murdered and she was innocent." Each word came out level and terrible. "And she traded it for a diversion. For time. For us."

The room was silent except for the lamp's faint buzz. Emrys had turned his face away, toward the window, toward the dark, and his shoulders were shaking in a way that had nothing to do with cold.

Then Halloran said, "Wait." She had the file open to the last page, the analysis page, and she was holding it closer to the lamp. "There's a second copy notation here. Look. *Duplicate lodged with—*" The typing was smudged, but the characters were legible enough, and Ivo read them upside down across the desk, and the world tilted.

*Duplicate lodged with J. Okonkwo, outfitter, Lowbridge.*

Old Tomas. The dying man who had begged her to search for Juniper. Who had pressed a notebook into the whole affair with his last strength. Who had known — of course he had known; he'd been her friend, her anchor, maybe her co-conspirator — and who had left Juniper's notebook at her door not only as guilt but as a key, because Tomas Okonkwo did nothing without a reason, and the reason, Ivo realized with a certainty that felt like falling, was that the duplicate had never left his shop. It was still there. In Lowbridge. In a dead outfitter's back room, behind the racks and the rope bins, waiting for someone to come and claim it.

"She has a way out," Ivo said. He was on his feet now, and the fever didn't matter, and the wound didn't matter. "If she's alive — if she made the river — she'll go to ground, and the only ground she has left is that shop. And if she's dead—" His voice caught on the word and climbed over it. "If she's dead, then that file is the only eulogy she gets, and I am not letting it rot in a back room while the people who wrote her blacklist read about their own arrests in the paper."

Halloran was already writing. "Lowbridge is nine hours by road. My jurisdiction ends at the county line, but my phone doesn't." She tore the page free. "Mr. Voss, you are technically a fugitive's associate and possibly a deserter, and I am technically harboring you, so let's be efficient about our crimes. There's a car downstairs."

Emrys stood too, swaying, refusing the wall. "The arrests," he said. "When?"

"Tonight. Warrants are signed; I've been sitting on them for a year waiting for exactly this weight of paper." Halloran snapped the document sleeve shut over the photographs, and for a moment the furnace in her face showed through again. "By morning, the mining ring will be coming down. Which means by morning, every friend they have left will be burning whatever they can reach — including, Mr. Voss, anything in a little outfitter's shop in Lowbridge that might clear the name of the woman they destroyed."

Ivo was already at the door.

Outside, the city smelled of rain and diesel, ordinary and obscene. Somewhere north of here a river ran through a gorge, high and cold and indifferent, and somewhere along it — he refused to believe otherwise, he would refuse until the earth itself told him — a woman who had carried his family's grief for seven years without telling him was climbing out of the water, or lying in it, or walking toward a shop with a broken sign in Lowbridge where a duplicate of her own salvation waited in the dark.

"Halloran," he said, as the elevator doors closed on them. "Drive faster."

She drove faster.
