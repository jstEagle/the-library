## 20. The Source Stands Open

The river spat her out like something it had decided not to keep.

Rosalind came to on a gravel shoal in the grey half-light before dawn, face down, one arm hooked over a stone as if she'd been holding the world's edge. Water still moved through her — in her ears, her sinuses, the deep cold chambers of her chest. She coughed and it came up brown and glittering with rock flour, and then she coughed again because the first cough had reminded her lungs they were supposed to be working.

She lay there a long time, letting the river's noise fill her head where thoughts should have been. The gorge was behind her now. She could see its mouth upstream, a black seam in the canyon wall where the water went from white violence to something almost gentle, spreading out across the wide valley floor in braided channels that caught the first light and turned it to tarnished silver.

Her raft was gone. Her decoy satchel was gone. Everything she'd carried into the water was either downstream or scattered along ten miles of riverbed, and none of it mattered, because none of it had been real. The real satchel — Emrys's documents, Ivo's warrant card tucked inside for luck, though he'd never admit he'd given it for luck — was moving down the gorge right now with two men who loved her, or had loved her, or would again if any of them lived long enough.

*If.* She said the word aloud just to hear how small it sounded against all that water.

"Get up," she told herself. "You're not done."

Her body disagreed. Every joint had been wrung like laundry. Her left shoulder screamed when she pushed herself onto her hands and knees, and her right boot had filled with river and emptied itself twice over until her foot was a numb pale thing she couldn't feel. But she got up. She stood swaying on the shoal like a heron, dripping, and looked at the sky, which was doing what Karst skies did at dawn: burning off the night's cloud in ragged strips of gold and slate.

Downstream, maybe four kilometers, the valley narrowed toward its mouth. And at the mouth, she knew, sat the checkpoint. Company men dressed as police, rifles under their jackets, a folding table and a radio mast and the patient confidence of people who had never once been stopped by anything smaller than weather.

That was where she was going. That was the whole point of last night's ruinous swim.

* * *

She walked the way she'd taught clients to walk when everything had gone wrong: small steps, weight low, no decisions made faster than the body could honor them. The gravel bars gave way to willow flats, the willows to old flood terraces where the spruce began, dark and dripping. She moved through the trees rather than along the open bank, an old habit from years of being someone she wasn't, though the habit felt different this morning. Lighter. Almost ceremonial.

Because here was the truth she'd turned over all night in the water, between waves: she had spent nine years perfecting invisibility. A false name stitched into every jacket collar. A face trained blank. A whole self folded up small enough to fit inside a lowland outfitter's stockroom, taking inventory of other people's adventures. She had done it to survive, and survival had been worth it, and she was not ashamed of the woman who had chosen it.

But the water had stripped something else out of her too — some final pretense that she could carry the truth down the mountain and hand it off and walk away clean. There was no walking away clean. There never had been. The client who died in the icefall nine years ago had deserved better than her silence. Juniper's grandfather had died believing his granddaughter was lost because men with company radios had decided she was inconvenient. Emrys had frozen half a hand guarding papers no one was supposed to find. And Ivo — Ivo had put his badge on a gravel bar like a man laying down a weapon, and asked her to tell him the truth, and she had, and the world hadn't ended. It had only gotten more honest.

So: the checkpoint. Voluntarily. With her name in her own mouth for the first time in nearly a decade.

*Rosalind Fairweather,* she practiced silently, and the syllables felt like boots two sizes too large, and also like coming home.

* * *

An hour later she smelled woodsmoke and stopped dead among the spruce.

Not the checkpoint's smoke — too close, too domestic. She circled wide, using the wind, and found a trapper's cabin tucked against a rock outcrop, its chimney breathing a thin grey thread. An old man was splitting kindling in the yard, unhurried, methodical, wearing the particular patience of someone who had outlived everyone who ever hurried him.

She knew him. Everyone who'd ever run the upper Karst knew Marta's father — old Semyon, who traded smoked fish for cartridges and news, who had guided her own first survey of the headwaters back when she was nineteen and the range belonged to nobody but the ice.

He saw her before she meant him to. He always had. He set down the axe without alarm and looked at her — soaked, limping, hollow-eyed — and his weathered face did something complicated.

"They said you drowned," he said.

"People say a lot of things." Her voice came out scraped raw. "Semyon. I need dry clothes and I need you to listen, because there isn't much time."

He listened. He was one of perhaps three people alive who would have. She told him about the mining operation under the glacier, about the ambush, about the documents moving downriver right now with a wounded warden and a frostbitten surveyor. She watched his eyes narrow at the names she gave him — names that included two deputy ministers and a man whose family owned half the valley's timber — and she watched him decide, in the space of one slow breath, which side of history he wanted to stand on.

"The checkpoint," he said. "Four men. They took my cousin's boat yesterday 'for security.' They are not police. Police do not ask about cargo manifests." He jerked his chin toward the cabin. "Come. Dry clothes. And then I'll show you the game trail down to the mouth, because if you walk the road they'll take you before you can say your own name."

Inside, while she stripped off the river's dead weight and pulled on wool that smelled of spruce smoke and decades, Semyon talked quietly, feeding the stove, telling her what the valley already knew. The radio had been alive all night. Something had happened in the lowlands — federal vehicles on the north road, arrests beginning at the company offices in Greywater before dawn, the prosecutor's office confirming nothing and denying nothing, which in Semyon's experience meant everything.

"It started," Rosalind said slowly. "It actually started."

"You carried it out." He handed her a tin mug of something hot and terrible and medicinal. "Or those two did. Either way — the mountain let it through."

She drank. Her hands shook around the mug, and she let them.

* * *

Semyon's game trail dropped through the last stand of timber and ended at a granite shoulder overlooking the valley mouth, and from there Rosalind saw the checkpoint laid out below her like a diagram of a dying lie.

Two vehicles angled across the road. The folding table. The radio mast guy-wired to a boulder. Four men in mismatched police surplus, one of them scanning the river with binoculars, another drinking coffee from a thermos with the bored insolence of a man being paid far too well to impersonate a constable. And beyond them, downstream, the low country opening out — fields, the first thread of the highway, the distant smudge of Greywater where, even now, doors were being kicked in.

She watched for a long time. Long enough to memorize the rhythm of the place: the binocular sweep, the coffee, the radio checks every twenty minutes, the way none of them ever looked up at the granite shoulder because men paid to watch roads forget that valleys have edges.

Then she stepped out of the trees and walked down.

She didn't take the road. She took the open slope, deliberately visible, deliberately slow, hands loose at her sides, and let the light find her. It took eleven minutes. On the fourth minute the man with the binoculars caught movement, shouted, and the whole little theater snapped taut — rifles out from under jackets, the table shoved aside, four bodies arranging themselves around the fiction they'd been hired to maintain.

"Hold there!" the nearest one called, voice cracking on the second word. "Identify yourself!"

Rosalind Fairweather stopped ten meters away, dripping river water off Semyon's borrowed wool, and looked each of them in the eye in turn. Up close they were younger than she'd expected. Frightened, underneath the posture. Men who had signed on to intimidate fishermen and were suddenly standing in front of something else entirely.

"My name is Rosalind Fairweather," she said. "Nine years ago your employers helped strip me of my guide's license and my name to bury what they were doing under the Karst glacier. Last night I wrecked a raft in the gorge carrying a decoy so that the real evidence could get past you. It has. Check your radios. Ask your handlers why Greywater woke up to warrants this morning."

Silence. The river filled it, talking endlessly to itself over the shallows.

One of the men — the coffee drinker, older, with the flat pragmatic face of a foreman — lowered his rifle six inches. "You're supposed to be dead," he said. Not hostile. Almost admiring. "They told us the river took you."

"The river tried." She held out her wrists, an old gesture from another life, from the night she'd been detained and released and erased. "I'm surrendering. Voluntarily. To whoever is actually going to write this down. Because I am done being invisible, and I want my name back, and I want it in the record next to theirs."

The young one wavered, rifle drifting. "Karel—"

"Put it down, Tomas." Karel, the foreman, slung his rifle across his back with the weary finality of a man watching a paycheck die. He keyed the radio instead, and Rosalind heard him report, in a voice gone carefully neutral: *"Checkpoint to base. We have a survivor from the survey incident walking out under her own power. She's giving herself up. Repeat — she says the evidence is already with the prosecutor."*

Static. Then, faintly, from a hundred kilometers away, a voice she didn't know saying words she'd waited nine years to hear: *"Copy, checkpoint. Federal units en route. Do not detain. Do not harm her. God help us, cooperate."*

* * *

They gave her a camp stool and, absurdly, coffee from the same thermos. She sat at the folding table that had been built to stop her friends, wrapped in a blanket one of them fetched from a vehicle, and answered questions from the radio in a clear, level voice while the morning climbed the canyon walls and turned the glacier's high shoulders rose-gold.

She gave her name three times, spelling it, savoring it. She described the ambush site, the blasting scars, the ore samples, the ice cave. She described the serac field and the moraine and the coal seam where she'd sewn a wound by headlamp. She described the raft, the rapids, the water closing over her — and found, to her surprise, that her voice stayed steady, because every hard thing she narrated had already been survived, and survival, she was learning, was just a story you told afterward with your pulse under control.

Near midday the federal vehicles came down the valley — two of them, unmarked, mud-splattered, moving fast. The men at the checkpoint straightened like schoolchildren. Rosalind stood up from the stool and did not sit back down.

A tall woman in a grey coat got out of the lead vehicle, flashed credentials at no one in particular, and crossed to the table where Rosalind waited. She looked at her for a moment — the ruined boots, the blanket, the bruised and salt-pale face — and something professional in her expression softened by a single degree.

"Rosalind Fairweather?"

"Yes."

"We've been looking for you for nine years. Half the file says you're a danger to the public. The other half—" she tapped a folder thick with photocopies, and Rosalind recognized her own handwriting, her own old reports, the exculpatory pages she'd hidden in the decoy satchel, the ones Ivo must have found and forwarded, "—says you were right all along, and someone very powerful made sure no one read it." The prosecutor extended her hand. "I've read it now. All of it. Would you come make a statement? There are people in custody who are going to need you to be exactly as stubborn as your file suggests you are."

Rosalind looked past her, up the valley, to where the glacier stood open in the full daylight — calved back, blue-shouldered, its forbidden source exposed to the sun for the first time in anyone's memory. Somewhere up there the ice was still shifting, settling, remembering. Somewhere downriver, if the mountain was kind, a wounded warden and a frostbitten surveyor were walking out of the gorge toward the same reckoning.

"In a minute," Rosalind said. "First — has anything come through from the lower gorge? Two men. One of them carries a warden's commission he may no longer want."

The prosecutor's radio crackled. She listened, and her face changed — not grief, Rosalind saw immediately, not grief, please not grief — and then she smiled, small and tired and real.

"Signal relay from the ranger station at Mile Forty," she said. "A surveyor named Emrys requesting medical transport for a warden named Voss. Gunshot wound, fever, but walking. Their words." She glanced down at her notes. "And a message. For you. They said—" the prosecutor frowned slightly, checking, "—*'Tell her the river keeps what matters. We'll meet her where the water goes flat.'*"

Rosalind closed her eyes. The river talked on behind her, patient, ancient, keeping its accounts.

"Well then," she said, opening them, and stepping forward to give her statement — her name, her whole name, spoken clearly into the record at last. "Let's begin."
