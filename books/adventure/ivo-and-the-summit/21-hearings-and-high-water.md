## 21. Hearings and High Water

The hearing room had been a ballroom once, back when the provincial capital still believed in its own grandeur. Now the gilt was flaking off the cornices and the crystal chandeliers had been replaced with fluorescent panels that hummed like trapped insects, but the bones of the place remained — tall windows, marble floors, an acoustic that made every cough and creak of a chair sound like testimony in itself.

Emrys Cadwallader sat at the witness table with his left hand resting on the wood where everyone could see it. The frostbite had taken two fingers and most of the feeling in a third, and the surgeons had done what surgeons do, which was to make him neat. He wore his one good jacket, the wool one with the leather buttons, because he had decided that if he was going to stand in front of a room full of men in expensive suits and tell them what their colleagues had ordered done to six people on a glacier, he would do it dressed like someone who expected to be believed.

He was not a natural witness. He knew this about himself. He had spent thirty years taking measurements — discharge rates, ice velocities, the slow arithmetic of mass balance — and numbers did not flinch or editorialize. But he had rehearsed this in the hospital, in the dark, going over it the way he used to check a traverse line before committing to it: *point by point, no shortcuts, no embellishment.* The truth needed no embellishment. The truth, he had learned on the Karst Glacier, was the most violent thing a man could carry.

The chairman, a federal appointee named Ostrava with a face like a closed ledger, read the terms into the record. Emrys listened with his chin up. When Ostrava asked him to state his name and position for the transcript, Emrys said them clearly, and then added, unprompted:

"For the record, I am the sole survivor of the Karst Range survey team. Six of us went up. I am what came down."

A murmur moved through the gallery like wind through grass. In the third row, a woman with grey-streaked hair pulled back in a practical knot sat very still. Rosalind Fairweather had been told she would be called later in the proceedings, and she had spent the morning learning how to sit in a public room without trying to disappear from it. It was harder than any pitch she had ever climbed. Every instinct she owned said *lower your eyes, be no one, be a shape in the corner.* She kept her eyes level and her hands loose in her lap and she watched Emrys the way you watch a partner on exposed ground.

Ostrava walked him through it. The survey's mandate. The route upriver. The day they reached the source and found the blasting scars beneath the glacier tongue — fresh cuts in ten-thousand-year-old ice, haul roads graded where no road should exist, ore samples that assayed rich enough to make grown men lie.

"Describe what happened on the descent," Ostrava said.

Emrys described it. He did it plainly, without theater, and the plainness was worse than any theater could have been. The company radios — not the scrambled criminal-band sets smugglers used, but licensed frequencies, government-issue, the kind that required paperwork signed three ministries deep. The men who came out of the whiteout wearing range service jackets over civilian body armor. Juniper — he had to stop when he said her name, and the room let him stop, and in the silence Rosalind thought about a dying old outfitter in a lowland bed and a notebook left on a doorstep like a debt being called in — Juniper had been the first. They had staged the camp afterward, neatened it, walked the gear out along a false line of travel so the searchers would read *wandering loss, exposure, misadventure.* "They were thorough," Emrys said. "It was professional work. That is what frightened me most. Not the guns. The bookkeeping."

"And you hid."

"I hid in the ice caves for eleven days with a satchel of documents and a knife, waiting either to die of cold or to be found by someone I could trust." His gaze went, briefly, deliberately, to the row where Ivo Voss sat in his warden's dress uniform. "I found two."

Counsel for the mining consortium — a silver-haired man named Dravec whose firm's name was embossed on more doors in the capital than the province's own seal — rose to cross-examine with the unhurried air of a man stirring cream into coffee. He was gentle with Emrys. That was the tactic; everyone saw it coming and it was still effective. He expressed sympathy for the ordeal. He wondered aloud whether eleven days in an ice cave, hypothermic, grieving, might not have affected the reliability of a witness's recollections. He noted that Mr. Cadwallader had no direct evidence linking any *named* official to the operational decisions — only documents whose provenance was, after all, a satchel carried out of a disaster zone.

"The provenance," Emrys said, "is that my five colleagues died carrying those papers out. Is that sufficient chain of custody for you, sir, or would you prefer they rise and sign affidavits?"

Someone in the gallery made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. Ostrava rapped for order. Dravec smiled thinly and tried another angle: the guide. Miss Fairweather, he observed, was herself a person of considerable interest to this inquiry — a blacklisted guide, an unauthorized entry into a closed range, a fugitive from administrative process. Could the tribunal really rely on testimony assembled in the company of such a person?

Emrys considered him for a long moment. When he answered, his voice was quiet enough that the whole room leaned in to hear it.

"I have spent my life in country that punishes error absolutely. Ice does not care about your reputation. On that glacier, the woman you are attempting to discredit held a rope under collapsing seracs for a stranger with frostbitten hands, and I am alive because of it. You may question her judgment, Mr. Dravec. I have seen her judgment tested at altitude, and yours tested in a warm room. I know which one I'd trust on a rope."

* * *

Ivo Voss had worn the uniform for nineteen years, and he had always believed the uniform meant something specific: that the man inside it stood between the wild country and the people who would strip it, and between the rules and the people who would bend them. For nineteen years the belief had held. Then a redacted file, a gravel bar, a slot gorge with a cut glove snagged on rebar, and a glacier full of men with rifles sent by the very ministry that paid his wages — and the belief had developed hairline fractures, the kind you don't see until the whole face comes off the mountain.

He was called to testify on the fourth day.

The early questions were routine. His discovery of the abandoned camp. His report, and its dismissal. Counsel for the inquiry wanted the timeline established, and Ivo gave it cleanly, dates and grid references, the language of reports, the dialect he was fluent in.

Then Dravec took over, and Ivo felt the room change temperature.

"Warden Voss. On the fourteenth of last month, you encountered an unauthorized party on the river inside a closed range. Is that correct?"

"It is."

"You had lawful authority to detain them. You did not. Why?"

Because she was Rosalind. Because the file was wrong. Because every instinct I trusted more than my own heartbeat said the closure itself was the crime. He said none of that. He said: "Because the closure order was issued on the basis of a falsified incident report, and I believed detaining the searchers would serve the interests of the persons who falsified it."

Dravec's smile deepened by a millimeter. "You are aware that the closure order was duly issued by your own directorate."

"I am aware of who drafted it. There are drafts in evidence. Exhibit forty-one."

"There is a suggestion in exhibit forty-one. A draft is not a decision."

"No," Ivo agreed. "Decisions get made higher up. That's generally the design."

The chairman cleared his throat. "Warden, you will confine yourself—"

"With respect, Mr. Chairman, I intend to." And here it was, the moment he had known was coming since the day he'd requested the old files and found her name redacted through them like a wound stitched shut. He had gone over it a hundred times in the nights since the checkpoint, since the prosecutor's office, since the news broke and the arrests began. He had weighed it the way you weigh a crossing: what it would hold, what it would cost.

Dravec leaned forward. "Warden Voss, during the events under inquiry, did you at any time act outside your authority? Did you knowingly fail to execute a lawful order? Did you aid and abet persons in defiance of a lawful closure?"

Ivo looked at the table. Under it, his hands were steady, which surprised him. Nineteen years of writing reports in which other people's small evasions were catalogued as misconduct. He knew exactly what the words *knowingly* and *lawful* were worth in a room like this, and he knew what Dravec was building: a wall of procedure behind which the real questions would hide forever. Answer carefully, hedge, plead the letter of things, and he would walk out with his pension and his badge and the blacklist intact in everything but name.

He thought of Rosalind in the coal seam, fever-light in her eyes as she finally told him the truth she'd swallowed for seven years. She had chosen justice over invisibility. She had walked into a checkpoint full of armed men to do it.

"Yes," Ivo said.

The hum of the fluorescent panels seemed suddenly very loud.

"To which question?" Dravec asked.

"All three. I tracked her instead of arresting her. I ignored the closure order. I carried those documents out of the range knowing I had no authority to do so, and I would do every part of it again, because the order was bought and the closure was a lid on a grave with six people in it." He turned slightly, addressing the tribunal directly now, his voice level as a surveyed line. "And I'll tell you what else, since we're establishing the record. If the tribunal intends to ask me to state that the blacklist against Rosalind Fairweather rested on legitimate findings, I cannot say so, because I have read the original incident file, and it was altered. If the tribunal intends to ask me to affirm that I discharged my duties faithfully throughout these events, I cannot say so, for the reasons just stated. So before counsel asks me anything further, you should know: I am resigning my commission. Effective today. I will answer every question you put to me as a private citizen, under oath, with nothing left to lose — which is, I've come to understand, the only position from which anyone in this building tells the whole truth."

He set his warrant card on the table, face up, next to the microphone. The laminate caught the light. Nineteen years fit inside something smaller than his palm.

Ostrava stared at him over his spectacles for a long moment. Then, quietly, in a voice that would be quoted in every paper in the country by morning: "The resignation is noted. The witness will continue."

* * *

Rosalind testified on the sixth day.

She had expected it to feel like the scaffold. Instead, walking the long floor toward the witness chair, past the gallery where strangers watched her with open faces — some hostile, some hungry, some simply stunned to see, in the flesh, the woman whose name had been a cautionary tale in guide huts for seven years — she found it felt like stepping onto rock she had already tested. She knew the weight of what she carried. She had chosen to carry it.

She told them all of it. The client death — Karel Brandt, falling on the Red Spire pitch, the rope cut by the edge she had warned the expedition organizer about in writing. The organizer's brother-in-law sat two ministries away from the men in Emrys's satchel; the finding against her had been written before the inquiry convened; she had taken the blacklist and the false name and the lowland outfitter's counter because the alternative was a fight she couldn't win alone. "I stayed silent to survive," she said. "That was a mistake, and I own it. Silence is how a thing like this ring works. It doesn't need many liars. It needs everyone honest to keep quiet."

Dravec tried her too. He got less far. Seven years of being hunted teaches a person exactly where the traps are set.

On the seventh day, the tribunal struck the blacklist. Ostrava read the finding aloud in his dry ledger-keeper's voice — the 20XX closure of Rosalind Fairweather's guiding certification *vacated*, the underlying incident determination *annulled*, referral of the alteration to federal prosecutors — and somewhere in the middle of the sentence the gallery began, against all decorum, to applaud. Rosalind sat with her hands flat on her knees and let the sound break over her like spray, and discovered that vindication earned feels nothing like vindication granted. Granted, it would have been a gift. Earned, it was a load coming off a rope — you feel it in your shoulders first, then your knees, and then you realize how long you'd been braced.

* * *

The courthouse steps were granite, worn hollow in the middle by a century of feet, and the afternoon light came down the street at a long gold slant that made even the ministry buildings look almost honest.

Ivo was waiting at the bottom, and for the first time in nineteen years he was out of uniform — a plain grey jacket, a plain shirt, a man shaped like any other man on the pavement. He looked strange to himself. He kept reaching for the warrant card that wasn't there and finding his hand closing on empty air, and each time it was less like losing something and more like setting down a pack at the end of a carry.

Rosalind came out of the doors into that light and stopped when she saw him. Neither of them spoke for a moment. Around them the city went on — trams, pigeons, a newsboy shouting names that had been untouchable a month ago — but the space on the steps was quiet, the way the air goes quiet in a col between storms.

"So," she said. "Unbadged."

"Unblacklisted." He looked at her — really looked, the way he hadn't been able to in the hearings, where watching her had felt like something he wasn't permitted. "How does it fit?"

"Loose." She laughed, and it cracked a little in the middle. "Seven years of being no one. I don't know how to be a person with a name yet. I signed the register this morning and my hand shook worse than it ever has on a traverse."

"It'll pass."

"Will it?"

"Everything passes," Ivo said. "That's the whole lesson of the mountains. Ice, grief, careers, governments. Everything moves or it gets buried." He hesitated, and then took something from his pocket and held it out on his palm: a battered notebook, water-stained, its elastic band perished. Juniper's notebook — the one that had started everything, returned from evidence that morning with a receipt Rosalind hadn't yet opened. "There's a memorial climb being organized. The families. They want to take the team's effects back up to the source, now that—" he glanced up the street, toward the distant blue smear of the Karst Range above the rooftops, "—now that the route stands open. Calving season ends in spring. Someone will need to lead it."

Rosalind took the notebook. Held it. It weighed almost nothing, and it had weighed everything.

"Spring," she said.

"Spring," Ivo agreed. "High water on the river, the glacier calved clean, the source in daylight for the first time in years." He paused, and the gold light caught the lines around his eyes. "I'm told the best guide in the Karst Range is available again."

She looked up at him — unbadged, unhidden, hers again in whatever way they had ever been — and the river of the city flowed around them, and somewhere high and white and patient, the source waited.

"They'll want a second on that climb," Rosalind said. "Someone who knows the gorge."

"I hear there's a former warden with nothing but time."

She smiled, and tucked the notebook under her arm like a compass bearing, and together they walked down the worn steps into the ordinary, astonishing world.
