## 22. The River Again

Spring came late to the Karst Range that year, the way it always did, arriving not as a season but as a negotiation. The valley floor greened first, a thin lie of color along the riverbanks, while the high country stayed white and silent for another month, holding its breath. Rosalind Fairweather watched it happen from the porch of the outfitter's post she now half-owned, coffee going cold in her hands, and she found she could no longer read the glacier's moods with dread. She read them the way she'd once read them as a girl — with appetite.

The source had opened on the fourteenth of May. She knew because Ivo called her from the fire lookout at dawn, his voice rough with sleep and something else, something lighter than he'd carried in years. "The serac field's dropped its winter skirt," he said. "You can see blue ice at the headwall from here. First time since before the inquiry." He paused, and she heard him turn toward the window, toward the pale gold light coming up over the eastern ridge. "Rosalind. It's open."

She stood there a long moment with the phone against her ear and the river loud below her, running high and brown with snowmelt, carrying the mountain down to the sea one grain at a time. *It's open.* Two words, and her chest did something complicated — grief and relief braided so tight she couldn't tell which was which. The route that had nearly killed them, that had taken Emrys's fingers and very nearly Ivo's life, that had sealed itself behind them like a door closing on a crime scene — it had unlocked. The mountain was showing them what it had kept all winter.

"We should go up," she said.

"I know."

"Together this time. Not running. Not hiding."

"I know," he said again, softer. And then, because he was Ivo, and because even after everything he still reached for procedure the way other men reached for prayer: "I'll file the permit tomorrow."

She laughed — actually laughed, out loud, alone on the porch with the steam rising off the river — because the idea of Ivo Voss filing a permit for anything ever again was almost too much tenderness to bear. He'd resigned his commission rather than perjure himself at the hearings. He'd walked out of the warden service with nothing but his warrant card in his pocket, the one he'd held up in an ice cave to buy a stranger's trust, and he hadn't replaced it with anything. No badge. No banner. Just a man who knew the range better than anyone alive and had finally stopped apologizing for it.

"File whatever you want," she told him. "But we're not going up as wardens or guides or anything with a title. We're going up for them."

* * *

They went up in June, when the meltwater ran so loud in the gorge that conversation was impossible and neither of them minded. There were eleven of them. Families of the lost survey team, mostly — Juniper's grandfather would have come but had died in March, quietly, in his sleep, having lived just long enough to see the arrests begin and the blacklist struck down. His daughter came instead, carrying his old brass compass, and Rosalind took one look at the woman's face at the trailhead and understood that this climb was not a memorial. It was a burial they'd never gotten to hold.

Emrys came too. He'd lost two fingers to frostbite and most of his faith in institutions, but he'd gained a stubbornness that made him excellent company on a rope team. He carried the satchel himself — empty now, its documents long since delivered and dissected in courtrooms in two countries — and he'd told Rosalind once, over bad whiskey, that he wanted to put it in the ice where his colleagues were. "Let it do some honest work," he'd said. "Guarding nothing."

Rosalind led. Of course she led; nobody even discussed it. But it was different now, the leading. A year ago she'd climbed these slopes under a false name with a bounty of silence on her back, every step an argument with the mountain about whether she deserved to be on it. Now her name was cleared — printed in the inquiry findings, exonerated in language so formal it felt almost absurd after years of whispers — and the mountain hadn't changed at all. That was the thing she'd learned, the hard lesson the Karst had ground into her across two decades: the wilderness forgives nothing. It doesn't remember your crimes or your vindications. It only remembers weight, and weather, and the angle of a slope. Everything else — guilt, blame, redemption — that was the country of people. And people, she had finally come to believe, could forgive. Could be forgiven. Could carry each other down a mountain when one of them couldn't walk.

Ivo walked beside her, unbadged, unhurried, reading the glacier the way he always had — with a professional's eye and, now, a free man's shoulders. At the moraine where the patrol had turned on them, where he'd taken the bullet that ended his career and nearly his life, he stopped and looked at the scatter of rock for a long moment. Rosalind watched him. He didn't say anything. Neither did she. Some ground you just have to stand on once more, on your own terms, before it stops owning you.

* * *

The source, when they reached it, was unrecognizable.

All winter the calving had chewed at the face, and spring had finished the work: the great wall of ice that had hidden the mining scars, the blasting chambers, the illegal adits bored into the mountain's root — all of it had come down in a series of collapses that the seismographs recorded and the newspapers made small mention of. What remained was a new face, raw and blue, streaked with sediment lines like tree rings, and open to the sky for the first time in living memory. Daylight fell into places that had been dark for centuries.

The families stood in a loose crescent at the edge of the proglacial lake, which was choked with brash ice, bobbing shards the size of tables and cars, clicking and grinding against each other in the meltwater. The air smelled of stone water and ancient cold. Somewhere under all that ice were the last resting places of eight surveyors who had died because they wrote down what they saw.

Juniper's aunt unwrapped the compass first. She held it a while, turning it over, then handed it to Rosalind without a word. Rosalind weighed it in her palm — brass worn bright at the edges by someone's thumb, decades of someone's thumb — and walked to the water's edge.

"You don't throw things into a glacier," she said quietly, to the group, in the voice she used for clients on their first morning. "You offer them. There's a difference." She crouched, set the compass on a flat slab of clean ice at the shoreline, and let the sun do the rest. By afternoon the meltwater would take it. By autumn it would be somewhere in the ice, beginning its slow thousand-year transit through the body of the glacier, carried toward the snout where, someday, if anyone cared to look, it would emerge again — scoured, dented, honest. Glaciers keep everything. They just make you wait for the truth of it.

One by one the families came forward. Photographs laminated against the wet. A wedding ring. A folded survey map, annotated in Juniper's small precise hand — the same hand Rosalind had studied for weeks in that notebook, learning a dead woman's shorthand until she felt she knew her. Emrys laid the empty satchel down last, pressing it flat against the ice with both hands, the missing fingers and all, and said something too low for anyone to catch. Rosalind didn't try to hear it. Some testimony is only for the ice.

Ivo stood apart, as he often did, watching the faces more than the offering. When it was done, when the last item had been given to the meltwater, he came and stood beside Rosalind at the shore, close enough that their shoulders touched through the windshell.

"You thought we'd never stand here," he said.

"I thought we'd never stand anywhere," she said. "Together, I mean. I thought the mountain would decide it, and the mountain doesn't negotiate."

"It negotiated." He nodded at the new blue face of the source, at the daylight falling into the old dark. "Look at it. It gave everything back."

"Not everything." She turned to look at him fully then, this man who had chosen her over his badge on a gravel bar two years ago and had never once made her feel indebted for it. "Nothing comes back whole. That's not how rivers work, or glaciers, or people. You get the water back but not the shape of the snowflake. You get the man but not the career. You get your name but not the years." She looked down at the brash ice grinding its slow music against the shore. "But you get enough. That's the bargain. Enough."

Ivo was quiet a moment. Then he reached into his jacket pocket and took out his warrant card — the old one, expired now, the laminate clouded, the photograph of a younger man with harder eyes. He'd carried it through everything. Through the hearings. Through the resignation. Through the long convalescence and the first clumsy season of learning to be a civilian. She'd seen it in his pocket a hundred times and never asked why.

He crouched at the waterline and set it on the ice beside the last of the offerings.

"You don't have to," she said.

"I know." He straightened up. "That's why I'm doing it."

They watched the meltwater take it together, the little rectangle of plastic riding a film of runoff, tilting, catching light, sliding out onto the brash and gone — absorbed into the same cold archive that held the compass and the photographs and the men and women who had told the truth at the cost of everything. Rosalind felt something in her chest unlock, some plate of armor she'd forgotten she was wearing, and she leaned into Ivo's shoulder and let the wind have her hair and did not cry, exactly, though the distinction hardly mattered at four thousand meters with meltwater singing everywhere.

* * *

They camped that night above the lake, on the bare moraine, and the sky did what Karst skies do in June — refused to go fully dark, holding a bruised pearl of light in the north while the peaks glowed faintly rose. Most of the party slept. Rosalind couldn't. She got up past midnight and walked a little way from the tents, boots crunching on frozen scree, and stood looking at the source in the strange half-light.

It looked different at night. Softer. The blue ice held the darkness and gave it back changed, the way water changes a stone. Somewhere beneath it, in channels no map would ever show, the river was already moving — the same river she'd slipped onto alone at dawn three years ago, a blacklisted woman with a dead friend's plea in her ears and nothing left to lose. It had carried her to everything: to the gorge, to the notebook, to Emrys and his knife and his satchel, to Ivo standing on a gravel bar with his duty in one hand and his heart in the other, choosing, choosing, always choosing her in the end.

She heard boots behind her and didn't turn. Only one person walked that quietly.

"They'll ask us to guide again," Ivo said, coming to stand beside her. "After today. The families talk. Word gets around."

"I know."

"The blacklist is gone, but the reputation economy isn't. Half the lowland outfitters still think you're cursed."

"Let them." She smiled at the ice. "Cursed guides get the best clients. The ones who actually listen."

He huffed — almost a laugh. Then, quieter: "I've been thinking about what comes next. For us. I'm not good at idle, Rosalind. You know that. A man can only reorganize his gear shed so many times."

"There's a school," she said, and felt her pulse pick up, the way it did on steep ground. "Downriver. Nothing fancy. Teach navigation, glacier travel, judgment. Teach people what the mountain actually is instead of what they saw in a film." She paused. "Teach them what happened here, eventually. When it's history instead of wound."

Ivo was silent long enough that she glanced over. In the pearl-dark his face was unreadable, and then it wasn't; it was the face he'd worn in the ice cave when he'd held up the warrant card for Emrys — certain, exposed, done with hedging.

"A school," he repeated. "With a name?"

"I've been working on that."

"And?"

And she told him. And the name was right, and he knew it was right, and he said yes the way he said everything that mattered — once, plainly, without taking it back.

Below them the river ran on through the dark, patient and immense, doing the only thing it had ever done: carrying the mountain down, grain by grain, year by year, keeping everything, forgiving nothing, waiting. Rosalind took Ivo's hand. His fingers were cold and scarred and sure, and around them the wilderness stood open under the light that never quite died, vast and indifferent and, tonight, almost kind.

In the morning they would descend. The route would stay open a few more weeks, and then calving season would close it again, as it closed every year, as it would close forever after they were gone. It didn't matter. They had learned what the mountain had to teach: that nothing up here is permanent except the truth of the ice, and that people — broken, stubborn, forgiven people — are the ones who carry each other home.

The river sang all night. In the morning, they followed it down.
