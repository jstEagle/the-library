## 1. Miles, Not Years

The morning Cyrus Sandoval decided the world was still turning properly, she was upside down over the Kessuma Valley with a crate of penicillin strapped where a passenger ought to be and a thermos of coffee wedged between her knees.

She wasn't upside down on purpose. The wind off the eastern ridges had a habit of shoving her little yellow De Havilland around like a drunk uncle at a wedding, and this morning it was in rare form. She rode it out — wings rocking, coffee sloshing against the lid — and when the air smoothed out again she righted herself, checked her heading, and said aloud to nobody in particular:

"Good morning to you too."

That was the thing about flying the interior. You talked to yourself, or to the plane, or to the weather, because there was precious little else to talk to. Three hundred miles of green country folded like a rumpled blanket, rivers threading through it bright as spilled wire, and maybe forty people scattered across all of it who'd wave at the sound of an engine before they even looked up to see whose.

Cyrus knew every one of those waves by heart.

She landed first at Mabu Station, which was less a station than a clearing with ambitions, where old Ferro met her at the strip with his hat held out like a collection plate.

"Medicine," she said, handing down the crate. "And don't tell me you're out of the good tobacco again, because I checked the manifest and I know what's in here."

"You wound me." Ferro peered into the box. "Also I am out of the good tobacco."

"Then we're both liars and we cancel each other out."

He laughed, gap-toothed, and pressed something into her hand anyway — a twist of paper containing three fingers of the *actual* good tobacco, hoarded against her arrival like a relic. "For the pilot," he said solemnly, "who is the only person in this territory who has never once lied to me about delivery times."

"That's because I never promise delivery times."

"And that," said Ferro, "is why you are a professional."

* * *

By noon she'd made four more stops. A bag of mail for the Okoro sisters, who ran the trading post at Two Rivers and read their letters standing in the open doorway so they could flag down gossip from passing boats. Vaccines for the mission school, packed in ice that had mostly given up but hadn't yet surrendered. A replacement carburetor for Delia Marsh, who waved from her porch with a shotgun across her lap — not hostile, just thorough; Delia greeted everything with the shotgun, including sunrises. And a live chicken, for reasons nobody had ever adequately explained to Cyrus, addressed simply to "the man upriver who will know."

The man upriver did know. He took the chicken without comment, paid without being asked, and tipped his hat, and Cyrus climbed back into the sky feeling like the whole day had been a well-oiled machine with her name stamped on the cowling.

This was the arithmetic she lived by. Not years — years were just weather, they came and went and left nothing behind but gray in your beard if you were unlucky enough to grow one. Miles. Miles you could count. Miles you could bank. Every mile flown was a mile that couldn't be taken back, couldn't crash twice, couldn't burn. She kept a little notebook in her breast pocket, oil-stained, and at the end of each day she wrote the total in pencil: **1,240 mi**, or **980 mi, headwind**, or once, memorably, **610 mi and a chicken**.

Three years of notebooks filled a shoebox under her bunk at the strip outside Port Calloway. Some nights she added them up. She'd never once told anyone why.

Because the answer had a shape. It was a ridge line, sixty miles long, standing up out of the interior like the spine of some drowned animal — the Escarpment. And it had teeth.

* * *

Port Calloway in the late afternoon smelled of diesel, river mud, and someone frying onions, which meant it smelled like home, though Cyrus would have denied this under oath. She taxied the De Havilland — the *Kestrel*, because naming a plane after a small fierce bird that ate things larger than itself seemed honest — onto her strip at the edge of town, tied her down, and covered the engine with a tarp weighted with bricks, all with the automatic tenderness of a woman putting a child to bed.

The mail office was closing when she got there, but old Mrs. Adeyemi saw her coming and left the door open, which was its own kind of greeting.

"Anything for me?" Cyrus asked, though she already knew the shape of the week's mail: two invoices, a parts catalog, and a postcard from her sister in the capital that would say *Are you eating enough?* in handwriting that got more disapproving every year.

Mrs. Adeyemi drew out one envelope and set it on the counter with the care of a woman handling something that might bite.

Cyrus looked at it.

It was good paper. That was the first wrong thing. Nobody in the interior used paper like that — heavy cream stock, the kind that cost more per sheet than most folks made in a day. The second wrong thing was the handwriting on the front, which was elegant and slanted and utterly familiar, the way a scar is familiar.

*M. VOSS* was not the return address. There was no return address. But the postmark was from a city Cyrus had no reason to think Mara Voss had ever set foot in, and the letter inside, when Cyrus turned it over, was sealed with wax, which was either pretension or paranoia, and knowing Voss it had been both.

"Came on the coastal boat Tuesday," Mrs. Adeyemi offered. "Been sitting in the rack. Didn't want to send it out with the runner — felt like the kind of letter a person should get face-to-face."

"What kind of letter is that?"

"The kind," said Mrs. Adeyemi, "that makes a person stand very still in my office for a long time."

Cyrus realized she was doing exactly that. She picked up the envelope, thanked the old woman, and walked out into the onion-smelling evening without opening it, because some part of her understood that once she opened it, the last three quiet years would be officially over.

She opened it anyway. Of course she did. She was Cyrus Sandoval, and patience had never once been mistaken for one of her virtues.

*Sandoval,* the letter began. No *Dear*. No *Cyrus*. Just the surname, like a challenge thrown down across a card table.

*I need a pilot for one run. Interior side of the Escarpment. Medical cargo, triple your going rate, and I'll pay half up front. No questions — I know how you feel about questions, you always did mistake them for accusations.*

*I'll come to your strip Thursday. Don't bother deciding before then. You won't decide until you see me. We both know that.*

*— V.*

Cyrus stood in the middle of the street while the lamps came on along the waterfront, and read it twice more, and then a fourth time, hunting for something in the words that wasn't there.

Triple rate. Interior side of the Escarpment. Those two facts should not have been able to exist in the same sentence, the way matter and antimatter shouldn't. Everyone who flew the interior knew the rule, and everyone knew whose rule it was: *Sandoval doesn't cross the Wall.* She'd said it often enough, and loudly enough, and with enough of a look behind it that even pilots who'd never met her repeated it like scripture. It wasn't superstition. It was arithmetic. Sixty miles of ridge, one storm cell, one overloaded aircraft, and two men who had trusted her with their lives and lost them — Wren Okafor, her copilot, twenty-nine years old and laughing when the wind hit; and Benji Sarr, the loadmaster, who'd been saving up to buy his mother a house with a roof that didn't sing in the rain.

Three years ago. One smuggling run that Voss had talked her into, because that was the other piece of scripture everyone knew: whatever Mara Voss wanted, Mara Voss eventually got, and the people around her paid the difference.

And then Voss had vanished. No note, no body, no debt collected. Just gone, the way weather goes — leaving damage behind and taking the explanation with it.

Now here was her handwriting, asking Cyrus to fly the exact sky where Wren and Benji had gone down, offering triple pay as if money were the currency this transaction would actually run on.

"No questions," Cyrus said aloud, to the street, to the gulls arguing over the fish market scraps. "Right."

A boy selling roasted corn watched her fold the letter into her breast pocket, next to the mileage notebook, and decided wisely not to ask if she wanted any corn.

* * *

That night she didn't sleep so much as lie in her bunk above the hangar listening to the river and doing the thing she did instead of sleeping, which was flying. Not real flying — memory flying. Her hands moved on the blanket through the old routine: throttle, trim, scan, breathe. Wren's voice in her earpiece, young and easy: *You've got a crosswind, boss, mind your drift.* Benji humming in the back, off-key, always off-key, bless him.

They'd been crossing the Escarpment at dusk in weather that Voss had sworn was clearing. It had not been clearing. It had been the leading edge of a cell that swallowed the ridge whole, and the *Meridian* — Voss's plane, Voss's contract, Voss's schedule — had gone into the updrafts over the Wall like a leaf into a furnace fan. Cyrus had held her own aircraft in the air by what she could only describe afterward as spite, limped back to the far side, and put down in a beet field. By the time searchers reached the crash site on the ridge, there was nothing to bring home but the tail number and a boot.

Voss had been on that flight too. Voss had walked away from the wreck site — that much the inquiry established, before the inquiry ran out of funding, before the syndicate whose cargo they'd been carrying made it very clear that further questions would be unhealthy. Voss had walked away, and Cyrus had spent three years not knowing whether that meant dead, or captured, or — and this was the thought that woke her at 3 a.m. more nights than she admitted — *running, alive, having chosen the door over the crew.*

The letter sat on her chest of drawers where she'd propped it against the lamp. Cream-colored. Patient. In the dark it glowed faintly, the way debts do.

Thursday, it said. Two days.

Cyrus got up, lit the lamp, and opened the mileage notebook to a fresh page. Old habit. She wrote the day's total — 1,410 miles, four stops, one chicken — and then, beneath it, for the first time in three years, she wrote something else:

**Escarpment: 0 mi.**

She looked at the zero for a long time. Then she closed the book, turned off the lamp, and lay back down, and told herself firmly that a zero was not an invitation, it was a record, and records existed precisely so you didn't have to relive them.

But sleep, when it finally came, brought the sound of wind over a ridge line, and a laugh she'd have given anything to hear again, and a voice like cut glass saying *triple your going rate, Sandoval* — and somewhere under the dream, in the place where stubbornness lives, Cyrus Sandoval began, without admitting it, to calculate.

Fuel for the range. Load limits. The weather window in October, narrow as a knife.

Two days. Thursday.

She'd decide when she saw her. They both knew that.
