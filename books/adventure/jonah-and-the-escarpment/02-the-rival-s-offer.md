## 2. The Rival's Offer

The letter had sat on my kitchen table for eleven days before I burned it, and I want it noted for the record that I lasted eleven days. That's restraint. That's practically sainthood, by Sandoval standards.

I'd read it maybe forty times first, which rather undercuts the burning, but a person can do two contradictory things at once if she's committed enough. That's basically the definition of being a pilot.

*Dear Cyrus,* it began, in handwriting I'd have known anywhere — that slanted, impatient script like the letters were trying to take off before the words were finished. *One run. Past the wall. Triple your rate. No questions asked, and none answered. You know where to find me. — M.V.*

No address. No explanation. Just the name at the bottom sitting there like a stone dropped in still water.

Mara Voss.

Three years of silence, and then four sentences and a signature. It was so exactly like her that I laughed out loud alone in my kitchen, which is the kind of thing that worries the neighbors, if you have neighbors. I have a dog named Biscuit who is technically a neighbor and he didn't even look up.

The morning she actually showed up, I was elbow-deep in the Cessna's cowling, arguing with a carburetor that had opinions of its own.

"You're flooding her," said a voice behind me. "You always flood her. You've flooded her since 2019."

I did not drop the wrench. I want that on the record too. I set it down very slowly and deliberately on the wing strut, because dropping things is for people who are surprised, and I had spent eleven days and one small fire preparing not to be surprised.

"Mara."

"Cyrus." She was leaning against the fuel drum with her arms crossed, wearing a jacket that cost more than my annual fuel budget and sunglasses that probably had their own insurance policy. Three years had put some weather on her face, but it was the kind of weather that improves coastline. "Your carburetor's a lost cause. So is your fence. And whatever that is—" she nodded at Biscuit, who was performing his signature move, lying in the sun looking like a rug that had given up mid-rolling-out, "—is that a dog or a spill?"

"He's a co-pilot."

"Your standards have fallen."

"My standards are exactly where they've always been. That's the whole problem, isn't it." I wiped my hands on a rag that was cleaner than my hands. "Eleven days, Mara. You couldn't have called?"

"I don't call. Calls leave records." She took off the sunglasses, and there it was — the thing underneath all the expensive leather and the practiced lean. Something tired. Something that had been running. "And this conversation shouldn't exist on paper either, so let's have it fast."

"The answer's no."

"You haven't heard the offer."

"I read the offer. Four sentences. One run past the Escarpment, triple rate, no questions. I've had three years to compose my answer and it hasn't changed: no." I picked the wrench back up just to have something honest in my hands. "There's a reason my route map has a hole in it shaped like that ridge, and you know the reason better than anybody alive."

Something crossed her face then — quick, gone before I could name it. Guilt has many disguises. On Mara Voss it wore the costume of a woman checking her watch.

"Triple rate," she said. "Cash. Half up front."

"I don't need money."

"Nobody needs money. Everybody wants it. There's a difference and it doesn't matter." She pushed off the fuel drum and walked the length of my strip, slow, like she was reading it. My strip is six hundred meters of crushed coral and stubbornness, and most people walk it once and never think about it again. Mara walked it like she was remembering every takeoff we'd ever raced each other out of, back when racing was how we said good morning.

She came back and stood at the wing, close enough that I could smell jet fuel and something floral fighting over her.

"It's medicine," she said. "A clinic upriver of the wall. Vaccines, antibiotics, antimalarials. Real cargo, real manifest, real patients. Kids, Cyrus. The kind of run you used to say you got into this business for, back when you gave speeches about it over cheap rum."

"I remember the speeches."

"Do you? Because from here it looks like you fix carburetors and haul gossip and engine parts to settlements that already have both, and you haven't crossed the ridge in three years, and everyone in four hundred miles knows why, and nobody says it to your face because they're scared of you." She smiled, and it almost reached her eyes. "I'm not scared of you. That's why we always worked."

"We never worked. We competed. There's a difference and it matters."

"Semantics."

"Geography," I said. "You're standing on my strip asking me to fly through the place where Danny and Okonkwo died, and you're calling it semantics."

The name landed between us like a dropped tool. Danny Reyes. My copilot. Twenty-six years old, laughed like an engine catching on a cold morning, dead on the north face of the Escarpment along with Okonkwo Abara, my loadmaster, who sang off-key in three languages and had promised his mother he'd be home for Christmas.

That was the run Mara had booked us. That was the contract with her name on it that she'd vanished from two days after the crash, leaving nothing but a forwarding address that turned out to be a defunct marina and a debt structure that would have made a cartel blush.

"I didn't come here to talk about that," she said quietly.

"No. You came here to talk about vaccines."

"Yes."

"And you thought the way to get me to fly the ridge again was money."

"I thought the way was to give you a reason bigger than the fear." She said it flat, no apology in it, which was the most honest thing she'd said yet. "Money works on most people. On you I figured it'd have to be the cargo. You never could say no to somebody who needed lifting."

I looked at her for a long moment. The wind came down the strip the way it does in the late afternoon, warm and smelling of cut grass and river, and somewhere behind us Biscuit sighed the sigh of a creature with no ambitions whatsoever, and I hated how well she knew me. That's the thing about old rivals. They know exactly where the soft places are, because they spent years mapping them for other purposes.

"The answer's still no," I said.

"Okay." She shrugged, put the sunglasses back on, and turned toward the road.

Just like that. No argument, no second pitch, no parting shot. And that — more than the letter, more than the triple rate, more than the word *vaccines* — is what stopped me. Mara Voss does not accept a no. Mara Voss treats a no as the opening bid in a negotiation that ends with you saying yes and feeling like it was your idea. If she was walking away, then walking away was the play, and I stood there holding my wrench like a fool while my own brain ran the trap backward and found the trigger.

"Mara."

She stopped. Didn't turn around. The sunglasses caught the sun and threw it back at me.

"Why now?" I said. "Why this clinic, why this week, why show up in person when calls leave records? What's actually going on?"

For a moment she was very still, the way water goes still over something deep. Then she turned, and the smile was back, the expensive one, the one that had closed a hundred deals and opened zero hearts.

"There's a boy," she said. "Up there past the wall. Eleven years old. He needs what's in those crates, and the syndicate that runs that valley won't let a supply truck through, and every pilot within range has been paid or persuaded to stay away. Except you. You're unpersuadable. It's your worst quality."

"A boy."

"His name is Ilo. He's got something in his lungs that antibiotics laugh at unless you catch it early, and it wasn't caught early, because Halvern Mining decided last month that medical shipments might be cover for something else, so they shut the road." She spread her hands. "So it's cover for medicine. Congratulations, the irony writes itself."

I should have said no again. I want to be clear that every alarm I owned was ringing — the ones about her, the ones about the ridge, the ones about a story with a child in it, which is the oldest lever in the world and I recognized it as such. But knowing a lever is being pulled doesn't stop it pulling. That's what makes them levers.

"One condition," I heard myself say, and the sound of my own voice surprised me, the way it does when it decides something ahead of the rest of you.

"Name it."

"Danny's name. You said it in your letter — no wait, you didn't. You wrote four sentences and a signature and not one word about him." I stepped closer. "You were the one who booked that flight. You were the one whose cargo schedule put us on that ridge in that weather window. And then you disappeared. So before I sign anything, before I touch those crates, you're going to stand on my strip and tell me what happened that night. All of it. No questions withheld, since we're doing business that way."

The wind moved the grass along the fence line. Somewhere far off, a boat engine buzzed on the river like a trapped fly.

"That's not a condition," she said at last. "That's a bill. And I can't pay it today."

"Then today we don't have a deal."

"Cyrus—"

"Tomorrow, though." I picked up my rag, folded it, unfolded it, bought myself three seconds. "Load your crates tonight. We fly at dawn. And somewhere over open country, with nothing but sky around us, you're going to start talking, and you're not going to stop until I understand why my crew is dead and you're wearing a jacket that costs more than my plane's annual inspection."

She studied me for a long time. Behind the sunglasses I couldn't see her eyes, but I could see her jaw working, the way it used to when we'd sit across a table splitting a route and she was deciding whether to cheat me or beat me fair.

"Dawn," she said finally. "Crates will be here by five."

"And Mara — the triple rate stays. Not because I need it. Because if I'm flying that ridge again, I'm charging the mountain itself for the privilege."

The ghost of a real smile, there and gone. "Still terrible at sentiment."

"Still better at it than you."

She walked down the strip toward the road, and I watched her go, and I'll admit to the page that I watched longer than was strictly necessary. There's a specific loneliness to watching someone walk away from your strip when they're the last person alive who remembers it the way you do.

Then I turned back to the Cessna, and Biscuit opened one eye as if to say, *you absolute idiot*, and I said, out loud, to a dog, "It's medicine. It's a kid. It's fine."

Biscuit put his head back down. Dogs are honest that way.

I worked until dark. New gaskets, fresh oil, the carburetor rebuilt twice because the first rebuild was done angry and anger makes sloppy threads. I checked the ridge charts even though I didn't need to — I could draw the Escarpment passes from memory, every saddle and shear line, the way you can draw the face of someone you loved. Weather briefing said a system building west. Monsoon season coming early, the radio man said, cheerful as a man announcing a sale.

At four-thirty in the morning I stood at the edge of my strip with coffee going cold in my hand, and I saw the headlights coming up the access road, two trucks deep, and I felt the old wound shift under its scar tissue like something turning over in its sleep.

Danny Reyes. Okonkwo Abara. Three years of never crossing the wall, and tomorrow at dawn I was going to point my nose straight at it, because a rival I didn't trust had said a name — *Ilo* — and mentioned crates, plural, and something in the way she'd said *the syndicate shut the road* had snagged on me like a burr in a sock.

Trucks don't get shut down for vaccines. I knew that. Some tired, honest corner of me knew it at four-thirty in the morning, standing in the dark with a cold cup.

But dawn was coming anyway, and the crates were coming with it, and I had given my word, and the truth — the plain, embarrassing, un-flyable truth — is that I wanted to see the ridge again. I wanted to look the thing that killed my crew dead in the eye from two thousand feet and keep flying.

Unfinished business is a powerful fuel. Nobody tells you it burns dirty.

The trucks rolled onto my strip, headlights sweeping the coral, and the first crate came off the bed heavier than any box of medicine has a right to be.
