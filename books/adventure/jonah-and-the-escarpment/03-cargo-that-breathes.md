## 3. Cargo That Breathes

Dawn at Sandoval Strip came the way it always did: gray, then gold, then the smell of wet grass and avgas that Cyrus had loved longer than she'd loved most people. She was on her knees under the Otter's belly with a grease gun before the sun cleared the treeline, because a plane you didn't greet in the morning was a plane that would introduce itself to you rudely at altitude.

"Morning, ugly," she told the fuselage. The Otter was thirty-one years old and looked every day of it, dents and patches and one door that required a conversation rather than a pull. But she was honest. Cyrus valued honesty above beauty, above comfort, and — if she was being strict about it — above several of the commandments.

The contract sat folded in her breast pocket. One run. Past the Escarpment. Triple pay. *No questions*, Voss had said, and Cyrus had signed anyway, which meant she'd already broken her own first rule of business: never sign anything while an old wound is talking.

She'd loaded the medical crates herself the night before. Or she'd stacked what she believed were medical crates — twelve of them, stenciled with a Red Crescent and a shipping mark out of Port Calloway, each one weighing about what a crate of ampoules and suture kits ought to weigh. She'd checked two of them. Two out of twelve. In her defense, it had been raining, and Voss had been standing there being Mara Voss, which was its own kind of weather.

Now the light came up long and low across the strip, and Cyrus finished the grease job, wiped her hands on a rag that had once been a shirt, and started the preflight.

That was when she heard the breathing.

It was very quiet. It was also unmistakably human, and it was coming from crate number seven.

Cyrus stood very still, the way you do when a snake is between your boots. Her hand went to the cargo bay's edge. She counted crates again, as though arithmetic might save her. Twelve. She'd loaded twelve. And twelve crates of medicine did not breathe in unison, hold their breath when she moved, then start breathing again in a rhythm that suggested six separate sets of lungs trying very hard to be furniture.

"Well," she said to the morning, "that's not tetanus."

She took the crowbar off the tool bench. Not to open the crate — not yet — but because a woman feels better holding a crowbar when her cargo turns out to have a pulse.

She pried the lid off crate seven in one motion, fast, the way you rip a bandage.

A boy looked up at her.

He was maybe eleven, maybe twelve, small for either, with a fever-bright stare and hair cut short in the uneven way of someone who'd done it themselves with a knife. He didn't scream. He didn't run. He just looked at her with the flat, calculating calm of a child who has already decided adults are weather — something that happens to you, not something you negotiate with.

Then he put his finger to his lips.

Behind him, packed into the crate like sardines with opinions, five more faces turned toward her. A heavyset man with a fisherman's forearms. A woman with a baby wrapped against her chest, the baby asleep, mercifully. Two younger men, brothers by the shape of their jaws. And a girl of perhaps sixteen who had positioned herself between the boy and the world and looked ready to prove something about it.

Six people. In crates marked MEDICAL SUPPLIES — HANDLE WITH CARE.

Cyrus stood there with a crowbar in her hand and let the whole thing settle into her chest like a stone dropping through cold water.

"You've got to be kidding me," she said.

* * *

Voss arrived twenty minutes later, walking up from the tree line where she'd clearly been sleeping rough rather than pay for a bunk, looking crisp in a way that was either discipline or denial. She carried two cups of coffee from the strip shack and handed one over like nothing in the world was wrong.

Cyrus did not take the coffee.

"Open crate seven lately?" Voss asked.

"I opened all twelve."

"Ah." Voss set both cups down on the wing strut, unhurried. "Then we should talk."

"We should talk." Cyrus laughed, and it came out wrong, too sharp. "Mara. There are *people* in my airplane. There are people in my airplane where the penicillin is supposed to be, and one of them is a *child*, and you stood on this strip three days ago and watched me load those crates and said not one word."

"They were always part of the deal."

"The deal was *medicine*. The deal was triple pay and no questions, and I assumed no questions meant smuggled cigarettes or untaxed fuel or, I don't know, a shipment of prayer flags for some temple with a customs problem. That is the tax on no questions, Mara. Cigarettes. Maybe a rifle. Not six human beings folded up like shirts."

"It's the same flight," Voss said. "Same fuel, same route, same risk. The medicine's still going. All of it. They're just riding along."

"Riding along." Cyrus pointed at crate seven, where the boy was now sitting up on the rim, watching them with the interest of someone at a tennis match. "That child rode along in a box for how long? Since Port Calloway?"

"Two days."

"*Two days.*"

"There wasn't time for a better plan. Halvern's people swept the dock district. If they'd waited—"

"If they'd waited, you'd have found another pilot. Don't dress this up as mercy, Mara. You found me because I'm cheap and I'm desperate and because you knew exactly which name to say to make me stupid."

Voss flinched. It was small — a tightening around the eyes, gone in half a second — but Cyrus had spent years learning to read that face across card tables and cockpit doors, and she caught it, and hated that catching it gave her no satisfaction at all.

"Say what you want about me," Voss said quietly. "But look at them before you decide what this is."

And Cyrus, because she was furious and because fury makes you stubborn about proving yourself right, went and looked.

* * *

They came out of the crates stiffly, unfolding joint by joint, the way old men get out of trucks. The heavyset man dropped to his knees on the gravel and pressed both palms flat to the ground like he was checking whether the earth was real. The woman unwrapped the baby and held it up to the light, counting fingers, counting breaths. The older brother helped the younger one stand; the younger one's legs had gone to sleep somewhere over the last forty miles of road and weren't taking calls.

The girl — the fierce one — kept herself between Cyrus and the boy until the boy himself pushed past her skirt and walked straight up to Cyrus, stopping an arm's length away, chin up.

"You're the pilot," he said.

"I'm the pilot."

"Can you really fly over the Escarpment?"

The question landed in Cyrus's chest like a hook finding meat. Three years. Three years since anyone had asked her that without knowing what they were asking.

"Sometimes," she said. "Not today, if today says no."

"My name is Ilo." He said it the way other children recited their full names for teachers — except there was no teacher here, and the recitation sounded more like a man laying down his cards. "Ilo Ferren. My father worked the Halvern survey office. Before."

"Before," Cyrus repeated.

"He'll tell you the rest," the girl said from behind him, arms crossed. "Or I will. Whichever he can stomach."

"Delia," the heavyset man said gently, getting to his feet. "Let the man ask his questions."

"You're the one asking questions?" Delia said to Cyrus. "We're the ones in boxes."

"She's not wrong," Cyrus admitted.

The man came forward and offered his hand. His grip was calloused and careful, the handshake of someone used to handling live things. "Renzo," he said. "This is my wife, Ama, and our daughter. The boys are Pell and Sef, cousins. And you've met Ilo." He paused. "You should know what you're carrying, Pilot. You deserve that much, even if nobody else thought so."

So they told her, right there on the gravel with the mist burning off the trees, in pieces, passing the story between them like a bucket at a fire.

Halvern Mining had come upriver eight years ago with contracts and cash and a private security force that grew teeth by degrees, the way these things do — first guarding the mine, then the roads, then the ledgers, then the truth. Renzo's village had held claim-stakes older than the company. Old claims, legal claims, the kind that sit in a district office in a folder tied with string. Last spring the district office burned. The claims burned with it. And by the time the ash cooled, Halvern's papers said the fire had been set by villagers — by *these* villagers, six names on a warrant, six bounties on a board.

"That's not how it happened," Ilo said. His voice had gone thin. "My father kept copies. Off-book copies. He was the survey clerk, he copied everything, he knew Halvern would—" He stopped. Swallowed. Started again, smaller. "Halvern came for the copies. My father sent me away with them first."

"Where are the copies now?" Cyrus asked.

Ilo looked at her, and behind the fever-brightness there was something older than a child, something that had been carrying weight up hills built for grown shoulders.

"Safe," he said. "That's the part I don't tell people."

* * *

Cyrus walked to the far end of the strip after that, alone, and stood looking north at the horizon, where the Escarpment waited sixty miles out like a wall somebody had forgotten to finish building.

Here was the arithmetic, and she ran it twice because she wanted it to come out different.

Turn back now. Land at any strip south of the ridge, unload six fugitives with warrants on their heads, and walk away clean. Halvern's patrols covered the southern roads — Voss had said so, and for once Cyrus believed her, because Voss lied best when the truth was convenient. The fugitives would be taken within a week. The boy would go wherever companies send inconvenient children, which is nowhere good, and the baby would go with her mother, and the string-tied history of an entire village would stay ash.

Go forward. Cross the Escarpment — the ridge where she'd lost Tomás and Wren and the plane she'd loved before this one, the ridge she had sworn, out loud, with witnesses, never to cross again. Deliver six people and twelve crates of actual medicine to a mission station past the wall, ahead of a monsoon season that was already stacking clouds on the northern horizon like plates nobody would wash.

Triple pay. No questions. Except now she had the answers, and the answers were worse than any question.

"You could still turn back," Voss said, coming up beside her. She'd brought the coffee again. This time Cyrus took it, mostly to have something to do with her hands.

"Don't talk to me."

"Noted." Voss drank her own coffee and looked north too. For a moment they were just two pilots watching weather, which was the oldest and easiest friendship they'd ever had. "For what it's worth, I didn't know about the boy until Port Calloway."

"You knew about the others."

"I knew the cargo breathed. Yes."

"And you let me load them in the dark and check two crates in the rain."

"Yes," Voss said. "Because if you'd known, you'd have said no. And if you'd said no, they'd be dead inside a month, and you'd sleep fine, and I'd—" She stopped. Recalibrated. "I need this run done, Cyrus. More than you know. Do the math however you want, but do it fast. Weather window closes tomorrow night."

Cyrus looked back down the strip. Ilo was helping Renzo re-stack the real medical crates, working with the grim competence of a child who'd learned that useful people get kept. Delia was teaching the baby to grab her finger. Ama had found the strip shack's kettle and was making tea for everyone, because apparently even fugitives on the run cannot skip tea, and Cyrus felt something in her chest crack slightly along an old fault line.

Her rule had been simple. Never cross the Escarpment. Rules like that are load-bearing. You build a whole life on top of them, and you tell yourself the life is the point, when really it's the rule underneath, holding everything up, keeping the sky on the correct side of your head.

She drained the coffee. It was terrible. Voss had made it, obviously.

"Fuel them, water them, feed them," Cyrus said. "We lift at first light. And Mara — if there is one more thing in this airplane that has a heartbeat, you're going to tell me about it *before* wheels-up, or so help me, I will fly you back to Port Calloway myself and bill you for the view."

Voss almost smiled. Almost. "There's nothing else."

"That's what I'm afraid of."

* * *

They lifted at dawn into air so smooth it felt apologetic, the Otter heavy and happy, climbing out over the green country with the river unspooling below like wire off a spool. Ilo rode up front in the copilot's seat — Cyrus had decided a child who'd spent two days in a crate had earned a window — and he sat very straight, hands in his lap, watching the instruments like they might teach him something if he was polite.

"What's that one?" he asked, pointing at the altimeter.

"How high we are."

"How high are we?"

"High enough."

He nodded solemnly, filing it away, and Cyrus realized with a small internal jolt that she liked him, which was dangerous, because everything she'd ever loved in the sky had eventually cost her.

Ahead, far ahead, the Escarpment stood up out of the morning haze — a blue-gray rampart running east to west as far as the eye could follow, wearing its first cloud like a scarf. Behind her, in the cabin, six exiles and a baby were quiet in the way of people who know the next hour decides things.

Cyrus keyed the radio out of habit, got the static she expected, and settled her hands on the yoke.

"Hold on to something," she told the cabin, and pushed the nose north, toward the wall.
