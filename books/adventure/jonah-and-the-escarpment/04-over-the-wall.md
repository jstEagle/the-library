## 4. Over the Wall

The Escarpment did not appear on most maps as anything more than a brown smear and a name nobody agreed on. The survey charts called it the Kessel Ridge. The river people called it the Wall. Cyrus Sandoval called it *none of your business*, because for three years she had flown a wide, respectful, fuel-wasting arc around it, and she had told herself the detour was about headwinds.

This morning there would be no arc. The cargo — six crates that breathed, though only she knew that — needed to be over the ridge before the monsoon cell building in the west closed the window entirely. Weather like this didn't knock. It came through the door with its boots on.

"Straps tight back there?" she called over her shoulder.

"Straps are tight," said Mara Voss from the jump seat, in the tone of a woman who had checked them twice already and resented being asked.

Cyrus ran the pre-takeoff flow by touch more than sight, the way you do when your hands have done it ten thousand times. Mixture rich. Props forward. Flaps set. Her old de Havilland — *the* old de Havilland, the one she'd bought off a missionary with a gambling problem and rebuilt bolt by bolt in a shed that leaked — shivered around her like a dog waking up.

She taxied out onto the strip and paused at the threshold, which was not procedure but habit. Habit was where she kept her dead.

*Danny always took the left side of the cockpit,* she thought, the way she thought it every morning, quieter now, the way a scar is quieter than a wound. *Wren rode in back with the mail sacks and complained about the smell of fish no matter what we carried.*

Three years ago there had been four of them. Danny Okonkwo, copilot, who hummed show tunes on approach and swore he'd retire to a beach. Wren Castellanos, loadmaster, who could fit a piano into a Cessna if you dared her. And Cyrus, who measured life in miles the way other people measured it in birthdays, and who had said yes to one run across the Escarpment because the money was good and the weather looked fine and fine, she had learned since, is what the sky says right before it lies.

The fourth seat was empty now. It had been empty ever since.

She pushed the throttle up.

The de Havilland gathered itself the way old dogs do — reluctantly, then all at once — and lifted off into air that felt thick as broth. Behind her Voss said nothing, which Cyrus appreciated. There were two kinds of passengers: the kind who talked on takeoff and the kind who understood that the first five minutes belonged to the airplane.

The river unspooled beneath them, brown and patient, and the country began to climb.

* * *

The Escarpment announced itself an hour out, first as a smudge, then as a wall — a green rampart rearing up out of the haze, its top edge sawed into ridgelines that caught the cloud and shredded it. Waterfalls hung off its face like silver stitching. On a clear day it was beautiful. Today it wore weather like a hood.

"Cell's moved east," Voss said, leaning toward the windscreen. "That wasn't there yesterday."

"Nothing stays where you leave it out here." Cyrus keyed the radio, got the static-and-mumble of the relay station, and filed a flight plan that was mostly fiction. Then she climbed.

The airplane clawed for altitude in air that kept shrugging it downward. Eight thousand feet. Nine. The ridge tops ahead stood at ten-four, and the cloud base sat somewhere around eleven, which meant the gap between rock and weather was going to be narrow enough to thread with a needle, except the needle was two tons of aluminum and the thread was her nerve.

"You can go around," Voss said. Not taunting. Worse — reasonable. "South passage adds six hours."

"Six hours puts us on the ground in the middle of that cell." Cyrus nodded west, where the sky had gone the color of a bruise and was busy getting worse. "We go over while it's still just ugly instead of murderous."

"You've done this crossing before."

It wasn't a question. It was a knife laid flat on the table between them, handle toward Cyrus, and they both knew whose hand had put it there.

"I've done this crossing," Cyrus said.

* * *

Three years ago, the weather had also been fine right up until it wasn't.

They'd been heavy — mail, diesel parts, a crate of chickens that Wren insisted on calling *the passengers* — and the ridge had looked exactly like this, green and stitched with waterfalls, and Danny had been humming something from *South Pacific* as they started the climb. Cyrus remembered that with terrible clarity: the humming. *Some Enchanted Evening*, off-key, half-swallowed by engine noise.

Then the downdraft hit.

People who don't fly imagine turbulence as shaking. What it actually is, sometimes, is the sky picking up your airplane like a hand picks up a toy and setting you down where it wants you. The de Havilland lost four hundred feet in seconds. Cyrus hauled back on the yoke and the mountain came up anyway, green filling the whole windscreen, and Danny stopped humming and said — calm as a man reading a grocery list — *"Updraft on the far face. Get over the spine, Cy. Get over the spine."*

She never got over the spine.

What she got instead was a memory she visited more often than church: the wingtip clipping a cedar, the world turning slow and sideways, Wren's voice saying *oh* — just *oh*, one syllable, surprised, like the sky had told a joke — and then impact, and then a silence so complete that for a long moment Cyrus believed she was the only person left alive anywhere.

She had been, almost. She crawled out of the wreck with a broken collarbone and burns on her hands and found Danny twenty feet away, and Wren under the cargo netting, and the chickens — this was the detail that broke her, afterward, the detail she never told anyone — the chickens had survived. A dozen of them, wandering the wreckage site, pecking at the moss, entirely unbothered, while the two funniest people Cyrus had ever known lay still in the ferns.

Rescue took nine days. She buried them both with rocks because the ground was too hard for digging. When the helicopter finally came, she made them take the chickens too. They're probably somebody's dinner by now, she thought sometimes. Even grief gets recycled out here.

Afterward, everyone wanted a story with a villain in it. The inquiry asked about weight, about judgment, about why she'd crossed at all. And there had been a second pilot on that manifest — a rival operator who'd taken the same contract, who'd turned back at the last minute citing weather, who'd stood at the inquiry and said words to the effect that Cyrus Sandoval flew like the rules were suggestions.

Mara Voss. Who then vanished from the territory for three years, and who was currently strapped into the jump seat behind her, watching the Wall come closer.

*You knew,* Cyrus thought, glancing at the mirror. *You knew this crossing would put me here. You counted on it.* Because that was the thing about Voss's offer — triple pay, no questions, the name *Danny* dropped like a coin down a well. It hadn't been charity. It had been bait, and Cyrus had known it was bait, and she'd bitten anyway, because unfinished business is the one cargo no pilot can weigh properly.

* * *

"Ten thousand feet," Voss said. "Cloud deck looks solid above."

"It's always solid above. That's the trick of it. You just have to believe there's sky on the other side of the gray." Cyrus eased the nose down a degree, trading altitude for airspeed, feeling the airplane settle into the work. "Danny used to say the mountains don't kill you. Your schedule does."

"Danny," Voss repeated softly. Testing the name. Watching Cyrus in her own mirror.

"Don't," Cyrus said.

"I wasn't—"

"You were. I know what you were doing. You wanted me on this run and you used the one lever that works, and I let you, and I'm angry about it, and I'll be angry about it in a more organized fashion once we're on the ground. Right now I need you to do one thing." She pointed at the window, at the ridge line sliding toward them, close enough now to see individual trees, individual waterfalls, the white scars of old slides. "Watch for the notch. Wide U-shape, twin peaks either side. If you see anything that looks like weather moving through it faster than we're flying, say so immediately and without editorializing."

Voss was quiet a moment. Then: "There's rain in the notch. Moving left to right."

"How fast?"

"Faster than we're flying."

"Thank you." Cyrus rolled the wings level and pushed the power up until the engines howled. "Then we go now, and we go low, and we don't look back."

The Wall rose to meet them.

Flying at a ridge is nothing like the pictures. In the pictures there's space, drama, room for music. In reality the rock fills your vision and the wind tries to fold your wings and every second is arithmetic — airspeed against sink rate, distance against time, the gap in the notch narrowing or widening depending on things you cannot control. Cyrus flew it the way she'd learned to fly it the hard way: eyes moving, hands light, trusting the machine because the machine had never lied to her. Only schedules lie. Only people.

The notch swallowed them.

For ninety seconds there was nothing in the world but gray stone on both sides, streaming mist, the engines' roar flattened into a single note, and Cyrus's own heartbeat counting time like a metronome with opinions. Somewhere below and behind her, six people breathed inside their crates. Somewhere three years deep, two people didn't breathe at all.

*"Get over the spine, Cy."*

"I'm trying, Danny," she whispered. "I'm trying."

And then — the way dawn comes, all at once after forever — the notch opened, and they were through, and the land fell away beneath them in long green folds running down toward the river country, and the cloud deck sat above them like a lid on a pot beginning to rattle.

Cyrus realized her hands ached. She peeled them off the yoke one finger at a time, flexed them, put them back.

"Nice flying," Voss said.

"Save it."

"No, really. I've watched a lot of pilots cross that thing. Most of them treat it like a door they're bursting through. You treated it like a conversation." A pause. "That's new. You used to fly it like a grudge."

Cyrus didn't answer, because the honest answer was that the grudge had gotten tired, and what was left underneath scared her more.

* * *

The river appeared fifteen minutes later, a brown ribbon winding through the lowlands, and Cyrus allowed herself one breath of something adjacent to relief. Adjacent. Out here you never got the real thing; relief was a town you flew past.

Which was when the western sky finished lying.

The cell had been building all morning, and now it built itself the rest of the way in about four minutes — a black-green mass shouldering up over the hills, lightning flickering inside it like a lamp behind a curtain. The wind swung forty degrees in a minute. Rain arrived not as drops but as a decision, a horizontal sheet of it, hammering the windscreen so hard the wipers gave up out of professional embarrassment.

"That's early," Voss shouted over the din. "Monsoon's not due for a week!"

"The monsoon," Cyrus shouted back, wrestling the nose level, "clearly didn't read the brochure."

She scanned below — river, forest, more forest, and then, thank whatever gods mind gravel bars, a pale sweep of open stones along the inside bend, maybe half a mile of it, glistening. A gravel bar. Not a runway. But a runway was just a gravel bar with self-esteem.

"Hold on!" she called to the back. "Landing! Now!"

She sideslipped down through the rain, trading altitude in gulps, the river swinging up at her out of the murk. The bar was shorter than it had looked — everything is, from the air — and soft-looking near the waterline, and there was a suggestion of driftwood at the upstream end like the river had left its tools out.

Full flaps. Slow as she dared. The de Havilland sank through the rain with the dignity of a brick that had gone to charm school, and Cyrus flared, held, held—

The wheels touched. Good. Firm. Rolling, decelerating, the bar stretching ahead, the driftwood coming up fast—

—and the left main dropped into a hidden channel cut by the last flood, a trench full of loose stone, and the airplane lurched, slewed, and Cyrus stood on the brakes while the propeller, still turning at idle, kissed the gravel with a sound like a giant biting a spoon.

*Shnk. Shnk-shnk. Shhhhnk.*

Then stillness. Rain. Engine ticking. The particular silence of a machine deciding whether to be offended.

Cyrus sat with her hands on the yoke and listened to her own pulse come down from the roof.

"Everyone alive?" she called.

A chorus of muffled confirmation from the crates — and she noted, distantly, that fugitives apparently answered roll call even while in hiding, which said something hopeful about human nature.

She shut the engine down and climbed out into the rain. Walked forward. Looked at the propeller.

One blade curled at the tip like a fern frond. The other had a chunk out of it the size of her fist. She'd seen worse — she'd *flown* worse, once, out of necessity, and never again — but worse or not, this propeller was finished, and there was no spare, and the nearest airstrip with a mechanic was two hundred miles behind a ridge she'd just sworn off forever.

Behind her, the crates were opening. Six figures unfolded into the rain, stiff and blinking, and among them the boy, Ilo, small and fever-bright-eyed, who looked at the ruined propeller and then at Cyrus with the frank appraisal of someone checking whether the adult in charge was about to fall apart.

She wasn't. Falling apart was scheduled for later, possibly never.

"Well," Cyrus said to the propeller, to the rain, to the Wall looming gray behind them all. "Now we're a boat crew."

Voss came around the tail, hair plastered flat, and stared at the blade. "You're joking."

"I never joke about machinery. Machinery has feelings." Cyrus pulled her collar up and squinted downstream, where the river bent away into the rain and the unknown. Two hundred miles of water between here and anywhere, a leaking-boat-shaped problem she hadn't met yet, a monsoon arriving a week early, and six breathing crates standing on a gravel bar looking at her like she was the plan.

Somewhere downriver — she couldn't have said how she knew, only that the river country always sent help or trouble, usually dressed as each other — a shape was moving on the water. Low. Narrow. Coming closer.

"Company," said Ilo, pointing.

Cyrus's hand drifted toward the flare gun in her jacket, old terror making itself new again, one more time, right on schedule.
