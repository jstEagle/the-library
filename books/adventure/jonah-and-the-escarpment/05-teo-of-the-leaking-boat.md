## 5. Teo of the Leaking Boat

The sound that woke Cyrus was not a bird, not an engine, and not the river doing its usual morning business against the gravel bar. It was a voice — a man's voice, singing badly, getting closer.

She came out of the tent with the pistol already in her hand, which surprised her more than the singing did. She hadn't carried a gun since the Escarpment took her crew. Apparently three years of pretending to be a peaceful woman could be undone by one bad night's sleep on wet gravel.

The singer appeared around the bend of the bar poling a long, narrow boat with the kind of unhurried confidence Cyrus associated with men who had never once been in a hurry in their lives. He was lean, sun-browned, somewhere between thirty and fifty depending on how much he'd laughed, and he wore a straw hat so ancient it had achieved a kind of architecture. The boat rode low. Very low. Low enough that Cyrus's eye — trained by twenty years of weight-and-balance calculations — did the math before her brain caught up.

*That boat is overloaded or taking water,* she thought. *Or both.*

He saw the six fugitives huddled under the tarp, saw the wrecked plane with its bent propeller, saw the pistol, and stopped singing mid-note without any sign of alarm.

"Morning," he said.

"Morning," said Cyrus, not lowering the pistol entirely. "You're a long way from anywhere."

"I live a long way from everywhere. It's cheaper." He looked at the plane again, then at the group, then back at her, and something shifted behind his eyes — the look of a man counting heads and arriving at a number he recognized. "Which one of you is Renzo?"

A stir went through the fugitives. A tall man with a bandaged forearm pushed forward, and the fisherman's whole face changed — the lazy calm dropping away like a mask slipping off a nail, revealing underneath it something raw and young.

"Teo?" Renzo said hoarsely.

"You're supposed to be dead," Teo said. "They told everyone at San Fermin you were dead. There was a — there was a thing we did. Mamá lit candles."

"The candles were wasted," Renzo said. "I'm extremely alive."

"They were *expensive*, Renzo."

Cyrus lowered the pistol all the way. Some conversations you weren't part of, no matter how much they concerned your cargo.

* * *

His name was Teo, he said, and the boat was called the *Marisol*, after nobody in particular, because naming a boat after a person was asking for trouble. It was thirty feet of river-cedar and salvaged tin, powered by an engine that had begun life in a rice mill, and it leaked.

"How much does it leak?" Cyrus asked, walking its length while Teo and his brother embraced for the third time and Ilo watched them with fever-bright eyes from under the tarp.

"Professionally," Teo said.

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only honest one." He knocked his heel on the deck planking. "She takes water about as fast as two people can bail her out. Three if they're enthusiastic. Four if they're panicking, but then the bailing gets sloppy and we're back where we started."

Cyrus crouched and put her palm flat on the boards. Cool. Damp. Not yet swimming. She'd flown aircraft in worse condition than this boat, though never on purpose, and never with passengers who couldn't swim away from their own mistakes.

"Where's the nearest place with a dock? A mechanic?"

"A mechanic for boats is four days downstream. For planes—" he gestured at the Cessna sitting on the gravel bar like a wounded bird, "—there is nothing until Port Amaya, and Port Amaya is nine days, and Port Amaya is Halvern country."

The name landed on the group like a dropped anchor. Even the children went still.

"So," Teo said, folding his arms, looking at Cyrus with the frank appraisal of a man pricing a horse. "You're the pilot."

"Cyrus Sandoval."

"I know. Everyone on this river knows the Sandoval route. You're the one who doesn't cross the Escarpment." He said it without malice, the way you'd say *you're the one who doesn't eat shellfish*. "And now here you are on the wrong side of it, with a broken airplane and six people Halvern wants buried, standing next to my brother."

"That about covers it."

"Mm." He chewed on something — a thought, possibly an actual leaf. "What do you want from me?"

"Passage downriver. All of us. Ahead of the monsoon."

"And what do I get?"

Cyrus had been expecting this. She'd spent the small hours of the night running numbers: what cash she had, what the medicine run paid, what triple pay from Voss would have been worth if Voss ever intended to pay it. She named a figure that made her wince internally and kept her face perfectly level.

Teo didn't blink. "That buys fuel and food. It doesn't buy me a new bottom for my boat, and it doesn't buy my mother a candle for every year her son was dead that he wasn't." He held up a hand before she could speak. "It also doesn't buy what you actually want, which is for me to say yes fast, because you've been watching the sky since dawn and you know what's coming."

Cyrus followed his gaze upward. The clouds over the western ridge had organized themselves overnight into something with intent. Stacked, bruised, patient. Monsoon weather didn't arrive so much as *take possession*.

"Two weeks early," she said quietly.

"Eleven days early, if you count the way my knees count." Teo rubbed one knee as if to demonstrate. "So here's my counter-offer, pilot. You fly nothing, you fix nothing, and you sit in my boat and bail water like everyone else, and when we reach San Fermin you pay me double what you said, because double is what it costs to move eight people past a gunboat, and we both know there'll be a gunboat."

"There might not be a gunboat."

"There's always a gunboat," Teo said. "Halvern built a road last year. Roads come with gunboats. That's just arithmetic."

Behind him, Renzo cleared his throat. "Teo. They saved me. All of them. The pilot especially — she flew through the storm cell to get us out."

"I heard the story. I'm sure it's very heroic." But Teo's eyes stayed on Cyrus, and Cyrus met them, and something passed between them that wasn't negotiation at all. It was recognition. She knew this man. She had been this man, on the other side of a hundred bargains: the one holding the only working vehicle, the one whose yes meant everything, the one who had learned — usually the hard way — that generosity given cheap got spent carelessly.

"You want to be paid properly," she said. "Not because you're greedy."

"Because free help is help nobody respects," Teo said. "My father taught me that. He charged fair prices his whole life and died respected and poor, which proves only half of it works."

Cyrus almost smiled. Almost. "Double. Paid at San Fermin. And if we don't reach San Fermin—"

"Then neither of us has to worry about money," Teo said, "which is the most comforting arrangement possible." He spat in his palm and held it out, and Cyrus, who had closed a hundred contracts with handshakes and logbooks, spat in hers and shook it, because on this river apparently that was how it was done, and when in Rome you bail water.

* * *

**Teo**

The woman piloted the boat like she was angry at it.

That was Teo's first real observation of Cyrus Sandoval, made within the first hour of the journey, and it amused him so much he had to go stand at the stern and pretend to check the engine so she wouldn't see him grinning. She sat in the bow with the tiller line in her fist and her eyes doing that thing pilots' eyes did — flicking, measuring, always measuring — and every time the *Marisol* wallowed in a trough she corrected with a yank instead of a lean, fighting the river the way you'd fight a stall.

"You're going to wear yourself out," he told her, ambling forward. "The river wins. It always wins. You just try to lose politely."

"I don't lose."

"Everyone loses. My grandfather lost to this river. My father lost to it slower. I intend to lose slowest of anyone in the family, which is why I charge what I charge." He nodded at the channel ahead, where the current split around a drowned tree. "Take the left side. Wide. The right looks faster but there's a snag under the surface that eats propellers, and we have no propeller to spare."

She studied the water, and he watched her not believe him, and then watch him anyway, and take the left side wide, and feel — he could see it in her shoulders — the faint insult of having obeyed.

"Good," he said cheerfully. "Again in ten minutes."

"You enjoy this."

"Immensely. Do you know how long I've fished this stretch alone? Since Renzo left for the mines. Three years of talking to a boat that answers only in leaks." He settled onto the thwart beside her, close enough to talk low, because the others were sleeping in shifts under the tarp and the boy — Ilo — was burning up again despite the aspirin the pilot rationed out like gold coins. "Your sick one. How long has he had the fever?"

"Since the mine camp. It comes and goes."

"It shouldn't come and go. Coming-and-going fevers are how rivers kill people — slowly, so you get used to them." Teo scratched under his hat. "There's a woman at the San Cristóbal bend. Halfway. She has medicines. Real ones, not the aspirin-and-prayer kind."

"What does she cost?"

"Less than me. She likes Renzo." He paused. "Liked him. Before he was dead. We'll see how the resurrection goes over."

Cyrus almost smiled again. Teo was keeping score; that was twice now. This woman, he decided, had not laughed properly in years, and he filed that away with the other things he was learning about her — that she checked the fuel gauge of a boat that ran on diesel out of pure habit, that she slept sitting up with her back against something solid, that when the boy cried out in his fever she was moving before anyone else, every time, as if some internal alarm bell rang only for him.

"You knew someone on the Escarpment," he said. It wasn't a question. Everyone on the river knew the shape of that story even if they didn't know the details: the Sandoval plane, three years back, gone into the cloud on the ridge with two crew aboard, and the pilot who walked out alone and never crossed again.

For a long moment he thought she wouldn't answer. Then: "Two someones. Copilot and loadmaster." Her hands stayed easy on the tiller line, but her voice went flat and careful, like ice on a puddle you tested before stepping. "We were crossing in weather I should have refused. Weather exactly like that." She tipped her chin at the west, where the monsoon was assembling itself out of heat and ocean. "I've spent three years making sure I never had to choose like that again. Then Mara Voss showed up with a contract and a name, and I chose stupid."

"Voss." Teo turned the name over. "The one who hired you. Where is she?"

"Sleeping. Or pretending to." Something moved across Cyrus's face then, quick and unreadable, there and gone like a fish shadow under the hull. "She's been very helpful since the crash. That's what bothers me."

Teo nodded slowly. In his experience, helpfulness was like the *Marisol*'s bilge pump: you were grateful right up until you asked yourself why it was working so hard.

"Well," he said, standing and stretching until his spine crackled like green firewood. "If she turns out to be trouble, throw her overboard upstream of me. I hate fishing bodies out of my own wake."

"That's a terrible joke."

"It's how we cope on the river. Terrible jokes and good bailing." He pointed at her with mock severity. "Speaking of which — your shift starts in an hour. Bucket's in the bow. And pilot?"

She looked up.

"When the rain comes — and it will come tonight, my knee has spoken — you'll want to fight the boat again. Don't. Bailing isn't winning. Bailing is just refusing to lose as fast as the river wants." He grinned. "It's the family specialty."

* * *

The rain arrived two hours after dark, exactly as promised, and it did not arrive so much as fall upon them all at once, as though the entire sky had been waiting overhead for days merely for the signal.

Cyrus bailed.

She bailed beside Voss, who worked in silence with the mechanical efficiency of a woman paying off a debt she couldn't name. She bailed while Ilo shivered under every blanket they owned and Renzo held him and sang something low in a language Cyrus didn't know. She bailed while lightning walked the ridgelines on both sides of the valley like sentries pacing a wall, and she thought — not for the first time, but with new weight — of a cockpit going dark on the Escarpment, of hands she had trusted letting go of theirs.

"Breathe," Teo shouted over the drumming rain, materializing beside her with a second bucket. "You're bailing angry again."

"I'm bailing *efficiently*."

"You're bailing like the water owes you money." He sloshed his bucket over the side and grinned at her through the deluge, hair plastered flat, utterly unbothered, and Cyrus realized with genuine irritation that she liked him. That she had liked him since the gravel bar, since *professionally*, since a man who named his leaks honestly was a man she could trust with her life and the lives of seven others, and trust was a currency she hadn't traded in for three years.

The boat groaned. Somewhere below the deck, water found a seam it hadn't known it had, and the rhythm of the bailing changed — suddenly, badly — from maintenance to emergency.

Teo's grin vanished. He was moving before Cyrus finished registering the sound, shouting orders in river-Spanish that needed no translation: Renzo, the tarp, aft; Voss, the crates, forward, redistribute the weight; the boy wrapped and held high and dry. He knelt and slapped the deck, listening to it like a doctor with a stethoscope, and then he looked up at Cyrus with an expression stripped of all humor.

"We're taking water faster than we bail," he said. "There's a slough two miles down — old channel, calm water. We make for it and we patch her at dawn, or we swim."

"Or?" Cyrus asked, though she already knew.

"There's no or," Teo said. "There's never an or. That's the river." He hauled on the throttle and the old rice-mill engine coughed, caught, and roared, and the *Marisol* leaned into the black water like a tired animal that understood, somehow, that tonight it was carrying everything any of them had left.

Ahead, through the rain, Cyrus saw the lightning find the western ridge — the Escarpment, far behind them now but never far enough — and she made herself look away, forward, downriver, toward the slough and the dawn and whatever waited in both.

The monsoon had come eleven days early.

Everything else, she suspected, would come early too.
