## 6. What the River Keeps

The river had a smell, Teo's father used to say, and if you couldn't name the smell, you had no business on the water. Teo had spent thirty-one years naming smells. This morning the river smelled of clay and drowned leaves and, faintly, of gasoline — because the pilot woman had insisted on topping off the outboard's tank from the jerrican she'd salvaged from the wrecked plane, and she had spilled some, and she had sworn at it in a language that was not quite Spanish and not quite prayer.

"You always this careful with fuel?" Teo asked, handing her a rag.

"I'm careful with everything," Cyrus Sandoval said. "That's why I'm alive."

"Could've fooled me. I saw the plane."

The plane sat on the gravel bar behind them like a dead bird with a bent beak, one propeller blade curled the way a fern curls, pointing back the way they'd come. Six fugitives were already aboard the boat — Teo's boat, the *Mariposa*, named for his mother, who had never in her life been light or fluttering — and among them, wrapped in a tarp, sat a boy with a fever.

That was the part Teo kept coming back to, the way his tongue kept returning to a chipped tooth. Not the pilot, not the rival woman with her city shoes ruined by river mud, not even the strangers who had climbed out of crates like a bad joke told by a desperate man. The boy. Ilo, they called him. Eleven, maybe twelve. Burning up.

"He needs a doctor," Teo said.

"He needs to not be here," Cyrus said, tying off the fuel cap. "But here's where we are, so he needs shade, water, and the medicine in that box, in that order. You have shade?"

"Under the canopy, if it doesn't leak."

"It leaks."

"Everything leaks," Teo said. "That's the river's whole personality."

Something happened to her face then — not a smile, exactly, more like a crack in a wall through which a little light got in — and she climbed aboard without another word.

* * *

The first hour was the worst hour, because it was the hour in which everyone discovered what the boat was.

The *Mariposa* was fourteen feet of riveted aluminum that Teo's father had bought secondhand from a man who had bought it secondhand from a man who had, according to family legend, won it in a card game and immediately regretted it. She took water the way old men take grievances — steadily, silently, and with a deep sense of entitlement. Teo bailed her with a cut-down bleach jug twice a day in ordinary times.

These were not ordinary times. There were nine people aboard, plus cargo, plus a dog that had appeared from nowhere at the gravel bar and appointed itself to the expedition without consulting anyone.

"We need a bailing rota," Cyrus announced, twenty minutes in, when the water in the bilge had reached the ankles of the woman named Petra, who had responded to this discovery with the calm of a person who had already survived worse and the vocabulary of a person who had not.

"A what?" asked Renzo.

"A schedule. Bailing shifts. Two people, twenty minutes, rotating."

Renzo looked at Teo. Teo looked at Renzo. They had not spoken more than ten words to each other since the reunion on the gravel bar — the reunion that had consisted of Renzo saying "Teo?" and Teo saying "Renzo?" and then both of them looking away because some things are too large to look at directly, like the sun, or your brother alive when you had spent a year assuming the river or the syndicate or simple bad luck had eaten him.

"The boat's mine," Teo said. "I bail it."

"Not alone you don't. Not at this load."

"She's right," Renzo said, and there it was again, that strange new authority in his little brother's — no, his *older* brother's voice, because Renzo had been gone three years and had come back a man with a scar and a way of standing that Teo didn't recognize. "Rota. Fine. I'll take first."

"You'll take nothing, you'll steer," Teo said. "I know the channels."

"You knew the channels three years ago."

"The channels don't change. The river changes *around* the channels. That's the whole trick."

Cyrus had stopped what she was doing — she was always doing something, coiling a line, checking a strap, adjusting the tarp with the fussiness of a woman who had spent her life trusting machines and was now stuck trusting a boat — and was watching them with the expression of someone taking inventory.

"You really know this water," she said. Not a question. A measurement.

"I was born on it."

"And the Escarpment side? The lower gorges?"

"I've run the delta a hundred times. The gorges—" He shrugged. "Nobody runs the gorges."

"Somebody's going to have to."

Nobody answered that, and the dog barked once at nothing, and the rota was born.

* * *

By midday Teo had learned three things about Cyrus Sandoval.

One: she rationed like a quartermaster. The food from the plane's emergency kit — hardtack, tinned meat, a tin of condensed milk that she guarded with the ferocity of a mother bear — was divided into portions so precisely equal that Petra accused her of owning a scale. She did not own a scale. She owned eyes that had clearly spent years measuring the distance between enough and not enough.

Two: she did not sit down. Ever. Teo counted. In six hours she sat for a total of four minutes, and those four minutes were spent examining the outboard with a wrench in her hand like a surgeon deciding where to cut.

Three: she was afraid. Not of the river. Of the sky.

He noticed it around noon, when the clouds over the western escarpment began to build — the tall, bright-headed towers that meant afternoon heat and, if they kept building, something worse. Teo glanced at them the way you glance at a neighbor you don't like but know. Cyrus looked at them the way you look at a dog that has bitten you before.

"You see that cloud?" she said.

"The big one."

"The big one. Watch its top. If it goes flat — anvil-shaped, like a blacksmith's — it's eating cold air and it's going to turn mean. If it stays round and boils straight up, it's just heat. All bark."

"And which is it doing?"

She watched for a long moment, longer than the question deserved, and Teo understood that she wasn't really watching the cloud. She was watching a different sky, three years ago, over a different ridge.

"Boiling," she said finally. "We've got till evening. Maybe."

"Then we make the narrows before evening," Teo said. "There's a channel through the reed beds — the main channel looks wider, but it silts out after the bend. The narrow one's deeper. Everyone always takes the wide one."

"Everyone always takes the wide one," Cyrus repeated, and for the first time all day she sat down, right there on the cargo crate beside him, close enough that he could see the salt lines on her shirt where sweat had dried and dried again. "You know, I've been flying this country for eleven years, and I just realized I've never once looked at it from down here."

"From down here it looks bigger."

"Everything does." She nodded at the reed beds sliding past, ten thousand green blades hissing in the current. "Which fork?"

"Left. Trust me."

"I don't trust you."

"No," Teo agreed. "But you trust the river, and the river trusts me, so it's practically the same thing."

That got the crack-in-the-wall look again. He was beginning to keep score of those.

* * *

The left channel was exactly as narrow as he'd promised, and exactly as deep, and for one glorious hour the *Mariposa* ran clean and fast between walls of reeds with the dog standing at the bow like a carved figurehead and Ilo sleeping under the tarp and the fever, for the moment, held at bay by the antibiotics Cyrus doled out like a woman counting her own heartbeats.

Then the reeds opened, and the afternoon opened, and the trouble arrived on schedule.

It came first as a sound — a low, wrong hum in the west, like the river had learned to growl. Teo felt it in his chest before his ears sorted it out. He cut the throttle and held up a hand, and to his mild astonishment, the whole boat went quiet, nine people and one dog listening to the sky.

"Storm?" Petra asked.

"Maybe." Cyrus was on her feet. "Where's the nearest cover?"

"There's a cutoff ahead — an old oxbow, half a mile. Overhanging bank on the north side. We can tuck under it."

"Under a bank." She said it flatly.

"Trees fall on you in a storm," Teo said. "Banks just drip."

For a second he thought she'd argue. He'd known her one day and already he knew she argued the way other people breathed. But she looked at the west — where the big cloud had, in fact, begun to flatten, its top spreading out like a bruise — and she said, "Go."

They made the oxbow with twenty minutes to spare, and Teo spent those twenty minutes learning the fourth thing about Cyrus Sandoval, which was that she could work. While the others tied down tarps and huddled cargo, she and Teo ran the anchor lines up the bank, drove stakes, doubled the bow line, and when the first gust came down the oxbow like a fist, the boat held and the tarp held and the boy under the tarp did not wake.

Then the rain came, and it did not come down so much as sideways, and there was nothing to do but bail.

* * *

They bailed together in the dark under the overhang, Teo with his bleach jug, Cyrus with a cooking pot she had claimed from the supplies, passing water out over the gunwale in a rhythm that needed no rota. The storm hammered the world flat. Lightning turned the river white for a heartbeat at a time, and in those heartbeats Teo could see her face: set, stubborn, entirely unafraid of the water and the dark, which was, he was beginning to understand, the only kind of fear she permitted herself.

"You flew through that once," he said. "Didn't you. That's what the cloud did to you."

The pot paused mid-pour.

"Three years ago. Over the Escarpment." She dumped the water and held the pot out for more. "I lost my crew. Both of them. Weather like this, except up there the weather has teeth."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Bail."

So they bailed. And after a while, because the dark and the drumming rain make honest people of everyone, she said, "The woman. Voss. She was my copilot. Before."

"The one with the city shoes."

"The one with the city shoes." Something that was almost a laugh. "She was the best stick-and-rudder pilot I ever flew with. That's the joke of it. That's the whole joke."

"What's the joke?"

"That the ones who fly best are the ones who run fastest." She handed the pot back. "Your brother. Renzo. He was in the crates too?"

"All of them were. Halvern's syndicate burned the claim records in their village and blamed them for the fire. Renzo got out. Most didn't." Teo bailed. "He's my brother. I'd have taken them aboard with no cargo at all. But I'm glad there was cargo, because cargo paid for the fuel."

"That's the most honest sentence anyone's said to me in a year."

"I have more. I think you're a bad passenger."

The lightning flashed. In the white instant he saw her looking at him, eyebrows up, genuinely delighted.

"Excuse me?"

"A bad passenger. You board my boat, you reorganize my lines, you invent a rota, you guard the condensed milk like it's your firstborn—"

"It *is* the most valuable thing aboard."

"—and you've thanked me exactly zero times for the boat, the channels, or the oxbow."

The thunder rolled over them, long and grinding, and when it had passed she was quiet for a moment, and then she said, in a voice with no armor on it at all:

"Thank you."

"You're welcome," Teo said. "Now bail. The river's winning."

* * *

The storm blew itself out by midnight, the way river storms do, all fury and no memory. They anchored in the oxbow because running black water at night with a feverish boy and a leaking hull was a stupidity even Cyrus wouldn't commit, and the boat settled into that wet, crowded sleep that boats enforce on their passengers — elbows in ribs, the dog across everyone's feet, the tarp dripping a slow metronome.

Teo took the middle watch. It was his boat; he'd have taken all the watches if they'd let him, but Renzo had insisted on splitting the night three ways, and arguing with his brother felt like a door he wasn't ready to open yet. So he sat at the stern with a line in his hand he wasn't fishing with, and the river breathed around him, and after a while he heard the tarp shift and Cyrus crawled aft and sat down without asking, which by now felt almost polite.

"Can't sleep?" he said.

"I sleep in planes. Boats make me nervous."

"Everything makes you nervous."

"Everything deserves it." She looked out at the black water. "The boy. Ilo. His fever's worse. I felt it when I checked an hour ago. The antibiotics are helping but they're not winning."

Teo had known that too. You didn't spend a life on the river without learning to read a fever the way you read the water — the shine of it, the restlessness, the way the body fought its own current.

"There's a mission station," he said. "San Rafael. Two days down, if the channels are kind. They have a doctor. Sister Anselma. She's mean as a piranha and twice as fast."

"Two days."

"Maybe three, if we run slow water for the boy's sake."

Cyrus was quiet a long time. Out in the dark, something big moved — a tapir, probably, or the river's own imagination — and the dog lifted its head, decided it wasn't worth barking about, and lay back down.

"Ilo's not just a boy with a fever," she said finally. "You know that. Voss let it slip, and I've been watching him. He's carrying something. The syndicate wants these people badly enough to send a gunboat, and a gunboat doesn't come for farmers."

"I figured."

"You figured."

"I'm a fisherman. I figure things. It's the whole job." He reset the useless line. "Whatever he's carrying — it's why you're still here. You could've taken Voss's extraction offer. Don't look at me like that; I was awake when the radio crackled, and I've seen that face before, on men deciding whether to run."

"I haven't decided anything."

"You decided the minute you started guarding the milk for nine people instead of one."

The tarp dripped. Somewhere downriver, faint as a thought, an engine — too steady, too far off to name, gone before he could be sure.

Cyrus heard it too. He saw it land on her like a hand on the shoulder.

"Teo," she said quietly. "How fast can this boat go?"
