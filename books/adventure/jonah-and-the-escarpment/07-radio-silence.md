## 7. Radio Silence

The beacon woke her at 2:14 in the morning, and it did it the polite way — a soft triple chirp, like a bird with something to say and no one to say it to. Cyrus was awake before the third chirp, hand already on the receiver, because a pilot learns to wake the way a mother learns to wake: completely, instantly, and already annoyed about it.

She'd rigged the set in the crook of a deadfall on the gravel bar, under a lean-to of the plane's torn wing fabric stretched over saplings. The rain had stopped an hour ago, which was worse than rain, because rain made noise and noise meant you could think you were safe without knowing you were wrong.

The chirp came again. Then, threaded underneath it, a voice.

Not a voice she knew. A flat, procedural voice, reading numbers. Coordinates. A grid reference that Cyrus recognized with her whole body before her mind caught up — because the grid reference was hers. The gravel bar. The bent propeller. The six fugitives and one fisherman and one traitor sleeping thirty feet behind her.

*Halvern patrol frequency,* the beacon's little display confirmed, in its smug green way. *Signal acquired. Signal acquired. Signal acquired.*

They knew the plane had gone down. Of course they did. Halvern's private army wasn't an army because it was large; it was an army because it was patient. Somewhere out in the dark, upstream or down, a patrol boat was turning toward her the way a dog turns toward a whistle.

Cyrus sat with her back against the deadfall and did the arithmetic she hated most, which was the arithmetic of other people's lives. Fuel: none to speak of. Aircraft: a Cessna with a propeller bent into a question mark. River: rising, monsoon coming maybe two days early, maybe tomorrow. And six people who had run from a mining syndicate's private army because the syndicate had burned their village's claim records and called it an accident, and who were now sleeping under a wing's worth of fabric with their feet pointed at a fire that was mostly smoke and wishful thinking.

She counted them in the firelight without meaning to. She'd been counting them all day, the way you count fingers after you've slammed one in a door.

Teo, curled around the boat's tiller like the tiller was a woman who'd once been kind to him. Renzo, flat on his back, snoring with the unembarrassed authority of a man who'd spent his life sleeping where work dropped him. The two older women from the village, Sef and Amara, who had organized the bailing shifts on the first day without being asked and had been running the boat's interior like a small, efficient nation ever since. Old Pem, who was mostly beard and opinions. And Ilo.

Ilo was not sleeping. Ilo was sitting at the edge of the fire with his knees drawn up, and he was looking at her, and he was twelve years old, and he was carrying a secret in his head that Cyrus didn't yet know the shape of — only that he guarded it like a coal, cupping his hands around it even in sleep.

"Can't sleep either?" she said, keeping her voice under the wind.

"Your radio is talking," Ilo said.

"It's not talking. It's listening."

"Same thing," Ilo said, "if you do it long enough." And he got up, and he came over, and he sat down next to her with the particular gravity children have when they've decided an adult is about to need company more than answers, and Cyrus felt something in her chest go tight and inconvenient, and she turned back to the receiver.

The patrol voice had stopped. The frequency sat open, hissing.

Then it didn't.

*"Cyrus."*

Cyrus's hand stopped moving.

She knew that voice. She'd have known it through a wall, through a monsoon, through six feet of river mud and her own grave. It was lower than it used to be, and it had a rasp in it that hadn't been there three years ago, but a voice is like a fingerprint that way — it survives a lot of damage.

Mara Voss. Calling on a frequency only Cyrus's beacon would catch, on a night only Halvern's patrols were flying. Which told Cyrus two things at once, and both of them were knives.

*"Cyrus, I know you're on this channel. Your beacon's been squawking since the storm cell. Pick up."*

Cyrus looked at the transmit key. She thought about not picking up. She thought about it for exactly as long as it took Ilo to glance at her face and then, with the tact of a child who has learned when adults need to lie alone, stand up and walk back toward the fire and pretend to sleep.

She keyed the transmit. "You're using Halvern's patrol band, Mara. That's an interesting choice of furniture for your new house."

A pause. Then a breath of a laugh, dry as old rope. *"Still counting syllables before you spend them."*

"One of us has to. You never met a word you didn't buy on credit." Cyrus kept her voice level, kept it low. "Talk fast. This frequency has ears, and I don't mean mine."

*"That's the point. That's why I'm on it — nobody listens to their own patrol band. It's the safest channel on the river tonight, if you can believe that."* A pause, and when Voss came back the performance had thinned, and Cyrus could hear the actual woman underneath it, the one who used to fly the coast runs in a jacket two sizes too big and laugh at her own landings before anyone else could. *"There's a boat. Halvern's own tender, out of the Kessin station. I can put you on it. Tomorrow, dusk, the mouth of the Sarel slough. Just you."*

"Just me," Cyrus said.

*"Just you."*

"And the other six?"

The pause this time was longer, and in it Cyrus heard everything: the ledger of debts Voss carried, the arithmetic she'd already done, the answer she'd prepared and the answer that was true, and the distance between them.

*"Cyrus."* Voss's voice did the thing it did when she was about to make a bad deal sound like a kindness. *"You pulled them out of the crates because you couldn't not. Nobody's arguing. But you know what they are. They're cargo that got heavy. Halvern doesn't want them dead — that's expensive and it makes paperwork. They want them back, quiet, before the monsoon closes the river for a month. If you walk away now, they get walked back, quiet, and everyone lives. If you stay —"* the rasp in her voice caught — *"if you stay, you're six more names on a list, and you know how I feel about lists. I've been on one for three years."*

Cyrus looked at the fire. Ilo had not gone to sleep. He was lying with his back to her, very still, in the way of a boy who had grown up in a village where adults lowered their voices for reasons, and who had therefore learned to hear through walls, through rain, through the specific silence of a woman deciding something.

*"Triple what I paid you,"* Voss said. *"And I'll clear your debt with the Kessin field office. And Cyrus — I'll tell you what happened on the Escarpment. All of it. The part you've spent three years building theories about at two in the morning. You want that. Don't tell me you don't."*

And there it was. Not the money — Cyrus could take or leave money, had left it often enough that her bank manager sent her greeting cards out of professional despair. Not even the debt. But the Escarpment. The crash. The two graves she'd never gotten to dig because the mountain had kept her crew, and the question she'd carried since, the one that woke her some nights with its little triple chirp: *why was Voss's crate manifest wrong, and why did my copilot argue with her about it an hour before we flew into that ridge?*

Temptation, Cyrus thought, is not a devil in a red coat. It's a voice on a radio at two in the morning, offering you the one thing you actually want, priced in other people.

"I'll be at the Sarel slough," Cyrus said.

The relief in Voss's exhale was audible even through the static, and it made Cyrus feel briefly, coldly sick — because Voss was relieved, which meant Voss was afraid, which meant Voss was not free either, whatever she'd sold to buy herself.

*"Dusk,"* Voss said. *"Come alone, come unarmed, and Cyrus — thank you. I know what this costs you."*

"You don't," Cyrus said. "But keep the channel open. I'll confirm before dusk."

She set the receiver down. The fire had burned to a red heart. Ilo's breathing had achieved the slow, theatrical rhythm of a child performing sleep, and Cyrus lay back against the deadfall and stared up at the wing-fabric roof, where rainwater gathered in the low spots and let go in single fat drops, one, then two, then a patient irregular drumming, like the river keeping time for something.

She had lied to Mara Voss.

She turned the lie over while the others slept, examining it the way she'd examine a new engine part — for flaws, for fit, for whether it would hold under load. The lie was this: *I'll be at the Sarel slough.* And it was a good lie, a load-bearing lie, because it was half true. She would go to the Sarel slough. She would stand at dusk on the mud and she would watch Halvern's tender come up the channel with its running lights doused, and she would watch Mara Voss standing in it, and she would let the whole machine of her own rescue assemble itself in front of her — and then she would not get in.

Because here was what the arithmetic actually said, and she'd known it before Voss finished the second sentence. Six people. A boy with a fever that had broken yesterday but might not stay broken. A fisherman whose brother was one of the six and who had grounded his own boat on a sandbar to save strangers because his brother had asked him to. Two grandmothers who had survived a fire and a false accusation and a week in a smuggling crate and who still, every morning, organized everyone else's breakfast before their own.

You could not put that on a list and cross it off. You could try. Halvern was trying. But a list was just paper, and paper burned — she'd learned that from the other direction, from the ashes of her crew, from the claim records of a village that had trusted a mining company's stamp on a document.

The truth was simpler and worse: she'd already made her choice, somewhere between the crates and the gravel bar, and the radio call was just the invoice arriving for a purchase she'd already made.

* * *

Dawn came up the color of dishwater, and the river had risen four inches in the night — she could tell by the new watermark on the gravel, a clean dark line like a tide mark, except it wasn't a tide, it was the monsoon introducing itself.

Teo found her at the boat, running her thumb along a seam in the hull that was weeping again.

"It's the same leak," he said. "It always comes back. Like a debt."

"Everything's a debt this week." She glanced up at him. "Teo. If I asked you to take this boat upriver to the Sarel slough at dusk, and wait there, and not ask why — what would you say?"

Teo considered this with the unhurried seriousness he gave everything, including bailers and weather and the question of whether Pem's beard contained a nest. "I'd say you're going to meet someone."

"I'm going to meet a boat."

"You're going to meet someone," Teo said again, gently, the way you'd correct a child who insisted the moon was following them, "and you're going to stand on the bank and argue with yourself. And then you're going to come back downriver and not tell anyone what you decided, and we'll all pretend we didn't see your face." He picked up the bail bucket. "So yes. I'll take you. I've got a brother on this boat. I know how it is, standing on a bank, doing arithmetic."

Cyrus looked at him for a moment. "You're an annoying man, Teo."

"My mother said the same." He handed her the bucket. "Your shift."

* * *

The day went long and low. They moved the boat downstream through the drowned willows, Teo reading channels out of the water the way Cyrus read weather out of cloud — a darker green meaning depth, a ripple standing still meaning a snag, a smell of mud meaning the river had been somewhere new since yesterday. Cyrus taught Sef and Amara to read the sky instead, and Amara, who had survived a mining company's fire, took to forecasting with a grim aptitude, announcing each new cloud like a witness naming a defendant.

Ilo's fever stayed down. Renzo's shoulder, wrenched in the crash landing, had stiffened into a complaint he voiced only in weather terms — *rain coming, in my shoulder* — and Cyrus had started using him as a barometer, which he pretended to hate and visibly didn't.

And all day, in the pocket of her jacket, the beacon chirped its little triple chirp at intervals, and Cyrus let it, because a beacon that chirps is a beacon that's working, and a beacon that's working is a lie she was telling with equipment.

At midday she checked the channel again. Patrol traffic, flat and procedural, grid references sliding past like cards being dealt. One of them, she noted, was the Sarel slough. They were already setting the table for her.

At dusk she stood on the bank at the mouth of the Sarel, exactly as promised, alone, unarmed, while Teo held the boat in the current thirty yards out with the engine at a whisper and six faces watching from the shadows under the tarp.

The tender came up the slough at 6:40, black on black, running lights doused, exactly as Voss had said. And standing in its bow, small and straight-backed against the last grey light, was Mara Voss, wearing a Halvern field jacket that fit her now — that was the detail that went through Cyrus like a splinter, that the jacket *fit*, that three years had tailored her into the shape of the thing she'd sold herself to.

Voss raised one hand. Cyrus raised one back.

And then Cyrus turned around, and walked back down the bank, and waded out to the boat, and Teo pulled her over the gunwale without a word, and the current took them all downstream into the dark.

She didn't tell anyone what she'd decided. She didn't have to. Teo was right — they all saw her face.

But she'd left the channel open, and at midnight, as the wind began to rise out of the southwest and the first true rain of the monsoon came hissing across the water like something with intent, the beacon chirped, and Voss's voice came through one last time, and it was not angry. It was worse than angry. It was frightened.

*"They know you didn't come, Cyrus. And the storm's coming down the valley faster than anyone forecast. Whatever you're doing tonight —"* static, a long tearing seam of it — *"— batten everything down. And whatever you do, don't be on open water when it hits."*

Cyrus looked up at the sky, which had gone the color of a bruise, and then down the river, where there was no bank, no bar, no shelter — nothing but open black water and the rising wind, and six people sleeping under a tarp who were about to learn what the monsoon did to a leaking boat.

She picked up the bail bucket and woke Teo.
