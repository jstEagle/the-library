## 8. The Bailing Watch

Ilo had learned to sleep anywhere. That was one of the things the fire taught you — that a body could curl itself small enough to fit in the bottom of a boat, under a tarpaulin, between a crate of rice and a stranger's knees, and call it rest.

He was not resting now. He was listening to the river.

The rain had started an hour after dark, first as a polite tapping on the tarp, then as something with opinions. The river answered it by rising — Ilo could feel the change in the way the boat moved, a new looseness, like the water beneath them had decided to stop being a road and start being a hand.

"Water's coming in," said his sister Naya, from her place at the bail bucket. She said it the way she said everything now: flat, factual, as if feeling anything too loudly might cost them.

"I know," Ilo said.

"You're supposed to be asleep."

"So are you."

They looked at each other in the dark. Somewhere above them, Cyrus Sandoval was shouting something to Teo over the wind — words torn apart before they arrived whole. The engine coughed. The boat swung sideways and the rain came in off the port side like it had been waiting for an invitation.

Then the lightning came, and Ilo saw the water in the bottom of the boat for the first time.

It was not coming in. It was *there* — ankle-deep, cold, holding their feet like the river was already shaking hands with all of them, introducing itself politely before it took them properly.

* * *

The bailing watch organized itself the way disasters do: badly, then suddenly well.

Cyrus came down the length of the boat half-crouched, moving with a sureness Ilo found almost offensive given how much the world was tilting. She'd been flying planes over this country for years, Teo had told him, and it showed — she treated the storm like weather, not like punishment.

"Two buckets," she said. "You bail, you pass, you bail again. Nobody bails alone, nobody stops. Naya, you're with me on the forward line. Ilo—"

"I can bail."

"I know you can bail." Her voice was rough but not unkind. "You bail. But you stay *in* the boat while you do it. That's the part people forget."

"That's the part you think children forget," Ilo said.

Cyrus paused. Lightning lit her face, and he saw — to his enormous surprise — that she was almost smiling. "Fair," she said. "Bail."

So they bailed. The rhythm of it became everything: scoop, lift, throw; scoop, lift, throw. The rain filled the sky and the river filled the boat and the six of them filled the night with work. Teo held the tiller and steered by some private argument he was having with the current, shouting corrections to himself. Voss sat amidships with her arms wrapped around her knees, doing nothing useful, which Ilo noticed, because noticing things was what he did now. Noticing things was the whole reason any of them were alive.

Renzo bailed beside him. His brother — no, not his brother, Teo's brother, though three days downriver had made the word looser than blood — worked with the steady economy of a man who had hauled nets since before he could read. Every fourth or fifth bucket, without appearing to plan it, Renzo checked on Ilo. A glance. A shoulder bump. Once, when a wave slapped over the gunwale and soaked Ilo completely, Renzo said, "Well, now you're committed," and Ilo laughed so hard he nearly missed his next scoop.

That was the thing about Renzo. He made the terrible feel temporary.

Ilo would remember that later. He would remember it for the rest of his life, in fact, which is why he tried very hard, in that moment, to memorize the exact sound of it.

* * *

The wave that nearly ended them came without ceremony.

There was no dramatic buildup, no roar of warning. There was simply a moment when the river rose up beside them — black, taller than the gunwale, carrying a log or a root mass or possibly just its own bad temper — and leaned against the boat like a drunk leaning on a door.

The boat went over on its beam. Water poured in across the low side in a single green sheet. Ilo heard Cyrus shout "weight to starboard!" and felt hands grab him, and then everything was sliding — crates, buckets, Naya's outflung arm, himself — toward the open side where the river waited with its hand still extended.

He went into the water.

It was warmer than he expected and much stronger. It took him the way you take a thing that belongs to you. For two full seconds — he counted them later, obsessively, the way he counted everything — Ilo understood, with perfect clarity, that he was going to die in the same river that had carried away his village's ashes, and that this was, in a way, tidy.

Then a fist closed on his collar.

Renzo had gone in after him. Of course he had. Ilo was hauled up through the black water into the blacker air, gasping, and found himself being shoved upward over the gunwale by hands that knew exactly what they were doing — and then the hands hesitated, and the boat lurched, and there was a sound Ilo felt through the hull rather than heard, a wet crack like a branch giving up.

Renzo came over the side a moment later, hauled by Teo and Cyrus both, and Ilo saw immediately that something was wrong. His left arm hung oddly. His face was white in a way the rain couldn't account for.

"The log," Renzo said, before anyone asked. He managed a grin. "It hit back."

* * *

They got the boat righted — mostly — and the water down — barely — and ran it into the lee of a clay bank where the storm's teeth couldn't reach them quite so easily. Teo wedged the hull against roots. Cyrus rigged the tarp into something resembling shelter. And in the cramped, dripping, heaving dark, they discovered the true extent of the damage.

Renzo's arm was broken. Not badly, Cyrus judged, feeling along it with a pilot's blunt competence — a clean break below the elbow, the kind that knits if it's set and splinted and kept still. The problem was that none of those things were available in generous supply on a leaking boat in monsoon country.

"We set it tonight," she said. "It'll hurt. Then it'll hurt less."

"I've had worse," Renzo said.

"From what?" Cyrus asked. "Fishing?"

Something passed across the boat then — a silence with weight to it, the kind of silence Ilo had come to know the way other children knew lullabies. Naya's hand found his in the dark. Voss, amidships, turned her face away toward the bank, pretending great interest in the rain.

"No," Renzo said quietly. "Not from fishing."

* * *

They set the arm. Ilo held his brother-of-convenience's good hand and told him, in exhaustive detail, about the time he'd fallen out of the mango tree behind the schoolhouse and broken nothing but his dignity and his grandmother's favorite pot, and Renzo laughed until Cyrus told him sternly to stop laughing and then laughed herself, one short bark, quickly suppressed, as if humor were cargo she hadn't declared.

And afterward, in the exhausted quiet that follows pain, Naya began to talk.

She didn't decide to. Ilo watched it happen — watched the story rise in her like floodwater, finding the cracks. She started with the records office, because everyone from Kesh Village started with the records office. It was the heart of the matter the way a hearth is the heart of a house.

"Our families had claims," she said. "Land claims. River claims. Grandfather filed them before Ilo was born — paper, real paper, stamped and sealed, in the district registry. Halvern wanted the valley for the new pit. They offered to buy. We said the land wasn't ours to sell; it was ours to keep. So they stopped offering."

"And then?" Cyrus asked. Her voice had changed. Ilo noticed that too. It had gone flat and careful, the voice of someone standing very still near something dangerous.

"And then one night in the dry season, the registry annex burned." Naya's voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "Every record for forty kilometers. Ours, and eleven other villages'. And the next morning, Halvern's men were in Kesh asking questions about who'd been seen near the annex, and wouldn't you know it, three boys from our village had been seen there. Including Ilo."

"I delivered bread," Ilo said, into the dark. "To the clerk. Tuesdays. Everyone knew I delivered bread on Tuesdays."

"They said the boys set the fire," Naya continued, "to cover a theft. They had witnesses. They always have witnesses. The magistrate —" she spat the word — "took two days to decide. Two days, for a verdict that was written before the ash cooled. Ilo was twelve."

The boat creaked. The rain kept on, gentler now, almost apologetic.

"We ran," Naya said. "Because staying meant the reeducation camp at Corren Hollow, and nobody comes back from Corren Hollow with their name intact. We ran, and Halvern put a price on us — not dead, mind you. Alive. Because a boy who burned the records is worth more alive, paraded around, confessing. Dead boys make poor confessions."

Ilo stared at his knees. He had never said any of this aloud to strangers. It had lived in him for two years like a stone swallowed whole, and now here it was, out in the open air of a leaking boat, and the world had not ended. The others were just — listening. That was all. Listening like it mattered.

"The fire didn't take everything," he said.

Everyone turned. Even Voss, despite herself.

"There was a copy book," Ilo said. "My grandfather was old-fashioned. He distrusted the district registry — said paper burns, so he kept his own duplicate. Claims, survey marks, the payments Halvern made to the magistrate — he wrote down *everything*, dates and amounts, because he said someday somebody would ask, and it's better to have the answer ready than to go looking for it." He swallowed. "When we ran, I took it. I memorized it. All of it. Then I buried the book where only I can find it."

Silence. The particular silence of people recalculating everything.

"You memorized it," Cyrus said slowly. "A ledger."

"Claims. Bribes. Names. The magistrate's name." Ilo lifted his chin, the way his grandfather used to when the Halvern men came through the village smiling their oil-and-chrome smiles. "Halvern doesn't want me because I'm a fugitive. They want me because I'm a *filing cabinet*. And filing cabinets can burn twice."

Naya made a sound that was half sob and half laugh. Renzo, one-armed, reached over and gripped the back of Ilo's neck, gently, the way you hold something you've decided to keep.

Teo spoke for the first time in an hour. "So that's why you're going to the coast. Not just away. *Toward.*"

"Toward," Ilo agreed. "There's a newspaper office in Puerto Verde. The *Coastal Ledger*, which my grandfather would have found funny. They print things Halvern can't buy. My grandfather said so. He said, 'Find the people who print the truth, m'ijo, and hand it to them with both hands.'"

"He sounds like he was a stubborn man," Cyrus said.

"He was," said Ilo. "Like me."

"Runs in boats," Teo murmured, and even in the dark Ilo could hear him grinning.

* * *

Later — much later, when the rain had thinned to a whisper and the bailers had thinned to watches of one — Ilo lay awake under the tarp and listened to the boat breathe.

Renzo slept with his splinted arm propped on a crate, breathing slow and even, the pain finally beaten into submission. Naya slept curled against him. Teo took the middle watch, humming something tuneless and riverish. Voss did not seem to sleep at all; Ilo could feel her wakefulness across the boat like heat from a stone.

And Cyrus sat at the bow with the tarp pulled back, watching the rain come down the river in silver curtains, and Ilo, who noticed things, noticed that she was looking south. Toward the Escarpment. Toward whatever had happened to her before any of them, the wound she carried the way he carried his grandfather's numbers.

Everybody on this boat was running from a fire, Ilo thought. Some fires were just quieter than others.

He closed his eyes. Sleep came for him at last, soft-footed, and the last thing he registered was the sound of Cyrus shifting at the bow, and then — faintly, curiously — the click and whisper of her emergency beacon being switched on, and a low voice murmuring into the static, too quiet to catch.

He was too tired to wonder about it.

Almost too tired.
