## 9. Voss's Ledger

The rain had stopped an hour before dawn, and the river had gone quiet in that particular way rivers do after a storm — not peaceful, but listening.

Cyrus found the notebook because her boots were wet and she was looking for dry ones.

They'd made camp on a shelf of mud above a side slough, under a stand of kapok trees whose roots gripped the bank like old men holding onto a railing. The boat sat half-hauled out of the water, listing to port, taking on water at a rate Teo described as "manageable" with the flat confidence of a man who had clearly redefined the word sometime around childhood. The fugitives slept in a row like spoons in a drawer. Renzo's shoulder was wrapped in a strip of sailcloth that had been white three days ago. Ilo's fever had broken in the night, which Cyrus chose to take as a good omen rather than a temporary reprieve, because a pilot learns early that you fly on the weather you hope for until it kills you.

And Mara Voss — Mara Voss was asleep sitting up against a tree trunk, arms crossed, chin dipped, looking for all the world like a woman napping at a board meeting she considered beneath her.

Cyrus had gone down to the boat to fetch her spare boots from the dry bag. They weren't there. The dry bag was there; the boots were not. Which meant either the river had eaten them through a seam, or someone had moved them, and since the only person on this expedition organized enough to move another person's boots was also the only person Cyrus actively distrusted, she went looking.

She found them tucked under the tarp in the bow, dry as toast, which — fine. Fine. Maybe Voss had moved them off the wet deck. Maybe it was almost considerate. Cyrus crouched to pull them free, and that was when her knee came down on something hard and rectangular, wedged between the fuel can and the hull rib.

A notebook. Oilcloth cover, brass clasp, the kind of thing that cost more than most people's boats. It was not hers. She knew every object on this boat now the way she knew every rivet of her plane, and this one belonged to exactly one person: the woman asleep twenty feet away who owned nothing else worth clasping shut.

Cyrus should have put it back.

She told herself that later, many times, in the firm voice she used on students and engines. She should have put it back, because whatever was in it wasn't hers to read, because reading it would change things, and there were still four days of river ahead and a boy with a recent fever and a man with a bad shoulder and six people whose survival currently depended on a truce she could not afford to break.

She opened it anyway.

She was forty-one years old and she'd been flying since she was seventeen, and in all that time she had learned exactly one thing about herself that never changed: when she saw a door, she went through it. It was why she was alive. It was also why her crew was dead. Both of those were true, and she carried them the way you carry two heavy bags — badly, but evenly.

* * *

The first pages were flight notes. Coordinates, headings, fuel calculations in Voss's small, vicious handwriting — the handwriting of a woman who wrote the way she flew, fast and slightly contemptuous of anyone watching. Cyrus recognized some of the strips. There was the mining road at Kilometer 84. There was the mission at San Rafael. There was a set of coordinates deep past the Escarpment that made her stomach tighten before she even knew why, and then she did know why, because three lines below it, dated three years ago last spring, was a note:

*Escarpment crossing. Weather window narrow. Halvern wants it done quiet. Double fee.*

Halvern wanted *what* done quiet? The page didn't say. But the date sat there on the paper like a splinter, and Cyrus sat very still on the cold aluminum deck of a leaking boat while the river muttered below her, and she thought: *that was the week of the run.* That was the week her copilot Denny Okafor had argued with her about the load manifest, and her navigator Pell had joked about buying a house, and they had crossed the Escarpment into weather that swallowed them whole, and Cyrus alone had walked out of the wreckage with burns on her hands and a story nobody believed about what they'd been carrying.

She had spent three years telling herself it was weather. Weather was clean. Weather didn't have a motive.

She turned the page.

*Payment schedule attached. Balance due on delivery. H. wants confirmation route is clear of patrols — I've given him the Sandoval window. She flies Tuesdays. Predictable as sunrise.*

*Predictable as sunrise.*

Cyrus read the line four times. The words didn't get worse with repetition, which surprised her; she'd assumed they would. Instead they got *smaller*, the way a wound does before it starts to hurt properly. He'd given him the window. Her window. Her Tuesday-morning crossing, the one she'd flown eleven times without incident, the route so reliable that Denny used to joke they could fly it blindfolded if Cyrus would just stop narrating the scenery.

Voss hadn't just known about the run. Voss had *sold* it. Sold the timing, sold the route, sold the habits of a rival she'd been drinking with in the Port Authority bar not ten days earlier, where Cyrus had bought the third round because Voss was "between contracts."

There were numbers further in. Payments received, payments owed — and here the handwriting changed, got cramped, pressed harder into the paper, the way writing does when the hand is shaking or the news is bad. Debts. Cyrus didn't know all the names attached to them, but she recognized the shape of the arithmetic: a woman drowning, selling anything that floated. A strip of land gone. A hangar lease defaulted. A debt to a name Cyrus did know — Halvern's own credit office, the polite vultures who bought up pilots' obligations the way other men bought stamps.

*Balance owed to H.C.: 62,400. Agreed settlement: full forgiveness upon delivery of Sandoval route + confirmation of interior corridor clear.*

Sixty-two thousand dollars. That was the price. That was what Denny Okafor's laugh had gone for, and Pell's terrible harmonica, and the eleven years Cyrus had spent since then refusing to cross a ridge of rock she could see from her own kitchen window.

Not malice. Not even greed, really. Just a drowning woman selling the first thing that floated, and the first thing that floated happened to be somebody else's life.

Cyrus closed the notebook.

Her hands were steady. That was the strange part. She'd expected them to shake. Instead they felt the way they felt on final approach in a crosswind — calm, precise, every part of her narrowed down to the single task of getting through the next thirty seconds without crashing. Fury banked like fuel. She could feel it sitting there in her chest, dense and patient, waiting to be burned.

* * *

"You're up early."

Voss stood at the edge of the camp with two tin cups, steam rising off both. She looked rested in the way that only genuinely ruthless people manage on a mud bank in monsoon country — hair tied back, jacket buttoned, eyes already doing inventory of the boat, the supplies, the sky.

"I couldn't sleep," Cyrus said. "Your elbow was in my boot."

"My elbow was twenty feet from your boot."

"Elbows travel."

Voss handed her a cup. Cyrus took it. This was the choreography they'd been performing for days now — the small courtesies, the dry exchanges, two women who hated each other politely across a shared deck. Except until five minutes ago, Cyrus hadn't known it was hate. She'd thought it was history. History you can live with. Hate you have to manage.

She watched Voss over the rim of her cup.

It was remarkable, actually, how much you could see once you knew what you were looking at. Voss checking the treeline — not for patrols, Cyrus realized, but for witnesses. Voss positioning herself during the day always within reach of the fuel can. Voss's habit, new since the church ruin, of asking questions that sounded casual and weren't: *How much fuel left? How far to the delta? Do you think the gunboat comes this far downstream?* Each one innocent alone. Together they were a shopping list.

"How's the boy?" Voss asked.

"Fever's down."

"Good." And then, because Voss could never leave sincerity standing unattended: "He snores like a two-stroke engine. If Halvern doesn't find us, his own lungs will."

"He gets that from his brother," Cyrus said. "Renzo sounds like a diesel with a rod knocking."

Something flickered across Voss's face at Renzo's name — quick, unreadable, gone. Cyrus filed it. She was filing everything now. Every glance, every question, every time Voss drifted toward the bow where the notebook lived wedged behind the fuel can, because Cyrus had put it back exactly where she'd found it, shifted two inches to the left, and was now conducting the quietest experiment in behavioral science the interior had ever seen.

The notebook stayed put all morning. Voss didn't go near it.

Which meant she didn't know it was missing. Which meant she didn't know Cyrus had read it. Which meant, for the moment, the advantage was hers, and Cyrus Sandoval — who had once told a customs officer a bald-faced lie about her cargo weight with such conviction that he'd apologized to *her* — intended to keep it.

* * *

Midday, they ran the slough channel Teo had scouted at first light. It was beautiful work, honestly — the fisherman poling them through water barely a hand deep, threading between drowned stumps by some instinct that operated entirely below the level of explanation. Cyrus sat amidships with the bail bucket within reach and watched him work and thought, not for the first time, that the river had produced this man the way mountains produce springs: inevitably, and from somewhere deep.

Ilo sat beside her, recovered enough to be bored, which Cyrus counted as the true sign of health in any child.

"Teo says there's a shortcut," Ilo said. "Through the flooded forest. Saves a day."

"Does Teo also say how deep the water is in this shortcut?"

"He says," Ilo said, with the scrupulousness of a child delivering testimony, "that it is probably fine."

"Probably fine," Cyrus repeated. "You know, I once flew through a pass that was probably fine. I'm the only one who came back, and I don't recommend the experience."

Ilo considered this. "So we shouldn't take the shortcut?"

"We should take the shortcut," Cyrus said. "Because Teo knows this river better than I know my own plane, and because staying in the main channel means staying where the gunboat can see us. I'm just informing the universe that I noticed it said *probably*."

Ilo grinned. Then, quieter, watching the water slide past: "Are you angry at Mara?"

Cyrus kept her eyes on the channel. "What makes you ask that?"

"You're being extra nice to her. You do that. When you're mad at somebody you get *polite*." He said it with the devastating simplicity of a nine-year-old who had spent his short life reading adults for survival. "Renzo does it too. When he's really mad at me he asks if I've eaten."

"Well," Cyrus said, "somebody has to ask if people have eaten."

"Mara hasn't eaten today."

Cyrus looked down the boat. Voss sat in the stern with her arms crossed, staring upstream the way she'd been staring all morning — north, back toward the Escarpment, toward whatever debts and ghosts she'd left on the other side of that wall of stone. She looked, Cyrus thought, less like a traitor than like a woman doing long division and not liking the remainder.

"Here," Cyrus said, and passed her own ration biscuit down the boat without ceremony. "Tell her the pilot's orders. Nobody flies hungry."

Ilo delivered it. Voss looked at the biscuit, then at Cyrus, and something passed between them that neither of them named — gratitude, suspicion, apology, warning, possibly all four at once, the way things traveled on this river: mixed cargo, no manifest.

"Thank you," Voss said.

"Don't mention it," said Cyrus, meaning it in every possible sense.

* * *

Evening came down gray and heavy. The air had that thick, sweet pressure to it that Cyrus had learned to read the way Teo read channels — the monsoon was close now, closer than the almanacs promised, gathering itself somewhere over the highlands like a debt coming due. She'd caught herself checking the sky every few minutes and making mental notes: the cloud base dropping, the wind swinging east, the smell of green rot in everything.

Teo crouched beside her at the fire, mending a net with fingers that moved faster than seemed legal.

"That sky," he said quietly.

"I know."

"How long?"

"If I were flying?" Cyrus studied the horizon. "I'd say I have maybe two days before I start looking seriously for somewhere to put this boat down and stay put. If I were smart, I'd start looking tomorrow."

"And are you smart?"

"On alternate days. Tomorrow happens to be one."

Teo nodded, unsurprised, and went back to his net. That was the thing about him, Cyrus thought. He absorbed forecasts the way the ground absorbed rain — completely, without argument, and straight down to the roots where decisions grew.

Across the fire, Voss had taken the first watch. She sat with her back to a root buttress, the rifle across her knees that she'd produced on the second day from nowhere and refused to explain, her face lit orange and blue by the flames. Watching the dark. Or watching the boat. Hard to say.

Cyrus watched her back, and let the fuel sit in her chest, banked and burning low and clean.

Here was the arithmetic she was running, the long division of her own: Voss had sold her crew. Voss was likely selling them again — the route, the timing, the predictable-as-sunrise pattern of their little flotilla, fed to Halvern in exchange for whatever remained of her freedom. Every hour Cyrus waited was an hour Voss might use. Every kindness was a door left open.

But.

But there were eight people on this boat who needed every pair of hands, and Voss's hands were competent, and the monsoon was coming, and a gunboat was somewhere behind them, and a boy had asked her — with a child's terrible X-ray vision — whether she was angry, and she had answered with a biscuit.

So she would wait. She would watch. She would keep the notebook where it was, two inches left of home, a loaded secret sitting in the bilge of this expedition, and she would choose her moment the way she chose her moments in weather: not early, not late, but exactly when the ceiling lifted enough to survive what came next.

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the black water, a fish jumped, or a patrol oar dipped — impossible to tell, and everyone at the fire pretended not to wonder.

Voss turned her head, just slightly, and caught Cyrus looking at her.

For a moment neither of them moved. Two women regarding each other across a fire, each certain — absolutely certain — that she was the one holding the cards.

"Quiet night," Voss said.

"So far," said Cyrus.

Behind them, far upstream, thunder rolled — long, low, and continuous, not a storm passing but a storm arriving, settling in for the duration. Teo looked up from his net. Ilo stopped mid-chew. Even the river seemed to lean forward to listen.

Cyrus checked the sky one more time, did the math, and didn't like the remainder at all.
