## 10. Monsoon Country

Cyrus felt the monsoon before she heard it — a pressure change in her bad knee that had never once lied to her in twenty years of flying. She was standing under the ruined bell tower of the church, watching the river climb its banks like something impatient, when the first fat drops came down through the broken roof and struck the stone floor with the sound of small applause.

"Early," Teo said beside her. He said it the way other men said *cancer*.

"By how much?"

"Two weeks. Maybe three." He squinted at the western sky, where the clouds had gone the color of wet slate. "My grandmother used to say the rain comes when it wants, not when the calendar says. The calendar is a suggestion. The rain is a decision."

"Well," Cyrus said, "I've never been fond of being told what to do by weather, but I'm willing to negotiate."

The church had been old when the river was young — walls of coral stone gone soft as bread, a roof that had surrendered half its tiles to some storm decades back, leaving the nave open to the sky like a mouth missing teeth. A wooden cross still hung over what had been the altar, tilted fifteen degrees, as if it too were leaning out to check on the water level. Someone had worshipped here once. Now it would shelter six fugitives, two pilots, a fisherman, and a boy with a fever.

They carried everything up the slope from the boat in three trips, Ilo insisting on making all three despite Renzo's bandaged arm and everyone's better judgment. By the time the last sack of rice was stowed behind the altar, the rain had stopped being drops and become a fact — a gray roaring curtain that erased the far bank of the river entirely.

Cyrus counted heads out of habit. Six villagers: Ilo, feverish and glassy-eyed but stubbornly upright; Renzo, arm bound, face gray; old Marta who moved like her joints were full of gravel; the brothers Anselm and Piet, twins who finished each other's sentences and, apparently, each other's work rations; and quiet Doro, who hadn't spoken since the fire and communicated mostly in nods and an uncanny ability to appear wherever heavy lifting needed doing. Then Voss, sitting apart on a fallen pillar with her arms crossed like a woman waiting for a train she intended to complain about. Then Teo, already coiling rope with the serenity of a man who had made peace with worse rooms than this.

And herself. Eleven souls, one leaking boat with a dead engine, forty pounds of rice, a tin of salt, and whatever the river saw fit to provide.

"Mara," Cyrus said, crouching by their supplies, "talk to me about the food math."

Voss didn't move. "The food math says we starve slowly if we all stay together."

* * *

Teo POV

Teo had known many kinds of silence in his life on the water, and he had learned to tell them apart the way a doctor reads a pulse. There was the silence of contentment, which hummed. There was the silence of fear, which rang. And there was the silence that fell over the church ruin that evening after Mara Voss said *we starve slowly if we all stay together* — that one was the silence of a family deciding whether it was still a family.

He kept bailing. It was what he did. Even ashore, even in a roofless church, his hands wanted a bucket, so he found a dented cooking pot and set it near the door where the rain ran in, tipping it out every few minutes. It gave him something to do besides listen to the argument, though the argument was not really an argument. Arguments had heat. This had none. That was what frightened him.

"The twins can't portage fast," Voss was saying, in the reasonable voice of a woman doing arithmetic rather than cruelty. "Marta can't walk more than four hours a day. Renzo's arm will go septic if we push him. Every day we wait for them is a day Halvern closes the net. I'm not saying abandon anyone. I'm saying split into two groups. Fast group goes ahead, sends help back—"

"With what?" Cyrus asked. Her voice was mild. Teo had learned in five days on the river that Cyrus Sandoval's mild voice was the dangerous one. "Send help back with what, Mara? We have no radio, no money worth the name, and no friends between here and the coast except people too scared to open their doors."

"Then we move faster. All of us. Push through the night if we have to."

"On a boat with a hole in it and no engine." Cyrus shook her head slowly. "You want to spend people like they're fuel."

"I want to save the ones who can be saved!" Voss stood, and for a moment the lamplight caught her face and Teo saw what she must have looked like before — before the debts, before the selling, before whatever it was that had hollowed her out. She had been magnificent once. You could see the architecture of it still. "That's not a crime, Cyrus. That's triage."

"It's arithmetic wearing a hero's coat," Cyrus said. "Sit down, Mara."

And Voss sat. But something went out of the room when she did, and it did not come back.

Later, when the others slept, Teo found Cyrus at the doorway, watching the rain come down in ropes. He handed her the pot. She tipped it without being asked, a small ritual they'd developed without ever discussing it, and he thought: *this is what trust looks like when it's working. Two people sharing a chore.*

"My brother," Teo said, "once told me a boat is just a promise you make to the water. You promise to keep bailing, and the water promises to let you float. My grandmother said a family is the same thing."

Cyrus glanced at him. "Your family talks a lot."

"We are fishermen. We talk so the fish don't hear us thinking." He smiled, and after a moment she did too, and the silence between them changed key — from the ringing kind to the humming kind.

"She's not wrong about the math," Cyrus said quietly. "That's the hell of it. Rice for eleven, maybe nine days. Fever medicine gone. And every day the river rises, Halvern's boats get bolder." She turned the empty pot in her hands. "But I flew supply runs for eleven years, Teo. You know what I learned? The cargo always thinks it's the problem. Too heavy, too slow, too fragile. And the pilot who starts believing that ends up landing light and lying about it."

"And you?"

"I've landed light before." Her jaw tightened. "Never again."

* * *

Cyrus POV

By the third day the church had become a village, because that was what human beings did — they built villages out of whatever they had, even inside ruins, even inside storms. Marta claimed the dry corner behind the altar and organized it with the authority of a general. The twins rigged a rain-catchment off the surviving roof section using the boat's torn tarpaulin, arguing the entire time about the angle of it, and produced, to everyone's astonishment, nearly a gallon of clean water an hour. Doro appeared one morning with six fish, having walked out into the flooded shallows with a sharpened stick and come back with dinner and no explanation whatsoever.

Cyrus started calling them her crew. Not out loud. But in the ledger she kept in her head, where she had measured her whole life in miles, she noticed she'd begun writing down different units: *Marta's cough, better. Twins' tarp, holding. Ilo's fever, down half a degree.* It alarmed her slightly, how natural it felt.

Voss stayed apart. She took the watch nobody wanted, midnight to four, alone in the bell tower with the rain hammering the stones around her. She ate last and least. And every day, once, she would look at the river and then at the slow ones — Marta, Renzo, Ilo — with an expression Cyrus recognized because she'd seen it in mirrors during the worst year of her own life. It was the look of a drowning woman counting the people holding onto her.

On the fourth day the rice sack came up light, and Cyrus weighed it in her hand and knew someone had been skimming. Not much. A handful a day, maybe. Enough.

She didn't announce it. She waited until the evening meal, watched, and saw it: Voss, ladling her own portion thin, and while the others' backs were turned, slipping a fistful of raw rice into a cloth bundle tucked under her jacket.

Cyrus said nothing. She banked the fury down low, the way you banked a fire you meant to use later, and she remembered the notebook in Voss's pack — coordinates, payments, the whole ugly ledger of a betrayal — and thought: *there are two kinds of thieves in this world, Mara, and I know which kind you are now.*

Teo POV

On the fifth night the rain stopped — not ended, but paused, the way a boxer pauses — and the moon came out over the flooded fields, and the whole world turned silver and dripping. Teo couldn't sleep. He climbed to the bell tower, meaning to sit alone, and found Voss there already, wrapped in her jacket, the stolen rice bundle beside her like a confession she hadn't made yet.

She didn't startle. "Fisherman."

"Pilot-who-doesn't-fly."

"That's cruel."

"That's accurate." He sat down anyway, an arm's length away. In the tower, with the wind moving through the broken louvers, you could hear the river breathing.

For a long time neither spoke. Then Voss said, "Do you know what I was doing three years ago tonight?"

"No."

"Neither do I. That's the truth of it. There's a week I can't account for. After the crash, after I walked out. I remember walking. I don't remember stopping." She laughed, a small dry sound with nothing in it. "Cyrus thinks I fled because I was guilty. Maybe I did. Or maybe I just couldn't stand still long enough to find out which."

Teo considered this. His grandmother had a saying about guilt: *it is a fishhook in your own thumb; only you can feel the barb.* "You're taking the rice," he said.

Voss went very still.

"I bail the boat. I count things. It's a habit." He shrugged. "A handful a day. For the road, yes? For when you go."

"If I say no, will you believe me?"

"No," Teo said pleasantly. "But I'll respect the effort."

To his surprise, she laughed — a real laugh, startled out of her, brief and rusty. Then she looked at him with something raw underneath. "They're going to die, Teo. The slow ones. Maybe all of them. And Cyrus keeps bailing, keeps feeding them, keeps believing, and I keep doing the math, and the math doesn't care how brave she is." Her voice cracked on the last word. "I sold those people's route to save my own skin. I know that. I'm not asking forgiveness. I'm asking — is it so wrong, wanting fewer graves?"

Teo looked out at the silvered water. Somewhere out there was a gunboat, and somewhere beyond that a mining company, and somewhere beyond that a coast full of police who took Halvern's money. And here in this tower was a woman doing terrible arithmetic because it was the only prayer she knew.

"My grandmother also said," he offered, "that a man who throws passengers overboard to lighten the boat arrives at shore lighter. But he arrives alone. And the shore is very large."

Voss stared at him. "Is everything in your family a proverb?"

"Everything important."

Cyrus POV

On the sixth morning, the river rose eight inches overnight and came lapping into the churchyard like a guest who wouldn't take a hint. Cyrus stood at the doorway with her coffee — burnt, precious, the last of it — and made her decision the way she made all of them: quietly, completely, and with no intention of holding a vote.

They would move at first light tomorrow. All eleven. Downriver to the narrows where Teo said the current would carry them even with a dead engine, then the long drag through the flooded forest to the old logging channel. Slow ones in the middle, strong ones at the oars, Voss at the bow reading water because — whatever else she was — she read water like scripture.

She found Voss repacking the rice bundle at dawn, and instead of rage, what surprised her most was the calm in her own chest.

"I know about the rice," Cyrus said.

Voss's hands stopped. "Cyrus—"

"You're not taking it. And you're not going anywhere alone. Those are the terms." She held up a finger before Voss could speak. "Not because I forgive you. I don't yet. Maybe I never do. But you're right about one thing — the math is against us, and I need every pair of hands, including yours, including the ones that stole from me." She stepped closer. "Here's what you need to understand, Mara. Three years ago you left me a wreck and a lie, and I built my whole life around hating you for it, and it turns out hate is just love with nowhere to land. So you're going to stay on this boat, and you're going to help me get these people to the coast, and when we get there, you and I are going to settle what happened on that ridge. Do you hear me? Nobody else dies to make your escape convenient. Not again."

The rain chose that moment to return, hard, drumming on the stones, filling the silence Voss left. Her face worked through several expressions and settled on something stripped bare — not defiance, not shame exactly, but the look of a woman seeing the true size of her debt for the first time.

"And if I run anyway?" she asked quietly.

"Then I'll come after you," Cyrus said. "I'm very good at covering distance. Ask anyone."

Behind them, a small voice piped up from the altar corner. Ilo, propped against Marta, fever-bright but grinning: "Are we going *now*? Because I packed last night."

Cyrus turned. The boy had indeed packed — a tin cup, the memorized ledger safe in its oilcloth, and, inexplicably, three smooth river stones he refused to explain. Marta rolled her eyes. The twins were already folding the tarp. Doro appeared from nowhere with the fish spear. Renzo stood, testing his arm, and nodded once.

Teo came through the door with the bailing pot in hand, took one look at the assembled crew, and grinned. "The water has decided," he announced. "It always does."

Cyrus shouldered her pack and looked out at the brown, rising, hungry river — and for the first time since the Escarpment, she wasn't afraid of it. She was annoyed by it, which was different, and better.

"All right," she said. "Everybody in. And somebody keep an eye on our navigator — he's running a fever and a schedule."

What none of them could know — what the rain hid and the river kept — was that two miles downstream, moored in a side slough with its engines cold, a Halvern patrol boat was waiting out the same storm. And its captain had just received new orders: *search the churches.*
