## 11. The Patrol Boat

Teo had grown up on this river the way other children grew up inside houses. He knew its moods the way you know a mother's — which bend sulked in low water, which channel lied about its depth, where the current went quiet and mean. So when the river went quiet that evening, he felt it before he heard anything, the way you feel a door open behind you in an empty room.

The birds stopped.

Not thinned out, not drifted off to roost. Stopped. The herons lifted off the sandbar in one motion, all of them at once, and the kingfishers went with them, and then there was nothing on the water but the boat, the bailing buckets, and six fugitives who had learned, in the worst school there was, what silence meant.

"Kill the fire," Teo said.

Cyrus Sandoval was already moving. That was the thing about her he'd been learning all week, the thing that kept surprising him: she didn't argue first and act second like most people did. She acted, and argued later, sometimes days later, usually about something entirely unrelated. She pinched out the cooking flame with her fingers, hissing at the burn, and kicked wet sand over the coals while Voss doused the lantern with river water.

"Engine?" Cyrus asked, low.

"Off." He let the boat drift. "We're coming up on the Narrows bend. If they're sweeping the channel, they'll run their light along the cut bank. We want to be hugging the reed side when it comes."

"And if we're not?"

"Then we're lit up like a church window."

Behind them, Ilo stirred under the tarp. Renzo's hand found the boy's shoulder in the dark and pressed him flat again without a word. Three years of practice, Teo thought. His brother had gotten good at being hunted. It was the kind of skill Teo hoped never to congratulate him on.

The engine noise came upriver ten minutes later. Not fast — patient. A big diesel idling down through the channel, the sound of something that had all night and knew it.

* * *

They drifted.

That was the whole plan, and Teo hated how thin it sounded even inside his own head: drift, low in the water, among the drowned trees and the reed islands, while the Halvern gunboat came down the main channel with its searchlight swinging like a lighthouse gone mad.

He got everyone flat. Ilo under the tarp with the medicine crate for company. Renzo beside him, gray-faced, his bandaged arm cradled against his chest. Voss folded herself into the bilge like she'd done this before, which, Teo reflected, she probably had, though not from this side of the searchlight. Cyrus lay along the gunwale with her hat pulled down and her eyes open, watching the sky above the treeline the way she always did, reading weather even now, even tonight.

"You see anything?" she murmured.

"Cloud's breaking up over the ridge," Teo said. "Moon by midnight."

"Wonderful. A spotlight with a schedule."

"That's the spirit."

"I'm told I'm very inspiring."

The searchlight hit the far bank first. It came through the reeds as a white smear, then sharpened, raking across the water in long slow strokes, and Teo discovered something about himself in that moment: his body wanted to bolt. Every muscle he owned voted for running, for the engine, for speed, for doing *something*, because drifting felt like lying down in front of a train. He gripped the tiller of the dead engine and made himself count the strokes of the light instead. One. Two. The beam crawled up the cut bank, painted the mud cliff orange-white, slid away.

Three.

It came back lower. Closer.

The diesel grew until he could feel it in the hull, a shudder in the planks under his palms, and then the gunboat itself resolved out of the dark — black shape, high bow, a man standing at the rail with a rifle held easy against his hip, the way you hold a tool you're bored of using. The light swept past them. Ten meters of black water between the beam and the tarp where Ilo lay breathing.

Teo realized Cyrus's hand had found his wrist. Not gripping. Just resting there, two fingers over his pulse, and he understood she wasn't comforting him — she was timing him, or maybe timing herself, some shared metronome so neither of them breathed wrong. Her pulse under his own skin, when he turned his hand to catch hers, was going like a hummingbird's. So much for the famous ice-water nerves of Cyrus Sandoval.

Somehow that helped more than calm would have.

The light swung back upstream. The diesel faded. Nobody on the gunboat had seen a leaking fisherman's boat full of ghosts, because nobody ever looks at the river they think they own; they look at the banks, the channels, the places things might hide, and never at the plain black water drifting right past their bow.

When the sound was gone entirely, Cyrus exhaled through her teeth. "Well," she said. "I've had friendlier welcomes."

"You've had worse ones too," Teo said. "You told me. The Escarpment."

"Fair point. This one at least didn't involve me falling out of the sky."

From under the tarp, Ilo's voice, small and dry: "Can I sit up now? The medicine crate is poking my ribs."

"It's been poking your ribs since Tuesday," Renzo said. "You'll live."

"That's the general idea, yes."

Cyrus laughed — a short, startled bark, quickly smothered, but real. Teo caught himself smiling into the dark and decided not to examine why her laugh did that to him. There would be time for examining later, if any of them had a later. For now there was a channel to steal and a moon coming up on schedule, and he was, despite everything, almost enjoying himself, which he suspected said something unflattering about him.

* * *

The problem with the moon was that it worked both ways.

By midnight the clouds had torn open and the river turned to hammered silver, every ripple catching light, and a boat full of people is not subtle on hammered silver. They ran dark anyway, Teo steering by memory and the pale smudge of the far hills, taking the side sloughs where the reeds closed overhead like a roof. Cyrus sat up front as lookout, and somewhere in the third hour they fell into a rhythm without discussing it: she'd lift two fingers for a snag, a fist for shallows, and he'd read her hands in the corner of his eye and adjust, and the boat would slide through gaps he half-believed weren't there until they were through them.

"You fly like this?" he asked once, quietly. "Reading the ground by flickers?"

"Worse. At a hundred miles an hour, in cloud, with your life depending on a needle and a guess." She paused. "This is slower. Slower is nicer."

"You're the first pilot I've met who thinks so."

"I'm the first pilot you've met who crashed."

He had no answer for that, so he steered, and she watched the water, and the moon rode them downstream like a chaperone neither of them had invited.

It was nearly dawn — that gray hour when the river goes the color of unpolished tin — when Teo heard the diesel again.

Behind them. Close. Too close.

"They doubled back," Voss said from the stern, and her voice had gone flat and hard, all the charm scraped off it. "They do that. Halvern patrols run a loop at first light. I told you people—"

"You told us a lot of things," Cyrus said, without heat, and Teo heard the weight in it, the thing she wasn't saying yet, and filed it away.

The slough ahead forked. Left: the main channel, wide and open and lit up like a stage the moment that searchlight cleared the last bend. Right: a side passage Teo knew only from the dry season, a reed-choked vein that threaded toward an old oxbow lake. In monsoon water it might be passable. Might. The word was doing a lot of work.

"How much draft do we have left?" he called back.

"Six inches of water over three inches of hope," Cyrus said.

"So we have room to spare."

He swung right.

* * *

The reeds closed over them like a green cathedral, dripping, whispering, and for four glorious minutes Teo thought he was going to get away with it. The diesel faded behind. The channel narrowed. The bottom rose to meet them the way bottoms do, gently at first, then all at once.

The log was invisible. Of course it was invisible. It was monsoon; everything was invisible; the entire world was brown water and green leaves and the occasional optimistic bird. The log was also approximately the size of a church pew, and the propeller found it with a crunch that Teo felt in his spine.

The engine shrieked, coughed, and died with a sound like a large animal giving up.

Silence. Reeds dripping. Somewhere behind them, faint but getting louder, the diesel.

"Tell me that's fixable," Cyrus said.

"The propeller's bent. Maybe the shaft. I can't fix it here, and I can't fix it floating in a patrol lane." Teo was already poling, shoving them off the bar and deeper into the slough, because staying put next to a dead engine and a rising sun was not a plan, it was a menu item on somebody else's plan. "There's an oxbow lake ahead. Old channel, thick cover. We can hide the boat there."

"And then?"

"And then we're a bunch of people standing in a swamp."

"Airtight," said Voss.

"It's the best offer anyone's made all week," said Renzo from under the tarp, and even Ilo snorted.

The searchlight found the mouth of the main channel behind them just as the reeds swallowed the boat completely. The beam played along the open water they'd abandoned, probed the reed line, lingered — Teo poled with his heart in his throat, each push of the pole a small blasphemy against silence — and moved on. The diesel idled. Waited. Patience was the syndicate's whole religion; they'd built an empire on outlasting everyone else's outrage.

They grounded the boat in the oxbow as the sun came up, wedged her into the willows where the canopy closed like a lid, and lay in the dripping green dark listening to the patrol circle the lake twice, three times, hunting a sound that no longer existed.

At some point during the third circuit, Teo noticed that Cyrus's hand had found his again in the dark. Or that he'd found hers. Neither of them had planned it, and neither of them let go, and the searchlight swept past the willows and touched nothing but leaves, and Teo thought: *so this is what it's like, being afraid with someone instead of just being afraid.*

His brother was asleep ten feet away with a fevered boy under his good arm. The boat was wrecked. The engine was wrecked. Their route downriver was, by any honest accounting, wrecked.

He held on anyway.

* * *

The patrol gave up an hour after full daylight, its diesel grumbling away south like a sulking animal. When the last sound of it had gone, Cyrus surfaced from the willows, looked around at their situation — grounded boat, bent prop, seven people, a swamp — and delivered her verdict with the gravity of a judge:

"Well. I've been stranded in worse company."

"Name one occasion," Voss said.

"The Escarpment. Though to be fair, the company was dead."

A pause. Then Renzo, appalled: "Did she just—"

"She did," Teo confirmed. "Give it a minute. It grows on you."

"Everything about her grows on you," Ilo muttered from the tarp. "Like mold."

Cyrus pointed at him. "You, fever-boy, are in no position to be funny. Drink your water."

But she was smiling when she said it, and Teo caught the smile and kept it, the way you keep a coal alive in wet weather. Because the truth underneath all the jokes was this: the engine was dead, the river was crawling with men who wanted Ilo's memory emptied out or Ilo buried, and the only road left to the coast was the one under their own feet.

He was still holding that thought — and her hand — when Cyrus went very still, her eyes fixed on the stern of the boat.

Voss's canvas bag had come open in the night's scrambling. And spilling from it onto the deck boards, plain as a confession, was a small black notebook, its cover stamped with a logo Teo knew from burned villages and armed men: the Halvern star.

Cyrus looked at the notebook. Looked at Voss. Said absolutely nothing at all.

And Teo, watching her say nothing, understood that whatever came next had already begun.
