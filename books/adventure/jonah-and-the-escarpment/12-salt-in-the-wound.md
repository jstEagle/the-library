## 12. Salt in the Wound

Renzo's leg had gone the color of a storm sky.

Cyrus noticed it first at dawn, when the mist lifted off the slough and she went to change his dressing — a ritual she'd been performing every six hours since the gunboat night, when the engine block had come apart in his hands and taken a strip of his calf with it. The wound itself had been ugly but honest. Wounds she understood. It was the red lines crawling up from the ankle, thin as wiring diagrams, that she didn't like. The heat coming off his shin that had nothing to do with the morning.

"You're running a fever," she said.

"I'm always warm," Renzo said. "I'm from the river country. We're warm-blooded people."

"Renzo."

He looked at her then, and whatever he saw in her face made the joke die in his throat. Teo's older brother had the same stubborn jaw, the same habit of meeting trouble with his chin up, but this morning his chin was trembling, and his eyes were glassy and too bright.

"How bad?" Teo asked. He'd appeared at the boat's rail without a sound, the way he did. Fisherman's feet. He looked at his brother's leg and Cyrus watched him do the math — the same math she was doing — and arrive at the same ugly answer.

"It needs antibiotics," she said. "Real ones. The herbal stuff Ilo's been brewing is good medicine for what it's good for, but this is past it." She pressed two fingers to the red line at Renzo's knee. He hissed. "If it hits the lymph nodes in his groin and keeps climbing, we're out of time. Days, not weeks."

"Then we get antibiotics," Teo said, as if it were a simple thing, as if the nearest pharmacy weren't a hundred and forty river miles away behind a syndicate gunboat patrol.

Cyrus sat back on her heels and thought about money.

Not the way most people think about money — as a thing that comes and goes. She thought about hers the way she thought about fuel: in exact quantities, tied to specific distances. Eleven years of flying medicine and mail and the occasional questionable crate across the interior, and she had saved four thousand, two hundred and sixty dollars, kept in a coffee tin inside a false panel behind the seat of the *Marta June*, her plane, currently sitting bent-propellered on a gravel bar like a heron with a broken neck. That tin was the down payment on the floatplane she'd been going to buy in two years. It was the margin that meant she never had to take a run she didn't want. It was, if she was honest with herself, the closest thing she had to a promise that the rest of her life would be different from the last three.

She looked at Renzo, who was trying very hard not to shiver and failing.

"Does your friend fly supply drops?" Teo asked.

* * *

The friend was Denny Okafor, and Denny Okafor owed her.

Not money — Cyrus never lent money to fellow pilots, a rule she'd learned watching two marriages and one airstrip dissolve over unpaid fuel. Denny owed her the kind of debt you couldn't put a figure on. Three years ago, after the crash on the Escarpment, when the accident board was circling and everyone wanted to know why Cyrus Sandoval's plane had gone down with a hold full of unmanifested cargo, Denny had stood up in the hearing and sworn the cargo was hers, loaded without Cyrus's knowledge, and produced a manifest that may or may not have been forged in his kitchen the night before. It had probably been forged. She'd never asked. That was the deal between pilots: you didn't ask about the manifest, and when the day came, you didn't ask about the favor either.

The radio in the boat's cabin was a joke — a marine set with a range of maybe thirty miles on a good day — but the slough they were holed up in sat high enough that Cyrus had rigged the *Marta June*'s long-range set to a car battery and a length of fence wire strung up a dead tree, and at 0600 local, on the frequency she and Denny had used for a decade of "you seeing this weather?" check-ins, she keyed the mic.

"Ghost Forty-One, Ghost Forty-One, this is Calico."

Static. The monsoon was three hundred miles south and closing, and even this far ahead of it the sky sounded like gravel in a drum.

"Calico." Denny's voice came through warm and crackling, and Cyrus felt something in her chest unclench that she hadn't known was clenched. "Calico, you old ghost. Word on the strip is your plane's down past the Wall. Word on the strip is you're dead."

"The strip talks too much. Denny, I need a drop."

Silence, the particular quality of silence that meant a man was sitting down. "Kind of drop?"

"Medical. Antibiotics, IV fluids, broad-spectrum if you can get it. Cephalexin, amoxicillin, whatever your vet will part with — don't ask how I know your vet sells to you. Five hundred cc bags, at least six. A surgical kit wouldn't hurt."

"That's not a drop, Calico, that's a pharmacy. That's money." A pause. "Whose money?"

Cyrus looked at the coffee tin, sitting in the boat's bilge where she'd moved it two nights ago, wrapped in oilcloth. "Mine."

Another silence. This one had a different texture. "Cyrus. Whatever you're mixed up in —"

"I'm not mixed up in anything. I'm sitting in a swamp with a hurt man and a leaking boat, and I'm asking an old friend for a supply drop, and I'm paying cash. That's the whole story."

"It's never the whole story with you." She could hear him chewing on it. Denny Okafor thought with his mouth full, always had. "Where am I dropping?"

She'd worked it out overnight with Ilo's map — the boy had a memory like a flight computer and had drawn the river country from Halvern's own survey charts, every slough and sandbar labeled in careful pencil. "Grid reference coming. Old mission airstrip at Kilometre Forty, decommissioned, the one with the collapsed hangar. You overfly at four hundred feet, north to south along the river line, and kick the load on my mark. I'll have a signal panel out. Blind drop, no landing, no questions."

"No landing," Denny repeated. "You're not going to tell me why I can't land."

"Because if you land, you're in it. And you're not in it, Denny. That's the point."

"Cyrus Sandoval." He said her whole name the way he said *final approach* — with weight, with meaning. "The last time I covered for you, it cost me my insurance rating. You know what it costs to insure a bush pilot with a flag on his file? I fly mail routes now. Mail routes, Cyrus. I'm a flying postman."

"I know," she said. "I'm sorry. And I'll pay for the flag too. Two thousand for the run and the risk. Three thousand if the drop's clean."

The number hung on the frequency. Three thousand dollars was most of what was left. It was the floatplane. It was the margin. She watched Renzo across the cabin, asleep now, his breath coming shallow and fast, and she heard herself say: "Three thousand, Denny. And a case of the good coffee. I'll pay for the coffee separately."

"You're bleeding yourself dry for strangers," Denny said.

"They stopped being strangers somewhere around the leeches."

He laughed — a short, defeated bark. "Tomorrow, 0600. Same frequency. If the weather's under a thousand feet, I'm not coming, and don't you dare be under my flight path when I kick that load, because my aim is emotional, not mathematical."

"Denny. Thank you."

"Yeah, yeah." A pause, and then, quieter: "Bring yourself home, Calico. The strip's boring without you."

* * *

They spent the day getting ready, and Cyrus learned that preparing for a supply drop with this crew was like preparing for a supply drop with a family of brilliant, argumentative raccoons.

Teo and Renzo — Renzo insisting on helping from his bunk, directing operations like a wounded admiral — stripped the boat's canvas cover and spread it on the gravel bar, then painted it with the only high-contrast material they had: the orange emergency dye from the raft kit, thinned with river water and applied with a broken oar. The result was a panel the size of a tennis court, the exact orange of a search-and-rescue marker, and roughly as subtle.

"Anyone with eyes will see that from the air," Voss said. She'd been quiet all day, sitting on the engine housing with her arms around her knees, watching the river with the flat attention of a woman counting gunboats that weren't there yet. "Including anyone who isn't Denny Okafor."

"That's why we're doing it at dawn, in fog, on a decommissioned strip in the middle of nowhere," Cyrus said.

"That's why you're *hoping*. There's a difference."

"There usually is."

Ilo, meanwhile, had appointed himself ballistics officer. The boy was ten days into knowing Cyrus and had already correctly identified that the weakest link in any supply chain was the part where the boxes met the ground, so he'd spent the morning building a drop cradle out of the life vests and the boat's spare line, cushioned with every scrap of soft material the fugitives owned, which turned out to be two blankets and Ilo's own shirt.

"It has to survive a hundred-foot fall," he explained to Cyrus with great seriousness, holding up his contraption. "Terminal velocity for a ten-kilo package is about fourteen meters per second. If the padding absorbs the impact over half a meter, the deceleration is —" he frowned — "a lot. But survivable."

"Where did you learn that?"

"Halvern's loading manual. I read everything." He said it simply, without self-pity, and Cyrus had to turn away and check the signal panel dye for a while.

Voss found her there, at dusk, kneeling on the orange-stained canvas with a rag in her hand that wasn't really doing anything.

"You're spending it all," Voss said.

Cyrus didn't look up. "Spending what."

"Your money. Your margin. Your escape fund — don't make that face, I know exactly what that coffee tin is, I've got one too, everybody who flies the interior has one, it's the tin that means you can always leave. You're pouring it into a sick man on a broken boat in a swamp, and when it's gone, Cyrus, what's your plan? Swim?"

"Renzo saved Ilo's life in the storm. You were there."

"I was there. I was also there when the gunboat came, and I was there when the engine died, and I'll be there when the food runs out, which is in four days, and the fuel, which is in three. Every dollar you send up in smoke is a dollar that isn't buying us out of this. That's not kindness, it's arithmetic." Voss crouched down to her level, and her voice dropped to something almost gentle, which was worse. "You're not a rescuer, Cyrus. You're a pilot with a hero complex and a finite fuel load. I've watched you do this before. The Escarpment —"

"Don't." The word came out flat and hard, a door closing.

"— you spent yourself on that run too," Voss went on, relentless, "and look what it bought."

Cyrus stood up. She was taller than Voss by two inches and had never once used it, but she used it now. "What it bought," she said, "was three years of you not having to say what happened up there. So let's not pretend you're worried about my arithmetic."

Voss's mouth opened, and closed, and for one unguarded second Cyrus saw something underneath the rival and the traitor and the woman who'd sold their route — something tired and scared and young. Then it was gone, and Voss walked to the far end of the bar and sat with her back to all of them, and the camp split the way it had been splitting for days, quiet as a crack in ice.

* * *

The drop came at 0610, in fog you could cut with a propeller.

Cyrus had the signal panel lit with two lanterns and her heart going like a supercharger. Renzo's fever had hit 103 overnight; Teo had sponged him down twice and hadn't slept at all, and now stood at the water's edge holding his brother's good hand and staring up into the grey like a man praying to a god he didn't believe in but was willing to audition.

Then, out of the fog, the sound. Not the roar of a big plane — Denny flew a battered old single-engine utility bird that sounded like a sewing machine having an argument — but the most beautiful sewing machine in the world. It materialized overhead at what felt like treetop height, waggled its wings once, which was Denny's way of saying *hello, you idiot, I see you*, and Cyrus keyed the radio.

"Kick. Kick. Kick."

The load came out clean. She watched the chute blossom, watched the package swing under it, watched the wind take it thirty meters east of the panel and drop it into the slough with a splash that stopped everyone's heart — and then Teo was already moving, already waist-deep in the brown water, hauling the chute and the cradle toward the bank while Ilo shouted deceleration figures at him, and Denny's sewing machine droned away north into the fog, and Cyrus stood on the gravel bar with the radio mic still in her hand and realized she was laughing. Actually laughing, wet-eyed and ridiculous, at a splash in a swamp.

The package, when they got it open, was a miracle of Denny Okafor's particular genius: six bags of saline, four courses of antibiotics, a surgical kit, a case of the good coffee — *separately billed, you weasel*, the note said — and, wrapped at the bottom in a page of newspaper, a second note in his terrible handwriting:

*Flag on my file was worth it. Don't tell me what this is. Come home. — D.*

*P.S. The vet says hello and wants to know if you're the reason his inventory's short. I told him no. Forgive me.*

* * *

The antibiotics went into Renzo within the hour, and by noon the fever had broken half a degree, and by evening he was well enough to complain about the coffee, which everyone agreed was the surest sign of recovery.

Cyrus sat in the boat's cabin doing the arithmetic she'd been avoiding. The tin was light in her hands in a way that had nothing to do with its weight. Two thousand, six hundred and change. No floatplane. No margin. No promise.

Voss appeared in the doorway, drawn by the sound of the tin, and for a moment the two of them just looked at each other across the small space.

"Was it worth it?" Voss asked, and her voice had none of yesterday's knife in it. Just the question, bare.

Cyrus looked past her, out the cabin window, to where Teo was showing Ilo how to tie a bowline while Renzo supervised from his bunk, all three of them arguing happily, a family assembled out of wreckage and river water.

"Ask me when we're at the coast," she said.

That was when Ilo came running up the bank with his shirt flapping, waving something in his fist — a page torn from the bottom of Denny's newspaper wrapping, his eyes enormous.

"Cyrus. Cyrus, look. Look at the date." He thrust the paper at her, and his finger, jabbing at the headline, was shaking. "It's today's. Denny flew out of the coastal strip this morning. And look — look what it says about Halvern."
