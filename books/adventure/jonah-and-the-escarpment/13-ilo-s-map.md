## 13. Ilo's Map

Ilo had been keeping the secret for eleven days, which was a personal record.

He was nine years old and had never kept a secret longer than four days in his life — that one being about the goat getting into his mother's seed store, which he'd eventually confessed because the guilt made his stomach hurt worse than the fever he'd faked to get out of school. But this secret wasn't like that. This secret lived somewhere behind his ribs, folded up small and flat like the paper it used to be written on, before Señor Anselmo fed the paper into the stove and told Ilo: *Now you are the paper.*

That had been the night Halvern men burned the village records house. Ilo had been hiding under the floorboards with his uncle's ledger book on his chest, listening to boots, listening to fire, listening to Renzo shouting his name from outside and Ilo not answering because answering meant dying. He'd memorized the pages by lamplight over three weeks, the way his mother made him memorize scripture — *again, again, until your mouth knows it without you* — except this wasn't scripture. It was names. Dates. Numbers with too many zeroes. Payments to a judge named Corvales. Payments to a police captain whose face Ilo knew from the market. A column labeled simply *PROBLEMS*, which was a list of people, and Ilo's father was on it, and so was Renzo, and so was half the village council.

The paper was ash now. The words were not.

The trouble was that nobody knew. And the trouble after that was that everybody wanted things from him lately — water carried, bailing done, fevers endured quietly — and there was never a moment when all six adults in the leaking boat were calm at once. Cyrus Sandoval was always counting something under her breath. Teo was always arguing with the river. Mara Voss was always watching the treeline like it owed her money. And Renzo's arm had gone bad, hot and swollen, and Ilo couldn't add his problem to that pile. He just couldn't.

So he waited for the right moment, the way you wait for a fish, and the right moment came on the fourth morning downriver, in a place where the channel widened into something almost gentle and Cyrus let the current do the work while she patched the tarp canopy with wire and patience.

* * *

Ilo had decided he would tell Cyrus first, because she was the one who flew, and flying seemed important to whatever happened next. Also she had given him the last of the honey three days ago when his throat was sore, and hadn't made a speech about it, which in Ilo's experience was the mark of a truly good person. Good people did kind things the way the river ran downhill — without announcing it.

She was sitting cross-legged near the stern, splicing wire with her teeth and her hands doing two different jobs, and she looked up when his shadow fell across her.

"You're supposed to be sleeping," she said. "Fever rules."

"Fever's gone." He held out his palm to prove it, as if fever were visible. "Mostly."

"Mostly." She snorted, but she shifted over to make room, which was how Cyrus said yes to everything. "Sit, then. Don't touch the wire. It bites."

He sat. The boat rocked. Teo, up front, was muttering to the engine in the low coaxing voice he used for it, like a man talking a stubborn mule off a ledge. The morning smelled of wet wood and fish and the green rot of the banks sliding past. Somewhere ahead, thunder cleared its throat but didn't commit to anything.

"Cyrus," Ilo said.

"Mm."

"I have to tell you something, and you have to not be scared."

Cyrus stopped working. She looked at him fully then, and Ilo saw her do the thing adults did where their whole face rearranged itself behind their eyes before anything showed on top. "Okay," she said carefully. "Try me."

"The night they burned our records house," Ilo said, "I was under the floor. With the ledger."

Her hands went very still around the wire.

"Not the village ledger. Theirs. Halvern's airstrip ledger. My uncle kept it — he was the paymaster at the strip, before he understood what he was paying for." Ilo's voice came out steadier than he expected, which surprised him, because inside he felt like a kettle. "Anselmo made me learn it. All of it. Before the fire. He said paper burns but—" His throat closed. He swallowed hard and finished it. "*Paper burns but a boy who reads is a library.* That's what he said. Then he sent me under the floor and they shot him in the doorway."

The river made its small noises. Teo had gone quiet up front — Ilo could feel him listening without turning around.

"What's in your head, Ilo?" Cyrus asked. Her voice had changed. It was the voice she used on the radio, maybe. Careful and clear.

"Names," Ilo said. "Forty-one names. Judges, captains, mayors. Judge Corvales takes eight thousand a month to sign away village claims. Captain Duarte takes five thousand plus fuel. There's a senator's brother-in-law who owns the barges. There's dates and amounts and account numbers, and there's a list called Problems, and everyone on that list is either dead or running or doesn't know yet." He took a breath. "I know all of it. Every page. Anselmo tested me every night for three weeks. I can say it forwards and backwards."

Cyrus set the wire down very slowly, the way you set down something that might be alive.

"Why didn't you say something sooner?"

Ilo considered several answers — that he'd been sick, that he'd been scared, that he'd forgotten — and discarded them all, because lying to Cyrus felt like lying to a compass. "Because saying it out loud makes it real," he said. "And real things can be taken away. Under the floor, it was mine. Up here, it belongs to everybody, and everybody is what Halvern shoots at."

For a long moment she didn't say anything. Then she laughed — a short, cracked sound, not really a laugh at all, more like surprise escaping through a hole.

"A library," she said softly. "Under my own deck, for four days, and I was worried about the bailing shifts." She rubbed her face with both hands. "Ilo. Do you understand what that is? What you're carrying?"

"It's why they burned everything," Ilo said. "It's why they chase us. Not because we ran. Because I *read*."

"Yes." Cyrus's jaw tightened. "Yes, exactly that."

Up front, Teo had abandoned all pretense of engine-tending. He came aft in three careful steps, crouching so the boat stayed balanced, and his eyes were wet and furious at the same time, which Ilo had learned was just what Teo's face did now whenever Renzo's name came within ten feet of any sentence.

"My brother," Teo said. "Is he on the list?"

"He's on the list," Ilo said gently. "Page six. So are you, since the fire. They think you helped us run."

Teo sat back on his heels. He looked at the river for a while. "Good," he said finally. "Then they'll be looking for me anyway. Might as well be worth the looking."

* * *

They convened the whole boat's worth of opinions by midday, anchored in the shade of a fallen ceiba, and it went about as smoothly as Ilo should have predicted, which was to say it went like weather.

Renzo heard the news propped against the gunwale, arm bound and burning, and laughed until he coughed. "A library," he wheezed. "We hid him under the floor thinking we were saving a boy, and we were saving the whole case against them. Anselmo, you sly old fox." Then his laugh failed and he pressed his good hand over his eyes, and Ilo pretended not to see, because some griefs deserved privacy even in a boat twelve feet long.

Mara Voss was the complication, as Mara Voss was always the complication.

"That changes nothing about where we're going," she said. "The delta camps. Safety first, sense second, heroics never. You hand that information to whoever buys newspapers in the capital someday, from somewhere safe, years from now—"

"No," Ilo said.

Everyone looked at him. Ilo was not accustomed to everyone looking at him; usually he was the smallest thing in any room, the one people talked *over*. But something had come loose in his chest when he'd spoken the words aloud, and now the words kept coming, like water finding a crack.

"Paper waits, you said. Years. But Judge Corvales signs claims every week. Every week another village becomes a mine pit. My friend Marta's family — they're still there. Waiting for papers that will never come unless somebody carries this." He tapped his own temple. "It doesn't keep. It's not rice. It rots if I sit on it, because every week it sits, it matters less and they get further. It has to go to people who print it *now*. Before they find us. Before I get sick again, or fall in the river, or—" He faltered, hearing his own list arrive at its obvious end, and pushed through anyway. "Or before anything. It has to go *now*."

There was a silence with weight to it.

"He's right," Cyrus said quietly. Voss turned on her with an expression Ilo had seen on cats. "Don't look at me like that, Mara. There's a newspaper office at Puerto Almendro — the *Coastal Ledger*, old man Ferrer, he printed stories about Halvern's labor camps two years ago before the suits shut him up. He never stopped wanting the proof. Everyone knows it. Proof walks in his door wearing shoes, he prints it before the ink dries." She looked around the boat. "Puerto Almendro is three days further than the delta camps. Downriver, mostly. Through the gorge."

"The gorge," Teo repeated, in the tone other people reserved for words like *landslide*.

"The gorge," Cyrus confirmed. "And Halvern's coastal strip is outside town. Which means patrol boats between here and there, and no guarantee Ferrer's office isn't watched." She ticked it off like a shopping list, which Ilo was beginning to understand was how she handled fear: she itemized it until it got bored. "But if we make it, this stops being six people running and starts being forty-one names in print. That's a different animal entirely."

"And if we don't make it," Voss said, "we're six corpses floating past a newspaper office with the story still locked in a child's head. Wonderful plan. Truly. Did anyone consult the cargo?" She gestured at Ilo, sarcastic, meaning *he's too young to decide this*.

"I'm the cargo," Ilo said, "and I've decided."

Voss blinked at him. For a moment something moved behind her face that wasn't sarcasm at all — something tired and old and almost sorry. "You sound like someone I used to know," she said, and didn't explain, and Cyrus looked at her strangely, and the moment passed over the boat like a bird's shadow.

They voted, because Teo insisted boats ran better on votes than on orders. Four to one. Puerto Almendro. Voss raised her hand last, after a long look at the treeline, and muttered something about idiots and rivers and the company she kept, but she raised it.

* * *

That evening Ilo taught.

It had been Cyrus's idea, and it was a good one, the kind that arrived in her head already practical: if the knowledge lived only in Ilo, then Ilo was a single point of failure, and pilots — she explained — hated single points of failure the way farmers hated hail. So they rigged the lantern in the stern, and Ilo recited, and Cyrus wrote, and Teo wrote, and even Renzo, gray-faced and feverish, copied names onto scraps of oilcloth with a pencil stub, his handwriting going crooked as his strength did.

"Corvales," Ilo said. "Eight thousand monthly, paid through the Banco Provincial account ending 4-4-1-7. First payment March of the year the records house burned. He signed the Tres Hermanos claim forfeiture on April ninth, the Palomar claim on June second—"

"Slower," Cyrus said, tongue between her teeth, writing.

"You said slower last time and we lost an hour of dark."

"I did not say slower last time, I said *breathe* last time, which is different, and also you're nine and lecturing a grown woman about pace."

"You're the one who wanted copies."

"I want copies, not a stroke." But she was smiling as she said it, that crooked sideways smile that meant she'd forgotten to be sad for a minute, and Ilo counted that smile as payment. He'd been keeping a tally, privately. Smiles earned: Cyrus, four. Teo, two. Renzo, one and a half. Voss, zero, though yesterday she'd fixed his sandal strap without being asked, which Ilo suspected counted for something in a currency she'd deny using.

By lantern-light the ledger spread itself across the boat in fragments — oilcloth scraps, a page torn from Teo's tide tables, the back of a ration label — each copy incomplete alone, together nearly whole. It looked like nothing, Ilo thought. Scraps. Trash. If a patrol boat stopped them tomorrow and tossed everything, the soldiers would find garbage and a sick man and a woman who flew and a boy.

That was the whole trick of it, he realized. He *was* the ledger. The safest place in the world to hide a thing worth killing for was inside a person nobody bothered to count.

"Again," Cyrus said. "From the top. And breathe this time."

Ilo breathed, and began again, and the river carried them south through the dark toward a printing press they'd never seen, in a town full of men who would very much like to meet him.

Near midnight, Teo shook him awake for his watch shift — everyone stood watches now, even Ilo, especially Ilo, though his job was mostly listening. He settled at the bow beside Teo and looked downstream, where the black water ran smooth as poured ink.

"Hear that?" Teo asked.

Ilo listened. Far off, downstream, beneath the thunder that never quite rained — engines. More than one. Steady, patient, sweeping the channel in slow arcs.

"Patrols," Ilo said.

"Patrols," Teo agreed. He began, unhurriedly, to unship the oars. "So. Tomorrow we learn whether this old boat can outrun a gunboat, or whether we take the side sloughs and pray the leeches are on our side." He glanced down at Ilo, and in the starlight his grin was fierce and bright. "Either way, library — hold onto those books."

Ilo held on.
