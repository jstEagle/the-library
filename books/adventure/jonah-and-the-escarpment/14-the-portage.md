## 14. The Portage

The river gave them one more day of mercy, and then it gave out entirely.

Cyrus felt the change before she saw it — a flattening in the current, a smell of silt and stagnation where there had been the cold green breath of moving water. Teo felt it too, or read it in the way the reeds leaned, and he brought the boat in against a mud bank with the particular gentleness of a man who had stopped trusting his vessel an hour ago.

"Slough's dead ahead," he said. "Past that, the channel braids out into marsh. We'd be poling for a week." He nodded up the bank, where the jungle climbed steep and dripping toward a ridge that ran parallel to the river like a green wall. "There's a trail. Old logging cut. Goes over and comes down at the deep channel below the gorge."

"How far?"

"Twelve kilometers as the crow lies. Twenty the way we'll walk it."

Cyrus looked at the boat. The engine was dead — had been dead since Teo grounded them in the slough, drowned in river muck that no amount of cursing or coaxing would revive. The hull sat in the shallows like a tired animal, taking on water at its own leisurely, insulting pace. And on the bank sat everything they owned in the world: tarps, rice, the resin tin, the medical kit, the fuel cans — one of them still a quarter full — and six fugitives, one of them a boy with a fever that had broken but left him thin as a heron's leg.

"We can't carry the boat," said Renzo. His arm was strapped across his chest, the infection in his shoulder finally answering the antibiotics, but he still moved like a man made of glass and apology. "Not twelve kilometers. Not one."

"We can drag it," Teo said. "Rollers. Cut saplings, put them under the keel, and walk it forward a boat-length at a time. I've seen it done."

"Seen it done," Renzo repeated. "By whom? Men with backs like bridge cables?"

"By my father," Teo said, "moving his boat up to the high lake when the drought took the channel. He said the river gives you the boat and the river takes it, and sometimes you have to be the river in between." He said it without poetry, the way he said everything, and Cyrus found herself nodding before she'd decided to.

"Then we're the river," she said.

Voss laughed. It was a short, ugly sound, and Cyrus turned to find her standing with her arms crossed, looking at the ridge the way other people looked at open graves.

"You're joking," Voss said. "You want to haul this leaking coffin over that, in this." She gestured at the sky, which had begun, with the patience of a thing that knew it would win, to rain again.

"I want to get to the coast," Cyrus said. "The boat's the fastest way down the last channel. We fix it on the other side, we run the gorge, we're in the delta in two days. On foot, we're a week, and we're a week of leaving tracks."

"And the leeches?"

"What about them?"

Voss smiled, and it was the smile of a woman who had decided that if the world was going to be terrible, she would at least be accurate about it. "You'll find out."

* * *

They found out by mid-morning of the first day.

The trail was a memory of a logging cut, half-swallowed by the forest, and the rain came down in a fine, persistent, personal way, as though the weather had taken each of them aside individually to explain their shortcomings. The boat went on sapling rollers, six of them, leapfrogged forward by whoever had hands free. It took eight of them all told — Cyrus, Teo, Renzo one-armed, Voss, and the four adults among the fugitives — with Ilo walking point because he refused, with the flat dignity of the fevered, to be carried or left behind.

The leeches found them within the hour.

They came off the wet leaves in ropes, dark and glistening, and they found every gap in every collar and cuff with the unerring instinct of tax collectors. The first hour was shrieking and slapping. The second hour was a grim, silent economy of motion — salt from the ration tin, cigarette ash from Teo's pocket, the flat of a machete applied with surgical disgust. By the third hour nobody screamed anymore. They had become, in the way of people in shared misery, almost professional about it.

"There's one," Ilo announced, with the calm of a field naturalist, "in your ear, Cyrus."

"There is not a leech in my ear."

"It's in your ear."

She put her hand up, and it was in her ear. She removed it with the detachment of long practice and flung it into the green, and Ilo nodded once, satisfied, as though a contract had been honored.

They made four kilometers the first day, which Teo declared respectable and Cyrus declared, privately, a war crime. They camped on a shelf of rock above the trail, hung the tarps, and ate cold rice because fire smoke in leech country was an invitation. The rain kept on. Somewhere below them, the river they'd left spoke in its sleep.

Cyrus did the fuel count before she slept, because she always did the fuel count. One can, quarter full — maybe four liters, enough for the boat's engine to run the gorge and a little besides, if the engine could be persuaded to run at all. She wedged it between a root and the boat's hull, under her own tarp, and she slept the way she'd learned to sleep in the bush: lightly, with one ear on the world.

* * *

She woke because the rain had changed its mind.

Not stopped — the monsoon didn't stop, it only reorganized — but shifted, gone softer, and in that softness she heard the thing she'd been listening for without knowing it: a footfall, wrong-weighted, moving away from camp instead of toward it.

She came out of the tarp with the machete and without her boots, which was stupid, and she did it anyway, because the sound was already fading and there wasn't time for sense. The trail was black under the canopy, blacker than black, and she followed it by feel — root, root, slope, the wet slap of fern across her face — and the footfall became a shape, and the shape became a woman with a fuel can on her shoulder, moving downhill through the dark with the surefootedness of someone who had spent her life leaving places.

"Voss."

The shape stopped. For a long moment the rain filled the silence, and then Mara Voss said, in a voice entirely without apology, "Go back to sleep, Cyrus."

"Put it down."

"It's four liters. It's not even your fuel. I bought it in—" She stopped herself, and Cyrus heard the recalculation happen in the dark. "It doesn't matter whose it is. Four liters gets me down to the channel. There'll be a boat, someone running fuel or fish, someone who'll take payment. I can be at the coast in three days. I can be gone."

"Gone where?"

"Anywhere. Away. That's the whole point of away, Cyrus, it doesn't have an address."

Cyrus came closer, machete low, boots silent and feet freezing in the wet. Voss hadn't moved. She stood with the can on her shoulder like a soldier with a rifle, and Cyrus realized, with a strange lurch, that she was looking at a woman who had done this before — who had walked out of things in the night, more than once, and gotten good at it.

"You'd leave them," Cyrus said. "The boy."

"The boy will be fine. The boy is Halvern's problem now, not mine, and I have spent the last three years being everyone's problem and no one's priority, and I am done." Voss's voice stayed level, which was how Cyrus knew it wasn't. Level was Voss's version of screaming. "You don't get to look at me like that. You don't get to stand there in your bare feet with your little knife and play the judge. You weren't there."

And there it was. Three years of it, banked and banked and banked, and the match finally touched it.

"No," Cyrus said, and her voice came out low and terrible, and she was distantly glad it did. "I wasn't there. I was at the bottom of the Escarpment pulling my copilot's body out of the wreck while you flew home. So no, Mara. I wasn't there. Tell me where you were."

"You don't want to know."

"I have wanted to know for three years. I have flown that ridge two hundred times and wanted to know every single time. Was it the load? Was it the weather call? Did you panic? Did you—"

"I flew back."

The words dropped into the rain between them and lay there.

Cyrus didn't understand them at first. Then she did, and wished she hadn't.

"The storm cell closed the pass," Voss said, and now the level was gone, and what was underneath was worse — it was tired, it was old, it was the voice of a woman reading from a document she'd memorized against her will. "I turned with the second plane. I was behind you. I saw you go into it, and I turned back to the strip, and I sat there for six hours telling myself you were dead, and then I heard you'd come out the far side, and I—" She laughed, or something near it. "I couldn't land it, Cyrus. Not the plane. The truth. Everyone wanted to know why the second plane turned around, and I couldn't stand in front of them and say I got scared. So I said the engine was rough. And then the inquiry came, and the engine was fine, and the only thing left to say was the true thing, and I couldn't. I got on a boat. That's the whole story. That's the coward's whole story, it's always the whole story."

The fuel can was still on her shoulder. The rain came down. Somewhere behind them, up the trail, Ilo coughed in his sleep, a small sound, and Cyrus watched Voss flinch at it like a struck animal.

"You let me carry it," Cyrus said. "Three years. You let me think it was the load, or the weather, or me — that I'd made the call that killed them, that I'd crossed when I shouldn't have. You knew I turned fine. You knew the pass was flyable. You let me hate myself for a decision that was right."

"Yes."

"Say something else. Give me something else."

"There isn't something else." Voss lowered the fuel can at last, set it in the mud with exaggerated care, the way you set down something you're afraid you'll use as a weapon. "You want me to be the villain. It would be easier. But I'm not a villain, Cyrus, I'm just the person who ran, and the world is full of us, and most of us are never caught, and I have been so tired for so long." She looked up, and even in the dark Cyrus could see what the last weeks had done to her — the hollows, the hands that wouldn't stay still. "You think I sold you to Halvern because I'm evil. I sold you because they had my debts and my name and a photograph of me signing something I shouldn't have signed, and I told myself one more betrayal and I'd be free, and it's never one more. That's the trap. It's never one more. You of all people should understand — you've been carrying your dead for three years because putting them down meant admitting you were still alive."

Cyrus's hand tightened on the machete. Not to use it. To have something to hold.

"You don't get to psychoanalyze me in a monsoon," she said. "You don't get to be wise. You ran out of wise the night you walked past that inquiry office."

"I know."

"You knew Ilo was in those crates."

"I knew."

"And you did it anyway."

"I did it anyway." Voss said it flat, no flinch, and somehow that was the thing that undid the rage — because there was nothing left to fight. Rage needs a wall. Voss had taken hers down and shown Cyrus the rubble behind it, and the rubble was just a woman, soaked and shaking, who had been running so long she'd forgotten there was any other direction.

Cyrus stood there for a long time, in her bare feet, in the rain, holding a machete she was never going to use, and she felt the strangest thing: pity, arriving like a leech, painless at first, and already fastened on.

She hated that. She hated it with her whole heart. She picked up the fuel can.

"Camp's uphill," she said. "Carry it yourself. You stole it, you haul it."

Voss blinked. "That's it? That's — Cyrus, I just told you—"

"You told me a lot of things. Some of them might even be true. We'll find out at the coast, in front of a newspaper man, with a pen in your hand instead of a fuel can." Cyrus turned uphill. "You want your freedom back, Mara? Freedom isn't away. I used to think it was. Freedom is the truth, said out loud, to people who can do something about it. It's the only thing I've found that holds weight."

She took two steps and stopped, because there was one more thing, and it had been in her chest for three years and it was coming out now or never.

"Ren," she said, without turning around. "My copilot. His name was Ren. He used to sing off-key on the radio when the tower got slow. If you'd stood up and said the truth, I'd have hated you, but I'd have had him back. Not his body. Just — the truth of how he died. That's what you took. Not the plane. The truth of how he died." Her voice held, which cost her everything. "Tomorrow you walk in the front of the line where I can see you. And when we reach the coast, you testify. That's your fare. That's what the four liters costs."

Behind her, in the rain, Mara Voss began — very quietly, with none of her old elegance — to cry.

Cyrus walked uphill and did not look back, because some mercies only work if you don't watch them being received.

* * *

They made the top of the ridge by noon of the second day, and the forest opened like a hand unclenching, and there below them lay the deep channel, high and fast and brown with the monsoon's appetite, and beyond it the white seam of the gorge.

They fixed the boat on the bank in the last of the light — tarp patched with resin, sapling rollers burned as firewood for the first hot meal in two days, the engine's drowned carburetor cleaned by Teo with a feather and infinite patience. It caught on the fourth pull, coughed, caught again, and settled into a ragged but honest rhythm, and the whole camp cheered like children, and Ilo fell asleep sitting up against the hull with rice still in his hand.

Cyrus stood at the water's edge with the fuel can at her feet — all four liters of it, accounted for — and watched the gorge water run.

Voss came and stood beside her. Neither of them spoke for a while. The truce between them was not peace; it was more like a ceasefire with terms, and both of them knew the terms.

"The gorge in this water," Voss said finally. "Even for Teo. Even with the engine running."

"I know."

"It's going to be bad."

"I know that too."

Voss looked down at the white water, and then at Cyrus, and something in her face had changed — not healed, nothing so kind as healed, but aimed. "When we go through," she said, "I want to be the one bailing. I want to be useful. I want—" She stopped, and tried again, smaller. "I want to do one thing on this river that isn't running."

Cyrus looked at her a long moment. Then she handed her the bail bucket.

"First watch is yours," she said.

And that was when Teo came up the bank at a run, which Teo never did, and pointed downstream to where, far off through the rain, a light was moving against the current — steady, white, and sweeping the water in slow, patient arcs, the way a searchlight does when it knows, sooner or later, that the river will give up what it's hiding.
