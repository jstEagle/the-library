## 15. Terms

Cyrus found her at the treeline, forty yards from the camp, standing over the jerrican of fuel like a woman deciding whether to say goodbye to a lover or a hostage.

The rain had thinned to a fine gray mist that beaded on everything and refused to fall. Through it, the portage trail climbed away into leech country — a slick green tunnel of roots and dripping ferns that would take them two days to cross with the boat broken down onto rollers and their own backs. Voss had a pack half-filled. The fuel can sat at her feet. She hadn't picked it up yet.

That was the thing about Mara Voss, Cyrus thought. Even her betrayals were always one second from being reconsidered.

"Mara."

Voss didn't startle. That was its own confession. "Cyrus."

"You were going to leave the fuel."

"I was going to leave *with* the fuel." Voss turned. In the gray light her face looked wrung out, older than three years should have made anyone. "There's a mining road eight kilometers north. Halvern trucks run it twice a week. I could have flagged one down, traded the fuel for a ride to the coast, been gone before you—"

"Before we woke up."

"Before you woke up," Voss agreed. "Yes."

Cyrus came closer, boots sucking in the mud. She was aware of every step, of the weight of the wrench in her jacket pocket — not because she intended to use it, but because she wanted Voss to know she'd brought it anyway. Some conversations were better had with hardware present.

"Three years ago," Cyrus said, "when the Cessna went down on the ridge, there were four of us aboard. You know that number. You've had three years to know it."

"Don't." Voss's voice cracked on the word.

"Deni Okafor. Benji Ruiz. And me. Deni and Benji didn't walk off that mountain. I did. Because you bailed at the last fuel stop in Puerto Oscuro and took the payment ledger with you, and when the syndicate came asking who'd been flying contraband over the Escarpment, the only name they couldn't find was yours."

"They would have killed me."

"They might have. Instead they bought you." Cyrus let that sit. "You sold us our route, Mara. Coordinates, weather windows, the gravel bar where we'd refuel. All of it. To buy your debts clean. And then you vanished so thoroughly that for three years I told myself you were dead, because dead was kinder than what you actually were."

Voss's jaw worked. Rain dripped off her chin. When she finally spoke, her voice was small in a way Cyrus had never heard from her — not in twenty years of racing bush strips and trading insults across fuel drums.

"I testified once," she said. "Early. Before the crash. I went to a magistrate in San Ferro with everything I knew about Halvern's river bribes. Do you know what happened? He thanked me for my civic spirit, wrote it all down, and two weeks later Halvern moved every drop point on the river. Every one. They knew within *days*, Cyrus. There is no law above the Escarpment. There's only Halvern and the weather, and the weather is more honest."

"So that's it. That's your defense. Everyone's crooked, so why not you."

"It's not a defense. It's an explanation, and explanations are all I've got left." Voss laughed, and it was an awful sound, dry as old paper. "You want to know the truth? I've been running for three years and I'm tired. I'm tired in my bones. I stood over that fuel can tonight and I couldn't make my legs want the road. Do you understand? My legs wouldn't go. Three years of running and my body finally voted against me."

Cyrus studied her. The rain ticked against the fuel can. Somewhere back toward the church ruin, Teo's voice rose and fell as he talked Ilo through the engine problem again, patient as tide.

Here was the fork. Cyrus felt it in her chest like a control yoke going neutral — that terrible free-fall moment where the aircraft stops answering and you have to decide, alone, at speed, which way to throw your weight.

She could let Voss walk. Take the fuel back, tell the others nothing, carry the betrayal like all her other cargo. It would be cleaner. It would be safer.

Or.

"Here are my terms," Cyrus said.

Voss blinked.

"You don't get to run, and you don't get to die, and you don't get to be sorry in private anymore. You come with us. You help haul this boat over that ridge with your own hands, same rations, same leeches, same bail bucket as everybody else. We reach the coast, and you testify. Not to some magistrate on Halvern's payroll — to newspapers. On the record. Names, payments, dates, everything in that notebook you've been carrying like a rosary since the day we met at my strip."

Voss went very still. "You read it."

"I read enough. Page nine has my route on it, Mara. My *route*." Cyrus stepped closer. "Here's what you get in return. Your freedom. Not the kind you've had — the running kind. The real kind. You testify, the ledger goes public, Halvern comes apart, and nobody on this earth has a reason to hunt you ever again. You stop being a fugitive by making sure there's nothing left to flee. That's the trade. That's the only trade."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I take the fuel, and you can explain to the others at dawn why your pack smells like gasoline and your boots point north."

For a long moment neither of them moved. The mist drifted between them like something alive.

"You hate me," Voss said quietly. It wasn't a question.

"Yeah," Cyrus said. "I do. Probably always will. But I flew medicine past a wall I swore never to cross because six strangers needed it, and I burned through my savings last week buying antibiotics blind for a man I'd known five days, so I guess I've established a pattern of doing things I hate." She held out her hand. "Terms, Mara. Take them or take the road."

Voss looked at the hand for a long time. Then she did something Cyrus didn't expect: she sat down. Right there in the mud, knees drawn up, and put her face in her hands, and her shoulders shook — not sobbing, exactly, but close, the way a wall shakes before it decides whether to come down.

"I kept the notebook," she said into her palms, "because I kept thinking someday I'd use it to buy my way back in. Back to a strip, a plane, a life. And then I watched you trade your savings for Renzo's leg and give your plane's soul away piece by piece, and I realized—" she lifted her head, eyes red-rimmed and furious with herself— "I realized I've been holding the wrong currency for three years. It was never worth anything. It was only ever evidence."

"That's the first honest thing you've said since Puerto Oscuro."

"It's the first honest thing I've said in three years." She took Cyrus's hand and let herself be pulled up. Mud clung to her everywhere. She looked, Cyrus thought, lighter. Not happy — lighter, the way a plane feels after you've dumped the load you were never supposed to be carrying.

"One condition of mine," Voss said. "When this is over — if we live — you let me buy the drinks. All of them. For a year."

"Deal. Start saving now. You owe me about twenty years of drinks."

They walked back to camp together, Voss dragging the fuel can, and Cyrus noticed that she walked on the inside of the trail, between Voss and the dark trees, and decided not to examine too closely whether that was distrust or protection. Maybe, after twenty years, it had stopped being possible to tell the difference.

* * *

Voss slept maybe two hours. She dreamed of Puerto Oscuro, of the fuel stop, of Deni Okafor laughing with a cigarette behind his ear and saying, *Mara, you fly like a woman being chased.* She had laughed then. She had taken the ledger and walked to the syndicate's man and she had told herself it was survival, and survival was a word that could cover almost anything if you didn't look at it directly.

She woke in the gray before dawn and lay still, listening to the camp breathe. Teo snored like a man with a clear conscience. Ilo murmured in his sleep, counting something — coordinates, probably, that poor brilliant child and his head full of numbers that could get everyone killed. Renzo's breathing whistled faintly through the bandage. And somewhere near the fire, Cyrus Sandoval sat awake, because of course she did, keeping a watch she didn't need to keep.

Voss got up quietly and went to the boat.

It lay on its side on the rollers they'd fashioned from green saplings, hull stripped, engine off, looking less like a vessel than a wound. The gash along the port side was longer than her arm. In daylight it seemed impossible they'd floated at all.

She knelt beside it and put her palm flat on the torn planking, the way you'd check a horse for fever.

"You're up early."

Teo stood at the edge of the trees with two mugs of something hot and terrible. He handed her one without ceremony, which she understood to be either forgiveness or surveillance, possibly both.

"I couldn't sleep."

"No," he agreed. "I imagine the conscience keeps irregular hours." He crouched next to her, studying the gash. "We patch it today. Tarp underneath, resin over, canvas on top. It'll hold to the delta if the river lets us."

"And if the river doesn't?"

"Then we fix it again." He said it the way other people said *the sun will rise*. Voss envied him that, fiercely and suddenly — this fisherman with his brother bleeding and his future confiscated, who treated catastrophe as a scheduling matter.

"Why aren't you angry with me?" she asked. "Cyrus told you. About the notebook. About what I sold."

"Oh, I'm angry." Teo sipped his drink. "I'm Angolan-Portuguese-river-folk angry. It lasts decades. Ask my cousin." He turned and looked at her, and his eyes were kind and absolutely without mercy. "But anger is a poor boatman, senhora. It steers by what it hates instead of where it's going. Cyrus figured that out before you woke up. I'm just catching up." He nodded at the hull. "Hold this plank flat. If we're going to hate each other, we may as well do it productively."

So Voss held the plank, and Teo stretched tarp across the wound, and when Ilo woke he mixed the resin with the solemnity of a pharmacist, measuring by eye and memory because the instructions had dissolved with the label weeks ago. Renzo supervised from a sitting position, one-armed, offering advice nobody had requested. Cyrus worked the canvas in silence, and once, passing the resin brush, her fingers brushed Voss's, and neither of them flinched, and both of them noticed.

By midmorning the patch was done — ugly as a scar, proud of it. They broke the boat down onto the rollers and began to haul.

The ridge trail was everything the rumors promised. Leeches dropped from the wet canopy like small determined parachutes; Ilo developed a shrieking, laughing technique of flicking them off with two fingers that became, by afternoon, the camp's only entertainment. Voss hauled until her shoulders screamed and discovered, to her astonishment, that the screaming felt good — pain with a purpose attached, pain she'd chosen, unlike the three years of pain that had chosen her.

At the crest of the ridge, near dusk, the whole crew stopped at once. Below them the river unspooled through the falling light, wide and brown and gold-edged, running toward the coast and whatever waited there.

"That's the water that takes us home," Teo said softly.

"That's the water that takes us to the newspapers," Cyrus corrected.

Voss stood at the back of the little line of people, mud-caked, blistered, hollowed out and somehow refilled, and understood that the bargain had already stopped being a bargain. Somewhere over the last two days of hauling and resin and shared terrible coffee, the terms had become simply the truth: she would testify, because these people had carried her over a mountain when she'd been preparing to abandon them, and you did not sell out people who carried you. It was the first rule she'd ever learned that no one had to enforce.

"Cyrus," she said.

Cyrus glanced back, eyebrows up.

"Page eleven of the notebook. The coastal airstrip. There's a records hangar — Halvern keeps duplicate ledgers there, paper copies, in case the river office burns. I signed for deliveries into it twice." She swallowed. "If you're planning what I think you're planning, you'll want to know that the night guard changes at eleven, and the east fence sensor has been broken since March, because I'm the one who told them not to fix it."

The whole crew went quiet. Down below, the river ran on, indifferent and golden, carrying them toward it.

Cyrus smiled slowly, and it was not entirely a comforting smile.

"Mara," she said, "I think this is the beginning of a beautiful criminal partnership."

"*Partnership*," Voss repeated, testing the word like a bad rivet. Then, despite everything, despite three years and a mountain of leeches, she laughed — a real one, rusty and surprised out of her. "God help us all. Fine. But I'm still buying the drinks."

They started down toward the water, six people and a patched boat, while far behind them — though none of them knew it yet — a Halvern patrol boat was already turning upstream, its searchlight sweeping the banks like a slow black finger, looking for exactly six people and a patched boat.
