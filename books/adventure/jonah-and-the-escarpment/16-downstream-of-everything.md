## 16. Downstream of Everything

Teo had a theory about rivers, which was that they were like people: mostly honest on the surface, and entirely different underneath.

The Páramo looked calm in the last hour before dawn — a wide gray ribbon sliding between black walls of forest, mist lying on it like a blanket someone had thrown down and forgotten. But Teo could feel the current pulling at the hull through the soles of his bare feet, tugging left, then right, then left again, the way it always did above the Narrows where two channels braided together and argued about which one was the real river. He'd grown up on this water. He knew its moods the way he knew his brother's laugh, and right now it was in one of its moods.

"Left channel," he said quietly. "Past the drowned tree. Don't ask me how I know the tree is there."

"I wasn't going to ask," Cyrus said from the bow, though her tone said she absolutely had been about to ask.

"It's there because it's always been there. It fell when my grandfather was young. The river keeps things." He adjusted the tiller a hand's width. "It just doesn't tell you where it put them."

Behind him, wrapped in tarps in the bottom of the boat, Ilo shivered and muttered something in his fever-sleep. Renzo shifted to cradle the boy closer, his bandaged arm held stiff against his chest, and made a sound that was half comfort, half wince. Voss sat amidships with her arms crossed, watching the banks the way a cat watches a room it doesn't trust yet.

And somewhere behind them — Teo couldn't hear it yet, but he could feel it in his back teeth, the way you felt weather coming or a lie being told — the Halvern gunboat was coming downriver after them.

* * *

They had run most of the night by moonlight when the moon allowed it and by faith when it didn't. The engine, resurrected from its drowning in the side slough with salvaged fuel line and more optimism than any machine deserved, ran with a knock that Teo had decided to interpret as encouragement rather than warning. The boat still took water. It always took water. But the bailing shifts had found their rhythm: Cyrus on the hour, Renzo on the half-hour, Voss grudgingly whenever anyone looked at her meaningfully.

"You know what my grandmother used to say," Teo offered, somewhere in the small hours, as Cyrus handed him the bail bucket dripping. "'A leaking boat teaches you humility. A sinking one teaches you everything else.'"

"Your grandmother sounds like she'd have made a good pilot."

"She made a good living selling fish and telling people they were wrong. Same skill set, really."

Cyrus laughed — actually laughed, short and surprised out of her, the first time he'd heard it — and Teo filed the sound away like a good fishing spot, something to return to when the water got rough.

The rough water, as it turned out, was not long in coming.

* * *

The Narrows announced themselves the way they always did: a change in the sound of the river, from a murmur to a mutter to an outright argument. In the gray pre-dawn light Teo saw the white water ahead and felt his stomach do the thing it did, the little drop, the old excitement with fear stitched to its underside.

"The gorge?" Cyrus asked, coming forward to crouch beside him. She didn't grab anything for balance, which he respected. Bush pilots, he was learning, had sea legs even without the sea.

"Around the bend. Then the last gorge, and after that it opens up. Delta water. Wide and slow and full of fish camps." He paused. "Also, if we're being followed, the gorge is where they'll expect us to die."

"Cheerful."

"I'm a fisherman. We're realistic."

Cyrus studied the water ahead, and Teo watched her study it — the way her eyes moved, reading the surface the way he did but differently, looking up at the sky between glances, checking wind, checking cloud, checking all the invisible machinery of weather that she'd spent three days teaching him to see. *Clouds with flat bottoms are just passing through,* she'd told him. *Clouds that build towers are moving in.* He'd thought about that a lot since. It seemed like it might be true of people too.

"There," she said suddenly, pointing. "That line on the water. What is that?"

Teo followed her finger. Where the channels braided, the current had drawn a seam across the river — a faint rippling thread, darker than the water on either side, running clean past the drowned tree and down toward the gorge mouth.

"That's..." He blinked. He'd seen that seam a thousand times and never once thought about it. "That's where the deep water runs. Cold water under warm. My father called it the river's spine. You set your nets along it."

"And does the spine go through the Narrows?"

He traced it downstream with his eyes, out over the standing waves, into the throat of rock where the whole river squeezed itself through a gap barely twice the boat's width. The seam ran straight through the middle of the worst of it, unbroken, like a path drawn on a map only the river could read.

"Yeah," Teo said slowly, wonder creeping into his voice despite himself. "Yeah, it does."

"Then that's our line." Cyrus stood, braced her feet, and looked back at the sleeping, shivering cargo of her conscience. "Everybody hold onto something that loves you back. We're going down the middle."

* * *

Ilo woke as the roar of the gorge filled the boat.

Teo heard the boy before he saw him — a thin, cracked voice asking where they were, and Renzo answering, *almost home, little brother, almost home* — and then the world narrowed to brown water and white water and the black walls of rock leaning in on both sides, spray rising like breath, the engine screaming its knocked-out scream, and Teo steering with his whole body, his arms, his hips, his history, reading the seam of deep water as it threaded between rocks that would have opened the hull like a fish.

There was a moment — every boatman knew the moment — when the river asked you a question. Not *are you skilled.* The river didn't care about skilled. The question was simpler and worse: *do you believe your own eyes?*

The seam bent left around a fang of rock that shouldn't have been there, according to every run Teo had ever made in daylight, in a better boat, with dry powder and a lighter heart. Every instinct screamed right. His eyes said left.

He went left.

The fang slid past close enough to touch, the boat dropped into a trough deep enough to swallow the propeller's roar, and then they were through — spat out the far side of the gorge into water gone suddenly wide and gentle and gold, because the sun had come up while nobody was watching, and it came up gold, pouring down the delta like honey off a spoon.

For a long moment nobody spoke. The engine knocked. Water dripped off everything. Somewhere a bird Teo couldn't name shouted the news to other birds.

"We're alive," Voss said finally, sounding personally offended by it.

"Speak for yourself," said Renzo. "I died twice in there. Once on each rock."

Then Ilo laughed.

It started as a cough and turned into a laugh, weak and rusty and real, and Teo turned to see the boy sitting up against his brother, his eyes clear for the first time in two days, his fever-bright gaze gone steady and ordinary and eleven years old. "You steered us *through* it," Ilo said. "I saw. You went where the shiny water goes."

"The shiny water," Teo repeated.

"That's what I call it. When the light comes down and you can see the deep part shining." Ilo shrugged, embarrassed, but his grin stayed. "You saw it too."

Teo looked at Cyrus. Cyrus looked at Teo. Something passed between them that neither of them would ever quite be able to explain to anybody else — the understanding that a child had named the thing they both steered by, and that the name was better than either of theirs.

"The shiny water," Cyrus agreed solemnly. "Best navigation instrument I've ever flown with, and I've flown with some expensive ones."

* * *

The delta opened around them like a hand letting go.

Channels split and rejoined. Islands appeared, low and green, hung with fishing nets drying like strange laundry. Smoke rose from cooking fires. And then, rounding a bend thick with reeds, they came upon the first camp: stilt houses, a dozen boats pulled up on the mud, children who spotted them first and raised a cry that brought adults out of doorways and up from nets.

Teo cut the engine and let them drift in, because you drifted into a camp; arriving under power meant you were selling something or fleeing something, and technically they were both, but no sense advertising.

An old woman waded out to meet them, hip-deep, hands on hips, taking in the tarps, the bandages, the strangers, the obvious weeks of river living written on all of them. Her eyes settled on Ilo and softened, then hardened as she worked out why a boy that age would look like that.

"You're the ones from upriver," she said. Not a question.

"You've heard?" Cyrus asked.

"Heard?" The old woman snorted. "Child, the river heard before you did. Fisherman at the second camp has a cousin whose radio picks up the coast stations. They're saying Halvern men are offering money for a boatload of fugitives and a pilot woman." She looked Cyrus up and down with frank appraisal. "You don't look worth much money."

"I get that a lot," Cyrus said.

"But the boy with the ledger—" The old woman's voice dropped, and so did every other voice in earshot, which was now considerable. Word had already spread along the stilts and the walkways; people were drifting toward the water's edge with the unhurried purpose of folks who intended to miss nothing. "The boy who carries the names. That's this boy?"

Ilo, to his credit, sat up straighter instead of hiding. "Yes, ma'am."

The old woman looked at him for a long moment. Then she turned and barked something up the bank, and the camp moved — really moved, boats launching, nets coming in, a boy sprinting down the shore path toward the next camp with the particular urgency of a child who has been given an errand that matters.

"What did you say?" Teo asked.

"I said light the signal fires and send runners to every camp in the delta." She was already wading back toward shore, waving them in. "Halvern owns the police in the town. Fine. Let them own the police. They cannot own forty fishing camps and six hundred cousins. By tonight, everyone on this water will know these faces and know them as friends. Your patrol boat can search every channel. It will find nothing but people who have never seen you." She glanced back, and her grin was quick and sharp as a filleting knife. "Word travels faster than patrols. It always has. That's why the powerful hate gossip, and why the rest of us keep it fed."

* * *

They came ashore to hot fish stew, dry clothes, and the particular miracle of solid ground that didn't leak. Renzo was carried — protesting — to the camp healer. Ilo was fed until he threatened to become spherical. Voss vanished briefly, and Teo noticed Cyrus notice, noticed Cyrus decide to let it go, and understood without being told that whatever bargain those two women had struck on the portage trail was still holding, fragile as wet paper but holding.

Toward evening, Teo found Cyrus at the end of the main walkway, looking out over the water where the sunset was doing things with color that no bush pilot's language could improve on. He handed her a bowl of stew and stood beside her.

"They'll come to the town," she said. "Agents. Maybe tomorrow, maybe the day after. The camps will hide us, but the town is theirs. And the ledger can't stay in a boy's memory forever. Paper burns, but memories drown, starve, get bought." She stirred the stew without eating. "There's a records room at Halvern's coastal airstrip. Everything they are, in filing cabinets. If the ledger reaches the newspapers and Halvern's lawyers bury it in a week, we've done nothing. But if the airstrip's own records burn the same day the story breaks—"

She stopped herself, the way she'd been stopping herself for two days, and Teo realized she hadn't finished the sentence for anyone yet. She was still deciding whether to say it out loud, because saying it out loud made it real, and real things cost.

Teo looked out at the water. At the camps lighting their lamps one by one down the channels, at the signal fires already glowing on the far islands, at Ilo sitting on a dock with his brother, laughing at something, alive, fever broken, carrying his terrible cargo of names like a lantern he refused to put down.

"My brother used to say," Teo offered, "that a fisherman who won't risk the net eats nothing."

Cyrus looked at him sideways. "Your family has a saying for everything."

"We're a talkative people. It's how we survived." He shrugged. "So. Say it out loud, pilot. Whatever it is you're planning that makes your face look like that. Say it, and we'll tell you honestly how crazy it is."

Cyrus Sandoval looked at him for a long moment. Then, out over the gold-and-rose water, she finally said it — and Teo, listening, felt his stomach do the drop again, the old excitement with fear stitched to its underside, and knew that the answer was: crazy, yes, and also exactly the kind of thing the river had been carrying them toward all along.
