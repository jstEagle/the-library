## 17. The Coast Is Not Safe

The delta announced itself the way an old friend announces a visit — by smell. Mud and fish and woodsmoke, and under it all the green rot of a thousand channels eating their way toward the sea. Cyrus stood in the bow beside Teo while the fishing camps slid past on either side, stilt houses leaning over brown water, nets hung out to dry like the sails of ships that had given up.

"You're grinning," Teo said.

"I'm not."

"Your face is doing something. I've been watching it for six days. That's a grin trying to get out."

Cyrus smoothed her expression with deliberate effort. "It's relief. It looks different from a grin. Grins have more teeth."

"Six days of bailing," Teo said to Renzo, who was propped against the gunwale with his bandaged arm cradled like a sleeping cat, "and she still won't admit to being happy."

"I'll admit to being alive," Cyrus said. "Happiness is a rumor I'll investigate once we're ashore."

The truth was that when the first camp had hailed them — three days after the gorge, after the fever broke, after Voss had stood at the tiller for eleven hours without complaint because Cyrus had told her to — something had loosened in Cyrus's chest that she hadn't known was clenched. They had made it. Six fugitives, one broken pilot, one fisherman with a boat held together by resin and stubbornness, one rival turned reluctant ally, and a boy carrying a ledger in his head worth more than all of them combined.

Made it. She kept testing the words like ice on a pond.

Ilo came up from under the tarp canopy, hair wild, eyes bright for the first time in a week. "Is that the sea?"

"That's the delta," Cyrus said. "The sea is what happens next."

"It's so *wide*." He gripped the gunwale and stared at the braided channels opening ahead of them like veins off a heart. "How do you know which one goes where?"

"You don't," Teo said. "You ask somebody who does, and you pay them in fish or flattery."

"Which are you better at?"

Teo considered this with the seriousness he brought to everything. "Fish."

They tied up at the third camp past the last channel marker, where an old woman named Berta took one look at their faces, one look at Renzo's arm, and started issuing orders before anyone introduced themselves. Her camp straddled a bend where the water ran slow and wide, a dozen stilt houses connected by walkways, drying racks, and forty years of accumulated rope. Children materialized to stare at Ilo and then, discovering he knew card games, vanished with him into the shadows beneath a house.

Berta fed them smoked fish and cassava and asked no questions until everyone had eaten. Then she sat back, folded her hands, and looked at Cyrus the way you look at weather coming in from offshore.

"You're the ones Halvern's looking for."

It wasn't a question, so Cyrus didn't treat it as one. "News travels fast."

"The river travels faster. A patrol boat went through yesterday asking after a group off a downed plane. Paid gold for information." Berta's mouth twisted. "Gold buys a lot around here. Not from me. But from somebody, always."

Voss set down her cup. "Then we shouldn't stay."

"You shouldn't stay *long*," Berta agreed. "But you can't move tonight, and your man there needs his arm cleaned properly, and none of you have slept a real sleep since the storm. So you'll stay tonight, and tomorrow I'll put you in a cargo skiff headed down to Puerto Sombra with the morning fish run. Nobody searches the fish boats. The smell offends even the righteous."

Cyrus felt herself smiling despite everything. "You've done this before."

"Honey, I'm eighty-one years old. I've done everything before. Most of it twice."

* * *

Puerto Sombra was not somber at all. It was loud, salt-crusted, and built entirely out of things that had once floated — ship timber, crate planks, an entire church roof that had clearly begun life as a warehouse. The town clung to its harbor the way barnacles cling to a hull: tenaciously, and with no intention whatsoever of going anywhere.

They came in with the fish boats, smelling appropriately terrible, and split up as Berta's nephew instructed. Teo took Renzo and two of the others to a rooming house above a chandlery. Voss walked Ilo and the remaining fugitives to a boarding room rented under a name Voss produced from some buried version of her past — "Marisol Quintero," delivered with such flawless boredom that Cyrus briefly wondered how many other names lived in that woman like seeds in a pod.

Cyrus herself walked to the harbor office to do the thing she'd been turning over since the delta: find out when the coastal packet left for the capital, and whether a newspaper office there would still be standing by the time they reached it.

She got her answer, and it wasn't the one she wanted.

"No packet till Friday," said the harbormaster, a vast woman named Odalys who was simultaneously stamping documents and berating a clerk. "Storm season. And half the coast road's washed out anyway, so don't ask about buses."

"Friday's fine. Where would I find—"

"And if you're looking for the telegraph office, it's closed. Has been since Tuesday."

"Closed? Since when does a telegraph office close?"

Odalys finally looked up. She had the eyes of someone who had watched a great deal arrive in her harbor and very little leave. "Since Halvern bought the building. They say it's for 'renovation.'" She made the word sound like a disease. "Funny kind of renovation. Men go in, papers come out. No wires come in anymore at all."

Cyrus kept her voice pleasant. "And the police? If a person needed to report something serious."

Odalys laughed. It was not a happy sound. "Report it to Chief Andrade? Sweetheart, Andrade's boat has a Halvern flag on it. Not officially. Officially it's a 'sponsorship.' Unofficially, everybody in this town knows whose payroll keeps his lights on." She leaned across the counter. "You're not from here, but I'll tell you something for free, because you've got river mud on your boots and that means you've come a long way to be disappointed. In Puerto Sombra, the law is whatever Halvern says it is. You want justice here, you'd best bring your own."

Cyrus thanked her, bought a cup of terrible coffee from the cart outside, and stood drinking it while the harbor worked around her.

That was when she saw them.

Two men at the far end of the pier, dressed in the particular way that city men dress when they want to look like they belong somewhere they don't — clean boots, dry shirts, the restless posture of people waiting rather than watching. One of them held a photograph. He showed it to a fish seller, who shook her head. He showed it to a boy with a bucket, who shook his head harder. Then he turned, and even at fifty meters Cyrus recognized the cut of the jacket, the corporate neatness of him.

Halvern. Here. Waiting.

Not searching — *waiting*. Which meant they weren't guessing. Which meant someone had told them the river route ended here, and the only person who'd known the whole plan end to end—

No. She stopped herself. Voss had sold the route once, months ago, to buy her freedom. That debt had been paid and confessed and bargained over on a ridge trail in leech country. Cyrus had watched the woman carry a share of the portage load that would have broken men half her age. Suspicion was a habit, and habits didn't care whether they were true.

Still. She finished her coffee slowly, memorizing the two men, their positions, the fact that they checked the fish boats as they unloaded. Then she walked back through the town with the unhurried gait of a tourist, and did not let herself run until she was behind the chandlery, out of sight.

* * *

The rooming house smelled of lamp oil and dried fish. They crowded into Teo's room — all nine of them, which meant most of them sat on the floor, and Ilo claimed the windowsill as sovereign territory.

"They're at the pier," Cyrus said. "Two of them, maybe more. Showing photographs. Working the fish boats."

"How good are the photographs?" Voss asked.

"Good enough that I didn't linger."

Renzo shifted, wincing as his arm bumped the wall. "Then we take a boat south. There are cays down the coast — fishing families, no officials, no Halvern. We could—"

"And do what there?" Cyrus asked. "Wait? For how long? For what?"

"For safety."

"Safety isn't a place, Renzo. I spent three years learning that." She looked around the room, at each of them in turn. "Here's what we know. The ledger in Ilo's head names judges, officers, paymasters — half the machinery Halvern uses to make itself legal. We came here to give it to a newspaper. But the telegraph office is closed. The roads are washed out. The packet doesn't leave till Friday, and those men on the pier will be checking every boat that leaves before then. And even if we got word out — even if a story reached the capital — what happens? Halvern's lawyers bury it for a year while their judge friends stall every inquiry. By the time anything moves, the records are gone. Shredded, burned, 'lost in a fire.' I've seen how these companies clean up. It's what they're *good* at."

"So what do we do?" Ilo asked quietly. "We didn't come all this way for nothing."

"No," Cyrus said. "We didn't."

She found she was looking at the wall, at nothing, at the map of the coast she was drawing in her head. Halvern's coastal airstrip sat on the point north of town — she'd seen it on the flight charts years ago, back when she still memorized charts for pleasure. A graded strip, a fuel dump, hangars. And attached to the main hangar complex, because every syndicate needed one, a records room. Flight manifests. Payroll. The paper skeleton of the whole operation.

Including, if Ilo was right about how Halvern kept duplicates, copies of everything the boy carried in his memory. Claim records. Bribery schedules. Names.

"There's an airstrip north of here," she said. "Halvern's. Their planes come in and out of it, which means their paperwork lives there too. Ledgers, manifests, duplicate files. Everything Ilo memorized, probably sitting in a filing cabinet a hundred meters from an aviation fuel dump."

Silence. Teo was the first to understand, because Teo understood engines and fire and the short distance between them.

"You want to burn it."

"I want to *take* it," Cyrus said. "Copy it, photograph it, carry it out if we can. And burn what we can't carry. If the evidence exists only in Ilo's head, Halvern only has to kill one boy to win. If it exists in a hundred pages mailed to a dozen newspapers—" she shrugged, "—then killing Ilo doesn't buy them anything, and they'll know it."

"And if we're caught?" Voss asked. Her voice was flat, professional, the voice of a woman running numbers.

"Then we're caught. But Voss — think about what they do to witnesses. Think about what they did to Renzo's village. There's no version of this where we hand ourselves to the police and get a fair trial. Odalys told me the chief of police eats out of Halvern's hand. Every official door in this town is locked. So we stop knocking on doors." She heard her own voice harden and let it. "We build our own."

Teo grinned — teeth and all, the full grin Cyrus had accused herself of hiding. "I knew you had it in you."

"Had what in me?"

"Recklessness. You fly like a librarian. I've been wondering where you kept it."

"In the same place I keep my money, Teo. Carefully, and not for long."

Renzo raised his good hand. "I'm in. Obviously. They burned my village's records. I'd like to return the favor."

One by one the others agreed — the quiet fisherman Dario with a nod, the two sisters Marta and Luz with a single exchanged glance that contained an entire negotiation, young Pilar with a fierce "yes" that surprised everyone including Pilar. Ilo raised his hand from the windowsill like a student.

"No," Cyrus said immediately.

"Yes," Ilo said. "It's my ledger. I know which pages matter. If you're taking files, you need me to sort them fast, in the dark, before anyone comes. Nobody else can do that. Not you, not Voss, nobody."

"She's right," Voss said, and Cyrus shot her a look that could have chilled beer. "She *is* right. Sentiment is expensive, Cyrus. You taught me that on the portage."

"I taught you that sentiment is expensive. I don't recall teaching you to spend other people's children."

"He's not spending himself," Voss said. "He's investing. There's a difference, and he knows it better than any of us."

Cyrus looked at Ilo. The fever had carved hollows under his eyes, but behind them was the same steady light she'd seen in the boat, in the church ruin, on the portage — the light of a kid who had decided, somewhere along six hundred miles of river, exactly who he intended to be.

"Conditions," she said. "You stay within arm's reach of me the entire time. The moment I say run, you run, and you don't argue, and you don't look back. Those are the terms. Break them and I turn this whole operation around and we take the Friday packet like respectable criminals."

Ilo considered. "Those are bad terms."

"They're the only terms."

"Fine." He stuck out his hand, and she shook it, and his grip was surprisingly firm for someone who'd nearly died of fever a week ago. "But I want it noted that I negotiated badly."

"Noted," Cyrus said solemnly. "For the record."

* * *

They planned until the lamp burned low.

Teo drew the airstrip's approaches from memory — he'd fished the point waters for years and knew the fence line ran down almost to the tideline on the seaward side, unpatrolled, because Halvern never imagined trouble coming out of the ocean. Voss, in the flat voice of a woman reading her own indictment, laid out the compound's routines: shift changes at midnight and four, the fuel dump's layout, the location of the records room she had signed delivery manifests for in another life. Each detail cost her something visible; each one she paid anyway.

"We'll need a diversion," Cyrus said. "Something that pulls the guards to one end of the strip while we're in the records room."

"Fire works," Teo offered.

"Fire always works. Fire is the solution of poets and arsonists, and I'm only licensed for one of those."

"I can cut the perimeter wire," Teo said. "Quietly. Give me ten minutes and a good pair of cutters and the seaward fence opens like a purse."

"And the planes?" Marta asked. "If we take the files and leave their aircraft, they fly patrols after us by dawn."

Cyrus looked at the lamp flame for a long moment. Everyone waited. They were getting used to doing that — waiting while she stared at fire or water or distance, waiting for the pilot's verdict.

"The planes stay," she said finally. "Too guarded, too big a job, too much noise. We take the paper and we go. Paper is the empire. Planes are just transportation." She paused. "Although if the fuel dump happened to have an accident on our way out, I wouldn't lose any sleep."

"You would lose sleep," Voss said. "You'd lie awake composing apology letters to the insurance company."

"That's true, and I'd enjoy every one of them."

Later, when the others had curled up on floors and benches and the room had gone quiet except for Renzo's careful breathing, Cyrus stood at the window looking north toward the point, where the airstrip's boundary lights would be burning in the dark like a string of patient orange beads.

Three years ago she had sworn never to cross the Escarpment again. Two weeks ago she'd crossed it twice. She had spent her whole adult life believing that the smart move, the survivor's move, was to see trouble coming and be elsewhere when it arrived. Miles, not years. Distance as a philosophy. Keep moving, keep clear, keep yourself intact.

And now she was planning to walk into the heart of the thing that owned the police, the courts, the telegraph lines, and probably the weather, armed with a fish knife, a stolen pair of bolt cutters, and a twelve-year-old archivist.

Her mother would have called it madness. Her dead crew would have called it Tuesday.

"Cyrus." Voss's voice, low, from the corner where she sat awake, because of course Voss was awake. Voss was always awake at the worst hours. "Are you sure about this?"

Cyrus thought about the two men on the pier with their photograph. About Odalys's laugh. About a village's claim records going up in smoke so a company could steal land with a signature instead of a gun.

"No," she said. "But sure is for people with options. We're past options. Now it's just aim."

Voss was quiet a moment. "That's the most honest thing you've said since I met you."

"Don't get used to it. Tomorrow I go back to lying about my feelings and my altitude."

Outside, far to the north, a plane droned in over the point — Halvern's nightly mail run, right on schedule, landing lights sweeping the dark like a lighthouse for people with something to hide. Cyrus watched it descend and thought, with a calm that surprised her:

Tomorrow night, that runway belongs to us.
