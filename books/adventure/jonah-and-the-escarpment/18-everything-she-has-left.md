## 18. Everything She Has Left

The plane came apart in the order Cyrus had memorized before she was old enough to vote.

She started at dawn, when the light through the hangar's broken slats fell in long gold bars across the concrete, and she worked with the same unhurried competence she brought to everything: cowling off first, then the battery, then the propeller she'd straightened twice herself after gravel bars and one memorable fence post. Each part went onto a canvas tarp laid out in rows, cleaned, catalogued, wrapped. She had done this ritual in reverse a thousand times — assembling the Cessna from crates and hope in her early years, learning every bolt by feel, by failure, by the particular language of an engine that talked back.

Now she was teaching it to leave.

"You don't have to do this," Teo said from the hangar doorway. He'd been standing there for ten minutes, which for Teo was practically a speech.

"Yes, I do."

"There are other planes."

"Not ones I can trade for what we need." She set the carburetor down on the tarp with a click that sounded, to her ears, like a period at the end of a sentence. "Halvern's airstrip has a fuel dump, two hangars, a radio shack, and a records room full of paper that's kept six villages landless for eleven years. To get to it we need boats, charges, and enough diversion to make their private army look the wrong way. Nobody sells that for cash out here. They sell it for parts." She wiped her hands on a rag already more grease than cloth. "And I've got the best parts on the coast."

Teo came in and crouched beside the tarp, studying the engine the way he studied water — looking for the channel under the surface. "You love this plane."

"I love what it does." That wasn't entirely true and they both knew it. "I loved my crew too, and I couldn't keep them. This one I can spend."

He didn't answer right away. Teo never wasted words; he spent them like a man counting coins in a current. "My grandmother used to say there's a difference between losing something and giving it away," he said finally. "Losing happens to you. Giving is something you do."

"Your grandmother sounds like she'd have made a hell of a bush pilot."

"She drowned. So maybe not." But he smiled when he said it, and stayed, and handed her wrenches without being asked, because that was how Teo loved things — by holding the other end of the work.

* * *

By noon the buyers started coming, drawn by word of mouth the way fish come to chum.

Old Ferro arrived first, in a truck that predated the war and possibly the wheel, and walked around the Cessna's engine like a priest approaching an altar. "This is sacrilege," he announced cheerfully. "A woman dismantling a perfectly good aircraft. In some countries you'd be arrested."

"In some countries I'd be decorated. What'll you give me for the powerplant?"

Ferro named a figure so insulting that Cyrus laughed out loud, and they were off, circling each other for forty minutes while Ferro's grandson ate mangoes on the tailgate and offered unsolicited commentary. In the end she got nearly double his opening offer plus a skiff with a patched hull and a promise of diesel, and Ferro got the engine and the satisfaction of telling everyone at the cantina he'd robbed a famous pilot blind. Both of them left happy. That was the trick of bargaining, Cyrus had learned long ago: if only one person walks away smiling, you did it wrong.

The radio came next, then the wheels, then the instruments — sold piecemeal to a scattering of pilots and river traders who would never know what the avionics bought: three cases of diversion charges from a man who owed Cyrus a debt dating back to a monsoon medevac, waterproof detonators, a second boat, rope, tarps, and a crate of medical supplies Renzo insisted on even though his arm was still strapped to his chest and he could barely lift it.

"It's not for me," Renzo said, catching her look. "It's for whoever gets shot tomorrow night."

"Don't jinx us."

"I'm not superstitious. I'm realistic. There's a difference." He tucked the crate under his good arm anyway, and Cyrus noticed he held it the way Ilo held his memorized ledger — carefully, like something that mattered more than its weight.

By late afternoon the hangar looked gutted. The Cessna sat on its gear with empty eyes where the instruments had been, and Cyrus stood in front of it longer than she meant to, doing arithmetic she didn't want to do. Eleven years of miles. Every strip she'd ever cut out of jungle, every storm threaded, every letter and medicine crate and homesick miner carried over the wall of the Escarpment. The plane had been the one constant in a life that measured itself in departures.

"You're stalling," said Voss from behind her.

"I'm inventorying."

"You're saying goodbye badly." Voss leaned against the doorframe, arms crossed, wearing the expression she'd worn since the portage — stripped down, watchful, no longer performing anything. It was strange, Cyrus thought, how much younger she looked without the performance. "I've watched you do this your whole career. Say goodbye to a place by pretending you're checking the weather."

"And how do you say goodbye?"

"I don't. I just leave." Voss pushed off the frame. "It's why I ended up here. Maybe try it differently this time."

Cyrus put her hand flat against the fuselage. The aluminum was cool and familiar as an old scar. "One last flight," she said. "Tonight. Just up the coast and back, no cargo, no plan. Then she goes to Ferro whole — airframe, tail, whatever's left."

Voss studied her. "That's not efficient."

"No," Cyrus agreed. "It isn't."

* * *

They flew at dusk, all six of them crammed into a plane built for four, because nobody had suggested otherwise and nobody wanted to be the one who did.

Teo took the right seat, uninvited but correct, and watched her hands on the controls with the focused hunger of a man memorizing. He'd been flying with her in his head since the river — asking questions at odd hours, tracing routes on fogged windows with one finger. Cyrus had started letting him hold the yoke on calm stretches, correcting him with a knuckle tap instead of words, and the boy fishederman turned out to have hands that listened.

Below them the delta opened up in the last light — braided channels silvering toward the sea, fishing camps sending up their small smokes, the world going soft and blue at the edges. Somewhere out there, past the darkening water, Halvern's airstrip waited with its floodlights and its ledgers and its private army drinking beer and playing cards, certain that no court and no pilot would ever come for them.

"Left rudder," Cyrus said quietly. "You're drifting."

Teo corrected. "Where'd you learn to fly?"

"Crash course." She let him groan. "My father ran mail runs up north. I soloed at sixteen because he broke his wrist and somebody had to get the doctor to Kessel's Landing. After that it was just — the thing I did. The thing I was." She banked gently south, showing him the turn. "People ask how many hours I have. Like that's the measurement. Hours, miles, strips logged. I used to think that was a life."

"What is it now?"

Cyrus looked back at the cabin. Renzo dozing against the fuselage with his bound arm propped on a supply crate. Voss staring out the window with an unreadable face, keeping her own counsel the way she had since agreeing to walk into Halvern's airstrip wearing her old credentials. And Ilo, wedged between crates, watching the instruments with fierce attention, lips moving as he counted something — altitudes, maybe, or names.

"Now?" she said. "Now it's about who's in the plane."

* * *

The council of war happened on the hangar floor that night, sitting on crates around a single lantern, because tables invited formality and formality invited arguments about rank, and none of them had any rank worth arguing about.

Cyrus laid it out with charcoal on a sheet of plywood: the airstrip's layout as Voss remembered it, the fence line, the fuel dump, the records hangar, the barracks, the patrol schedule Voss claimed still hadn't changed in three years. "We go in at 0300. Teo cuts the fence here, north side, away from the lights. Voss walks in the main gate with her old Halvern pass and walks out again having confirmed the records location and unlocked the hangar side door. I rig the records room. Charges on the fuel dump as insurance — if anything goes loud, Halvern loses more than paper."

"And us?" Renzo asked.

"You're the reserve. You and Ilo stay with the boats."

"No," said Ilo.

Everyone stopped. The boy had barely spoken in planning sessions, communicating mostly through glances and the occasional devastating factual correction. Now he stood up, small and straight-backed in the lantern light, and Cyrus saw that his fever was fully gone and something else had taken its place — a settled quality, like ballast.

"The records room has a card index," Ilo said. "Cross-referenced. I memorized the ledger's numbering system when I copied it. If anything's misfiled, or if Halvern moved documents after I escaped, you'll need someone who knows what page proves what. You'll need me."

"Ilo—"

"You said everyone volunteers." His chin came up. "I heard you tell Teo. Everyone chooses their part. Well, I choose mine. Those files have my village's name in them. My father's name, in the column where Halvern wrote what they took from us. I'm not waiting in a boat while strangers decide whether my family gets its land back."

The silence stretched. Cyrus looked at Teo, expecting objection, and found instead a complicated pride he was trying to hide and failing at completely.

"He's right," Teo said quietly. "About the index. And about the rest."

"He's twelve."

"He's carried that ledger in his head across half a country," Teo said. "He's earned the choosing."

Cyrus rubbed her face. She thought about arguing, and recognized the argument for what it was — fear wearing the costume of caution. She'd been afraid for Ilo since the gravel bar. She'd be afraid for him the rest of her life, probably, the way she was afraid for every soul she'd ever carried. That was the job. That had always been the job, and she'd mistaken it for distance for eleven years.

"Fine," she said. "But you stay within arm's reach of me inside the wire, you wear the dark clothes Renzo's sewing, and if I say run, you run. Deal?"

Ilo considered it with the gravity of a man negotiating a treaty. "Deal."

"Renzo — reserve and extraction. You're our driver if this goes sideways. Voss—"

"I know my part." Voss didn't look up from the plywood map. Her voice was flat and steady. "I walk in wearing their colors one more time. Fitting, really. It's how I got into this mess; might as well be how I get out of it."

"Voss." Cyrus waited until the woman raised her eyes. "Once you're through that gate, we can't cover you. You know that."

"I've known worse odds." A ghost of the old smirk, gone almost immediately. "Besides. I owe a debt to a copilot neither of us can pay anymore. This is the closest thing to interest available."

Nobody spoke for a moment. The lantern hissed. Outside, the monsoon wind worried at the hangar roof, and somewhere down the channel a fishing boat's engine coughed and caught.

Then Teo raised his hand like a schoolboy, deadpan. "For the record — I volunteer for the fence. And for learning to fly, starting the day after tomorrow, assuming we survive."

"Assuming we survive," Cyrus agreed, and was surprised to find herself smiling.

* * *

She slept two hours and gave up on it around one, and went back to the hangar not to work but simply to sit with what remained of the plane.

The moon was up, thin behind cloud. The Cessna sat in the silver light with its hollowed instrument panel and its bare engine mount, and Cyrus climbed into the pilot's seat out of pure habit and put her hands where the yoke used to be, gripping empty air.

"Eleven years," she told it. "You never once quit on me. Not once. Even bent prop, even over the Escarpment in that cell — you got them down alive, and that's the whole job, isn't it? Get them down alive."

The plane said nothing, being a plane, but the wind moved through the hangar and the control cables ticked softly in their housings, and Cyrus chose to take it as agreement.

She thought about her rule — the one she'd built her whole life on, the one Voss had shattered with a dead man's name. Never cross the Escarpment. Never carry what you can't set down. Never measure yourself by anything you can lose. She'd thought those rules were wisdom. They'd been fear, dressed well, and she'd worn them so long she'd forgotten they weren't her skin.

Tomorrow night she'd cross a different kind of escarpment, on foot, into gunfire, for people she'd known less than a month. And it felt — this was the strange part, the part she'd turn over for years afterward — it felt lighter than any solo crossing she'd ever made.

She wasn't losing her life. She finally understood that. She was spending it. On purpose. On them.

Cyrus climbed out, patted the cold fuselage once — goodbye, old girl, you were the best miles I ever flew — and went to find Renzo's sewing kit, because if she was going to break into a syndicate airstrip she was damn well going to do it in clothes that fit.

At the door she paused and looked back once. The plane sat patient in the moonlight, already becoming someone else's story.

Behind her, far down the coast, a light blinked twice from the direction of Halvern's strip — regular, patient, green.

Floodlights. Or a signal.

Either way, dawn was coming, and with it, the last day of everything she had left.
