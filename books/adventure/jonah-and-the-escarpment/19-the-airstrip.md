## 19. The Airstrip

*Teo*

The fence was the kind of fence built by men who had never once worried about fences.

Teo crouched in the elephant grass a hundred meters out and studied it the way he'd study water before running a gorge — not for what it looked like, but for what it was hiding. Chain-link, two meters high, topped with three strands of wire that glinted wrong in the moonlight. Not barbed. Charged. Behind it, Halvern's coastal airstrip lay flat and pale as a scar: two hangars, a fuel farm with its silver tanks standing like fat soldiers, a control shack with one lit window, and four aircraft parked wingtip to wingtip on the apron, fat and smug and full of other people's money.

"Three strands," he whispered. "You touch any of them, you light up like a festival."

Beside him, Cyrus Sandoval lay flat in the wet grass with wire cutters in her fist and an expression Teo had learned to read over two hundred river miles: calm on top, furnace underneath.

"And the ground?" she asked.

"The ground is honest. It's only the fence that lies." He pointed. "There. Where the grass grows tall against the mesh. The syndicate mows everything except there, because there's a drainage culvert and they don't want their machines in the mud. Men who own judges still don't like mud."

Cyrus turned her head and looked at him, and in the dark her eyes were just two points of borrowed moonlight. "You've been here before."

"I delivered fish to the cook, once. Two years ago." He shrugged. "The cook complained about the mud. Everyone complains about the mud. Nobody fixes the mud, because fixing things costs money and Halvern only spends money on taking it."

She almost smiled. He'd seen it happen maybe six times now, always sideways, always gone before it landed. "Cut low," she said. "Bottom corner, where the tension's loosest. We go through one at a time, roll the flap back down behind us. If anyone asks the fence why it failed, I want the fence to say *erosion*."

"You want the fence to lie for us."

"I want everything tonight to lie for us," Cyrus said. "It's the last night anything will."

Teo took the cutters and crawled forward through the grass, and the grass closed over him like water, and he thought — not for the first time, but more clearly than ever — about Renzo lying in the fishing camp with a bandaged arm and a rifle he barely knew how to hold, insisting he was coming anyway. About Ilo, twelve years old, memorizing a ledger the way other children memorized songs. About his brother saying, the night before, *we didn't cross all that river to stop at a fence.*

The bottom wire gave with a sound like a small sigh. Teo cut a flap wide enough for a man, rolled it back, and held it.

"Cyrus," he breathed. "Come through."

* * *

*Mara Voss*

The badge weighed nothing and everything.

Mara stood at the main gate in her old Halvern security uniform — kept folded in a dry bag for three years, pressed under a stack of Cyrus's spare clothes, waiting for a night exactly like this one or, more honestly, for no night at all because she'd never truly believed she'd have the courage — and showed her credentials to a guard named Ossie who had been junior staff when she left and was now senior enough to be bored.

"Voss," Ossie said, squinting at the laminated card. "Mara Voss? You're on the inactive roster."

"I'm on the *reactivation* roster as of this week," she said, and let her voice do what it had always done best: close doors before questions could walk through them. "Regional audit. There's been irregularities flagged in the fuel reconciliation. Corporate sent me. You can call Port Authority if you like, but while you're on the phone, ask them why your manifest numbers don't match your customs filings, because that's the conversation we'll be having next."

Ossie's face went through a short, efficient journey from suspicion to self-preservation. "I— no, ma'am. That won't be necessary."

"No," Mara agreed. "It won't."

He waved her through. She walked across the apron with her boots ringing on the concrete, past the parked aircraft, past the fuel tanks, past the records hangar with its padlock and its single floodlight, and every step of it felt like walking into a house she'd burned down herself and finding the furniture exactly where she'd left it.

That was the thing about Halvern nobody understood from the outside. It wasn't a fortress. It was a club. And clubs only worked if everyone inside agreed not to look too hard at anyone else's hands.

Her hands, tonight, were shaking. She put them in her pockets.

At the corner of the records hangar, in the shadow between the floodlight's reach and the fuel farm's pipes, a shape detached itself from the darkness: Cyrus, in coveralls two sizes too big, carrying a canvas bag that clinked.

"You're late," Cyrus murmured.

"I'm *punctual*. You're early. There's a difference and it matters." Mara glanced back at the gate, at Ossie's little lit window, at the whole sleeping architecture of the place that had owned her debts and her silence for three years. "Audit story bought us until shift change. Four o'clock. Maybe less — Ossie gets nervous, and nervous men make phone calls."

"Then we work fast." Cyrus knelt and opened the bag: det cord, timers, tape, a collection of small terrible promises. "Records hangar first. Then the planes. You keep walking your rounds so anyone watching sees a guard doing guard things."

"And if someone stops me?"

Cyrus looked up at her, and for once there was no old history in her face at all — just the flat practical light of a woman doing arithmetic. "Then you talk, Mara. It's the one thing you've always been better at than me."

Mara felt that one land where it was aimed, nodded once, and walked back out into the floodlight with her spine straight and her badge showing, a traitor in both directions now, and freer than she had been in years.

* * *

*Cyrus*

The padlock was brass and proud of itself. Cyrus respected it, shot it off with a suppressed round into a sandbag, and let herself into the records hangar like a woman entering a church she intended to rob.

Inside: filing cabinets in ranks. Shelving. Crates marked AIRCRAFT PARTS that smelled of paper instead of metal. Somewhere in these cabinets was the duplicate set — the real set, the one that never went to the coast office, the one Voss had sworn on her brother's grave was kept here. Flight manifests. Payment schedules. The judge's name, spelled out in expense codes. Three years of a company writing down, in its own tidy hand, everything it had ever done to everyone it had ever broken.

Ilo had half of it in his head. But heads could be hurt, and paper, properly handled, could testify.

She taped charges to the structural posts — not to destroy the files, never the files, just to bring the roof down on any attempt to save them — and set the timers, and her hands were steady, and she noticed they were steady, and thought: *this is what I'm for, then. Not miles. This.*

A radio crackled at her hip. Teo's voice, a thread of sound: *"Fence line, west side. Two guards walking the perimeter. Early. They weren't supposed to—"*

And then the world turned white.

Every floodlight on the strip came up at once, and a siren began somewhere above the control shack, a flat mechanical scream, and Cyrus knew — with the particular cold clarity that had visited her once before, on a ridge in weather, three years ago — that the plan was over and the price had started collecting.

The alarm hadn't tripped on the fence. It hadn't tripped on the lock. Later she would learn it was a motion sensor on the records hangar's rear door, a sensor that appeared on no blueprint Voss remembered, installed eight months ago by a site manager covering his own thefts.

Every plan meets its price. Tonight the price was time.

*"Cyrus,"* Teo said in her ear, very calm, the way he got calm when the river got bad. *"They're running. Toward the hangars. You have maybe ninety seconds of being invisible, and then none."*

She grabbed the satchel of documents she'd already pulled — the judge's file, the payment ledgers, the thickest, truest stack — slung it across her chest, and ran.

* * *

*Ilo*

Ilo had been told to stay at the drainage ditch, and he had stayed at the drainage ditch, mostly, in the way that a person stays near a thing rather than at it.

Renzo crouched beside him in the reeds with the rifle across his knees, and Ilo could hear his brother breathing — quick, shallow, the breathing of a man whose arm hurt and who had decided not to mention it. Above the grass, the floodlights swept. The siren screamed. Somewhere across the strip, Cyrus was doing whatever Cyrus did, which Ilo privately believed was the most reliable phenomenon in the world, more reliable than rain.

"The radio shack," Renzo whispered. "When she signals. That's our job. Cut their ears."

"I know the job."

"You know the job." Renzo's hand found his shoulder, heavy and warm. "Ilo. If it goes wrong — if I say run — you run to the boat and you don't argue. Swear it."

Ilo looked at his brother. Renzo had carried him across a portage ridge through leech country; Renzo had pulled him out of a flooding boat; Renzo had gotten himself cut open saving him from a storm swell, and had laughed about it afterward, badly. There was no version of tonight in which Ilo ran anywhere without him, and they both knew it, and Renzo asked anyway, because that was the kind of lie brothers told each other to sleep.

"I swear," Ilo lied.

The signal came — two clicks on the radio — and they moved, low through the reeds, along the fence line toward the squat concrete building with the antenna mast. Ilo had the wire cutters. Renzo had the rifle and the bad arm. The radio shack door was a ordinary door with an ordinary lock, because Halvern guarded its money and its planes and had never once imagined that its *voice* might be worth stealing.

Ilo picked the lock in forty seconds, the way Teo had taught him on the boat, with a fishhook and patience.

Inside: a desk, a transmitter bank, a logbook open to a page of patrol schedules. Ilo swept the logbook into his shirt — evidence, more evidence, it was everywhere once you started looking — and began unscrewing the microphone panel while Renzo watched the window.

"They're coming," Renzo said suddenly. "Ilo, the yard — three of them, with lights—"

And then the glass exploded.

* * *

*Cyrus*

She came out of the records hangar into a strip gone mad.

Floodlights raked the apron in long white blades. Men were spilling from the barracks block, pulling jackets on over bare chests, and two of them had rifles already, and the fuel farm stood between her and the fence like a silver promise: *burn me and everything ends.* The parked aircraft sat beyond it, fat and full, and Cyrus ran the line she'd rehearsed in her head for two days — det cord on each wing root, timer, next plane, don't think, *miles*, count it in miles, ninety seconds a plane—

The first charge went on clean. The second. On the third, a voice shouted from the control shack, high and cracking: *"Contact! Contact at the flight line!"*

Gunfire opened up across the fuel dump — wild, terrified, the shooting of men who had never been shot at and were discovering they hated it. Rounds cracked overhead. One sparked off the concrete a meter from her boot. Cyrus dropped flat beside the landing gear, heart slamming, and did the arithmetic she'd done her whole life, except now the units were seconds and lives.

*"Teo,"* she said into the radio. *"Tell me you're clear."*

*"We're not,"* Teo said, and underneath his calm she heard the shape of something breaking. *"Radio shack. They came early. Renzo's hit — grazed, head, he's bleeding but talking, Ilo's got the transmitter apart, but there are men coming around the fuel tanks and we are pinned at the fence and Cyrus — the boats are far."*

Everything, meeting its price, all at once.

Cyrus pressed her back against the warm aluminum of the third aircraft, closed her eyes for exactly one second — one second, that was the budget — and saw it all laid out like a chart: the charges ticking on two planes, the satchel of testimony against her ribs, Renzo bleeding at a fence, Ilo alone with a broken radio, Voss somewhere in the floodlights wearing a uniform that fit her like a confession.

One second. Then she keyed the radio.

"Mara. The gate. Now. Bring your beautiful lying face to the west fence and get my people out."

Static. Then, ragged: "On my way. Cyrus — the planes—"

"Burn when I say burn." She rolled out from cover, sprinted the gap between aircraft with bullets snapping at the concrete behind her, slapped the last charge onto the fourth plane's wing root, and set every timer to the same number: four minutes. Four minutes to clear the strip, or be cleared by fire.

Across the yard, at the west fence, a flap of chain-link lifted, and a small figure with wire cutters slipped through into the dark, dragging a taller figure after him.

Ilo. Renzo. Moving.

Cyrus ran for the fence with the ledger against her heart and the sirens screaming her name, and behind her the first timer reached into the fuel-soaked dark and struck its match.

* * *

*Teo*

Later, Teo would remember it in pieces, the way rivers break a wreck into parts.

The fence flap. Ilo's face, gray with fear and furious with pride. Renzo's blood on his hands, shockingly bright, shockingly little — a furrow along the scalp, ugly, survivable, *survivable*, he made himself believe it. Mara Voss appearing out of the floodlight chaos in a Halvern uniform, hauling Renzo's good arm over her shoulder without a word, without hesitation, as if she'd been waiting three years for exactly this job.

And then the fuel dump went up.

It did not explode so much as *bloom* — a soft orange flower unfolding over the tanks, followed a heartbeat later by the sound, which arrived through the ground and up through Teo's boots and into his teeth. Heat washed across the grass. The first aircraft caught, then the second, twin bonfires of aluminum and money, and men who had been shooting scattered like ants from a kicked nest, and the sky over Halvern's private empire turned the color of dawn arriving four hours early.

Through the smoke, running low and fast, came Cyrus Sandoval with a satchel strapped across her chest and her coveralls singed at the shoulder, and she hit the fence flap at a slide, rolled through, and came up on one knee in the elephant grass with firelight painting her face gold.

"Everybody alive?" she demanded.

"Renzo needs a doctor," Teo said. "Ilo's fine. Voss—"

"Is a traitor twice over and I'll thank her for it later." Cyrus looked back once at the burning strip — at the planes, the hangars, the tidy paper empire collapsing into heat — and Teo watched something leave her face, some old weight she'd been carrying since before he knew her.

"Four minutes," she said. "We have four minutes before they organize, and a long way to the boats. Teo — can you find us a channel in the dark?"

He looked at the black line of the coast, at the mangrove maze beyond it where the water hid and only he could read the signs. His brother leaned against Voss, bleeding and grinning. Ilo gripped the stolen radio logbook like a hymnal.

"Give me five," Teo said, "and I'll give you a river."

Behind them, the last timer found its mark, and the night filled with fire.
