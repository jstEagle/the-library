## 20. Burn the Ledger

The fire found the fuel dump before Cyrus found the door.

She heard it first — a soft, almost polite *whump* from across the airstrip, like a giant drawing breath — and then the night turned orange at the edges of the hangar's broken windows. Heat pressed against her back like a hand. Somewhere outside, Teo was shouting her name into the dark, and somewhere farther off, Halvern's private army was discovering that diversion charges make a very persuasive argument about where to point your guns.

None of that mattered as much as the filing cabinets.

They stood against the far wall of the records hangar in two neat rows, gray and indifferent, the most boring objects on the whole coast. Inside them: eleven years of Halvern's airstrip ledger. Fuel purchases. Landing fees paid in cash to men with no names. Bribes itemized with the tidy precision of accountants who had never once imagined anyone would come for them. A judge's initials, appearing monthly, regular as rain.

Ilo had carried those names in his head across three hundred miles of river. But heads could be hurt. Heads could be drowned or shot or simply disbelieved. Paper was paper. Paper was proof.

Cyrus wrenched open the first drawer.

It stuck, naturally. Everything in this country stuck — boat engines, hangar doors, old wounds. She put her boot against the cabinet and hauled, and the drawer came out with a shriek that she felt in her teeth, spilling folders across the concrete like startled birds.

"Come on," she muttered to them. "Fly."

* * *

Three minutes earlier, everything had gone according to plan, which should have been her first warning.

Voss had walked through the front gate wearing her old Halvern credentials and an expression of such bland administrative boredom that the two guards had actually waved her through. Teo had cut the fence on the north side in ninety seconds flat, apologizing under his breath to the wire the whole time, as if it were a living thing he was inconveniencing. Renzo had taken the overwatch position on the fuel drum stack with Ilo beside him, small and fierce, clutching the radio shack key like a talisman.

And Cyrus had slipped into the records hangar with a satchel of incendiary charges and one instruction from herself to herself, delivered in the calm voice she used for passengers in turbulence: *rig it, light it, walk away.*

That had been the plan. Rig it, light it, walk away. Let the fire do the work. The ledger would burn along with the hangar, the planes, the whole paper empire, and Halvern's history would end in smoke with no witnesses but the moths.

It was a good plan. It was even a just plan.

It was also, she realized now, standing ankle-deep in scattered files while the sky outside went the color of a forge, the plan of a woman who still measured things in miles. Burn it all and fly on. Leave nothing behind you but heat.

She looked at the folders in her hands. She looked at the door, forty feet away, glowing at its edges.

"Oh, hell," she said, to nobody, and started stuffing papers into her flight jacket.

* * *

There is a particular kind of arithmetic a pilot learns, usually too late and always the hard way, about weight. Every pound is a decision. Fuel or cargo. Speed or safety. Cyrus had spent twenty years making those trades in the air, where the consequences arrived fast and clean.

Here they arrived fast and dirty.

The jacket held maybe ten pounds of files before it stopped closing. Ten pounds out of four hundred. She could hear the fuel dump roaring now, a sound like the ocean deciding to be angry, and the heat through the windows had graduated from hand-on-your-back to hand-around-your-throat. The second cabinet drawer yielded easier than the first, as if the cabinet itself had given up on dignity.

"You can't carry it all," she told herself, and then, because she was who she was, "so carry what matters."

Judge Ferro's payment schedule. The Kessel Ridge strip logs — the same ridge, she noted with a laugh that came out wrong, where her crew had died hauling someone else's contraband. The payroll for the men who burned the village claim records. She built a brick of paper against her chest, cinched the jacket, and grabbed a canvas map case off the wall to stuff the overflow into.

The smoke found the ceiling and began to lean down into the room like a curious animal.

* * *

Outside, the world had become weather.

Teo crouched behind the wrecked fuel truck, watching the compound dissolve into orange chaos, doing the thing he did best — reading water, except tonight the water was fire and gunfire, and the channels were gaps between muzzle flashes. Renzo had Ilo flat behind the drums, one arm over the boy like a wing. Voss was supposed to be at the gate, walking out the way she'd walked in, bland as paperwork.

Instead Voss was running toward the burning hangar.

"You absolute idiot," Teo breathed, meaning all of them, meaning himself, meaning every stubborn soul on this river, and went after her.

* * *

Inside, Cyrus had discovered the flaw in her revised plan, which was that doors swell in fires, and this one had swelled shut.

She stood before it with a brick of evidence against her ribs and felt something old and familiar settle over her shoulders — not panic, exactly. Recognition. Three years ago, on the Escarpment, she had watched a cargo door jam in exactly this way, with wind screaming through the gap and her copilot's voice in her ear saying *we can hold it, Cy, we can hold* — and they couldn't, and she had lived, which was its own kind of sentence.

She had promised herself, since, that she would never again be trapped by a closed door. So she stopped trying the handle and looked around for another way out, because there was always another way out; Teo had taught her that on the river. You don't fight the current. You find the slough.

The roof vent. Of course. Every hangar had one, because hot air has to go somewhere, and so, if you were foolish and lucky, could a woman with an armful of burning-adjacent paper.

She dragged a maintenance ladder — thank you, Halvern, for your impeccable facilities budget — to the spot beneath it, climbed, and put her shoulder into the vent hatch. It gave on the third hit with a bang she felt through her whole skeleton, and a column of superheated air punched past her face hard enough to take her breath and part of her eyebrows with it.

Through the hole: the night sky, black and beautiful, streaked with sparks rising like the world's most expensive fireworks.

Below her, inside the hangar, the first of the incendiary charges caught.

* * *

Getting out through a roof vent while carrying forty pounds of other people's crimes turns out to be less like flying and more like being born, if birth were conducted in a chimney. Cyrus went headfirst, hooked her elbows over the roof edge, and hauled, jacket buttons popping, files rasping against corrugated metal, the heat below her climbing her spine like a ladder of its own.

She got one leg over. Then the other. Then she lay flat on the hangar roof, cheek against hot tin, gasping air that tasted of kerosene and victory, and allowed herself exactly three seconds of triumph.

In the fourth second, the fuel dump went up.

* * *

Later, people at the fishing camps would describe the sound differently. Some said thunder. Some said the river coughing. Ilo, who had the best ears of any of them, said it sounded like a door opening.

What it actually sounded like was the end of an argument eleven years long. Four hundred drums of aviation fuel let go in a chain, each one passing the invitation to its neighbor, and the shockwave rolled across the compound and lifted Cyrus off the roof and set her down six feet away in the gravel like a discarded doll.

Her ears rang. Her brick of files had flown free and lay scattered around her, pages fluttering down through the firelight, and she crawled after them on hands and knees — *the judge, get the judge, get the ridge logs* — gathering them against her chest while Halvern's planes burned in their rows, beautiful and doomed, their aluminum skins blooming with color like autumn leaves that had finally been allowed to change.

Somewhere in the roar, a voice. Close. Furious.

"*Cyrus!*"

Voss dropped to her knees beside her, hair singed, face black with soot except for two white circles around wide eyes. Behind her came Teo, and then Renzo limping with his arm around Ilo, and the five of them knelt in the gravel of their enemy's airstrip, gathering scattered paper out of the firelight like children collecting fallen leaves.

"The hangar—" Voss shouted over the noise.

"Gone!" Cyrus shouted back.

"The planes—"

"Burning!"

"The ledger?" And here Mara Voss, who had sold them all for her freedom, gripped Cyrus's shoulder with both hands, and her eyes were not the eyes of a traitor or a survivor or even a rival. They were the eyes of a woman who needed the answer to be yes the way drowning people need air.

Cyrus pulled the map case and the ruined brick of files tight against her chest, felt the paper crumple, felt it hold.

"Got it," she said.

For one moment — one strange, suspended, roaring moment — Mara Voss smiled at her. Not the sharp smile from the strip, not the bargaining smile from the river. An old smile. Maybe the first honest one in three years.

Then the secondaries started going off, and everyone remembered simultaneously that standing up in a fuel fire was a bad idea, and they ran.

* * *

They ran the way rivers run — finding the low ground, the gaps, the channel between the flames. Teo led, because Teo always led when the way was made of chaos; he moved through the burning compound the way he moved through rapids, reading it, trusting it, choosing lines no one else could see until he was suddenly through them and waving the rest onward. Renzo carried Ilo when the boy stumbled. Voss kept a hand fisted in the back of Cyrus's jacket, and Cyrus let her, which was its own kind of testimony.

They came over the fence — through the hole Teo had cut, which now seemed funny beyond all reason, all that fire and all their salvation resting on ninety seconds of work with a pair of cutters — and dropped into the darkness of the coastal scrub, and ran until the heat on their backs became memory instead of fact.

At the treeline, Cyrus turned once.

Halvern's airstrip was a lake of light. The hangar was a torch. The planes were funeral pyres, one after another after another, and above it all the smoke climbed straight up into the windless night, a black column visible, she estimated, from thirty miles in every direction. From the delta camps. From the patrol boats. From everywhere.

Everyone would see it. Everyone would know.

"Good," she said quietly. Let them look. Let them read it like a headline written in flame: *the ledger is loose.*

* * *

They didn't stop until the old church ruin, two miles inland, where the rest of their small nation had waited with the boats and the supplies and a pot of tea Renzo's wife's recipe demanded even at the end of the world. There was a stretch of perhaps ten minutes where nobody spoke at all — just hands checking hands, counting each other like coins, confirming that everyone who had gone into the fire had come back out of it.

Renzo's arm was scorched through his sleeve where a round had grazed him covering Ilo's run. He waved it off. Ilo had a burn blister on one palm from the radio shack door and refused to let anyone look at it, holding the boy's equivalent of a trophy: a fistful of torn transmission logs he'd ripped from the shack on his way out, on his own initiative, because evidence was evidence.

"He took souvenirs," Renzo said, half laughing, half undone.

"I took *proof*," Ilo corrected, with the gravity of a supreme court.

Cyrus sat down hard on a fallen beam and laughed until it turned into something else, and Teo put a mug of terrible tea in her hands and sat beside her without a word, which was the correct amount of words.

Across the ruin, Voss stood apart, as she always did, looking back toward the glow on the horizon. Cyrus watched her for a while. The firelight reached even here, staining the edge of the sky, staining Voss's profile orange and gold.

"They'll have seen it from the town," Voss said finally, without turning. "Halvern will know within the hour what burned. They'll come for the paper before it can be read." She paused. "They'll come for us faster."

"I know."

"And the ledger survived. You're sure."

Cyrus opened the map case. Inside, smoke-stained but whole, the files lay in their brick — Ferro's payments, the ridge logs, the payroll, the names. Eleven years of Halvern's empire, folded up small enough to carry against a beating heart.

"It survived," she said.

Voss nodded slowly. Then she did something Cyrus had never seen her do in all their years of rivalry and all these weeks of betrayal and bargain: she sat down on the ground, deliberately, in the dirt, among the fugitives and the fishermen and the burned pilot, and accepted a mug of terrible tea.

"What happens tomorrow?" Ilo asked, from the safety of Renzo's good side.

Cyrus looked at the files. At the people. At the glow of everything she'd spent, still burning on the horizon — the plane parts traded away, the savings gone, the identity dismantled mile by mile. Tomorrow the delta town. Tomorrow the newspaper office, if the elder's cousin's contact was real. Tomorrow Halvern's lawyers and Halvern's guns, all of it converging on a child's memorized numbers and a sack of scorched paper.

Tomorrow, the cost came due.

But tonight — tonight the tea was terrible, the fire was theirs, and for the first time in longer than she could measure, Cyrus Sandoval wasn't counting anything at all.

"Halvern knows we're coming," she said, and drank. "So we'd better not keep them waiting."
