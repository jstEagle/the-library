## 21. Testimony

### Cyrus

The newspaper office smelled of ink and burnt coffee and the particular ambition of young reporters who had never been shot at. Cyrus stood at the counter with the ledger wrapped in oilcloth against her chest like a colicky infant, and she did not let go of it until the editor himself — a gray-templed woman named Ondine Marr whose hands were permanently stained black — signed a receipt for it.

"You understand," Marr said, tapping the receipt, "that this is going to make some very powerful people very unhappy."

"Madam," Cyrus said, "I have spent three weeks being made unhappy by powerful people in a leaking boat. They'll have to take a number."

Marr almost smiled. Almost. Then she opened the ledger to its first page and stopped smiling entirely, because the first page was a list of payments, dated and initialed, and the third name down belonged to a circuit judge whose face was on posters in every courthouse from here to the capital.

Cyrus left her to it. Outside, the delta town was doing what delta towns do in the morning: arguing about fish prices, hosing down the dock, pretending nothing in the world had ever gone wrong. Ilo sat on a bollard eating fried plantains with the concentration of a scholar, and when she came out he held one up to her without looking away from the harbor.

"Teo says the boat's fixed enough to go home tomorrow."

"Teo says a lot of things."

"He also says you snore."

"Teo," Cyrus said, "is a liar with a good engine." She sat down beside him on the bollard, which groaned politely under their combined weight. For a while they watched the water do its slow brown business between the pilings.

"Ilo. When they go back upriver — Teo and Renzo — what do you want to do?"

He was quiet long enough that she thought he hadn't heard. Then he said, "Renzo's arm is better. He can work again. And Teo needs someone to bail."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the answer for them." He turned the plantain over in his fingers. "The answer for me is — I don't want to go back to the river yet. Not because of Halvern. Because of the map." He tapped his own temple, where the airstrip ledger lived behind his eyes alongside everything else he'd memorized to survive. "If there are more trials, they'll need me to say what I saw. What I wrote down. I'm not done being useful, and I don't want to be useful alone."

Cyrus looked at this boy — thirteen years old, maybe fourteen, who had carried a syndicate's sins in his head across two hundred miles of monsoon country — and felt something in her chest rearrange itself, quietly, the way cargo shifts in turbulence.

"Well," she said. "I could use a navigator. Mine has a tendency to fly into weather."

"Your copilot died flying into weather."

"Thank you, Ilo. Very comforting."

"You're welcome." He handed her the last plantain, which was the closest thing to affection his generation offered, and she took it.

### Teo

The arrests came the way rain comes at the end of dry season — first one drop, then a rumor of drops, then the whole sky letting go all at once.

Teo heard about the first one at the fuel dock, where a man with a radio was reading names off a wire report: two Halvern site managers, detained at the coast. By evening there were four more names, and by morning the name everyone had been waiting for — the judge — though the report used careful words like *under review* and *recused pending inquiry*, which everyone understood to mean *caught*.

"They'll say he didn't know," Renzo said. He was sitting on the boat's gunwale with his bandaged arm propped on a cushion Ilo had sewn out of a flour sack, which was lopsided but sincere. "Rich men never know anything. That's how they stay rich."

"Then why are they arresting anyone?"

"Because somebody made them." Renzo nodded toward the town, where the newspaper office had run a special edition with the ledger's first page reproduced across the front, the numbers so large even people who couldn't read could see the shape of the theft. "Somebody carried paper through hell to put those numbers where they'd hurt."

Teo grunted. In his family, praise was distributed the way salt was — sparingly, and only where needed. But privately he agreed. Privately he was proud of all of them, including the pilot, including even Voss, though he'd have sooner swallowed the anchor than say so.

They were leaving tomorrow. The boat was patched — new planking amidships, resin cured, the engine running with a sound Teo described as *honest* and Cyrus described as *a dying goose*, which had led to a wager still unresolved. Renzo's fever had broken a week ago. The river was dropping. Everything said *go home*.

Except that home was a burned village with no claim records, and the claim records were the whole point.

"The lawyer woman says the claims get restored," Renzo said, as if reading his brother's mind, which he usually was. "She said the village filing is first in line because of the testimony. Our testimony. Yours and mine and Ilo's."

"Our testimony is us talking into a machine in a room that smelled like fish."

"And it worked." Renzo smiled, and the smile was still strange on his face, still new, like a tool recovered from a wreck and cleaned up. "Brother. It worked."

Teo looked at the water instead of answering, because the water was easier. But that night, packing, he set aside the bail bucket he'd carved for Ilo — the small one, the one sized for a boy's arms — and then, after a long moment, put it back in the boat.

Habit. That was all. You built a thing for someone and your hands forgot they weren't coming.

His hands were sentimental fools. He'd have to speak to them.

### Voss

The prison was not what Mara Voss had expected, which was disappointing, because she had expected things her whole life and been wrong every time, and she'd hoped the streak might end somewhere pleasant.

It was clean, at least. Whitewashed. A window in the interview room with an actual view of a tree. The matron had taken her statement with the weary efficiency of a woman who processed one confession per decade and found this one unusually cooperative.

"You understand you're waiving—" the matron began.

"Yes. Yes. Waiving, surrendering, testifying, implicating myself in the sale of six human beings' escape route to the people hunting them." Voss folded her hands on the table. "I've had three weeks in a flooded church to compose the list. Shall I begin alphabetically or by severity?"

The matron blinked. "Alphabetically is fine."

What Voss had not expected — what she genuinely, professionally had not prepared for — was Cyrus Sandoval showing up on visiting day with a paper bag of oranges and the expression of a woman delivering a court summons to a funeral.

"You came," Voss said.

"You kept the bargain. I keep score." Cyrus sat down across the table and pushed the bag over. "These are contraband, probably. Don't tell the matron; she seems tired."

Voss took an orange because her hands needed something to do that wasn't shaking. Three weeks ago she would have had a line ready — she always had a line ready, lines were how she'd survived, lines and exits and other people's trust converted to cash. Now she turned the orange over and said, "I keep waiting to feel worse. Or better. Everyone told me I'd feel one or the other."

"Do you?"

"I feel like a woman in a room with a window." She looked at the tree. "It's more than I gave anyone else. It's less than I deserve. I'm trying to hold both of those at once without dropping either."

Cyrus was quiet for a moment. Outside, somewhere, a bird was making an idiot of itself in the tree, and neither woman mentioned it, and both heard it.

"The trial's in six weeks," Cyrus said finally. "Ondine Marr's lawyers are calling it the Escarpment Papers. There's already talk of a federal commission. Your testimony is the hinge, Mara. You sold the route; only you know who bought it."

"I know." Voss peeled the orange in one unbroken spiral, an old bar trick, muscle memory from a hundred rooms where the trick bought her ten minutes of goodwill. She laid the peel on the table like a specimen. "You know what the worst part is? I did the arithmetic a hundred times, on the river, in that church. Every version came out the same: my freedom cost less than theirs. That's all betrayal is, Cyrus. Bad arithmetic performed by a frightened person."

"And now?"

"Now I'm doing the arithmetic again." She met Cyrus's eyes. "Six fugitives. One boy who trusted me because I brought him water when he was sick. One pilot who had every reason to leave me on that ridge and didn't." Her voice did something complicated and she smoothed it flat. "The sums come out different when you're not the denominator."

Cyrus stood, tucked the chair in, and paused at the door.

"For what it's worth," she said, "the arithmetic was always wrong. Even three years ago. Even on the ridge." She tapped the doorframe twice — an old pilot's knock, *clear prop* — and was gone.

Voss sat alone with the orange segments arranged in a perfect circle, and ate them one at a time, and cried exactly as much as she allowed herself, which was four tears, counted, budgeted, spent.

Then she asked the matron for paper, and began writing her testimony, and did not stop until lights-out.

### Ilo

The courtroom was smaller than Ilo had imagined and colder, full of wood paneling and men in suits who kept saying *the witness will confine herself to the question*. He wasn't testifying yet — that was next week, after Voss — but Cyrus had brought him to watch, saying he should see the machinery before he was asked to feed it.

So he sat in the gallery between Cyrus and Ondine Marr and watched Mara Voss testify.

She was different in here. On the river she'd been sharp edges and deflection, always moving, always negotiating. In the witness box she was still as a post and answered everything — everything — including the questions designed to destroy her. Yes, she had sold the route. Yes, she had known Halvern's patrol schedule. Yes, she had accepted debt forgiveness as payment. No, she had not known the fugitives included children, and here her voice cracked on the word *children*, and Ilo watched the prosecutor lean forward like a fisherman feeling a bite, and watched Voss steady herself and continue.

"Why?" the prosecutor asked. "Why confess at all? You were beyond their reach. The debts were forgiven. You could have disappeared."

There was a long silence. Somewhere a fan ticked. Ilo found he was holding Cyrus's sleeve without remembering grabbing it.

"Because a boy memorized a ledger," Voss said, "and carried it through a monsoon to give it to strangers, and never once asked what was in it for him. I ran the numbers against that boy for three weeks and lost every time." She folded her hands. "Also, I promised a pilot I'd keep my word, and I have discovered, late, that promises are the only currency that doesn't devalue."

The judge — not *that* judge, a new one, imported — asked if counsel had further questions. Nobody did. The room had the specific silence of people who had come expecting a performance and gotten a confession instead.

Afterward, on the courthouse steps, Ilo said, "She meant it. About the boy."

"She meant it," Cyrus agreed.

"Was it me?"

"It was you."

He considered this. It sat strangely in him — being the reason someone did a hard right thing. He'd spent a year being the reason people did hard wrong things, or so Halvern's men had implied, burning the village records and pinning the fire on families who couldn't read the accusations anyway. Being the reason for something good was a weight too, but it fit differently. Closer to the shoulders. Easier to carry uphill.

"Cyrus. When this is over. The mail runs." He scuffed his shoe against the step. "Is there really a job? Or are you being kind?"

"Ilo Sandoval, I have never once in my life been kind before nine a.m. The job is real. Navigation, radio, and yelling at me when I fly into weather, which per your earlier commentary is apparently a documented habit."

"Documented by witnesses."

"Hostile witnesses."

"Testifying under oath."

And Cyrus laughed — a real one, surprised out of her, the kind Ilo had heard maybe four times and was beginning to collect like river stones — and the photographers on the steps caught the two of them mid-laugh, and the picture ran the next morning under a headline about the Escarpment Papers, which was wrong, but only about half wrong, and nobody minded.

### Teo

They went home the way the river wanted: slow, wide, unhurried, the boat riding light with no fugitives in her hold and no syndicate on the water. Renzo fished off the stern with his good arm and caught nothing and declared it a philosophical success. Teo steered.

At the bend above the old gravel bar — the one where the plane had come down — he cut the engine out of respect, or superstition, or whatever you called the feeling that some places deserved quiet. The propeller-bent Cessna was long gone, hauled out by salvage men, but the bar was still there, pale in the afternoon sun, holding its shape against the current the way stubborn things do.

"There's the spot," Renzo said.

"There's the spot."

"We nearly died there."

"We nearly died in eleven places. That one just had the best view."

Renzo laughed, then winced, then laughed anyway, and after a while Teo let himself laugh too, low, the way he did, like water finding a crack.

That night, camped on a sand spit with the fire down to coals, Renzo said, "You left the bail bucket in the boat."

"I know."

"You carved it for Ilo."

"I know, Renzo."

"He's staying with the pilot. Going to school in the coast town. Flying with her on mail runs." Renzo poked the coals. "You could visit. Boats go downstream faster than they come up, but they do come up."

Teo looked at the fire. Above them the sky was doing its enormous indifferent beautiful business, stars thick as spawn, and somewhere south of it a boy was learning instruments and a pilot was learning, slowly and against her nature, to count something other than miles.

"I'll visit," Teo said.

"You'll write letters complaining about her flying."

"I'll write letters complaining about her flying," Teo agreed, "and Ilo will read them aloud at dinner, and she'll deny everything, and it'll be the best entertainment that coast town's had since the arrests."

Renzo grinned and lay back with his hat over his face. Within minutes he was asleep, breathing easy, his bandaged arm rising and falling like something finally at anchor.

Teo stayed up a while longer, keeping the watch he'd kept his whole life, and found — this was the strange part, the part he'd turn over for years — that the watch felt lighter now. Not because the river was safer. Rivers are never safer. But because the people he'd have been watching out for alone were scattered along two hundred miles of water and coastline, awake in shifts, keeping their own watches, and somewhere upstream a lamp was probably burning in a whitewashed cell where a woman was writing the truth in longhand, and somewhere downstream a boy was asleep with a ledger's worth of numbers finally set down on paper where they belonged.

Shared watch. That was all it was.

It was everything.

### Cyrus

The verdicts came in autumn, in batches, like fruit.

Two site managers: guilty. The procurement chief: guilty, and weeping, which Cyrus listened to on the radio in her hangar with Ilo taking notes for no reason except that taking notes made him happy. The judge: resigned, indicted, and — this was the part the papers loved — arrested trying to board a flight booked under a false name, which Cyrus found so aesthetically satisfying she framed the clipping and hung it next to her airworthiness certificates, where it offended the inspector tremendously and improved her mood measurably.

The village claims were restored in November. Renzo sent a letter — dictated, because his handwriting was still crooked, though Ilo swore the crookedness was improving — describing the ceremony: the registrar arriving by boat, the new claim book with its ribbon and seal, the old men of the village touching the pages like they were checking whether justice was wet paint or dry. *It is dry,* Renzo wrote. *It will hold.*

Voss's trial ended in December. Eight years, reduced for cooperation to five, and when the sentence was read she turned to the gallery and found Cyrus's face in it and nodded once, the smallest possible transaction, bargain honored on both sides. Her first letter arrived in January, written in a hand far steadier than Cyrus remembered, opening with the sentence *You will be pleased to know the food here is terrible in ways I am learning to find funny,* and closing with *Tell the boy the spiral trick works on any citrus. Tell yourself nothing. You never listen anyway.*

And the mail runs went on.

That was the thing Cyrus kept noticing, the thing that snuck up on her between loads: the runs went on, but the counting had changed. She used to log miles the way misers logged coin — every leg tallied, every hour banked, the total the only honest measure of a day. Now the logbook filled up with other entries. *Ilo, nav, first solo radio call, nailed it.* *Teo visited, complained about my landing, was correct, hated admitting it.* *Package for Renzo: fishing hooks, aspirin, one (1) letter from M.V., read aloud at dinner per tradition, much laughing.*

One gray morning in February she flew the medicine run past the Escarpment — the same ridge, the same wind combing over the top like fingers through hair — and Ilo, in the right seat, tracked their position on a chart with a pencil stub and said, "You're not doing the thing."

"What thing?"

"The thing where you go quiet and grip the yoke and pretend you're fine."

"I'm always fine."

"You're doing it right now."

Cyrus looked at the ridge sliding beneath them, gray-green and indifferent, keeping its dead the way rivers keep theirs, and realized the boy was right. The old terror was there — it would always be there, scar tissue doesn't vanish, it just stops deciding where you fly. But it was riding in the back now. Cargo, not captain.

"No," she said. "I'm not doing the thing."

Ilo marked their position, satisfied, and below them the river went on toward the coast, carrying its boats and its fish and its restored claims and its stubborn, surviving people, and Cyrus Sandoval — who had once measured her life in miles and found the sum insufficient — banked gently around the ridge's shoulder and headed for home, where the mail was waiting, and the crew, and the long uncounted rest of it.
