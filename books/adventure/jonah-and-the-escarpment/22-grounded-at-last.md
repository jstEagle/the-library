## 22. Grounded, At Last

The letter came on a Tuesday, which was how Cyrus Sandoval knew it would be trouble. Voss had always been a Tuesday sort of person — the kind who arrived precisely when you'd settled into something comfortable and rearranged the furniture of your life without asking.

Cyrus read it standing in the shade of the hangar at Meridian Strip, one boot propped against the tire of the Cessna she no longer owned and had somehow stopped mourning. The paper smelled faintly of prison soap and government ink.

*Dear Cyrus,* it began, in handwriting that was trying very hard to be neat and mostly succeeding. *The trial moved to the capital. I testified for six hours today. My lawyer says I did well, which I have decided to take as a compliment even though it probably wasn't. The judge they bought — Ostrander — has been suspended pending investigation. Halvern's coastal operations are frozen. Renzo's village got its claim records reissued last month; Teo sent me a photograph of the ceremony. In it, everyone is crying, including a man I once watched threaten to shoot a child for touching a radio. People contain multitudes. So do I, apparently, most of them regrettable.*

*I have taken up gardening. The soil here is terrible and so am I at this, but the tomatoes persist out of spite, which I respect.*

*You don't have to write back. But if you do, tell me about the flying. Not the miles. You know what I mean.*

— M.V.

Cyrus folded the letter along its creases and put it in the tin box under the chart table, where three others already lived. Then she stood there a moment with her hand on the lid, listening to the wind come up the valley the way it always did before noon, smelling of cut grass and river and the particular green rot of the lowland forests far to the south.

"You're smiling," said Ilo from the doorway of the office, with the flat accusatory tone only a fourteen-year-old can manage. "That's suspicious."

"I'm reading my mail."

"That's what makes it suspicious." He came out into the light, squinting, a clipboard under his arm that he had appointed himself keeper of back in the spring and defended ever since like a small territorial animal. "Teo says the mail run leaves at ten. It is now ten-oh-six."

"Teo says a lot of things."

"Teo is *right* a lot of things." Ilo consulted his clipboard with tremendous gravity. "We have forty-one pounds of mail, two crates of penicillin for San Rafael, a sewing machine for somebody named Beatriz who apparently will kill us if it arrives late, and — " he paused, savoring it — "a live chicken."

"A live chicken."

"For the mission at Kettle Bend. It's a rooster. There was an incident with the old rooster and a dog, and now the mission needs a new one before Easter or Father Anselmo will have nothing to crow about at dawn." Ilo looked up. "I put it in the cargo pod. It has opinions."

Everything had opinions. That was the thing about a life measured in people instead of distance: people were heavier than freight, louder than engines, and considerably less predictable than weather. Cyrus had spent thirty years trusting aluminum and air because they never surprised her. Now her whole operation ran on a fisherman who'd never touched a yoke until eight months ago, a boy navigator who kept the logbooks better than any dispatcher she'd ever hired, and a rooster.

She wouldn't have traded it for anything. She'd checked. She'd done the arithmetic one night over coffee, honestly, like a pilot doing weight and balance, and every column came out the same way.

"All right," she said. "Load the chicken."

* * *

Teo flew the first leg.

He flew it the way he did everything — hands easy on the controls, eyes moving constantly, reading the country below the way he used to read water: not as terrain but as a text, full of hidden channels and things about to change. He still sat slightly too far forward in the seat, the habit of a man accustomed to boats where your weight mattered, and he still talked to the airplane under his breath in a low murmur that Cyrus pretended not to hear but privately found charming. Today he was telling it about the river.

"You remember her," he murmured as they crossed the broad brown ribbon of the main channel, far downstream of the slough where he'd grounded them under the gunboat's searchlights. "Yes, you do. Everybody remembers her eventually."

"He's narrating again," Ilo reported from the right seat, not looking up from his chart.

"I can hear him."

"He does it when he's happy."

"I know," Cyrus said. "That's how I can tell."

It had been Teo's idea, the flying. Eight months ago, when the trial was grinding through its early motions and the delta camps were still buzzing with the story of the airstrip fire — *the woman who burned the ledger*, they called her, which Cyrus found embarrassing and Ilo found extremely cool — Teo had shown up at Meridian Strip with his duffel bag and his brother, whose arm had healed crooked but worked fine, and had said, in the manner of a man announcing the weather:

"You need a second pilot. I need to learn. Renzo wants to run the boat trade full-time anyway. This solves everything."

"It takes years to learn to fly."

"I have time," Teo said. "I used to think I didn't. Then I spent a season being hunted down a river by men with guns, and now I have all the time in the world."

There was no arguing with that kind of logic. Cyrus had tried, briefly, and lost, and then spent the next months discovering that teaching was its own strange pleasure — that watching someone else's hands learn the yoke made her feel the controls differently too, made the whole craft new again, the way it had been when she was nineteen and everything was possible and nothing had gone wrong yet.

He soloed in June. She had stood at the edge of the strip pretending to check the windsock while he took the 180 around the pattern alone, and when he shut down and climbed out he was grinning so hard his face looked like it might come apart, and she had said, "Your flare was late," because she was who she was, and he had said, "Your student is magnificent," because he was who he was, and that had been that.

Now he flew the mail runs some days, and she rode right seat with her hands folded and let the country go by, and discovered that the view was better from over there. Who knew.

"San Rafael ahead," Ilo announced. "Wind looks calm. Beatriz's sewing machine is priority one, then medicine, then the rooster stays crated until we're parked or he'll stage a coup."

"The boy runs a tight ship," Teo observed.

"The boy is going to run *my* ship someday if he keeps it up," Cyrus said, and saw Ilo's ears go pink in the mirror, and pretended she hadn't seen that either.

* * *

San Rafael took an hour, the way villages do. The penicillin went to the clinic, where Sister Amara counted the vials twice and made Cyrus drink something bitter and herbal "for the cough," though Cyrus hadn't mentioned a cough. Word traveled. Everything traveled, out here — gossip moved faster than any patrol ever had, faster than newspapers, faster sometimes than the planes themselves. The whole interior was one long conversation and Cyrus had finally understood, somewhere over the past year, that she'd spent her life as one of its couriers without ever joining in.

Beatriz received her sewing machine like a queen receiving tribute. The rooster was handed over to a solemn altar boy who introduced himself as Mateo and informed the bird, seriously, "You have big shoes to fill."

And then there was the inevitable hour on the veranda of the mission with sweet tea and plantains, because you could not land anywhere in this country without being fed, and refusing was a crime worse than smuggling.

It was Father Anselmo, easing into the chair beside her with the groan of a man whose knees had opinions of their own, who brought up the Escarpment.

"They say the syndicate's survey crews are going back up there," he said quietly. "Old claims. The government's re-examining everything Halvern touched, and where there's paperwork, there's money, and where there's money —"

"There's survey crews," Cyrus finished. She turned her cup slowly on its saucer. "I heard."

"You could fly over it now, you know," the old priest said, watching her face with the gentle directness of a man who had heard a great many confessions and learned what courage actually looked like. "The weather station at the pass is open again. The trail's marked. Nobody would think anything of it."

Nobody would think anything of it. That was the remarkable part, Cyrus thought. Three years ago the Escarpment had been the edge of her map, the place where her rule lived — *never cross the wall* — carved so deep into her that she'd built a whole career around respecting it, routing around it, treating that ridge of broken stone like a headstone. And now a priest was mentioning it over tea the way you'd mention a road reopening.

Because the world had moved on. Because the crash had happened, and the grief had happened, and the return had happened — the crossing itself, storm cells and bent propeller and all — and she had come out the other side of it carrying six strangers and a grudge and, eventually, a purpose, and somewhere in all of that the wall had stopped being a wall. It was just country now. High, hard, beautiful country.

"I'm thinking about it," she said, which was true, and had been true for weeks.

Anselmo nodded and said nothing more, because he was wise in the specific way of men who knew that some flights you had to make yourself.

* * *

They crossed the Escarpment four days later.

Not on a mail run — there was no delivery waiting on the other side, no contract, no triple pay, no name of a dead copilot whispered like a hook. Teo flew, because Cyrus asked him to, and Ilo navigated from the right seat with the chart spread across his knees, and Cyrus sat behind them both with her forehead nearly against the window and watched the wall come up out of the haze.

It looked smaller than she remembered. That was the first thing. All these years it had loomed in her mind like a fortress, a rampart of cloud and rock where her crew had died and her luck had run out, and here it was: just stone, just forest climbing into cloud, gorgeous and indifferent and entirely survivable. They'd cleared it once in a dying plane in a storm. Today they crossed it in a healthy aircraft on a blue morning, and the updrafts lifted them gently, almost courteously, like the mountain itself had decided to let bygones be.

"There," Ilo said quietly, pointing. "Isn't that — "

The gravel bar. Their gravel bar. The river had shifted since the monsoon, as rivers do, and the bar was longer now, fringed with new green, but she could see the shape of it, the exact place where the propeller had kissed stone and changed all their lives. Somewhere downstream of it, Teo had found them in his leaking boat. Somewhere beyond that ridge, Voss had walked into the trees with the last fuel can and the whole rotten truth in her pocket.

"Circle it?" Teo asked.

"No," Cyrus said, and then, surprising herself: "Yes. One circle. Slow."

So they circled, once, wings tilted, the wreck-site-turned-campsite turning beneath them, and Cyrus found that what rose in her chest wasn't the old cold dread. It was something more like gratitude — fierce, complicated, aimed at nobody and everybody. At Renny and Okonkwo, her dead, who were dead and stayed dead and whom she had finally, sometime during the trial, forgiven herself for surviving. At the frightened people in the crates who had become a crew. At the leaking boat and the stubborn fisherman and the boy with the fever and the ledger in his head. Even, God help her, at Mara Voss, writing letters from prison about spiteful tomatoes.

"You okay back there?" Teo asked, glancing over his shoulder.

Cyrus realized she was smiling. Suspicious.

"Log it," she said. "Escarpment crossing. Uneventful."

Ilo wrote it down. His pen hovered, then added something extra, and when Cyrus leaned forward to see, he showed her: *First crossing she enjoyed.*

"That's editorializing," she said.

"That's accurate," said Ilo, and closed the logbook.

* * *

They camped that night at the overlook above the pass — there was a flat meadow there, and a cold spring, and a view south across the whole drowned-green immensity of the interior with its rivers threading silver through it like veins. Teo built a fire because Teo could not exist near wood without building a fire. Ilo spread the charts out and planned tomorrow's route with the seriousness of a general, then abandoned it halfway through to skip stones in the spring, because he was fourteen and generals are overrated.

Cyrus sat on a boulder with her knees up and her coffee cooling and did the thing she had done her whole life, automatically, the way other people breathed: she counted.

Meridian to San Rafael, ninety-two miles. San Rafael to the pass, sixty. The river below them, two hundred miles of it to the delta town where an elder had once hidden them from Halvern's agents and fed them fish stew and told her, *the police are bought, mija, so the justice must come from elsewhere* — and it had, hadn't it, in the end, from a burned hangar and a newspaper office and a rival's testimony given freely in a courtroom full of strangers. Miles stacked on miles, the whole map of her life laid out in distances, and she lay the numbers end to end the way she always did, and waited for the old restlessness to come with them.

It didn't come.

She frowned at that, genuinely puzzled, and turned the feeling over like an unfamiliar part, checking it for cracks. Thirty years she had measured herself against the horizon — miles flown, miles remaining, the next strip, the next contract, the next escape from whatever was behind her. Distance had been her currency, her armor, her excuse. A life in transit needed no accounting; you couldn't audit a woman who was never in one place long enough.

But sitting here, with Teo humming off-key across the fire and Ilo's laughter skimming off the water and a tin box in a hangar full of letters from a woman planting spiteful tomatoes, Cyrus Sandoval counted her miles and found they added up to nothing at all. Not zero — nothing. The unit simply didn't apply anymore. You didn't measure a family in miles. You measured it in mornings, in letters answered, in a boy's fever breaking at dawn, in a student's first solo, in the number of people who knew where you slept and were glad of it.

Four people, currently. Plus a rooster, if you counted him, and Ilo insisted you did.

"Cyrus." Teo, from the fire, patting the ground beside him. "You're brooding. Come brood where it's warm."

"I'm not brooding. I'm doing arithmetic."

"What's the total?"

She got up, carried her coffee over, and sat down in the warm space between them — between the man who'd pulled her out of the river's logic and the boy who'd carried a nation's evidence in his memory and neither of whom she'd known existed three years ago. Below them the interior went dark by degrees, lights kindling in the distant camps, each one a kitchen, a sickbed, a mailbox waiting for tomorrow's run.

"Zero," she said. "Total comes to zero."

Teo considered this with the gravity he gave all her pronouncements, then nodded slowly. "Good year for zero."

"Best one yet."

Ilo flopped down on her other side, smelling of spring water and woodsmoke, and pulled the flight bag into his lap, and from it — because the boy never let anything surprise him unprepared — produced a letter, already opened, edges soft from rereading.

"She sent you another one," he said. "It came to the mission while we were at San Rafael. Father Anselmo gave it to me. I only peeked a little."

"You peeked a lot."

"I peeked a *medium* amount." He handed it over, and by the firelight Cyrus saw the familiar cramped handwriting, and the postmark from the capital, and beneath the address, in the corner where Voss always doodled some small defiant emblem, a drawing that made her breath catch and then laugh out loud.

A tomato plant. Bearing fruit. Growing, absurdly, out of the outline of an airplane.

"Well?" Teo asked. "What does it say?"

Cyrus unfolded the page. The first line was visible even in the dim light, written larger than usual, the way Voss wrote things when she wanted them impossible to take back:

*Cyrus — they've approved my parole hearing for the spring. If it goes well, I'll need a ride home. I know exactly who to call.*

Cyrus read it twice. Then she folded it carefully, tucked it into her shirt pocket over her heart, where pilots keep the things they're not ready to say out loud, and looked up at her crew — her impossible, unplanned, unmeasurable crew — and found them both watching her with identical expressions of patient, knowing amusement.

"We're going to need a bigger plane," she said.

Ilo was already reaching for his clipboard.
