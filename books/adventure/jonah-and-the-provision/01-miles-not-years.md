## 1. Miles, Not Years

Wendell Galloway did not measure her life in years. Years were a currency she'd stopped carrying somewhere around the age of twenty-six, when she'd stood in a hospital corridor in Nairobi holding a phone that wouldn't stop ringing and understood, with the flat clarity of heatstroke, that her father was not going to come. He was in the Danakil, they said. A discovery. Something in the ash layers. He would send his love.

He had sent his love the way a man sends a postcard from a country he never intends to visit again.

So she counted miles instead. Miles were honest. Miles accumulated under her boots like sediment, layer upon layer of elsewhere, and unlike years they never asked her to account for what she'd done with them. She could point to any stretch of road between here and the Red Sea and tell you what it had cost her to cross it, what it had given back, which of her crew had joined or left along its length. The map of her wandering hung inside her skull more vividly than any atlas: the ferry crossing at Manda, the washout on the Isiolo–Moyale road where they'd lost a tire and nearly a axle, the long grey trudge through the Chalbi where Tam had wept from the beauty of it and pretended it was dust in his eyes.

Three years since she'd struck out on her own. Or rather — three years since she'd stopped being Elias Galloway's daughter in public and become simply Wendell, the site boss who read ground like other people read weather.

* * *

The Turkana site was nothing. That was precisely why she'd taken it.

A roadside salvage dig, really — the government was cutting a new stretch of tarmac toward Lodwar, and someone in a ministry office had flagged a scatter of lithics eroding out of a low terrace above the Kerio. Contract work. Two weeks, maybe three, paid at rates that made Odile snort into her tea. But it was far from anywhere that knew the name Galloway, and the ground was good ground, and for two weeks nobody would ask her anything harder than *pass the trowel*.

She knelt at the edge of Trench Three in the hour before dawn, when the light came up blue and forgiving, and let her fingers do the talking.

The instruments said one thing. The magnetometer, hauled out at obscene expense, hummed its vague song about anomalies in the eastern quadrant; the survey team had pegged their grids off its readings and found, over four days, mostly nothing. But Wendell had learned young — kneeling beside her father in red Nubian sand before she was old enough to be angry at him — that ground speaks first to the hands. The soil here had a particular give, a looseness beneath the caliche crust, as though something had been turned and returned by human intention rather than flood. She pressed her palm flat against the cool earth and read it the way Ravi read a pulse.

*Here,* the ground said. *Not there. Here.*

She moved the grid herself, chalk lines crossed against the magnetometer's counsel, and by noon Tam's brush had uncovered a burial — flexed, intact, ornamented with ostrich-eggshell beads strung in a pattern no one in the region had documented. By evening the ministry man on-site was calling Nairobi, and Odile was looking at Wendell with an expression caught between pride and unease.

"You moved the trench," Odile said. It wasn't an accusation. Odile didn't make accusations; she made observations the way she laid out surgical tools, precisely and without judgment, and somehow both were worse.

"The ground told me."

"Mm." Odile crouched, examined the beadwork without touching it. "And the ground tells you this how?"

Wendell wiped her hands on her thighs. "You've seen a dog circle before it lies down. Same thing. People are animals. We circle the places we mean to come back to." She nodded at the skeleton's patient curve. "She came back here. Somebody buried her where somebody had lived."

Odile studied her a moment longer than was comfortable. "Your father used to say something like that. In his lectures. I attended one, once. Nairobi, years ago." She said it lightly, the way you toss a stone into a well to hear how deep it goes.

"Did he now." Wendell stood, brushed sand from her knees, and walked to the far end of the trench where there was work to do and no conversation waiting.

* * *

That night the camp sat around the fire while the Turkana wind came down off the escarpment and combed through the acacias with its dry fingers. Ravi was stitching a gash on Tam's forearm — a slip with the mattock, nothing deep, but Tam had gone white as paper and Ravi had talked him through it in his low, unhurried voice, the voice he used for wounds and confessions alike. Odile cleaned her boots. Wendell drank tea gone bitter with over-steeping and watched the coals rearrange themselves like continents drifting.

Three years. She did the arithmetic anyway, because she couldn't help it. Three years, and if you laid all her roads end to end — the convoy routes, the border crossings, the detours around flooded rivers and closed frontiers — she calculated she had traveled something in the range of forty thousand kilometers. Enough to circle the planet once. Enough, if distance were redemption, to have been redeemed several times over.

It wasn't. She knew that. Distance was just time wearing boots.

"Wendell." Ravi finished the last stitch, tied it off, and looked up at her across the fire. There was a quality to his attention that she both relied on and dreaded; he was the keeper of everyone's small secrets — Tam's fear of enclosed spaces, Odile's letters from a sister she never wrote back to, Wendell's own supply of the migraine pills she took for headaches that were not entirely headaches. "You went quiet when Odile mentioned the lecture."

"I go quiet lots of places."

"Not like that."

Tam, bandaged and pale, glanced between them with the anxious alertness of a boy who had grown up reading rooms for danger. Odile kept polishing her boot, but her brush strokes had slowed.

"There was a radio interview," Wendell said finally, because lying to Ravi was like lying to a mirror. "Years ago. My father gave one, after—" She stopped. After the inquiry. After the fraud hearings. After the profession he loved had taken his name and run it through the mud until *Galloway* became shorthand for beautiful lies, for the forgery that had made him famous and then infamous, depending on which journal you read. "After some things. Someone asked him about his family. He said archaeology was his only child."

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the dark a hyena laughed its idiot laugh.

"He said that?" Tam asked softly.

"On record." She smiled, and it was the smile she'd practiced in a hundred truck mirrors, the one that meant *this doesn't touch me*. "Which is efficient of him. One child, no custody disputes."

Nobody laughed. That was the trouble with a crew like hers — she'd assembled them out of people who paid attention, the unattached, the ones with their own reasons for being nowhere in particular, and attentive people saw through practiced smiles the way her fingers saw through soil.

"You don't talk about him," Odile observed. "In three years. Not once."

"There's nothing to say. He chose a hole in the ground over my mother's deathbed. The hole turned out to be full of lies. End of story. Very tidy. Could be a monograph."

"And yet," Ravi said, packing away his needle case with gentle clicks, "you keep his field methods. You move trenches the way he moved them. You read ground with your hands because he taught you to. You carry him into every site we dig, Wendell. You just refuse to set him down."

The wind moved through the camp, lifting grit in little ghosts. Wendell looked at the coals and felt the old familiar thing turn over in her chest — not grief exactly, grief was too clean a word — something more like a splinter worked so deep into the grain that the tree had grown around it, and pulling it out now would hurt more than leaving it, except that leaving it meant it stayed forever, and forever was a very long time to walk with iron in your heart.

"I'm going to check the trench covers," she said, and stood, and left them to the fire and their kindness, which she had not asked for and could not quite refuse.

* * *

The night was enormous. Out here the sky didn't arch so much as descend, pressing close, thick with stars to the horizon line, and the land lay under it dark and breathing. She walked the perimeter past the tents, past the stacked finds trays, to the edge of the terrace where the ground fell away toward the dry riverbed, and she stood there letting the wind take her hair out of its braid.

Below, moonlight silvered the ancient channels of the Kerio's floods — braided scars, each one a decision the water had made and abandoned. Her father would have loved this view. That was the cruelty of it: everything she loved about this work, she had learned from him, and everything she hated about herself circled back to the same man. She had spent three years trying to excavate him out of her own foundations and he was load-bearing. Every careful hand, every instinct for where the ground hid its dead — his. His patience in her fingers. His wonder, even. She carried him the way the terrace carried the burial: unknowing, structural, impossible to remove without collapse.

Her mother had understood that, near the end. *You're more his than mine, my darling,* she'd said from the hospital bed, laughing her thin laugh, *and God forgive me, so am I.* Then she'd asked Wendell to call him anyway, one more time, and Wendell had called, and the satellite phone in the Danakil had rung and rung in a camp where everyone later swore he'd been three days' walk from signal, chasing his ash layers, chasing the find that would turn out to be the ruin of him.

Chasing, Wendell thought now, watching the moonlight crawl. Always chasing. Never arriving.

She heard footsteps behind her and didn't turn. Odile's tread was unmistakable — heavy, deliberate, the walk of a woman who had carried things across distances most people couldn't imagine.

"Trench covers are fine," Odile said, stopping beside her. "I checked."

"I was going to check."

"I know." They stood together in the wind. Below them the riverbed gleamed like a vein of hammered tin. "Ravi talks too much."

"He talks exactly enough. That's the problem."

Odile grunted, which from her was agreement. Then, after a silence long enough for three stars to fall: "There's mail coming up from Lodwar tomorrow. Supply run. The driver says there's a letter for you. Official-looking. Thick envelope."

Something shifted in Wendell's chest, small and cold, like a stone turning over in a streambed. Official-looking. Thick. Nobody sent her official correspondence; her contracts came by email and her invoices went unanswered for months. The last thick envelope she'd received had been the solicitor's letter settling her mother's estate, and she had burned it unread in a brazier in Hargeisa, a piece of theater she still wasn't sure whether to regret.

"Probably the ministry," she said. "Paperwork on the burial."

"Maybe." Odile turned to go, then paused. "For what it's worth — the way you moved that trench today? Whatever name taught you that, it's yours now. You earned it. It doesn't belong to him anymore."

Wendell watched her go, a broad silhouette swallowed gradually by the dark of the camp, and stood alone at the edge of the terrace a while longer, counting not miles, not years, but the hours until the supply truck came — and telling herself, in the voice she used for stubborn juniors and frightened self, that whatever was in the envelope, it would change nothing. She had built a life out of elsewhere. She was very good at elsewhere. Elsewhere was the only home she'd ever kept.

But sleep came hard that night, and when it came, she dreamed of red sand and a man's voice saying *look with your hands, Wendell, look with your hands*, and woke before dawn with the taste of dust in her mouth and the strange, dread-heavy certainty that the miles were about to stop carrying her away from something — and start carrying her back.
