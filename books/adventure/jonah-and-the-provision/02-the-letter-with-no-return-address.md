## 2. The Letter with No Return Address

The letter arrived on a Tuesday, which meant nothing to anyone but me.

Tuesdays were mail days at the Turkana camp — the one day a week the supply truck ground its way up from Lodwar with diesel, flour, paracetamol, and whatever the world had decided we deserved. I had built my life out of such small calendars. Mail day. Fuel day. The day Odile let Tam run the theodolite because his hands were steadier after dark. If you measured time in events instead of dates, you could go years without noticing you'd gone anywhere at all. That was not an accident. That was architecture.

I was in the survey tent when Ravi brought it in, and I knew before he said a word that something was wrong, because Ravi never handed anyone anything without narrating it. He was our medic and our confessor and the only man I'd ever met who could take a bullet out of a shoulder while asking about your mother. He talked constantly. It was how he kept other people's secrets — buried under the sediment of his own chatter, like artifacts under flood silt.

So when he came through the flap holding a single envelope flat on his palm, silent as a man carrying a scorpion, I put down my calipers.

"That came at the bottom of the mail sack," he said finally. "Wrapped around the diesel invoices."

"Then it's probably an invoice."

"It's not an invoice."

"How do you know?"

"Because," he said, "it has no return address, and nobody writes to people they're not afraid of."

He set it on my table and left, which was unlike him, and which told me more than anything he might have said. The tent was dim and smelled of graphite dust and the paraffin stove Odile refused to let us use for tea. Outside, the wind was doing what the wind always did here — worrying the guy-lines, sifting fine red grit through every seam we'd ever sewn, reminding us that this landscape had been erasing things for four million years and considered us a brief experiment.

The envelope was heavy stock, cream-colored, absurdly clean against everything else in my life. My name was typed: **W. GALLOWAY**. Not Wendell. Not Ms. Not Dr., though I technically had the right to it and had spent a decade declining the right. Just the initial and the surname, the way you'd address someone whose full identity you already possessed and didn't need confirmed. Someone who wanted me to know they knew exactly who I was.

No return address. A postmark from Nairobi. And in the lower corner, where a sender's name would go, a small embossed device I recognized the way you recognize a scar in a mirror — not because you look at it often, but because once, long ago, you looked at nothing else.

A stylized *V* inside a broken circle.

Marguerite Voss.

I sat with it for a long time. Long enough that the light through the canvas shifted from amber to grey. Long enough that Tam's voice drifted over from the trench line, arguing cheerfully with Odile about whether a scatter of potsherds constituted a feature or just the universe being messy. The ordinary sounds of a working dig, a season that was dying quietly beneath us, and all of us pretending not to hear it rattle.

Because here is what nobody tells you about running an independent crew: the archaeology is the easy part. The ground gives up its dead eventually; it has no choice. What it doesn't give up is funding. We were three months into a six-month permit with eleven weeks of diesel, a generator that ate fuel like a wound eats blood, and a payroll I had been covering out of the last of my father's money — the irony of that so exquisite I had never once said it aloud. Elias Galloway's settlement, doled out to keep his daughter in the field he'd been banished from. Every liter of petrol we burned was, in some accounting sense, him.

Odile knew. Odile knew everything; she was the foreman, and foremanship is mostly arithmetic performed in public while smiling. She hadn't said a word either. There was a whole language of silence between us, fluent on both sides.

I opened the letter with a fingernail, because I didn't trust my hands not to shake if I used them properly.

*Dr. Galloway* — there it was, the title deployed like a hook baited with something I wanted — *I will be brief, as I understand brevity is valued in the field.*

*My foundation is commissioning a full salvage excavation of the Wadi al-Nakhl complex, beginning this coming season. I am given to understand your crew is available. I am further given to understand that your methods are unorthodox, your overheads are low, and your discretion is total. All three qualities interest me.*

*The terms are enclosed. I will note only two things here. First: the fee is not negotiable upward, and does not need to be. You will find it covers your season, your debts, and your ambitions. Second: Clause Seventeen. Read it carefully. I have found that people who skip clauses are people who intend to break them.*

*I look forward to your signature.*

*M.V.*

There was a second sheet. A contract, dense and typeset, and I read it the way I read stratigraphy — top down, oldest lie first — and the numbers swam up off the page like fish rising to a lure. Enough to clear the season. Enough to clear the season after it. Enough to buy the Land Cruiser Odile had been coveting for two years and to pay Tam enough that he could stop sending half his wages home in envelopes with prayers written on the outside. Enough, in short, to make the word *yes* feel less like a decision than a rescue.

And then I read the site designation, though I had already read it. I had read it in the first sentence, in fact, and had spent the intervening minutes pretending I hadn't, the way you can walk past a grave you know is there by keeping your eyes on the middle distance.

*Wadi al-Nakhl.*

The valley of the palms. Though there hadn't been palms in living memory — the wadi was named for what it had been, three thousand years ago, when it held water and caravans and a temple complex that my father had stood in front of a room full of his colleagues and sworn was the discovery of the century.

Twenty-two years ago, that valley had eaten him alive.

* *

You grow up inside a scandal the way a tree grows around a fence wire. You don't decide to incorporate it. You just find, year after year, that you cannot grow in certain directions anymore.

I was nine when the committee published its findings. I remember the house going quiet first — that specific archaeological silence, the kind that means something has been deliberately backfilled — and then I remember the phone calls, and my mother's face, and my father standing in the garden at night smoking in the rain because he'd stopped caring whether the cigarettes got wet. Accused of fabrication. Of salting the site. Of forging inscriptions to resurrect a career that was, everyone agreed, already sufficiently glorious and needed no help. The evidence had been circumstantial. The damage had not been.

My mother died four years later, slowly, in a hospital two hundred miles from the nearest dig, and my father missed the last three weeks of it because he was in the field. Because there was a trench open. Because he said — and I heard him say it, I was thirteen and standing in the doorway and I will hear it until I die — *"This is the most important thing I will ever find."*

He chose it. That was the verdict I handed down at thirteen, from a doorway, in a house that smelled of antiseptic and lilies, and I have never once convened a review of the ruling. Some finds you don't re-excavate. You leave them sealed and you build your road around them and you tell yourself the detour is scenic.

After her funeral he went back to the wadi one last time — to close it out, to surrender the permit, to watch the authorities strip his name from the site records. Then he swore off it. Publicly, at a symposium, with the particular gray finality of a man renouncing a religion. *That valley is finished,* he said. *Whatever is in it, let it stay in it.* And the Galloway family — which by then consisted of him and me and a silence the size of a continent — swore off it too. Unspoken, but binding. The way family law usually is.

I became very good at digging everywhere else. Three continents' worth of everywhere else. I learned to read ground the way other people read faces — a discoloration in the soil, a shift in the grain of the windblown sand, a place where the grass grew wrong, and underneath: walls, or bones, or the cold ash of somebody's fire four thousand years cold. Instruments were useful for confirming what I already knew. I never needed them for knowing. This gift, if it was a gift, was the one thing he gave me that I kept, and I hated that I kept it, and I used it every single day.

And now Marguerite Voss — his old rival, the woman who had testified before the committee with that soft, regretful voice, *I take no pleasure in this, but the data speak for themselves* — was offering me a fortune to walk into the one valley on earth my family had promised, in whatever unspoken dialect families promise in, never to enter again.

Dread is a strange thing to carry in a body. People expect it to feel like fear, sharp and fast. Mostly it feels like weather. A pressure drop. A heaviness behind the sternum, a taste of iron at the back of the throat, the sense that something enormous is standing just behind you, patient, waiting for you to turn around. I had felt it twice before in my life: the day the hospital called, and the day I left home at nineteen with one duffel bag and a forged letter of reference. Now it settled over me again, familiar as malaria, and I did what I always did with dread.

I dressed it up as opportunity and took it outside to show people.

* *

The crew gathered in the mess tent after dinner, which was how we made all decisions, because Odile maintained — correctly — that no one should sign anything on an empty stomach, including their own ruin.

They were, the five of them plus me, the finest excavation team I had ever assembled and the strangest collection of people ever to share a cookfire. Odile Kanté, my foreman, Senegalese, sixty-one, built like the acacia she resembled — all leverage and patience, roots going down farther than anyone suspected. She had dug sites on three continents and buried one husband and carried both losses with the same unsentimental competence. Tam Wanjiru, twenty-four, our surveyor, who kept a chipped wooden amulet in his instrument case and apologized to the ground before cutting into it, and whom the rest of us teased mercilessly and protected absolutely. Ravi, of course. And the Okonkwo brothers, Femi and Chidi, who ran the heavy work and the cooking respectively and argued about both with the fluency of thirty years of sibling grievance.

I laid the contract on the table between the teapot and the lamp.

"Salvage job," I said. "Full season, funded. Big site. Big fee."

Femi read the number upside down and whistled low, the way you whistle at a horizon.

"Wadi al-Nakhl," Chidi read aloud, and the name dropped into the tent like a stone into a well.

Nobody spoke for a moment. They all knew the story — everyone in the profession knew the story; it was taught, in a sanitized footnote, in first-year surveys — but they also knew, in the way crews know things, that it was mine in some particular way. I had never told them. I had never told anyone. But Odile's eyes came up from the contract to my face and stayed there, steady and unreadable, and I understood that she had known for years and had simply been waiting for me to arrive at the place where knowing out loud became necessary.

"The Galloway site," Tam said softly, and then went pink, because he was the only one of us who still said things before deciding whether to.

"The Galloway site," I agreed, keeping my voice level, keeping my hands flat on the table so no one could see what they wanted to do. "Which is why I'm bringing it to a vote. If anyone wants out, this is the moment, and there'll be no hard feelings and a paid ride to Lodwar."

"And why would anyone want out?" Femi asked, genuinely puzzled. "It's money. It's work. It's a famous hole in the ground." He shrugged. "Holes don't hold grudges."

"Holes remember," Tam said, almost inaudibly, touching the amulet through his shirt.

Chidi cuffed him affectionately. "The hole remembers. The hole pays better than the hole we're currently sitting in, which remembers nothing except that we owe the cook fuel money." He looked at me. "I vote yes."

"I vote yes," Femi said. "Obviously."

Ravi said, "I vote yes, provided someone tells me why the boss has gone the color of the dust." Which was Ravi's way of voting yes and filing a complaint simultaneously.

Tam hesitated, glancing at the lamp, at the dark beyond the canvas, at the amulet. "Yes," he said at last. "But we should pour something on the ground when we get there. Before the first cut. It's only polite."

"We'll pour something," Odile said, and it wasn't agreement, it was permission, and it was the first time in the entire conversation she had looked away from me.

She voted last, of course. She turned the contract over, read the back of the final page even though the backs of contracts never contain anything, and then she signed the crew's assent line in her small, ruthless handwriting and slid it across to me.

"Well, patronne?" she said. "Do we have a job?"

I picked up the pen. My hand was steady. I want that recorded somewhere, by someone: my hand did not shake. Whatever else the valley took from me, it would not get the satisfaction of watching my hand shake.

I signed.

* *

Later — much later, past midnight, when the generator was off and the stars over Turkana were doing their ancient indifferent business — I sat outside my tent with the contract copy in my lap and read Clause Seventeen by moonlight for the fourth time.

*The Contractor shall neither contact, correspond with, nor accept communication from any member of the Galloway family for the duration of the engagement, nor disclose the identity of any Galloway family member associated with the site's history to any third party. Breach renders this agreement void and invokes Schedule C penalties.*

Any member of the Galloway family. As if we were a species. As if we were a stratum to be mapped and avoided.

I thought about what it meant, that Voss had written such a clause. It meant she expected a Galloway to matter. It meant she feared one. Or — colder thought, arriving on schedule, the way the worst thoughts always do — it meant she wanted the valley's history sealed shut, owned outright, published exclusively under her own name, with the Galloways locked outside the story forever. My father's disgrace had made her career once. Perhaps she intended to feed on the corpse a second time.

Or perhaps — and this thought I folded up small and put away, because it was too large to look at directly — perhaps there was something still in that valley. Something my father had found and fought for and lost everything over. Something true.

I looked north, past the black shoulder of the ridge, toward a border I couldn't see and a desert I hadn't entered in twenty-two years. The wind came down off it smelling of dust and old stone and, impossibly, faintly, of water.

Three hundred and forty kilometers, I thought. I had already measured it. I always measured. Miles, not years; distance instead of grief.

In the morning I would tell Odile to start packing the trucks.

Tonight I sat alone with the letter that had no return address, and understood, finally, what Ravi had known the moment he put it in my hands:

Nobody writes to people they're not afraid of.

Marguerite Voss was afraid of something in that valley. And she was paying me a fortune to be the one who went in first.
