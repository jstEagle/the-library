## 3. Crew of the Unattached

The Turkana camp broke apart the way all camps did: not with ceremony but with a slow unbraiding. Tents came down in the grey hour before the heat arrived, canvas folded still damp with dew, and by midmorning the site was nothing again — just earth, just wind, just the shallow scars of trenches already softening at their edges as if the ground were healing itself over the intrusion of being known.

Wendell stood where the main trench had been and watched Odile's crew of two local laborers sweep the last of the backfill flat. It was a small excavation, six weeks of careful work that had yielded potsherds, a hearth, three obsidian flakes, and one very opinionated hornbill who had adopted the mess tent and refused all offers of relocation. Enough to justify the season on paper. Not enough to keep anyone fed through the next one.

That was the arithmetic she carried everywhere now, the way other people carried keys or worry stones: *eleven weeks of runway*. She had counted it in the truck on the drive down from Lodwar, counted it again while signing the final site report, counted it a third time this morning while the hornbill screamed at her from the tent pole for no reason she could determine. Eleven weeks until the crew's wages ran dry, and after that — what? She had watched crews dissolve before. It didn't happen dramatically. It happened in emails. A surveyor writing to say he'd taken a position with a mining concern. A medic whose sister needed help with the children. People didn't leave; they thinned out, like watercolor left in rain.

"Boss." Odile appeared at her shoulder, hands jammed in the pockets of a vest that had once been khaki and was now the color of every dust storm it had survived. "Ravi wants to know if we're doing the thing."

"The thing?"

"You know." Odile tilted her head toward the cook fire, where Tam was already laying out the folding stools in a circle with the fussy precision of someone arranging an altar. "The thing."

They were doing the thing.

* * *

The crew of the unattached gathered around the fire because there was nowhere else any of them belonged, and they all knew it, and none of them said it, which was its own kind of belonging.

Odile sat with her boots stretched toward the flames and her notebook open on her knee, though she never wrote anything during these meetings; the notebook was armor, a way of looking occupied. She was fifty-one, Senegalese by birth, French by education, and had spent twenty-six years running field operations for institutions that paid her late and credited her never. Her forearms were a topography of old burns from a camp stove explosion outside Timbuktu that she described, when she described it at all, as "a learning experience." She could stretch a budget past the point where accountants gave up on it. She could read a laborer's mood from thirty meters. She had buried one husband and outlived two institutional directors, and she treated both facts with the same unsentimental shrug.

Tam sat cross-legged, youngest of them by fifteen years, twenty-three and built like a heron, all wrists and attention. He wore his grandmother's copper bracelet under his sleeve on every dig and would not enter a new trench without first walking its perimeter counterclockwise — Wendell had timed him once, out of curiosity, and he took exactly ninety seconds, no more, as though the dead preferred punctuality. He was the best surveyor she had ever worked with, which was saying something, because instruments loved him the way instruments loved almost no one. Total stations aligned themselves for him. Drones flew straighter when he held the controller. Ravi claimed it was because Tam actually believed the equipment had feelings, and the equipment, flattered, performed. Tam did not dispute this.

And Ravi — Ravi sat slightly outside the circle, the way he always did, close enough to be part of it, far enough to watch all of it. Goan by heritage, raised in Nairobi, trained in medicine and then in the particular specialty of keeping people alive in places with no hospitals. His kit was legendary among the small community of East African field archaeologists: sutures, antimalarials, a dental extraction kit, antibiotics with expiration dates he tracked like a hawk tracks movement, and — though he never admitted this — a locked box of things that had nothing to do with medicine. Letters people asked him to hold. A wedding ring Odile had stopped wearing but couldn't throw away. Whatever Tam kept in the oilcloth packet he'd handed over in Ethiopia two seasons ago with shaking hands and a request never to ask. Ravi kept everyone's secrets the way he kept drugs: inventoried, protected, dispensed only when necessary, and never, ever discussed.

"So," Odile said, when the tea had gone around. "We're broke."

"We're not broke," Tam said. "We're pre-broke."

"We are eleven weeks from broke," Wendell said, "if nothing changes."

"Then something should change." Odile clicked her pen once, a habit that meant she'd already run the numbers twice. "Options. One: we sit here and wait for the institute to remember we exist. Two: I take the Kisimu survey alone and we split into two operations, which halves our costs and also halves our crew, which is another way of saying goodbye slowly. Three—"

She paused. And Wendell felt it before Odile even reached into her jacket — felt it the way you feel weather change, a pressure drop, a taste of metal — because she had seen the envelope in Odile's kit bag three days ago and had been pretending ever since that she hadn't.

"Three," Odile said, laying the envelope on the crate between them, "we talk about this."

The envelope was heavy stock, cream-colored, absurdly formal against the scarred plywood of the supply crate. No return address. The postage was Kenyan, which meant it had been hand-delivered to the Lodwar post office by someone with money and patience, because Marguerite Voss did not trust her correspondence to chance.

"I opened it," Odile said. "I want that on the record. I opened it because it was addressed to the operation, not to you personally, and I'm the one who handles the operation's mail. Also because you've been weird about it for three days."

"I have not been weird."

"You moved your cot four meters further from the mess tent," Tam said quietly. "You do that when something's wrong. You did it before the Kerio thing too."

Wendell looked at the fire. The hornbill had gone silent, which somehow made everything worse.

"Read it," she said. "Since you've already read it."

Odile read it. Her voice was level, professional, giving the letter the same treatment she'd give a stratigraphic report, and maybe that was mercy, or maybe it was strategy — Wendell couldn't tell, and the not-being-able-to-tell was its own small ache. *Dr. Voss extends an invitation to the Galloway field unit for a full-season excavation of the Wadi al-Nakhl complex, commencing within the month. Terms generous. Full funding, equipment allowance, hazard premiums, and completion bonuses detailed in the enclosed schedule.* There was a second page, dense with clauses. Odile skipped most of it and landed on the sentence that mattered:

"*This engagement is offered in recognition of the unit's reputation for discretion and rigor.*"

"Discretion," Ravi repeated. He said the word the way he said the names of unfamiliar symptoms — tasting it, weighing it.

"It pays for eighteen months," Odile said. "Eighteen months, Wendell. Not eleven weeks. Eighteen. Plus equipment we could never afford — the ground-penetrating array alone is worth more than our last three contracts combined."

"And the wadi?" Tam asked. He was looking at Wendell, not at Odile, and his eyes were wide and dark and entirely too perceptive. "The Wadi al-Nakhl. That name means something. I looked it up after Odile showed me. There's almost nothing published. Just—" he hesitated, and Wendell watched him decide whether to finish the sentence, and watched him finish it anyway, because Tam had never once learned to leave a dangerous thing unsaid, "—just papers from the nineties. About a fraud."

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the dark a hyena laughed its idiot laugh, high and hysterical, and let it die.

"There are papers about a fraud," Wendell said.

"About *your* fraud." Tam's voice dropped. "Galloway. That's — that's the same name as—"

"Yes."

The word came out of her flatter than she intended. She heard it land in the circle and saw each of them absorb it differently: Odile with a slow blink, filing it; Ravi going very still, the stillness of a man adding a fact to a collection he'd been building for years; Tam simply staring, mouth open, the picture of a young man watching two maps fail to match.

"You're a Galloway," Ravi said. Not a question. "Elias Galloway's daughter."

"You knew."

"I suspected. I keep files." He almost smiled. "Not paper files. Just — observations. You flinch at your own surname on manifests. You sign documents as W. Galloway and then get angry at yourself for it. And three years ago, in Addis, when someone at the conference mentioned the Nakhl controversy, you left the room so fast you forgot your hat, and I brought it to your tent, and you were sitting in the dark."

Wendell's hands had gone cold around the tin cup. She drank anyway. The tea had gone bitter with sitting, and she tasted it all the way down.

"The story," she said, because they deserved the story, or some version of it, some shape with edges she could control. "Twenty years ago my father found something in that valley. Something big. Inscriptions, a chamber, artifacts — the kind of find that makes careers. He announced it. Then the materials started falling apart under review. Inconsistencies in the stratigraphy records. Photographs that didn't match the field notes. A colleague — a respected one — produced evidence that the artifacts had been planted. Salted. My father swore the evidence was fabricated, that *he* was being framed, but he couldn't prove it, and the man who accused him had better connections and a cleaner reputation, and within two years my father was finished. Blacklisted. A cautionary tale taught to graduate students."

"And your mother," Odile said softly. Not cruelly. Odile was never cruel, but she never let a silence do work that words should do either.

"My mother was dying that same year. Cancer. He was in the valley when she got bad. He stayed — he said he was one week from the chamber, one week from proof, one week from saving everything. She died while he was in the field. I was nineteen." Wendell set the cup down. "He came home to a funeral and a daughter who wouldn't speak to him, and he never dug again. Retired. Disappeared into a house in Cape Town full of boxes nobody's allowed to open. And the valley got written off. Cursed ground. Everyone agreed the best thing was to let it stay buried."

"And now Voss wants to dig it up," Tam said.

"Now Voss wants to dig it up, with my crew, and put my family name on the permit application whether I like it or not, because the wadi's history is exactly why she wants us. Our association with the site gives her excavation credibility it doesn't deserve. Or—" Wendell stopped herself. She was saying more than she meant to. She could feel the truth straining against the version of it she'd prepared, the way water strains against a dam, and she made herself stop. "Or she has her reasons. The contract will tell us. The question isn't why she wants us. The question is whether we can afford to care."

Silence. The hyena had moved on. The coals ticked and settled.

"Eighteen months," Odile said again, quieter now. "I have people who depend on this payroll. Not you four — the wider network. The drivers, the lab techs in Nairobi, the families in Lodwar who work our seasons. When we're solvent, forty households eat. When we're not, they don't. I don't have the luxury of caring why the money comes. I wish I did. I used to." She looked at Wendell across the fire, and her eyes in the coal-light were steady and unreadable and very, very tired. "But I won't walk my people into a place that destroyed you without knowing what we're walking into. So. Vote. All of us. And Wendell — you vote last, and you vote honest, or I'll make the call myself and you can hate me in comfort."

* * *

They voted the way they always voted, which was to say Ravi went first because he claimed decisions looked smaller in the morning and he liked to sleep on other people's courage, and he said yes — "the money's clean enough, and I've worked for dirtier men than Voss, and so have you" — and then Tam, who took off his grandmother's bracelet and held it in both hands for a long moment, turning it, asking it something silently, and then put it back on and said, "Yes. But I want it noted that valleys that remember things are not places to go casually, and I want salt in the trucks, and I want to be the one to walk the first trench perimeter." And then Odile, who said only, "Yes. Obviously. Someone has to keep you all alive," and closed her notebook.

Four eyes turned to Wendell.

She looked into the fire and saw, instead of coals, the valley. She had never been there — her father had forbidden it before she was old enough to argue, and later she had forbidden it to herself, a double lock on a door neither of them had the key for. But she had seen it a thousand times in photographs she'd studied secretly, in the plates of her father's disgraced monograph, which she owned in a pirated reprint she'd bought in Cairo under a false name like a woman buying contraband. Wind-carved cliffs the color of dried blood. A floor of pale silt. A place that looked, in every image, like the inside of something that had once been alive.

If she said no, the crew dissolved in eleven weeks. If she said yes, she walked back into the one landscape on earth that held her whole history — and she would be walking in holding Voss's leash in one hand and her father's ghost in the other, and she did not know yet which of them pulled harder.

But there was a third thing, underneath, that she did not say and would not say, the thing that had been sitting in her chest since she first read the letter's cream-colored pages: *if anything is still buried in that valley, I will be the one who decides what comes to light.* Not Voss. Not the committees. Not the colleagues who had fed her father to his career. Her. She could control what got found. She could stand between the past and everyone who wanted to use it.

It wasn't forgiveness. It wasn't even curiosity, exactly. It was closer to custody.

"Yes," Wendell said. "We take the contract."

Tam exhaled like a man surfacing. Odile uncapped her pen and began writing actual notes for the first time all evening. Ravi leaned back and laced his fingers over his knee and looked at Wendell with an expression she couldn't parse — something warm and sorrowful at once, the look of a man watching someone walk toward a thing he'd already diagnosed.

"One condition," she added. "Nobody tells anyone my name. Not the porters, not the permits if we can avoid it, not Voss's people. On the manifest I'm W. Galloway, unit director, and that's all it says. As far as the world knows, there's no relation."

"You're ashamed of him," Tam said. It wasn't an accusation. It was worse — it was wonder, a young man discovering that heroes came with rust.

"I'm protecting this crew," Wendell said. "A Galloway on the paperwork brings scrutiny we can't afford. That's all."

It was mostly a lie, and she thought probably all three of them knew it was mostly a lie, and that was the strange grace of these people: they let her keep it. They let her keep the lie the way Ravi kept their secrets — inventoried, protected, dispensed only when necessary. She sat in the circle of their firelight and felt the belonging settle over her like a garment cut for someone else, beautiful and ill-fitting, and she wore it anyway, because she had nothing else, and because taking it off would mean admitting how long she'd been cold.

Later, when the others had gone to their tents, Odile lingered. She pressed a single page into Wendell's hand — the second page of Voss's letter, the clause-dense one Wendell hadn't asked to see.

"Page four," Odile said. "Section seventeen. Read it before we sign anything." She paused at the edge of the firelight. "And Wendell. Whatever's really in that valley — whatever you're really going there for — when it comes time to tell us, we'll be here. That's not a threat. It's just how crews work. Eventually everybody's business becomes everybody's business."

She left. Wendell stood alone with the page and the dying fire and read section seventeen by coal-light, twice, and the third time she read it aloud to the dark, because hearing it made it somehow less true.

*The client reserves the right to restrict contact between contracted personnel and any member of the Galloway family, immediate or extended, for the duration of the engagement.*

Voss hadn't hired her despite her father.

Voss had hired her to keep him away.

Wendell folded the page into quarters, small, smaller, until it fit inside her fist, and stood in the dark of a camp that was already dissolving, already becoming miles, and understood that the decision had never really been hers to make — that the letter had been written years ago, by people she'd never met, in a valley she'd never seen, and she had simply been walking toward it her whole life without knowing the address.

They crossed the border in nine days.
