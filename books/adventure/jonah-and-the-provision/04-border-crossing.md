## 4. Border Crossing

Odile Marchand had driven enough bad roads to know that a convoy tells you everything about itself in the first hour. Not the vehicles — the vehicles were just machines, and machines lied politely. It was the spacing between them, the rhythm of the stops, the way the drivers' eyes moved or didn't move in the mirrors. A convoy was a body, and by the time they'd left the last paved kilometer behind them outside Lodwar, she had already read this one the way she read ground: quickly, completely, and without much comfort.

Ravi's Land Cruiser led, medical kit lashed where a back seat used to be, Tam riding shotgun with the GPS unit balanced on his knees like something holy and breakable. Then the flatbed with the water drums and the trench timbers, driven by Odile herself because she trusted no one else's hands on her gearbox. Wendell brought up the rear in the pickup with the survey gear, alone, as she always arranged to be — *I'll take sweep,* she'd say, *someone has to watch for what falls behind* — and Odile had let her have it for three years without asking why a woman who hated being followed also hated being ahead.

She knew why now. Or she suspected, which in Odile's arithmetic amounted to the same thing.

The road south from Lodwar was a rumor of itself: two ruts of caliche stitched together by thorn scrub and old flood scars, the kind of track that existed less as a path than as an argument between the land and everyone who'd ever tried to cross it. Heat came off the hood in visible waves. The sky was the color of tin left out too long. Somewhere behind them, three hundred kilometers north, their insolvent season sat in a locked container waiting for this job to pay for its own rescue.

*Two hundred and forty kilometers to the border post,* Tam announced over the radio, in the tone he used for numbers he didn't like. *Then another hundred and ten to the wadi turnoff.*

Nobody answered. In a healthy convoy somebody always answered — a joke, a complaint about the suspension, Ravi threatening to prescribe everyone water. The silence that came back instead lasted four full seconds, and Odile, watching her mirrors, saw Wendell's pickup hold its position at the rear with the mechanical patience of a hearse.

She keyed the radio anyway. *Copy. Fuel stop at the post. Everyone drinks a liter before we cross, no arguments.*

*Aye,* said Ravi.

*Yes boss,* said Tam.

Nothing from the rear vehicle. Just the crackle of open channel, wind against a microphone, and then Wendell's voice, distant and even: *Copy.*

One word. Odile had counted Wendell Galloway's words the way other people counted money, and she had never once seen her run short until lately.

* * *

They made the border post by late afternoon, a scatter of concrete buildings and a striped pole going rust-brown, acacia shadow lying across the yard like spilled ink. The customs officer was a young man with immaculate boots and the weary authority of someone who processed idealists weekly. He stamped their permits, squinted at the equipment manifest, asked the standard questions — purpose of entry, duration, goods — and Odile answered them all while Wendell stood slightly apart, arms folded, looking not at the officer but south, past the pole, past the fence, at the horizon where the land began its long descent into country none of them had worked before.

*Archaeological survey,* the officer read aloud. *Wadi al-Nakhl.* He glanced up, and there was a flicker in his face — recognition, the small pleasure of a man who knew a name from somewhere. *That is a famous place, no? There was a scandal, many years ago. A professor who—*

*Permits are in order,* Odile said, mild as milk, and slid the folder closed over his finger before the sentence could land.

Because she'd been watching, she caught it: Wendell's head turning, quick, involuntary, the way a person flinches from a sound. Two seconds of stillness. Then she walked to the water drum and filled her bottle with great attention, as though hydration were a discipline requiring total concentration.

The officer waved them through with the cheerful indifference of bureaucracy. Odie drove out of the yard last, and in her mirror she watched Wendell's truck wait until the very edge of the checkpoint before following — as if the border were not a line on a map but a threshold of some older, less negotiable kind.

*Everyone accounted for?* Ravi asked over the radio, light, easy.

*All present,* Odile said, and did not add *and none of us accounted for*, which was the truth of every crew she'd ever run, hers included.

* * *

They camped that night in the lee of a basalt ridge, engines ticking as they cooled, the fire built low because low fires were law in Odile's camps. Tam cooked — he always cooked on driving days, said it kept his hands from thinking — and produced the flatbread and lentils that constituted ninety percent of his repertoire and one hundred percent of his pride. Ravi doled out malaria tabs and checked the blister on Tam's heel and listened, in his unhurried way, to everything nobody said.

Odile ate slowly and watched Wendell.

It was a habit she'd developed in the Turkana years, when Wendell had come to her dig site with a forged reference and hands that could read soil strata like scripture, and Odile had hired her inside a day because talent like that arrives maybe twice in a working life, and because she'd recognized the other thing too — the thing underneath the talent. The running. She'd seen it in deserters and debt-fleers and one memorable French aristocrat fleeing a marriage; she knew the posture of a person whose forward motion was not ambition but escape velocity. She'd hired Wendell anyway, on the theory that people outrunning something will work harder than people chasing something, and for three years the theory had paid for itself.

But theory had limits, and the limit was named Wadi al-Nakhl.

Since the letter, Wendell had gone quiet in a way that wasn't her usual quiet. Wendell's ordinary silence was a working silence — dense, occupied, the silence of a mind mapping. This was different. This was a silence with holes in it. She answered when addressed, precisely and briefly. She smiled at Tam's jokes with a delay of half a second, like a translation happening in real time. And all day, mile after mile, Odile had watched her in the mirrors doing the same thing: not looking at the road ahead but at the country beside it, at the ridgelines and dry washes, with an expression Odile could only call *counting*. As if she were measuring the distance to something not on any manifest.

Now, at the fire, she sat apart again — always apart, but tonight the apartness had a texture, a gravity. She'd taken off her boots and was working a stone out of her sock with her head bowed, and the firelight moved on her face, and Odile thought: *there it is.*

She'd seen that face before. Not on Wendell. On her own mother, twenty-two years ago, in a Peugeot 504 on the road to the family plot outside Aurillac, Odile seventeen and driving because no one else would, and her mother sitting in the passenger seat with exactly that expression — not grief, grief comes later, but the terrible composed attention of a person traveling toward a grave they have already dug in themselves. The face of someone for whom every kilometer is simultaneously too many and not nearly enough.

*Tam,* Odile said quietly, *check the tie-downs on the flatbed, would you. All of them.*

Tam went, gratefully, because Tam would rather check tie-downs nine times than sit in a silence he couldn't name. Ravi stayed, because Ravi never missed anything, and after a moment he rose too, murmuring something about the med kit and the cold, and took his lantern up the slope to give the night its privacy. Odile let them go. A foreman's real skill was not reading ground; it was reading which silences needed breaking and which needed guarding.

She crossed the camp and sat down next to Wendell, not close, just within the radius where words travel without effort.

*Long day,* she said.

*Long day.*

*Road gets worse tomorrow. The wadi turnoff is a goat track if the maps are honest, and the maps are French, so they're optimistic.*

Something moved at the corner of Wendell's mouth. Almost a smile. *French maps. God help us.*

*I've been meaning to ask you something,* Odile said, to the fire, because it was easier to tell truths to fire than to faces. *Three years you've worked for me. Best field eyes I've ever had, and I've had men with thirty years and government funding. You read ground like it's talking to you. And in three years you have never once told me where you trained. Never mentioned a university, a mentor, a single excavation before Turkana. I stopped asking because you're good and because everyone's entitled to one locked room. But we're crossing into a valley tomorrow that your whole crew knows is famous for one reason only, and I'm going to ask you now, once, and you can throw me in the dry wash if you want.*

Wendell had gone very still. The stone from her sock lay forgotten in her palm.

*Are we driving toward that valley,* Odile said, *or away from something else? Because I've driven both, and the difference matters, and right now, from where I sit, it looks like neither. It looks like you're driving toward a grave.*

The fire popped. Down the slope, Tam's flashlight swept the flatbed, methodical, faithful. Above them the stars came out in the brutal quantities the desert allows, indifferent and enormous.

For a long moment Odile thought Wendell wouldn't answer, and she prepared herself for that too — she was, above all things, a patient woman; patience was the entire job. But then Wendell spoke, and her voice was level in the way that ice is level, thin ice, load-bearing ice.

*If I tell you the truth tomorrow, will it change anything?*

*No,* Odile said. *You're the best I've got and the crew eats because of you. Truth doesn't touch that.*

*And if the truth costs you? If it costs the contract?*

*Contracts are paper. I've buried better friends than paper.*

Wendell looked at her then, fully, for the first time in weeks, and Odile saw in her eyes the exact arithmetic of a person deciding how much weight a bridge can carry. Whatever number she arrived at, she kept it. She closed her hand around the little stone and stood, and brushed sand from her trousers, and said:

*Get some sleep, boss. Early start.*

*Wendell.*

*Yeah?*

*Whatever's in that valley — it doesn't get to have my crew. Remember that. Whatever it wants from you, it takes you, not us. That's the deal you make when you drive with me.*

Wendell stood there a moment, firelight on her face, and Odile would swear afterward that something in her almost broke open — some word rising toward the surface like an artifact toward an auger — and then it sank again, and she nodded once, and said, *Goodnight, Odile,* and walked to her tent with the straight-backed care of a woman carrying something breakable in both hands.

* * *

Odile stayed up after the camp slept. She did the rounds out of habit — generators off, fuel cans squared, the flatbed's cargo net checked a fourth time because checking was prayer in her religion. At Wendell's tent she paused. Through the canvas, faintly, the beam of a penlight moved. Wendell was awake, and whatever she was doing, she was doing it bent over something small — Odile could tell by the tightness of the light's arc, the way it never traveled more than a few inches. Reading. Or writing. Or holding a page folded into quarters, small, smaller, until it fit inside a fist.

Odile stood in the cold a while longer, looking south, where the land fell away into darkness and, a hundred and ten kilometers on, into a valley that remembered things. She thought about graves and the difference between visiting one and being dragged to one. She thought about her mother's hand on the seat between them, and how she had never once, in all the years since, regretted that drive, only that she hadn't asked sooner.

Tomorrow they crossed into the borderlands proper. Tomorrow the maps ran out and the wadi began. And somewhere behind them in the dark, in a tent lit by a penlight no bigger than a match, the truth sat folded up small — and Odile Marchand, who had spent thirty years digging things out of the earth, decided she could wait for it to surface on its own.

But only as far as the valley wall.

Past that line, she told the stars, past that line, whatever it is, it comes out — or I start digging myself.

In the morning, the borderlands received them without ceremony: a road that dissolved into gravel, gravel into sand, and on the southern horizon, faint as a scar on glass, the first blue suggestion of cliffs that Odile had never seen and yet recognized instantly, because Wendell, leading the convoy for the first time in three years, had gone so quiet that the radio itself seemed afraid to speak.
