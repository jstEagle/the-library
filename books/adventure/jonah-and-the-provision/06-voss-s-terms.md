## 6. Voss's Terms

Marguerite Voss had always believed that a helicopter was the only honest way to arrive anywhere. A car announced you gradually, apologetically, letting the dust of your approach dull the impression before you ever stepped out. But a helicopter — a helicopter descended like a verdict. It filled the wadi with noise and displaced air, bent the acacias flat, sent the porters scattering with their hands over their caps, and then, when the rotors finally wound down into that ticking silence, it left you standing in the center of everyone's attention without having walked a single step to earn it.

She stepped out onto the gravel shelf above the main trench in her field whites, pressed and immaculate despite eleven hours of flight connections, and let the wind of the dying rotors pull at her silver hair just enough to suggest vigor rather than vanity. Below her, the excavation spread out along the wadi floor like an opened letter: survey stakes in neat rows, tarped spoil heaps, three trenches already cut into the terraces with a precision she noted and approved of. Galloway's daughter ran a clean site. Of course she did. Blood told.

*Wendell*, they called her now. Marguerite had known her as a girl of nine or ten, all elbows and scraped knees, trailing her father through the British Museum's basement collections while Elias held forth on stratigraphy to anyone who would listen. The girl had watched Marguerite across a case of Cypriot pottery once with those pale, unblinking eyes, and Marguerite had thought even then: *that one sees too much.* She had been right. It was why the clause existed.

"Dr. Voss." The foreman came up the slope to meet her — Odile something, Congolese-French, a woman built like a drill bit, handshake dry and firm and exactly long enough. "We weren't told to expect you until Thursday."

"I was in the region." Marguerite smiled the smile that had opened forty site gates and closed twenty careers. "I prefer to see a dig before it knows it's being seen. You've done beautiful work here. Whose grid system is this?"

"The boss's."

"Mm." She looked past the woman, down into the trenches, where figures straightened and shaded their eyes against the rotor-blown dust. One figure did not straighten. One figure stood at the edge of Trench Two with her back half-turned, trowel still in hand, and continued working as though a helicopter were merely weather. *Wendell Galloway, performing indifference.* Marguerite almost admired it. Almost.

She descended the cut steps into the main trench, boots finding the footholds without looking down — a trick she'd learned in Nubia in '89, when she'd been young and hungry and Elias Galloway had been the sun around which every ambitious orbit turned. That was the thing no one understood about her hatred of him. It wasn't jealousy. Jealousy was for people who wanted what another person had. Marguerite had wanted what another person *deserved* — deserved by talent, by rigor, by thirty years of patient work — and had watched it handed instead to a charming man with a gift for narrative and a wife who organized his citations. When the Wadi al-Nakhl scandal broke in 1998, when the artifacts were declared forged and Elias's name became a cautionary tale told over conference coffee, Marguerite had felt something she had never confessed to anyone: not triumph. *Loss.* Because if his find was fake, then the valley itself was empty, and twenty years of her own buried ambition — the grant proposals she'd written about al-Nakhl and never submitted, the maps she'd kept locked in a drawer at Cambridge — all of it had been longing after a mirage.

And then, eighteen months ago, a deaccessioned private collection had surfaced in a Zurich warehouse, and inside a crate of undistinguished Bronze Age lamps there had been a sherd with an inscription that made her hands shake so badly she'd had to set it down.

The valley was not empty. It had never been empty. Which meant Elias Galloway had been telling the truth — or had hidden the truth somewhere in it — and either possibility was intolerable unless Marguerite controlled which version reached the light.

That was why she was here. Not for the site. For the *authorship*.

* * *

They received her in the mess tent at dusk, canvas glowing amber against the blue-black cliffs, the table cleared of dinner and laid instead with the contract in its leather folder, two glasses, and a bottle of something French and absurd that she had carried in herself, because generosity was cheapest when it was also theater.

The crew sat in a loose arc: the foreman Odile, arms crossed; the medic Ravi, watchful and soft-spoken, who poured the wine without being asked and got the vintage right, which Marguerite filed away; the young surveyor Tam, who looked at the contract the way villagers once looked at tax collectors. And Wendell, seated directly opposite, hair tied back, hands wrapped around a tin mug of tea she did not drink. Up close the resemblance to Elias was a physical blow — the same long jaw, the same gray eyes set slightly deep, the same habit of listening with the whole body while appearing to look at nothing.

"You have questions," Marguerite said pleasantly. "You should. I wrote this contract myself, and I'm very good."

"Clause Nine," Wendell said. No preamble. Good. Preamble was for people with something to hide.

"Exclusive publication rights. All finds, all interpretations, all imagery. My institute publishes first, and nothing — a photograph, a radiocarbon date, a single line of text — leaves this valley under any other name until we say so."

"That's not unusual for funded work."

"No," Marguerite agreed, "it isn't. What is unusual is Clause Eleven." She turned the folder around and slid it down the table with one finger. "Sealed finds. Anything recovered from below the capstone stratum — and my geological consultants can tell you exactly where that lies — goes into sealed, logged containers. No on-site photography. No handling outside the recording protocol. The containers travel under my permits, in my shipment, at season's end."

Silence. The lantern hissed. Somewhere outside, a hyena gave its looping, idiot laugh, and Tam flinched.

"And Clause Seventeen," Wendell said, and her voice did not change at all, which was how Marguerite knew she had read ahead, had read everything, had probably read it twice by lamplight last night with those gray eyes moving line to line. "No member of this expedition will initiate or maintain contact with any member of the Galloway family for the duration of the contract and five years following."

"It's standard conflict-of-interest language."

"It's a wall."

"It's a *boundary*," Marguerite said, and let warmth into her voice, warm as bathwater, warm as the smile she'd used on donors and ministers and the one man in Cairo she'd loved and ruined. "Miss Galloway. May I call you Wendell? Wendell. I know what your father's name costs in this profession. I watched it happen. I was in the room in Athens when the committee read the findings aloud, and I watched a man's life's work get fed into a shredder because he couldn't produce provenance." She paused, precisely, letting the memory do its work. "Whatever you feel about him — and I imagine it is complicated — you do not want this dig associated with that name. Neither do I. This clause protects *you*. If the world learns a Galloway found what's down there, every result will be pre-contaminated. Every date questioned. Every inscription called a forgery before the ink dries. The name is poison, and I am offering you an antidote."

It was a good speech. She had rehearsed it on the flight from Nairobi, watching the Rift Valley scroll beneath her like a scarred palm, and she delivered it now with the timing of someone who had spent thirty years learning that truth was a material like any other — it could be cut, polished, set, and sold.

But she watched Wendell's face as she spoke, and what she saw there frightened her more than any audit ever had. Not anger. Not suspicion. *Recognition.* The girl — the woman — was listening to the speech and hearing, underneath it, its skeleton. She was doing the arithmetic of motive the way her father used to do the arithmetic of strata: quickly, silently, correctly.

"You knew him," Wendell said quietly. "Before."

"I knew everyone before."

"You hated him."

The tent went very still. Ravi's hand paused on the wine bottle. Odile's arms tightened.

Marguerite laughed — a real laugh, startled out of her, and she hated herself for the startlement. "People keep saying that. He was brilliant and careless and the most maddening colleague of my generation. Hate is such a small word for it." She lifted her glass. "I respected him, Wendell. That's rarer, and it hurts more. Now — the terms are the terms. Your crew voted. Your signatures are on the crew agreements. All that remains is yours."

She set a pen on the folder. Black lacquer, gold nib, a pen chosen for exactly this moment, because Marguerite Voss believed in props the way priests believed in incense.

* * *

Later — much later, after the tent had emptied and the camp had gone down to generator hum and wind — Marguerite sat alone in the guest tent she'd insisted on pitching apart from the others, and reviewed the day the way she reviewed all her operations: coldly, twice, looking for the flaw.

The flaw was that Wendell had signed.

Not hesitantly. Not bitterly. She had read the entire document again at the table, all forty pages, while the crew waited and the wine went warm and Marguerite performed patience, and then she had signed each initial block and the final page with a steady hand, and had said only: "My crew gets paid on the first of the month regardless of weather. That's in the rider. If it slips, the contract voids."

"Of course," Marguerite had said, and meant: *you drive a harder bargain than your father ever did.*

And that was the problem. Elias, at the end, had been easy to defeat because Elias wanted to be loved more than he wanted to be right. His daughter wanted neither. She wanted — Marguerite had seen it flash across her face for half a second when she mentioned the sealed containers — she wanted *control*. Control of what came out of the ground. Control of the story. She had taken the job, Marguerite was now nearly certain, not for the money but to stand between the valley and the world, a gatekeeper with her dead mother's chin and her disgraced father's eyes.

Which meant the contract was not a cage. It was a truce. And truces, Marguerite knew from long experience, lasted exactly as long as both parties' fear.

She took out the thing she had shown no one — could show no one, not yet, not until the shipment was safe — a photograph, old and creased, taken from the 1998 expedition's published report. It showed a side gallery in the western cliff, collapsed, its rubble face unremarkable, marked in Elias's own handwriting on the reverse: *G-7, sealed for winter, contents catalogued separately.* Contents catalogued separately. In twenty years, no separate catalogue had ever surfaced. Which meant either it had been destroyed, or it had never existed, or —

Or it was still in the valley, under the collapse, waiting.

Her consultants' ground radar had flagged an anomaly in exactly that sector three weeks ago. A void. Small, deep, and precisely where a man who feared theft would put the things he could not bear to lose.

Marguerite poured the last of the wine and drank it looking out through the tent flap at the cliffs, black against the stars, the wadi running away northward like a dark sentence awaiting its final word. Ambition, she thought, was supposed to burn hot. Everyone wrote about it as fire. No one warned you that after enough years it cooled into something else — something patient and mineral and cold, a thing that could wait underground for decades and come up sharp.

She had waited twenty years. She could wait six more days. The shipment would leave under her permits, the publication would carry her name alone, and the Galloway clause would make sure that no voice rose to contradict her — not the father's, and not the daughter's.

Outside, faintly, she heard footsteps pass her tent and stop some distance off. Through the gap in the canvas she saw a slim figure standing at the edge of the lamplight, facing the western cliffs, motionless as a stake driven into the ground. Wendell. Standing vigil over a valley she had sworn never to enter, holding secrets of her own behind those gray eyes.

Marguerite smiled in the dark and finished the wine.

Let her stand there. Let her guard it. By the time the daughter understood what her mother's chin and her father's eyes had actually witnessed in this place, the crates would be crated, the manifests stamped, the story written — and Marguerite Voss, at last, would be the one whose name the valley remembered.

In Trench Two, she had noticed that morning, the spoil heap had been turned over recently. Recently, and unevenly, and by someone digging deeper than the recorded levels allowed.

Tomorrow she would walk the trenches herself, slowly, and count every shovel's worth of earth.

The valley had kept its secret for twenty years. Marguerite intended to make sure it kept it six days more — and then give it up to her, whole, and to no one else.
