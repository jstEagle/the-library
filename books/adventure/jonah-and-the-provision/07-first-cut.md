## 7. First Cut

Tam had a rule about augers: you never trusted the first meter. The first meter was lies — topsoil, windblown sand, the loose gossip of centuries. Everything worth knowing lived deeper, in the silence under the silence, and you got there by turning steel and listening.

So Tam listened.

The morning came up pale gold over Wadi al-Nakhl, light sliding down the cliff faces like water finding its level, and the cold still had teeth in it. Tam crouched at the lip of Trench Three with the auger between both hands, breath fogging, fingers stiff around the grips. Below, Odile's voice carried up from the trench floor in clipped French-accented syllables — *deux mètres, non plus* — directing Ravi and two of the porters as they cleared the day's first spoil. Wendell stood apart, as she always stood apart, at the edge of the survey grid with her notebook open against her forearm, writing nothing. Just watching the ground.

She watched ground the way other people watched people. Tam had noticed it back in Turkana, on that roadside dig where they'd all found each other: how Wendell could stand at the edge of a site for ten minutes and then say, flatly, *the wall runs there*, pointing at nothing, at bare earth, and be right. No instrument. No resistivity reading. Just her eyes moving over the surface like fingers over braille. Tam had asked her once how she did it, and Wendell had said, "The ground tells you. You just have to stop talking long enough to hear." Which was exactly the kind of thing Tam's grandmother would have said, except her grandmother had meant it about rivers and ancestors, and Wendell meant it about dirt.

Tam believed in dirt too. But Tam also believed in the other thing — the thing Odile rolled her eyes at and Ravi politely never commented on: that places kept accounts. That some ground remembered what had been done on it. Her grandmother used to say you should always greet a new place before you put a blade into it, because a cut is a question and the earth answers honestly whether or not you're ready for the answer.

That morning, before the first stake went in, Tam had walked the length of the wadi floor alone and murmured good morning to it, quietly, in her grandmother's language, feeling slightly foolish and doing it anyway. The cliffs had not answered. The cliffs never did. They just stood there, banded red and grey like old muscle, wearing twenty centuries of wind the way an old man wears his scars — without comment.

"Tam!" Odile's voice cracked across the site. "You're being paid to drill, not to pray."

"I can do both," Tam called back, and heard Ravi laugh from down in the trench, and saw even Odile's mouth twitch before she turned away.

* * *

By mid-morning the trenches were giving up their usual confessions, and they were boring ones.

Trench One: body sherds, coarse ware, the orange grit of everyday pots, a scatter of them lying in the matrix like fallen leaves. Trench Two: more of the same, plus a copper bead gone green with age and a fragment of glass so thin the sun shone through it blue when Ravi held it up. Ordinary things. Trade goods. The debris of caravans that had passed through the wadi for a thousand years, stopping at whatever well or way-station had once stood here, drinking, breaking pots, moving on. Tam catalogued them with the patient shorthand of the surveyor's log — *sherd, plain, 1 frag; bead, Cu, corroded; glass, 1 frag, blue* — and felt the small reliable pleasure of work going the way work should go.

Because that was the thing nobody told you about archaeology, the thing the documentaries never showed: most of it was nothing. Most of it was dust and repetition and hauling buckets, and the wonder, when it came, came in thimbles. Tam had learned to live on thimbles. A bead. A rim. A coin worn nearly blank. You built a whole career out of almost-nothing, and you were grateful.

Voss's preliminary report — the one circulated to the crew, all crisp typography and confident stratigraphy — dated the wadi's occupation to somewhere between the ninth and fourteenth centuries CE. Caravan period. Way-station culture. Nothing older, nothing stranger. The report said it twice, in different sections, as if repeating it would make it true, and Tam had read those sentences three times without knowing why they bothered her. There was something in the prose like a door held firmly shut.

Wendell hadn't written anything in her notebook for an hour. Tam glanced over and saw her standing very still, head tilted, looking at the north face of the valley where the rock stepped down in terraces toward the wadi floor. Looking at it the way she'd looked at the Turkana ground before saying *the wall runs there.*

Then Wendell looked at Tam. Not at the trench. At Tam.

"Trench Three," she said. "Take it deeper."

Odile looked up from the section drawing. "We're at a meter forty. Sterile deposit. The report says—"

"The report says caravan period." Wendell didn't raise her voice. She never raised her voice; she just set words down like stones. "I want to know what's under the sterile layer."

"Sterile means sterile, Wendell. It means nothing was ever here."

"It means nothing was ever *found* here." A pause, one beat, the wind moving grit along the trench edge with a dry whispering sound. "There's a difference. Take it down another half meter. Slowly."

Odile held her gaze for a moment — Tam watched the silent negotiation pass between them, foreman and boss, the argument they seemed able to have entirely in eyebrow movements — and then Odile shrugged in the particular way that meant *your name is on the contract, not mine,* and waved Tam down into the trench.

* * *

The bottom of Trench Three was cool enough that Tam's sweat went cold on her back. The walls rose a meter and a half above her, cutting off the horizon, leaving only a strip of sky the color of hammered tin. Down here the world was smell and texture: damp earth, mineral salt, the faint sourness of the matting they'd laid to protect the section faces. Tam knelt, greased the auger shaft, and greeted the ground again under her breath, because down here it felt less foolish.

First turn: soft fill, easy, the auger biting and lifting clean.

Second turn: resistance. Compact. Old.

Third turn—

The sound changed.

Every digger knew that sound, the way a sailor knows the sound of weather coming. Loose sediment sang a certain song through the shaft — a grinding, granular whisper. But this was different. This was a hard, bright, ringing bite, a sound with edges, and Tam felt it come up the steel and into her wrists and sternum like a struck bell.

She stopped. Lifted the auger. Turned the flights into the light.

Wedged in the spiral of the blade, crumbly and pale and unmistakable, was stone dust. Not the soft crushed caliche of the natural deposits. This dust had a different grain, finer, whiter, and mixed through it were tiny flecks that caught the light with a dull metallic glint — mica, or something like it, something that did not belong in this stratum, in this century, in this story.

"Odile," Tam said. Her voice came out strange, thin, from someone else's throat. "Odile, I've hit something."

Boots on the ladder rungs. Odile dropped into the trench with the ease of a woman who had been climbing in and out of holes for thirty years, took the auger, examined the flights, rubbed the dust between finger and thumb. Tam watched her face do something complicated — the professional mask slipping just enough to show the thought underneath, quick and sharp as a fish turning.

"That's dressed stone," Odile said quietly. "Or I'm a donkey."

"How deep?"

"Your bit's at two meters ten." Odile looked up at the section face, at the clean layers stacked above them like pages — the caravan sherds, the sterile band, the windblown cap. "Two meters ten below a stratum that isn't supposed to exist until nine hundred years from now."

Ravi appeared at the trench lip, silhouetted against the sky, and for once he didn't make a joke. He just looked down at them, and then at Wendell, who had come to the edge without Tam hearing her approach — she moved that quietly, always, like someone practicing for invisibility — and stood there with her notebook closed now, pressed flat against her thigh, knuckles white around it.

"Open it properly," Wendell said. "Hand tools only. Trowels and brushes. Nobody touches that stone until we've photographed the section."

They worked through the afternoon while the light went from gold to copper to bruise. Tam troweled with the small controlled strokes she'd been taught, peeling the compact fill back centimeter by centimeter, and the shape emerged slowly, the way shapes do in dreams — first a straightness that no natural process makes, then a corner, square and deliberate, then the long clean face of a block dressed by human hands and fitted against its neighbor with a seam so fine Tam couldn't slide a fingernail into it.

It was beautiful. That was the terrible part. It was beautiful the way a locked door is beautiful when you are alone in a house.

By the time the shadows filled the trench, they had exposed perhaps half a square meter of it: a paved surface, or the top of a wall, cut stone laid in courses, precise as scripture. And it sat *below* everything. Below the caravan sherds. Below the sterile layer. Below, if Voss's report was right, below reason itself.

Nobody spoke much on the walk back to camp. The porters chattered softly among themselves, but the crew was quiet, and Tam understood why. It wasn't joy. Joy would have been simple. This was the other thing — the feeling of having opened a door in your own house and found a room you'd never known was there, and hearing, faintly, from inside it, breathing.

* * *

At supper the generator hummed and the pressure lamps made islands of yellow light, and Odile laid the day's photographs out on the folding table like a hand of cards. Wendell sat opposite her, elbows on the table, studying them.

"This predates Elias Galloway's claim by six hundred years," Odile said. Flat. Factual. Saying aloud what everyone had been not-saying since the auger rang. "His find — the one they stripped his name over — was dated late. Thirteenth century, maybe. Controversial. This—" she tapped the photograph of the seamed stone, "—this is older than his controversy. Older than the accusation. If this is what it looks like, then the wadi has been lying to everyone for twenty years, including the people who wrote the report we were hired under."

"Voss's report," Ravi said mildly, ladling out stew. He said it the way he said everything, gently, but he let it sit there on the table between the plates like a stone of its own.

"She commissioned the dating," Odile said. "She wrote the summary. She chose what went in it."

"And she's arriving Thursday," Ravi said, "with a helicopter."

Silence. The lamp hissed. Out past the light, the wadi breathed its night sounds — wind in the rocks, the far complaint of a jackal, sand ticking against canvas.

Tam looked at the photographs and felt the fear move in her chest, the specific fear of the witness, the one her grandmother had named for her when she was small: *the fear of knowing something too big for your hands.* Because that was what this was. Not treasure — Tam had seen treasure, everyone dreamed of treasure, and this wasn't it. This was worse and better. This was proof. Proof that the ground remembered, that the accounts were kept, that somewhere under the sterile layer of an official report a truth had been waiting two meters ten down, patient as bone, for someone with an auger and a habit of greeting the dark.

And proof, once found, belonged to whoever held the pen.

"We log it tonight," Wendell said at last. Her voice was steady, but Tam, watching her hands, saw that they weren't. "Full context shots. I'll write the field entry myself. And Tam—" she looked up, and her eyes in the lamplight were the color of the deep strata, dark and layered and hiding something, "—you struck it. Your name goes in the record first. Whatever happens next, that's yours."

Tam nodded, throat tight, and looked back down at the photographs, at the clean impossible seam of the stone.

Later, in her tent, she lay listening to the wind work the guy-lines and thought about doors held shut, and reports written twice, and a helicopter coming on Thursday out of a sky the color of hammered tin. She thought about her grandmother's warning — *a cut is a question, and the earth answers honestly* — and she understood, finally, with a cold settling certainty, that the wadi had answered.

The only question left was what Marguerite Voss intended to do with the answer — and whether any of them were going to be allowed to tell it.
