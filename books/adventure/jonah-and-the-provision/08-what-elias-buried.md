## 8. What Elias Buried

The side gallery had been dying for a long time.

Wendell felt it before she saw it — a wrongness in the acoustics of the trench, the way her bootfalls came back to her flattened and dead instead of ringing off living stone. Sound was honest in a way people weren't. A healthy gallery sang. This one had swallowed its own voice sometime in the last two decades, its ceiling slumped into a fan of rubble that fanned out from the eastern wall like a collapsed lung.

She should have flagged it and moved on. Protocol said so. Odile would have said so, in her flat foreman's voice that made caution sound like arithmetic rather than fear. *Unstable matrix, no visible features, not worth the risk.* And on any other site, in any other season of her life, Wendell Galloway would have agreed without argument, because she had built her whole career on knowing which fights were worth the dust they raised.

But the light was doing something strange in there.

It came through a fissure near the ceiling — a blade of late-morning sun that cut through the disturbed sediment still hanging in the air from Tam's auger work three trenches over, and it fell across the rubble field at an angle that made one stone glow amber while everything around it sat in blue shadow. Ordinary physics. She knew that. Refraction and suspended particulate and nothing more. But her father had taught her to read light the way other archaeologists read stratigraphy, and Elias Galloway had a saying he'd repeated until she'd memorized it at nine years old, sitting cross-legged on a dig tarp with sand in her hair:

*The valley shows you what it wants you to find. Your job is to ask why it wants you to find it.*

She hadn't thought of that saying in years. She'd buried it deeper than any artifact, under three years of Turkana roadsides and a phone number she never called and a name she'd shortened at university so no professor would look at her twice and say *Galloway — any relation?* And now here it was, surfacing whole, in a dead gallery in Wadi al-Nakhl, because the light wanted her to look at one particular stone.

Wendell stood at the gallery mouth for a long moment, letting the cool air spill out over her — underground air has a temperature all its own, older than the day, and this air smelled of crushed limestone and something faintly mineral-sweet, like water that had passed through gypsum. Then she keyed her radio.

"Odile. East trench, the side gallery past marker fourteen. I'm going in."

A pause long enough to contain an entire argument. "Matrix is compromised, Wendell. Shoring first."

"Shoring's an hour. The light moves." She heard herself say it and winced — *the light moves*, as if that were a reason, as if she were nine years old again and asking permission with someone else's logic in her mouth.

Another pause. "Forty minutes," Odile said finally. "Ravi's on standby. You feel anything shift, you call it and walk out. Not run. Walk. Panic kills more diggers than collapse does."

"Copy." Wendell clipped the radio to her belt and stepped into the dark.

* * *

Inside, the collapse told its story the way collapses always did, if you knew how to listen. She moved along the margin where the rubble met the wall, testing each foothold, reading the fan of debris: the big blocks nearest the wall had fallen first and farthest, the finer material had sifted down later, and the whole mass had been undisturbed since — no animal burrows cutting through it, no modern intrusion, no footprints but her own in the skin of fine dust that lay over everything like grey snow. Whatever was under that rubble had been sealed away since before the roof came down. Since 1998, said a voice in her head she didn't want to hear. Since him.

She reached the glowing stone and knelt beside it.

Up close the amber light resolved into something mundane — a chunk of calcite veining in the limestone, catching the sun-blade and burning. She almost laughed at herself. Almost. But kneeling put her face level with the base of the wall, and at the base of the wall, half-hidden behind a slab that had leaned against it like a drunk against a doorframe, there was a niche.

Not natural. Natural solution cavities in this formation ran vertical, carved by water hunting downward. This one was horizontal, roughly rectangular, maybe forty centimeters wide, its edges too regular, and when she swept her hand across its lip the dust came away in a clean line revealing chisel marks — small, patient, deliberate marks, the work of someone who had cut into living rock with a hand tool and all the time in the world.

Or all the time in one night, she corrected herself. Because she knew those marks. She had grown up watching men make them. Her father had taught her to dress stone when she was eleven, his big cracked hands closed over hers on the chisel, telling her *let the tool do the remembering, not your arm.*

Wendell's heart was doing something complicated and unpleasant. She worked the leaning slab aside inch by inch, wedging it with a rock hammer handle, breathing carefully, and then she reached into the niche.

Her fingers found canvas. Stiff, dry, waxy with age — a groundsheet roll, tied with cord gone brittle as cooked spaghetti. She drew it out into the light blade and unwound it with hands that were not entirely steady, and inside, wrapped in a second layer of oilcloth, was a book.

A standard Rite-in-the-Rain field journal, green cover, the kind sold in every outfitter between Nairobi and Cairo. The elastic band had perished into a smear of black gum. On the cover, in grease pencil, in handwriting she would have known blind, underwater, at gunpoint — the tall ascenders, the impatient crossbars, the *G* that always looked like it was trying to escape the line —

**E. GALLOWAY — WADI AL-NAKHL — 1998**

The valley went very quiet around her. Or perhaps she did.

* * *

She didn't open it right away. That surprised her. She had imagined, in the worst hours of the last twenty years, all the things she might say to her father, and none of her imaginings had included sitting in the dirt holding his notebook like it was a live grenade. But her body refused to move, and eventually she understood why: opening it was a door, and doors only swing one way. Everything on this side of it — the exile, the shortened name, the careful miles — was a life she had constructed out of not-knowing. The book in her hands was knowing. It weighed less than four hundred grams and it terrified her.

Somewhere above, the sun-blade crept across the rubble. Dust turned gold in it. Tam's auger thudded distantly, three trenches over, steady as a heartbeat.

She opened it.

The early pages were ordinary — grid references, soil colors described in the idiosyncratic shorthand every field archaeologist invents and no two share (*buff-grey, tastes of chalk; red-brown, angry*), sketches of pottery sherds with measurements crawling up the margins. His hand was fast and sure. She could hear his voice in it, the way he talked when he was working, clipped and delighted. She turned pages faster than she read them, hungry and afraid in equal measure, and then, forty pages in, the delight began to change.

*Third chamber east. The stratum is wrong. Wrong the way a witness is wrong — not lying, just standing somewhere else. Voss's team dated the fill from the upper levels and called it done. But the fill isn't native. It's imported. Someone backfilled this chamber deliberately, and the material they used is younger than what's underneath. We are digging through a disguise.*

Wendell stopped breathing.

*Sampled the basal deposit myself. Ceramic inclusions consistent with 2nd millennium fabrication. The inscriptions are not intrusive. They are original. Whoever cut them did it when the stone was fresh, and the patina in the incisions cannot be faked — I've tried, God help me, I spent six weeks trying to fake it so I could prove how easy it would be, and I couldn't do it. Nobody could. Not in 1974, not ever.*

The pages after that grew tighter, the handwriting smaller, pressed hard enough to emboss the paper beneath. Dates. Sample numbers. A chain of custody written out in obsessive detail — *core sample 12-B, sealed, witnessed by Haddad, stored in my tent, NOT the finds tent* — and threaded through it all, recurring like a refrain, one name appearing and reappearing with a bitterness that bled through the grease pencil into something closer to a knife:

*M.V. denies receipt of correspondence.*
*M.V.'s preliminary report cites the 1974 survey. The 1974 survey was hers. She wrote the stratum date herself and now she uses it against me.*
*If M.V. gets access to the storage crates, the record dies. Not disputed — died. There is a difference and she knows it.*

Wendell sat back on her heels. The cold of the ground had climbed through her trousers into her bones and she hadn't noticed. Her breath hung faintly in the gallery air.

She had grown up on the public version of this story, the version told in faculty lounges and journal editorials and, once, catastrophically, on television: Elias Galloway, the brilliant man who broke, who fabricated a dynasty out of wishful thinking, who destroyed his own credibility and his family in the same season. She had built her exile on that version. She had measured every mile of the last three years against it. And here, in forty pages of his cramped furious hand, was the other version — not vindication exactly, not yet, vindication required evidence and witnesses and the slow grinding machinery of institutions — but testimony. A man writing alone at night, in a valley that had already begun to turn on him, recording everything, trusting the paper the way he had never trusted people.

*They will say I made it up,* he wrote on one page, the letters wobbling in a way she had never seen in his hand, as if the pen had been shaking. *Let them. Stone doesn't care what they say. Stone keeps its own minutes.*

And beneath that, boxed in double lines as if he'd meant to come back to it and never had:

*Originals removed to secure location. Details follow. If anything happens to me, the location dies with me rather than with them.*

Then the next page.

Torn out.

Not ripped — torn with a ruler's help, a clean vertical tear at the spine, leaving a ragged stub an inch wide. She turned the stub over. Nothing on the reverse. She flipped forward, backward, checking for displaced fragments, loose fibers, anything. The binding was intact. The page had been removed carefully, deliberately, by someone with time and a steady hand, and the entry immediately following began mid-thought, as though whatever had come between had been excised from the record itself:

*—so that settles it. No one sees the location. Not the committee, not the lawyers, not her. Especially not her. I'll take it out of the country myself if I have to, one crate at a time, and when the season ends I'll—*

The sentence ended there. Not finished — abandoned, the way a sentence is abandoned when its author hears footsteps, or changes his mind, or simply runs out of night. The remaining pages of the journal deteriorated into inventory lists and weather notes and then stopped altogether, three days before the excavation officially collapsed and her father walked out of this valley for the last time with his reputation in pieces behind him.

Wendell closed the book.

For a long time she didn't move. The sun-blade had traveled; the calcite no longer glowed; the gallery had gone the color of old bone. Somewhere overhead, grit ticked down through the fissure, patient as a clock. She became aware, distantly, that her face was wet, and that she had no memory of it starting, and that crying in a collapsed gallery forty minutes from help was objectively stupid, and that none of that knowledge changed anything.

He hadn't broken. He had hidden something. He had cut a niche in the dark with a hand chisel and buried his own testimony in the one place he swore never to return to, and he had torn out the page that said where — torn it out and taken it with him, or burned it, or lost it, or—

Or hidden it somewhere else.

The thought arrived whole, unbidden, the way her best readings always did — not reasoned but recognized, the ground speaking through her. A man paranoid enough to chisel a secret niche wouldn't trust a single hiding place. A man who wrote *the location dies with me* had already decided that redundancy was survival. Which meant the torn page wasn't an ending.

It was a pointer.

"Wendell." Odile's voice crackled in her ear, flat and unhurried. "Your forty minutes died ten minutes ago. Report."

Wendell looked down at the journal in her lap, at the green cover with her family name on it, at the ragged stub of the missing page. Around her the valley held its silence, twenty years deep, keeping its minutes.

"Found something," she said. Her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "I'm bringing it up."

She wrapped the journal back in its oilcloth, tucked it inside her jacket against her ribs — not the sample bag, not the finds tray, not anywhere it would appear on a manifest Marguerite Voss had the right to seal — and started the careful walk back toward the light.

Behind her, in the niche at the base of the wall, her fingers had noticed something she hadn't let herself think about yet: a second cavity behind the first, its chisel marks fresher, its depth unknown. Empty now.

But not empty when he left it.

Whatever Elias Galloway had buried in this valley, he had buried it twice — and the second grave was still waiting to be opened.
