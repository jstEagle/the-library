## 9. Sandstorm Protocol

The wind arrived before the wind did.

Ravi felt it first in his teeth — a pressure change, a faint metallic taste on the back of his tongue, the same warning his grandmother's knees used to give her about the monsoon. He was sterilizing suture trays when the light through the mess tent's canvas went the color of old brass, and Tam came stumbling in with his anemometer readings and his eyes wide as coins.

"Barometer's dropped eleven millibars in two hours," he said. "That's not weather. That's a wall."

Ravi looked out across the camp. The wadi had gone strange and still, the way a room goes still before someone says something unforgivable. The cliffs that ringed them stood pale and patient against a sky bruising violet at the eastern rim, and the flag lines on the survey stakes had all swung around to point the same direction at once, like a congregation turning to face a door.

"Get Odile," he said. "And start tying down anything you love."

* * *

They had a protocol for this. Every crew did. Ravi had written half of theirs himself, back in Turkana, after a season when a haboob had taken their latrine tent and very nearly taken their water drums with it. Sandstorm Protocol: collapse the shade structures, double-stake the mess and sleeping tents, seal the equipment crates, fill every container with water because the pumps would choke, and then get inside and stay inside and be kind to each other, because three days in a canvas box with the same four people was a longer test than any dig.

By dusk the storm made landfall.

It didn't arrive so much as descend — a brown ocean tipping over the eastern cliffs, swallowing them whole. The tents became drums, then became lungs, breathing in great shuddering gusts. Inside the mess tent they sat in lamplight while the world outside erased itself, sand hissing against the fly like static, like rain, like whispering. Ravi distributed earplugs and antacids and the good chocolate he'd been hoarding since Nairobi, and told them all to sleep while they could.

Nobody slept.

* * *

Day one broke — if you could call it breaking; the sun never showed, just a dim orange coin behind the murk — with Odile re-tensioning guy lines in sixty-kilometer winds because sitting still made her itch. Tam narrated the barometer hourly like a man reading scripture aloud in a burning church. And Wendell Galloway sat in the corner of the mess tent with the site plans spread around her, redrawing trench sections from memory, and said almost nothing at all.

Ravi noticed things. It was his job, officially — pulses, pupils, hydration, the small betrayals of the body — but he'd gotten good at noticing the other kind too, the unlicensed medicine. He noticed that Wendell hadn't touched the site plans of the north gallery in six hours, just kept redrawing the same collapsed side chamber over and over, each version identical down to the pencil strokes, like she was trying to wear a groove in the paper. He noticed she'd stopped eating the dates he left by her elbow. He noticed that when Tam mentioned the cut stone again — the auger strike, the impossible depth of it — Wendell's hand went flat against her sternum, a small unconscious gesture, the way people touch themselves where something hurts.

He also noticed, on the second morning, that she was favoring her right side.

"Let me see it."

"It's nothing."

"You've been bracing your ribs for a day and a half. You flinched when Odile handed you the crate of sample bags." He set his kit down between them, deliberate as a chess move. "Wendell. Let me see it."

Something crossed her face — he watched it happen, watched the shutters try to come down and fail halfway, which frightened him more than any injury would have. Wendell Galloway did not do halfway. She was either sealed or she wasn't there at all.

It was the storm that undid her, he thought later. Three days of wind grinding away at everything, the tents groaning, no trenches to lose herself in, no miles to put between herself and whatever she was carrying. Exile worked fine when you could keep moving. Trap a woman in a canvas room with her own history and the history started talking.

She pulled up the hem of her shirt.

The bruise bloomed along her lower ribs, purple going green at the edges — a fall, probably, in the side gallery where the footing was treacherous, though she'd never reported it. Ravi palpated gently, watching her face. Two ribs, maybe three, cracked but not displaced. Painful. Not dangerous. The kind of injury that healed fine if you rested and turned septic-sloppy if you didn't.

"When?"

"Day before the storm."

"In the gallery."

A pause long enough to have weight. "Yes."

He taped her ribs with the efficient tenderness he'd learned in a trauma ward in Mumbai and a clinic in Port Sudan, wrapping snug but not tight, and she sat very still under his hands, staring at the lantern, and when he finished she didn't pull away. That was new. That was the crack in the armor, and Ravi, who kept everyone's secrets including his own, understood that what you did with a crack was you didn't poke it. You let the light do the work.

"There's a painkiller question," he said lightly. "Ibuprofen and tape, or the good stuff, which will make you stupid."

"Ibuprofen."

"I thought so." He packed the kit. Then, without looking at her, because looking would have made it a thing: "You know, my mother used to say the desert doesn't hide anything. It just waits. Everything buried comes up eventually — bones, water, truth. Usually all three together."

Wendell went very quiet. Outside, the wind screamed against the canvas like something trying to get in, or trying to get out.

"That's a terrible proverb," she said finally.

"It's a true one. Those are usually terrible."

* * *

The tempers went on day two.

It began small — Tam snapping at Odile over a misplaced headlamp, Odile's reply carrying an edge it didn't need — and by afternoon the mess tent had the atmosphere of a lifeboat. Too many hours, too little space, the storm pressing on the walls like a thumb on a nerve. Ravi dosed everyone with patience and electrolytes and made lunch last ninety minutes because an occupied mouth couldn't argue.

It was Tam who cracked first, which surprised no one and everyone. The boy ran on superstition and wonder in equal parts, and the cut stone had been sitting in him for days now like a swallowed coal.

"It doesn't make sense," he burst out, mid-afternoon, apropos of nothing, his hands shaking around his tea. "The stratigraphy is wrong. The depth is wrong. Either the entire dating sequence for this valley is wrong, or—" He stopped. Looked at Wendell. Looked away. "Or somebody knew. Somebody already knew what was down there and never said."

The silence had texture. Ravi heard the wind, and under the wind he heard Odile set down her cup with exaggerated care, and under that he heard Wendell breathe once, slow, the way he'd taught her to breathe during the worst of the altitude sickness she pretended she didn't have.

"Tam," Odile said, a warning.

"No, I want to know." The boy's voice cracked and he pushed through it. "I've been telling myself it's a geological anomaly, I've been telling myself the auger hit a fallen block, and none of it holds, because stone doesn't lie about its stratum. Somebody knew. And whoever knew has been walking around this camp for weeks looking like they're attending their own funeral, and I'm done pretending I haven't noticed."

"Wendell," Ravi said quietly, "you don't have to—"

"My father dug here."

The words came out flat, stripped of everything, and landed in the tent like a dropped tool.

"Twenty years ago. Before I was born, before—" She stopped, corrected herself with visible effort. "Before the end of it. His name was Elias Galloway. You might know the name. It comes with a word attached. *Fraud.*" She laughed, and there was nothing in it. "He claimed he found a chamber complex under the north escarpment. No one could verify it. The artifacts he produced were declared fabricated, the provenance chain collapsed, and he lost everything — career, reputation, marriage. My mother died believing she'd married a liar. I've spent three years digging everywhere else in the world because of it."

Tam stared. Odile had gone very still, the stillness she wore like armor, the foreman's stillness that meant she was recalculating everything.

"The contract," Odile said slowly. "Voss's contract. Clause seventeen forbids contact with any Galloway. We all read it and laughed, because who on earth would—"

"Yes," Wendell said. "That clause was written for me. Or against me. Marguerite Voss built her career on my father's ruin, and now she's hired his daughter to excavate the valley he swore off, with exclusive publication rights and sealed finds, and I signed it because we needed the money and because I wanted to control what came out of this ground." Her jaw tightened. "I wanted to be the one holding the shovel when the past came up. Instead the past came up on its own. Cut stone below the dated stratum. Which means either my father was right, or—"

"Or he was right," Ravi said simply, "and someone made sure no one ever found out."

The wind howled. Somewhere outside, something loose banged against a crate, rhythmic, obsessive, like a heartbeat.

Wendell pressed both hands flat against the table, and Ravi saw her ribs cost her the motion and saw her pay it anyway. "I found a journal," she said, barely above the wind. "In the collapsed gallery. Field notes, 1998, his handwriting. He insisted the find was genuine. There's a page torn out." Her voice did something complicated on the last words. "I don't know yet what he tore out. But I'm going to find out."

Nobody spoke for a long moment. Then Odile reached across the table and refilled Wendell's tea, which in Odile's language was a speech, a treaty, and an embrace all at once.

"Well," the foreman said. "Now we know why the boss drives like she's being chased."

* * *

The third night the storm began to die.

Ravi lay awake in the medical tent listening to the wind lose its argument with the world, dropping register by register until it was only a long exhausted sighing across the sand. Around midnight the pressure lifted off his teeth. By two, the silence was so complete it rang.

He got up to check on Wendell — professional habit, personal devotion, the line between those had blurred seasons ago — and found her sleeping tent empty.

He found her at the edge of camp, standing where the fly had been tied back, looking east. The storm had scoured the sky clean. Stars in brutal abundance, more stars than seemed legal, and beneath them the wadi emerging from its brown cocoon, reshaped, the dunes migrated, the trenches half-swallowed, the whole valley rearranged like furniture in a house where something violent had happened.

Wendell stood with her arms wrapped around her taped ribs, and she was crying. Not loudly. Not dramatically. Just tears, running steadily down a face pointed at the stars, the way water finds its level.

Ravi didn't announce himself. He came and stood beside her, close enough that she'd know he was there, far enough that she could pretend he wasn't. That was the medicine he was best at.

"He drew maps of this place on napkins," she said, after a while, to the stars or to him, he couldn't tell. "When I was small. Little wadis with little X marks. I thought every father did that." A breath. "I spent twenty years hating him for choosing the ground over us. And now I'm standing on the ground, and I think maybe he chose it because he was trying to protect us from what was buried in it. From *her.*"

"From Voss."

"From what Voss's people did to him." She wiped her face with the back of her hand, rough, impatient, the gesture of a woman furious at her own leaking. "The torn page, Ravi. I keep thinking about the torn page. What did he tear out? What was so important he hid it even from his own journal?"

Ravi looked east, where the dawn would eventually come, where the pass road waited nine days from closing, where Marguerite Voss's helicopter would return with its cargo of charm and fine print.

"Tomorrow," he said, "we dig it out."

Beside him, Wendell went still. When she turned, her eyes in the starlight were red-rimmed and absolutely clear, clearer than he'd seen them since Turkana, since the letter, since the beginning of all of it.

"Not tomorrow," she said. "Tonight. While the valley's soft and Voss thinks we're buried. Wake Odile. Wake Tam." She pressed a hand to her taped ribs and smiled, and it was not entirely a sane smile, and it was the most alive Ravi had ever seen her. "We're going to reconstruct a page."

* * *

Dawn came up gold behind the eastern cliffs, the wadi washed and gleaming, the air scrubbed thin and cold enough to bite. Ravi brewed coffee for four and carried the fifth cup — the good one, the one he saved — out to the edge of camp, where Wendell stood over the field table with the water-stained fragments of a torn page laid out before her like a shattered mosaic, tweezers in hand, her father's ghost rising off the paper in faded ink.

Behind her, the storm's debris stretched to the horizon, and somewhere out beyond it, a satellite phone was already buzzing in Voss's command tent.

Ravi set the cup down and said nothing. Some mornings announced themselves.
