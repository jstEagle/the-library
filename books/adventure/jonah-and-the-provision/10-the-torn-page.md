## 10. The Torn Page

The storm had passed two days ago and left the wadi smelling of wet stone and something older underneath it, like the breath of a cellar that had been sealed for a century. Wendell worked alone in her tent with the flap half-open to the morning light, a headlamp clipped to a tent pole for shadowless illumination, and before her on the camp table — the one Odile had built from packing crates and stubbornness — lay the ruins of a single page.

She had counted the fragments eleven times. Eleven times they had come to thirty-four pieces, some no larger than a thumbnail, all of them water-stained to the color of weak tea, all of them soft at the edges as though the paper had begun to remember being pulp. The storm that had trapped them in their tents for three days had also found its way into the collapsed side gallery through a fissure none of them had mapped, and it had found the niche where Elias Galloway had hidden his field journal twenty-six years ago, and it had done what water does: it had gotten into everything.

The journal itself she had dried page by page over two nights, weighting each leaf flat between sheets of blotting paper Ravi had sacrificed from the medical stores, apologizing to the paper as he handed it over as if it were a patient. Most of the journal had survived. The ink had feathered but held. Her father's handwriting was exactly as she remembered it from the margins of her childhood textbooks — small, slanted leftward, urgent, the writing of a man who always feared he would run out of time before he ran out of things to say.

Except for one page. One page had been torn out — not by the water, not by time. Torn deliberately, close to the spine, by hands that had wanted it gone and then, evidently, had wanted it back, because the fragments had been folded into a separate packet at the back of the journal, wrapped in a scrap of plastic sheeting that had crumbled like autumn leaf when she touched it. He had torn it out and kept the pieces. She had turned that fact over all night, the way you turn a stone over to see what lives beneath it.

Now she laid the fragments out on a grid of graph paper, each piece assigned a number in pencil, and began again.

* * *

The work demanded a particular kind of silence, and Wendell had spent three years perfecting that silence. It was the same silence she used for reading ground — the stillness that let the landscape speak first. Her mother had called it *listening with your hands*. Her father had called it nothing at all, because by the time Wendell was old enough to show him what she could do, he had stopped looking at her long enough to notice.

She started with the fragments that had whole words. There were perhaps nine of these. *Voss* appeared on four of them. That word alone had kept her awake — her father's rival's name, in her father's hand, on a page he had destroyed and then preserved. She placed those pieces at the center of the grid and built outward from them, matching fiber patterns along the tear lines, the way the ragged edges interlocked like coastline and continent. The paper was cheap field stock, wood-pulp, the kind that came in government-issue survey pads; the tear lines were correspondingly rough, and where two fragments met, the fit was honest. Paper did not lie about its breaks. People did. Paper just broke.

An hour in, her tea had gone cold and Tam had looked in twice without speaking, which was Tam's way of asking whether she needed anything while making clear he would accept *no* as an answer. The second time she had caught him studying the grid with wide eyes and she had said, gently, "It's my father's," and he had nodded once, very seriously, and withdrawn like a boy leaving a shrine.

By midday she had assembled perhaps sixty percent of the page. Enough to read the argument of it, if not every sentence. She copied what she had into her own notebook, transcribing fragment by fragment, and only when the transcription was complete did she let herself read it as a whole.

Her father's voice rose off the page like heat off rock.

* * *

*—refuse to sign the deposition. Voss has taken my stratigraphic readings and inverted the sequence. I have seen her working notes — R. showed me copies — and the "inconsistencies" she presented to the committee are not inconsistencies at all but alterations. She has changed the dates. She has changed MY dates, in my own hand, photographed and enlarged until the emendations disappear into the grain of the paper.*

*I know how this will sound. I know that the man who accuses the rising star of forgery looks, to the committee, like the forger himself. She has arranged it so that any defense I mount becomes further evidence of desperation. Clever. Cold. She learned from H——, and H—— taught her everything except restraint.*

*So I will do the thing they least expect. I will not defend the find. I will bury it.*

*The original artifacts — the ones that matter, the ones with the inscriptions intact — are not in the museum crates. Those are casts. Good ones, my own work, made from the originals before anyone knew what we had. Let the committee examine their "evidence." Let them declare it fraudulent. It IS fraudulent — I made it so, deliberately, and I am the only person alive who knows the difference. The originals went back into the ground yesterday, in the lower gallery, behind the collapse, in a place I have recorded nowhere except here.*

*Let Voss win. Let her build her career on my ruin. A career built on a lie can be dismantled by the truth whenever the truth is ready. But if I fight her now, with the committee already decided and H——'s fingerprints on half their careers, I lose the artifacts forever. They will be seized, dispersed, mislabeled, or worse — attributed to HER. Better they sleep in the wadi than wake up wearing her name.*

*Wendell turns eight next month. E. says I should be there. I will be there. After the lower gallery is sealed I will drive straight through—*

Here the fragments failed. A section the size of a palm had been lost entirely — dissolved, or scattered beyond recovery in the gallery's debris — and when the text resumed, the handwriting had changed. Not the letters themselves, but the pressure of them. The pen had been pressed harder. The words crowded against each other.

*—did not make it. I chose the gallery. I chose the gallery and E. sat alone at the hospital and Wendell—*

The sentence stopped. Below it, in a different ink, bluer, added later — much later, judging by the way the blue sat on top of the faded black like frost on soil — a single line:

*There is no version of this where I get to explain. She will read this or she won't. If she does: the valley keeps what it is given. Come and take it back.*

* * *

Wendell set down the pencil.

She did not move for a long time. Outside, the wadi performed its afternoon liturgy — wind worrying the cliff faces, a ratcheting call of some bird she had never identified, the tick of cooling stone. Inside the tent, the light through the canvas made everything the color of old bone.

Fury came first. It came the way it always came, instant and total, a flash flood with no bank to hold it. *He hid the proof.* Twenty-six years. Twenty-six years of colleagues crossing the street to avoid the Galloway name, of grant applications returned unopened, of her mother answering the phone with her spine straightening before she even said hello. Twenty-six years during which the truth had lain in a hole in the ground *because he put it there*. Because he had decided — alone, in the arrogance of a man certain history would eventually require him — that the artifacts were safer buried than defended, and that his daughter's eighth birthday was a thing that could be postponed indefinitely, rescheduled against a horizon that never arrived.

He had chosen the gallery. He had written it himself, pressed hard enough to emboss the page: *I chose the gallery.* And her mother had died with the telephone quiet, and Wendell had grown up learning to measure love in distances, and all of it — all of it — had been a strategy. A calculation. Cold as anything Marguerite Voss had ever done, and dressed up as principle.

She stood up so fast the camp table rocked and the fragments slid, and she caught the edge of the graph paper with both hands before thirty-four numbered pieces became an unnumbered scatter, and the catching of them — the instinctive, full-body protection of the thing — stopped her colder than the fury had.

Because she had protected it. Without thinking. The way her father's hands must have moved to fold the torn pieces into plastic and tuck them at the back of the journal before he walked out of that gallery for the last time. The way his hands had packed the real artifacts into their case and carried them down into the dark. She stood there holding the edges of a ruined page and understood that she and her father shared a reflex: when the world threatened to take something true, they both grabbed hold and did not let go.

That was the terrible part. Not the fury — fury was familiar, fury was furniture. The terrible part was the understanding arriving underneath it, patient as groundwater.

* * *

She thought about Voss.

She thought about the helicopter, the linen suit somehow unwrinkled at four thousand meters, the smile that engaged exactly the muscles required and no others. *Exclusive publication rights. Sealed finds. No contact with any Galloway.* Clause seventeen waiting in the fine print like a scorpion under a stone. Wendell had signed anyway, telling herself it was for the crew, for the insolvent season, for Odile's mortgage and Tam's survey certification and Ravi's quietly dwindling savings. All true. But underneath the truth had run another current, one she had refused to name: she had wanted to stand in the valley where her father's life broke, and see it with her own eyes, and decide for herself what kind of man had broken there.

And now the fragments told her what kind. A man who, cornered by a rival willing to falsify data — a rival whose mentor, *H——*, whoever he was, had enough reach to decide the committee before it convened — had looked at his options and seen only bad ones, and had chosen the one that cost him everything except the truth itself. He had let the world call him a fraud rather than let the artifacts fall into Voss's hands. He had traded his name to keep the evidence clean. And then he had missed his daughter's birthday, and her mother's deathbed, and every year after, because — she read the line again, the blue ink, the frost-line of years later — *there is no version of this where I get to explain.*

No. That wasn't quite it either. She paced the length of the tent, six steps, six steps back. Because there had been versions. There had been twenty-six years of versions. He could have written. He could have come. Whatever shame or strategy had sealed him off, the choice to stay silent had been renewed daily, like a wound dressed each morning. Understanding why he had hidden the artifacts did not excuse the hiding of himself. She held both facts in her two hands and refused, with everything in her, to set either one down.

But the understanding remained, and it was terrible precisely because it fit. Voss had not destroyed Elias Galloway with a falsified photograph. She had done something more efficient: she had made him so certain that no institution could be trusted that he had entrusted the truth to dirt and silence, and silence, unlike dirt, compounds. Every year of it had made confession harder, until explanation became impossible and absence became habit and habit became a life. Wendell recognized the architecture. She had been building the same structure herself for three years, dig site to dig site, calling it freedom, mistaking distance for safety. She was her father's daughter down to the foundations. The exile she had chosen was the exile he had been sentenced to, and she had inherited it without ever seeing the deed.

Outside, Tam's voice rose in argument with one of the porters about the proper angle for a spoil heap, and Odile's deeper voice cut through both with the efficiency of a blade, and somewhere Ravi laughed at something — the crew, her improbable, unearned family, going about the ordinary business of an extraordinary lie of a season. They thought they were excavating a routine contract. They were standing on top of the answer to a scandal that had ended careers and hollowed a family, and the woman who had hired them intended to keep it that way.

* * *

Wendell sat back down. She finished the reconstruction as the light went amber and then gray, fitting the last fragments by feel more than sight, and when she was done she had ninety percent of a page and one hundred percent of its meaning.

The lower gallery. Behind the collapse. Recorded nowhere except this journal — which meant Voss did not know. Voss believed the museum crates held whatever Elias had ever found; she had bought the excavation of Wadi al-Nakhl for reasons Wendell was only beginning to map, and if she learned that the original cache existed, that it was reachable, that it sat in the dark with inscriptions intact enough to hang her mentor's fraud around her own neck —

Wendell looked at the last line her father had written in blue. *Come and take it back.*

"I'm here," she said aloud, to the empty tent, to the valley, to a man she hadn't spoken to in three years and hadn't forgiven in twenty. Her voice sounded strange in the canvas hush, smaller than she expected. "I'm here, and I found it, and I don't forgive you yet. But I understand."

Understanding, she was learning, was worse. Forgiveness would have been a door closing. Understanding was a door opening onto a room she would now have to live in.

She folded the reconstructed page into a folder, wrapped the folder in oilcloth, and was weighing whether to hide it in the bottom of her instrument case when she heard footsteps outside — quick ones, Tam's rhythm, too fast for ordinary news. The flap opened and Tam stood silhouetted against the dusk, chest working, and the look on his face was one Wendell had learned to read across a hundred dig sites and hoped never to see again.

"Odile sent me," Tam said. "A porter came back early from the supply run. He says there's a car at the pass road. Two men, asking questions." He swallowed. "They asked who'd been in the side gallery."

Behind him, far off toward the valley mouth, headlights swept the cliff face once, briefly, like a searchlight testing the dark — and went out.
