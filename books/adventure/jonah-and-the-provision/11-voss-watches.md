## 11. Voss Watches

Marguerite Voss had learned long ago that the desert told on itself, if you knew how to listen. Wind moved sand the way a tongue moves over a wound — compulsively, revealingly — and every morning the wadi wore a new face that had to be read before breakfast, before the light flattened it into ordinariness. She stood at the mouth of her command tent with coffee going cold in her hand and read the valley the way other women read letters from lovers: hungrily, suspiciously, for what was hidden between the lines.

The trench grid below her had changed overnight.

Not much. A layman would have seen nothing at all — the same pale scars in the wadi floor, the same patient geometry of string and stake. But Marguerite had spent forty years teaching herself to see the second story beneath the first, and the second story this morning said: *someone worked here after dark.*

The tailings pile beside Trench Four had grown by perhaps two cubic meters. The wheelbarrow tracks were wrong — too deep for the heat-softened ground of midday, cut crisp by night cold. And the tarps. Someone had re-laid the tarps over the eastern gallery, and they had done it well, but not perfectly; one corner lifted now in the dawn wind with a small insistent flutter, like a finger beckoning.

She drank her coffee and let none of this reach her face. Below, the crew was stirring — Odile's voice carrying up the slope in its flat, unhurried way, Tam's laugh answering it, high and nervous as ever. And Galloway. Wendell Galloway, moving through the site with that strange economy of hers, touching nothing she didn't intend to touch, looking at nothing she didn't mean to look at. The girl was good. Marguerite had known she would be good; she'd hired her precisely because she was good, and because she was Elias Galloway's daughter, and because a woman who wanted something buried would dig toward it faster than any stranger could.

But good was not the same as controllable. Good, in Marguerite's experience, was usually the opposite of controllable.

* * *

The porter came to her tent at noon, when the camp slept off the worst of the heat and even the wind lay down among the stones. His name was Yusuf, and he had been hers since the second week — a modest stipend deposited monthly, a modest loyalty purchased more cheaply than any instrument in her kit. He crouched at the tent flap and spoke without entering, eyes down, voice pitched under the hiss of the canvas.

"The surveyor," he said. "Tam. He asked me last week about the old galleries. Which ones were safe to enter alone."

Marguerite set down her cup. "And?"

"And last night he did not sleep in his tent. I counted the tents at midnight. Eleven. At four o'clock there were twelve." Yusuf's shoulders moved in something too dry to be a shrug. "Also, madam, the foreman drew extra lamp oil from stores three days running. She logged it as trench lighting. The trenches do not need so much."

"Anything else?"

He hesitated, and hesitation from Yusuf was worth more than speech from most men. "The Galloway woman washed her hands at the water point past midnight. Her sleeves were wet to the shoulder. There is dust in the wadi that stains yellow — you have seen it on the cliffs. It does not wash out quickly." He looked up then, briefly, and his eyes held the particular satisfaction of a man who has delivered exactly what was paid for. "Her cuffs were still yellow this morning."

Marguerite dismissed him with a nod and sat alone in the dim green shade of the tent, listening to the canvas breathe, arranging the facts like shards on a sorting table.

A hidden gallery entered by night. Extra lamp oil drawn quietly. Yellow dust on the arms of Wendell Galloway.

There was only one place in the wadi where the yellow staining ran deep enough to mark a person to the shoulder: the collapsed side galleries along the northern scarp, the ones Elias Galloway himself had opened and abandoned in '98, the ones Marguerite had walked through twenty years ago with a headlamp and a heart full of vindication and found — nothing. Nothing but rubble and bat droppings and the great mocking silence of a valley that had swallowed her rival's career whole.

Unless there had been something after all. Unless Elias had found it, and hidden it, and let the world call him a fraud rather than give it up.

Her hands were not quite steady when she reached for the field ledger. She noticed this, and disliked noticing it, and made them steady by force of will, the way she made everything steady.

* * *

She spent the afternoon being charming, which was the hardest work she did.

She walked the trenches in her pressed khakis and her wide-brimmed hat, complimenting Odile's shoring work, asking Tam earnest questions about his auger readings, standing beside Ravi while he repacked a med kit and telling him a story about a dig in Oman where the medic had saved a man's fingers with nothing but gauze and audacity. She laughed in the right places. She touched shoulders. She was, she knew from long practice, wonderful — warm without intimacy, generous without cost, a woman everyone liked and no one knew.

All the while she watched Wendell.

The girl was cataloguing potsherds at the main grid, and she was doing it correctly, methodically, with the unshowy competence that Marguerite had privately graded as excellent. But there was a quality to her attention today that hadn't been there before — a stillness at the center of her motion, the stillness of a person who has found what she was looking for and is now performing the not-finding of it. Marguerite recognized the performance because she had invented half its vocabulary. You didn't survive forty years in this profession by believing people's faces. You survived by watching their hands.

Wendell's hands were careful. Too careful. They handled each sherd like an alibi.

"Ms. Galloway." Marguerite crouched beside her in the trench, letting her shadow fall across the sorting tray, casual as weather. "How are we coming? Anything interesting turning up?"

"Ordinary ware," Wendell said, not looking up. "Domestic stratum. It'll date the occupation nicely."

"It will indeed." Marguerite smiled at the top of her bent head. "You know, your father had a gift for exactly this — reading a stratum like a page. I used to tell him he wasted it on stubbornness." She watched the girl's shoulders set, a millimeter's change, tectonic if you knew how to feel for it. "You have his eye. I hope you also have his patience."

"I have my own," Wendell said pleasantly, and turned another sherd, and gave Marguerite nothing else.

That evening Marguerite climbed alone to the ridge above the northern scarp and looked down at the collapsed galleries until the light failed. Somewhere under that jumble of stone — or somewhere beyond it, in some chamber the collapse had sealed rather than revealed — Elias Galloway had put the truth of Wadi al-Nakhl away like a letter he couldn't bear to send. Twenty years it had waited. Twenty years of disgrace, of the name Galloway spoken in conferences with a curl of the lip, of Marguerite's own career built brick by patient brick on the certainty that she had been right about him all along.

And now his daughter was digging it out by lamplight, quietly, secretly, behind tarps and false logs and a surveyor who couldn't lie without sweating.

Marguerite stood in the blue dusk and felt the two halves of herself pull against each other like men hauling opposite ends of a rope. The first half was cold and delighted: *so the find is real.* Everything she had suspected, everything she had never been able to prove — real, intact, waiting. The second half was colder still and not delighted at all, because the first half's delight assumed she would be the one holding it, and the facts on the table no longer guaranteed anything of the kind.

Wendell Galloway had signed Clause Seventeen. Exclusive publication rights. Sealed finds. No contact with any Galloway. Marguerite had written those clauses herself, in her own hand, with a fountain pen she kept for contracts the way other people kept knives for enemies. The paper should have been enough. Paper was always enough; she had built her life on the sufficiency of paper.

But paper governed finds. It did not govern knowledge. If Wendell already knew what was in that chamber — if she had read whatever Elias had left there, journals, inscriptions, proof — then the contract was a fence around a field the horse had already left. Marguerite could seize crates. She could not seize certainty, once it had entered another mind. And certainty, in the wrong mind, was a bomb with a long fuse and no return address.

She turned back toward camp, and the panic came up in her then, quiet and total, the way floodwater comes up a wadi — not suddenly, but all at once, everywhere, having traveled a long way out of sight.

* * *

She worked through the night by lamplight, and by morning the new timeline lay on her folding table like a diagnosis.

The original plan had been elegant in its patience: a full season of excavation under her permits, the significant material crated and shipped in her name at season's end, publication sequenced over eighteen months so that each announcement landed like a step downstairs — the discovery, the confirmation, the reinterpretation of the '98 affair, and finally, crowning it, the rehabilitation of the site's true history with Marguerite Voss's name on the spine of every volume. The Galloway name would appear nowhere. Not in acknowledgments, not in footnotes, not in the dusty appendix of contested prior claims. History was written by whoever filed first and paid the typesetter, and Marguerite had always intended to file first.

Patience, however, was a luxury priced in risk, and the risk had just repriced itself.

She drew a line through the calendar. New schedule: full stripping of the accessible chambers within ten days. Crating under her personal supervision — no porters, no crew hands beyond the two she trusted least and therefore watched most. Shipment out on the pass road the moment the trucks could run, under her institute's export permits, which she had, with characteristic foresight, arranged months ago in duplicate. And the narrative — the narrative would go out ahead of the artifacts, a preliminary announcement wired to three journals simultaneously, establishing priority of claim so thoroughly that any later protest would sound like jealousy wearing scholarship's clothes.

As for Wendell Galloway —

Marguerite paused over that part of the plan, pen hovering.

The girl would have to be managed. Not destroyed — destruction invited scrutiny, and scrutiny was the one commodity Marguerite had spent her whole career refusing to purchase. Managed. Given a graceful exit: a penalty clause invoked, a license threatened, a settlement offered quietly, a career permitted to continue in the small provincial digs where small provincial careers went to be comfortable. The girl would hate her. Girls like that always hated her. But hatred faded, and precedence did not. In thirty years, when the textbooks were printed, no one would remember there had ever been a question, let alone a daughter with yellow dust on her sleeves.

It was a good plan. It was, she told herself, examining it from every angle the way she examined stratigraphy, airtight.

So why did her hand keep drifting back to the clause she had almost not written — Seventeen, subsection four, the penalty schedule — and why did the numbers there look smaller tonight than they had in her office in London, where everything looked smaller?

Because of the journal. That was the answer, and she made herself look at it straight. Because somewhere in that chamber, if Elias Galloway had been half the archivist he was an archaeologist, there was documentation. Dates, signatures, correspondence — and Marguerite knew what might be in that correspondence, because she knew what she herself had sent him in the winter of '97, and what her mentor had sent, and what had passed between the three of them in the poisoned year before the scandal broke. If Elias had kept the letters — and men like Elias always kept the letters; it was their vanity and their undoing — then the crate Marguerite planned to ship contained not just her vindication but her indictment, packed in excelsior, addressed in her own hand.

She could not ship it. She could not leave it. She could not burn it without explaining why it was gone.

For the first time in twenty years, Marguerite Voss understood what it must have been like to be Elias Galloway: sitting on the truth of a valley, discovering that the truth had teeth on both ends.

She laughed once, short and without humor, and the sound died immediately in the canvas dark.

* * *

At dawn she summoned Odile to the command tent and laid out the accelerated schedule with the bright briskness she used for orders dressed as enthusiasm. Full excavation push. Extended hours. Bonus payments for the pace. She watched the foreman's broad, unreadable face take it in, watched the small delay before the nod — Odile delayed before everything, a woman who weighed her words like ballast — and catalogued the delay as either caution or complicity and could not yet tell which.

"Of course, Dr. Voss," Odile said. "The crew will want to know why the rush."

"The pass road closes early this year. Weather." Marguerite spread her hands, rueful, reasonable. "Science keeps its own clock, but the mountains don't consult it."

"No," Odile agreed. "They don't." And she went out to rouse the crew, and Marguerite stood at the tent flap and watched her go, and watched her stop first at the survey tent, where Wendell Galloway was rolling maps, and say something brief that made both women look — just for a moment, just a flicker — toward the command tent.

Toward her.

Marguerite smiled to herself in the grey morning light, and the smile had nothing warm in it anywhere.

Let them look. Let them whisper over their lamp oil and their yellow dust. She had stripped sites before; she had buried careers before, including, though the world had never proven it, a piece of one that wasn't hers to bury. She had a timeline now, trucks on retainer, permits in triplicate, and a valley full of proof that would carry her name out of it or not carry anyone's at all.

Below her, the crew gathered at the trenches, and Wendell Galloway drove the day's first stake into the wadi floor with one clean blow, and Marguerite thought, with a clarity that felt almost like calm:

*Nine days. Whatever you've found, little Galloway, you have nine days to decide what you're willing to lose to keep it — and I have already decided.*

She turned back inside and began, in her precise fountain-pen hand, to write the wire to the journals.
