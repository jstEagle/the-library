## 12. Night Survey

Tam had never been good at waiting in the dark. Waiting in the dark *alone*, with a rifle-slit of stars overhead and the wadi breathing around him like something with lungs, was a different order of suffering entirely.

He crouched at the mouth of the side gallery where it branched off the main trench, a red-lensed torch cupped in his palm, and counted his heartbeats the way Odile had taught him to count rope lengths — steady, deliberate, one for each thing that could go wrong. Voss's security men made their rounds on the half hour. The last set of boots had passed eleven minutes ago, crunching east along the rim toward the generator tent. That gave him nineteen minutes before the next pass, and Wendell had told him — quietly, in the blue hour before full dark, her voice stripped down to wire — *nineteen minutes is enough if nothing goes wrong.*

Nothing ever went wrong only in other people's stories.

The wind moved through Wadi al-Nakhl the way water moves through a throat: low, continuous, shaped by stone. By day it carried heat and grit; by night it turned cold and mineral, smelling of dust that had never once known rain. Tam pressed his back against the gallery wall and felt the rock's chill seep through his shirt into his spine. His grandmother used to say that some places kept their dead close to the surface, that you could feel them the way you felt a draft under a door. He'd laughed when he was fourteen. He was not laughing now.

Above him, the camp slept under false pretenses. Voss's tents glowed faintly with banked lanterns; the hired men dozed in shifts around a fire pit they were not supposed to have. And somewhere below the dated stratum, below the layer of ordinary shards and unremarkable sand that the contract said was all this valley contained, Wendell Galloway was moving stone by hand.

* * *

She'd come to his tent two hours after midnight, no lamp, just her silhouette against the starlight and her voice pitched so low he had to lean out of his cot to hear it.

"Tam. I need your eyes and I need your silence, and I need both tonight."

He'd known, of course. He'd known since the auger struck cut stone three weeks ago, since the moment his hands went numb on the shaft and Wendell had looked at the core sample and then at the sky and then at nothing at all. He'd known since Ravi started rationing words instead of just water, since Odile began sleeping with her boots on. Secrets in a dig camp are like groundwater — everyone can taste them.

But knowing and being asked were different animals. Being asked meant complicity. Being asked meant that whatever came next would have his fingerprints on it too.

"She'll lock the site," Wendell had said. "Clause seventeen or clause nine or whichever number she reaches for first. Once she seals the lower galleries, we never get another look. Neither does anyone else." A pause, and then, quieter, the part that wasn't strategy: "I need to know what's down there before she decides it doesn't exist."

Tam had thought about his surveyor's license. He'd thought about the money still owed to him from an insolvent season, about the letter his mother sent every month asking when he was coming home to take a respectable job. He'd thought about all of it for perhaps four seconds.

"What do you need me to watch?"

"The north approach. If anyone comes, you cough twice and kill your light."

"And if they don't stop coming?"

Wendell had looked at him then, and in the dark her face was all planes and shadow, and there was something in it he hadn't seen before — not the flat competence she wore like armor, but something rawer underneath, something that looked almost like fear wearing courage's clothes.

"Then you run," she said. "You don't warn me. You run, and you let them find me alone, and you tell Odile everything."

"I'm not going to do that."

"You're going to promise me anyway."

He hadn't promised. She hadn't pushed. Some agreements are made in the space between what's demanded and what's given.

* * *

Now he waited, and the wadi waited with him, and his nineteen minutes bled away into seventeen into fifteen.

The stars here were merciless — no city glow to soften them, just the whole cold machinery of the sky laid bare. Tam had grown up believing the stars were witnesses. Tonight he wished they'd look elsewhere.

At minute twelve, he heard it: the scrape of stone against stone, muffled by cloth — Wendell had wrapped her pry bar in canvas, he realized, had planned even the sound of her work — rising from the depths of the gallery like something exhaling. Then silence. Then a soft double-tap, their signal: *clear, come down.*

He killed his torch and descended.

The gallery swallowed light the way the desert swallowed sound. He felt his way along guide-ropes Wendell had strung that afternoon, hand over hand, the walls narrowing until his shoulders brushed stone on both sides. The air changed as he went deeper — cooler, denser, carrying a sweetness under the mineral smell, the ghost of something organic that had no business surviving twenty years underground. Beeswax, maybe. Or resin. Something that had been sealed away from time and was now, slowly, meeting it again.

He found her in a chamber no bigger than his grandmother's kitchen, kneeling before a wall of rubble that was no longer rubble. She'd cleared a gap maybe sixty centimeters wide, and through it — his red lens found it first as color, then as shape — a sealed doorway. Cut stone, dressed and fitted, older than anything Elias Galloway had been accused of faking, older than the stratum above it by centuries according to every textbook Tam had memorized to earn his license.

And on the lintel above the doorway, incised deep enough to survive the fall of empires: writing.

"It's a cache chamber," Wendell said. Her voice came out strange, flattened, as though she were reading someone else's words. "Sealed deliberately. Not abandoned — *sealed*. There's a difference. Abandonment is accident. This is intent."

Tam crouched beside her, and his light joined hers, and together the two small red circles fell across the inscription.

That was when he saw her hands.

They were shaking. Not the fine tremor of cold or exertion — he knew that tremor intimately, lived inside it most nights — but a deeper oscillation, the kind that comes up from somewhere below the muscle. Wendell Galloway, who set stakes in gale winds without a waver, whose hands were the steadiest thing in any trench she'd ever worked, sat with her palms open on her thighs and watched them shake as though they belonged to a stranger.

"Tamsin," she said — his full name, which she never used, which meant the moment had gotten too large for nicknames — "read it to me. My eyes aren't—" She stopped. Started again. "Read it aloud. Please."

He read. His classical script was serviceable, his pronunciation probably criminal, but the words came clear enough: a dedication, a name, a lineage of names, a record of things placed here for keeping — vessels, tablets, seals — *so that what was true might outlast those who would call it false.*

The last line stopped his tongue.

*So that what was true might outlast those who would call it false.* Someone had carved that into stone thousands of years ago, and it might as well have been written yesterday, in English, addressed by name.

Beside him, Wendell made a sound.

It was small. It was barely anything — a caught breath, a fracture in the silence no wider than a hairline crack in pottery. But in that chamber it was enormous, because Tam had worked beside this woman for two seasons and he had never once heard her make a sound like that. Not when the funding collapsed. Not when the storm took the north tents. Not when Voss's helicopter had landed and Wendell had signed away her rights with a face like closed shutters.

She was crying.

Not elegantly, not the way people cry when they know they're being watched — there was no dignity in it at all. Her shoulders had curled inward, her forehead nearly touching her knees, and the sobs came out of her in a rhythm that sounded less like grief than like something being *excavated*, layer by layer, after years of careful burial. Her hand rose to the wall — not to the inscription, but to a spot beside it, low, near the floor, where the plaster was newer.

Where someone had written in charcoal, in a quick sure hand Tam recognized from a hundred site reports:

*E.G. — verified 14 March 1998. They will say otherwise. Leave it for whoever comes honest.*

Elias Galloway's handwriting. On the wall of the chamber he'd sworn off forever. Left like a message in a bottle, like a prayer, like an apology addressed to a future he couldn't have imagined would include his own daughter kneeling in front of it with a red torch and her whole body shaking.

"He knew," Wendell said, and the words came out broken, wet, barely words at all. "He *knew*, Tam. He found this. He stood exactly here. And he—" Her fist pressed against the charcoal letters, careful even now, careful always, as though the wall might bruise. "He hid it. He sealed it back up and he let them destroy him rather than lead Voss to it. He chose this. He chose twenty years of everyone calling him a fraud, and my mother dying thinking—"

She couldn't finish. Tam didn't need her to. The shape of it was plain: a father who had protected the truth the only way left to him, and lost everything except the truth itself, and left it here anyway — *for whoever comes honest* — never dreaming that honesty would arrive wearing his own stubborn jaw.

Tam did the only thing he knew how to do. He didn't speak. He didn't touch her. He sat down in the cold and the dust beside her, shoulder to shoulder, and held his little red light steady on the charcoal words so that neither of them had to look away, and he let her cry the way you let a flooded wadi run — not stopping it, not redirecting it, just staying out of its path until it spent itself.

The wind moved far above them. Somewhere up on the rim, a jackal gave its one long note and received no answer.

When Wendell finally spoke again, her voice was scraped clean, quiet, level in the way of things that have broken and been reassembled along new lines.

"We open it tonight," she said. "Before dawn. Before her next inspection. Everything that comes out of this chamber gets photographed, catalogued, wrapped, and hidden where her porters will never find it. Every mark, every seal, every scrap of context recorded twice." She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, an efficient, almost angry gesture, and when she turned to him her eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely clear. "Voss wants this valley to forget. So we're going to be its memory."

Tam nodded. His terror was still there — it would be there all night, sitting in his chest like a stone he'd swallowed — but beneath it, unexpected and immovable, something else had settled. Devotion, maybe. Or its twin, purpose. The distinction hardly mattered. Both of them meant the same thing: that when the boots came crunching along the rim, when the lights swept the trenches, when Voss's lawyers descended with their clauses and their penalties, he would not run. She had asked him to promise, and he had refused, and now the refusal hardened into fact.

"I'll get the crates from the equipment cache," he said. "Odile's foam padding. Ravi's instrument wraps. We can move it all before the four o'clock round."

Wendell looked at him for a long moment. In the red light her face was unreadable and completely legible at once.

"Thank you," she said, and it came out rough, as though the words had been stored a long time in a dry place.

* * *

They worked until the stars began to pale.

The doorway yielded to patience rather than force — Wendell insisted on that, whispering, *we do this right or we don't do it at all* — and when the final fitted stone came free, the breath of the sealed chamber washed over them: cool, dry, sweet with resin, air that had waited decades beyond its allotted span. Inside, shapes emerged from the dark in layers. Sealed jars with intact stamp impressions. Tablets wrapped in oiled leather. A bronze mirror green with age. And beneath them all, nested in a bed of woven matting, a second journal — Elias Galloway's hand again, this one complete, its cover stiff with mineral crust.

Wendell lifted it with both hands, the way you lift a sleeping child, and held it against her chest, and closed her eyes.

Tam photographed everything. Twice. His hands, he noticed distantly, had stopped shaking the moment there was work to do.

Far above, the eastern sky turned the color of an old scar, and down the wadi, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of a helicopter — early, much too early, rotors beating against the dawn like a fist on a door.

Wendell opened her eyes.

"She's here," she said.
