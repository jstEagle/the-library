## 13. The Sealed Chamber

The stone came away at 3:47 in the morning, and it did not groan the way Tam had promised it would. It sighed.

Wendell felt the sound through her palms more than she heard it — a long exhalation of trapped air, forty centuries old, escaping through the seam like a held breath finally released. The slab tilted inward a hand's width. Dust breathed out past them, fine and pale in the lamplight, carrying a smell that no one in the canyon had smelled in living memory: cedar oil, bitumen, something floral and gone to resin, the ghost of a funeral that had happened before iron was common in this country.

Nobody spoke. That was the discipline of it — Odile's discipline, drilled into all of them across four seasons and two continents: *you do not cheer at a door until you know what's behind it.* So they stood in the narrow gallery with their headlamps dimmed to embers and let the chamber exhale against their faces, and Wendell thought, absurdly, of her mother's hospital room in the last hour, how the whole family had gone quiet in the same way, as if loudness might disturb whatever was happening at the edge of things.

"Air's moving," Ravi murmured behind her. "Slow exchange. It's been sealed, but not perfectly. There's a fissure somewhere — probably why your father found the gallery at all."

*Your father.* Ravi said it without weight, the way he said everything medical and true, and Wendell let it pass because there was no time to flinch from it. Not tonight. Tonight the night was theirs; Voss's security detail rotated at five, and the porter who sold information to Marguerite Voss for the price of a new motorcycle was asleep in the laborers' tent, dreaming his transactional dreams. They had perhaps seventy minutes.

"Tam," Wendell said. "The props."

Tam had built them himself over three days of pretending to repair survey markers — wooden A-frames padded with rubber from a tire inner tube, sized to the millimeter from the laser scan. He passed them forward one by one, hands steady now, though Wendell remembered the boy who'd thrown up behind the water truck during his first trench collapse drill two years ago. People changed in the dark. She was counting on it.

They worked the slab open in increments. Ten centimeters. Twenty. The gap widened and the smell deepened, richer now, and beneath the resin something else — salt, maybe, or the mineral breath of limestone that had never seen sun. Odile fed the fiber-optic scope through first, angling it low, and Wendell watched the handheld monitor with her heart going so hard she could feel it in her teeth.

Gray nothing. Then gray resolving into floor. Then floor resolving into *order.*

"Oh," Tam said softly. Just that. *Oh.* Like a man seeing the sea for the first time.

* * *

They gave the air another twenty minutes. Ravi insisted — particulates, oxygen fraction, the whole litany he'd recited before every confined entry since the incident in Nubia that none of them talked about. Then Wendell went first, because it was her name on the risk assessment she'd forged three days ago and because, though she'd have died before saying it aloud, she wanted to be alone with it for ten seconds. Just ten. Before anyone else's breathing joined hers.

She dropped through the gap onto a ledge of dressed stone and stood very still.

The chamber was not large. That was the first thing. All her life she'd imagined her father's lost find as something vast — a tomb, a complex, a city block of buried grandeur big enough to justify the ruin it made of him. But this was modest. Maybe six meters by nine, a single room cut into the wadi's deep limestone, its ceiling black with ancient soot where lamps had once burned. What it lacked in scale it made in *integrity*. Nothing here had collapsed. Nothing here had flooded. The fissure that had admitted air had admitted nothing else, and so the room had simply waited, patient as a letter in a drawer, for someone to come read it.

Niche shelves lined the walls, cut with a precision that made her fingertips ache. On them, wrapped in linen gone the color of weak tea, sat vessels. Dozens of them. Alabaster with the translucence of frozen honey. Basalt ground so fine the lamplight slid off it. A set of four canopic-style jars whose lids were carved not with the faces she'd been taught to expect but with sigils — the same sigils, her mind supplied instantly, that appeared on the fragment Elias had produced in 1998 and been destroyed for producing. The fragment everyone had called too clean, too convenient, too *conveniently Galloway*.

It hadn't been too clean. It had been *real*, and it had come from here, and here it still was, in rows, in context, in situ, with the dust of millennia undisturbed around their bases. You could not fake dust. You could not fake the way a vessel sits in the floor matrix, its own weight pressing its own shadow into the plaster over four thousand years. Any first-year student could see it. Any hostile reviewer with an agenda could see it too, which was the point, which had always been the point.

Wendell crouched at the edge of the ledge and put her hand flat against the stone and did not move for a long time.

Behind her, faintly, she heard the others come through — Odile's boots deliberate, Ravi's soft, Tam barely breathing. Nobody rushed her. That was the other thing about her crew, the thing she'd never earned and never stopped being grateful for: they understood when a silence belonged to someone.

"The wall," Odile said at last, quietly, nodding toward the far side. "Boss. Look at the wall."

* * *

The inscription ran the length of the eastern wall in sunk relief, half a meter high, executed by a hand that had known what it was doing. Wendell crossed the chamber slowly, lamp raised, reading as she went — not translating, not yet, translation would take weeks and proper light and probably Tam's cousin at the university who owed him favors — but *reading*, the way you can read a face before you know what the person is saying. Titles. Names. Dates in a regnal sequence that made her knees go strange.

And then a passage, longer than the rest, that mentioned a dispute. An accusation. A weighing of evidence before some council of priests or officials, rendered in the careful formulaic language of official judgment — and then a verdict. The verdict named a man. The verdict cleared him. And the verdict recorded, with bureaucratic finality, the disposition of the objects in this room: sealed, witnessed, hidden, *so that no false hand should claim them.*

Her father had spent twenty years unable to prove his find was genuine. The proof had been sitting thirty meters below the wadi floor the entire time, carved in stone, addressed — it felt like — directly to whoever finally dug far enough. *You doubted him. Here is the record. He told the truth.*

Wendell became aware that she was crying only when a drop hit the back of her own hand, warm in the cold air of the chamber. She didn't wipe her face. There was no one here she needed to perform for, and that realization — that for once, in the most important moment of her professional life, she was not alone and did not have to be invisible inside herself — broke something loose that she'd have sworn was load-bearing.

Tam pretended to study the jars. Ravi studied his monitor. Odile looked at her the way Odile looked at everything: straight on, without mercy, without judgment either.

"There's more," Wendell said, when she could trust her voice. "Look at the lower register. The second inscription."

* * *

The second text was older than the first — cruder, re-cut, carved over an erasure. Someone had chiseled away an original passage and replaced it, and the replacement told a different story: an account of objects removed from a treasury under false inventory, of a scribe who falsified seals, of restitution demanded. And beside the re-cut text, incised smaller, almost as an afterthought or a curse, was a list of names. Officials. Keepers. Men entrusted with verification.

One of the names matched, glyph for glyph, a cartouche fragment published in 1974 — in a monograph co-authored by a young scholar named Armand Deverell, Marguerite Voss's mentor, the man whose authoritative dismissal of Elias Galloway's find had carried more weight than any physical evidence, the man whose reputation had been the wall her father broke against.

Wendell stood before it with her lamp held high and felt the shape of the thing settle over her, cold and complete. It wasn't just vindication. It was inversion. The fraud had happened — decades ago, in a different excavation, by different hands — and the man who'd built his career policing everyone else's integrity had signed his own name, in his own publication, next to a name the stone called a falsifier. Either he'd never checked his own source material, or he had, and knew exactly what he was burying when he buried Elias.

Either way, Marguerite Voss had inherited the lie the way you inherit a house: furnished, mortgaged, and haunted.

"She knows," Wendell said. Her voice came out flat. "Or she suspects. That clause forbidding contact with any Galloway — that wasn't legal caution. That was a woman making sure the one person alive who might dig in the right place never got near it." She turned to look at her crew, three faces in lamplight, and heard herself say the rest of it, the part she'd been carrying since the letter arrived: "I didn't take this job to excavate. I took it to control what got found. I've known what she is since before we crossed the border."

Odile nodded slowly, unsurprised, which somehow hurt more than anger would have.

"And now?" Odile asked.

Wendell looked around the chamber — at the jars in their rows, at the verdict carved in stone, at the erased and rewritten truth that powerful men had been managing for three thousand years. Her father's handwriting was waiting in a journal in her tent. This room was the journal's witness. Together they were a case no symposium could wave away.

"Now we document everything before dawn," she said. "Photogrammetry, full coverage, twice. Copies go three separate places tonight — Tam's encrypted drive, Ravi's med kit false bottom, my jacket. No single person carries the whole truth." She paused. "Because starting tomorrow morning, Marguerite Voss is going to fight for this site like it's her own name buried in that wall. And it is."

Above them, faint through the fissure, came the first thin sound of wind rising along the canyon rim — the dry season clearing its throat.

They had six days.
