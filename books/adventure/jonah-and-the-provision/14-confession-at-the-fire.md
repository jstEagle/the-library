## 14. Confession at the Fire

Odile Marchand had learned to read fires the way other people read faces.

Twenty-two years of fieldwork taught her that a campfire was never just heat and light. It was a barometer of the crew. A fire that burned low and tidy meant a team that trusted each other enough to sleep. A fire fed too often, too anxiously, meant someone was lying awake with something they couldn't say. And a fire built high and bright on a night when nobody had asked for one — that meant someone wanted an audience.

Tonight's fire was the third kind.

Wendell had stacked it herself, after dinner, without explanation. She'd carried up three times the usual deadfall from the wadi's edge — greasewood and the pale ribs of long-dead acacia — and she'd built it the way she did everything: precisely, quietly, as if the wood might object to being handled carelessly. Now it stood taller than any fire Odile had seen in four seasons of working with her, throwing light halfway up the canyon wall, turning the sandstone the color of old blood.

The others had noticed too. Tam sat closest to it, knees drawn up, sketchbook forgotten in his lap. Ravi leaned against the water trailer with his arms crossed, watching Wendell over the rim of his tea tin with the particular stillness he used when assessing a patient who hadn't yet admitted what was wrong.

Odile took the camp stool she always took — the one with the mended leg, positioned where she could see both the fire and the dark mouth of the side gallery above the camp. The gallery where Wendell had spent three nights doing something she hadn't explained. Where crates now sat wrapped in canvas, heavier than their size suggested, guarded by a woman who slept beside them like a dog beside a wound.

"Wendell," Odile said, because someone had to.

"I know." Wendell didn't look up from the fire. She was feeding it another branch, holding it until the flame caught, watching it burn. "I'm getting there."

"Take your time," Ravi said mildly. "We've got nowhere to be except exactly here."

But they all knew that wasn't true anymore. Voss's security detail had arrived two days ago — six men in matching khaki who patrolled the trench lines with radios and didn't smile. The site was locked down under Clause Seventeen. Their permits hung by a thread. Whatever Wendell was carrying toward this fire, it had been gaining weight for days, and Odile could see it pressing on her shoulders like sediment.

* * *

Odile had hired her in a bus depot café in Marsabit, three years ago, off nothing but a handshake and a demonstration.

The demonstration had been this: Wendell had picked up a handful of soil from the café's cracked parking lot — dust, gravel, oil-stained nothing — and told Odile there was a buried culvert two meters down running east-northeast, probably colonial-era, probably concrete, and that the ground above it subsided in a way you could feel through your boots if you walked the right line twice. Odile had laughed. Then she'd borrowed a shovel from the café owner, mostly for the joke, and dug.

Two meters. Concrete. East-northeast.

She'd asked Wendell where she'd trained. Wendell had said, "Around." She'd asked for references. Wendell had said, "I don't use my real name on paper, if that's a problem." It should have been a problem. Odile had run enough crews to know that a person without a name was a person carrying something, and people carrying things were liabilities. But she'd looked at those hands — scarred, sure, moving over soil like they were reading braille — and she'd hired her anyway, and in three years she had never once regretted it, and never once stopped wondering.

Because Wendell Galloway — though Odile wouldn't learn that name until tonight — was the best field archaeologist Odile had ever worked with, and she worked like a woman serving a sentence. She took the jobs nobody wanted. She moved on before contracts renewed. She never gave an address, never mentioned family, never once in three years let slip a single fact about where she came from. When she drank, which was rarely, she drank alone. When the crew sang around fires like this one, she smiled and tapped her boot and gave away nothing.

Tam had once theorized she was hiding from an ex-husband. Ravi had guessed debt. Odile had guessed worse, and kept her guess to herself, because that was her job: keep the crew whole, keep the secrets that needed keeping, and know the difference between a secret that poisons and one that protects.

This one, she'd begun to suspect lately, was poisoning.

* * *

"You've all been patient," Wendell said finally. She set down the last branch and wiped her hands on her thighs, and when she lifted her face into the firelight, Odile saw that she'd made some kind of decision — the same set of the jaw Odile had seen from her a hundred times at the edge of a trench, right before a difficult cut. "And you've earned better than patience. So I'm going to tell you things I've never told anyone, and then I'm going to ask you for something, and you're allowed to say no."

"Ominous," Ravi said. "Good start."

A small sound came out of Wendell that might have been a laugh in a former life. She reached into her shirt pocket and drew out a notebook — not the waterproof survey log she carried everywhere, but an older thing, soft-cornered, its cover stained the brown of old water damage. She held it a moment without opening it, the way you hold something hot.

"This is a field journal," she said. "Dated 1998. Wadi al-Nakhl. It belonged to Elias Galloway."

The name landed in the firelight and none of them moved.

Tam's pencil, which had been hovering over his sketchbook, went still. Ravi lowered his tea. Odile felt something turn over in her chest, slow and heavy, like a stone shifting in a scree slope — because everyone in this profession knew that name, knew it the way sailors know the names of ships that went down. Elias Galloway. The man who had found a city where the maps said there was only nomad camp and wind. The man whose find had been declared fraudulent within eighteen months, whose data had been picked apart in three journals, whose career had ended not with a bang but with a long public unraveling. The cautionary tale they showed every graduate student: *this is what happens when you want it too much.*

"My father," Wendell said.

The word *father* dropped into the silence like a coin down a well. Odile listened for it to hit bottom.

"He died?" Tam asked quietly. It was the gentle guess — the boy had a gift for gentleness under all his superstitions.

"No." Wendell's voice was flat as a trowel blade. "He's alive. He lives outside Nairobi. He grows tomatoes and doesn't answer letters. We haven't spoken in eleven years." She turned the journal over in her hands. "My mother got sick the year I was nineteen. Pancreatic. Fast. My father was in the final season of the Nakhl excavation — the discovery of his life, the thing he'd chased for nine years. She asked him to come home. He said the stratigraphy was at a critical juncture. Those were his words. *Critical juncture.* She died on a Tuesday in March and he arrived on the following Saturday, and by then—" Wendell stopped. Breathed. Started again. "By then there was nothing left to say to him that either of us could have survived."

Nobody spoke. The fire cracked and settled, sending up a fistful of sparks that climbed toward the stars and died.

"The fraud charges came eight months later," she went on. "Somebody produced evidence that the inscriptions had been cut recently — tool marks, modern pigment in the incisions. The academic world ate him alive. Marguerite Voss wrote the most-cited demolition. His funding evaporated. His colleagues stopped returning calls. And I—" She looked at her hands. "I believed it. That's the part I can't get around. Everyone believed it, and I believed it hardest, because believing it explained why he'd chosen dirt over my mother. Fraud was a reason. Ambition was a reason. It made him a monster instead of just a coward, and a monster was easier to hate than a man who simply loved stones more than people."

"And now?" Ravi asked. Not pushing. Just opening the door a little wider.

"Now I've been down in that collapsed gallery for three nights." Wendell opened the journal at last, carefully, the spine crackling like a knuckle. "He hid it in a niche behind a fallen lintel, wrapped in wax cloth, along with these." She nodded toward the canvas-wrapped crates at the edge of the firelight. "His notes. His original drawings. And page after page documenting what actually happened in 1998 — including the fact that the 'modern tool marks' were planted. That the pigment analysis was doctored. That Voss's mentor, Henri Delacroix, had access to the finds during the conservation phase, and that Delacroix had spent two years trying to buy into the excavation and been refused."

Tam made a small involuntary sound. "Voss's—"

"Her teacher. Her patron. The man whose methods she inherited and whose reputation she's spent twenty years defending, because if Delacroix was the fraudster, everything she built on his foundation burns too." Wendell closed the journal. "And tonight I'm telling you the rest, because you've already figured out half of it anyway. My name isn't Wendell Marsh. It's Wendell Galloway. I changed it when I was twenty-two so that no hiring committee would ever connect me to the most famous fraud in Near Eastern archaeology. I've been digging under a false name for eleven years. Every permit I've ever signed has been signed with a lie."

There it was. Odile watched it land on each of them in turn — on Tam, whose eyes had gone wide and wet in the firelight; on Ravi, who set down his tea with the deliberate slowness of a man buying himself time; on herself, feeling her own three-year-old guess settle into place with an almost audible click.

"And the crates," Odile said. Because someone had to say it. "Those aren't survey equipment."

"No." Wendell met her eyes across the fire, and Odile saw that she wasn't flinching. Whatever this cost her, she'd decided to pay it standing up. "They're artifacts from the sealed chamber we opened on the night survey. Intact context. Original inscriptions — the ones that match Elias's drawings stroke for stroke, twenty years before anyone could have forged them. Provenance that clears him completely and hangs Delacroix with his own rope. Voss knows I have them. Clause Seventeen exists specifically to keep me from proving it. If her shipment leaves in six days, the truth leaves with it — buried, sealed, filed under her exclusive publication rights, and my father dies a fraud in every textbook ever printed."

The fire burned. Somewhere out in the dark, one of Voss's security men coughed, a small human sound that reminded them all exactly how close the walls had gotten.

"So," Wendell said. Her voice didn't shake, but Odile could hear the effort of keeping it level, the way you can hear a load-bearing beam working. "That's what you're holding. That's who you've been working for. A liar, a runaway, and the daughter of a disgraced man, sitting on stolen artifacts inside a locked-down site with forged papers and a plan I haven't even told you yet. I'm asking you to help me get this out of the valley. And I'm telling you — all of you — that if you say no, I'll take that answer, and I'll carry the rest alone, and no one will ever know you were asked. That's a promise."

* * *

The silence afterward lasted long enough for Odile to count her own heartbeats. Eleven. Twelve. Thirteen.

It was Tam who broke it, and Odile would remember this until she died: not Ravi with his steadiness, not herself with her seniority, but the boy. Twenty-three years old, who still wouldn't whistle at night because it summoned djinn, who crossed himself before every auger drop. He stood up, walked around the fire, and sat down next to Wendell on the sand close enough that their shoulders touched.

"My grandmother says a name is a debt you carry for your whole family," he said. "You've been carrying yours alone for eleven years. That's not lying. That's just heavy." He shrugged, awkward, sincere. "Also, I already knew. About the name. Since the storm."

Wendell turned to look at him. "What?"

"You talk in your sleep. During the sandstorm, feverish, after Ravi patched your ribs. You said 'Papa' and then you said 'the wadi keeps everything.'" Tam's ears went red. "I didn't tell anyone. It wasn't mine to tell."

Ravi snorted softly. "I knew about the fever. Didn't know what you said." He picked his tea back up and gestured at the crates with the tin. "For the record: I don't care whose daughter you are. I care that you've been carrying altitude sickness and a dead father's vindication in the same body for a month and told no one. That stops. Doctor's orders. You want to run contraband through flash-flood country, you'll do it with your oxygen saturation above ninety or you won't do it at all."

"That's conditional help," Wendell said.

"That's help," Ravi said. "Conditioned on you not dying. Take it or leave it."

Odile stayed on her stool a moment longer, letting the fire write its moving shadows across all their faces, and understood that she was the last gate. That was how it worked with crews — not formally, never spoken, but everyone knew the foreman's word was the keystone, and Wendell knew it too. She was watching Odile now with something that wasn't quite fear and wasn't quite hope. Something rawer. The expression of a woman who had paid eleven years in invisibility and was waiting to learn whether the currency was worthless.

Odile thought about the parking lot in Marsabit. The culvert, two meters down, called true from a handful of dust. She thought about every site Wendell had saved, every young digger Wendell had quietly corrected without humiliating, every night she'd taken last watch so someone else could sleep. She thought about what it must have cost to walk into Wadi al-Nakhl — into the valley that had eaten her family — and put a stake in its ground with shaking hands, and keep putting stakes in, day after day, for a father she claimed to hate.

People lied about big things, Odile knew. They lied about love and money and where they'd been. But nobody faked that. Nobody faked three years of that.

"Here's what happens now," Odile said, and stood up, and every head turned. "Tomorrow morning, Voss's men do their first sweep at oh-six-hundred, same as always. We're going to be very visible doing something boring — re-flagging the north trench, lots of measuring, lots of yawning. While they watch us be boring, those crates go into the empty water drums in the second truck, the ones due for refilling at the depot in Nekemte. Tam rides with them. Ravi preps a medical kit that looks like it weighs what five full drums weigh, in case anyone checks. And I—" she looked at Wendell, and let the ghost of a smile show, "—I will lie to Marguerite Voss's face, which after twenty-two years in this profession will give me enormous pleasure."

Wendell stared at her. "You're not angry."

"Angry about what? That you had a name you couldn't use?" Odile shook her head. "Wendell. *Galloway.* Listen to me. You think you bought our trust with lies and we're refunding it out of pity. That's not what this is. Trust isn't a clean record. It's what's left after you show someone the worst of it and they stay. You just showed us. We're staying." She picked up her tea, drained the cold dregs, and tossed them into the fire, where they hissed like a small applause. "Now. Tell us about this decoy manifest. And then tell us the route through the canyon, because I've walked that country and I already know you're going to say something insane about the flood channel."

Wendell blinked — twice, quickly, the way people blink when they are absolutely not crying — and then she laughed, short and startled, a sound Odile realized she had never heard from her in three years. She pulled the survey map from her pack and spread it on the sand between them all, weighting the corners with stones, and the crew leaned in around it, shoulders touching, one circle in the firelight.

"The flood channel," Wendell began, and Odile hid her smile.

Because of course it was insane. Of course it was. But the girl had read a buried culvert out of a parking lot, and she'd read her father's truth out of a collapsed gallery, and tomorrow she would try to read a way out of a valley that had swallowed her family whole — and this time, whatever the valley wanted, it would have to go through all of them first.

Above the camp, high on the canyon wall, the moon cleared the ridge and lit the wadi silver from end to end. And far to the south, Odile noticed without wanting to, the lights of Voss's compound burned steady and wakeful, like eyes that had stopped blinking.

Six days.
