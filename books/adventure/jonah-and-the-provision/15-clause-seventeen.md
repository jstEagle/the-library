## 15. Clause Seventeen

Marguerite Voss had learned long ago that power lived in paper.

Not in the ground — the ground was a liar, full of promises it never kept. Not in people — people wept, people defected, people grew consciences at inconvenient hours. But paper. Paper held its shape. Paper remembered what you told it to remember and forgot everything else. Paper could be served, filed, invoked, and enforced while you slept soundly in an air-conditioned tent with a glass of something cold sweating on the folding table beside you.

She sat now in her command tent — she had stopped calling it a field tent years ago; command was more accurate — with the contract spread before her on a board of polished walnut she'd had flown in from Nairobi, because if one was going to destroy a person, one ought to do it on a proper surface.

The document was forty-one pages of dense, elegant legalese drafted by a firm in Geneva that charged by the hour and never asked questions. She had read every clause herself, twice, before sending it to Wendell Galloway's crew, because Marguerite Voss did not sign her name to anything she had not memorized. And there, in the seventeenth clause — the one buried beneath sub-sections and cross-references like a scorpion under a flat stone — was the instrument she had built for exactly this moment.

*In the event that the Contractor, its principals, employees, or agents shall be found to have conducted unauthorized excavation, removal, documentation, or dissemination of Materials pertaining to the Site, the Client reserves the right to: (a) terminate all agreements without compensation; (b) report violations to relevant national antiquities authorities, with recommendation for revocation of professional licenses; (c) pursue recovery of all advance payments plus damages not exceeding three times the contract value.*

Three times the contract value. She had chosen the multiplier herself, sitting in her office in Cairo, feeling rather like a composer choosing a key signature. D minor, perhaps. Something grave.

The porter — Yusuf, broad-shouldered, soft-eyed, cheap — had come to her two nights ago with his hat in his hands and his conscience already sold. He'd seen them, he said. The tall surveyor boy keeping watch at the north trench after midnight, and the Galloway woman coming up out of the ground at three in the morning with dust on her knees and something wrapped in canvas slung across her back. He'd seen where they went, too. The old side gallery, the collapsed one, the one everyone had written off as rubble.

Voss had paid him triple what he asked, which was itself a lesson: always let them name their price first. Greedy men named small numbers. It was the desperate ones who understood what silence was worth.

Now she poured herself a measure of the good whisky — the Islay, not the blend she offered to visitors — and considered the situation the way she considered all situations: as a sequence of moves already made by someone who had not yet realized the game was over.

Wendell Galloway had found the cache. Of course she had. Voss had known from the first week that hiring Elias Galloway's daughter was either the stupidest decision of her career or the most necessary, and she had decided it was necessary precisely because it was dangerous. The girl had the Galloway eye — that uncanny, un-instrumented gift for reading ground, the thing that had made her father famous and then infamous. If the cache existed anywhere in that valley, Wendell would find it the way water finds a crack.

Which was why the contract existed. Which was why clause seventeen existed.

Let the girl dig. Let her find whatever Elias had hidden twenty years ago — and Voss was now quite certain something *was* hidden, because the stratigraphy reports Tam had filed showed cut stone where no cut stone should be, older than the fraud, older than the accusation, older than the whole miserable legend of Elias Galloway's forged dynasty. Let her hold it in her hands. Then Voss would take it, seal it, ship it under her own permits, publish it under her own name, and the Galloway name would be erased from the story so thoroughly that in fifty years no graduate student would bother looking it up.

That was the plan. It had always been the plan. The only variable was timing, and timing, Voss thought, turning her glass so the ice clicked against the crystal, was everything.

She pressed the intercom button on the satellite unit. "Send her in."

* * *

Wendell Galloway came into the tent the way weather comes into a valley — without knocking, without apology, changing the pressure of the air simply by arriving.

Voss watched her take the chair across the walnut board. The woman was thinner than her photographs, browner, with sun-cracked lips and hands that looked like they had been carved from the same material as the wadi walls. She wore her exhaustion openly, almost defiantly, the way some women wore jewelry. There was dust in the creases of her knuckles. Fresh dust. Night dust.

*You were down there again last night,* Voss thought, and smiled, and let the smile do its work.

"Ms. Galloway. Thank you for coming."

"You said it was about the shipment schedule." Wendell's voice was flat, unhurried. "We're behind on the north trench. If this is a lecture about productivity—"

"It's about clause seventeen."

Voss let the words sit there. She had rehearsed this moment — not the words, which were simple, but the *tempo*, which was everything. She slid the contract across the board, rotated it so it faced the other woman, and tapped one fingernail against the page. The sound was very small in the quiet tent. A tick. A clock starting.

"Read it aloud," Voss said. "I find people understand consequences better when they hear them in their own voice."

For a long moment Wendell didn't move. Voss watched her eyes move across the text — fast, unerring, the reading speed of someone who had grown up in a house full of field reports and grant applications, someone who knew exactly how documents like this were built because she'd been raised inside their wreckage. Something moved behind those eyes. Not fear. Voss had expected fear, had *planned* for fear, had budgeted for fear the way she budgeted for diesel and drinking water. What she saw instead was worse.

Calculation.

"'Unauthorized excavation,'" Wendell read, her voice level as a spirit gauge. "'Removal, documentation, or dissemination of Materials pertaining to the Site.' You want to define 'Materials.'"

"I don't think I do."

"Because your own annex defines them. Annex C, item four. 'Materials' means artifacts recovered under permit number—" Wendell recited the number from memory, and Voss felt the first hairline crack of unease open somewhere beneath her sternum, "—which covers the main gallery complex. The side galleries were excluded from the survey grid. Your own surveyor flagged them as unstable and they were struck from the work order in week two. Anything found outside the permitted grid isn't covered by your contract at all. It falls under national antiquities law, which means it belongs to the state, which means—" she looked up, and her eyes were the color of the wadi at noon, dry and bright and utterly without mercy, "—you have no claim. You have a penalty clause aimed at nothing."

The tent was very quiet. Outside, the wind worried at the guy-lines, a low persistent moan, the valley's constant throat-sound. Somewhere a generator coughed and steadied.

Voss recovered the way she always recovered: by smiling wider. "How interesting. You've read the annexes. Most contractors don't."

"My father taught me to read contracts before he taught me to drive."

"Your father," Voss said softly, "taught you many useful things, I imagine. Though I notice he neglected to teach you the most important one." She leaned back in her chair and laced her fingers over her stomach, settling into the posture she used for closing arguments. "Because here is what you seem to have missed, Ms. Galloway. Clause seventeen doesn't require me to prove anything. It requires me to *allege*. Read the language again. 'Found to have conducted.' Found by whom? By me. My determination triggers my remedies. The burden then shifts to *you* — to appeal, to arbitrate, to litigate, in jurisdictions of my choosing, on timelines of my choosing, while your crew sits idle on a site I've locked down and your season rots around you."

She saw it land. She saw it land exactly where she'd aimed it — not at Wendell, who might absorb a blow like that and keep walking, but outward, at the faces behind her. Odile with her foreman's license and her mortgage in Marseilles. Young Tam, whose certification was barely a year old, whose entire future fit inside a laminated card. Ravi, whose medical registration crossed three borders and could be questioned at any one of them. Voss had done her homework. She always did her homework. People imagined she was ruthless, but ruthlessness was merely the visible half of thoroughness.

"Your crew signed individually," Voss continued, gently now, almost kindly, because cruelty was most effective when it wore a nurse's expression. "Each of them carries personal liability under section seventeen-b. Licenses revoked, reported to four separate national boards. Do you know what happens to a surveyor who can't survey? He goes home. He explains to everyone who ever believed in him why the promising young man from the wadi expedition is now working his cousin's fishing boat." She tilted her head. "I'm told Tam has a mother who brags about him at church."

Something flickered across Wendell's face — quick, involuntary, gone. But Voss caught it, and catalogued it, and felt the old cold pleasure rise in her chest like water filling a lock chamber. *There.* That was the look. That was the look people got when they understood that the fight wasn't between them and her anymore; it was between them and everyone they loved.

"So here is what happens now," Voss said, rising, moving to the tent flap, speaking to the valley as much as to the woman in the chair. "Effective this morning, the site is sealed. I've retained Halvorsen's people — eight men, ex-military, very discreet, arriving within the hour. No one enters or leaves the excavation perimeter without my written authorization. All finds are consolidated under armed inventory. All documentation is surrendered for review. Your crew remains on payroll through the end of the season — I'm not a monster, Ms. Galloway, and idle licensed professionals make poor martyrs — but they touch nothing."

She turned back. Wendell hadn't risen. She sat very still, hands flat on her thighs, staring at the contract as though it were a body laid out for identification.

"And if I refuse?" she said quietly.

"Then I invoke seventeen-c this afternoon, file the violation reports tonight, and by Friday your crew's names are in four capitals and your father's name is in a fifth — because I will make very sure the press understands that the daughter of Elias Galloway attempted to smuggle artifacts out of Wadi al-Nakhl to rehabilitate a fraud." Voss let that breathe. "History does love a sequel."

The wind gusted. Sand hissed against the canvas like static. And Wendell Galloway — who Voss had spent three weeks underestimating and three days fearing in equal, secret measure — did the one thing Voss had not prepared for.

She capitulated.

Completely. Cleanly. Without argument, without a single flare of the defiance Voss had stockpiled rebuttals against. She stood, smoothed her shirt, and said, "Fine. We'll comply. The crew will be informed within the hour. You'll have our documentation by evening."

Just like that.

And it was wrong. Every instinct Voss possessed — instincts honed in thirty years of academic knife-fights, instincts that had smelled falsified data and buried evidence and betrayal before instruments confirmed any of them — every one of those instincts stood up and began shouting at once. People did not surrender like this. Not Galloways. Pride was the family disease; she'd built half her career on the ruins of Elias's pride. The daughter should have fought. Should have blustered, threatened, negotiated, *something*.

Instead Wendell walked to the tent flap and paused there, half in shadow, and looked back with an expression Voss would spend the next several nights taking apart like a core sample.

It wasn't defeat. That was the terrible thing. It was courtesy. The courtesy of a woman granting a favor.

"Clause seventeen," Wendell said. "You wrote it well. Truly. My father always said you were the best draftsman he ever lost to." A pause, thin as a razor. "He also said you always counted your winnings too early. Something about the 1994 season in the Delta. Ask him sometime, if you still speak."

Then she was gone, into the white glare of morning, and Voss stood alone with her whisky and her walnut board and the sudden, irrational conviction that she had just signed something herself — some counter-document, unwritten, unfiled, already in force.

She shook it off. She poured a second measure. She told herself the girl was beaten, that compliance was compliance, that locked gates and armed men and surrendered paperwork were facts, and facts did not have expressions.

But that night, reviewing the manifest her new security chief delivered — crates inventoried, seals photographed, everything accounted for — she paused over line item forty-seven. A single crate, logged as ceramic fragments, low value, storage priority. Weighed in at eleven kilograms.

Eleven kilograms of potsherds should have weighed six.

Voss stared at the number for a long time. Outside, far off in the dark, the wadi wind changed direction — swinging south, warm, carrying the first faint mineral smell of the season's coming storms.

Nine days until the pass road closed. Six until her shipment left.

She reached for the telephone to double the guard on the storage tents, and told herself it was routine.
