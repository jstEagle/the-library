## 16. The Dry Season Clock

Ravi counted the crew's pulse the way he counted their pills: quietly, and with more attention than anyone ever thanked him for.

He kept the medical tent the way other men kept chapels. A folding table scrubbed to bare aluminum, a battery lamp hung from a guyline, a lockbox of ampoules and antibiotics that he inventoried every night by lamplight like a priest counting relics. The desert had taught him that medicine was mostly arithmetic — how many liters of water a body needed, how many degrees of fever before you worried, how many days until the pass road closed and everything they'd done in this valley became either legend or evidence, depending on who told the story first.

Nine days. That was what the satellite uplink said, when he'd thumbed through the weather feed at dawn while the others slept. Nine days until the seasonal rains closed the canyon pass for six months, sealing Wadi al-Nakhl behind a wall of water and mud until spring. And Voss's shipment — the decoy crates, the ones Odile was supposed to let them seize — left in six.

Three days of margin. Ravi had spent enough years patching up people who lived on thin margins to know that three days was not a plan. It was a prayer wearing a plan's clothes.

* * *

He found Wendell at the survey stake line just after first light, when the wadi still held its blue hour and the cliffs hadn't yet begun their daily performance of turning gold. She was crouched over the transit, one eye to the scope, and she didn't look up when his boots crunched the gravel beside her. She never did. That was the thing about Wendell Galloway — or whatever name she was using this season; Ravi had stopped keeping track — she could make herself so still that you'd swear the ground had grown her out of it.

"Headache," he said. Not a question.

Her eye stayed at the scope. "I'm fine."

"You've been fine for eleven days. You're fine the way a dam is fine." He held out two tablets and the canteen. "Chew them. Don't argue."

She took them, finally, because Wendell argued with contracts and rivals and the ghost of her father, but she didn't argue with Ravi, not about bodies. He watched her chew the tablets dry, watched the small grimace, and made his private note: third morning in a row she'd come down to the trench floor without being reminded to descend slowly, third morning her hands had that faint tremor that wasn't grief, wasn't fear, was altitude. They were working at 2,400 meters now, up on the shelf where the cache chamber had been opened, and Wendell spent fourteen hours a day up there cataloguing inscriptions by lamplight because she couldn't bear to leave them alone overnight, as if the stones might change their testimony if no one witnessed it.

"You need to sleep lower," Ravi said. "Down at base camp. Eight hours minimum."

"I sleep here."

"You doze here. There's a difference, and the difference is called cerebral edema, and I've seen it kill a man in Ladakh who was tougher than both of us put together." He crouched, made himself smaller, made it a conversation between equals instead of a lecture. "Wendell. Look at me."

She looked at him. Her eyes were bloodshot and bright, too bright, the way they got when the pressure in her skull was climbing along with everything else.

"I can't leave," she said quietly. "Voss is coming back Thursday. If those crates aren't staged right, if the manifest doesn't read exactly like something she'd expect—"

"Odile is staging the crates. Tam is forging the manifest, and honestly, the boy has a gift that worries me. You are not load-bearing, Wendell. You're allowed to be a person."

Something moved across her face — not quite a smile, more like the memory of one, worn smooth by disuse. "You sound like my mother."

"I never met your mother."

"No," Wendell said. "You didn't." And she went back to the transit, and Ravi understood that the conversation was over, the way he understood weather: not happily, but completely.

* * *

The planning happened at noon in Odile's tent, where the heat pressed down like a thumb and everyone spoke in the low, fast register of people who had learned to be quiet around hired security. Voss had left four men at the site gate — ex-military, sunburned, bored, which Ravi considered the most dangerous combination short of actual malice. The men drank tea at the checkpoint and watched the convoy road and believed, because Marguerite Voss wanted them to believe, that the excavation was winding down into an ordinary season's end.

Six days until Voss's shipment. Nine until the pass closed. The math sat on the camp table between them like a fourth person.

"The decoy goes on the trucks," Odile said, tapping the manifest Tam had drafted. She was a broad, square woman who moved like a door closing, and her voice never rose above the volume of someone describing a recipe. "Crates marked as survey equipment, ceramic sherds, soil samples. Boring. Voss opens them, she finds boring, she files her fraud charges, she makes her speech at the symposium, and by the time she realizes the ceramics are planted fakes—" Odile shrugged, a small continental movement. "By then the real collection is over the mountains."

"And we're all named in the charges," Tam said. He was twenty-three and had the kind of face that showed every thought like weather across a plain. "She'll have our licenses. She said clause seventeen, she said—"

"She said a lot of things," Odile said. "Licenses get suspended. People don't die of suspended licenses. People die of flash floods in canyons, which is what we're proposing instead, so let's be precise about our risks."

The mule route was Tam's contribution, actually — Ravi had watched the boy spread the topo map with shaking hands three nights ago and trace the old caravan path through the canyon narrows, the one the survey had flagged as impassable for vehicles but passable, barely, for animals and madmen. Fourteen kilometers of slot canyon, then the switchbacks over the shoulder of Jebel Harra, then the long descent to the oasis town where a man Tam's cousin knew would hold cargo for a fee that Ravi chose not to ask about. Two nights' travel if the weather held. If the weather held. Everyone in the tent understood that the dry season's last storms didn't announce themselves; they arrived like debts.

"Wendell leads the mule train," Odile said. "I take the decoy trucks. Tam—"

"Tam stays here," Wendell said from the tent flap, where she'd appeared without anyone hearing her arrive, which was her particular talent. "If Voss interrogates anyone, it should be someone who genuinely doesn't know where the real crates went. I'm not telling him the route either."

"That's insane," Tam said. "I found the route. I'm coming."

"You're staying." Wendell's voice was gentle and absolute, the voice of someone who had already lost too many people to risk. "That's not a punishment. It's the plan working correctly."

Ravi watched the boy's jaw work, watched him swallow the argument, and filed away the look on his face — the specific misery of the loyal young, being protected against their will. He'd treat that too, later, if there was time. There was always something to treat.

* * *

That evening he climbed to the shelf with a headlamp and a thermos and found Wendell cross-legged inside the cache chamber itself, surrounded by the soft gold light of the battery lamps, copying inscriptions onto acetate sheets with the concentration of a scribe who expected to be interrupted by history at any moment. The chamber breathed cold air, older than the desert above it, and the walls carried Elias Galloway's vindication in carved lines that Ravi couldn't read but could feel, the way you feel a sermon in a language you don't speak.

"Pulse and oxygen," he said, sitting down across from her. "Humor me."

She gave him her wrist without looking up. Her pulse was fast and thready. The oximeter read 88 percent. At rest. Ravi looked at the number for a long moment, and Wendell watched him look at it, and neither of them said anything for a while, because they both knew what the number meant: she had been running on thin air for days, spending oxygen like the crew was spending hope, and the bill was coming due.

"Descend tonight," Ravi said. "Or I pull you off the mule train entirely. I'll tell Odile you're medically unfit. I'll mean it."

"You wouldn't."

"I've sedated patients for less stubbornness than yours." He packed the oximeter away with deliberate slowness. "Wendell. Listen to me. You've spent three years making yourself invisible — different names, different sites, no photographs, no history. Fine. That was survival. But invisible people forget they have bodies. Bodies keep score." He nodded at the walls, at the carved testimony of her father's honesty. "This valley already proved him right. It doesn't need you to die proving it again."

She sat with that. Outside, the wind had started up, the dry-season wind that came down the wadi every evening like a tide going out, carrying the smell of dust and distance. Somewhere below, Tam was singing badly to the mules, and Odile was arguing on the satellite phone with a fuel supplier, and the camp sounded like what it was: a handful of unattached people who had decided, against all their training, to belong to each other.

"If I go down tonight," Wendell said finally, "I come back up tomorrow. One more day with the inscriptions. Then I lead the train."

"One day. And you sleep at base camp, at the lower tents, and I check your saturation at midnight and again at dawn. Non-negotiable."

"Non-negotiable," she agreed, and there it was again, the almost-smile, surfacing like something rising through water.

Ravi gathered his things and paused at the chamber mouth, looking back at her small figure among the lamps and the ancient stone, this woman who measured her life in miles to avoid measuring it in years, who had smuggled her father's truth out of the earth and was now preparing to smuggle it over a mountain while pretending to be nobody at all.

"Get some sleep," he said. "Hope's rationed like water out here. Don't waste yours on all-nighters."

* * *

At midnight he checked her saturation in the lower tent and it had climbed to 91, and at dawn it was 93, and she was already awake, already dressed, already standing at the edge of camp watching the eastern sky with the expression of a woman doing arithmetic she hated.

Ravi joined her with two cups of tea and followed her gaze. The clouds were building over the high country — towering, white-shouldered, beautiful in the way that dangerous things are beautiful. The last storms of the dry season, gathering early.

"Nine days to the closure," he said. "Maybe less, now."

"Six until Voss's trucks roll." Wendell blew on her tea without drinking it. "And the storm front's moving faster than the forecast said. Ravi — if it breaks early, the canyon fills before the mules are through."

"Then we move tonight instead of tomorrow."

She turned to look at him, and for once there was nothing hidden in her face — just exhaustion, and resolve, and something that might have been gratitude trying to find its way out through three years of disuse.

"Tell Odile," she said. "Saddle the mules. We go at dark."

Behind them, far down the convoy road, a helicopter rose from Voss's forward camp, banking north toward the pass — early, ahead of schedule, carrying paperwork and permits and a woman's cold ambition toward the lowlands. Ravi watched it shrink to a speck and felt the clock in his chest tick over.

They were out of margin now. All that was left was the canyon, the storm, and whatever the valley decided to keep.
