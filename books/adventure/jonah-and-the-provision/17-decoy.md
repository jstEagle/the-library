## 17. Decoy

Tam had never been good at lying. It was a childhood affliction — his mother used to say his face was a ledger that anyone could read, every entry written in ink that wouldn't blot. When he was nine he'd broken the neighbor's window with a cricket ball and confessed before the glass finished falling. So it was a particular kind of irony, he thought, crouched in the shadow of the loading tent at four in the morning, that the fate of everything they'd found beneath Wadi al-Nakhl now rested on his ability to deceive Marguerite Voss.

The generator throbbed somewhere behind him, a heartbeat he could feel through the soles of his boots. Floodlights raked the compound in long white blades, and between them lay the dark places where Tam had learned to move these past weeks — not gracefully, never gracefully, but quietly, which was what mattered. He carried a manifest clipboard he didn't need and wore the expression of a man doing exactly what he was supposed to be doing, which was the first rule of deception according to Odile: *look bored, not guilty. Guilty people look like they're carrying something. Bored people look like they're carrying crates.*

There were forty-one crates stacked in the loading yard. That number had been drilled into him until he dreamed it: forty-one. Thirty-four of them were real — sandstone blocks, potsherds, the ordinary dross of an honest excavation, packed to satisfy Voss's inventory. Seven were lies. Beautifully constructed lies, each one weighted with rock and packed with straw, each one stenciled with catalog numbers that corresponded to artifacts that no longer sat in the cache chamber because Wendell and Ravi had spent two sleepless nights moving them into the false floor of Crate 12, the one with the cracked lid that Voss's security chief had already inspected and dismissed as damaged goods.

And the real cargo — the chamber pieces, the inscriptions, the vindication of Elias Galloway — had left three hours ago in the bed of the water truck, driven by Odile along the canyon track toward the mule route, wrapped in tarpaulin under a hundred liters of brackish water that nobody would want to steal.

Tam knew all this. He repeated it to himself like a litany as he walked the perimeter of the yard, clipboard against his chest, checking seals that didn't need checking. *Forty-one crates. Odile is gone. I am tired. I am allowed to be tired.* The sky above the wadi rim was the color of hammered tin, the last stars dissolving into it. Somewhere out on the flats a jackal laughed twice and went silent, as if embarrassed by its own voice.

He heard the helicopter at half past five.

It came in low over the eastern cliffs without lights, a black insect shape against the graying sky, and Tam's stomach dropped through the ground and kept going. Voss wasn't due until noon. She was early because she was suspicious, and she was suspicious because someone had talked — Tam had known for two days, ever since young Musa the porter started avoiding his eyes, ever since the extra cigarettes appeared in Musa's pocket that no one had paid him for. He'd told Wendell. Wendell had only nodded and said, *Then we go tonight instead of tomorrow,* and she hadn't looked afraid, and Tam had wondered briefly if she was capable of fear at all, or whether grief had burned that capacity out of her years ago, leaving something harder and stranger in its place.

The helicopter touched down in its own storm of grit. Tam turned away from it deliberately — the second rule of deception, also Odile's: *never watch the thing you're afraid of* — and made himself walk, unhurried, toward the survey tent, as though a helicopter at dawn were the most tedious thing in the world.

It might have worked. He would like to believe, later, that it almost worked.

But Voss came out of the rotor wash like weather, coat immaculate despite the dust, flanked by her two security men whose names Tam refused to learn because learning names made people real, and real people frightened him. She stopped in the middle of the loading yard and stood among the forty-one crates with her hands clasped behind her back, turning slowly, the way a woman turns in an empty house she has just bought.

"Good morning," she said, to no one in particular, in the tone of a woman who did not believe mornings were good but expected them to be profitable.

Tam stopped walking. There was nothing else to do. Running was confession; standing was merely stupidity, and stupidity was common enough to be invisible.

"Surveyor," Voss said, without looking at him. "You're up early."

"Crates don't seal themselves." His voice came out steadier than he deserved.

"No," she agreed pleasantly. "They don't. People seal them." She drifted toward Crate 12, the cracked one, and Tam felt his pulse climb into his throat and sit there like a swallowed stone. "This one's damaged."

"Hairline fracture in the lid. Logged it on arrival. Contents are stable."

"Contents being?"

"Sherds. Fragmentary storage vessels, mid-stratum." The lie came out smooth and Tam marveled at himself even as terror sang in his ears. *Sherds. Fragmentary. Mid-stratum.* Words were just words. His mother's ledger-face held its entries shut.

Voss ran one gloved finger along the crack in the lid, thoughtful, a jeweler appraising a stone she already intended to buy. Then she straightened and smiled at him, and it was the smile from the contract negotiations, the one that showed all her teeth and warmed none of them.

"Well," she said. "I've moved my schedule. We load today. Everything ships this afternoon — my permits cleared faster than anticipated." She let that hang in the air a moment, watching him. "You'll want to wake Dr. Galloway."

*Dr. Galloway.* Not Wendell. The surname landed between them like a dropped tool, and Tam understood that she knew — had perhaps always known — and had simply enjoyed saying it aloud for the first time.

"She's asleep," Tam said.

"Then she'll forgive you for waking her. Won't she?" And there it was again, that pleasantness with a blade inside it. "Or perhaps she won't. Perhaps she doesn't forgive easily. Family rarely does."

*

They woke Wendell, and Tam watched from the edge of things while Voss walked the yard with her, two women speaking in the clipped currency of contract law while the sun came up over the cliffs and turned the wadi walls the color of old blood. Wendell was magnificent, Tam thought. She didn't argue, didn't stall, didn't do any of the things guilt does. She yawned. She complained about the schedule change in the flat tones of a contractor whose profit margin had just been inconvenienced. She pointed out that the last three crates weren't sealed yet and that rushing the packing would risk damage Voss herself would have to write off.

"Then seal them quickly," Voss said sweetly.

So they sealed them quickly, and Tam helped, and his hands shook only a little, and the shaking was hidden inside honest labor where shaking belongs. By ten o'clock all forty-one crates sat in rows on the loading pallets, strapped and stenciled and inventoried, and Voss's men photographed each one for the manifest, and Tam stood beside the camera operator holding the clipboard and thought: *thirty-four true, seven false, one containing everything.* It was the strangest arithmetic of his life. The most important object in the valley weighed less than the lies around it.

At eleven, Voss called for a final count.

That was when it began to come apart.

One of the security men — the tall one, the one who smelled of cheap aftershave and did his counting with a grease pencil — recounted the pallets and frowned, and recounted again, and then walked the yard's edge with his head tilted like a bird dog, and Tam knew, with the sick certainty of the bad liar, exactly what the man had found before the man himself knelt down and touched it.

Tire tracks. Two days old, wind-scoured but not erased, leading out the western gate toward the canyon track. The water truck's tires. Odile's route.

"Someone's been running vehicles off-manifest," the tall man said.

Voss turned. The yard went very quiet. Even the generator seemed to hold its breath.

"Have we," she said.

"It's the supply run," Tam heard himself say, and the words came out of him before courage or cowardice could claim them. "Water resupply. Wednesday nights. It's on the logistics sheet — ask Ravi, ask anyone, we run the truck up-canyon to the spring because the well water here fouls the drilling slurry." He flipped his clipboard pages as if consulting them, heart slamming, face — his mother's ledger-face — giving away, he was certain, absolutely nothing, because for the first time in his life the lie mattered more than the truth and some deep animal part of him had simply decided to be good at this. "Page six. You can check."

Voss looked at him for a long time. The wind moved dust between them in slow gray ribbons. Behind her, Wendell stood very still by Crate 12, and Tam did not look at her, because looking at her would have been the end of everything.

"I will check," Voss said softly. "In fact, I think we'll all check. Surveyor — you'll ride up-canyon with my men this afternoon and show me this spring of yours."

*

They took him at two o'clock, after the convoy had rolled — after Voss, satisfied or unsatisfied, had put thirty-eight crates on her trucks and left three behind pending her inspection, after the helicopters and the dust and the long grinding departure had faded into the heat-shimmer — and they sat him in the survey tent with the flaps tied shut, and the tall man asked questions while the other one stood by the door and said nothing at all, which was worse.

"Who drove the truck Wednesday night."

"The driver drives it. That's generally how trucks work."

A backhand across the mouth, quick and professional, more insult than injury. Tam tasted copper and kept his eyes on the tent pole, on the little knot in the canvas where the fabric met the frame, and fixed his whole mind on that knot as though his life depended on understanding it.

"Names," the tall man said. "Who went up-canyon. What was in the truck besides water."

"Water," Tam said. "And a broken auger head we took in for repair. And Tam, who is me, and I'm telling you about it right now, so technically I'm not even hiding anything."

Another blow. This one to the ribs, and it folded him sideways off the camp stool onto the ground, and the ground was cool and gritty and he stayed there a moment, breathing, tasting blood and dust, feeling oddly calm. That was the thing nobody had warned him about — that fear, once it got large enough, went quiet. That past a certain size it stopped being a noise and became a landscape, and you could stand in it.

His grandmother had died in a detention camp before he was born; his mother had crossed two borders with forged papers and a baby; and Tam had grown up soft and careful and superstitious, the boy who knocked wood and read omens in tea leaves, and he had always believed courage was a thing other people were issued at birth, like height. Standing on the floor of the survey tent with blood in his mouth, he discovered that courage was not issued at all. It was manufactured, on site, out of whatever materials lay to hand — and what lay to hand, in his case, was a single stubborn fact:

Odile was gone. Wendell's name was safe. The crate was over the pass by now, maybe, if the mules were fast, if the canyon held, if the weather —

If. All of it hung on *if*, and every *if* was purchased with his silence.

"Last chance," the tall man said. "Dr. Voss wants names. Give them, and you keep your license, your job, your clean record. Refuse, and I file a report that ends your career before it starts. You're what, twenty-three? Twenty-four? You have a very long career to lose."

Tam got up off the ground. It took a while. His ribs had opinions. But he got up, and he sat back on the stool, and he looked at the tall man directly for the first time, and he said:

"You know what I do? I'm a surveyor. I read ground. Everyone thinks it's instruments, but it isn't — it's attention. You pay attention long enough, the ground tells you everything. Where the water went. Where the fire was. Where somebody buried something and meant to come back." He wiped his mouth with the back of his hand. "I've been paying attention to Dr. Voss since she landed. And here's what the ground tells me about her: she's scared. Scared people hurt the small ones first because it's easy and it makes them feel big. So hit me again if it helps. But you're not getting names, because there aren't any names to get. A truck ran on Wednesday. Read page six."

The tall man studied him for a moment with something that was almost respect, which was somehow more frightening than the fists.

Then the tent flap opened and Voss came in, and the temperature of the room changed the way it changes before a storm.

She didn't shout. She sat down across from him, folded her hands, and spoke gently, reasonably, about contracts and clause seventeen and the difference between a felony charge in one jurisdiction and another, about porters who had seen things at night and were willing to describe them, about how young men with talent had such fragile futures. She laid it all out like a hand of cards, face up, generous.

And Tam listened, and when she finished he said, "Page six," and nothing else, and he said it eleven more times over the next four hours, until his voice wore down to a thread, until the light through the canvas went amber and then gray, until Voss finally rose, smoothed her coat, and looked down at him with an expression he couldn't read — frustration, or curiosity, or the faintest unwilling flicker of something like regard.

"You're loyal," she said. "That's rare. It's wasted on her, but it's rare." At the flap she paused. "Your friends are driving into a canyon that floods, surveyor. Weather service says the storm's coming early this year. Think about which is worth more — your silence, or their lives. You have until morning."

The flap fell shut. Tam sat alone in the dying light with blood on his teeth and his whole body trembling now that it was permitted to, and outside, far to the south, over the canyon country where Odile and the mules and the truth of Elias Galloway were picking their way through the dark, the first thunder of the season rolled up the valley like a door being closed.

He thought of Wendell. He thought of the flash-flood country, and the storm coming early, and the words *you have until morning.*

Then he got up, quietly, and went to find Ravi.
