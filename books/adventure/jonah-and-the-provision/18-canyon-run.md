## 18. Canyon Run

The storm was supposed to have three more days of mercy in it.

Ravi had said so that morning, holding his battered satellite phone against the sky as if altitude might improve its reception. *Three days*, he'd repeated, and Odile had nodded, and Tam had written it on the manifest board in his careful surveyor's hand: **PASS CLOSES — 9 DAYS. SHIPMENT LEAVES TONIGHT.** The whole plan had been built on those three days the way a bridge is built on a single pier, and Wendell had known — had known in her teeth, in the old ache of her left knee, in the way the air tasted like copper and iron filings when they broke camp at dusk — that the desert does not honor schedules.

She said nothing. There was nothing to say that would change the arithmetic. Six crates, one mule train, eleven miles of canyon country between the cache chamber's false back wall and the dry riverbed where Odile's truck waited with its forged papers and its innocent-looking tarpaulins. Eleven miles of ground that could turn from stone to river in twenty minutes if the sky decided to spend what it had been hoarding all season.

So she led them out under a sky the color of a bruise going green at the edges, and she did not look back at the camp where Voss's hired security dozed around their generator-powered floodlights, believing — because Tam had built it so beautifully — that the crates moving tonight were empty decoys bound for the road.

The mules went first, laden and patient, their hooves striking little bells of sound off the canyon walls. Then Ravi, who had insisted on coming despite Wendell telling him twice that his job was the camp, the sick, the ones staying behind. He'd looked at her with that medic's flat patience and said, "You'll want me closer than the camp," and she hadn't argued, because he was right. Her headaches had been getting worse for a week. The world kept developing soft edges, like a photograph left too long in the sun.

Then Tam. Then herself, walking point, reading the ground.

* * *

Reading ground was the one inheritance from her father she had never managed to refuse. Elias Galloway could look at a hillside and see the Roman road sleeping under it; Wendell could look at a canyon floor and see where the water wanted to go, had gone, would go again. It wasn't mystical. It was just attention — years of it, miles of it, the thousand small lessons the earth teaches anyone humble enough to keep failing in front of it.

And the ground tonight was saying things she didn't like.

The wadi sand was packed hard and dark, damp an inch down. The lichens on the north-facing walls were swollen and black-green instead of gray. A spider had rebuilt its funnel web three times in the same spot near the canyon mouth, which meant water had taken it three times recently, which meant the flash-flood season wasn't waiting politely for its calendar date. And high on the walls, above the reach of any ordinary rain, ran the flood lines — pale mineral scars marking every historic deluge — and the newest of them sat lower than the ones below it, fresher, as if the canyon had been rehearsing.

"Talk to me," Ravi said quietly, drawing his mule up beside hers. He didn't ask *what's wrong*. He never asked that. He asked for data, because data was how he loved people.

"Watershed's saturated," Wendell said. "Storm breaks anywhere in forty kilometers of high country, we get the whole thing funneled through here inside the hour. Canyon narrows at the Narrows—" she almost smiled at the honesty of the name "—and there's no high ground past Kilometer Seven except the shelf on the east wall, and that's a scramble even in daylight."

"So we don't stop."

"We don't stop."

Tam, overhearing, pulled his cap lower. He'd been quiet since the night survey, quieter than usual, and Wendell knew why. He'd seen her cry over her father's handwriting on the chamber wall, and he carried that knowledge the way he carried everything — carefully, like something borrowed that must be returned undamaged. She wanted to tell him it was all right, that being witnessed hadn't killed her, but the words belonged to a different language than the one this canyon spoke, so she let the silence stand and walked on.

* * *

Kilometer Five. The moon came out from behind the cloud deck for ten minutes and showed her the sky, and what she saw made her stomach drop through the canyon floor.

The clouds over the high plateau to the south were not clouds anymore. They were a wall — a standing, boiling rampart lit from within by heat lightning, purple-white flashes strobing behind their own mass like a signal fire burning inside a closed fist. It was not coming toward them. It was worse than that. It was sitting on the watershed, directly above the forty kilometers of catchment that all drained, eventually, into the canyon they were standing in.

"Ravi." Her voice came out level, which surprised her. "Time check."

He read the sky the way she read the ground, fast and professional. "It holds together, we get runoff in ninety minutes. Maybe two hours."

"The mules make the Narrows in fifty minutes if we push."

"Then we push."

They pushed. Wendell set a pace that drew protest from the mules and silence from the humans, boots striking the packed sand, breath sawing in the thin air, her headache blooming from ache to white pressure behind her eyes. The canyon walls rose on either side, beautiful and indifferent, their flood-line scars now reading less like history and more like instructions. *This high*, they said. *The water comes this high.*

At Kilometer Six and a half, the first drops fell.

Not rain — not yet — just the canyon exhaling ahead of the storm, a cold wind pushing up-valley against their direction of travel, carrying the smell of wet stone and ozone. The mules' ears flattened. Tam's mule balked and he spoke to it in low Arabic, hand flat on its neck, and Wendell heard him say, *I know, I know, I don't like it either,* and thought: *this boy has never once complained about anything that mattered.*

Then the rain arrived, and it did not arrive gently. It arrived the way the desert does everything — absolutely, without negotiation, a gray curtain drawn across the world, turning the canyon into a drum. Visibility collapsed to thirty meters. The trail beneath their feet began to change character underfoot, sand giving way to slick clay, and Wendell made the call she'd hoped not to make.

"Narrows is twenty minutes out. We run it or we climb. Nobody stops in the Narrows for anything. Anything. You hear me?"

Tam nodded. Ravi nodded. The mules, mercifully, agreed.

* * *

The Narrows were exactly what the name promised: a slot where the canyon pinched to barely four meters across, walls rising sheer for thirty meters on either side, polished smooth by centuries of exactly the event Wendell was now gambling against. In dry conditions it was a dramatic corridor, photogenic, the kind of place Tam photographed and labeled *stratigraphic section, east wall.* In wet conditions it was a rifle barrel, and they were the bullet traveling the wrong way.

They were halfway through when Wendell heard it.

Everyone who lives long enough around desert rivers learns the sound, and everyone learns it once. It comes before the water. It is not thunder — it is underneath thunder, a deep rolling grind, boulders being introduced to each other at speed somewhere upstream in the dark. It sounds, Ravi had told her once, like the earth clearing its throat.

"MOVE!" she screamed, and the word was torn sideways by the wind, and she grabbed the lead mule's halter rope and hauled, and the little train broke into the stumbling gallop that mules reserve for genuine emergencies, hooves skidding on wet clay, Tam shouting behind her, Ravi bringing up the rear, and the grinding roar swelling behind them like a door opening onto a furnace—

They burst out of the Narrows' downstream mouth into the wider canyon as the wall of water entered it behind them.

Wendell turned once, one second, one look. What she saw would live behind her eyelids for the rest of her life: a brown vertical thing filling the Narrows wall to wall, carrying entire trees, carrying a boulder the size of a truck cab surfing along its crest, the flood line scar on the wall suddenly revealed as prophecy rather than history. The water hit the wider canyon and spread, dropped from three meters to one, slowed from lethal to merely terrible, and kept coming.

"East wall! The shelf! GO!"

* * *

What happened next happened in pieces, the way disasters do, each fragment complete and separate as a specimen in a tray.

A fragment: the mules scrambling up the talus slope toward the shelf, Ravi shoving them from behind, water already ankle-deep and pulling like hands.

A fragment: Tam, halfway up, turning back — turning *back* — because the last crate, the one strapped to the spare pack frame they'd been hauling between two of them, had been knocked loose in the scramble and was sliding down the slope toward the flood.

A fragment: Wendell doing the arithmetic that would define her, faster than thought, colder than feeling. The crate held the inscribed foundation tablets. The provenance chain. Twenty years of her father's vindication, sealed in a waterproof case rated for submersion, floating free if it reached the water — *rated for submersion, it will float, it will survive* — versus Tam, who was not rated for anything, who was sixteen kilos lighter than the crate, who was reaching for it with the water climbing his thighs.

A fragment: her own voice, screaming his name, and her body already moving before the choice finished making itself, which was perhaps the truest answer to the question of what she valued — that her body knew before her mind consented.

She caught his wrist as the flood took the crate.

The case bobbed once, twice, tilted, and then the brown water folded over it and it was gone — swept downstream into the dark, riding the crest, a small pale rectangle diminishing with obscene calm, and Wendell felt the loss go through her like a spear and she *held on anyway*, hauled Tam up the slope with Ravi's arms locked around her waist, the three of them collapsing onto the shelf in a tangle of limbs and rope and mule, the flood roaring past six feet below them, devouring the canyon they'd been standing in ninety seconds ago.

Tam was shaking. Not crying — shaking, the full-body tremor of adrenaline with nowhere to go. "The tablets," he gasped. "Wendell, the tablets, I lost—"

"I know." She pulled him against her shoulder and held him there, this superstitious, careful, brave boy who had refused to give names under interrogation and had turned back for the cargo because duty was the only religion he'd ever been taught. "I know. Listen to me. Listen." She waited until his eyes found hers. "The case floats. It's sealed. It's designed for exactly this — your father's generation built those cases to survive river crossings, and I chose them because I don't trust deserts. It's on the water, and water goes downhill, and downhill is where Odile is."

"You can't know that."

"No," she admitted. "I can't. But I know you're here, and you're here because I put my hands on you, and that part I *do* know." She heard her own voice crack on the last words and let it. "Cargo comes back. People don't. That's the whole lesson, Tam. That's the one I crossed three continents trying not to learn."

Ravi, kneeling in the rain with his med kit open, said nothing at all, which from Ravi was a benediction.

* * *

They came down out of the canyon at dawn, wrung out, half-drowned, leading exhausted mules, and found Odile standing beside her truck at the riverbed crossing with her arms crossed and her face arranged into its most formidable neutrality.

Behind her, stacked in the truck bed under the innocent tarpaulin, sat six crates.

Six.

Wendell stared. Counted again. Stared.

"Found it at first light," Odile said, "wedged in the root ball of a dead acacia two hundred meters downstream of the confluence, sitting on a gravel bar like it had been *delivered*. Case isn't even scuffed." She paused, and the neutrality cracked just enough to show the fear underneath. "I've watched you calculate odds for three years, Wendell. I have never once seen you bet wrong. But I'm telling you as your foreman and as your friend — if you ever make me watch a flood come down a canyon knowing you're in it, I will quit, and I will take the mules."

Wendell opened the case with shaking hands. Inside, nested in foam, the foundation tablets lay intact, their inscriptions dry, their testimony undamaged — Elias vindicated, Voss condemned, the truth heavier and safer than it had ever been.

Tam stood beside her, dripping, and began, very quietly, to laugh.

Above the eastern rim, the sky was washing itself clean, and the pass road — Ravi confirmed, phone pressed to the sky — would close in eight days.

Which left Voss six.
