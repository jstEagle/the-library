## 20. Symposium

The hall smelled of lilies and old carpet, the way institutions smell when they are trying to convince you of their own permanence. The season's closing symposium had been held in the same auditorium for forty years — Wendell knew this because she'd read the program twice on the train, then a third time to keep her hands occupied — and everything about it was designed to make the people inside feel like the building itself: load-bearing, permanent, beyond question.

She stood at the edge of the registration desk with her credentials in her hand and felt, for one long moment, like a woman holding someone else's ticket.

*W. Galloway.* She'd registered under the name she'd used for three years, the name that appeared on every permit and manifest from Turkana to the wadi. But Voss knew. Voss had known since the helicopter, since clause seventeen, since the moment she'd looked across a folding table and said *Galloway* like a diagnosis. There would be no hiding behind it today. That had been the point of coming.

"Name?" asked the woman at the desk, not looking up.

"Wendell Galloway."

The typing stopped. The woman looked up, and Wendell watched the recognition move through her face like water through sand — first confusion, then the quick arithmetic of gossip, then something almost like pity. "You're listed as a respondent," the woman said carefully. "Dr. Voss has filed—"

"I know what she's filed." Wendell took her badge. It was printed on cheap card stock, and the lanyard scratched. "Where do I go to be accused?"

* * *

They put her in the third row, which told her everything about how the morning had been choreographed. Not the front, where respondents with standing sat; not the back, where the press clustered. The third row was purgatory — visible enough to be controlled, close enough to be cut off if things went wrong. Odile slid in beside her, wearing the one good jacket she owned, charcoal wool gone shiny at the elbows, and Ravi took the aisle seat with his medic's calm folded around him like a coat. Tam had refused to sit down at all and hovered by the wall near the doors, arms crossed, surveyor's eyes sweeping the room as though measuring it for collapse.

"They've got four lawyers," Odile murmured. "Counted them during the coffee thing. Four, plus a man who keeps touching his briefcase like it might bolt."

"How many do we have?"

Odile's mouth twitched. "One retired archivist who owes me a favor, and whatever's in your bag."

What was in her bag weighed less than two kilos and had cost her father twenty years of his life. Wendell let her hand rest on the canvas strap and did not answer.

Onstage, the moderator — a silver-haired man whose nameplate said DR. HALLORAN and whose expression said he wished he were anywhere else — tapped the microphone and began the liturgy of introductions. The Wadi al-Nakhl excavation would be presented as the season's flagship project. Dr. Marguerite Voss, principal investigator. And following her presentation, a procedural matter concerning contract violations, evidence tampering, and — Halloran read the phrase with visible distaste — *the fraudulent misrepresentation of recovered materials.*

In the second row, Marguerite Voss sat with her spine straight as a plumb line, silver hair pinned with architectural precision, listening to her own crimes described as administrative business. She wore dove gray. She had always worn colors that made other people look like they were apologizing for being in the room.

"She's enjoying this," Ravi said quietly.

"No," Wendell said. She had spent three weeks learning to read Voss the way she read ground — not the surface but the strata beneath it, the compression, the fault lines. "She's performing enjoyment. Look at her hands."

Voss's hands were folded on the program in her lap, and they were perfectly still, and that was the tell. A woman at ease fidgets. A woman performing stillness is holding herself together with both hands.

Halloran called the session to order. Voss rose, buttoned her jacket, and went to war.

* * *

She was good. That was the worst of it. Wendell had prepared herself for bluster and got brilliance instead — forty minutes of slides so clean they looked laundered, stratigraphic diagrams rendered in soothing blues, a narrative assembled with the precision of a jeweler setting stones. The Wadi al-Nakhl as Voss told it was a story of vision vindicated: a difficult site, long avoided because of its unfortunate associations, finally opened under competent management. She spoke of her team with warm condescension. She spoke of the decoy crates — seized, she said, before they could be smuggled out of the country — as an attempted theft, orchestrated by persons unknown, possibly disgruntled former employees.

"And I want to address directly," she said, turning from the screen, her voice dropping into the register of reluctant sorrow, "the matter of the individual seated in this hall today. Ms. Galloway." A pause, calibrated. Heads turned; Wendell felt the weight of three hundred gazes settle on her shoulders like silt. "I hired her crew in good faith. I extended professional courtesy to the daughter of a man whose name this institution remembers with pain. And in return, she stole from the site, forged documents, and planted counterfeits in materials destined for proper curation. I take no pleasure in saying this. But archaeology survives only on trust, and trust must be defended."

It was beautiful. It was airtight. It was also, Wendell thought, watching Voss's hands stay perfectly flat against the lectern, built on one assumption: that no one in this room had ever seen the inside of the sealed chamber, or the water-stained pages of a 1998 field journal, or the way a forged provenance chain looks next to a real one.

Voss concluded to polite applause. Halloran adjusted his glasses and said, with the tone of a man pulling a splinter, "We'll now hear from the respondent. Ms. Galloway, whenever you're ready."

Wendell stood. Her knees held. That surprised her more than anything else that day.

* * *

She didn't go to the lectern. She walked instead to the center of the stage, where the light fell without the shadow of the screen, and set her bag on the floor beside her, and looked out at the room until the room began to look back.

Three years of being nobody had taught her exactly one useful skill: she knew how invisible worked. She knew how a room decides not to see you — how it files you under *crew*, under *daughter of*, under *problem*, and stops looking. She had lived in the blind spots of powerful people her whole adult life. And she knew, too, that the only way out was not to shout. Shouting gave them somewhere else to look. You had to stand where the light already was and simply refuse to leave it.

"My name is Wendell Galloway," she said. "I'm the daughter of Elias Galloway, and for three years I have done everything I could to make sure no one in this profession knew that. I registered under my mother's maiden name. I took jobs at sites no one wanted. I measured my life in miles because miles don't ask questions." She let that sit. Somewhere in the third row, a pen stopped moving. "Dr. Voss is counting on that history. She's counting on you hearing *Galloway* and thinking *fraud*, the way she taught this institution to think it twenty years ago. So I'm not going to ask you to take my word for anything. I'm going to show you paper."

She opened the bag.

"The first document is a field journal, kept between March and November of 1998, in my father's hand, recovered from a collapsed side gallery at the Wadi al-Nakhl — a gallery Dr. Voss's own survey flagged as unstable and ordered sealed, which is convenient, because it's also the gallery that contains the original cache." She held the journal up, open to the water-warred page she had reconstructed fragment by fragment through three nights of lamplight while Tam kept watch and Ravi counted her heart rate like weather. "Page forty-one was torn out. These fragments were found separately, tucked into the binding. My father hid them there deliberately — because he had already learned, in 1998, that his data was being altered, and he knew who was doing it."

She turned toward the screen. "May I?"

Halloran hesitated, glanced at Voss, and — to his credit, or perhaps just to his exhaustion — nodded.

The fragments went up enlarged, gray-brown ghosts of ink. Wendell read aloud, slowly, letting her father's voice come through her own: *"Cache relocated to secondary chamber, north wall, below the flood line. Coordinates recorded here only. If anything happens to these notes, the material will be attributed to whoever finds it — and I know exactly who is waiting for me to fail."*

"In 1998," Wendell said, "my father was accused of fabricating artifacts to support a theory about pre-dynastic trade routes through the wadi. The accusation came from within his own team. The institutional review relied on documentation supplied by his field director — a woman named Corinne Ashby, whose protégé, I'll note for the record, is Dr. Marguerite Voss." She heard the shift in the room, the small collective intake, the sound of three hundred people recalculating. "Ashby falsified the stratigraphic logs. My father couldn't prove it then. He buried the proof instead — literally — and spent the rest of his career being the cautionary tale this institution tells its graduate students."

She moved to the provenance chain next, and this was the part she had rehearsed until her voice went hoarse in the hotel bathroom: the sealed chamber opened on the ninth of last month, witnessed and photographed; the intact artifacts with inscriptions matching the 1998 journal's transcriptions point for point; the independent lab results, run in triplicate through two facilities Voss did not control; the thermoluminescence dates that placed the material centuries earlier than anything Elias Galloway had ever been accused of inventing. Each document she laid on the table at the front of the stage, in sequence, like a mason laying course after course of stone.

"Fraud leaves seams," she said. "I've spent my career reading ground, and ground doesn't lie, but people do, and they lie in patterns. Dr. Voss's provenance for the decoy crates has eleven seams. Mine has none. Her experts can examine both chains side by side — I've brought copies, and I've already provided sets to the journal editors and to two members of the ethics committee, because I assumed I wouldn't get this much time."

Then she stepped back from the table. "And I have witnesses."

* * *

Odile testified like a foreman reading a load rating — flat, precise, unimpressable. She described the decoy operation, the false manifest, the night loading, and she described Voss's security chief seizing crates that contained, as subsequent analysis confirmed, replicas Wendell's own team had fabricated and marked. "We planted them," Odile said, when the lawyer pounced on it. "Yes. To protect real artifacts from being shipped out under permits that didn't cover them. You're welcome to charge us. We'll plead guilty to saving the site."

Ravi spoke briefly, quietly, about the storm, about the porter who admitted to selling information to Voss's people, about the arm he'd set and the confidence he'd rebuilt and the night Wendell had finally told them her name. He didn't perform the emotion. He just set it down on the table like an instrument and let the room pick it up.

Tam was the one who broke it open. Tam, who had been caught covering tracks and had refused under interrogation to give names, stood at the witness table with hands that shook and voice that didn't, and described the night survey — the true chamber, the inscriptions on the north wall, and Wendell kneeling in the dust with her father's handwriting above her head, weeping.

"She wasn't stealing anything," Tam said, looking not at the lawyers but at Voss. "She was reading. And I want the record to say that whatever happens to us, the writing on that wall matches the journal page for page, and everyone in this room knows what that means."

Voss's counsel objected four times. Halloran overruled him three.

* * *

It ended the way collapses end — not with a crash but with a slow settling, load transferring, structure finding its true alignment. The ethics committee chair requested the full document set. The journal editors requested exclusive review. A young woman from the front row stood up during the open session and asked, in a voice that carried, whether the institution intended to revisit the 1998 findings formally, and Halloran, after a silence long enough to measure in geological terms, said, "Yes. We do."

Voss did not speak again. Wendell watched her from the stage as the session dissolved into noise — watched her gather her pages with those perfectly controlled hands, watch the control slip for exactly one second when a colleague she'd considered an ally turned away without meeting her eyes. Voss caught Wendell looking. For a moment the two of them held each other's gaze across the emptied rows, and Wendell saw there what she had seen in the valley's cliffs: not malice, finally, but erosion. Twenty years of a career built on a falsified foundation, and the flood line had come at last.

"You should know," Voss said quietly, pausing in the aisle beside the stage, her voice pitched for Wendell alone, "your father never had the courage to face me like this."

"He didn't need courage," Wendell said. "He had the truth. He just needed someone willing to carry it out of the valley." She picked up her bag. "Turns out it runs in the family."

* * *

Afterward the hall emptied slowly, the way a wound stops bleeding — in stages, reluctantly. Reporters wanted statements; committee members wanted signatures; Odile stood guard at the end of the row and rerouted all of them with the efficiency of a customs checkpoint. Tam had gone outside for air. Ravi pressed a water bottle into Wendell's hand and said nothing, which was his way of saying everything.

Wendell stayed on the stage after the others drifted away, alone under the lights, looking out at the rows of vacant seats. Three years of empty seats. Three years of choosing them. She thought of her mother's hospital corridor, of a phone that never rang, of a man who chose a discovery over a deathbed — and of a journal hidden in a niche for twenty years, waiting, the way valleys wait, for someone to come back and read it properly.

Her father's name was clean. That was the sentence she turned over in her mind, testing it, the way you test a restored artifact for stress points. *Elias Galloway's name is clean.* It fit strangely. She had carried the broken version so long she'd forgotten what the whole one weighed.

She shouldered the bag and walked up the aisle toward the great double doors, where afternoon light fell in a long gold bar across the marble, and the noise of the city came through like surf.

Through the glass panels of the doors, she saw a figure standing at the bottom of the steps.

An old man in a coat too thin for the season, leaning on a cane, hat in his hands — a man who had followed the controversy for years, apparently, and had chosen today, of all days, to stop waiting outside the story. He was smaller than she remembered. His hair was white as gypsum. And he was looking at the doors with an expression she recognized instantly, because she had worn it her whole life in mirrors: the look of someone standing at the edge of a dig site, wanting desperately to be let in, afraid of what the ground will say.

Wendell stopped with her hand on the door.

He hadn't seen her yet. She had one breath — maybe two — to decide whether to walk out into the light or turn back into the comfortable dark of the hall, where there were still reporters to dodge and forms to sign and a thousand small legitimate reasons to stay invisible.

She pushed the door open.
