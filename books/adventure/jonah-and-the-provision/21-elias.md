## 21. Elias

The symposium hall emptied the way a wound stops bleeding — slowly at first, then all at once.

Wendell stood at the podium long after the last question had been asked and answered, her hands still flat on the lectern as if it might pitch beneath her like a deck in weather. The projection screen behind her had gone dark, but she could still see the ghost of it burned into her vision: the provenance chain, the stratigraphic cross-sections, the photograph of the sealed chamber with its inscriptions running in unbroken lines like a river finally allowed to reach the sea. Two hundred people had watched her dismantle Marguerite Voss in forty minutes, and now they were gone, and the silence they left behind was enormous.

She hadn't felt triumphant. She had felt, strangely, like a diver surfacing too fast — everything bright and ringing and slightly unreal, her lungs aching for air that wasn't there.

*You should go,* Odile had said on her way out, squeezing Wendell's shoulder once, hard, the way she did when she meant something. *Before you talk yourself out of whatever you're about to do.*

So Wendell gathered her notes without seeing them, rolled them into a cylinder she would later find crushed beyond reading in her coat pocket, and walked out through the service corridor because she couldn't bear the gauntlet of the main doors — the handshakes, the colleagues suddenly eager to know her name, the cameras someone had mentioned. Twenty years of being invisible had not prepared her for being seen. It turned out light could burn as badly as shadow.

The corridor smelled of floor wax and old coffee. Emergency lighting made the walls the color of weak tea. She pushed through the final door into the evening —

— and stopped.

He was sitting on the low stone wall beside the bicycle racks, exactly where a man might sit if he had been waiting a long time and wanted to look like he hadn't. His coat was too thin for the season, a brown wool thing gone soft at the elbows, and his hands were folded in his lap with a stillness that frightened her more than any tremor would have. He had always been a man who moved constantly — gesturing, pacing, sketching in the dirt with one finger while he talked. This man sat like something set down carefully by hands that were tired of carrying it.

Elias Galloway was seventy-three years old, and he looked every year of it and then some.

Wendell's body reacted before her mind did. Her feet simply refused to move forward, and refused equally to turn back, so that she stood in the doorway like a hinge between two rooms, the cold evening air moving past her into the corridor and out again.

He saw her. Of course he saw her; he had probably watched the entire proceedings through the glass of the lobby doors, standing where the reflection would hide him, the way he'd taught her to watch birds when she was small — *don't look straight at them, Wren, look just beside them, and they'll forget you're there.* She hadn't thought of that nickname in fifteen years. It arrived now like a stone dropped down a well, taking a long time to hit bottom.

"Dr. Galloway," she said, because she couldn't say *Dad*, and couldn't say nothing either.

"Dr. Galloway," he agreed, and there was something in the echo — an acknowledgment, maybe, or an apology wearing acknowledgment's clothes. "That was quite a lecture."

"It wasn't a lecture."

"No." He looked down at his hands. "It wasn't."

* * *

She didn't remember deciding to sit, but she found herself on the wall beside him, a full arm's length of cold stone between them, which felt both cowardly and correct. The parking lot stretched out before them, half empty now, sodium lamps buzzing on one by one as the light failed. Somewhere behind the building a generator thumped. The ordinary sounds of an ordinary evening, indifferent to the fact that the two most famous disgraced-and-redeemed archaeologists in the discipline were sitting three feet apart and unable to look at each other.

"You followed it," she said. "The controversy. For years."

"Every word anyone printed about it." A dry sound that might have been a laugh. "Which, until tonight, wasn't much. Voss kept it quiet. Quiet was worth money to her." He turned his head, and in the lamplight she could see what the years had done: the deep creases around his eyes had become canyons, the skin of his throat loose, a tremor in his jaw that came and went like weather. "I read your name in the conference program three weeks ago. Wendell Galloway. I sat in my kitchen and read it eleven times. I thought — either she wants to be found, or she'll never forgive me for looking. I decided to come and let you decide which."

"And if I'd walked out the other door?"

"Then I'd have bought a coffee from the machine in the lobby and driven home, and you'd never have known I was here." He said it simply, without self-pity, and that simplicity undid her more than pleading would have. "That was always the arrangement, wasn't it. You got to choose whether I existed."

"Elias—" She stopped. Started again. "Don't do that. Don't make yourself noble about it. You weren't there. You know that, right? You know what you actually did?"

"I know exactly what I did." His voice didn't waver, but his hands did, a fine shiver he pressed flat against his knees. "My wife was dying in a hospital in Nairobi, and I was four hundred kilometers away arguing with a customs officer about a crate of potsherds. And when they called me — when Ravi's predecessor called me, the clinic medic, a good man whose name I've tried to forget for twenty years — I told him I'd come when the shipment was secured." He swallowed. "She died on a Tuesday. I landed on a Thursday. You were seventeen, and you met me at the airport, and you didn't cry, and you said, *the dig was more important, wasn't it? Don't lie to me, everyone else already has.*"

"I don't remember saying that."

"I remember everything," he said quietly. "That's the disease of it. The mind holds on while everything else lets go."

The lamps hummed. A moth threw itself against the glass of the nearest fixture, over and over, with the patience of something that has no other plan.

"The find was real," Wendell said. Not a question. She needed to hear him say it anyway.

"The find was real." He closed his eyes. "Every shard, every inscription, every date. I knew it the moment Tam-level augers — no. Your surveyor's auger. I read the preliminary report Voss leaked to the funding committee before she leaked it anywhere else. Clever of her, using your crew's data to legitimize her own salvage permits. She always did think other people's work was raw material." He opened his eyes. "But you know the worst part, Wren? When I heard the chamber was intact — when I read the inscription naming the archive, the one that proves the 1998 fraud was Halvard's, that the forged tablet was planted in *my* trench by *his* people — do you know what I felt first?"

She waited.

"Relief," he said. "And underneath the relief, this terrible little voice saying: *twenty years, Elias. Twenty years you could have gone back. You had the field journal. You had the coordinates of the cache. You chose exile because exile was easier than fighting, and easier than admitting you'd let them take everything from you because you'd already given away the thing that mattered most.*" His breath caught. "I hid those artifacts to protect them from Voss, and then I hid myself to protect no one from anything, and I let you grow up thinking your father loved stones more than your mother, because it was simpler than the truth. Which is that I loved her so much I couldn't stand in the room with her dying. That I ran toward a hole in the ground because a hospital bed was more than I could—"

His voice broke. Not dramatically — a small structural failure, like a hairline crack in fired clay, the kind that means the whole vessel is compromised. He pressed his knuckles to his mouth and breathed.

Wendell stared at the parking lot. Something enormous was happening inside her chest, some tectonic shift she had spent twenty years refusing to feel the tremors of, and she found she could not look at him while it happened.

"I spent three years measuring my life in miles," she said at last. "Did you know that? Turkana to Nubia, Nubia to the Danakil. I could tell you the distance between any two dig sites in the Horn better than I could tell you how old I was. Miles were clean. Years were—" She shook her head. "Years had you in them."

"I'm sorry." He said it the way he used to describe soil horizons: precisely, completely, leaving nothing out. "I am sorry for all of it, and I am not asking you to unsay twenty years, and I am not asking you to drive me anywhere, and I am not—" The tremor took his jaw again and he mastered it. "I'm not asking, Wren. I want you to understand that. I came to see you stand up in front of two hundred people and tell the truth, and it was the finest thing I've ever witnessed in fifty years of this profession, and I wanted you to know that your mother would have — "

He stopped. Pressed his lips together. Started over, smaller.

"She'd have filled the back row. She'd have brought flowers, embarrassingly early, and heckled Voss, and cried at the stratigraphy slides because she cried at everything beautiful. She'd have been so proud of you that it would've been a public nuisance."

Wendell laughed. It came out of her sideways, wet and startled, and once it started she couldn't stop it, and then she was crying in the way she hadn't allowed herself since the sandstorm — openly, ugly, in front of him — and he didn't touch her, didn't move closer, just sat there with his ruined hands folded and let her do it, giving her the distance she'd asked for at seventeen and had never stopped asking for since.

When it passed, she wiped her face with her sleeve and discovered that somewhere in the middle of it, the arm's length of stone between them had become six inches. Neither of them remarked on it.

* * *

They sat while the lot emptied entirely, while the moth gave up or died, while the temperature dropped enough that their breath began to show. He told her about the cottage outside Exeter, small, near a chalk stream; about the arthritis in his hands that had ended his fieldwork eight years ago; about the diagnosis the doctors had given him in the spring, delivered in the careful voice doctors use, six letters long, not immediately fatal but not interested in waiting forever either. He told it plainly, without performance, and she understood that he was not telling her to make her stay, or to make her forgive, but because he had decided she deserved the truth the way he'd once believed artifacts deserved provenance: complete, documented, however painful to hold.

"That's why the valley," she said. "In your letter. *See it again through your eyes.*"

He nodded slowly.

"My eyes aren't reliable anymore. The cataracts are winning. But yours—" He finally turned to look at her fully, and his gaze was steady even if his hands were not. "Yours are better than mine ever were. You read ground like your mother read weather. So I'm asking for one thing, and it isn't forgiveness, and it isn't absolution, and you can say no and I will get in my taxi and that will be that. I'm asking to walk the wadi with you. One day. Let me stand where the trench was and have you tell me what the ground says now. Let me borrow your eyes before mine close. That's all. That's the whole of it."

Wendell looked out at the dark. In her chest, the door she had welded shut at seventeen stood open, and she examined it the way she'd learned to examine everything — cautiously, professionally, aware that open doors in old structures could mean wind, could mean collapse, could mean simply that someone had finally come home and left it ajar.

"I'm not forgiving you yet," she said. "I want that on the record. There are things I still need to say, and things you need to answer for, and I'm not going to skip to the ending because you're sick and I'm tired. We do this properly. Stratigraphically. Layer by layer."

Something moved across his face — grief and relief and a fierce, ruined joy all at once. "Layer by layer," he agreed. "Of course. You're your mother's daughter."

"And yours," she said, before she could stop herself, and watched him receive it like a man receiving water after a long crossing.

They sat a while longer. Then she stood, and offered him her hand — not to shake, but to help him up, and after a moment's surprise he took it, and she felt the fine bird-light weight of him, the tremor running through his arm like current through a wire, and she held on a beat longer than balance required.

"There's a crew driving north tomorrow," she said. "Odile, Tam, Ravi. They've heard of you. Tam's convinced you're a ghost. Ravi will want to look at your medication and I'd let him, he's insufferable and usually right." She kept her voice level, professional, a foreman discussing logistics, because if she let it soften she might break again. "There's room in the second vehicle. The pass road opens next week, if the weather holds."

Elias Galloway stood in the lamplight holding his daughter's hand, an old man at the edge of the dark, and for the first time in twenty years he smiled the smile from the old photographs — crooked, hopeful, entirely undefended.

"Wadi al-Nakhl," he said, tasting it. "Through your eyes."

Through hers. Through the ground she would read for him, layer by layer, truth by truth, all the way down.

Behind them, through the glass doors of the darkened hall, Wendell could see the cleaning crew starting their rounds — and paused. At the far end of the lobby, beneath the conference banner still hanging from the day's proceedings, a woman in a camel-hair coat stood talking rapidly into a phone, her silhouette sharp and furious against the lit display case. Even at this distance, even in silhouette, Wendell knew the set of those shoulders.

Marguerite Voss had not gone home either.
