## 22. Homecoming Miles

Odile Marchand had driven enough kilometers in her life to circle the equator twice and still have change left over for a second life entirely. She measured roads the way other people measured years — by what they cost, by what they took, by the particular quality of dust they threw up against a windshield at dusk. So when she settled behind the wheel of the crew's battered Land Cruiser on the morning they left the borderlands behind, she did not think of it as an ending. She thought of it as arithmetic.

Two thousand four hundred kilometers to the coast road. Another six hundred after that to the depot where their permits would be stamped and their names — all their names, including one that had spent three years hiding inside another — would finally be entered into the record.

She pulled out onto the track with the engine complaining in its usual register, low and gravel-voiced, like an old man asked to rise before dawn. Behind her, the wadi shrank in the mirror: a brown seam in the brown hills, unremarkable to anyone who hadn't bled into it. Odile looked at it for exactly as long as it deserved, then let the mirror take it.

In the passenger seat, Wendell Galloway had the map open across her knees.

That was the first thing Odile noticed, and she noticed it the way she noticed everything about her people — sideways, without staring, cataloguing. For three years Wendell had navigated the way a fugitive flees: eyes forward, routes chosen by instinct and avoidance, never asking where a road led so long as it led away. She'd once rerouted an entire season because a supply town reminded her, she said later, of somewhere she didn't want to be. Nobody had pressed her. That was the covenant of the crew of the unattached — you didn't ask why a person had no fixed address, same as you didn't ask Ravi why he kept his medical license laminated in a waterproof pouch under the seat, or why Tam slept with a saint's medal clutched in one fist despite claiming not to believe in saints.

But this morning Wendell had spread the map flat. This morning she was tracing the route north with one finger, deliberate as a surveyor, and when she spoke, her voice had a direction in it.

"We can cut east at the escarpment," she said. "Save us half a day, if the ford's still passable."

"You want to gamble the ford?" Odile shifted gears. The truck bucked over a rut and settled.

"I want to see it." Wendell said it simply, without apology, and Odile felt something loosen in her chest that had been tight since the storm, since the canyon, since the night she'd watched Wendell stand in the firelight and confess her name to them like a woman laying down a weight she'd carried so long she'd forgotten it wasn't part of her body. "I've never taken that road. I always went around."

"Because?"

Wendell was quiet for a moment. Wind came through the gap in the window and moved the hair off her forehead, and Odile saw that the new scar along her temple — the canyon's signature, thin and pale — had already begun to fade into the older geography of her face.

"Because going around was the whole point," Wendell said. "Of everything. And I'm tired of it."

Odile grunted, which in her language was agreement, applause, and blessing all at once. She turned east.

* * *

They stopped at midday where the track crossed a dry wash, and Ravi got out first to check Tam's arm. The break from the storm had healed clean — Ravi's work, splinted with tent poles and bound with strapping tape while the sand screamed outside — but he still checked it every day with the fussiness of a man who had once lost a patient and never told anyone which one. Odile watched him turn Tam's wrist gently, watch Tam wince theatrically, watch both of them pretend the ritual wasn't really about something else. About the interrogation room in Voss's compound, where Tam had sat alone for six hours with two hired men and a folder of threats, and had come out with nothing in her mouth but silence.

"You'll make a full recovery," Ravi announced, "provided you stop trying to carry crates."

"The crates were decoys," Tam said. "They were empty."

"They were heavy."

"That's what made them convincing."

Odile left them to it and walked out onto the lip of the wash, where Wendell stood with the map folded down to a square the size of her palm, looking north. The land here opened out in long tawny sweeps, acacia thorn standing black against the grass like punctuation, and the sky did that thing it did in the high country — went on forever, and then kept going.

"He called last night," Wendell said, without turning. Odile had learned that Wendell delivered her hardest sentences facing away, though lately the habit was losing its grip. "Elias. From the clinic. He wants to know if we'll come through on the way back, or if—" She stopped. Started again. "He wants to know if there's anything left to say."

"What did you tell him?"

"That we'd be there Thursday." Wendell's thumb moved over the map's crease, back and forth, wearing the paper smooth. "I told him about the chamber. All of it. He listened for forty minutes and didn't say a word, and then at the end he said—" Her voice caught, just barely, a stone in the current. "He said, *you found it where I couldn't bear to look.*"

The wind moved the grass in long silver strokes. Odile said nothing, because there was nothing in her considerable inventory of hard-won wisdom that belonged in that silence. Some things you drove toward. Some things you waited out.

"He's invited on the return expedition," Wendell went on, steadier now. "If his doctors clear him. I want him to stand in that valley and have it be different for him than it was. I want him to see it through—" She laughed, short and surprised at herself. "Through my eyes. That's what he asked for. Like I'm some kind of view worth seeing."

"You are," Odile said.

Wendell looked at her then, and Odile held the look, because she'd earned the right to. Three years of shared tents and shared water and shared silences; a canyon flood survived together; a fraud exposed together; a name given back together. You didn't look away from family when they finally let themselves be seen. That was the entire job.

"Thursday," Odile repeated. "I'll fuel up Wednesday night."

* * *

The escarpment road was everything the map promised and half of what it threatened — narrow, washed out in places, but passable, and beautiful in the way that only roads nobody takes are beautiful. They camped that night on a granite shelf above a river gorge, and Odile cooked while the others built the fire, and the meal was nothing special — rice, tinned tomatoes, the last of the good tea — but everyone ate it slowly, the way you eat when you're not eating to survive a schedule.

Tam produced a battered harmonica from somewhere and played three songs badly and one song well, and Ravi sang harmony in a voice that suggested a past involving choir school and a falling-out, and neither fact was asked about, per the covenant. But the covenant had changed shape, Odile thought, watching them. It used to be a wall they all hid behind. Now it was more like a roof — shelter freely given, rather than concealment mutually enforced. Wendell had paid for that change with her name, and the rest of them had matched it, piece by piece, without ever discussing it aloud.

Later, when the fire had burned down to its red arithmetic, Odile found Wendell awake at the edge of the shelf, knees drawn up, watching stars come out over the gorge.

"Can't sleep either?" Odile lowered herself onto the rock beside her. Below them the river muttered in the dark, running full from the early storm.

"I'm counting," Wendell said.

"Miles?"

"Miles." A pause. "It's what I do. Did. Three years of measuring my life in distances from things. Distance from Elias. Distance from the symposium circuit. Distance from anyone who might ask me who I was." She turned her hands over in the firelight, palms up, as if checking them for calluses or evidence. "And now I'm sitting here doing the same thing, except—" She shook her head, frustrated with the shape of the sentence.

"Except now the numbers go somewhere," Odile finished.

Wendell exhaled, something between a laugh and surrender. "Yeah. Exactly that. Two hundred forty kilometers to the clinic. Six hundred to the depot. Eleven hundred to the university archive where the provenance chain gets formally lodged. Every single one of them leading to a thing instead of away from it." She looked over. "Is that how it works? When you stop running? The miles don't change, but they mean differently?"

Odile considered the question with the seriousness it deserved. She had driven away from a marriage once, and toward a war once, and around a grief for so many years it had worn a groove in her like water wears stone. She knew distances the way priests knew psalms.

"It's how it works," she said. "The road doesn't care which way you're living. But you do. And that's enough."

Wendell nodded slowly, and then, because she was who she was, she pulled out the notebook — the field journal, a new one, its first pages already dense with her small precise handwriting — and began to write by firelight. Odile leaned over, shamelessly, and read upside down:

*Return expedition, Wadi al-Nakhl. Personnel: Marchand (foreman), Okonkwo-Rao (medic), Tam (survey). Pending clearance: E. Galloway. Objectives: full documentation of lower gallery; conservation assessment; establish permanent marker naming the find correctly.*

Naming the find correctly. Not *the Voss discovery*, which would never be written anywhere again except in retraction notices. Not even *the Galloway find*, though God knew Elias had earned it back. Just: correctly. As if truth were a place on a map and all you had to do was navigate honestly to reach it.

Maybe, Odile thought, it was.

* * *

They reached the clinic town on Thursday afternoon, and Odile parked the Cruiser in the shade of a fig tree whose roots had cracked the pavement, and they all sat in the vehicle for a moment without moving, which was its own kind of ritual. Then Wendell opened her door and got out, and the others followed, and they walked up the cracked path to the low white building together, because that was what crews did.

Elias Galloway was waiting in the garden.

He was smaller than Odile had imagined, and grayer, and he stood with the careful economy of a man rationing his strength, one hand resting on a cane that looked recently acquired. Twenty years of disgrace had done to his face what weather does to a cliff face — stripped it down to structure. But when he saw Wendell coming up the path, the whole ruined architecture of him rearranged itself around one expression, plain and undisguised, and Odile had to look away, out at the fig tree, because some things were not hers to witness and also were.

"Wendell," he said.

"Dad." She stopped an arm's length away. Neither of them moved to close the distance. It wasn't coldness, Odile saw. It was precision. Two people who understood that some structures needed to be approached along surveyed lines or they'd collapse. "You look terrible."

A sound came out of him that was mostly laugh. "Your mother used to say the same thing. Right before she fixed me tea." His eyes went past Wendell to the three of them standing in a loose line among the flowerbeds, and something in his face shifted — recognition, maybe, of a shape he knew. "These are your people."

"This is Odile. She runs everything, including me. Ravi keeps us alive. Tam found your cut stone when every instrument we owned said it shouldn't be there."

"Then I'm in debt to all of you," Elias said, and bowed slightly, an old-fashioned gesture that sat oddly on his broken frame and perfectly on his dignity. "More than I can calculate."

"You can start calculating on the return expedition," Wendell said. "If your doctors sign off. We leave in six weeks. Full season. I want the lower gallery mapped properly, and I want—" She paused, and Odile watched her choose the next words with the care of someone placing a fragile artifact in foam. "I want you there when we open the publication drafts. Your name goes on the primary attribution. That's not negotiable."

Elias's hand tightened on the cane. For a long moment he said nothing, and the garden held its breath — bees in the lavender, wind in the fig leaves, the far traffic of the town going about its indifferent business.

"I spent twenty years thinking I'd die with that valley hating me," he said finally, quietly. "And instead my daughter walks into it and pulls the truth out of the ground like a splinter." He looked at Wendell, and his voice roughened. "I don't deserve—"

"No," Wendell agreed. "You don't. Neither do I, probably. Neither does anybody." She stepped forward then, one step, closing half the distance, leaving the rest as an invitation rather than a debt. "But we're going back anyway. Both of us. And you're going to tell me everything — the real story, all of it, starting with 1998 and ending with why you really left. And I'm going to listen. And it's going to be unfinished for a long time, maybe forever. And that's the deal."

Elias Galloway looked at his daughter for a long time. Then he set his cane aside — deliberately, carefully, leaning it against the garden wall — and closed the remaining distance, and put his arms around her, and she let him, stiffly at first and then not stiffly at all, and above them the fig tree dropped a leaf that spiraled down between them like something being signed.

Odile turned fully away this time. Ravi had materialized beside her, and Tam, and the three of them stood in a row studying the flowerbeds with tremendous scholarly interest until it was safe to look back.

* * *

They left the next morning, all four of them in the Cruiser, plus one duffel bag of Elias's effects that would travel ahead to the expedition staging point, because he'd insisted on packing himself and had apparently packed like a man who expected to be excavated someday. Wendell rode shotgun with the map open, and this time she'd marked it — actual ink, actual circles, the route north annotated with dates and fuel stops and one star drawn at the escarpment ford with the word *again* beside it.

The country unrolled. Long tawny miles, black thorn trees, the mountains rising blue in the distance like a promise the land intended to keep. Odile drove and let the engine sing its gravel song, and around kilometer ninety Wendell started talking — about the expedition logistics, the conservation budget, the paper she and Elias would co-author, the possibility of a second site in the hills east of the wadi that Tam's auger data hinted at — and her voice had a velocity in it that Odile had never heard in three years of knowing her. Not flight-velocity. Direction-velocity.

At the top of the pass they stopped, because Wendell asked to, and got out, and stood looking back south at the way they'd come: the borderlands, the haze, the faint brown seam where a valley held everything it remembered.

"Counting?" Odile asked, coming to stand beside her.

"Always." Wendell smiled — the real one, the rare one, the one that rearranged her whole face into someone younger and less defended. "Four hundred twelve kilometers to the depot. Nine hundred to the archive. Six weeks to the wadi." She folded the map with a snap, decisive as a gavel. "Every mile of it going somewhere."

She got back in the truck. Odile followed, turned the key, and pointed them north — into the light, into the season ahead, into the long unfinished road that, for the first time in anyone's memory, the whole crew was driving down together, on purpose, with the windows down and the future coming at them fast enough to feel like wind.
