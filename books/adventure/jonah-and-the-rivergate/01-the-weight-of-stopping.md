## 1. The Weight of Stopping

The tracks told the whole story before Yarrow ever saw the man who'd made them.

He crouched at the edge of the alkali flat where the hardpan gave way to sand, and read them the way other men read letters from home. A shod horse, near sixteen hands, off-fore shoe worn on the outside heel — a animal that favored its left, or a rider who leaned wrong. The stride was long but uneven, lengthening and shortening in a rhythm that meant the rider had been pushing too hard for too long. And beneath the horse's prints, smaller ones: a man walking beside it more often than riding, saving what he could of the animal because he knew he'd need it later.

A thief with sense. That made him rare, and it made him harder to catch, and it made Yarrow almost glad to be out here.

Three days now. The man had stolen the bay gelding from the livery at Rivergate Crossing on a Tuesday night while the owner slept off his whiskey, and by Wednesday morning half the territory knew of it, because the owner was the brother-in-law of a man who mattered, and a horse worth forty dollars became a matter of principle the moment powerful people were embarrassed. Notices went up. Rewards were whispered. And Marguerite Yarrow, desert ranger, nine years a ranger, had saddled up before dawn because tracking was the one thing he still did without thinking, and not-thinking was the only way he knew how to live.

He rose and walked the line of prints another quarter mile, letting the sun climb his back like something patient. The flats stretched westward, white and blinding, heat shimmer standing on them in columns so that the distant mesas seemed to breathe. There was no shade within twenty miles. There was water at Sulfur Spring, thirty miles southwest, if the spring hadn't gone brackish again — and the tracks bent southwest.

The thief was making for Sulfur Spring. Which meant the thief didn't know it had gone bad in August, which meant the thief hadn't ranged this country before, which meant Yarrow could stop worrying about being clever and simply be thorough.

Thoroughness was all he had left. He'd traded everything else away, piece by piece, over nine years, until what remained was a man who could read ground and keep moving and wanted nothing enough that wanting nothing had become its own kind of hunger.

He swung into the saddle. The dun mare — his fourth horse in three years; horses wore out, and so did names, and he'd stopped naming them after the second one died under him — picked her way down onto the flat and settled into the long trot that ate miles without spending either of them.

*Keep moving.* That was the rule. He'd written it nowhere, sworn it to no one, but it governed him more faithfully than any law he'd ever been paid to enforce. Keep moving and you're a shape on the horizon, a rumor, a rider nobody looks at twice. Stop, and you become a fact. Facts can be checked. Facts have histories. Facts get asked their names.

Nine years ago he had stopped, just once, just long enough, and a man had died, and since then Yarrow had been a rumor with a warrant somewhere behind it, growing older in every direction except the one that mattered.

* * *

He found the first camp at dusk, twelve miles short of the spring.

It was a good camp, hidden in the lee of a fallen rock slab, fireless, the horse picketed on a scrap of bunchgrass some previous traveler's mule had missed. Yarrow read it from horseback without dismounting: the man had slept four hours, no more, then moved on in the dark. Afraid of pursuit. Careful anyway. He'd scraped a hollow in the sand and lain in it, which broke the wind and broke his outline both — a trick Yarrow himself used, and seeing it used by someone else produced an odd feeling, like hearing your own voice on a phonograph.

There was a tin can by the hollow, licked clean. Beans. And beside it, pressed flat into the sand where the wind hadn't reached, a boot print with a split sole. The left boot. Water would be getting through that split soon, and sand with it, and by Sulfur Spring the man would be walking on raw foot if he walked at all.

Yarrow sat his horse in the failing light and did the arithmetic he always did, the cold ledger of it. Thirty miles to the spring. Brackish water there, drinkable if a man was desperate, sickening if he wasn't careful. Beyond the spring, sixty miles of nothing until the stage road. The thief had maybe two gallons in his canteens. Two gallons was four days in this heat if he drank like a wise man, two days if he drank like a scared one, and scared men drank like scared men.

By the ledger, Yarrow didn't need to hurry. The desert would do the work. It always did. It was the most reliable deputy in the territory, and it never asked questions about jurisdiction.

But the ledger wasn't why he nudged the mare forward into the dark.

He told himself it was professionalism. A ranger brings them in alive when he can; dead men can't stand trial, and the reward notices had said *alive if possible*, which was the kind of phrase that meant nobody would ask hard questions if the possibility quietly failed. He told himself it was the horse — the bay deserved better than to die of thirst under a fool. He told himself several things as the stars came out hard and cold above the flats, and the mare's hooves kept their soft metronome, and none of the things he told himself were the true thing, which was simpler and worse:

That catching the man would take a day, maybe two. That after that there would be another track, another job, another horizon, and the moving would go on, and the moving was the only conversation he had left with the world.

* * *

He caught him at noon the next day, five miles past Sulfur Spring, where the country began to lift into low broken hills the color of old rust.

Not because Yarrow was faster. Because the thief had done exactly what Yarrow knew he would do: drunk the brackish water against all sense, because two days of fear will beat any amount of wisdom, and spent the night after it vomiting and shivering between spells of sleep so heavy it was closer to collapse. His tracks came out of the spring wash staggering, the long confident strides gone, the walking-beside-the-horse replaced by hanging onto the stirrup. The last mile he'd ridden, slumped, letting the bay choose the way, and the bay — sensible animal — had chosen the shade of an overhanging bank where a dry creek cut through the hills, and stopped there, and stood.

Yarrow came up the creek bed slow, boots silent on the cracked clay, and stepped around the bend with his rifle already level, and said, in a voice he kept flat and unhurried, "Don't."

The man came off the horse like something falling out of a tree. Hit the ground on hands and knees, scrambled, got a look at the rifle and the badge-less shirt and the dust-caked face above it, and stopped. He knelt there in the shade, swaying, and Yarrow took him in the way he took in tracks: young, maybe twenty-five, sandy-haired, sunburned to the color of the hills themselves, lips cracked and gray. Left boot split at the sole. Eyes that had gone past fear into the flat country beyond it, where fear couldn't follow anymore.

"You're the law," the man said. Not a question. His voice was ruined, sanded down to a whisper.

"I'm the one that's been following you since Rivergate Crossing."

"Three days." The man laughed, or tried to, and it came out as a cough. "I watched my backtrail every hour. I never once saw you."

"Nobody does." Yarrow kept the rifle where it was and looked at the bay. Good horse. Standing quiet, head low, grateful for the shade. "Dismount proper and sit against the bank. Hands where I can see them."

The man did it, moving like his joints had rusted. When he was seated, Yarrow came closer, set the rifle across his saddle horn within easy reach, and crouched a few feet away. Close enough to talk. Far enough to move.

"Name," Yarrow said.

Silence. Then, because silence is a currency the desperate can't hold: "Tobin. Eli Tobin."

"Eli Tobin stole a bay gelding from Amos Creed's livery in Rivergate Crossing on the night of the twelfth."

"Yes." No denial. Yarrow noted that. Liars deny fast; honest men and broken men let the words sit.

"Why?"

Tobin's hands opened and closed in the dirt. "My sister's boy is sick. Fever in his lungs. Doctor in Crossing wants fifty dollars before he'll ride out to our place, and Amos Creed offered me thirty for the season to break horses, and I —" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "I was going to sell the bay in Terrapin and send the money home. I know how it sounds. Everybody's story sounds like that. But it's mine, and it's true, and I don't expect it counts for anything."

Yarrow said nothing. The heat stood on the hills. Somewhere above the wash a rock wren ran through its few notes and quit, as if even the birds found the country too poor to sing in.

"It counts," Yarrow said finally, "for exactly what it counts for. Which is that you'll tell it again to a judge, and the judge has heard it four hundred times, and some of those times it was even true."

"And you?" Tobin looked up. There was something in his face beyond the desperation now — a curiosity, the last luxury of a man who thinks he's already lost. "You tracked me three days across open ground alone. You don't wear a star. You talk like a book. Who are you?"

And there it was. The question. Nine years of riding and the question always arrived eventually, wearing different faces, and every time it did, Yarrow felt the same small cold thing turn over inside him, like a stone shifting in a riverbed.

"A ranger," he said. "Out of the Rivergate station."

"Tobin," the man repeated, tasting it. "Ranger Tobin. Well." He managed something like a smile, gray-lipped. "At least they sent somebody good. If it had to happen, I'm glad it was you and not some posse that'd shoot me for the fun of the paperwork."

*Keep moving,* said the rule, in the voice of nine years. *Cuff him, take the bay, take him to Crossing, collect whatever the Creeds pay, and be on the horizon by dark. Keep moving.*

Yarrow looked at the man's split boot. At the tin can he'd carried three days rather than leave litter of himself on the land. At the bay, standing patient, that had been loved before it was stolen — it stood for Yarrow's hand the way horses only stand for people who've been kind to them.

"Your nephew," Yarrow said. "How old?"

"Six." Tobin's voice cracked on it. "He calls the horse in the story I wrote him *the red one*. I stole a bay. I couldn't even steal right."

Something happened then that Yarrow had no rule for. It happened every time, and it never got easier, and he had never once found a way to make it not happen: the ledger in him closed itself, and underneath the arithmetic there was just a man kneeling in the dirt of a dry creek, and the distance between that man and the man Yarrow had been nine years ago was a distance Yarrow could cross in a single step, and did, every time, whether he wanted to or not.

"The doctor wants fifty," Yarrow said.

"Thirty-five, he said, if it's paid by month's end."

Yarrow reached into his coat, into the inner pocket where he kept the flat oilskin packet that held everything he owned that wasn't on his horse, and took out the roll of bills wrapped in a rubber band, and peeled off forty dollars, and held it out.

Tobin stared at it. Didn't reach for it. Men who've been caught learn fast that gifts from lawmen are traps with softer teeth.

"That's yours," Tobin said. "That's — mister, that's more than the horse was worth."

"The horse goes back to Creed. You walk to the stage road — there's water cached at the old windmill two miles east, I put it there yesterday, knowing you'd come through short. You take the stage east and you don't come back to this territory, Eli Tobin, because next time the man who finds you won't crouch down to talk."

"I don't understand," Tobin said. And then, because he was young and honest and broken in the right places: "Why? Why would you do this?"

And Yarrow, who had answers for everything except the true questions, heard himself say the closest thing to truth he'd said aloud in years:

"Because stopping is the hardest thing there is. And you haven't learned how yet. And I'm not going to teach you the way I learned."

He put the bills in Tobin's hand and closed the man's fingers over them.

* * *

He watched Tobin out of sight — east, toward the windmill, limping on the split boot but walking straighter than he had any right to — and then he stood a long time in the shade of the bank with the bay's reins in his hand, and the loneliness came up out of the ground the way the heat did, total and without argument.

This was the part nobody saw. Not the tracking, which was clean work, work he was made for. This: the hour after, when the mercy was done and there was no one to have done it for, no witness, no word of it ever passing between him and another living soul. Restless mercy, mercy that cost him forty dollars and bought him nothing but another day of being a rumor. He'd let six men go in nine years. He remembered every one of their faces the way other men remember the faces of their children. They were, in a way he never said and barely let himself think, the only family he had: people whose lives he'd bent slightly aside from ruin and then walked away from forever.

He rode the bay back north through the afternoon, leading his dun, and the flats turned gold and then violet, and the mesas breathed their heat-shimmer breath, and by full dark he could see the light of the Rivergate station far off, one yellow smudge against the black — lamp-oil and whiskey and the snoring of Supervisor Dreele, who was half-drunk by ten most nights and entirely drunk by midnight on the good ones.

Yarrow thought about the notice board at the station. About the new paper that had gone up on it two weeks ago, the one Dreele had taken down quick when Yarrow walked in, too quick, the way a man covers a card. He'd thought nothing of it then. He thought nothing of it now, mostly.

Mostly.

He watered the horses at the trough, rubbed them down, and pushed open the station door into the smell of lamp oil and tobacco, and Dreele looked up from his desk with a bottle at his elbow and an expression Yarrow had never seen on him before — an expression that was trying very hard to be casual and failing at it the way a bad liar fails.

"Yarrow," Dreele said. "Sit down. We ought to talk."

Outside, the wind came down off the flats and rattled the notice board, and somewhere under the sound of it, faint as water under stone, the past began to move.
