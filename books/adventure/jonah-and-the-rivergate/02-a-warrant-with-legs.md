## 2. A Warrant with Legs

The Rivergate station sat at the edge of the world like a tooth that hadn't decided whether to fall out or hold on. It was a long, low building of adobe and salvaged rail timber, its roof weighted down with stones against the wind, and it smelled always of the same three things: lamp oil, horse sweat, and the dust that came in through every seam no matter how much mud Marguerite Yarrow packed into them. He had been stationed there four years now, long enough to know which floorboards creaked under a man's weight at two in the morning, long enough to stop noticing the smell entirely — until he came back from the flats, as he had tonight, and the smell hit him again like a hand on the chest.

He put his horse away first. That was the rule of him, one of the few rules left: the animal before the man. He curried the dust out of her coat by lamplight, checked her hooves for the fine cracked stone of the flats, gave her water in small measures because he had seen men kill horses with kindness and he did not intend to be one of them. The stable was quiet except for the soft working of her jaw and somewhere far off the wind moving through the greasewood, a sound like a crowd whispering behind a door.

Nine years. He counted them the way other men counted money or sins, which was to say constantly, and without ever arriving at a total that satisfied him. Nine years since the night in Cane Hollow. Nine years of keeping his name folded small inside himself like a letter he was afraid to open. The warrant had a name on it — *Yarrow* — but names were strange things out here. Half the territory went by handles earned or invented, and a ranger who signed his reports with nothing but an initial raised no eyebrows worth raising. The desert kept him the way the desert kept everything: not kindly, but thoroughly.

He shouldered his saddlebag and crossed the yard.

* * *

The station's common room was lit by a single lamp turned low, and Supervisor Amos Dree was sitting beneath it like something the river had left behind. Dree had been a good ranger once, everyone said so, back before his knees went and his wife went and whatever else had gone that no one named aloud. Now he supervised the way a fencepost supervises a field: present, upright, growing slowly more weathered. Tonight he had a bottle open beside a cup that had stopped pretending to hold coffee some hours ago, and he was reading the same page of a supply ledger he had probably been reading since noon.

"You're back," Dree said, without looking up. "Flats?"

"Flats."

"Anything?"

"Wind." Yarrow hung his hat on the peg by the door, then thought better of it and moved it to the second peg, the one farther from the draft. Small habits. A man's whole life was small habits stacked like sandbags against a flood. "Lost a thief's trail at the salt pan. He'll surface again when he gets hungry enough."

Dree grunted. It might have been agreement; it might have been the bottle talking through him in its usual dialect. Yarrow went to the stove, found the coffee pot, and poured what remained into himself rather than the pot. It was the color of tannin and tasted like it had been brewed during a previous administration. He drank it anyway. Out here you learned to take what the day offered and call it provision.

He was halfway to the stairs when Dree said his name.

Not his name. The name. The word itself, spoken flat and careless into the lamplight, and Yarrow felt it go through him the way a splinter goes through a palm — not deep, not yet, but unmistakably there, and already beginning to fester.

"Yarrow," Dree said again, tasting it. "Funny thing. Had a wire come through yesterday. Marshal's office, over in Terrapin Fork." He turned the ledger page he wasn't reading. "Asking after a ranger named Yarrow. Nine years back. You'd have been what, nineteen? Twenty?"

The room got very slightly smaller. Yarrow had lived enough of his life in rooms that were getting smaller to recognize the sensation and to know better than to show it. He set his cup down on the shelf by the stove, carefully, because a man's hands will betray him if he lets them move fast.

"Lot of Yarrows in the world," he said.

"Sure." Dree looked up then, and his eyes were drunk but not stupid, which was the worst combination a man could wear. There had been a time, Yarrow suspected, when Amos Dree had been sharp as a skinning knife, and the knife was still in there somewhere, dulled but whole. "Sure there are. But how many rangers named Yarrow passed through this station nine years ago?" He let the question sit. He seemed to enjoy letting questions sit; it was the only part of supervision he still took pleasure in. "One, near as I can figure. And here you are."

"Here I am," Yarrow agreed. His voice came out level. He was grateful to it. He was often grateful to his voice, which had learned long ago to work independently of whatever was happening underneath it. "What does the marshal want with a ranger from nine years ago?"

"That's the thing." Dree picked up the bottle, considered it, put it down. Some private negotiation concluded unsatisfactorily. "Wire didn't say. Just asked if we had record of one. Asked where he might be reached." A dry sound that was almost a laugh. "Where any of us might be reached. Like we're letters."

Yarrow stood very still. Outside, the wind found the corner of the building and worried it, a low rhythmic knocking, patient as anything. He thought about the thief on the flats that afternoon, the man on his knees in the alkali with his hands up and his eyes wet, saying *please, mister, please*, and how Yarrow had looked down at him and felt the old arithmetic turn over in his chest — *one more body, one more story, one more reason to keep moving* — and had instead cut the man's bindings and pointed him south toward water he could trust.

Restless mercy, he'd heard it called once, though not to his face. Mercy that never stayed anywhere long enough to be thanked.

"What did you tell them?" he asked.

Dree blinked at him. "Told them what I know. Which is nothing. Told them records out here are what they are — half burned, half eaten by mice, half lies." He held up three fingers, frowned at them, lowered two. "Man comes asking official questions in this territory, you give him official answers, and official answers are mostly fog. That's not dishonesty. That's climate."

Something in Yarrow's chest loosened by a single degree. Not relief — he had forgotten what relief felt like, or perhaps he had never known it, only its rumor — but a fractional loosening, the way a bootlace gives when you shift your weight. Then it drew tight again, tighter than before, because Dree was still looking at him, and there was something new in the look. Something almost kind. Kindness, from a supervisor, was more frightening than suspicion. Suspicion you could answer. Kindness meant the man had already answered for you, in his own head, and decided what he thought.

"Sit down, Yarrow."

"I'm tired, is all—"

"Sit down." Dree nudged the bottle an inch across the table, an offer and a test in one motion. "Have a drink. It's bad. Everything out here is bad; that's why we're out here, so the bad has somewhere to live besides town."

Yarrow sat. He took the bottle, poured a finger's worth into Dree's cup — not his own; he wanted his head clear tonight, needed it clear the way a drowning man needs air, though he could not have said why yet, only that the need had arrived ahead of its reasons, the way storms arrive — and slid it back across the wood.

Dree drank. Wiped his mouth with the back of his hand. Looked at the lamp flame for a while as if it owed him an explanation.

"I'm going to tell you something," he said finally, "and you can forget I said it, because officially I never opened my mouth. Unofficially—" he gestured at the room, the station, the whole sagging apparatus of the Rivergate district, "—unofficially is all I've got left."

Yarrow waited. Waiting was a skill. He had refined it for nine years.

"There's work coming," Dree said. "Big work. Government survey outfit lost a team out past the badlands — eleven souls, instruments, the whole parade. Vanished clean. No bodies, no tracks worth the name, no word. The agency's spinning up a search, and they'll want local men, men who know the ground." He tapped the table with one knuckle. "They'll want *you*. Everyone knows it. Best tracker in the district, maybe the territory, and too dumb or too stubborn to have taken a desk job anywhere."

"And?"

"And the survey chief is coming through here herself within the week to hire her trackers. Ada Okonjo. Big name, bigger connections. And where big names travel, marshals travel." Dree's eyes came around slow as a weathervane in no wind. "So there's going to be a marshal sitting in this station, drinking bad coffee, reading our roster, asking friendly questions. And one of those questions is going to be about a ranger named Yarrow, and I am going to have to stand there with my thumb up my ambition and think of something to say."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere in the walls, a mouse conducted its small affairs.

"You don't owe me anything," Yarrow said.

"No," Dree agreed. "I don't. But I watched you work four years, and in four years I never saw you take credit, never saw you lie on a report, never saw you leave a horse lame or a man stranded. Whatever you did nine years ago—" he raised the cup slightly, a toast to nothing in particular, "—whatever it was, either it wasn't what they say, or it was, and you've paid for it in miles. Either way, I'd rather the marshal ask me his questions while I'm sober enough to waste his time properly." He drained the cup. "Which means I need to stay drunk until he leaves. It's a sacrifice. I make it gladly."

Yarrow almost smiled. It didn't fully arrive, but it sent word that it was coming.

"Why are you telling me this?"

Dree set the cup down and looked at him for a long moment, and in that moment Yarrow saw the man he must have been twenty years ago, riding some border nobody wanted, young and straight-backed and certain the territory could be saved if only a fellow worked hard enough.

"Because I'm going to give you advice," Dree said, "and advice is the one thing in this world cheaper than whiskey, and I want you to understand you're getting what you paid for." He leaned forward. The lamplight caught the broken veins in his face, the map of every hard year drawn in red. "Take the work. Whatever work comes. Take the survey job, take the next one, take the one after that. Because a man standing still is a man a warrant can find. A warrant with legs can't catch a man who won't quit walking. You've known that nine years — hell, you invented it. So keep inventing it. Ride out with Okonjo's people, get paid, come back, ride out again. Stay useful and stay gone, in that order, and maybe the paper in Terrapin Fork stays a paper."

"And if it doesn't?"

Dree shrugged, vast and sad. "Then it doesn't. But I'd rather you be two hundred miles into the badlands chasing ghosts when it doesn't than sitting right here where the finding's easy." He picked up the bottle again, and his voice dropped, went rougher, went honest in the way only drunk men and dying men bother to be. "There's another thing. You won't like it."

"Probably not."

"The wire asked about Yarrow by name. But wires travel both directions, and wires get read." He turned the bottle slowly in his fingers, watching the lamplight swim in the glass. "Whoever's asking — marshal, agency, whoever's paying the marshal — they knew the name before they wired. Which means somebody told them. Somebody who knew a ranger named Yarrow passed through here nine years ago and signed on here four years back." He looked up. "Somebody close enough to know your habits."

The cold that went through Yarrow then was not fear exactly. Fear was a familiar animal; he had fed it and starved it by turns for nearly a decade and knew its gaits. This was older and quieter — the particular chill of realizing that the wall you had built around yourself had a door in it, and the door had been open the whole time, and you had simply never thought to check.

He kept his face still. He had gotten very good at that. It was, along with tracking and lying awake, among his three great accomplishments.

"Station rosters aren't secret," he said.

"No. They aren't." Dree drank again, and this time his hand wasn't quite steady, and Yarrow understood suddenly that the man was frightened too — frightened for him, or frightened of the machinery now turning somewhere east of them, the slow iron gears of warrants and agencies and concessions, gears that ground slow but ground fine. "Get some sleep, Yarrow. Okonjo arrives Thursday. Be clean-shaven and agreeable. Agreeable men get hired, and hired men get to be two hundred miles away." He waved a hand, dismissal and benediction in one. "And Yarrow — when you find those eleven people, and you will, because you're the best I've got and the best I've got is the best there is — find them alive. Dead men make paperwork, and paperwork makes marshals, and marshals make wires, and wires—" he tapped the table, twice, softly, "—wires make trouble for men with names."

Yarrow rose. Took his hat off the second peg. At the door he paused, because some instinct older than caution made him pause, and looked back at the supervisor slumped under the lamp with his bottle and his unread ledger, guarding a stranger's secret the only way he knew how: badly, drunkenly, and completely.

"Dree."

"Mm."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me. Thank the mice that ate my records." He flapped a hand. "Go on. Sleep. Tomorrow we pretend none of this happened, same as every day."

* * *

Yarrow climbed the narrow stair to the room he kept at the end of the hall — the one with the window facing east, chosen years ago for reasons he had never examined and refused to examine now. He shut the door. Sat on the cot. Did not light the lamp.

Moonlight came through the glass in a pale wedge and lay across the floorboards like something spilled. He listened to the station settle around him: the tick of cooling timber, the wind at the eaves, Dree below pouring one more drink with the exaggerated care of the very tired. Ordinary sounds. Safe sounds. He catalogued them the way he catalogued sign on a trail, out of habit, out of the bone-deep conviction that safety was just danger you hadn't read correctly yet.

*A warrant with legs.* That was what Dree had called it, and the phrase lodged in him and would not come loose. Warrants were supposed to be paper — inert, filed, waiting. Paper you could outrun, outlast, out-mouse. But this one had legs. It had walked nine years across the territory and arrived, at last, at a wire into a half-drunk supervisor's hands, and now it stood somewhere out in the dark between here and Terrapin Fork, sniffing the wind, narrowing its search.

He thought about the thief again. The man's face, alkali-white, tears cutting clean lines through the dust. *Please, mister.* And Yarrow, standing over him with a rope in his hand, feeling the whole weight of nine years lean on that moment — because every capture was a small rehearsal, wasn't it, every man brought to heel a dry run for the day someone brought *him* to heel. He had let the thief go not out of goodness, whatever Dree would have said, but because he could not bear, even once more, even in miniature, to be the law closing its hand around a running man.

Tomorrow he would sharpen his knives and check his gear and say yes to Ada Okonjo before she finished asking. Two hundred miles into the badlands after eleven vanished souls. It was the best hiding place in the territory: a job so large it swallowed a man whole.

But sleep, when it finally came near dawn, brought him no comfort. It brought him Cane Hollow — the rain that year, the lantern swinging, a voice saying his name in the dark in a tone he had spent nine years refusing to remember — and he woke with his heart going like a drum in a parade and the bootlace drawn so tight around his chest he had to sit up and breathe slow, counting, until the room remembered it was only a room.

Thursday, he reminded himself. Okonjo on Thursday. Work, and distance, and the mercy of being needed.

Outside, the wind changed direction, and carried with it, faint and impossible, the smell of green water from a country he had never seen.
