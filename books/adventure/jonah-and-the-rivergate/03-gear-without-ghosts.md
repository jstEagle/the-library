## 3. Gear Without Ghosts

The cache sat in a shallow draw forty miles north of the Rivergate station, and it was wrong the way a room is wrong when someone has just left it — not ruined, not scattered, but *vacated*, as if eleven people had stood up mid-sentence and walked out of their own lives.

Yarrow circled it twice on horseback before he let his mount stop. Habit. Nine years of habit. You never rode straight at anything in open country; you came at it sideways, the way light comes through a crack, so that whatever was waiting for you would have to guess. Nothing waited here. He could feel the emptiness of the place from two hundred yards, the particular silence of ground that had been abandoned rather than lost.

He dismounted anyway, and hobbled the mare, and walked the last stretch with his boots making small sounds in the gravel, because some part of him still wanted to be polite to whatever had happened here.

The draw held three canvas wall tents, guy-lines taut, stakes driven deep and true. A mess fly stretched between two of them, and beneath it a folding table bearing a brass transit in its case, a barometer, a sextant wrapped in oilcloth, an aneroid whose needle he could see trembling faintly in the wind through its crystal face. Crates of canned peaches and condensed milk, stacked by weight, heaviest on the bottom. A lantern with its wick trimmed. A water barrel — and this stopped him longer than anything else — a water barrel better than half full, bung tight, no dust ring showing where the level had dropped.

Nobody leaves water in the Rivergate badlands. Nobody leaves water anywhere between here and the Sere Basin unless they are dead or they have found more of it than God intended this country to hold.

He crouched at the edge of the fly's shade and looked without touching, the way he'd been taught by a tracker out of the Pahute country who used to say you read a camp like a face: the eyes first, then the mouth, then whatever the hands have been doing. The eyes were fine. The tents were orderly. The instruments were clean and oiled and put away with the care of men who expected to come back to them.

The hands were the problem.

Eleven bedrolls lay where they'd been laid, most of them still rolled, tied with their straps. Boots — Yarrow counted twice, walking the perimeter slow — boots enough for eleven people stood in pairs along the tent walls or tucked under cot frames, laces tucked in, some of them nearly new. Eleven people had walked out of this camp into the hardest country in the territory wearing what looked, from the sign, like socks and broken-in field shoes.

And no tracks led away.

That wasn't quite true, and he made himself correct it, because sloppy thinking got rangers killed. Tracks led away in every direction — but they were old, weeks old, weathered to gray ghosts, wind-nibbled at the edges until they read less like sign than like memory. They went out toward the rimrock and faded. They went south toward the alkali and vanished there entirely, because alkali keeps nothing. They went nowhere useful. It was as if the team had dissolved at the edge of the firelight and blown away on the same wind that had polished their boots' absence from the trail.

He built no fire. He ate cold beans from a tin and drank from his own skins, not the barrel, because the barrel belonged to the mystery and he didn't yet know whether the mystery was generous. Around midnight a coyote sang from the rimrock and something about the sound made him look up at the stars and think, unaccountably, of the horse thief's face when he'd let him go — that raw, astonished gratitude, the look of a man who had already said goodbye to himself and been handed back. Yarrow turned his back on the thought. Gratitude was a debt he hadn't asked to carry.

In the morning he measured the fire rings. Three of them, cold, ash raked and doused properly — disciplined people, careful people, people who understood that fire discipline in dry country is a form of prayer. The ash was old. Six weeks, maybe seven. Which meant the team had lived here, worked here, kept their instruments oiled and their boots paired and their water hoarded, and then one day folded themselves out of existence without taking so much as a tin of peaches.

Professional unease was the honest name for what sat in his chest. But underneath it, quieter, older, was the other thing — the thing he didn't name, the thing that had made him a ranger in the first place before it made him a fugitive. Fascination. The badlands had handed him a knot, and some stubborn strand of him wanted nothing in the world except to work it loose.

* * *

Ada Okonjo received him in the survey office at Rivergate station, a converted freight depot that smelled of lamp oil, paper, and the iron smell of rock samples. She was a tall woman with gray coming in at her temples like frost advancing across a field, and she did not stand when he entered, and she did not offer him a chair, and he liked her immediately for both omissions.

"You're the ranger." Not a question. "Yarrow."

"That's the name I use."

Something flickered behind her eyes at that — quick, cataloguing, filed away. "Sit down, Ranger Yarrow-the-name-you-use. I've been reading your file."

"I don't have a file."

"Everyone has a file. Yours is thin, which is its own kind of information." She pushed a photograph across the desk: the camp, taken from the draw's rim, looking exactly as he'd left it an hour ago. "My team has been missing six weeks. Eleven people. Geologists, a surveyor, a cartographer, a medic, support staff. The agency sent a man named Vess to coordinate the search." Her mouth did something small and controlled around the name. "Vess found nothing. Vess reported that the team was likely lost in the Sere Basin flash floods. Case closed, condolences sent, insurance paperwork begun."

"And you don't believe it."

"I believe Vess believes it, which is different, and I believe my people are alive, which is different again." She leaned forward. "I've surveyed dead country for twenty years, Ranger. I know what losing eleven trained people looks like. It looks like wreckage. It looks like grief. It does not look like—" she tapped the photograph, "—a tidy camp with the instruments put away. Lost people don't fold their bedrolls. Drowned people don't leave their boots."

"No," Yarrow agreed. "They don't."

"The agency won't reopen the file. So I'm reopening it privately. My money, my authority, my risk." She slid a paper across the desk — a contract, already drawn, his name already written in a firm hand. "Find them. Or find what happened to them. Either answer will do, but I'll know a lie when I hear one, and so will you."

He read the contract. The figure at the bottom was generous enough to make him uneasy, because generous money usually meant someone wanted a particular answer, and he'd learned to ask which answer before he started looking for it.

"One question," he said. "What were they surveying?"

"Water," Okonjo said. "Groundwater. The deep aquifers. It's a government study — mapping what's under the Rivergate lease blocks before the concessions finalize." She said it flatly, administratively, the way you'd say *gravel* or *railroad ties*. But her hand, resting on the desk beside the photograph, had gone very still.

"And the search Vess ran. Where did he search?"

"Around the flood channels. Where the water would have taken them." Now her hand moved, straightening papers that didn't need straightening. "It's where anyone sensible would look."

"It is," Yarrow said. "Except that nobody sensible walks into the Sere Basin in June, and your team didn't walk anywhere. They left their boots."

Okonjo looked at him for a long moment, and he watched her decide something — decide how much truth this stranger could carry, or perhaps only how much she was willing to hand him before she knew whether he'd drop it.

"Ranger," she said, "I hired you to find my people. I did not hire you to find answers I haven't asked for. Can you live inside those lines?"

Nine years of living inside lines — inside aliases, inside empty country, inside the narrow corridor between who he was and who the warrant said he was. He could live inside any lines she drew. That was the whole tragedy of him.

"I can," he said, and signed.

* * *

He spent the rest of the day at the cache, alone, because the first rule of reading ground was that too many feet turn a page into mud. The afternoon heat came off the rocks in visible waves, and he worked the camp the way he'd once worked crime scenes for a marshal whose name he no longer let himself say aloud.

The details assembled themselves slowly, reluctantly, like animals coming to a waterhole at dusk.

The tents had been struck and re-pitched at least once — fresh stake scuffs over old ones, oriented differently. A camp doesn't move itself. Someone had chosen a new position within the same draw, deliberately, and the reason for choosing it became clear when he stood where the first fire ring had been and looked outward: from the original position, the camp was visible from the rimrock. From the new one, it was hidden. They had moved to stop being seen. Not by weather. By people.

The instrument case held a field notebook, and the notebook held nothing — pages torn out cleanly, close to the binding, by someone unhurried. You tear pages out in panic in fistfuls, ragged, half of them left behind. This was surgical. This was a record being erased by someone with time and a knife-edge of purpose.

And the boots. He came back to the boots last, kneeling over them in the long amber light. Eleven pairs, give or take — he counted thirteen, actually, two extra, which meant either spares or someone had left in a hurry after all. But it was the arrangement that spoke. They weren't discarded. They were *arranged*, paired at the tent walls with the neatness of people performing order, and Yarrow had seen that kind of performed order once before, long ago, in a house where a family had staged their own departure to buy themselves a night's head start.

They hadn't vanished. They had *disappeared* — the active verb, the verb with intent. Eleven people had decided, together, to erase themselves from this camp, and they had done it well enough to fool a professional search party, and they had done it badly enough — leaving the water, leaving the instruments, leaving the ghost-tracks — to leave a message for anyone patient enough to read it.

Or they'd left in such fear that even their caution had cracks in it.

Both possibilities pointed the same direction: whoever they feared had come from outside, and might still be watching.

He stood at the draw's rim as the sun went down, the camp below him going blue then black, and felt the unease in his chest finish its slow curdle into fascination — that dangerous, sweet intoxication of a puzzle with teeth. Somewhere out in that darkness, eleven ghosts were alive and hiding, and they had hidden from the government that sent them, and Ada Okonjo had hired a man with no past to go and knock on their grave.

Behind him, at the edge of hearing, a horse moved in the dark. One horse, walking soft, stopping when he stopped.

Yarrow didn't turn around. He stood very still and listened to the night decide what it was going to do, and after a while the hoofbeats faded north, unhurried, deliberate — the sound of someone who had seen all they needed to see.

Whoever it was now knew the cache had a visitor.

Which meant, Yarrow thought, swinging into the saddle, that somewhere out in the badlands, the hunt had just turned around and started looking back at him.
