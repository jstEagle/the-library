## 4. Eleven Names

The Rivergate station smelled of coffee gone bitter and lamp oil and the particular staleness of a building that had been argued in for too long. Yarrow stood in Ada Okonjo's office with his hat in his hands — not out of courtesy, though he could perform courtesy when it paid, but because the ceiling fan swung low and he'd learned years ago that a man who kept his hat on in a small room was a man announcing something, and he had no wish to announce anything to anyone.

Okonjo sat behind a desk built for a bigger man, or perhaps for a man with more papers than she had, which was saying nothing; her papers were stacked like sedimentary rock, three strata deep, edges aligned with a precision that told him more about her than any introduction could have. She was fifty, maybe, gray coming in at the temples like frost advancing across a field, and she had the kind of face that did its work in the muscles around the eyes rather than the mouth. He'd met survey chiefs before. Most of them wore their authority like borrowed coats. She wore hers like skin.

"You found the cache," she said.

"Found what's left of it."

"And?"

"And I'm wondering," Yarrow said, "why you're asking me instead of telling me."

Something moved behind her eyes — not quite approval, but the shape of its shadow. She opened the middle drawer of the desk, took out a leather folio, and laid it flat between them without opening it.

"Eleven people went into the badlands on the fourteenth of last month. Eleven names." She tapped the folio once. "Surveyors, geologists, a cartographer, a medic, two teamsters. The best crew I've assembled in nineteen years of fieldwork. They stopped transmitting nine days ago. Their last position places them forty miles past Split Rock, in country where there is no reason to be and less reason to stay."

"The cache was intact," Yarrow said. "Tents folded. Instruments crated. Boots."

"I know about the boots."

"Do you?" He let that sit a moment. "Nobody walks out of the desert barefoot, ma'am. And nobody leaves boots behind unless they mean to come back for them, or they're leaving in a hurry so complete they didn't dare make noise packing. There weren't tracks leading away. Not obscured — absent. Somebody swept that ground, and swept it well, and sweeping ground takes time and numbers. That wasn't panic. That was discipline."

Okonjo was quiet for a long moment. Outside, the wind worried at the station's eaves, a dry persistent sound, like someone trying doors down a long corridor.

"What exactly are you asking me to find?" Yarrow said. "Because there are two versions of this job, and they don't pay the same."

"Explain that."

"Version one: eleven people lost in the badlands, and you want them brought home. That's a recovery. I ride out, I read the country, I follow what sign there is, and I bring back whatever's left — living, if the desert's been generous, and dead if it hasn't. Sad work, but simple."

"And version two?"

"Version two is that your team isn't lost at all. Version two is that they left those boots on purpose, swept their own trail, and went to ground somewhere in two hundred square miles of nothing, which means they're hiding from somebody — and the only question worth asking is whether they're hiding from you."

The fan ticked overhead. Somewhere down the hall a door closed, soft as a held breath released.

Okonjo didn't flinch. He gave her credit for that. "You have an insubordinate way of speaking to a client, Mr. Yarrow."

"I have an accurate way of speaking to a client. It's cheaper in the long run."

For the first time, almost, she smiled — a movement of the mouth that never reached anywhere near her eyes. "Then let me pay you for accuracy." She slid the folio across the desk. "The roster. Eleven names, descriptions, last known positions, medical notes where we have them. You'll want to memorize it and burn it."

"And the other thing?"

Her hand paused on the drawer again. When she took out the second object, it was an envelope — heavy paper, sealed with wax, the agency's stamp pressed deep into it. She set it on top of the folio with both hands, the way a person sets down something that might go off.

"This goes with you. Unopened."

Yarrow looked at it. "I don't carry mail I can't read."

"It isn't mail. It's instructions — for the situation you may find out there. Contingencies. If you locate the team alive, you break the seal and follow what's inside. If you find them dead, you burn it unopened and report back. Either way, it comes back to me or it doesn't come back at all."

"That's a lot of weight for one envelope."

"Yes," Okonjo said. "It is."

He should have refused it. Later — much later, standing in other rooms, in worse light — he would take the moment out and turn it over and ask himself why he hadn't. Part of it was the money, which was good, better than tracking horse thieves through the flats would ever pay. Part of it was the puzzle of those missing tracks, which had already gotten its hooks into him the way a good mystery does, working quietly under the skin. But part of it — the part he least wanted to examine — was that a sealed letter full of contingencies meant someone above Ada Okonjo expected this to end badly, and a man who'd spent nine years outrunning one ending badly develops a certain appetite for watching how others arrange theirs.

He picked up the envelope. The wax was warm from her hands. He turned it over once, feeling the weight of the paper, the faint ridge of a second sheet folded inside — thick paper, official stock, the kind that took an impression of a seal deeply enough to read by touch.

"Who signs off on contingencies?" he asked. "You, or the agency?"

"The agency authorizes. I write." Her voice was level, but he heard the seam in it, fine as a hairline crack in fired clay. "Mr. Yarrow. My people are out there. Whatever else is true, that is true, and it is the truest thing in this room."

He believed her. That was the trouble. Believing her made everything else harder.

* * *

He read the roster that night in the bunkroom with the door barred and the lamp turned low, memorizing the way he'd been taught to memorize — not the words but the shapes of the words, the way a face is remembered as light and shadow before it is ever remembered as features.

**Calloway, Ruth.** Surveyor, team lead. Thirty-four. Former army engineers, discharged honorably, noted for "excessive thoroughness" in a fitness report that had clearly meant it as an insult. A woman who wrote maps the way other people wrote confessions, according to some prior supervisor whose comment had made it into the file.

**Pell, Daniel.** Geologist. Twenty-six. First field posting. A note in the margin, in Okonjo's own hand: *green, eager, asks too many questions — keep.* 

**Brandt, Ilsa.** Cartographer. Sixty-one. Forty years in the territory. The oldest name on the list and, reading between the lines of her service record, possibly the most dangerous; she had mapped half the drainage systems in the territory and forgotten more about water than the agency had ever learned.

And eight more. A medic named Osei. Teamsters, brothers, the Ferro boys. An instrument tech named Quist who'd once been fined for refusing to log readings he considered unreliable. Names, ages, heights, distinguishing marks. Yarrow said them aloud to the empty room until they stopped being ink and started being people — a trick, an old trick, because it was harder to abandon people than paper, and he wanted to feel that difficulty now, while the choice was still cheap.

Eleven names. He burned the roster in the stove as instructed and watched the corners curl, and thought: *somebody knows these eleven well enough to hunt them, and somebody else knows them well enough to want them hunted, and I am currently employed by one of those somebodies.*

The envelope he did not burn. He weighed it in his palm a long time, turning it, feeling that inner fold, and then put it in his breast pocket, over the heart, where a man carries things he hasn't decided to trust.

* * *

He saw Vess properly for the first time the next morning, in the yard behind the station, and understood within ten seconds that he had seen him before without registering it — which was itself a registration, the man having a talent for occupying space without committing to it.

Vess was leaning against the corral rail with a cup of coffee he wasn't drinking, watching the remuda the way a card player watches a shuffle. Medium height, medium build, medium everything except the eyes, which were pale as river ice and entirely still. He wore a contractor's badge on his vest — Halvard Security Services, the stamp crisp and new — and a gun belt worn low and tied, which meant he expected to use it and wanted it where his hand already knew the way.

"Ranger Yarrow." Vess's voice matched the rest of him: pleasant, unremarkable, a voice designed to be forgotten. "Ada says you're taking the contract."

"Ada says a lot of things."

"She does." Vess smiled, and the smile was the worst thing about him, because it reached all the way to those still eyes and died there without disturbing anything. "Nine years with the district, I hear. Desert work mostly. You must know that country out past Split Rock like your own kitchen."

"Better. My kitchen doesn't try to kill me."

"No," Vess agreed. "I imagine it doesn't." He drank the coffee at last, unhurried. "Big job, finding eleven people in open desert. Most men would want help."

"Most men would slow me down."

"Mm." Vess looked out at the horses a while, and Yarrow used the time to look at him — really look, the way he'd look at unfamiliar sign on a trail. Dust on the boot heels that wasn't station dust; it had the redder cast of the basin country, forty miles east, where nobody from the security detail had any assigned business. A rope scar on the pommel of his saddle, fresh, the kind a man gets dragging something heavy and unwilling. And under the coffee smell, when the wind came right, the ghost of woodsmoke — a cold camp, banked fast, doused hard.

Somebody had been burning things out in the badlands recently. Somebody careful.

"Well," Vess said, pushing off the rail. "Good luck out there. Deserts eat people." He said it lightly, the way a man comments on weather. Then, pausing, not quite turning back: "You find them, Ranger — you'll send word straight away, won't you? Ada will want to know. We'll all want to know."

"We all will," Yarrow echoed, and watched him walk away, and noticed that Vess walked the way water finds a channel — no wasted motion, no decision visible, just arrival at the low point of things.

* * *

He saddled up an hour before dawn, when the station was still dark and the stars still sharp, and he was tightening the cinch when Okonjo appeared at the edge of the yard, wrapped in a coat against the cold that wouldn't last past sunrise.

"You're leaving without saying goodbye," she said.

"I don't say goodbye. It implies return."

"Then say something useful." She came closer, and in the blue half-light her face looked older, the frost at her temples brighter. "My brother ran survey crews in the northern ranges, years ago. He told me once that the dangerous men aren't the ones who hate the desert. It's the ones who understand it and go out anyway. Which are you?"

"Neither, yet." Yarrow swung up into the saddle and gathered the reins. "Ask me in three weeks."

She almost laughed. Then she didn't. "The letter," she said. "You'll keep it sealed."

"I'll keep it sealed."

"And if you find my people—"

"If I find your people, Mrs. Okonjo, I'll ask them why they left their boots."

He turned the horse toward the eastern dark and rode out through the gate, and it was only a mile along the trail, with the station sunk behind him and the badlands opening ahead like a held breath, that he admitted to himself what had been watering quietly in him since the corral rail.

It wasn't Vess's dust, or the rope scar, or even the woodsmoke. Those were facts, and facts could point either direction. It was smaller than that. It was the way Vess had asked about the country past Split Rock — *like your own kitchen* — a friendly question wearing a measuring tape. A man asked that question when he wanted to know how far ahead of him you'd be. And it was the way Okonjo had handed over the envelope with both hands, like something that might go off, and then told him to keep it sealed — a woman who wrote her own contingencies letting someone else's stand unopened in another man's pocket.

Two people in that station knew what was in that letter. One of them had given it to him. The other one hadn't needed to, because the other one already had people in the field.

Yarrow rode east into the rising light, the envelope riding over his heart like a coal, and behind him the Rivergate station shrank to a smudge, and ahead of him the badlands waited with their swept trails and their buried water and their eleven scattered names, and somewhere among the mesas a man with river-ice eyes was waiting to learn how far ahead of him Yarrow intended to be.

He touched his heels to the horse and did not look back again.
