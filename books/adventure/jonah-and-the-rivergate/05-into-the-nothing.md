## 5. Into the Nothing

The badlands did not begin so much as they accumulated. First the grass thinned, then the grass gave out entirely, and then the earth itself began to come apart — shale shelves the color of old bruises, clay hills that bled red in the rain and cracked like fired pottery in the drought, hoodoos standing in their hundreds like a congregation that had been waiting since before there was anyone to preach to. Yarrow rode into it with the sun climbing his back and let the land close over him the way water closes over a dropped stone.

He had worked this country for nine years, off and on, under three names and one warrant, and he knew its moods the way a man knows a wife's moods: not by listening to what it said but by watching what it did when it thought no one was looking. The badlands lied constantly. They lied with mirage and with distance and with the way a shadow at two in the afternoon looked exactly like a man crouched behind a rock and was not. But they were honest about one thing, and one thing only, and that was sign. A boot print could not be faked. A broken branch could not lie about which way it had been bent. The desert forgot nothing; it simply buried things slowly, and a tracker's whole art was digging faster than the wind.

By midmorning he had found the first thing worth finding, and it was wrong.

It stood on a low rise above a dry wash: a cairn, waist-high, built of flat sandstone slabs stacked in the manner surveyors used to mark instrument stations. Yarrow circled it twice without dismounting, then got down and squatted beside it and put his hand flat against the third course of stones.

Wrong. Not badly wrong — not the way an amateur would build it, all top-heavy and proud of itself — but wrong the way a right-handed man writes with his left. The slabs were seated carefully, each one chosen for fit, but the chinking between them was packed with gravel where a practiced hand would have used smaller stone, and the whole structure leaned a half-degree east, as if the builder had been working in a hurry or in pain or in the dark. And the base course sat on soil that had been disturbed recently enough that the crust of cryptobiotic ground — that black, brittle skin the desert grew over decades, the skin that took a boot heel a single second to break and fifty years to heal — was still pale and raw around the edges.

Yarrow straightened up and looked east, the direction the cairn pointed, because surveyors' cairns always pointed at something, and what it pointed at was nothing. Empty basin. Heat shimmer. A horizon drawn in pencil.

Somebody had built a marker here within the last six weeks, pointing at nothing, and had built it slightly wrong, and had walked away.

He mounted up and rode on.

* * *

The second thing he found was older, and it told him more.

Late in the day, in the lee of a mesa whose base was hollowed into shallow caves by ten thousand years of wind, he found a fire ring. He almost rode past it. That was the point of it. Whoever had made it had made it to be ridden past — had built it back from the trail, tucked behind a fallen slab, screened by a dead juniper, and had used wood that was already down and gray, so that even the smoke-blackened stones would read as years old to anyone glancing through.

But Yarrow did not glance. He stopped, because something else had stopped him first: a single thread of wool, rust-colored, snagged on the juniper's lowest branch at the height of a man's chest. Wool did not last out here. Wool lasted days, maybe weeks, before the sun ate it and the wind took it. This thread was still supple when he rolled it between his fingers.

He cleared the fire ring apart stone by stone. Under the ash — and the ash was old, deliberately scattered, mixed with sand — he found what the ash was hiding: a scatter of tin foil wrappers, the kind hardtack came in from government stores. A button, brass, military pattern. And pressed deep into the sheltered floor of the ring, where no rain had reached it, the ghost of a boot heel. Government issue hobnail. Size eleven or thereabouts.

Three camps ago, he thought. Maybe four. This was not the team's first stop after they left their cache; this was well along whatever route they'd taken, and they had taken it the way a wounded animal takes a route — slow, careful, doubling back, choosing cover over speed. They had been hiding from someone from very early on. Before they were missing. Perhaps before they were lost.

Yarrow rebuilt the fire ring exactly as he'd found it, replaced the ash, repositioned the juniper branch, and stood a long moment looking at the rust-colored thread. Then he wound it around his little finger, because a tracker kept his evidence on his body where the wind couldn't have it, and rode on into the failing light.

That night he made no fire himself. He ate cold beans in the dark with his back against his saddle and his hat over his eyes, and he listened to the badlands do their night work — rock cooling and ticking, wind worrying the canyon mouths, somewhere far off a coyote saying the same three things coyotes always said. He thought about Vess, the security contractor with the river-ice eyes, and about how a man hired to guard a survey team would know these trails as well as any ranger. He thought about the envelope over his heart, which he had not opened, and which felt heavier tonight than it had that morning, the way some weights gain mass in the dark.

Sleep came late and thin, and left early.

* * *

On the third day the country turned cruel.

There was no other word for it. The mesas fell away and the land opened into a pan of white alkali that threw the sun back up at a man from below while it hammered him from above, so that he cooked on both sides at once like meat in a press. There was no shade. There was no water. There was not even the suggestion of water, no dry wash, no cottonwood skeleton, nothing for the eye to rest on that wasn't glare. The heat came off the pan in visible sheets, and through those sheets the distant buttes swam and wobbled like things drowning.

This was the Nothing. Every desert had one — a stretch the old freighters named and refused and went around, adding days to a route rather than cross it. Yarrow had crossed it twice before, both times in spring, both times with full canteens, and both times he had sworn he wouldn't do it again. Now he stood at its edge at dawn, having waited out the worst of the day before in a slot of shade, and did the arithmetic every traveler in that country eventually had to do.

Forty miles across. His horse could make it if he walked her half the distance and carried water for her, which meant carrying water for himself too, which meant the numbers got ugly fast. Two gallons for the horse spread over two days if he was lucky. Three quarts for himself, drunk by the ounce, sipped, never gulped. If anything went wrong — a thrown shoe, a storm of dust, a twisted ankle — the pan would keep him, and it would keep him neatly, because alkali preserved what it took.

He looked at the shimmering white distance and thought about eleven names on a roster, and about a cairn built wrong, and about a rust-colored thread, and he touched his heels to the horse and went out onto the pan.

The crossing took everything he had and most of what the horse had left. He walked her the first twenty miles, leading her, sharing the shade of his own body when he stopped, which was never long enough. By the second noon the alkali had gotten into everything — his teeth, the creases of his hands, the horse's eyes, crusted white at the corners like old tears. He drank by counting. One swallow at each mile, and he counted the miles aloud, because a man alone on the pan needed to hear a voice, even his own, even saying nothing but numbers.

It was near dusk on the second day, with the far rim of the pan finally resolving out of the shimmer into real brown rock, that he saw the thing that was not a rock and was not a mirage either, though at first he wished it were.

A figure, small with distance, sitting upright in the open maybe a mile off, facing west. Facing back the way Yarrow had come. Sitting perfectly still, the way nothing sits still in a living desert except a dead thing or a waiting thing.

Yarrow's hand went to his rifle out of nine years of habit, and then, reading the figure more closely — the slump beneath the stillness, the angle of the head, the clothes that hung like wet laundry on a frame that had been bigger once — he holstered it and changed his line and rode toward it at a walk.

* * *

Up close, the figure resolved into a man, and the man resolved into a ruin.

He was sitting on a folded blanket with his legs straight out in front of him, boots pointing west, hands resting palms-up on his thighs like a man waiting to receive something. He wore the remains of a government survey uniform gone the color of the pan itself, salt-stiffened, torn at both knees. His lips were split and white. His eyes, when Yarrow swung down and stepped into the man's field of view, took a long time to arrive — they moved toward him slowly, swimming, like fish rising through murky water.

"Water," Yarrow said, before hello, before anything, because you learned the order of words in that country. "I've got water."

The man's mouth worked. What came out was not a word. It was a sound like a hinge.

Yarrow knelt, uncapped his canteen, and held it to the man's lips — tilted, small sips, his hand cupped under the man's chin to catch what spilled, because he had seen men kill themselves with water before, gulping until their bodies quit in revolt, and he was not going to lose this one to mercy administered wrong. "Slow," he said. "Small. Slow." The man drank with a terrible concentration, his whole ruined face gathered around the spout of the canteen, and when Yarrow pulled it away he made the hinge sound again, louder, and his hands came up and grabbed Yarrow's wrist with a strength that surprised them both.

"Green," the man said.

"I know. Easy now."

"Green stone." The eyes had arrived now, and they were the worst part — wide and wet and lit from inside with a fever-brightness that had nothing to do with health. "Down there. Underneath. Miles of it. You dig down and it's green, do you understand, it's *green*, the whole world underneath is green and wet—" He started to laugh, and the laugh broke in the middle and became coughing, and the coughing bent him double, and Yarrow held his shoulders until it passed.

"Pell," Yarrow said. He had memorized the roster the first night, lying awake. Eleven names, eleven trades. "Elias Pell. Geologist. That's you."

Something flickered in the wrecked face. Recognition, maybe, or just the reflex of a man hearing his own name after however many days of hearing nothing but wind. "You're not one of them," Pell said. It wasn't a question, but Yarrow answered it anyway.

"No."

"They said someone would come." Pell's grip tightened. "They said — no. No, they said nobody would come. They said nobody comes out here. That's why we picked it." The laugh again, worse this time. "We picked the Nothing because nobody comes to the Nothing, and I stayed behind, I stayed to watch our backtrail, and they went on, and I watched, and then the water — my water — I don't—"

His gaze slid off Yarrow's face and fixed on the middle distance, on the western horizon, and his voice dropped to a whisper that the wind nearly took.

"He came at night. The one with the ice eyes. I heard horses and I put out my fire and I lay flat and I watched him ride our line, slow, reading it, the way you're reading me right now. He stopped at our marker. He looked at it a long time." Pell swallowed, and the swallow cost him visibly. "Then he turned around and rode back the way he came. Like a man who already knew where we were going. Like a man checking his watch."

Yarrow kept his face still and his voice level, the way you talked to a man standing on a ledge. "How many days ago?"

Pell's brow furrowed. The arithmetic was clearly happening somewhere far away in the ruined house of his mind. "Four," he said finally. "Or five. Or the same day over and over. The sun does that out here. It comes up and it's the same sun."

"Which way did the team go? After you stayed behind?"

Pell raised one arm — it shook like a branch in wind — and pointed north-northeast, toward a line of black mesas on the far rim of the world. "Into the Labyrinth," he whispered. "Ruth said — Calloway said — the maps are wrong there. All of them. Even ours." His arm dropped. His eyes came back to Yarrow's, and in them the fever-light guttered, and for one moment something sane and exhausted and terribly young looked out. "You're going after them."

"Yes."

"You shouldn't." The whisper was urgent now, and Pell leaned forward, gripping Yarrow's sleeve with both hands. "You should ride away. Ride anywhere. Because he isn't following them, do you understand? He's *herding* them. He lets them run and then he closes the gate. And when he closes it—" The sentence collapsed. Pell's head drooped, then snapped up, then drooped again, the strings cut one by one.

Yarrow made his decisions the way he made all of them out here: quickly, and without ceremony. He could not carry a grown man forty miles back the way he'd come; the numbers didn't work and the man wouldn't survive the delay besides. But the northern rim was eight miles, and there was water at the springs under the black mesa — bitter water, sulfur water, water you boiled and cursed and lived on — and there was shade, and there was rock a man could defend. He rigged a sling from his bedroll and the blanket Pell sat on, got the geologist lashed into the saddle ahead of his own weight as best the geometry allowed, and took up the lead rope.

Pell drifted in and out the whole eight miles. Between the drifting he talked. Green stone. Water where water could not be. A core sample sealed in wax and canvas, carried like a relic. Ruth Calloway's voice saying *we don't falsify, we don't falsify, we don't.* And once, suddenly lucid, staring at Yarrow's profile in the last violet light:

"What's your name, Ranger?"

And Yarrow, who had answered that question with three different names in nine years, who had a warrant with legs walking the earth asking after one of them, heard himself say, quietly, "Yarrow."

Pell nodded as if this confirmed something. "Good," he murmured, sliding down into sleep or something near it. "Good. Names matter. She'll want to know your name."

They reached the springs under the black mesa an hour past full dark. Yarrow boiled water, forced broth and more small sips into Pell, wrapped him in every scrap of cloth he owned, and sat awake beside him through the cold hours with the rifle across his knees, watching the northern darkness where the Labyrinth began.

Eight miles north, behind walls of stone that no map told the truth about, ten people were hiding from a man who already knew where they were.

And somewhere between here and there, Yarrow thought, turning the rust-colored thread around his finger in the starlight, that man had stopped at a wrong-built cairn and looked at it a long time — and then turned back, patient as winter, because a man who is herding has no need to hurry.

Pell stirred and muttered in his sleep. The word, when Yarrow caught it, was *green.*

He banked the fire down to coals and sat on watching the dark, and the dark watched back.
