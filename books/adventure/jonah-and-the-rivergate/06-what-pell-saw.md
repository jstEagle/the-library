## 6. What Pell Saw

The man had crawled the last quarter mile on his hands and knees, and the desert had written its verdict across him in red dust and salt. Yarrow found him at the lip of a dry wash, face down, one arm flung forward as if reaching for something just past his fingers. For a long moment Yarrow sat his horse and watched, because dead men and dying men looked alike out here, and there was no sense spending water on either.

Then the man's shoulder blades moved. A shallow hitch of breath, another. Alive, then. Barely.

Yarrow dismounted and came at him from downwind, the way you'd approach any wild thing, and crouched where the man could see him without turning his head. He was young — younger than Yarrow had guessed from a distance — with the soft hands of someone who'd spent years holding pencils instead of reins. His lips were split and gray. His eyes, when they found Yarrow's, were the color of old tin, and they did not focus so much as fix, the way a nail fixes in wood.

"Water," Yarrow said, and held the canteen to the cracked mouth.

The man drank like an animal, ugly and desperate, and Yarrow let him, counting swallows, pulling the canteen away before the stomach could rebel. He knew this arithmetic by heart. Too much too fast killed as surely as none at all.

"Pell," the man rasped, when he could speak. "Geological survey. Third party. We were — " His voice snagged on something. "You're not with them."

It wasn't a question, but it wasn't quite a statement either. Yarrow studied him. The man's shirt had been torn into strips and knotted around his forearms — protection from the sun, improvised by someone who knew what he was doing, or by Pell himself before his mind went soft. There was no pack. No hat worth the name. Just a man dissolved to his essentials, left behind by whatever he'd been running from.

"Yarrow," he said. "Ranger, Rivergate district. Okonjo hired me to find your team."

Something moved behind Pell's eyes at the name Okonjo — a flicker, quickly buried, like a fish turning under ice. Yarrow filed it away.

"How many of you?" Yarrow asked. "Eleven, according to the roster."

Pell laughed then, and it was a terrible sound, dry as sand sliding off a dune. "Eleven." He said it the way a man says a number that has stopped meaning anything. "Eleven names on paper. You count them and tell me what you get."

He wouldn't say more. Not then. Yarrow got him up onto the horse — the geologist weighed nothing, a bundle of sticks wrapped in skin — and walked beside him through the heat-shimmered afternoon, and all the while Pell talked. That was the strange mercy of heat-madness: it loosened the tongue even as it scrambled the sense. Yarrow had learned to listen to madmen the way other men listened to sober witnesses. Madness told the truth; it simply couldn't organize it.

"They said it couldn't be there," Pell murmured, as the sun leaned west and the shadows grew teeth. "Two hundred feet of caliche, then shale, then more shale. The whole basin is a closed bowl. No recharge. No catchment. The models said nothing. The models were *beautiful*." He giggled. "And then we drilled through the beautiful models and the core came up wet."

"Wet," Yarrow said.

"Green stone." Pell's hand rose, trembling, and made a shape in the air — a cylinder, a length of something held between invisible fingers. "You have to understand what green means. Green means iron in a reduced state. Green means this rock has never seen air. Green means it has been sitting under two hundred feet of dead ground, sealed, since before there were men to name it — and it comes up *soaked*." His voice climbed, cracked, dropped to a whisper. "An aquifer the size of a sea under land everyone agreed was worthless. Under land already promised. Do you know what that does to a map? Do you know what that does to *everything*?"

Yarrow knew what it did to everything. It turned paper into money and money into blood. But he said nothing. He'd found that silence pulled more out of people than questions did.

They made camp in the lee of a basalt shelf, and Yarrow fed Pell small sips of broth from his own stores, watching the color crawl back into the man's face by degrees. The stars came out hard and close. Somewhere east, a coyote ran its needle through the dark.

"The others," Yarrow said. "Where are they?"

Pell was quiet a long time. When he answered, his voice had changed — steadier now, or pretending to be. "Safe. Safer than me. I volunteered to be the one who got lost." He smiled, and it was a child's smile, proud and broken. "A decoy. I walked south carrying our spare instruments and made as much noise as I could. They wanted whoever came looking to find *a* surveyor, just not the right ones."

"And who sent whoever came looking?"

The smile died. Pell looked at the fire, or through it, and his hands began their trembling again.

"You should know," he said slowly, "that we weren't lost when you started looking for us. We were hiding. There's a difference. Lost is an accident. Hiding is a decision, and decisions have reasons, and the reason has a name—"

He stopped. Mid-word, mid-breath, like a door slamming. Yarrow felt the silence come off him in waves.

"Pell. Who sent them?"

The geologist turned his head, very slowly, and looked at Yarrow with those tin-colored eyes, and Yarrow saw the madness recede and something colder take its place — calculation, or fear wearing calculation's clothes.

"No," Pell said. "Not out loud. Not in the open. Not with—" He pressed his lips together until they went white. "Ask me inside walls. Ask me where sound doesn't carry. Because they hear things, ranger. They hear *everything*, and the man who runs them—" 

His jaw worked. The name sat behind his teeth like a stone he could neither swallow nor spit.

"He'll be watching you too," Pell whispered. "By now he'll know Okonjo hired someone. He'll want to know who. And when he finds out—" He shook his head, once, sharply, like a horse shying off a snake. "When he finds out who you are, he won't send men. He'll send *paper*. Paper travels faster than horses, and it kills cleaner."

Yarrow kept his face still, but something turned over in his chest, slow and heavy. Nine years of paper with his name on it, folded into drawers in a dozen marshals' offices. Nine years of being a rumor with legs.

"Sleep," he said. "We move at first light."

* * *

But sleep didn't come for Yarrow, and after a while he gave up pretending and sat with his back against the basalt, watching the fire burn down to its orange eye. Pell slept like the dead, or like the rescued — utterly, bonelessly, one hand curled near the warmth.

Yarrow took out the roster and read the eleven names again by firelight. Calloway, R. — surveyor. Brandt, I. — cartographer. Osei, T. — hydrologist. Down the list, each name a small cold fact. Eleven people who had drilled into the earth and pulled up a secret, and then walked off the face of the map so thoroughly that even their own agency's trackers had found nothing.

Except that wasn't true, was it? Someone had found something. Someone had chased these people hard enough that they'd scattered, sacrificed Pell, erased themselves. And the agency — Okonjo's agency, the one paying Yarrow's fee — had reported them *missing*. Had hired a ranger to search. Had expressed, through intermediaries, grave concern.

Either the agency was telling the truth and didn't know where its own team had gone. Or the agency was lying, and knew exactly where they'd gone, and wanted them found by someone deniable. Someone expendable. Someone with a warrant folded in a drawer three territories away.

Yarrow looked at Pell sleeping and thought: *I am the paper they sent.*

The thought sat with him a long time, uncomfortable as a stone in a boot. He turned it over. He couldn't yet see all its faces.

Before dawn he saddled up and rode a slow circle around the camp, wider and wider, the way he always did — reading the ground the way a priest reads scripture. And it was on the third circle, half a mile out, that he found it.

Tracks. Horse tracks, four riders, moving in a loose line. They'd passed within a mile of his camp sometime in the night, heading the same direction he intended to go, and they hadn't stopped, hadn't circled, hadn't shown the least interest in his little fire. Which meant they hadn't seen it. Which meant they weren't looking for him.

*Yet.*

He crouched over the prints. Shod horses, good shoes, recently set — the marks crisp-edged, unweathered. Four riders traveling light and fast, spaced like men who'd done this before, who knew to keep apart in case of a fall or a shot. And leading north-northeast, toward the broken country where the land folded into itself and hid whatever it liked.

Toward where a vanished team would hide, if a team wanted to vanish well.

Yarrow straightened slowly. The wind came off the flats and stirred the dust around the tracks, erasing them grain by grain, and he understood suddenly that he was watching the desert do what these riders did professionally: make things disappear.

He'd been hired to follow a trail. Now there were two trails — the team's and the riders' — running parallel through the same emptiness, and Yarrow standing between them like a hair trigger. If the riders reached the team first, eleven people would become a footnote. If Yarrow warned the team, he'd be announcing himself to men who killed for a living. And if the riders caught *him* alone out here, far from any witness—

Well. A ranger with a warrant was hardly missed. A ranger with a warrant who died in the badlands was a story that wrote itself.

He rode back to camp and woke Pell gently, and the geologist came up out of sleep fighting, hands flailing, until Yarrow caught his wrists and held them.

"Riders," Yarrow said quietly. "Four of them. Passed in the night, headed northeast. Your friends' direction."

Pell went white under his sunburn. "Vess," he breathed. Then he clamped his mouth shut, horrified, as if the name had escaped without permission, and turned his face away.

So. A name after all — given in terror, at dawn, by a man too frightened to say it twice.

Yarrow helped him onto the horse, and they rode north into the rising heat, following tracks that weren't theirs toward people who didn't want to be found, and Yarrow understood, with the flat certainty of a man reading his own death warrant, that the hunt had turned inside out somewhere in the night while he sat sleepless by the fire.

He had come out here thinking himself the hunter.

The desert, patient as always, had other ideas.
