## 7. The Hidden Camp

The canyon found him before he found it.

Yarrow had been reading the land all morning the way a man reads a face — the slight tightening around the eyes, the tell that means something is being held back. Here it was the wash that narrowed too neatly between two walls of red sandstone, as if the desert had drawn a curtain and forgotten to close it all the way. Water had cut this slot ten thousand years ago and then moved on, leaving behind a corridor of stone smooth as the inside of a throat.

He should have gone around. Every instinct he owned said go around. But Pell's broken muttering — *green stone, green in the deep rock* — pointed into the badlands like a compass needle, and compass needles did not care about instinct.

He dismounted at the mouth of the slot and walked his horse in on foot, one hand on the animal's neck, feeling its tension translate through the hide into his palm. The horse knew something. Horses always knew first.

The walls closed to shoulder width, then opened again into a chamber no wider than a wagon, where light fell in a single amber column from a crack in the rimrock forty feet above. Sand lay on the floor in soft drifts, and the air was cool — genuinely cool, the first cool air he'd felt since leaving Rivergate station — and there was water here somewhere, because the stone wept. Thin dark streaks ran down the canyon walls like old tears, and in their wake grew a fuzz of green moss so improbable against the red that it looked painted on by a child who had never seen a desert.

He was still looking at the moss when the world went white.

Not a blow to the head — he would remember that later with professional resentment — but a sack over the head, smelling of burlap and horse sweat, yanked down hard enough to grind his teeth together. Hands took his arms, his wrists, his throat. More hands than he could count, moving with the panicked coordination of people who had rehearsed this and prayed they'd never have to do it. Someone said, low and fierce, *"Don't kill him, don't kill him, we need to know—"*

Then a boot caught him behind the knee, and the sand came up to meet him through the burlap, and the last thing he heard before the dark took him was the sound of his own horse being led away, hooves striking hollow notes off the canyon walls like a bell counting down.

* * *

He woke to rope and cold.

His wrists were bound behind him with a hitch he recognized — a surveyor's knot, of all things, the kind used to stake transit lines — and he was sitting against the wall of the slot canyon with his legs stretched out on stone that held the night's chill like a grudge. The amber column of light had gone gray-blue. Evening, or near dawn. His head throbbed where the burlap had scraped his brow raw.

They were watching him.

He counted them without moving his head more than he had to, the way you count money you suspect is short. Eleven figures in the gloom of the chamber, arranged in a rough arc, and every one of them armed — not with guns, or not only with guns, but with pry bars and rock hammers and a surveyor's tripod broken down into a spear-length of aluminum. They had made weapons out of their instruments. That told him more than their faces did. These were people who had decided, some time ago, that they were never going back to the work they'd been sent to do.

A cook fire burned small and shielded in a ring of stones, giving off more smoke than heat. Over it hung a tin pot, and the smell rising from it was coffee stretched three times past decency, and under the coffee something else — wet stone, green growth, the mineral breath of actual water. His mouth flooded. He hadn't realized how long it had been since he'd tasted anything but dust.

"Ranger."

The voice came from the center of the arc. A woman, mid-forties maybe, sitting on a supply crate with her forearms on her knees. She had sun-cured skin and gray coming in at her temples in two hard wings, and she held a pistol the way Pell had held his canteen in the last hour before Yarrow found him — too tightly, with the desperate grip of someone who has learned what it costs to put a thing down.

"Ranger," she said again. "We watched you read our trail for six days. We watched you find what Vess's men couldn't find in three weeks." She let that sit. "So before anyone decides anything, you're going to tell me who sent you."

"No one sent me."

"Everyone is sent."

"Not me." He worked moisture into his mouth. It tasted like blood and sand. "I'm a contract tracker out of Rivergate station. Your chief hired me. Ada Okonjo. Nine days ago."

Something moved through the arc of watchers — not relief. He'd expected relief, and its absence told him everything before the woman spoke again.

"Okonjo," she repeated, flat.

"You're Calloway," he said. "Ruth Calloway. Chief surveyor. Okonjo described you. Said you'd be the last one standing even if the rest quit."

"I don't quit." Her eyes didn't leave his face. "Neither does she. That's the problem."

Behind her, a young man with a bandaged hand shifted, and the motion of his shifting brought his rifle barrel up an inch, and an older woman — heavyset, spectacles fogged in the canyon damp — put a hand on his arm without looking at him. Discipline, then. Frayed, but present. Yarrow catalogued it the way he catalogued water sources: this much, here, might hold.

"Your letter," Calloway said. "Okonjo gave you a letter. Sealed."

"She did."

"Where is it?"

"Inside coat, left breast. If your people haven't already gone through my pockets while I slept, which I'd have done in their position."

No one laughed. He hadn't expected them to. Fear like theirs didn't have room for humor; fear like theirs was load-bearing.

"Calloway." He kept his voice level, conversational, a man discussing weather across a fence. "You've got eleven people in a hole in the ground eating whatever's left of a cache you buried in a hurry. You've got a security contractor named Vess running riders over two hundred square miles looking for you, and those riders aren't looking to bring you home. You know that. That's why you're down here instead of reporting in." He tilted his head back against the stone. "I'm the only person in four days' ride who isn't working for whoever wants you dead. You might consider that arithmetic."

"We considered it," said the young man with the bandaged hand. "That's why you're tied up."

"Hush, Deel." Calloway hadn't moved. "He's right about the arithmetic. That's what worries me."

She stood. Up close she was shorter than she'd seemed, built like something riveted rather than assembled, and when she crossed the chamber to stand over him the others adjusted around her movement like iron filings around a magnet. She crouched, bringing her eyes level with his, and searched his face with the unhurried thoroughness of someone reading a core sample — layer by layer, down through whatever he showed the world.

"Nine years ago," she said, "there was a ranger in this territory named Marguerite Yarrow. Good tracker. Better than good. Then a man died outside Cane Fork and the ranger disappeared off the face of the earth, and the warrant's been gathering dust ever since because nobody could find him." She paused. "Rivergate station has a ranger now. No surname on the payroll ledger. Been there eight years. Never takes leave. Never gets drunk at the spring dance. Keeps to himself." Her voice didn't rise at all. "You have his eyes. I checked the old territorial gazette. There was a photograph."

The chamber went very quiet. Even the fire seemed to lean in.

Yarrow let the silence run its length. There was no point denying it — she'd have checked the build, the hands, the way he sat a horse. And lying to this woman, in this place, surrounded by these people, would cost him the only currency he had left: the truth was the single thing he carried that they needed.

"That was a long time ago," he said.

"It was nine years," Calloway said. "Long enough for a man to forget what he's running from. Not long enough for the running to stop mattering." She straightened. "I'm not the law. I don't care about your warrant. What I care about is whether a man who's spent nine years keeping his name buried can be trusted to keep eleven other names alive. Those are different questions, and I need to know if you understand the difference."

"I understand it better than most."

"Do you." Not quite a question. She turned to the others. "Give us the room."

"They're not going anywhere," Deel started, "he's—"

"*Room*, Deel."

They went, reluctantly, filing deeper into the slot canyon's throat, boots whispering on sand, until it was the two of them and the fire and the weeping stone and the thin column of failing light. Calloway fed a stick to the flames and watched it catch.

"Pell," she said. "You found Pell."

"Half-dead of heat, twenty miles east. Talking about green stone."

"He'll live?"

"If the fever breaks. He asked for water that wasn't there and then he asked for you. I figured you for dead by then. All of you."

"We're harder to kill than the agency budgeted for." She almost smiled. It didn't reach anywhere near her eyes. "You want to know why we ran."

"I stopped wanting things years ago. But yes. It would help."

She studied him a moment longer, weighing something, and then she reached into her shirt and drew out a length of oilcloth wrapped tight and tied with wire. She set it on her knee but didn't open it.

"Three months ago we drilled core samples at grid point forty-one. Under the Halvard lease land. Do you know what's under that land, Ranger-who-has-no-name?"

"Dry country. Everyone says dry country."

"Everyone is wrong." Her hand rested flat on the oilcloth bundle, possessive, protective, the way a mother's hand rests on a sleeping child. "There's an aquifer down there the size of a sea. Fresh water. Enough to green half the territory. Enough to make deserts into towns." She looked up at him, and in the firelight her eyes were the color of the canyon walls at dusk. "And enough to make certain men very rich, if it stays secret long enough to be sold quietly. The agency ordered us to log the samples as dry. Ordered us in writing. When we refused, our own security detail stopped guarding us and started hunting us."

The fire popped. Somewhere above, wind moved through the crack in the rimrock and made a low moan, like the canyon itself breathing.

"So," Calloway said. "Now you know what we are. Fugitives carrying the most valuable secret in the territory, and the men chasing us carry badges we signed for ourselves." She rose, and the oilcloth bundle stayed on her knee, deliberate as a card laid face-down. "Here's my question, and think carefully, because Deel's finger is heavier than his judgment. Okonjo sent you to find us. Fine. But Okonjo wrote the order to falsify the findings — I've seen her signature on it. So either she sent you to bring us home, or she sent you to find out where we're hiding so someone else can finish the job." She crouched again, close, her voice dropping to almost nothing. "Which man are you? Because I've known both kinds, and the second kind smiles just like you do."

Yarrow held her gaze. Behind his back, his bound hands had spent the last quarter hour working the surveyor's knot loose — not because he meant to fight, but because a man who couldn't get free couldn't choose freely, and choosing was all he had left. The knot gave a fraction. He let it.

"I don't know Okonjo's game," he said honestly. "But I know mine. I was hired to find eleven missing people. I found them alive. As far as I'm concerned, the job's done, and what happens after is none of my business — unless you make it my business."

"And if we make it your business?"

"Then I'd want to see what's in that bundle."

Calloway looked at him for a long moment. Then, slowly, she began to laugh — a dry, exhausted sound with no real humor in it, the laugh of a woman who has been afraid so long that surprise feels like luxury.

"You're either the bravest man I've ever met," she said, "or the biggest fool in the territory, and I've met both."

"Often the same man."

"Yes." She picked up the bundle. "Often the same man."

She untied the wire. Unrolled the oilcloth. And inside, nested in cotton batting like relics in a saint's hand, lay three cylinders of pale stone, each one banded with a seam of unmistakable, impossible green — wet-looking, vivid, alive — the color of the moss on the canyon walls, the color of a thing the desert had been hiding all along.

Yarrow stared at it, and felt nine years of careful nothing shift inside his chest like sand deciding, at last, to move.

"Green stone," he said quietly. "Pell wasn't mad."

"No," Calloway said. "Pell was the first of us to believe it. Now the question is whether you are."
