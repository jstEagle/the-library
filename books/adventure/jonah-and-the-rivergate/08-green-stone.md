## 8. Green Stone

The slot canyon swallowed the last of the daylight an hour before the sun actually set, so that Yarrow experienced dusk twice — once in the walls' shadow and once in the sky above it. They walked him between them with his hands still bound at the wrists, eleven people moving through the narrows like water finding its way downhill, quiet, practiced, afraid.

Ruth Calloway led. She was not tall, but she moved as though the canyon had been cut to her specifications, stepping over the same stones her people stepped over, never looking back to see if they followed. He'd watched that kind of leadership before, usually in soldiers, occasionally in mules. It did not require height or volume. It required that everyone behind you believe you already knew where the bottom of the next hour lay.

They came up out of the narrows into a bowl of red rock walled on three sides, and there the camp revealed itself — not a camp, really. A concealment. Tarps the color of sandstone stretched on wire between boulders, broken by shadow-lines so that from any distance the bowl would read as empty rock. A cook fire burned down to coals in a pit dug into a hollow of stone, smokeless. Eleven bedrolls laid out in the open but arranged so that from above they would look like scattered debris. These people had been hiding for a long time, and hiding well, and the discipline of it showed in everything: the gear stowed, the weapons near, the silence.

Someone took his bindle off his horse. Someone else saw to the horse itself, and did it correctly, which told him more about these people than anything they might have said. You could fake a lot of things in the desert. You could not fake knowing how to cinch a saddle for a night's picket without strangling the animal.

Calloway crouched in front of him where he sat against a boulder, and studied him the way he had studied the wrong-built cairn three days ago — not reading what it appeared to be, but what it was made of.

"You're the ranger Okonjo sent," she said.

"I'm a ranger."

"Yarrow." She said it flatly, tasting it. "She gave us your name before we left Rivergate. Said if anything went wrong, find you, or send for you. That you were the only honest man on the payroll."

"And then you ran instead."

"And then we ran instead." Something moved through her face — not quite a smile, too tired for that. "You want to know why. I can see it sitting behind your eyes like a man waiting outside a door. Fine. But you're going to see it first. Words are cheap out here. Rock doesn't lie."

She stood. "Cut him loose, Pell."

Pell — the geologist Yarrow had found half-cooked under the rimrock, the man who had babbled about green stone and gone silent as a held breath when Vess's name came up — knelt and sawed through the cord at Yarrow's wrists. His hands shook. Up close, in the coal-light, Pell looked better than he had in the open desert, fed and rested, but the heat had taken something out of him that food hadn't given back. His eyes went to Calloway constantly, the way a man's eyes go to a lamp in a dark room.

Yarrow worked feeling back into his fingers and said nothing. Patience was the oldest tool he owned.

* * *

Calloway had rehearsed this moment, she realized, without ever admitting she was rehearsing it. In the long evenings of their hiding, while Brandt drew maps by lamplight and the others pretended to sleep, she had imagined showing the cores to someone — a judge, a journalist, her father, God — and had composed the speech each time, and discarded each time, because every version sounded either like begging or like madness.

Now the moment was here, and the man she was showing it to was a stranger with a warrant somewhere behind him and dust in the creases of his face, and all her speeches evaporated, and what remained was just the thing itself. Which was, she supposed, how it should be. The thing itself had always been enough. It was everything around the thing that was rotten.

"Bring the box," she said.

Okafor and Lindqvist carried it out from under the false wall of the supply cache — a steel core case, government-stamped, one of six, this one opened. Inside, nested in straw, lay cylinders of stone the length of a man's forearm, each one split lengthwise and polished flat along the cut.

"Drill core," she said. "From the borehole at Station Four. We were contracted to run a hydrological survey of the Rivergate basin ahead of the Halvard lease — standard work, confirm the aquifer picture, depth to groundwater, salinity gradients. The agency wanted numbers to put in a drawer. Nobody expected anything."

Yarrow leaned over the case. The coal-light caught the cut faces of the cores, and even Calloway, who had looked at them a hundred times, felt her breath do the small catch it always did.

The upper sections were the usual story — the rusty caliche, the pale sandstone, the dry red mudstone of the basin, colors like old brick and bone. Then, forty feet down the sequence, the color changed.

Green.

Not the green of copper stain, not the sickly green of mineralized fracture. A deep, wet, living green — stone shot through with it, saturated with it, the way cloth is saturated with dye. And along the polished face of each section, where the drill had passed through the water-bearing stratum, the stone glistened. Actually glistened, weeks after it came out of the ground, because the pore water in it had never stopped weeping.

"That's tuff," Calloway said, and heard her own voice drop into the register she used for lectures, the one that made students lean forward. "Volcanic ash, laid down millions of years ago, buried, compacted. It should be bone-dry at this depth. Instead it's holding water like a sponge holds it — clean water, cold, under pressure. We ran the tests four times. Four different days, four different instruments, Lindqvist checked the calibration until he dreamed about it." She nodded at the Swede, who lifted his chin once, confirming. "It's real."

"How much?" Yarrow asked. Two words, no inflection, but she had learned to hear the questions underneath men's questions, and this one had a whole cathedral under it.

She let him wait for it. Not for drama. Because she wanted him to carry the weight of it the way she carried it, all day, every day, in the pit of her stomach.

"The aquifer underlies roughly nine hundred square miles of the basin," she said. "Conservative estimate — conservative — sixty trillion gallons of fresh water. Renewable. There's recharge coming off the high mesas to the west; we traced the isotope signature. This isn't a fossil lake being mined. It's a living river, Yarrow. An underground river bigger than anything on the surface between here and the coast, running under land that the territorial office has already leased to Halvard Mining for strip extraction." She closed the case slowly. "Halvard will dig through that green stone to get at the copper seams below it. And when they breach the confining layer, the pressure head will drain the whole system into their pit in a matter of years. Sixty trillion gallons, gone into the air and the tailings, and nine hundred square miles of desert stays a desert forever, and everyone downstream of it — every ranch, every town, every station — drinks the consequence."

The coals ticked. Somewhere above the canyon rim, wind moved through juniper with a sound like the desert breathing through its teeth.

Yarrow hadn't moved. He was still crouched over the case, and Calloway watched his reflection in the wet green face of the topmost core — a small dark figure bent inside the stone, as if the water had already claimed him.

"They knew," he said at last. Not a question.

"The agency knew before we did." Her jaw ached; she realized she'd been clenching it since the narrows. "We transmitted our preliminary findings on the field telegraph relay, per protocol. Within a week we had a directive from the Director's office. Reclassify the stratum. Log it as impermeable shale, low yield, non-potable. Sign the falsified summary. The permits for Halvard were, quote, 'a settled matter of territorial policy,' and our findings were, quote, 'inconsistent with the settled record.'" She laughed, briefly, without any humor in it at all. "Settled. Like sediment. Like a grave."

"And you refused."

"Eleven signatures on the original data. Eleven refusals on the order." She looked around the fire at her people — Okafor with his burned forearms, Lindqvist with his calibration dreams, young Ferro who still flinched at aircraft engines, old Brandt with her maps and her failing heart, Pell, poor shattered Pell, who had been the one to say aloud what they were all thinking: *they won't just bury the data.* "Then Vess's security detail stopped pretending to be security and started pretending to be friendly, and we understood what 'survey casualties' would mean, and we ran. We've been running for five weeks. Eating our pack strings, boiling rock seep, moving at night. Waiting for the permit clock to give us something worth running toward."

Yarrow straightened. He looked at her for a long moment, and she braced herself for the question she couldn't answer, the one about what he intended to do now that he knew — whether he was theirs or Okonjo's, whether the ranger was a man or a weapon pointed at them.

He didn't ask it. Instead he said, quietly, "Show me the rest of the cores."

* * *

Yarrow turned each section in his hands like a man handling evidence, which was exactly what he was doing, though whose case it belonged to he could not yet have said.

He had spent nine years learning what truth cost. He knew its price down to the penny. Truth had cost him his name, his badge, his right to sleep in the same bed two nights running, his face in any photograph, his mother's grave unvisited. Truth was the most expensive commodity in the territory, and most people, given the invoice, chose the lie and slept fine.

These eleven had seen the invoice and refused to pay it anyway. He kept turning that over, looking for the flaw in it, the self-interest underneath, the angle. Nine years of running taught a man that everyone had an angle. He found none. That frightened him more than Vess's riders did.

Because if they were telling the truth — and the stone in his hands was telling it too, weeping its impossible water, green as nothing else in this country of rust and bone — then the world was not the shape he had believed it was this morning. Under all that emptiness he had crossed a thousand times, there had been a river the whole time. All that drought, all those ranchers selling out, all those wells going brackish, and beneath it a sea of clean water, hidden, and men had found it and decided to kill it rather than share it.

He thought of the flats where he'd caught the horse thief. Of the man begging. Of the whole starved, thirsty, lying surface of the territory. And under it, this.

"It's beautiful," he said, and meant it the way you mean a thing that hurts.

"It's a death sentence," Calloway said. "That's what nobody tells you about the truth. Everyone says it'll set you free. Nobody mentions the price of admission." She sat down across the coals from him, finally letting her body have its exhaustion. "We are eleven people who know where sixty trillion gallons of water are buried, and the most powerful mining concern in the territory knows we know, and the agency that signs my paycheck has ordered us erased. Tell me, Ranger — and I ask this as the woman who decides whether you walk out of this canyon tonight or stay in it permanently — what does a man with your history do with a fact like that?"

There it was. *Your history.* She knew. Of course she knew — Okonjo had hired him, and Okonjo knew, and whatever else the survey chief was, she was not careless with information.

Yarrow set the core down in its straw with more gentleness than he had shown anything in years.

"I keep moving," he said. "That's what I do. Nine years, I've kept moving, because stopping is where they catch you." He looked up at her across the small red fire, and heard himself say the rest before he had decided to. "But I'm getting tired, Mrs. Calloway. Tired of carrying a story I can't put down. So maybe — maybe this time — I stop."

Calloway searched his face for the lie and didn't find it, and that scared her worse than anything, because she needed him, and needing things was how the agency got its hooks into people.

It was then that Pell spoke. He had been silent at the edge of the firelight all evening, wrapped in a blanket, watching the cores with an expression Yarrow now recognized — not madness, but memory. Pell's voice came out cracked and small.

"He asked me, out on the flats. Whether I'd seen Vess." Pell's eyes came up, fixed on Yarrow with terrible clarity. "I need to tell you something about Vess, Ranger. Before you decide anything. Before anyone decides anything." He swallowed. "Vess wasn't sent to guard us. I heard the radio traffic the night we ran. Vess reports to Halvard. He always has. And Vess asked the Director one question before we broke camp — one question, I swear it on my mother — he asked whether the survey chief's brother had been told yet."

The fire popped. Calloway went very still.

"Brother," Yarrow said. The word sat in his chest like a stone he had swallowed nine years ago and never passed. "Whose brother?"

Pell looked at him, and then at Calloway, and the blanket slipped from his shoulders.

"Yours," he said to Yarrow. "The man you killed at Rivergate crossing. The warrant man. He was Ada Okonjo's brother."
