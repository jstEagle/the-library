## 9. The Sealed Letter

Calloway had not slept in a slot canyon before. She had not known that stone could hold a person the way a hand holds a bird — gently, and without any intention of letting go. The walls pressed close enough that she could touch both sides with her elbows, and the sky above was a blue ribbon so narrow it seemed less like weather than like a wound. She sat with her back against the sandstone and watched the ranger named Yarrow sleep, or pretend to sleep, ten feet away, his wrists still raw from the rope they'd cut off him that morning.

The others were scattered through the canyon's bends. She could hear Pell humming to himself, the tuneless sound of a man stitching his mind back together with whatever thread he had left. Okafor was cleaning the rifles again, though they'd been cleaned twice already. The sound of oiled cloth on oiled steel was the closest thing they had to a heartbeat.

Eleven people. She counted them the way she counted everything now — constantly, compulsively, the way a gambler counts a dwindling stack. Eleven names on a roster she had memorized in a government office in Rivergate City, eleven names she had read aloud in the dark on the first night of their running, like a prayer or an indictment. Ten now, plus Pell's broken half, plus this stranger the desert had delivered to them bound and blinking.

Yarrow. She turned the name over. He had given it freely enough once they'd decided not to kill him, and that itself was strange — a man with nothing to hide, or a man so long hidden that the name had worn smooth as river stone.

He opened his eyes. Not slowly, not with the fumbling return of a sleeping man, but all at once, the way animals woke. His gaze found her first, then swept the canyon, then returned.

"You count us in your sleep," he said. His voice was low, gravel over sand. "I can hear your lips moving."

"Do you blame me?"

"No." He sat up, wincing at the stiffness. "I'd count too."

* * *

It was Okafor who found the letter. That was how these things went, Calloway thought later — not with drama, but with a hand idly turning out a stranger's saddlebag while looking for a spare water skin.

"Ruth." Okafor's voice carried down the canyon, flat in the way that meant trouble. "You should see this."

Yarrow came around the bend first, Calloway behind him, and there was Okafor holding a letter in both hands as though it were something that might go off. It was wrapped in oilcloth, tied with waxed cord, and the seal at the fold was unbroken — a circle pressed in dark green wax, and within the circle, a stylized river bending through a gate.

Calloway felt the recognition land in her body before her mind caught up. A coldness starting at the base of her skull and running down her spine like water finding a crack.

"That's the agency seal," she said. "The Bureau's field seal. I've seen it on every order we ever received." She looked at Yarrow. "That's *Okonjo's* seal. Ada Okonjo. Your employer."

"My employer," Yarrow said. His face did something complicated and then went still, the way a pool goes still after a stone. "She hired me to find you. Said you were missing. Said your families were asking."

"We know what she said." Calloway held out her hand. "Give it to me."

"It's addressed to me."

"It's sealed with the seal of the woman who ordered us to falsify our findings and then, when we wouldn't, erased our trail from the record and sent a security contractor to hunt us across two hundred miles of desert." She kept her voice level. That was the discipline of it, the surveyor's discipline: you did not let the instrument shake, no matter what the ground was doing. "Give it to me, or open it yourself. Either way, it gets opened."

Yarrow looked at the letter a long moment. Around them, quietly, the others had gathered — drawn down the canyon by some animal instinct for the scent of a turning point. Pell stood at the edge of the group, humming stopped, his eyes fixed on the green wax with an expression Calloway couldn't read. Ilsa Brandt, seventy-one years old and the toughest cartographer in the territory, leaned on her walking staff and waited.

"You think it says something bad," Yarrow said.

"I think," Calloway said, "that Vess found our first cache two days after we buried it. That he knew which wash we'd take before we took it. That a man who has spent nine years outrunning the law managed to be hired by the one agency hunting us within a week of our disappearance." She let that sit. "I think coincidences are for people who haven't been betrayed yet."

Something moved behind Yarrow's eyes — not anger. Agreement. He took out his knife, and Calloway watched his hands, which were steady, and thought: *he has cut things open before and not cared what was inside.* Then he cut the cord and broke the seal, and the wax cracked with a sound like a small bone breaking, and the canyon was so quiet that every one of them heard it.

He read it once, silently. Then he stopped. Calloway watched the stillness come over him — a stillness deeper than the one before, the stillness of a man reading his own sentence.

"Read it aloud," she said.

"I don't—"

"Read it *aloud.*" Her voice cracked on the second word, and she hated that, and she let it stand anyway, because there were things the others had a right to hear with their own ears and not secondhand through her. "We've been erased once already, Mr. Yarrow. We won't be erased twice by silence."

He read it aloud.

*Yarrow — By the time you find this, you will have located the survey team or their remains. Confirm the aquifer finding. If the core samples exist, secure them. The team itself is to be handled as survey casualties — exposure, misadventure, whatever the country provides. No names, no traces. The concession cannot be jeopardized. You will be paid triple on completion, and your outstanding matter with the territorial marshal will be quietly resolved. This letter does not exist. Burn it. — A.O.*

The words hung in the canyon air like dust that wouldn't settle.

*Survey casualties.* Calloway heard Okafor exhale, a long controlled breath, the sound of a man keeping himself from violence. She heard Pell make a small noise, high and lost. She heard Brandt say, very quietly, "I told them. I told the whole team in the first week — no one disappears in a bureaucracy by accident."

Calloway didn't hear herself at first. Then she realized the sound was coming from her, a low continuous tone, and she made herself stop it.

"Exposure," she said. "Misadventure. Whatever the country provides." She looked up at the ribbon of sky. "The country provided us a canyon and a canteen apiece. We were supposed to die of thirst with our instruments in our hands, and some clerk in Rivergate City was going to write *lost to the desert* on our files, and the Halvard Concession was going to drill a hundred miles of the only freshwater aquifer in the territory down to brine."

"Ada Okonjo signed my contract," Yarrow said. His voice was very quiet. "Shook my hand. Asked after my family, and I told her I didn't have one, and she said, *good, that makes a man reliable.*" He folded the letter along its original creases, precisely, as though the precision mattered. "I've been paid by a lot of people to do a lot of dirty work. I always told myself I knew what I was carrying. I didn't know I was carrying this."

"You were going to deliver us," Okafor said. Not a question. His rifle was in his hands, not pointed anywhere, but in his hands.

"I was going to find you," Yarrow said. "That's what I was paid for. What I did after finding you was always going to be my own decision." He met Okafor's eyes and didn't look away. "I've decided."

"Say it."

"I'm not a survey casualty."

* * *

They sat in a circle after that, because Calloway insisted, because the letter had to be answered with something more orderly than rage. The canyon floor was cool sand, and the afternoon light had gone the color of brass, and she made them pass the letter around — every one of them, every hand, so that no one could ever say they hadn't seen it with their own eyes. Brandt read it twice. Pell read it and then folded into himself like a compass closing, and the young hydrologist, Amara, put an arm around him and held on.

"Okonjo wrote this before we were found," Calloway said. "Which means the decision to kill us was made the moment we refused to falsify. Not after we ran. Before." She looked at her own hands, ink-stained, instrument-callused. "I keep thinking there was a moment, some meeting, some afternoon, where she sat at a desk and wrote *eliminate the team* in her own phrasing — *whatever the country provides* — and then went home and ate dinner."

"People do," Brandt said. "That's the lesson of my whole life, Ruth. People do, and then they eat dinner."

"Then we make it so she can't." Calloway heard her own voice harden. "The letter is evidence. It's her hand, her seal, her instruction. Combined with the cores, combined with Brandt's field notes, combined with our testimony — that's not a rumor anymore. That's a conspiracy with a signature on it."

"It's also a death sentence with a signature on it," Okafor said. "For us. If Vess catches us with that letter, we're not witnesses anymore. We're carriers."

"Then we don't get caught."

Yarrow had been silent through all of it, turning the letter over in his hands, and now he spoke without looking up. "The seal's intact. The cord's cut, but the wax is whole enough to show it was hers. A court man can match it against Bureau records." He paused. "There's a problem you haven't said out loud."

"Say it," Calloway said, because she was learning that this was how he worked — he made you ask, so that the truth would be yours and not his.

"Okonjo wrote *your outstanding matter with the territorial marshal will be quietly resolved.*" He looked up at her then, and his eyes in the brass light were unreadable and very old. "She knew about the warrant. Nine years old, and she knew. Which means she didn't hire a stranger off the board at the Rivergate station. She went looking for a man who could be bought with a pardon." He folded the letter closed. "She picked me because I was already buried."

The canyon held its breath. Calloway watched him — this man they had roped and interrogated two days ago, this stranger the desert had handed them — and she understood that something was being offered to her, or tested, or both. A man showing you the exact shape of his own corruption and waiting to see if you'd flinch.

"Then she misjudged," Calloway said. "Because buried things are the ones that come up."

* * *

Night came down into the slot canyon like water into a well. They built no fire — Vess's riders might be anywhere — and ate cold beans and hardtack in the dark, and Calloway sat apart at the canyon's mouth where she could see the stars through the crack of sky.

Brandt found her there. The old woman lowered herself onto the sand with the care of someone dismantling her own body piece by piece, and for a while neither of them spoke. The desert made its small night sounds — wind worrying the canyon lip, a rock cooling with a tick like a clock.

"You knew," Brandt said at last. "Some part of you. From the beginning."

"I hoped I was wrong."

"Hope's not a survey instrument." Brandt's voice was dry as the sand under them. "I've been drawing maps for fifty years, girl. You know what a map is? It's a bet that the truth will still be there when you come back for it. Every line I've ever drawn, I was betting on that." She was quiet a moment. "Okonjo bet against it. She bet the truth would stay buried under that rock, and we'd stay buried with it."

"We didn't."

"No." Brandt looked up at the stars. "But Ruth — listen to me, because I'm old and old women get to say true things. That letter doesn't just tell us what Okonjo wanted. It tells us what she'll do next. She's written us off once. She won't write us off twice. Whatever comes for us now won't be a security contractor and a burned cache. It'll be something with more men and less patience." She turned her head, and her eyes caught starlight. "How many days to the courthouse?"

Calloway did the arithmetic she'd been doing in her head for two days. "Nineteen until the permits expire. Seventeen if we want to arrive with time to file, not just arrive."

"And Vess is behind us, and Okonjo is ahead of us, and the desert is all around." Brandt smiled, a small crooked thing. "I've mapped worse odds. Barely."

From deeper in the canyon came the sound of Yarrow's voice, low, talking with Okafor — the two hardest men among them, negotiating something in the dark. Calloway listened without trying to hear the words, and what she heard in the tone was not trust. It was something more useful: two people agreeing to watch each other.

She looked down at her own hands again, at the ink in the creases that no amount of washing would lift, and thought about the letter folded in her breast pocket, against her heartbeat, the way you'd carry a coal.

Ada Okonjo had signed her name to their deaths and mailed it into the desert, trusting the country to do the rest.

The country had other ideas. The country, it turned out, had sent them a killer with a conscience, an old woman with a pencil, and eleven names that refused to stop being people.

Calloway stood, stiffly, and looked north — the direction of the courthouse, two hundred miles of open ground, and whatever Okonjo would send when she learned her letter had failed to burn.

"We move at first light," she said to the dark, and the dark, for once, agreed.
