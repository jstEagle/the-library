## 10. Permits and Clocks

The letter lay on the flat stone between them like something venomous that had been killed but not yet buried. Nobody wanted to touch it. The sun came down the slot canyon in a single blade of light, and dust turned slowly in that blade, and eleven people sat around a dead fire ring looking at a piece of paper that had been written to kill them.

Yarrow broke the silence because someone had to, and because silence, in his experience, was where bad decisions went to gestate.

"Tell me about the permits," he said.

Calloway looked up. She had not slept — none of them had, he suspected, since Pell had come stumbling into their hidden camp two nights ago with word that a ranger was on their trail — but she wore her exhaustion the way some men wore a badge, squared at the shoulders, displayed on purpose. "You know how concessions work?"

"I know how land works. Land doesn't care about paper."

"Paper is all the Halvard Concession cares about." She drew in the dirt with a stick, and the others leaned in, and Yarrow watched the geometry of it assemble: the territory, the leasehold, the courthouse town two hundred miles east. "The territorial office granted Halvard exclusive mineral rights to four hundred square miles of Rivergate badlands. Provisional grant. Ninety days to file a survey of record confirming the land's assessed value. After ninety days, if no survey is filed, the grant lapses and the parcel returns to open claim."

"And your survey was supposed to be that filing."

"Our survey *was* that filing." Her stick stopped. "We filed nothing. We ran instead. Which means right now, somewhere in a clerk's drawer in Perdition Flats, there is an empty folder with Halvard's name on it, and a clock running out."

"How long?"

"Twenty days from yesterday."

Yarrow did the arithmetic without wanting to. Nineteen days. Two hundred miles of open desert carrying eleven people, one of them already half-broken from heat, through country where water lived only in the memory of old maps. And somewhere behind them, a security contractor named Vess who had been paid, by the sound of the letter, to make sure the folder stayed empty by making sure the surveyors stayed dead.

"Nineteen days," he said.

"Nineteen days," Calloway agreed. "After they lapse, the lease dies. And here's the part Halvard can't allow: once the parcel returns to open claim, testimony becomes admissible. Sworn testimony, before a territorial judge, that the concession's own survey found a freshwater aquifer under that land — an aquifer Halvard's mining operation would poison inside a year. Testimony plus evidence voids any future claim they might file. It isn't just that we'd stop them from getting the land. It's that we'd stop them forever."

A murmur went around the circle. Yarrow let it run itself out. He had learned long ago that fear needed to be spoken aloud before it could be carried, the same way a pack animal needed the load settled before it would move.

"So they don't need to hide what you found," he said. "They need to hide *you*. Dead witnesses can't testify, and dead witnesses don't need a lapsed lease to be silenced."

"That's the shape of it."

He looked at the letter again. Okonjo's seal, neat as a wax button. *Confirm the aquifer exists. File findings as inconclusive. Handle remaining personnel as survey casualties.* He had carried that letter for six days against his chest and never once felt it warm. Now it seemed to radiate cold like a stone pulled from deep water.

"Okonjo hired me to find you," he said. "Not to bury you."

"Are you sure?" That was Pell, the geologist, still gray around the mouth, his voice thin as wire. "Because the money that wrote that letter is the money that hired you."

"It's a fair question." Yarrow met his eyes and held them. "Here's my answer. I read that letter and I'm still sitting here. If I were theirs, you'd have woken up dead two nights ago."

Nobody laughed, but something in the circle loosened, a half-inch of slack in a taut rope. It wasn't trust. Trust took longer than a night and more proof than a pulse. But it was enough to work with.

* * *

They talked until the blade of sunlight climbed the canyon wall and went out, and then they talked by lamplight, shields of tin around the flames so nothing showed from the rim. Yarrow drew in the dirt with a piece of charcoal, and the others watched the map of the territory take shape under his hand — not the surveyor's map of lines and sections, but the ranger's map, the one made of water and shadow and distance.

"Two hundred miles to Perdition Flats," he said. "There are three ways across."

He touched the southern line first. "The stage road. Water every thirty miles, wells at Redgate and Cane Springs. Also the only route a wagon can take, which means it's the only route Vess needs to watch. He'd pin us in a canyon and it would be over in an afternoon."

"The northern route," Calloway said.

"The Salt Fork. Open flats, hard going, but you can see a rider ten miles off in every direction." He shook his head. "Which sounds like safety until you understand that being seen is the whole problem. Eleven people leave a dust plume you could read from the moon. And there's no cover for sixty miles. If Vess picks up our sign out there, we run and he rides, and horses beat feet every time God ever dealt a hand."

"So the middle," said Ruth Calloway.

"The middle is the Sere Basin." His charcoal hovered over it without touching down. On every official map the Basin was blank — not unexplored, just uninteresting, a great pale nothing between the river country and the flats. "No water marked. No roads. No reason for anyone to go in or look. Sixty miles of country that swallows tracks overnight."

"You've crossed it."

"Twice." He did not say when, or why, or what he had been running from either time, and the quality of his silence told them not to ask. "There's water in it. Three seeps. Enough, if we're careful, if we go slow at night and hole up by day. But it will cost us. Everyone drinks less than they want. Everyone walks more than they've ever walked. And if anything goes wrong in there — a horse goes lame, a man goes down — there is no help and no exit except forward."

Ilsa Brandt spoke from the edge of the firelight. She was the oldest of them, a cartographer with hands like folded paper, and her voice was dry and unhurried, as though death were a colleague she'd corresponded with for years. "How much water per person per day, on your crossing?"

"A gallon if we're lucky. Half that if we're honest."

Brandt nodded slowly, doing sums behind her eyes. "Then we carry everything and pray for the seeps. I've mapped deserts forty years, Ranger. The desert always wins the argument eventually. The trick is to be somewhere else when it does."

"We'll be somewhere else," Yarrow said. "In nineteen days, we'll be on the courthouse steps."

* * *

Later, when the others had rolled into their blankets and the camp had gone quiet except for the tick of cooling stones, Calloway found him at the canyon mouth, standing watch under a sky salted thick with stars.

"You should sleep," she said.

"In a while."

She stood beside him, not too close. Below them the desert lay black and enormous, breathing its night heat up against the rocks. Somewhere out in that darkness, Vess and his riders were doing exactly what Yarrow was doing — counting days, counting miles, counting heads.

"Nine years," Calloway said quietly. "Pell talks when he's feverish. He told us you're the one who caught him on the flats, back before any of this. Said you looked at him a long time and let him walk. Said men who do that are either saints or running from something."

"Pell talks too much for a man whose life depends on my good opinion."

"He also said you never asked his name before you cut his hands loose."

Yarrow said nothing. A coyote started up somewhere south, a thin high stitching of sound across the dark, and was answered, and went silent.

"Nineteen days," Calloway said. "That's not much time to cross two hundred miles and convince a territorial judge that a government agency tried to murder its own survey team. We'll need the cores. We'll need Brandt's records. We'll need all eleven of us alive and able to stand in a courtroom and swear to what we saw." She paused. "And we'll need you to testify about that letter."

"I know."

"Do you understand what that means? Standing up in open court. Your name entered into the record. Whatever you've been outrunning since before we ever went into the field — it ends the moment you swear that oath. There's no trail out the back of a courtroom. Not from sworn testimony."

He had known it since he'd read the letter, in the way a man knows a storm is coming from the ache in an old break. But hearing it said aloud, plainly, by lamplight, made it solid in a way his private knowing never had. Nine years of moving. Nine years of never sleeping twice in the same hollow, of giving names like a gambler spending chips, of catching a thief on the flats and letting him go because stopping — even to punish — felt like the beginning of a trap closing.

Nineteen days, and then he would stop. In front of God and a judge and whatever marshal happened to be holding his warrant.

"Yes," he said. "I understand what it means."

Calloway studied him for a moment. Whatever she saw, she didn't flinch from it. "Good," she said. "Because I've spent three weeks learning what it costs to tell the truth in this territory. I'd hate to do it alone."

She left him at the canyon mouth. Yarrow stayed, watching the dark, and let himself do the thing he almost never allowed: he counted backward. Nineteen days to the courthouse. Two hundred miles, minus sixty for the Sere, minus the days they'd lose holed up through the daylight hours. Call it fourteen riding days if nothing broke. Call it seventeen if things broke the way things broke.

Three days of margin. In desert arithmetic, three days was nothing. Three days was the width of a hair laid across a razor.

And Vess knew the clock too. Every hour Yarrow gained by caution, Vess could spend by force. The contractor didn't need to catch them anymore — he only needed to slow them. Burn a cache. Cut a horse down. Put a bullet in a leg. Steal a single day anywhere on two hundred miles of open ground, and the permit clock would run out with the surveyors still in the wilderness, and the whole march would have been for nothing, and the killing could proceed at leisure.

Yarrow turned that over, turning it like a stone in his hand, looking for the flaw.

It came to him near midnight, arriving whole the way the worst truths do. They couldn't win by running. Running was Vess's game; he had horses and patience and permission to be cruel. The only way across was to make speed matter more than safety — to travel so fast, so exposed, so far ahead of any pursuit that Vess's riders spent their strength chasing dust instead of shaping events.

Fast meant risk. Risk meant the weak ones would start failing soon. He thought of Brandt's dry voice, of Pell's wire-thin courage, of the medic Okonjo-sent — no, not Okonjo-sent, he corrected himself, *team-chosen*, the distinction mattered now — a quiet woman named Ferro with steady hands. Eleven lives, weighed against a deadline, and every ounce of his own skill poured into the balance.

He went back into the camp and woke Calloway.

"We leave tomorrow at dusk," he said. "Not the day after. Tomorrow. And we don't stop moving until the Sere."

Her eyes cleared of sleep instantly, the way a soldier's do. "What changed?"

"The math. We have three days of margin and he knows it. So we take the margin away from him. We make this a race he has to win in the open, on ground I choose, instead of a hunt he wins by attrition." He crouched, pulling the charcoal map toward him. "Wake Brandt. I need her hands. If we're going to cross sixty miles of unmapped basin in five days, I want a map of it drawn before we take the first step — seeps, bearings, fallback points, all of it inked so that if I go down, anyone can lead them out."

Calloway was already reaching for her boots. "You think something will happen to you."

"I think something happens to everyone," Yarrow said. "The trick is to be useful before it does."

* * *

Dusk came up gold and then rust and then ash, and they moved.

Eleven people, twenty-three head, the sealed core samples wrapped in canvas and slung on the strongest mule. They went down out of the slot canyon single file, boots soft in the sand, and swung north by a star Yarrow named for them, and within an hour the badlands had closed behind them like a door.

He rode point with Brandt beside him, her notebook open on her knee, sketching by starlight the features he called out — a fallen spire, a gravel bar, the bones of an old wash. Behind them the column stretched out long and quiet, and above them the stars wheeled, indifferent and abundant, and below everything, deeper than any of them could dig, the river they had found ran dark and patient under the stone.

Nineteen days. Two hundred miles. One chance.

Yarrow checked the eastern horizon out of habit and saw nothing — no dust, no firelight, no rider silhouetted against the last of the light. Nothing at all.

That was what troubled him. Vess was good. Vess would not simply be gone.

Somewhere behind them, in the blue dark, the hunt had already changed shape.
