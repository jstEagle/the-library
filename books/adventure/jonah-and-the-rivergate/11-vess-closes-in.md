## 11. Vess Closes In

Ruth Calloway smelled the smoke before she saw it.

She had been awake since the small hours anyway — she slept badly now, in the way of a woman who had made one enormous decision and spent every night since auditing it. The camp lay quiet around her: eleven people breathing under canvas and lean-tos in the fold of a dry wash, the horses staked downwind, the core samples wrapped in oilcloth beneath her own bedroll because no one else would sleep if they knew where they were. Above the canyon rim the stars were hard and close, desert stars, indifferent as nails.

Then the wind shifted, and with it came the smell of burning juniper. Not their fire. Theirs was ash by midnight; Yarrow had taught them that much. This smoke was green and thick, resinous, the smoke of living wood cut and stacked and lit on purpose.

She sat up. Listened. Counted, without meaning to, the way she counted everything now: eleven sleepers, nine horses, four days of water in the good canteens and six days in the bad ones, nineteen days until the permits expired. Survival arithmetic. She had gotten frighteningly quick at it.

By the time she reached the rim of the wash, the eastern horizon had gone the color of an old bruise, and out on the flat below — two miles off, maybe three, at the edge of sight where the land gave way to the Sere Basin's pale beginning — three columns of black smoke stood up into the dawn like things planted.

Their caches. All three of them.

* * *

They had buried the caches themselves, weeks ago, when running still seemed like a plan rather than a postponement. Water tins, biscuit, salt pork, spare ammunition for the two rifles and the shotgun, medical stores, the duplicate survey notebooks. Buried deep, marked with cairns built wrong on purpose — Yarrow's trick, borrowed from whatever school of survival had graduated him — so that only someone who knew to read the wrongness could find them.

Someone had found them.

"Three," Pell said beside her. He had come up the slope silent as a man who'd learned silence the hard way, out there alone in the heat. His eyes tracked the smoke columns and his hands, which had mostly stopped shaking in the last month, began again. "He burned all three."

"He doesn't know we know yet." Calloway kept her voice level. Below them the camp was waking — she could hear Okoro's low voice, the clink of the mess kit, a horse blowing through its nostrils. "Smoke travels. If he wanted surprise he'd have done it quiet. This is a message."

"What's it say?"

Calloway watched the smoke bend eastward on the morning wind, toward the courthouse town, toward the permits, toward everything they were trying to reach. "It says run."

* * *

Yarrow arrived at midmorning, riding out of the south with his hat brim pulled low and dust sheeted to his horse's hocks. He sat his animal at the edge of camp and looked at the three smoke ghosts still hanging over the flats, and Calloway watched him do the same arithmetic she had done, watched it move across his face like weather.

"You went out to check?" she asked.

"Went out to check." He swung down. His movements had a terrible economy to them, nothing wasted, and she had come to understand that this economy extended to his words. "Ashes were cold. Burned yesterday evening. They took what was portable first — I found boot prints, eight or nine men, and drag marks where they pulled the water tins out whole. Didn't even open them. Just piled everything and fired it."

"They didn't need our supplies."

"No." He looked at her then, and his eyes were the gray of the basin stones. "They needed us to have none."

The camp had gathered in a loose half-circle, the way it always did when Yarrow spoke, though half of them still didn't trust him and said so openly. Okoro stood with his arms folded, the big geologist's hands knotted. Dr. Anselm — Fen, everyone called her, their medic, a small sharp-eyed woman who had once stitched Pell's split heel by lantern light while telling jokes — stood near the horse line, and Calloway saw her already doing her own arithmetic, the medical kind: how many days of laudanum left, how many dressings, what happened when those numbers ran out.

"So we go now," Okoro said. "Today. Straight across the Basin."

"Straight across the Basin is a hundred and ten miles of nothing," Yarrow said. "With what water?"

"There's a well at—"

"There's a dry hole at Corrigan's Well, and Vess knows about Corrigan's Well because it's on every map ever drawn of this territory. That's the point of burning the caches. He isn't hunting us anymore. He's herding us." Yarrow crouched and drew in the dirt with one finger: a long arc, the Basin's curve, and small marks along it. "Every water source within three days of here is either poisoned, watched, or printed on a map. He wants us to take the obvious route so he can take us on the obvious route. Or—" the finger paused, "—he wants us to stay put and starve slow, and he doesn't much care which."

"Then what?" That was Marsh, the youngest, the instrument tech, whose voice cracked more than he liked. "We can't stay and we can't go. What's the third thing?"

Calloway answered before Yarrow could, because she had been turning it over since dawn and because she was, whatever else had happened to her, still the chief of this survey. "The third thing is the route nobody's mapped. Yarrow's route. Through the middle of the Basin, off every map, to water that isn't water anyone else knows about."

Everyone looked at Yarrow. He didn't confirm it and didn't deny it. He stood, brushed the dirt from his knee, and said, "I'll want two days to scout the first stretch. And I'll want quiet discipline while I'm gone. Nobody fires at anything. Nobody follows me. Nobody writes anything down."

"He means me," Brandt said from her seat on the supply crate, and there was dry humor in it, but Calloway heard the effort underneath. Ilsa Brandt was sixty-one years old and had been failing since the last hard march — Calloway had watched her ration herself down to half shares and lie about it with a cartographer's precision. Her hands, those miraculous drafting hands, had developed a tremor she disguised by resting her wrist on things.

"He means everybody," Yarrow said. But he looked at Brandt a moment longer than he needed to, and Calloway understood that he had seen it too.

* * *

The two days Yarrow was gone were the longest of the running, and Calloway filled them the way she filled all unbearable time: with work. She re-inventoried the remaining stores with Anselm, item by item, writing nothing down, holding the figures in her head the way she'd once held triangulations. The result was not good. Nine days of short rations. Water for five days at full measure, seven at the measure Yarrow would inevitably impose. Ammunition: forty-one rifle rounds, twelve shotgun shells, and one pistol belonging to nobody who would admit to owning it.

"We're not a fighting unit," Anselm said quietly, folding the last dressing back into its tin. "Eleven scientists and a guide. If it comes to a battle—"

"It won't come to a battle. It'll come to a skirmish, or it won't come at all. Vess doesn't want a fight he has to explain. Dead surveyors are paperwork; missing surveyors are just absence." Calloway closed the tin. "He wants us to vanish cleanly. That's the whole design. That's why the agency hired a man like him instead of sending marshals."

"And you're sure it's the agency? Still?"

Calloway didn't answer right away. The letter had changed something in all of them — Okonjo's seal broken, Okonjo's handwriting instructing Yarrow to confirm the aquifer and then see the team recorded as casualties of the survey. Betrayal was a rockfall: it landed once, and then the echoes went on rolling downhill for days. "I'm sure someone above Okonjo wants it," she said finally. "Whether Okonjo wrote those words with her whole heart or had them written for her, I don't know. It matters for the testimony. It doesn't matter tonight."

Anselm looked at her with those clinical eyes. "You've thought about what happens if we make it. The courthouse. Nineteen days."

"Seventeen now."

"You've thought about whether anyone will believe us. Eleven fugitives and a man with a warrant, carrying rocks in oilcloth."

"I've thought about it every hour," Calloway said, "and I keep arriving at the same place. We don't have to be believed. We have to be *heard*. Once the cores are in evidence and the map is filed, believing becomes somebody else's job. That's all science ever was, Fen. Making truth expensive to ignore."

Anselm almost smiled. "You should write that down."

"I'm not allowed to write anything down."

* * *

Yarrow came back at dusk on the second day with dust on him and a look on his face that Calloway had learned to read as cautious hope, which in Yarrow was practically jubilation. There was a route, he said. A crack in the world that ran northwest through the Basin's spine, following an old fault seam where water had once moved and might still, seeping, pooled in stone tanks no survey had ever recorded because no survey had ever been stupid enough to cross there. It would cost them eight days. It would cost them comfort, and probably blood, and certainly Brandt — he said this part privately to Calloway, apart from the others, his voice low and stripped of anything soft.

"She can't make eight days at march pace."

"She can ride."

"The horses can't carry her and the water both. You know the loads better than I do."

"I know," Yarrow said. And then, because he was capable of a blunt mercy that sometimes felt worse than kindness: "Ask her yourself. Don't decide for her. People get to choose what they spend."

So Calloway went and sat with Brandt in the blue hour, and Brandt listened, and looked north toward the invisible Basin, and said, "Eight days. And after the Basin, how far to the courthouse?"

"Sixty miles. Maybe less, if the country allows."

"Then I'll make it or I won't, and either way my map gets there." Brandt's hand went to the satchel she never let leave her side, the one with the drawings in it — the aquifer's extent rendered in her precise fading ink, the thing they were all dying to deliver. "That's the arithmetic, Ruthie. Stop pretending it's anything else."

* * *

They broke camp before dawn on the third day, and Vess came for them that same night.

Later — much later, in another life — Calloway would try to reconstruct how he had found them so fast, and she would keep circling back to the horses. Nine animals made nine trails, and trails in the desert were sentences written in a language any tracker could read. Yarrow had swept and doubled and walked them over rock, but nine is nine, and Vess, whoever he truly served, had bought himself professionals.

It began, as these things did, with the horses again — with the picket line suddenly screaming.

Calloway came out of her blanket with the shotgun before her mind fully arrived, and the camp was already chaos: dark shapes among the stock, a rifle cracking somewhere up the wash rim, Okoro bellowing, a horse going down with a sound she would hear for years afterward. Someone had cut loose most of the remuda — she heard hoofbeats scatter into the dark, six or seven sets, driven off by riders whooping low and cruel — and the shapes among the remaining animals were firing the picket ropes and the feed tins, firing everything, keeping the camp's heads down with shot after shot from the rim while two men in the dark did the butchering work.

"Down! Everybody down!" She grabbed Marsh by the collar and put him in the dirt. "Return fire only on muzzle flash! Nothing else!"

Pell, of all people, was shooting — steady, measured, the madman calmest of them all, laying rounds at the flashes along the rim with a marksman's patience nobody had suspected in him. Okoro had the team flattened behind the supply crates. Anselm was already moving low along the horse line, because Anselm went toward wounds the way other people went away from them.

It lasted perhaps four minutes. Then the rim went quiet, and the two shapes among the horses mounted and were gone, and the night closed over the sound of them, and what remained was the bill.

Two horses dead in their traces — shot where they stood, deliberately, wastefully, a message written in blood. Of the remuda, six animals scattered into the dark and gone. That left three. Three horses, eleven people, sixty-some miles beyond the Basin, and the Basin itself unmapped and unkind.

And Anselm kneeling in the trampled sand with her hands pressed high on Okoro's thigh, saying, in a voice that stayed perfectly level the way it always did, "I need light. Somebody give me light and hold him still."

The bullet had gone through the outside of his leg, high, missing the bone by luck or by the shooter's indifference. Anselm probed it, cleaned it with the last of the good spirits, sewed it with hands that did not tremble, and talked to Okoro the entire time in a low stream of nonsense and reassurance. Calloway held the lantern and watched the medic work and thought: *there goes our margin.* Because Anselm had used the spirits, and the dressings, and a third of the laudanum, and Okoro could not walk fast for a week, and the week had just become the most expensive currency they had.

When it was done, they gathered in the wreckage of the camp, in the smell of powder and spilled grain and horse blood, and Calloway laid it out for them because someone had to and because she was chief.

"Three horses. One rider per horse, walking rotation for the rest. Nine days through the Basin at Yarrow's pace, and Okoro rides more than walks regardless of rotation. We carry everything else on our backs." She looked around the circle of faces, gray in the starlight, exhausted, frightened, and still — God help them — still hers. "Vess took our supplies and our mounts and drew blood, and he still hasn't got what he came for. Every day we're moving is a day his herding fails. Seventeen days to the permit deadline. We need maybe fifteen. The difference between those numbers is the whole game."

Marsh started laughing — a thin, unhinged sound that he strangled quickly. "The whole game. Right. And if we lose?"

Calloway looked north, toward the Basin, toward the crack in the world Yarrow had promised them, and she thought about green stone and water where water cannot be, and about a letter sealed with a chief's own insignia ordering her death in tidy bureaucratic language, and about a dead horse cooling ten feet away.

"Then the aquifer dies with us," she said, "and Halvard Mining pumps this desert empty for fifty years, and nobody will ever even know there was a question worth asking." She shouldered her pack. "So we don't lose. Wake whoever can sleep. We move at first light."

Behind her, at the edge of the firelight, Yarrow stood looking out into the dark where the riders had gone, and Calloway saw his head turn slowly, tracking something she couldn't see — some sound, some sign, some old instinct of his own. When he spoke, it was barely above the wind.

"We're not alone out here," he said. "There's a third party on our trail. Has been for two days."
