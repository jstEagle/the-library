## 12. The Dry Crossing

The Sere Basin did not appear on any map worth trusting, and that was the whole reason Yarrow trusted it.

He had found it nine years ago, in the first season of his running, when he was still learning what a man could survive and how little of himself the desert needed to take to make the lesson stick. He'd been chasing water then too — chasing it the way a chased animal chases it, without dignity or plan — and he had ridden down into a fold of country so flat and so white with alkali that his horse had refused it, planting her feet and rolling her eyes until he dismounted and led her. The basin had swallowed three days of his life and given back nothing but a cracked tongue and one secret: a line of seeps, thin as thread, stitched across forty miles of ground that every map called dead.

He stood at the rim of it now, at first light, and looked out over the same whiteness. Behind him, eleven people and twenty-three horses waited in the shadow of a basalt shelf. Ahead lay two days of nothing, then a night of climbing through broken hills, then two more days of nothing, and at the end of it — if the seeps still ran, if the rains of nine years had not finally finished them — water enough to live on until the courthouse town.

"Rationing starts now," he said, turning to face them. He kept his voice flat, the way you keep a blade flat against a stone. "Everyone drinks from their own canteen. Nobody shares. When I say drink, you drink two swallows and stop. When I say stop, you stop even if your throat is bleeding."

A young surveyor named Anselm — one of the ones who still believed things would work out — raised his hand like they were in a classroom. "What if someone's dying?"

"Then they're dying," Yarrow said. "Water won't change that, and giving it away will just make it two people instead of one."

The words came out of him hard and clean, and he hated them, and he watched them land on eleven faces and do exactly what they were meant to do: frighten everyone into obedience. Ruth Calloway stood at the edge of the group with her arms folded, watching him the way she always watched him, as if she were taking his measure for a report no one would ever read. She didn't flinch at what he'd said. That was worse, somehow. It meant she understood it was true.

They went down into the basin an hour after sunrise, single file, and the world closed over them like a lid.

* * *

By noon the heat had risen off the alkali in visible waves, and the light came up at them from below as well as above, so that they walked inside a brightness with no edges to it. The horses' shadows shrank to coins beneath their bellies. Sweat appeared on skin and vanished before it could fall, stolen straight out of the air, and by mid-afternoon the salt stood on their faces in pale maps of itself.

Yarrow rode point and made himself count things. Counting was the discipline that kept a man's mind from opening like a door in a windstorm. He counted the riders behind him — eleven, twelve counting himself, thirteen counting the thief, Deke, who had taken the rear position without being asked and held it with the silent competence of a man making amends at a distance. He counted the packhorses. He counted his own breaths when the heat pressed down hardest, four in, four out, the way an old Apache tracker had taught him a lifetime ago, back when he'd been someone else's man with someone else's name.

Ilsa Brandt rode near the middle of the column.

She was sixty-one years old and had drawn maps on four continents, though she never said so herself; Calloway had told him that on the second night, quietly, the way you pass along information about a person you respect. Brandt had hands like knotted rope and eyes the color of river water, and she sat her horse the way old soldiers sit them — badly, permanently, having long ago stopped caring what it cost. She had been failing since Split Rock, Yarrow thought. Not fast. Not in any way you could put a name to. Just a slow dimming, like a lamp running low on oil, and he had been watching her the way he watched everything, cataloguing, measuring, telling himself it wasn't yet his problem.

At the first rest halt, in the meager shade of a leaning rock no bigger than a wagon, he said, "Drink," and watched them all drink, and watched Brandt drink last and least, tipping her canteen with a steadiness that fooled nobody who was looking.

"You're not drinking," he said to her.

"I'm drinking exactly what you told me to." Her voice was dry as the ground under them. "Two swallows. I counted."

"Your lips are cracked."

"So are yours." She smiled, and there was iron in it. "Mr. Yarrow. I have crossed the Kalahari with less than this. What I don't have anymore is the years. Don't spend my water on sentiment. Spend it on the young ones. They're the ones who'll testify."

She turned away from him before he could answer, which was a mercy, because he had no answer, and because looking at her face any longer would have shown him something he wasn't ready to carry.

* * *

The first night they climbed out of the basin floor into the broken hills, and the cold came down on them like a change of government. Desert nights in the high country could drop forty degrees, and the team had left most of their gear burning in Vess's fires, so they huddled in pairs and trios under shared blankets while the horses stamped and blew. Yarrow walked the perimeter twice, then a third time, not because he expected Vess this far out but because walking was easier than sleeping, and sleeping meant dreams, and dreams lately meant Vess's face, and other faces older than that.

Calloway found him on the third circuit. She didn't say anything at first, just fell into step beside him, and they walked together through the moon-silvered rocks like two people who had run out of conversation and discovered they preferred it that way.

"She's getting worse," Calloway said finally.

"I know."

"How much worse?"

Yarrow considered lying to her and rejected it. Lies between the two of them had a short half-life; they decayed into trust problems, and trust was the only supply they had more of than water. "Her pulse is thready. She's hiding how much pain she's in. By tomorrow night she'll be riding double or not riding."

"Can we slow down?"

"No." He said it before the softer part of him could get a word in. "Every hour we lose is an hour Vess gains. And the seeps—" He stopped.

"The seeps might be dry," Calloway finished.

"They might be dry."

She was quiet for a long moment. Somewhere out in the dark, a horse nickered and was answered. "You've done this before," she said. It wasn't a question. "Led people through something like this. Decided things like this."

"I've led mules through worse country," Yarrow said. "Mules don't ask why."

"And people do?"

"People ask why right up until the moment asking gets them killed. Then they either trust you or they don't." He looked at her, and the moonlight showed him her face, tired and lined and absolutely steady. "Do you?"

"I'm here, aren't I."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only kind I give." She turned back toward the camp. "Get some sleep, Mr. Yarrow. You can't lead us dead on our feet."

He slept two hours and dreamed of green stone.

* * *

The second day in the basin was the worst day, and Yarrow knew it would be, and made them start before dawn anyway because the arithmetic allowed no other choice. Forty miles of alkali lay between them and the far rim, and the sun would turn the last fifteen of those miles into a furnace, so they moved in the dark and the gray hours, leading the horses by the reins to spare them, speaking only when speech was necessary.

The rationing bit deep by midmorning. Two swallows, four times a day, was not enough for men and women doing hard labor in killing heat, and Yarrow knew it, and gave the order anyway, and watched the knowledge settle over the team like a second sun. This was the cruelty of it, he thought, the thing no one told you about leadership: that it was mostly the administration of small sufferings, each one defensible, each one necessary, each one a stone added to a load you carried whether anyone thanked you or not. A good leader was just a man who had learned to be cruel slowly, precisely, lovingly — lovingly, because the love was the only thing that kept the cruelty from curdling into mere hardness.

Anselm broke at noon.

Not badly — he didn't bolt or scream. He just stopped drinking when told to stop, and kept the canteen at his mouth, gulping, eyes glazed, and when Yarrow took it from his hands the boy looked at him with an expression of such naked, animal betrayal that Yarrow felt it go through him like a spear.

"There's a schedule," Yarrow said, keeping his voice level. "The schedule is what keeps you alive."

"We're all going to die out here anyway." Anselm's voice cracked on the last word. "You know it and I know it. Why are we pretending?"

Yarrow crouched so they were eye to eye. Around them the column had halted, and everyone was watching, and he let them watch, because this lesson was not only for Anselm.

"Because pretending is a discipline," he said. "Same as walking. Same as drinking on schedule. You pretend you're going to make it, and your body believes you, and your body does what it's told. The day you stop pretending is the day your legs find out." He held out the canteen. "Two swallows. On my mark. And then you get back on your horse and you ride, and you hate me all afternoon if you need to. Hate is free. It doesn't weigh anything."

Anselm drank his two swallows. His hands were shaking. But he got back on his horse.

Behind him, Yarrow heard Brandt say softly, to no one in particular: "Where do they teach them that? There should be a school."

* * *

Brandt fell behind in the last hour of light.

It happened the way these things happen — gradually, then suddenly. First her horse drifted out of line. Then the drift became a lag. Then Pell, riding sweep near her, shouted forward, and Yarrow turned his own horse and went back along the column against its slow current, and found her slumped sideways in the saddle with her eyes open and fixed on nothing, her lips gray, her breathing shallow and fast.

They got her down. They got water to her — real water, a full cup, breaking his own rule, because rules were for the living and this woman was standing at the border. She drank it in small sips, obedient even now, and some of the gray went out of her face, and her eyes found his and focused.

"Well," she said. "There it is."

"There what is," Yarrow said.

"The moment you've been waiting for since we started. Where you decide whether I'm cargo." She smiled again, that iron smile, though it cost her visibly now. "Don't look so stricken, Ranger. I've read your ledger. You've been carrying me in the debit column for two days."

"You're not cargo."

"No. But I'm close, and we both know the arithmetic." She looked past him, at the rim of the basin rising gold and violet in the last light, at the broken hills beyond where the seeps waited like a promise neither of them could verify. "How far to water?"

"Tonight. Maybe midnight. If it's there."

"If it's not there?" 

"Then it isn't," Yarrow said, "and we keep moving, because stopping is just dying with better posture."

Brandt laughed — a real laugh, rusty and surprising, that turned into coughing that frightened everyone within earshot. When it passed she gripped his wrist with a strength that astonished him, rope-knotted fingers closing like a trap.

"Listen to me. I've been mapping since before you were born, and I know what's happening to me better than you do, and better than I'll admit out loud. So here is my price for not becoming cargo." Her grip tightened. "When we reach water, I want paper. Whatever you have. Pencil. I'm going to draw the aquifer. All of it. Every reading, every core log, every depth measurement, from memory if I have to — and my memory, Ranger, is the finest instrument this team owns. Calloway has the data. I have the shape of it. A map that lives in one head dies with the head. A map on paper can be nailed to a courthouse door."

"You can do that when you're rested—"

"When I'm dead, you mean." She said it gently, the way you correct a child. "We both know the ledger. Let me spend what's left on something that lasts. That's all any of us are doing out here, Mr. Yarrow. Spending what's left. Some of us just get to choose what we buy."

He looked at her for a long time. The column waited. The light died by degrees off the rim of the world.

"All right," he said finally. "Paper and pencil. At the seep."

"At the seep," she agreed, and released his wrist, and let them lift her back onto her horse, and rode on.

* * *

They reached the first seep a little after midnight, by Yarrow's reckoning, in a fold of the broken hills where a shelf of limestone caught the runoff of storms that had fallen decades ago and held it the way a miser holds coin.

He found it by feel more than sight — a slight greening of the rock in the starlight, a smell of damp mineral, the sudden cool that rises off hidden water like a whisper. He knelt and dug with his hands in the wet sand at the base of the shelf, and the sand darkened, and darkened, and then a thin silver thread of water welled up between his fingers, cold as a confession.

"Water," he called back up the trail, and heard the word travel down the column from voice to voice like a coal passed hand to hand.

They came down to it in the dark and drank in shifts, two minutes each, no more, because a body half-empty that drinks its fill cramps and vomits and loses more than it takes. Horses stamped and shoved at the trickle and he had to hold them off with shouts and a thrown rock, because stock would kill themselves at water and leave none for the morning. The team filled every container they owned. Someone laughed — Anselm, of all people, laughing like a child — and the laugh spread and then quieted, and then there was only the sound of water dripping into hats and canteens into the black night, the holiest sound Yarrow knew.

Brandt drank her fill. Then she sat propped against the limestone with a blanket around her shoulders, and Yarrow brought her what he had: the back half of a field notebook, a pencil stub, and the flat of his forearm to brace on.

She worked until the sky went gray.

He woke once in the deepest hour and saw her by the light of a shielded lantern, bent over the page, her hand moving in small precise strokes, pausing sometimes to close her eyes and consult whatever vast interior archive she carried. Calloway knelt beside her feeding her numbers in a whisper — depths, flow rates, dates — and Brandt absorbed them and translated them into lines, into contours, into the elegant shorthand of a woman who had spent her life making the invisible visible. The aquifer grew on the page as they watched: a great blind lake, forty miles across, lying under the leased land like a heart under a rib cage.

Near dawn Yarrow brought her tea, warmed over a twig fire, and she took it without looking up.

"My daughter wanted me to stop," she said, conversational, her pencil still moving. "Years ago. Said I'd made enough maps. I told her maps outlive men, and she said that was a grim way to choose a life, and I said perhaps, but it's an honest one." She paused, studying a contour line, adjusting it by a hair. "This one will outlive all of us. That's the point of it. That's the whole point of anything, as far as I can tell."

"You'll deliver it yourself," Yarrow said. "Courthouse steps. Your own hand."

Brandt looked up at him then, and her river-colored eyes were clear and kind and entirely without illusion, and she patted his hand once, twice, the way you pat a horse you're fond of.

"Of course I will," she said.

The sun came up over the eastern rim and lit the basin behind them, forty miles of white emptiness already turning to heat-shimmer, and somewhere out on that emptiness, Yarrow knew, Vess was riding with the patience of a man who had all the water in the world and all the time. The team slept in the seep's thin shade. Brandt slept too, the notebook zipped inside her coat, her pencil still in her fist.

And when they saddled an hour later, she needed help mounting — needed it badly, and didn't pretend otherwise — and Yarrow stood at her stirrup with his hands cupped for her boot and understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water, that the ledger had turned its page overnight. That the next crossing would be harder. That the arithmetic was coming due.

He lifted her into the saddle. She gathered the reins and nodded forward, toward the courthouse town, two hundred miles and six days away, and the column moved out into the morning with the map riding against an old woman's heart.

Behind them, far back on the white floor of the Sere Basin, dust rose where no dust should have been — a thin brown plume, patient and deliberate, moving the way they were moving.

Vess had found the trail.
