## 13. Brandt's Map

The Sere Basin did not forgive. It did not punish either; it simply waited, patient as stone, for whatever was foolish enough to cross it to become part of it.

Yarrow had crossed it twice before in his life, both times alone, both times with water enough and legs enough and no one else's life folded into his own. Now he had ten people behind him, one grave already dug in his mind, and a sky like the inside of a bell — vast, bronze, ringing with heat that never quite became sound.

Ilsa Brandt rode in the wagon bed between the water barrels, propped upright against a rolled tarp, and she was drawing.

She had been drawing since the second day, when the rationing began and Yarrow cut everyone down to three swallows at noon and three at dusk. The others had raged quietly or gone silent or wept without knowing they wept, sweat carrying their grief out of them before their mouths could shape it. Brandt alone had asked for her kit — the oilcloth case she guarded the way other people guarded children — and had begun to work with the last of her strength, as if the map were not a thing she was making but a thing she was becoming.

Yarrow checked on her each time they halted. Each time her hand moved slower across the paper, and each time the lines came surer.

"You're wasting your water on me," she said the first evening, when he held the canteen to her cracked lips.

"I'm spending it where it does the most good."

"Sentiment." She took her swallow anyway. Her eyes were pale as weathered bone, and they missed nothing. "You think I don't know what you're doing, Ranger. You're keeping me alive because keeping me alive is easier than deciding I'm dead."

He said nothing. The basin said nothing back. Somewhere ahead, a hawk turned once against the glare and folded itself away.

"I'm seventy-one years old," Brandt said. "I've mapped four territories and half of another one that didn't have a name yet. I know exactly what my body is telling me, and it is not telling me about tomorrow." She lifted the pencil — a stub worn to the length of a thumb, its point kept sharp with a razor she used like a surgeon. "So we're going to come to an arrangement, you and I. You keep me breathing long enough to finish this. That's all. Don't waste kindness on anything past that line."

* * *

By the third day the basin had eaten their margins. The route Yarrow knew threaded between salt flats that would swallow a horse to the hock and ridges of red shale sharp enough to open a boot like a letter. There was no shade taller than a man. The sun came up already angry and stayed that way until it dropped, and then the cold arrived like a debt collector, taking back everything the day had given.

They traveled at night when the moon allowed, and by day they lay under tarps stretched on rifle barrels, breathing through wetted cloth, and the hours passed in a silence so complete Yarrow could hear the salt crystals ticking as they expanded in the heat.

It was during one of those blind daylight halts that he heard the pencil stop.

Not pause. Stop. The small dry scratch of graphite on paper that had been the basin's only music for two days simply ended, and in its absence he heard something worse: Brandt's breath, which had been shallow all morning, catch and stutter and resume with an effort he could see from ten feet away, the way you can see wind moving through grass.

He crawled to the wagon bed. Her face had gone the color of the salt around them, and her lips were gray, and her hands — those extraordinary hands — lay flat on the oilcloth as if they had been set there deliberately, finished.

"Brandt."

"I'm resting." But her eyes found him, and they were clear, clearer than they'd been in days, the terrible clarity of someone standing at a window with a view of everything. "How far to the wells?"

"Two days. Three if the moon fails us."

"Then I have time." She tried to lift the pencil and could not make her fingers close on it. She looked at her own hand with mild professional disappointment, the way she might regard a faulty instrument. "Ranger. My case. Open it."

Inside, beneath the pencils and the razors and the little brass scale, wrapped in waxed linen, were maps. Not one. A dozen. Yarrow understood, turning them over with hands suddenly clumsy, that she had been working every hour of every halt for longer than this crossing — that some part of her had known from the beginning of the whole affair, from the moment the agency's order came down and Calloway burned it, exactly how this would end and what would need to survive it.

There was the aquifer itself, rendered in cross-section: the water-bearing strata shown as blue-veined bands running under the Halvard lease like a river under a floor. There was the extent map, the boundaries drawn in her fine, certain hand, with elevations and depths noted in a shorthand only a cartographer could read. There were the well sites, marked and ranked. There was a smaller sheet — and this one stopped his breath — showing the aquifer's reach extending north beyond the concession lands entirely, onto unclaimed territory, territory that could be protected by law rather than leased away by it.

"Every figure is from our own instruments," Brandt said. She had propped herself higher to watch him read, and speaking cost her visibly now, each word carried up from somewhere deep. "Calloway's numbers, Pell's assays, my surveys. Cross-checked twice. There isn't a man alive who can call it guesswork."

"Why give me these? They should go to Calloway. Or the marshal."

"Because Calloway will be testifying, and testifying people get searched, detained, discredited. Because marshals file things, and filing cabinets burn." Her mouth twitched — almost a smile. "And because you, Ranger, are a man who knows how to carry something across a desert without being caught. It's the only trade you have. You may as well practice it."

"That's not a reason. That's flattery."

"It's arithmetic." She closed her eyes for a moment, and he thought she'd gone, but she opened them again. "Yarrow. Look at me."

He looked.

"Maps outlive men. That's the whole secret of the work. Every surveyor learns it eventually, usually the hard way. You draw a river and forty years later you're dust and the river is still exactly where you drew it, and some stranger finds your line and follows it and lives, or builds, or drinks, and never knows your name." Her breath whistled. "I have spent my whole life making things that would outlast me. This is the last one. It's also the first one that matters. Do you understand the difference?"

"Yes," he said, and found his voice rougher than he intended.

"No, you don't, not yet. But you will." She nodded at the waxed linen. "Wrap them properly. Keep them against your body — oilcloth hates cold nights less than it hates sweat, so mind the middle of the day. And when you deliver them — not if, when — you tell whoever takes them that Ilsa Brandt drew them true. Not for glory. For accuracy. Glory rots. Accuracy doesn't."

* * *

They moved again at moonrise. Yarrow drove the team through the dark with the maps wrapped flat against his chest, under his shirt, where his heartbeat pressed against them like a second pulse. He had carried warrants and stolen gold and once, for eleven days, a bullet lodged near his collarbone, and none of it had weighed what this weighed — a few ounces of paper that a woman was dying to finish.

Near midnight, at the halt, she called him over.

"One more thing," she said. "The northern extension. The sheet with the unclaimed land."

"I saw it."

"The agency will want that page destroyed most of all. More than the aquifer itself. Do you know why?" She didn't wait for him to answer. "Because water on leased land is a dispute. Water on free land is a future. A dispute can be settled with money and lawyers. A future belongs to whoever shows up for it." Her hand found his wrist — light as a moth landing, and just as deliberate. "You've spent nine years running from what you did. I've watched you. You carry it like a stone in a pack you never set down."

"Brandt—"

"Hush. Dying women talk; it's in the manual." Her grip tightened fractionally. "I'm not telling you to put it down. Some stones are load-bearing. I'm telling you that after the courthouse, when this is done, you'll get to choose what you carry forward. Choose carefully. Maps outlive men, Yarrow. So do choices."

Above them the moon stood in the last quarter, worn thin as a coin passed through too many hands. He sat with her until her breathing evened into something like sleep, and then he went and checked the horses and the water and the dark horizon where Vess's riders might be, because grief was a luxury and the basin charged interest on luxuries.

* * *

She died at dawn.

Of course she did. Yarrow had known it would be dawn from the moment she'd made her arrangement with him; Brandt was not a woman who would let a sunset do a sunrise's work. The eastern sky went from iron to ash to a thin burning rose, and the light came down the long slope of the basin and touched the wagon bed, and she opened her eyes and looked at it with the expression of a surveyor confirming a benchmark.

"There," she said. "That's the light I wanted."

Pell was beside her — Pell, who had been half-mad with heat when Yarrow found him and had come back to himself by inches across the crossing, drawn back the way men are drawn back by a task. He had been grinding pigment for her, charcoal and crushed sandstone, holding the little dishes while her hand shook too badly to hold them herself. His face was wet. He didn't seem to know it.

"Finish the shading on the eastern boundary," Brandt told him. "My hand's not steady enough and it wants to be right. It's the last thing that wants to be right."

"I'll finish it," Pell said. "Ilsa. I'll finish all of it."

"I know you will. That's why I taught you." She turned her head, found Yarrow. "Ranger. The arrangement stands?"

"The arrangement stands."

"Good." She looked at the dawn a while longer. "It's a fine basin. Everyone says it's empty. It was never empty. Nothing is ever empty; people just haven't learned to read it." Her voice was going quiet, not fading so much as settling, like sediment in still water. "Read it for me someday. Tell me what it says."

And then she closed her eyes, and the breath she'd been hoarding so carefully, rationing like everything else in this crossing, she finally spent — one long exhale that seemed to go on longer than any breath could, as if she were emptying herself out onto the land the way she'd emptied herself onto paper, leaving nothing wasted, nothing unlabeled.

The team stood around the wagon in the growing light, ten living people and one finished woman, and nobody spoke. Calloway took off her hat. Vess was somewhere behind them, maybe twenty miles, maybe thirty, hunting them with a patience like their own, and there was no time, and Yarrow made them take it anyway.

* * *

They buried her where the shale gave way to gravel, two miles on, at a spot Yarrow chose with a surveyor's eye: ground that looked like every other ground, indistinguishable in a landscape designed by indifference. No cairn. No marker. No name.

"She'd hate that," Pell said, shoveling. His hands were shaking. "She signed everything. Every map, every margin. She believed in names."

"She believed in accuracy," Yarrow said. "A marked grave is a message. Right now the only message we can afford is silence. If Vess finds her, he finds proof of direction, of pace, of how many we are. If he finds nothing—" He let it hang. Pell shoveled.

They worked fast, in the cold of the early hour, and the gravel went back over her like water closing over a stone, and in twenty minutes there was nothing there at all, just basin, just the enormous indifferent morning. Calloway spoke the burial service from memory, the short one, the field version, her voice low and level, and when she reached the part about the earth receiving what was given to it, Yarrow looked east, where the light was coming up full and gold now, pouring across the flats the way Brandt had poured herself across her maps.

*Maps outlive men.*

He pressed his hand flat against his chest, against the waxed linen riding over his heart, and felt the stiff edges of the sheets beneath the wrapping. Twelve maps. A river under a floor. A future on free land. A dead woman's handwriting, truer than any living man's word.

They mounted up. The basin stretched ahead of them, two days to the wells, and beyond the wells the long climb out, and beyond that the world with its lawyers and permits and guns.

Behind them, the unmarked grave kept its silence. Ahead, the desert went on reading itself to anyone patient enough to listen.

It was Pell who broke the stillness, an hour on, reining up beside Yarrow's horse with the finished map in his saddlebag and his eyes red-rimmed and steady.

"She said you'd know what to do with them," Pell said. "She said you were the kind of man who gets things across."

Yarrow looked at the horizon, where the land climbed toward the wells and the wells toward the courthouse and the courthouse toward whatever name was waiting for him there — his real one, the one with the warrant attached, the one he'd been outrunning for nine years.

"She was right," he said, and put his heels to his horse.

They rode. And far behind them, faint on the southern skyline, dust rose where no dust should be — a thin brown thread against the morning, moving the way they moved, fast and straight and closing.

Vess had found the trail after all.
