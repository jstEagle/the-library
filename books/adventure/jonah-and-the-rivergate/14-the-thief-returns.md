## 14. The Thief Returns

The horses came to them in the hour before dawn, when the desert does its best lying.

Yarrow was on second watch, wrapped in a blanket against the cold that the Sere Basin threw at them like a debt collector, when he heard it — not hoofbeats, which would have woken him from any sleep, but the soft complaint of leather, the creak of a cinch being loosened. He came off the rock he'd been leaning against with his rifle already in his hands and moved toward the sound through the blue-black murk, placing his feet the way you place them in a house where someone is sleeping.

Three horses stood picketed forty yards beyond the camp's edge, where no horses had been an hour ago. They were good animals — dun, bay, and a gray with the deep chest and short cannon bones of something bred for distance, not show. They stood calmly, as if they had always belonged here, as if they had simply grown out of the ground like the creosote and the saltbush.

And sitting against a boulder beside them, hat tipped over his eyes, hands laced across his belly in a posture of profound and insulting ease, was the horse thief.

Yarrow knew him even before the man lifted the brim of his hat. Nine years of reading faces in bad light had made a certain kind of literacy in him, and this face he had read before — on a flat of cracked white earth under a sun like a hammer, with a rope around the man's wrists and terror coming off him in waves. The face had been younger then. Softer. Now it was leaner, weathered into something harder and more honest, if honesty could be said to live in a face like that — all angles and watchfulness, a mouth that looked like it had learned to keep secrets by keeping them badly first.

"You," Yarrow said.

"Me." The man didn't move. His voice was low, pitched to carry only as far as Yarrow and no farther, and there was a discipline in that that Yarrow noted despite himself. "Evening, Ranger. Or morning. I never did learn what this desert calls itself at this hour."

"I let you go once."

"You did." The thief's hands came up slowly, palms out, an old gesture performed without fear. "And I've thought about that every day since. Not the way most men think about it — not grateful, exactly, though I am that too. I mean I've *studied* it. A man with a warrant on him catches a thief red-handed, and instead of dragging him to a cell or putting a bullet in him and calling it justice, he cuts the rope and says *get gone*. That's not mercy. Mercy's cheap. That was something else. That was a man who understood what it is to be running."

Yarrow kept the rifle where it was, angled down but ready. Behind him, the camp slept on — Calloway in her bedroll with her boots still laced, Pell sleeping fitfully and muttering numbers, the medic Ruiz with his bandaged shoulder propped on a rolled saddle blanket, ten exhausted people breathing in the dark like a single wounded animal. Vess was out there somewhere behind them, burning caches and closing ground. And here, in front of them, sat a stranger with three horses and a story.

"How long have you been following us?"

"Since Split Rock." The thief said it plainly, without apology. "I saw your dust two days ahead of me and I knew the shape of it. One man leading a herd of people who don't ride like riders. I figured either you'd found work worth doing or you'd lost your mind, and knowing you, both."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the truest one I've got." The thief took off his hat entirely now, and in the last of the starlight Yarrow could see the lines around his eyes, the particular hollows of a man who had been hungry recently and often. "My name's Teodor Finch. Most folks call me Finch. I stole those horses in Redwater — well, *borrowed* is a strong word for what I did, but I'll get to that. And I've been trailing you because I watched you walk eleven strangers into the worst country in the territory, and I wanted to know what kind of man does that, and whether he might need what I'm carrying."

Yarrow crouched, slow, bringing himself level without ever quite relaxing. "Which is?"

"Horses." Finch nodded at the three animals grazing the sparse bunchgrass with the untroubled air of creatures who trusted their situation completely. "And more where these came from, if we can come to terms. And a pair of legs that know every stock trail between here and the courthouse town, including two crossings of the Salt Fork that aren't on any map Brandt ever drew — no offense to the dead."

The name landed between them like a dropped stone. Yarrow felt it in his chest, the fresh bruise of it — Brandt, buried yesterday morning in a cairn of dark stones with no marker, her map folded against his own skin inside his shirt. "You knew her name."

"I listen." Finch shrugged. "A man alone in open country learns to listen or he learns to die. I heard you bury her. I stayed back. Seemed like the kind of thing a man shouldn't have witnesses for unless he wanted them."

* * *

They talked while the sky went from black to iron to the color of an old scar. Yarrow woke Calloway quietly, and she came and sat on a flat rock with her rifle across her knees and her exhaustion worn openly, like a coat she'd stopped having the strength to take off. She listened to Finch the way she listened to everything now — with the whole of her attention and none of her trust.

"Redwater," she said. "That's a concession company town. Company store, company law, company everything. What were you doing there?"

"Working. Sort of." Finch turned his hat in his hands. "I signed on with Halvard's outfit as a wrangler eight months back. Decent pay, terrible company. You want to know what the concession looks like from the inside? It looks like men who talk about water the way other men talk about money, except they're wrong about which one matters. There's a foreman named Grieves who keeps a ledger of everybody's debts, and the ledger has a way of growing, and there's no way out of Redwater once the ledger says you belong to it. I owed them four months' wages by their arithmetic. So one night I took three horses out of their remuda and rode, and I've been riding since."

"You stole from them," Calloway said.

"I liberated property that was being used to hold men in bondage, if you want the fancy version." Finch's grin flashed and vanished. "But yes. Stole. I'm a thief. I've been one since I was fourteen years old, and I expect I'll be one the day I die, and I'm not asking anybody to pretend otherwise. That's the whole point of what I'm offering."

"And what are you offering?" Yarrow asked. "Exactly."

Finch laid it out with the strange precision of a man who had rehearsed this in the saddle for days: the three horses now, free and clear, because the team needed them and couldn't pay and he knew it. Beyond that, guideship — the Salt Fork crossings, the springs that didn't appear on government maps because he'd found them while running from men with ledgers. In exchange: not money. Not pardon. Just this — when they reached the courthouse, when Calloway stood up in front of the clerk and the marshal and whatever judge the territory scraped together, Yarrow would stand with her and say, out loud, on the record, that Teodor Finch was a man whose word was good. That whatever he'd taken, he'd taken from men who deserved worse. That his testimony about the concession's practices — and he had practices to testify about, names, dates, the ledger itself if he could get back to where he'd cached it — ought to count for something.

"A vouch," Finch said. "That's all. One man standing up in a room full of law and saying *this man is worth believing*. I've never had that. I don't expect I've earned it. But I'm asking anyway, because I watched you cut my ropes on that flat, and I've decided you're the only man in this territory whose word is worth the risk of betting my neck on."

* * *

Calloway walked away from the fire ring after that, and Yarrow followed her, and they stood together looking east where the sky was beginning to think about light.

"He's lying about something," she said.

"Probably."

"Then how can you consider it?"

Yarrow was quiet for a while. Below them the camp stirred — Ruiz up and moving stiffly, Pell sitting upright with his hands pressed flat against the earth as if checking that it was still there. The three new horses stood among their own tired remnant like reinforcements arriving late to a battle.

"Because I know what it is to need somebody to vouch for you," Yarrow said finally. "Nine years ago I had a name and a life and a version of myself I could live inside. Then one night went wrong, and after that, every door in the territory closed except the ones that lead deeper into the desert. Nobody vouched for me. Nobody was going to. And I learned to make a virtue of it — *keep moving, never stop, trust nobody* — but I'll tell you what I've learned since I picked up your eleven names, Calloway. The virtue was just the loneliness wearing a uniform."

She looked at him sideways. "That's not an argument. That's a confession."

"They're the same thing, out here." He turned the problem over the way he turned over tracks, looking for the lie in the pattern. "Here's what I can read. His horses are real. His knowledge of the country checks against what I know of it — the Salt Fork crossings he named are ones I've used, and he described them right down to the broken bank on the north side. Men invent lies out of whole cloth; they don't usually invent accurate details. And his hunger is real. I've seen that look in mirrors."

"And the part you can't read?"

"The part where he says he's been shadowing us for days without ever once making a mistake I could catch." Yarrow almost smiled. "Either he's the finest tracker in the territory, or he wanted to be found. And a man who wants to be found wants something, and a man who wants something will tell you the truth about ninety percent of it and build the rest out of hope. The question isn't whether he's honest. It's whether the ten percent he's hiding can kill us."

"Can it?"

"Everything out here can kill us. The question is whether it kills us faster than Vess will." He looked at the sky, where the first hard edge of light was coming up over the eastern mesas, gilding the teeth of the ridgeline. Nineteen days had become twelve. Twelve days to cover better than a hundred miles of the worst ground left, with a medic carrying a bullet, a geologist carrying ghosts, and a remuda of six horses, half of them played out. Three more horses wasn't a gift. It was the difference between arithmetic and catastrophe.

Calloway followed his gaze to the horses, and he watched her do the same math and arrive at the same ugly sum. "If he betrays us," she said, "we're dead in country with no water."

"If he betrays us, we were probably dead anyway." Yarrow started back down the slope. "Trust isn't a wall, Calloway. It's a bridge. You build it knowing it might drop you in the river, because the far side is the only place worth going."

* * *

He gave Finch his answer at full light, with the team gathered and watching — ten faces hollowed by grief and thirst, every one of them measuring this stranger the way starving men measure bread.

"Here's the terms," Yarrow said. "You ride with us, you guide us, and you take orders same as everyone else. First order: nobody knows our route but me, and that doesn't change. Second: if Vess's riders come, you fight or you run, but you don't negotiate — nothing you have is worth what they'd pay for what we're carrying. Third—" and here he stopped, and made himself say it clean, because the man deserved it said clean, "—if we reach the courthouse, I'll stand up in front of whoever's listening and tell them Teodor Finch kept eleven people alive across the Sere Basin when the law had written him off. I'll vouch. Not because you've earned it yet. Because somebody cut my rope once, in a manner of speaking, and I've decided the debt gets paid forward."

Finch didn't grin this time. Something moved through his face instead — quick and unguarded, there and gone like a bird crossing a window — and he put out his hand and Yarrow took it, and the grip was dry and hard and honest as bone.

"Won't regret it," Finch said.

"Probably will," Yarrow said. "Do it anyway."

They broke camp within the hour, and it was like watching a body remember it had blood. The new horses took weight off the spent ones; Ruiz rode instead of staggering; even Pell seemed to sit straighter in the saddle, as if motion itself were a medicine. Finch worked the remuda with a wrangler's easy hands, and Calloway watched him do it with narrowed eyes, cataloguing, withholding judgment, and Yarrow understood that she would withhold it until the courthouse steps if she had to, and respected her for it.

They made nine miles before noon, threading north along the spine of a ridge that hid them from the low country where Vess's riders would be working. At the noon halt, watering the horses from canvas bags in the shade of an overhang, Finch drifted close to Yarrow and spoke without turning his head, the way men speak when they don't want a sentence to have witnesses.

"There's something you should know," he said. "Reason I caught up to you when I did. Two nights back, I crossed fresh sign in the Salt Fork bottoms — eight, maybe ten riders, shod horses, military spacing. They weren't hunting you. They were ahead of you. Moving toward the courthouse town."

Yarrow's hands went still on the water bag. Ahead of them. Between them and the permits, between them and the testimony, between them and the end of everything — Vess hadn't been chasing at all. He'd been herding.

"Which crossing?" Yarrow asked.

"The lower one. The one I was going to sell you." Finch's mouth was a thin line now, all the ease gone out of it. "Ranger — whoever sent those men doesn't mean to stop you on the road. They mean to be standing on the courthouse steps when you get there. All of them. Waiting."

Behind them, far to the south, a smudge of dust rose off the flats and hung in the windless air, patient as a held breath.
