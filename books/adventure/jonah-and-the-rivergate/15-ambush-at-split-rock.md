## 15. Ambush at Split Rock

Ruth Calloway had learned to read the desert the way other people read faces — by what it refused to say. For eleven days it had been telling her nothing, and she had trusted that silence the way a drowning woman trusts a plank: not because it was sound, but because there was nothing else.

The silence broke on the morning they reached Split Rock.

She felt it first in the birds. A pair of ravens had been pacing them for two days, riding the thermals off the horses' bodies, patient as tax collectors. That morning they were gone. She reined up and looked back along the line of riders — nine now, plus Yarrow and the thief, whose name was Dree and who still moved like a man apologizing to the ground beneath him — and counted the shadows of the canyon walls ahead, tall and red and leaning toward each other like conspirators.

Split Rock was a seam in the earth where an ancient river had sawed through a mesa and left two walls of stone close enough that a rider could touch both with outstretched hands. It was the only passage east for forty miles. Yarrow had said this the night before, crouched over Brandt's map in the firelight, his voice flat as slate: *It's the only way through, and he knows it, and we know he knows it.* They had argued until the coals went gray. There was no alternative but six days around through country without water, and the medic — Alvarez, with the bullet still lodged above her hip — could not last six days. So Split Rock it was, and everyone pretended this was a decision instead of a surrender.

"We go through fast," Yarrow had said. "No talking. No stopping. If anything feels wrong, you ride."

Calloway had nodded, and hated herself for nodding, because she was the one who had insisted on bringing the cores. Forty pounds of sealed steel tubes wrapped in canvas, lashed to a pack frame on her own horse, because she would not let them out of her sight and would not ask anyone else to die for them. *They're just rocks,* Pell had told her once, gently, when the fever was still on him. *You can find more rocks.* But they were not just rocks. They were the only argument in the world that could not be shouted down — green stone and clean water pulled up from four hundred feet, the aquifer's own testimony, sealed and dated and stamped. Everything else — Brandt's map, their word, their lives — was paper. The cores were bedrock.

Now she sat her horse at the mouth of the canyon and listened to the birds' absence, and thought: *bedrock can be buried too.*

* * *

The first hundred yards were fine.

That was the thing she would remember afterward, the thing that would sit in her chest like a swallowed stone for years: how ordinary it began. Sunlight fell in a bright ribbon down the canyon floor. Someone's saddle creaked. Old Ferro, the hydrologist, hummed three notes of something and stopped himself. The walls rose on either side, striped red and ochre, so close that the horses' hooves echoed doubled, as if another party rode beside them in the stone.

Then the echo answered wrong.

Calloway heard it before she understood it — a hoofbeat that came back a half-second late, from above rather than behind. She looked up. The morning sun had cleared the eastern rim, and against its glare the western wall showed her what the shadow had hidden: men. Six, eight, a dozen, prone along the ledge like ticks in a pelt, rifles laid out before them in a neat row, glinting.

"Vess!" she shouted, and the canyon took her voice and threw it everywhere at once, and the world came apart.

The first volley went high — deliberately, she realized later; Vess did not want dead witnesses, he wanted stopped ones — and the noise of it in that stone throat was beyond anything she had ever heard, not a sound but a physical event, a fist of air that slapped her hat sideways and made her horse scream and rear. Dust cascaded off the walls in ropes. Somewhere behind her a man was shouting about the far end, and she twisted in the saddle and saw it: riders. Four of them, maybe five, coming up the canyon's throat from the east at a walk, unhurried, closing the door.

Ambush from both ends. He had not chased them across two hundred miles of desert. He had simply known where they must arrive, and waited there with the patience of the landscape itself.

"Back!" Yarrow's voice cut through everything, low and absolute. "Back the way we came — go!"

But going back meant passing under the ledge, under the rifles, forty yards of open kill-zone, and the horses knew it too; they bunched and wheeled and screamed at each other, and for ten seconds — ten seconds she would relive every night after — the whole company was a knot of animals and fear in the bottom of a stone funnel with death standing calmly on both rims.

Dree solved it. Or broke it, which amounted to the same thing.

Later, piecing it together from the others' accounts, she understood what the thief had done. While the rest of them froze, he had turned his horse straight at the eastern riders — alone, hatless, waving his arms and singing, actually singing, some drunken mining song at the top of his lungs — and fired his pistol into the air twice, and shouted in a voice pitched to carry: *"They're flanking the rim! Left side! Go left, boys, they're on the left!"*

It was nonsense. It was beautiful nonsense. And it worked, because fear is a listening animal. The riflemen on the ledge shifted, scopes swinging west. The riders below checked, wavered, turned to look at their own flank. Ten seconds of confusion — bought with one madman's courage, spent like the last coin in a dying man's purse.

"NOW," Yarrow roared, and the knot came undone.

They went back through the kill-zone at a dead gallop, under the ledge, bullets kicking dust off the canyon floor around them, and Calloway rode with her body flat to her horse's neck and the core samples hammering against her spine with every stride, forty pounds of truth beating on her back like a heart. Behind her she heard Dree's song cut off. She heard a horse go down — the terrible wet crunch of it — and she did not look back, because looking back was how people died, and because if she looked back she would have to see whether it was him.

She was the last but one through the canyon mouth. Yarrow was the last. He came out at a hard gallop, alone, and did not stop for half a mile, and when he finally reined in among the shattered, gasping remnant of them, his face was gray and his eyes went over the group once, twice, counting.

"Where's Dree?"

Nobody answered. Then Ferro, shaking: "He didn't come out."

* * *

They lost two more horses in the next hour — foundered, broken-legged, put down with Yarrow's pistol at close range while their owners stood with their backs turned — and it was not until the middle of that gray afternoon, watering the animals from the skins in a dry wash twelve miles south, that the arithmetic finished itself and Calloway understood the full shape of what had happened.

Nine of them remained. Nine, plus Yarrow. Eight horses. One medic with a bullet in her, one map drawn by a dead woman, and zero core samples.

Because the cores were gone. She had run the count three times, disbelieving it, unwrapping the empty canvas with hands that would not obey her. In the chaos of the reversal — the wheeling, the gallop back under the guns — someone's knife had cut the lashings. Or the lashings had failed. It did not matter which. The pack frame lay somewhere in Split Rock, and Vess had it, and Vess had her.

Because Calloway was gone too.

She learned it from Yarrow, who told it plainly, the way he told everything, as though feeling were a luxury the desert did not stock. He had gone back. Of course he had gone back — she would understand later that this was simply what he was, a man who went back — slipping Dree's abandoned horse into the canyon's shadow while the rest of them ran for their lives. He had found the thief alive, dragged clear of his fallen horse with a bullet-creased scalp, hiding in a crack in the wall while the eastern riders milled and shouted. He had gotten Dree out over the rim on foot, a two-hour climb in the heat, and come down the far side expecting to find the company waiting.

Instead he had found the place where the company had been.

Vess had not been content with the ambush's leavings. Half his riders had swept south after the fleeing survivors, harrying them, herding them — and in that harrying, in the fog of dust and screaming, they had taken Calloway. Taken her quietly, the others said; she had dropped back to help Alvarez when the medic's horse stumbled, and then a dozen riders came out of a side wash, and then she simply wasn't there anymore. No shot fired. No body. Just an empty stretch of trail and, planted upright in the sand where she'd last been seen, one sealed core sample, standing like a grave marker.

A message. *We have what matters. Come and get it.*

"They left one," Yarrow said, turning the tube in his hands. "One out of six. That's not theft. That's an invitation."

* * *

They camped that night in a basin of black rock with no fire, because fires were invitations too, and Calloway — no. Calloway was not there. That was the wound of it, the thing her mind kept snagging on like a nail in a board: every time she thought *we*, she corrected herself, and the correction hurt worse than the original error. *They* camped. *They* rationed the water. *They* looked at Yarrow for decisions.

She was lying on a cot in a tent that smelled of coffee and gun oil, her wrists bound in front of her with soft cord — considerate, even courteous, which was somehow worse — staring at the underside of canvas lit amber by a lantern, listening to Vess talk.

He talked the way water talks in a dripping cave. Patiently. Endlessly. Wearing grooves.

"You have to understand my position, Dr. Calloway." He sat across from her on a folding stool, a neat man, a tidy man, silver at the temples, boots polished in the middle of a desert. Everything about him was wrong for the badlands and right for a boardroom, and she understood suddenly that the badlands were only his commute. "I have contracts. I have obligations. What I don't have is any particular interest in eleven corpses. Corpses generate inquiries. Inquiries generate delays. I am offering you the opposite of a corpse."

"I noticed," she said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "You left one core. Very generous."

"The others are already on a wagon east." He let that land. He was good at letting things land. "Along with your instruments, your field notes, and Ms. Brandt's belongings — we recovered those from your burned cache, incidentally. Tragic. So much paper lost to accidents in this country." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and the lantern caught his face and drained it of warmth. "Here is what happens now. Tomorrow, or the day after, or whenever you're ready, you make a statement. On the record, witnessed, signed. You state that the preliminary findings were erroneous. Contaminated samples. A misread of the strata. It happens all the time — geology is full of honest mistakes. You recant, I release you and your people unharmed, and everyone goes home. The concession proceeds. The territory gets its industry. And you get to be a scientist again instead of a fugitive."

"And if I don't?"

"Then in nineteen days the permits renew, the leases consolidate, and your testimony becomes a curiosity instead of an obstacle. And you become—" he paused, selecting the word with a craftsman's care, "—a casualty. Survey casualties are regrettable. They happen. Deserts are dangerous. Nobody asks many questions about deserts."

She should have been afraid. Some part of her was — the animal part, the part that had kept her alive through nine days of hiding and eleven days of flight, the part that catalogued the distance to the tent flap and the thickness of the guard's silhouette outside. But underneath the fear, rising through it like water through chalk, was something hotter and older and cleaner.

Fury.

Not the hot fury of the ambush — that had burned off in the first hour, leaving only the sick arithmetic of loss. This was the other kind, the kind Brandt had carried to her grave, the kind that maps itself. She thought of Ilsa Brandt's cracked hands drawing the aquifer's extent by lantern light, working against her own failing heart, saying *maps outlive men, Ruthie, draw it true and let them choke on it.* She thought of Pell, half-mad with heat, whispering *green stone, green stone where nothing green should be.* She thought of eleven names on a roster that Okonjo had handed her like a weapon disguised as paperwork, and of the letter she had never opened, and of the word *casualties* sitting in Vess's mouth like a coin he'd bitten.

"You keep saying casualties," she said. "As if the survey died of exposure."

"It did."

"No." She sat up as far as the cord allowed, and made him look at her. "The survey was murdered. By its own agency. By whatever pays for your boots. And you want me to sign the death certificate and call it weather." She held his gaze and did not blink, and saw — this was the discovery, the small cold gift of the whole terrible day — that he did not enjoy this. That there was nothing in his face but function. He was not a sadist or a zealot. He was a tool, a very well-made tool, and tools do not hate the work; they only perform it.

Which meant he could be predicted. Tools could always be predicted.

"I'll think about your statement," she said.

"Take tonight." He stood, folded the stool, and paused at the tent flap with his hand on the canvas, and for one moment the courtesy thinned and she saw the calculation underneath, plain as strata. "Your ranger got away with one of my men's horses and a great deal of luck. Luck runs out in this country, Dr. Calloway. It's the one resource nobody owns." He lifted the flap. "Sleep well."

* * *

She did not sleep. She lay in the dark doing what she had done her whole life, what had made her a surveyor and not a settler: she measured.

The guards: four she could account for, two at the fire, one walking a circuit, one asleep sitting up with his rifle between his knees. The terrain: she had been brought in blindfolded for the last mile, but she had counted turns and timed the intervals, and her inner map was already drafting itself, contour lines blooming in the dark behind her eyes. The cores: five gone east on a wagon, moving at wagon speed, which was slow, which was *catchable*. One here, in this camp, under a tarp on a supply sled ten feet from her tent — she had heard the sled's runners drag past at dusk, had noted it the way she noted everything now, compulsively, the way a starving woman counts crumbs.

And Yarrow. Out there in the dark with eight frightened scientists, a wounded medic, a scalped horse thief, and no plan — but with something better than a plan, maybe. With nothing left to lose, which was the one condition under which Ruth Calloway had watched men become capable of anything.

She thought about the invitation. *Come and get it.* Vess believed he had taken the thing that mattered. He believed she was the keystone, that without her testimony the cores were just rocks and the map was just ink.

He was almost right. That was the trap in it, and also — if she was brave enough, if she was cold enough — the door. Because he was right about everything except the one variable he had never met, the quiet man with the warrant and the rule about never stopping, the man who had walked into an armed camp once already and woken up bound in a slot canyon and somehow talked his way out of it. Vess thought he had cornered them at Split Rock.

Ruth Calloway lay in the dark and let herself, for the first time since Brandt died, hope — and hated the hope even as she held it, because hope was a debt and she already owed too much. Two horses. Dree's blood. Brandt's grave dug by lantern light in ground so hard it took them till dawn. Every mile of this escape had been paid for, and the bill was not yet settled, and somewhere out beyond the picket lines, under a sky salted thick with stars, a man who traded in other people's second chances was counting horses and watching the dark.

She closed her eyes and began, silently, methodically, to memorize the camp.
