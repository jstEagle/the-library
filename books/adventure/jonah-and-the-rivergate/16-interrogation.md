## 16. Interrogation

They took her boots first.

Calloway noticed that the way she noticed everything now — with a surveyor's detachment, cataloguing details because cataloguing was the only thing keeping her mind from running down into the dark. Vess's men had bound her wrists with rawhide and put her on a mule, and somewhere in the night ride back through Split Rock they had pulled her boots off. Not to torture her. To keep her from walking. It was a practical cruelty, and practical cruelties told you more about a man than passionate ones ever could.

The camp they brought her to sat in a box canyon forty miles south of where Yarrow had slipped away with most of her people. She'd heard the shooting at Split Rock — three shots, then the thief's whooping diversion echoing off the walls like a madman's choir — and then hands on her arms, and the core samples pried out of the pack frame while she kicked and cursed, and Vess's voice, calm as a man reading a freight manifest: *Take the cases. Leave the woman. No — bring the woman.*

Bring the woman. She had turned those four words over all night, lying on a bedroll in a tent she was not permitted to leave, staring at the canvas glowing orange with lamplight from outside. Bring the woman meant the woman mattered. Dead witnesses were cheap; this one they wanted breathing.

Her boots sat by the tent flap, laces pulled out, placed neatly side by side. Someone had done it deliberately — arranged them so she would see them every time she looked toward the door. A message without words: *you are not going anywhere.*

She was still looking at them when the flap opened and Vess came in carrying two tin cups.

* * *

He was not what she had expected. She had built him out of nine days of pursuit — the burned caches, the shot horses, the medic Delgado bleeding under a wagon tarp — and in her mind he had become something large and faceless, a force of the desert itself. The man who ducked through her tent flap was medium-sized, gray at the temples, with the careful movements of someone whose knees had been bad for years. He wore a canvas duster gone soft with age and a hat that had been reshaped too many times. He looked like a hundred men she'd passed in a hundred towns. That was the frightening part. Evil, she thought, mostly dressed like somebody's uncle.

"Water," he said, and handed her a cup. "Not poisoned. If I wanted you dead you'd have been left at Split Rock for the buzzards."

She took it because her body overruled her pride. Her throat was sandpaper; she hadn't drunk since morning and the morning had been one swallow. She drank half and made herself stop, set the cup down on the bedroll between them like a piece of equipment she might need later.

"You have my cores," she said.

"I have your cores." He settled onto his heels across from her, unhurried, a man prepared to sit there until the stars burned out. "Eleven sealed tubes in steel cases. Heavy little devils. My men complained the whole ride."

"They're worthless without the field notes."

"They're worthless without the woman who can testify to them," Vess said. "Which is why you're here, Dr. Calloway. Or Miss. I never did learn which you prefer."

"Doctor is fine."

"Doctor." He nodded, as if filing it away. Outside, a wind came up the canyon and pressed against the canvas, and the lamp hanging from the center pole swung and threw their shadows long and wavering up the walls. "You've had time to think. I'd like to know what you've concluded."

"That you're not working for Ada Okonjo."

Something moved behind his eyes — not surprise exactly. Recalculation. "Go on."

"The agency ordered us to falsify the aquifer findings. Okonjo signed that order. If she wanted me dead, she'd have written me off as a casualty and been done — that was already the plan, we found the letter, we know how it reads. But you didn't kill us at the hidden camp when you had the chance. You burned our caches instead. You drove us into the open rather than burying us quietly. And at Split Rock you told your men to take the samples intact and bring me alive." She lifted her chin. "An agency covering its own mistake wants silence. You want something else. You want the findings *confirmed* — by me, publicly, on the record — and then you want me discredited or dead afterward, whichever's cheaper. That's not an agency's play. That's a company's play. Halvard."

The lamp swung. Their shadows leaned and righted themselves.

Vess smiled, and it transformed his whole face into something almost kind, which was worse than anything else he could have done. "They said you were the best surveyor west of the Pecos," he said. "I see the reputation wasn't padding. Yes. Halvard Mining Concession. I've been on their payroll for six years. Okonjo thinks I report to her. Okonjo thinks a great many things." He picked up her water cup, drank from it himself — a small theatrical courtesy, proving again it wasn't poisoned — and set it back down. "The agency leased them the land before anyone knew what was under it. Then your team went and found an ocean of sweet water beneath the driest ground in the territory, and suddenly that lease is worth more than every mine in the territory combined. Do you understand what an aquifer like that means? Not wells. Not a town. A *civilization*. Railroads. Irrigation districts. Forty years of contracts, mortgages, land scrip, political careers. All of it sitting on top of eleven tubes of green stone in a canvas bag."

"And all of it voided if the truth comes out before the permits expire."

"Nineteen days," Vess said. "Fifteen, now." He said it lightly, but she saw his hand tighten on the cup. He was feeling the clock too. That was useful to know. "So here is what happens next, Doctor, and I'll say it once, plainly, because I respect you enough not to dress it up."

* * *

What he offered her was simple, and that was what made it obscene.

Recant. Stand up — in Rivergate, at the permit hearing, wherever the record lived — and declare the aquifer findings erroneous. Instrument error, she'd say. Contaminated samples. A junior geologist's wishful thinking, Pell's heat-madness given a laboratory coat. She would sign an affidavit attesting that no commercially viable water existed under the concession lands, and Halvard's permits would roll forward unchallenged, and in exchange —

"In exchange, you live," Vess said. "You live, and your people live. All of them. The ones who ran with the ranger, the medic with the bullet in her, the old cartographer if she's still breathing. I have riders enough to find every last one of them, Doctor, and I have orders that change depending entirely on your answer. Recant, and those orders become *find them and feed them*. Refuse—" He spread his hands, an apology shaped like a shrug. "Refuse, and the orders stay what they are."

"You'd murder eleven people over water they'll never drink."

"I'd do arithmetic over water," Vess said, without heat. "You're the one calling it murder. I call it the price of the map. Every road, every railroad, every canal ever cut through this country cost somebody something. You people found the water. Fine. But finding a thing doesn't entitle you to decide what happens to it. That decision belongs to men with capital and vision, and they have vision, Doctor. They're going to make the desert bloom. There will be orchards where your team nearly died of thirst. Children who've never seen a river will swim in reservoirs fed by your aquifer. Can you tell me honestly that isn't worth eleven lives? Can you even tell me it isn't worth *yours*?"

And here was the trap within the trap, Calloway thought, watching him: the argument was good. That was what frightened her. She had lain awake half the night constructing refutations and Vess had just walked past all of them in four sentences, because he wasn't arguing science at all. He was arguing consequence, and consequence always sounded bigger than principle. Somewhere in her chest she felt the old surveyor's temptation — the desire to be reasonable, to split the difference, to find the datum point between two claims and stake it. Vess was staking a claim on her conscience, and he was using her own instruments to do it.

"No," she said.

Vess waited.

"No," she said again, and found her voice steadying as she went, the way it did in testimony, the way it did when the numbers were true. "I won't recant, because the findings are correct and I won't put my name on a lie. But I want you to understand that I know what you are now, Mr. Vess. You stood in front of me and offered to trade eleven lives for a lease, and you dressed it up in orchards. You're not a security contractor. You're not even a killer — killers are simpler than you. You're a man who has convinced himself that arithmetic is a moral philosophy. And I have spent twenty years learning that the desert does not care about arithmetic. The desert cares about water, and the water belongs to whoever's standing on it, and that includes every soul your employers intend to bill for it."

The lamp flame guttered in the wind. For a long moment Vess said nothing, and in the silence Calloway became aware of her own heart, steady, and of her bare feet on the cold canvas floor, and of the strange lightness that comes after a decision that cannot be taken back.

"You understand," Vess said finally, quietly, "that I now have to send the other orders."

"I understand."

"And you understand that I gave you the chance. Whatever happens to your people, you were offered peace and you chose the truth. When it goes badly for them, that will be on your ledger, not mine."

"It was always going to be on mine," Calloway said. "That's what leadership is. That's what you wouldn't understand about the letter Okonjo wrote — she wanted casualties, clean ones, deaths she could file. I have eleven names and I carry every one of them awake. You sleep fine. I never will. I've made my peace with the trade."

Vess studied her for a long moment, head tilted, like a man reading an instrument whose needle had jumped somewhere unexpected. Then he rose, joints cracking, and gathered both cups.

"For what it's worth," he said at the flap, "I believe you. About the water. About all of it. That was never in question — I read your preliminary reports before you ever filed them. Good work is easy to recognize." He paused, silhouetted against the orange glow beyond. "It's the *why* that separates us, Doctor. You think the truth is a thing you serve. I think it's a thing you spend."

He let the flap fall behind him.

* * *

She did not sleep. She sat cross-legged on the bedroll with her bare feet tucked under her and reconstructed the camp from sound, the way she'd once reconstructed terrain from contour lines. Two guards at the fire, talking low — one young, one older, the older one coughing. Horses picketed downstream, maybe thirty yards, maybe forty; she could hear one shifting and the dull knock of a hoof against stone. A cook's bell. The canyon walls close enough that the wind funneled and gusted in pulses, which meant narrow, which meant defensible, which meant hard to escape and hard to assault both.

Yarrow would come. She didn't know how she knew it, except that she had watched the man for nine days, watched him read broken ground like scripture, watched him ration water down to sips and give himself the smallest share and lie about it. Men like that did not leave people in Vess's hands. He would come, and he would come wrong-footed and outnumbered, and the cores would be in that tent fifty feet from her, guarded by men who believed the woman inside was beaten.

Let them believe it.

She flexed her bare toes against the canvas and began, silently, methodically, to count the seconds between the sentry's footfalls. Nineteen days on the permits. Fifteen now. Somewhere out in the dark, a ranger with a warrant and a stolen horse was riding toward her with nothing left to lose, and Brandt's map was folded against his chest, and the truth was moving through the desert faster than any rider, the way truth did once you stopped trying to hold it.

The lamp outside dimmed. The watch changed. Calloway closed her eyes and kept counting.
