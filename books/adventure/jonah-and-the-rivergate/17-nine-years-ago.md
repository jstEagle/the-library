## 17. Nine Years Ago

The face came to him in the worst way — sideways, unasked, in the blue hour before dawn when the fire had burned down to a red eye and everyone else slept.

Yarrow sat his watch with his back against a boulder, rifle across his knees, and Vess lay ten feet away, breathing slow and even on his bedroll. The man had taken second watch after Yarrow's, and he slept the way soldiers sleep: lightly, completely, one hand curled near the knife at his belt even in dreams. In sleep his face had gone slack and younger, and it was that slackness that did it — that loosened something in Yarrow's memory like a knot pulled free.

He knew that jaw. He knew the set of those cheekbones, the wide flat plane of the brow, the ears that sat a little too high on the head. He knew them from another face, nine years older now or nine years dead, he couldn't say which, because he had never let himself count.

*Oh,* he thought. *Oh, no.*

The desert around him went very quiet. Not silent — the desert was never silent; there was always wind working the rock, always some small thing moving through the brush — but quiet in the way of a room when someone says a name you have spent your whole life avoiding. The coals ticked. Somewhere down the canyon a nightjar called its two-note question and got no answer.

Yarrow did not move. He had spent nine years learning how not to move, how to keep his body still while everything under it ran, and now all that discipline came home to roost. He sat with Vess's sleeping face in front of him and let the memory come, because it was going to come anyway, and he had learned long ago that a thing you refuse to look at will stand behind you forever.

---

It had rained that day. That was the detail he could never shake, the one that made the whole memory wrong-feeling every time it surfaced: it had *rained*, in a country where rain was a rumor, and the whole world smelled of wet creosote and cold dust, and the arroyo that cut through the Halvard spread ran with an inch of brown water for the first time in three years.

Nine years ago. He had been twenty-six and already tired, already carrying the warrant like a stone in his boot, though back then it wasn't a warrant yet — it was just a name on a paper in a marshal's office in Rivergate, a name attached to questions nobody had asked out loud. A ranger named Marguerite Yarrow, last seen riding toward the concession lands. A man found dead in the arroyo below the old Halvard survey camp. No witnesses. No weapon recovered. Rain falling on everything, erasing what little there was to read.

He remembered the rain because he remembered thinking, as he rode up the flooded wash with water sheeting around his horse's fetlocks, *this is the kind of day things happen.* He'd been young enough then to believe he could feel the future coming, like weather in his teeth.

There had been a survey camp above the arroyo. He hadn't known whose it was — that was the truth, whatever else was true, that part had always been the truth. He'd been tracking stolen stock, six head of mules lifted off a homestead south of the Sere, and the trail led into concession country, and concession country meant the Halvard Mining Company's hired men, and their hired men did not love rangers asking questions on land they considered private by purchase if not by law.

The man in the arroyo had been waiting for him. Or not waiting — standing, rather, at the lip of the wash, in the rain, with a rifle broken open over his arm like a man who has just finished cleaning it. Younger than Yarrow expected. Broad through the shoulders. Dark-haired, going gray early at the temples, with high flat cheekbones and ears set too high on his head, and eyes that were not hostile exactly but were not friendly either — the eyes of a man doing a job he'd been told to do.

"You're the ranger," the man had said. Not a question. "Yarrow."

"I'm looking for mules."

"I know what you're looking for." And then, oddly, almost gently: "You should ride on. Whatever they told you is out here, it isn't worth finding."

Yarrow had thought about that sentence for nine years without once letting himself finish thinking it. Because if he finished it — if he admitted the man had known why Yarrow was really there, had known about the mules being a pretext or the mules being bait, had known what the company wanted kept buried out in that empty country — then the whole shape of that afternoon changed. Then it wasn't a chance meeting gone wrong. Then it was a door, and the man had been standing in front of it, and behind the door was something worth killing for, and someone had sent Yarrow through it.

But he hadn't finished the thought. For nine years he had picked the sentence up and put it down, picked it up and put it down, the way a tongue worries a cracked tooth.

What he remembered clearly, what he had never managed to blur no matter how hard he drank or rode or starved himself: the man's hand moving. Just that. The rifle coming off his arm, or seeming to come off his arm, in the rain, and Yarrow's own body answering before his mind did — nine years of frontier reflexes compressed into half a second, the draw, the shot, the flat crack of it swallowed by the wet air. The man folding down into the arroyo mud with the water running red off him and away, downstream, thinning, gone.

And then the silence after. The rain ticking on his hat brim. The man's hat rolling slowly in the shallows, bumping against a stone, turning, bumping again.

Yarrow had knelt beside him. He remembered that too, though he had tried to lose it. He'd turned the man over and the man was still alive, barely, mouth working, and what he said — or what Yarrow had needed him to say, which might have been the same thing — was a name. One word. It sounded like *Halvard* or it sounded like *half-brother* or it sounded like nothing at all, and Yarrow had stood up in the rain with blood diluting off his hands and told himself it didn't matter, the man would die either way, the shot had gone where shots go, and he had mounted up and ridden south and not stopped since.

Not stopped. Nine years. He had made a religion of it. Keep moving, never stop, never stay anywhere long enough for the story to catch you, because the story was faster than any horse and it knew your name.

---

Now he sat in the dark with a sleeping killer ten feet away and finally, finally finished the sentence.

*Whatever they told you is out there, it isn't worth finding.*

The man had known. He had known about the aquifer — or something, some piece of it, some whisper of green stone and deep water — and he had been posted in that arroyo to turn Yarrow back, and when he reached for his rifle he had been reaching to enforce it, and Yarrow had killed him for the crime of standing in a doorway. And the company had looked at the dead man and looked at the missing ranger and seen a gift: a body, a fugitive, a story so clean it wrote itself. Ranger kills man, ranger runs. Case closed. Nobody asks what the man was guarding.

And the name — the name the dying man said, or seemed to say —

Yarrow closed his eyes and let himself hear it for the first time in nine years, really hear it, not the smear of sound he'd allowed himself. *Vess.* The man had said *Vess*. His own name, or the name he'd go by later, or — no. No, that wasn't it either. He turned it over. The dying man had said a surname, and the surname had been the same as the surname of the broad-shouldered, high-eared man lying asleep by the fire, and Yarrow understood with a lurch like a floor giving way that the resemblance he'd felt was not resemblance at all. It was family. Brothers. The dead man in the arroyo had a brother, and the brother had spent nine years — or however long he'd been hunting — wearing the same jaw, the same brow, the same too-high ears, walking around out here in the world wearing the face of the man Yarrow killed.

Ada Okonjo. Survey chief. He thought of her steady dark eyes, the careful way she'd handed him the roster, the sealed letter he carried unopened against his ribs. She had hired him. She had known his name — the real one, the warrant name — and hired him anyway. Had she known? Had she chosen him *because*? Sent the brother's killer out to find her team, a cruelty dressed as trust, or a test, or a trap with a human hinge?

Or worse — simpler — she had simply needed a man who could not afford to fail, and grief makes good leverage.

The fire settled. A coal collapsed with a small sigh.

Yarrow looked at Vess's sleeping face and felt something crack in him, quietly, the way ice cracks on a river — not loud, not dramatic, just a line running through the middle of a thing that had held. For nine years he had been a man running from a killing he told himself might not have been a killing, a mistake, a reflex, a tragedy with no author. Now he was a man who had killed a surveyor guarding water, on land the company wanted dry, and the company had used his flight to bury the whole story, and the story had eleven living witnesses hiding in a slot canyon, and the brother of the man he'd shot was asleep ten feet away with a knife under his hand.

He could not run from this anymore. There was nowhere left that the story didn't live. It lived in the aquifer under the Halvard lease. It lived in Brandt's map pressed into his saddlebag. It lived in the face of every member of Calloway's team who had looked at him across a fire and decided, God help them, to trust him. It lived in the courthouse two hundred miles east, waiting for him like a debt.

And it lived here, in this face, this jaw, these ears set too high.

Vess stirred. His breathing changed — that soldier's lightness, surfacing toward wakefulness — and Yarrow watched him come up out of sleep the way a man surfaces from deep water, and he did not reach for his rifle, and he did not look away.

Vess opened his eyes. Found Yarrow watching him. Held it.

For a long moment neither moved. The fire was nearly dead between them, and in its failing light Yarrow saw the recognition move through the other man too — slower, heavier, a man realizing the shape of a thing he'd maybe always half-known — and Vess's hand drifted, casual as a man scratching, to rest on the knife at his belt.

"You watch a man sleep long enough," Vess said quietly, "he starts to wonder why."

"Your brother," Yarrow said. His voice came out low and even, and it surprised him, that steadiness, as if the crack had cleared something out. "In the arroyo. Nine years ago. The rain."

Vess went absolutely still. Stillness like that takes practice; Yarrow recognized it, because he'd invented it.

"I don't know what you're talking about," Vess said.

"Yes, you do." Yarrow rose, slow, keeping his hands loose and visible, an old habit that tonight felt like something new — an offering, or a surrender rehearsed. "And I'm done pretending I don't either."

Down the canyon, the nightjar called again, two notes, question and question. East, past the black shoulder of the ridge, the sky had begun to gray, and somewhere out there, two hundred miles of desert away, a courthouse waited with its clocks and its permits and its stamp, and Yarrow understood at last that he had been riding toward it for nine years without knowing it, every mile of every flight, every town abandoned before dawn, every name given and discarded — all of it one long approach, and the road ended at those steps, and on those steps he would have to stop.

Vess sat up slowly, knife loose in his grip, eyes never leaving Yarrow's face.

"Now," Vess said softly, "that's a problem."
