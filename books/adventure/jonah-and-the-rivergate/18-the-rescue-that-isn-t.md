## 18. The Rescue That Isn't

The moon was a sliver of bone over the Split Rock country, and Yarrow used its absence the way other men used darkness — completely.

He had left the horses three miles back, hobbled in a wash where the wind erased their tracks as fast as they made them, and he had walked the last mile barefoot, boots slung around his neck by their laces. It was an old trick and it worked because most men never thought to listen for feet. Vess's camp sat in the mouth of a box canyon below him now: eleven fires banked low, picket lines strung along the eastern wall, two sentries walking a lazy circuit that hadn't changed in the hour and a half he'd watched it. Lazy circuits meant confident men. Confident men slept deep.

Somewhere down there, in one of those canvas wall tents with the company stamp on the flap, Ruth Calloway was either alive or she wasn't. And somewhere near her, packed in waxed crates under guard, were the core samples — eleven cylinders of green-gray stone and saturated sand that weighed less than a dead man's opinion and were worth more than everything within four hundred miles.

Yarrow crouched among the rocks and let the cold of the stone come up through his knees, and he did what he always did before a thing like this: he counted his reasons.

For nine years his reason had been simple. Keep moving. Don't stop. A man with no name and no past needs no reason beyond the next ridge, the next water, the next season's work. But the desert had been busy stripping him down all winter, the way water strips a canyon, and what was left at the bottom was this: a woman who'd looked at him across a fire in a slot canyon and decided to trust him with eleven lives; an old cartographer buried under unmarked sand who'd pressed a map into his hands and told him maps outlive men; a horse thief whose word he'd staked everything on and hadn't yet been made a fool for it.

And beneath all of it, deeper than the aquifer itself, the thing he'd finally admitted on the ride back from Split Rock: the man he'd killed nine years ago had worn a surveyor's brass on his coat, and the survey chief's brother had gone missing that same season, and Yarrow had spent nine years not letting himself finish the arithmetic. He couldn't outrun a sum forever. Sooner or later every equation came due.

So tonight wasn't really about rescue. Tonight was about finishing things.

* * *

He went down the canyon wall on the talus slope, placing each foot like a man setting type, and crossed the open ground during the gap between sentries — forty seconds, maybe fifty, when both men turned together at the picket line's far end. He'd timed it twice. The third time he moved.

The camp smelled of horses and woodsmoke and coffee gone bitter in the pot, and under it all the faint chemical tang of blasting supplies, which told him Vess meant to do more than scare people. He moved tent to tent, staying in the black seams between firelight, counting. Eight tents. Two supply wagons. A mess fly. And there, set apart near the canyon mouth where the escape route ran, a wagon under a tarp with two armed men sitting against its wheels, passing a bottle between them, talking too loud the way guards do when they want the night to feel shorter.

The crates would be under the tarp. Calloway would be in the command tent — the big one, center camp, with a lantern burning behind the canvas and a shadow moving inside it. Vess's shadow, broad-shouldered, pacing.

Yarrow knelt in the lee of the mess fly and took stock, and here is where a smarter man might have turned back. Eleven guards awake or half-awake, two at the crate wagon, one in with the prisoner, sentries on rotation, and himself with a knife, a pistol with five rounds, and no plan worth the name beyond *get in, get her, get out*. Nine years ago he'd have turned back. Nine years ago he still had something to lose.

But the warrant had legs now. The marshal from Rivergate station had been asking after him since before any of this started, which meant his name was already loose in the world like a dropped coin rolling toward a drain. There was no going back to being nobody. The only question left was what name he'd be carrying when they caught up to him — a fugitive's, or a witness's.

That left only the doing.

* * *

The command tent had a back seam where the canvas overlapped, and Yarrow opened it with his knife point, slow as sap, and slipped through into lantern light and the smell of tobacco and ink.

Ruth Calloway sat on a camp cot with her wrists tied in front of her with telegraph wire, and she was not asleep, and she was not crying, and the first thing she did when she saw him was look past his shoulder toward the tent entrance and shake her head once — *trap?* — and he shook his head back — *no trap* — and only then did her face change, and the change was small: a loosening around the eyes, quickly mastered, like ice shifting on a pond.

"You're either the best sight I've had in a week," she whispered, "or the worst."

"Jury's out." He knelt and worked at the wire. It had been twisted tight with pliers and had bitten through to the meat of her wrists; her blood had dried black in the lantern light. "How many know you're here?"

"All of them. Vess wants me to recant. Publicly. On paper, with my seal, saying the aquifer findings were instrument error." Her voice was flat and steady and it frightened him more than screaming would have, because it was the voice of a woman who had already done all her despairing in private and come out the other side with nothing soft left. "He gives me until the permits expire. Then I become a survey casualty."

"Then we're leaving tonight."

"We're not." She said it simply, the way you'd correct a man's arithmetic. "Not without the cores."

Yarrow looked up from the wire. "Calloway—"

"Listen to me." She leaned close, and the lantern caught the raw rings on her wrists, the grit of nine days' captivity in the creases of her face. "I've had six days in this tent to think about nothing else, so hear the whole of it. The cores are the only physical proof. Brandt's map is testimony. Pell's memory is testimony. My word is testimony. Testimony can be bought, discredited, buried under a hundred pounds of counter-affidavit — I watched the agency try to buy mine for two years before we ran. But stone doesn't recant. Stone sits in a courtroom and says *I was drawn from ninety feet down, and I am waterlogged sandstone, and there is a river under the Halvard lease*, and no lawyer alive can cross-examine it." Her eyes held his, gray and unblinking. "You leave me here and take the map to the courthouse, and Vess rides those cores to the concession office tomorrow and drops them down a dry well, and everything Brandt died for becomes a story old men argue about. You understand me? We don't get another core. The ground doesn't get drilled twice."

Outside, a sentry coughed. Yarrow froze with the wire half-twisted, counting heartbeats, then went back to work.

"There are two guards on that wagon," he said. "Maybe more asleep nearby. Eleven of them, one of me."

"Two of us," Calloway said.

"You can barely stand."

"I can walk, I can drive a wagon, and I can shoot better than you, probably." Something flickered at the corner of her mouth — not quite humor, but the place where humor lived before the desert taxed it. "Yarrow. I've spent nine days rehearsing arguments with a man I wasn't sure would come. Don't make me spend the good ones on convincing you."

The last twist of wire gave way. She flexed her hands, wincing, and blood came back into her fingers like ink into blotting paper. Yarrow looked at her a long moment — this woman who had run from her own government rather than bury a river, who had sat in a cage sharpening her refusal into a blade — and felt the strange lightness that had been growing in him all week settle into something solid. Having nothing left to lose was not, it turned out, a weight off a man. It was wings he didn't trust. But wings were wings.

"All right," he said. "Then it's not a rescue. It's a theft."

* * *

They went out the way he'd come in, through the back seam, and lay flat in the shadow of the mess fly while the sentries made their turn. Calloway breathed beside him, controlled and quiet, and he understood suddenly that she had done hard things in the dark before; you didn't lead a survey team into hiding without learning to move at night.

"The wagon," she murmured. "Two guards. The fat one drinks, the thin one doesn't. Wait for the bottle to go around twice — the fat one gets stupid on the second pass, starts arguing with the thin one about cards. Every night since they brought me in. Twenty minutes past midnight, give or take."

"You've been timing them."

"I've had nothing else to time." 

They waited. The moon stayed behind its cloud. Coyotes sang far off in the Sere Basin, and the horses on the picket line shifted and stamped, and the fat guard took the bottle, and drank, and began, right on schedule, to complain about the thin one's card playing in a voice gone loose at the edges.

Yarrow moved first, a low fast line across the open ground while the argument built, and Calloway followed three seconds behind, and if either sentry glanced their way the glance found nothing but night arranging itself. At the wagon the tarp was lashed down with rope, and Yarrow cut it in three pulls while Calloway drifted to the wheel and stood in the guards' blind spot, close enough to hear the fat one say *you cheated me in March and you'll cheat me in heaven*.

Under the tarp: six crates, stenciled HALVARD CONCESSION — SURVEY MATERIALS, each heavy with wax-sealed cylinders packed in straw. Too heavy to carry. Which meant the wagon itself.

Yarrow put his mouth near Calloway's ear. "Can you handle a team cold?"

"I grew up driving a grain wagon in Kansas. Can you cut a picket line quietly?"

Between them they could, and did. The nearest horses were four bays on the end of the line, draft crosses, heavy enough to pull a loaded wagon out of soft sand. Yarrow slipped their halters free one at a time, whispering to them the way he talked to every horse, nonsense syllables that meant *easy, easy, nothing happening here*, and led them back by their cheek straps. The fat guard was now standing, gesturing broadly, narrating some ancient injustice; the thin guard had his arms crossed and his back half-turned, enduring.

They harnessing worked in silence, leather whispering on leather, and the wagon's wheels were the problem — iron-shod, and the canyon floor was gravel. Yarrow solved it the way the desert solves things, cheaply and finally: he cut two saddle blankets in strips and wrapped the wheel rims, ugly, insufficient, better than nothing.

Calloway climbed onto the bench and gathered the lines, and her wrists were shaking now — reaction, cold, nine days of wire — and she steadied them against her knees and said, so quietly he almost missed it:

"When we're clear. There's something you need to know about why Vess has me. It's not Okonjo. It goes higher, and it's worse than you think, and I couldn't say it with a guard outside the canvas."

"Tell me on the road."

"No." She turned and looked at him, and in the dark her face was mostly eyes. "When we're clear. Because once I say it, you'll want to do something about it, and I need you driving first and thinking after. Deal?"

Behind them, the fat guard laughed at his own grievance, a great braying sound that covered the first creak of the wheels. The wagon rolled. The blankets on the rims turned iron shriek into a low woolen mutter, and the mutter became gravel-rattle became distance, and the camp fell away behind them tent by tent, fire by fire, until there was only the canyon mouth ahead, black and wide as an open door.

They were a quarter mile out, walking the team to keep the sound down, when the camp woke behind them — a shout, then a lantern flaring, then the particular ragged chorus of armed men discovering an empty tent and an empty wagon at the same moment. Shots cracked off the canyon walls, wild, hitting nothing but geology.

Calloway didn't whip the horses. She kept them at a steady trot, economical, patient, a woman who understood that races were lost by panic long before they were lost by speed. Yarrow rode the tailboard with his pistol out, watching the lanterns shrink.

"They'll follow at first light," he said. "Vess won't run us down in the dark — he knows these flats favor whoever knows them, and he doesn't."

"He'll do worse than follow." Calloway's voice came back level, but her knuckles were white on the lines. "First light, Yarrow. And then I tell you what's worse."

Ahead, the desert opened under the returning stars — two hundred miles of it, and nineteen days gone to seventeen, and a courthouse waiting at the end like a verdict. Yarrow settled the cores against his boots and felt their weight through the crate boards, small and dense as the truth itself, and thought: *we have stolen a river.* 

Behind them, faint on the wind, Vess's riders were saddling already.
