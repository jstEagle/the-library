## 19. Sandstorm

The sky told them first.

Yarrow had learned to read weather the way other men read scripture — slowly, superstitiously, with the understanding that the text would kill you if you misinterpreted it. All morning the horizon east of Split Rock had been the wrong color: not blue, not gray, but a bruised yellow-brown like old brass, and the wind had gone strange, coming in hot pulses from the southeast instead of its steady western habit. The horses felt it before he did. They stood hobbled in the lee of a rock shelf with their ears pinned flat and their nostrils flared at air that smelled of iron and something older, something mineral and buried.

"Storm," he said to Calloway, and she was already looking at him.

"How bad?"

"Bad enough." He squinted east, where the horizon had begun to boil. It didn't look like clouds. It looked like the land itself standing up — a wall of dust two thousand feet high, rolling toward them with the slow, patient certainty of a thing that had never once been in a hurry because it had never once needed to be. "We can't outrun it. We shelter and we wait."

They had made six miles from the canyon where Vess had taken Calloway's people apart, six miles of hard riding through broken country with the rescued surveyor riding double behind Pell and the core samples strapped across Yarrow's own saddle like a sleeping child. Calloway had not spoken much since he'd pulled her out of Vess's camp in the dark. She carried her fury the way she carried everything — close to the body, organized, rationed. But when Yarrow said *shelter*, she moved without argument, and that trust sat on him heavier than the saddle.

The rock shelf gave them what they needed: an overhang of sandstone tilted against the wind, deep enough for eight people and five horses, with a floor of drifted sand soft as flour. They got the animals packed in tight at the back, blankets over their eyes, and then the humans folded themselves into the remaining space, shoulder to shoulder, and waited for the world to end.

It came at midday, though you couldn't have called it day anymore. The light went first — not dimming but *thickening*, turning the color of tea, then of rust, then of nothing at all. Then the sound arrived, a low grinding roar like a millstone the size of the sky, and then the wall hit them and there was no wall, no sky, no east or west. There was only the storm: a whiteout of red, a blindness with teeth.

Sand came through every gap in the overhang. It hissed against the stone, sifted down their collars, found the corners of their eyes and the creases of their knuckles. Yarrow tied his kerchief over his face and did the same for Brandt's map, which rode in an oilcloth sleeve against his chest, next to the letter he still could not bring himself to burn. He put his back against the rock and let the desert scream.

Beside him, Calloway shouted something. He leaned close.

"I said — how long?"

"Could be an hour. Could be three days."

She nodded. In the murk her face was just a pale suggestion, eyes and jaw floating in the red. "And Vess?"

Yarrow turned his head toward the storm, listening past the roar. Somewhere out in that howling blindness were eleven riders who wanted them dead, and somewhere behind those riders was the man whose face had cracked open the locked room of nine years ago. He thought about the night in Vess's camp — the knife in his hand, the guard's throat under it, the way killing had come back to him like a language he'd sworn he'd forgotten. He thought about the man in the river-bottom nine years ago, and the blood, and the running.

"He's out there," Yarrow said. "Praying we're buried."

* * *

The storm held them for a day and a night.

They rationed water in sips measured by Calloway's steady voice. They fed the horses by hand, handfuls of compressed fodder passed muzzle to muzzle in the dark. The medic — a quiet man named Osei whose arm Yarrow had stitched himself two weeks ago — kept count of everyone's breathing the way he counted everything, and twice in the night he put a hand on Yarrow's shoulder and said nothing, which meant someone was struggling, which meant Yarrow crawled over and held a canteen to their lips until they stopped shivering.

He slept maybe an hour. In his dreams the river-bottom came back, as it always did now, and this time the man he had killed had Vess's face, and when Yarrow woke with his heart slamming he could not remember whether that was the dream lying to him or the truth finally arriving late.

Near dawn the roar began to falter. By full light it had dropped to a whisper, and then to nothing, and the world reassembled itself out of the murk in pieces: the rock shelf, the horses steaming and crusted orange, the sky washed clean and hard and blue as enamel. The land beyond had been rearranged. Dunes had walked. Arroyos had filled. Their own tracks from yesterday were gone as if they had never existed — and so, Yarrow understood immediately, were Vess's.

"They'll be blind too," Calloway said, scanning the featureless west. "No tracks. No bearings. If they were caught in the open—"

"Some of them were." Yarrow had seen riders on the storm's edge before, years ago, near Cimarron Wells. He did not describe what he'd seen afterward. "But Vess isn't a fool. He'll have found stone. And when the air clears, he'll come for us, because we're the only thing left in his world that matters."

They broke camp fast and moved north, toward the courthouse road, toward the last hundred and forty miles. The storm had cost them time they did not have. Nineteen days had become twelve, and twelve were burning down like a fuse.

* * *

Vess found them at midafternoon.

Yarrow heard the shots before he saw anything — three cracks echoing off the mesa walls ahead, spanging off rock well wide of them but close enough to say *I see you*. He swung his glass up and found them: four riders on the rim of the box canyon they'd been descending into, silhouetted against the hard blue sky, and below, cutting off the canyon mouth, more of them dismounted and moving with rifles. Vess had spent the storm doing exactly what Yarrow would have done — holding position on high ground he knew, letting the desert herd his quarry into him.

"He's boxed the canyon," Calloway said, glass to her eye beside him. "Both ends?"

"Both ends. He wants us to stop. He wants us tired." Yarrow lowered the glass. His mind was already running the arithmetic it ran best — ground, water, distance, men — and the arithmetic kept coming back ugly. Eight of them now, counting Calloway. Five horses. Twelve days. A canyon with teeth at both ends.

"There's a way through," he said slowly. "A slot. Half a mile of narrows, too tight for horses, too twisted for rifle lines. We go through on foot, single file, and come out behind his eastern picket."

"And if he's watching the slot?"

"Then we fight in the dark of it, and somebody dies in a place the sun hasn't touched in ten thousand years."

Calloway looked at him for a long moment. "You've done this before," she said. Not a question.

"Once." He looked away. "It went badly."

They went anyway, because the arithmetic left them nothing else. They abandoned two horses in the canyon mouth — a loss that made the medic close his eyes — and went into the slot on foot in the middle of the day, when the sun stood directly overhead and even the shadows were thin. The walls closed in around them, smooth and red and dripping cold, and the noise of the world narrowed to boots on stone and water dripping somewhere in the black ahead. Yarrow went first with a pistol in one hand and the memory of nine years ago walking beside him like a second man wearing his skin.

Halfway through, the shooting started.

It came from behind — from the canyon mouth they'd left — and Yarrow understood in one cold flash that Vess hadn't boxed the canyon to starve them out. He'd boxed it to drive them into the slot, because Vess had read the same ground Yarrow had, and the slot was not an escape. It was a throat, and throats are for choking.

"Back-to-back!" he roared, and the team bunched in the narrows as bullets came whining down the slot and ricocheted off the walls in screaming streaks of light. Stone dust filled the air. A ricochet opened a line across Pell's scalp and the geologist went down swearing and bleeding and got up again. Yarrow pressed himself into a niche and returned fire at the muzzle flashes, and the noise in the slot was apocalyptic, thunder in a bottle, and above it all he heard Vess's voice — calm, carrying, almost gentle:

**"Yarrow! I know who you are!"**

The words dropped into the slot like a stone into a well.

**"Jonah Yarrow. Nine years I've been reading your file. I know about the man in the river-bottom. I know what you did!"**

The team couldn't hear it over the gunfire. Or they could, and chose not to. Either way, Yarrow stood in the dark of the earth with his pistol smoking and felt the past reach through nine years and nine hundred miles and take hold of his collar.

They fought through. It took twenty minutes and cost them nothing but blood and hearing, and then they were out the far end, blinking in the open light, mounted on the three horses they'd staged, running north while the slot spat echoes behind them. But Yarrow rode with Vess's voice lodged in his skull like a splinter, working deeper with every mile.

*I know what you did.*

So did he. That was the trouble. For nine years he had kept the knowing at arm's length, wrapped in fog, wrapped in the rule — keep moving, never stop — and now the rule was breaking, the fog was lifting, and the man in the river-bottom was coming into focus with terrible clarity. Not Vess's face. Someone else's. Someone whose name he had never let himself learn, because learning it would make it real.

Tonight, he thought. Tonight, when they camped, he would stop running inside his own head. Tonight he would look at it.

The desert, as always, had other plans.

* * *

The storm's second half came at dusk.

It rolled out of the southwest without warning — the same bruised brass light, the same hot pulsing wind — and Yarrow realized with a kind of grim awe that the first blow had only been the vanguard, that the whole system was still turning overhead like a wheel, and they were caught in the spokes. There was no shelter this time, no shelf, no slot. There was open basin and failing light and the wall of dust coming on like the end of the world, and behind them, faintly, rifle shots — Vess driving them, herding them, using the storm the way he used everything.

"We run with it," Yarrow shouted. "We run ahead of the wall and we don't stop!"

They ran. Three hours in the roaring dark, the horses screaming, the people bent low over their necks, the storm eating the miles behind them and gaining. When the wall finally swallowed them it swallowed everything — Vess's riders too, Yarrow realized later, because the storm did not take sides. It simply erased. And in that erasure, in that total white blindness of red, with visibility measured in feet and sound shredded to meaningless roar, the two parties that had been hunting each other across two hundred miles of desert were thrown together whether they willed it or not.

It happened at the dry wash.

Yarrow saw the shape materialize out of the murk — a horse, riderless, bolting past them shrieking — and then another shape, a man on foot, stumbling, and then a third, and he had his pistol out and was shouting for Calloway to get the team clear when the biggest shape resolved itself six feet away and it was Vess.

No rifle. Lost or empty or dropped, it didn't matter. Just the man himself, big and dark in the swirling red, and he saw Yarrow at the same instant Yarrow saw him, and whatever calculations either of them had ever made burned away in that moment, because some things are older than strategy.

Vess charged.

They went down together into the sand. The storm closed over them like a fist. Yarrow lost the pistol in the first second — felt it torn from his hand by the impact or the wind, gone — and after that it was hands and knees and elbows and breath, the oldest fight there is, two men trying to end each other with nothing but what God gave them. Vess was stronger. Yarrow had known he would be, had seen it in the man's shoulders, and now he learned it in his ribs as Vess's forearm drove the air out of him and pinned him into the drift. Sand poured into his mouth, his eyes, his ears. Above him Vess's face swam in the murk, wild and streaming, and the man was shouting something — the storm ate most of it, but Yarrow caught fragments: *you killed him, you killed him, I'm going to—*

His hand found the knife at his belt.

Nine years ago, in the river-bottom, his hand had found a knife too. He remembered it now with total, merciless clarity — the way he'd sworn he never would, here in the dark at the bottom of the world with a man's weight crushing the life out of him. He remembered the argument, the survey maps the dead man had been carrying, the drunk fury of it, the moment the blade went in and the man's face changed from anger to astonishment to something worse, something asking *why*, and Yarrow had run, and had been running ever since, and the running had never once been escape. It had only been delay. Every mile of it. Every year.

The knife came free of the sheath.

Vess saw it. Yarrow watched him see it — watched the man's eyes fix on the blade rising between them, watched something flicker there that wasn't fear exactly, wasn't triumph either, but recognition, the look of a man who has chased a story to its final page and found his own name in it. Vess's grip shifted. His hands came up — not to strike, Yarrow realized, but to catch the wrist, to meet the blade honestly, man to man, the way the river-bottom should have been fought if anyone in it had been sober or brave.

And Yarrow stopped.

It was not a decision, or it was a decision made somewhere deeper than deciding, in the place where a man keeps the self he actually is beneath the self he performs. The knife hovered. Vess's chest heaved beneath him — no, above him; Yarrow had reversed them somehow, had rolled the bigger man into the sand, and now he knelt over him with the blade raised and the storm howling and the whole red world waiting to see what he would do.

*This once,* he thought. *This once, not again.*

He threw the knife away. It vanished into the storm, gone as if it had never existed, and he heard it was the truest thing he'd done in nine years.

Vess stared up at him. Sand streamed across them both like time itself made visible. The big man's fists were still clenched, his body still coiled for death, and for a long moment neither of them moved, two enemies kneeling in the belly of the storm with murder suspended between them like a held breath.

"You should have killed me," Vess shouted, and there was something raw under it, something almost bewildered.

"Probably," Yarrow shouted back. "But the man I killed nine years ago — I didn't give him the choice. So you get it. You get the choice I never gave anybody." He leaned close, close enough that Vess could see his eyes through the streaming grit, and he said the rest plainly, without armor: "His name. The man in the river-bottom. Tell me his name, Vess. I've earned that much, and so has he."

Something moved through Vess's face then — a crack in the professional mask, a grief he'd been carrying as long as Yarrow had been carrying guilt, and Yarrow understood suddenly, completely, why the man had hunted him across the desert with such personal fire. This was never only a contract. It never had been.

"His name was Daniel," Vess said. "Daniel Okonjo."

The storm roared. The name landed in Yarrow like a rockfall, and he knelt there in the red blindness with it ringing through him — *Okonjo, Okonjo* — Ada Okonjo, the survey chief who had hired him, who had given him the sealed letter, whose brother had died with Yarrow's knife in him nine years ago in a river-bottom two hundred miles from anywhere.

The storm began, very slowly, to lift.

Out of the thinning murk, shapes were already moving — Calloway's people regrouping to the north, Vess's scattered riders finding each other to the south — and between the two parties, in the widening clearing air, the two men rose from the sand and faced each other across the space where a killing had almost happened twice.

Behind them, far to the north, the first stars were coming out over the courthouse road, and twelve days had become eleven.
