## 20. Two Hundred Miles in Six Days

The storm had scoured everything — the trail, the horses' strength, the last of their illusions. When the sand settled on the morning of the first day, Ruth Calloway stood at the edge of the basin and counted them the way a woman counts coins she knows will not cover the debt: nine survivors, four horses, one crate of core samples wrapped in oilcloth and strapped to the strongest animal, and Yarrow, standing apart with blood crusted in the creases of his knuckles and something new in his face that she could not yet name.

Vess was gone. Swallowed by the storm or limping back to whatever hand held his leash — it didn't matter which. What mattered was distance. Two hundred miles of open desert between them and the courthouse town of Meridian Flats, and six days left on the permit clock before Halvard's lawyers could file the extension that would seal the aquifer under a hundred years of slag and tailings.

"We walk," Yarrow said.

Calloway had expected an argument about the horses. There wasn't one to make. The animals could carry the water and the cores or they could carry people, and the cores were the whole point — eleven names reduced now to nine, but still names, still witnesses, still the living proof that the government's own surveyors had refused to lie.

"Six days," she said. "It took us three weeks to come out here."

"It took you three weeks because you were surveying." He swung up into the saddle, and she saw him wince — ribs, probably, from the fight she'd only heard described in the whiteout, Pell's breathless secondhand account of two men locked together in a world of screaming grit. "We're not surveying anymore. We're testifying. It's faster."

* * *

She learned on that march what her body was actually for.

Not the instruments. Not the transit and the chain and the careful arithmetic of elevation. Those were the tools she'd been trained in, the tools she'd trusted. But somewhere past the first night, when Brandt's absence opened like a hole in the column where a person used to be, Calloway discovered that what she carried was heavier than any theodolite and lighter than any pack: the simple, brutal authority of being the one who did not stop.

Yarrow led. That was his country, his route, his arithmetic of miles and shade and the angle of the sun off the Sere Basin's baked clay. But the pace — the pace was hers. She set it by walking at the front of the column beside his horse, and when someone faltered she walked slower without appearing to slow, so that the faltering one caught up rather than fell behind, so that no one was ever seen to fail.

Pell went first. He always went first, in every way — first to spot trouble, first to laugh, first to break. By noon of the second day he was talking to Brandt. Not to her memory; to her. Calloway heard him describing the terrain ahead in surveyor's shorthand, the way he'd have done if Ilsa were alive and sketching, and she made the decision then that she would never correct him. Grief needed a shape. If his shape was a conversation with a dead cartographer, then let him talk.

Hollis, the youngest, wept openly on the third morning and was ashamed of it. Calloway stopped the column — the only time in six days she allowed a full stop outside the rationed rests — and put the boy's hands on the core crate.

"You feel that weight?"

He nodded, sniffling.

"That's not rock. That's water. Eleven thousand years of it, sitting under land they mean to poison. You're carrying a river, Hollis. Rivers don't apologize for being heavy." She took his canteen, drank nothing from it, handed it back. "Walk."

It was a small cruelty and a large kindness and she could no longer tell the difference between them. That was the thing about leadership nobody warned you of: done right, it looked exactly like doing harm slowly, lovingly, until the harm came due and turned out to be salvation.

* * *

The water arithmetic was the worst part.

Yarrow kept the numbers, but Calloway checked them every evening, kneeling over his field book in the fireless dark while the team slept in the lee of whatever stone the desert offered. Four gallons per person per day had become three on day two, then two and a half, then — after the horses began to founder — two. At two gallons a head in that heat, men died. She knew the tables. She'd written half of them herself, in another life, for another agency, in reports that had been filed and forgotten.

"Two is not survivable," she told him on the fourth night, keeping her voice low. Behind them, Pell was murmuring elevations to Ilsa Brandt's ghost.

"Two is survivable for four more days," Yarrow said. "At three, we run dry tomorrow afternoon and everyone dies at once. At two, some of us die slowly and maybe not all of us die at all." He closed the book. His face in the starlight was all planes and shadow, a face like something carved out of the country itself. "I've done this math before, Calloway. Alone. It's better with company, even the ugly kind."

She wanted to hate him for it and found she couldn't. He rationed himself down to sips — less than anyone, she was certain, though he'd deny it — and when the old mule finally quit on the fifth morning, he shot it cleanly behind the ear without breaking stride, and no one had to watch it suffer, and no one thanked him either, because there was no time and thanks were a luxury measured in seconds they didn't have.

That was the other thing she learned: mercy, on a forced march, is mostly logistics.

* * *

The unraveling came on the fifth day, in the hour she would think about for the rest of her life.

They had crossed into the long flats west of Meridian — ground that shimmered, ground that lied, mirage lakes lying flat and silver and unreachable. The heat came up off the clay like a living thing with its hands around your throat. And Hollis sat down.

Just sat down, mid-stride, in the middle of nowhere, and said, "I'll catch up," in a voice with nothing behind it, and Calloway knew with a cold professional certainty that he would not. The desert was full of people who'd meant to catch up.

Yarrow's horse turned automatically, but it was Calloway who walked back. She stood over the boy and let the sun beat on both of them and said nothing for a long moment, because she understood — she had read enough dying expeditions to know — that what Hollis needed was not encouragement. Encouragement implied the situation was negotiable.

"Get up or die here," she said quietly. "Those are the options. Nobody's coming. Nobody's carrying you. The cores go on the horse and the horse doesn't carry men." She crouched, took his chin, forced his eyes up to hers. "But listen to me, Hollis. Listen. In nineteen hours there's a courthouse with your name in it. In nineteen hours you stand up in front of a judge and say *I measured the water myself*, and everything you're feeling right now becomes evidence. Every blister, every mile, every minute of this. They can't bury us if we arrive. So arrive."

Something moved behind his eyes — not hope, exactly. Something older and tougher than hope. Duty, maybe. Or spite. On the fifth day of a death march, spite will do.

He got up. He walked. And when the lights of Meridian Flats finally pricked the dusk on the sixth evening — coal-oil yellow, impossibly domestic, the most beautiful thing any of them had ever seen — it was Hollis who was singing, a cracked and tuneless marching song, and Pell who answered him verse for verse, talking to the living again as though Ilsa had released him somewhere in the flats, and Calloway brought up the rear of her ragged column and felt tears cutting clean lines through the dust on her face and did not wipe them away, because there was no one left whose opinion of her mattered enough to hide from.

Nine of them had gone into the badlands. Nine came out. One grave unmarked in the Sere Basin, one mule, two horses, and a river riding on the back of the strongest animal, wrapped in oilcloth against the dark.

* * *

Meridian Flats smelled of woodsmoke and horse livery and frying onions, and the smell nearly dropped them all where they stood. Civilization has a scent and it is grease and smoke and it is holy.

Yarrow reined in at the edge of the main street and did not go in.

"The courthouse closes at eight," he said. "The clerk files the extension papers at opening tomorrow — seven sharp, if Halvard's man is here, and he will be here. We file our testimony tonight or we lose." He looked down at Calloway, and in the lamplight spilling from the saloon windows she saw how hollowed he was, how far past his own limits, and how utterly unbent. "You have the map?"

"Brandt's map. On my body. Always on my body."

"The cores?"

"Hollis hasn't let go of the crate since the flats."

"Then go." He turned his horse's head toward the livery at the end of the street. "Get your people fed, get the clerk's stamp on that testimony, and be in that courtroom at opening with every name on the roster ready to swear. I'll see the horses watered."

"Yarrow." She caught his stirrup. "Come with us. You've earned—"

"No." It was gentle and absolute, a door closing on a room she hadn't known existed. He looked past her, up the street, to where a light burned in the marshal's office — the same marshal, she realized, remembering fragments of his story across six days of miles, the same warrant, the same nine years. "There's a thing I have to do tonight. I should have done it nine years ago. Go file your paper, Calloway. Save the river."

He rode off toward the livery alone, and she watched him go, understanding suddenly — the way you understand a contour line only after you've walked the ridge — that the route he'd been leading them down these six days had two destinations, and she had only ever known about one.

Behind her, her team stood in the lamplight, gaunt and staggering and upright, waiting for her word.

"Come on," Ruth Calloway said. "We have a river to save."

And she led them up the courthouse steps, into the light.
