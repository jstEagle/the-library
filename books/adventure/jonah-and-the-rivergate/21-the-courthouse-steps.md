## 21. The Courthouse Steps

They came down out of the hills in the gray hour before dawn, when the desert forgives nothing and promises less, and the town of Rivergate lay below them like something spilled — a scatter of lamplight along the flat, the courthouse a dark block at its center with a flagpole beside it thin as a needle. Yarrow counted heads one last time, out of habit more than need. Ten. Calloway at the front with the core samples wrapped in canvas across her mule's back, riding straight-backed even now, even after six days of sand and thirst and the memory of Brandt going into the ground unmarked. Behind her the geologist Pell, steadier than he'd been in weeks; the two technicians who hadn't spoken above a whisper since Split Rock; the rest strung out like beads on a frayed cord.

Ten. Eleven had gone into the badlands. Eleven names on a roster he'd carried folded against his chest for a month. The arithmetic of it sat in him like a swallowed stone.

"You don't have to do this," Calloway said. She'd reined in beside him where the trail narrowed between two stands of mesquite, and her voice was low, meant only for him. "The map, the cores — that's enough. Testimony is testimony. They don't need your name."

"They don't," Yarrow agreed.

Below them the town was waking. A bakery chimney put up its first smoke, straight as a plumb line in the windless air. Somewhere a dog began to bark and was answered by another, and then another, the whole valley trading the news of morning back and forth.

"Then why?"

He looked at his hands on the reins. Nine years of looking at these hands, of keeping them moving, of never letting them be still long enough to be seen. He thought about what he would tell her — that a man who testifies from hiding has given half a truth, and half a truth is what got them all into this; that Vess would still be out there, and Okonjo, and every man who signed his name to a lie, and they would hunt the witness whose name they didn't know until knowing it became unnecessary. He thought about telling her the true thing, which was simpler and worse: that he had stopped once, nine years ago, in a room with a dying man and a gun going hot in his hand, and everything since had been motion — motion as penance, motion as disguise, motion because a moving target is hard to read and harder to hold. And that this morning, standing at the edge of a town with a courthouse in it, he could feel the motion ending the way you feel a fever break.

"Because the map has my fingerprints on it," he said instead. "And so does the killing."

Calloway looked at him for a long moment. In the graying light her face was all planes and patience, a surveyor's face, a face that measured things and did not flinch from the measurement. "Yarrow," she said. "That isn't your name."

"No," he said. "It isn't."

* * *

They came into Rivergate single file, hooves loud on the packed earth of the main street, and the town turned out to watch them the way towns turn out for anything — children on fences, women in doorways, men outside the saloon shading their eyes. Yarrow knew how they looked. Eleven skeletons on horseback, or ten, dust-caked, sun-blackened, leading mules loaded with what looked like stovepipe sections but were cores of green-veined stone that would, before the day was out, be worth more than every building on this street. He kept his eyes forward. He had learned long ago not to look at faces, because faces were how you got remembered, and being remembered was how a warrant found legs.

But today he let himself look. Today he wanted to be remembered.

The courthouse steps were white limestone, scuffed and stained, wide enough for five men abreast, and they rose maybe twenty feet to a pair of oak doors through which came the smell of paper and lamp oil and floor wax — the smell, Yarrow thought, of the whole weight of the territory gathered up and filed away. On the top step stood the clerk, a small bald man with ink on his fingers and spectacles pushed up into thinning hair, watching them come with the expression of a man who has seen many strange things arrive at his door and expects stranger ones.

At the bottom of the steps stood a marshal.

Yarrow had seen him once before, nine years ago and again three weeks past, in the Rivergate station house, asking after a ranger named Yarrow from nine years ago. Marshal Dade Cray. Big through the shoulders, gray at the temples, with a face like a closed ledger. He'd been polite both times, in the way of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't share. Now he stood with his thumbs in his belt and watched the column come up the street, and when his eyes found Yarrow's, there was no surprise in them at all. Only recognition, patient as stone, the look of a man who has been waiting for a debt to present itself and knows it always does.

Yarrow swung down. His legs nearly buckled — six days of riding and walking and rationing water down to sips will do that — and he made a joke of it, catching the saddle horn, laughing once, a dry sound. Around him the team was dismounting too, and the crowd was pressing closer, and someone had begun to shout questions at Calloway, and she was answering in her flat clear voice, *we are the Halvard survey team, we are alive, we have evidence,* each word laid down like a rail.

Yarrow walked toward the steps.

He heard Cray shift behind him, heard the creak of a belt, the soft double-click of a man deciding whether to draw. He didn't turn around. He climbed. The limestone was cool through his boot soles, and each step sounded louder than it should have, the way things do when your blood is loud. At the fifth step he stopped, and opened his coat.

Under it, strapped against his ribs, were three things. Brandt's map, rolled tight in oilcloth, the old woman's last work, the aquifer drawn in her failing hand spreading beneath the land like a buried lake made visible — she had pressed it into his hands at dawn and told him maps outlive men, and he had carried those words two hundred miles like coals. The sealed letter with Okonjo's broken seal, the one that ordered the team confirmed and then erased. And a warrant, yellowed and soft at the folds, nine years old, with a name on it that wasn't Yarrow.

"Marshal Cray," he said, and his voice carried farther than he intended, out over the crowd, into the sudden hush. "My name is Elias Vann. There's a warrant out of Pinal County with my name on it. Manslaughter." He drew the papers out slowly, holding them high so the clerk and the marshal and everyone below could see. "I'm surrendering."

The word went through the crowd like wind through wheat. Someone said *that's the ranger*, someone else said *the one who found them*, and a boy on the fence repeated *surrendering, surrendering* in a delighted whisper, tasting it.

Cray came up the steps without hurry. Up close he smelled of coffee and gun oil and, faintly, of the livery — a working man's smell, an honest one. He took the warrant first, unfolded it, and read the name there for a long time though Yarrow was certain he'd known it for weeks.

"Elias Vann," Cray said. "Nine years I've had that name in a drawer." He looked up, and his eyes were tired rather than triumphant, which was somehow worse. "You could've run. You had a hundred miles of head start and nobody watching the south road."

"I know."

"Could've handed the girl the map and walked away clean. Nobody here knows your face."

"Somebody does," Yarrow said. "Somebody always does. That's the whole lesson, Marshal. I just finally learned it."

Cray studied him. Then, gently, almost courteously, he turned Yarrow by the shoulder and took his wrist. The irons were cold and heavier than Yarrow remembered iron being, or perhaps he had simply forgotten, in nine years, what stopping felt like. The click of the ratchet was very small and very final, a sound like a period set at the end of a long sentence.

"You'll get your say," Cray said quietly. "Whatever you did out there — whatever you did nine years ago — the judge will hear it all. That's more than Vess would've given you."

"That's why I'm here."

* * *

Inside, the courthouse was cool and dim and smelled of the paper it was made of, walls of stacked files, decades of claims and deeds and water rights, the territory's whole memory pressed flat and shelved. They put him in a chair by the clerk's desk, not a cell — there wasn't time, and besides, Cray seemed to understand that the man in the irons was also the man who had just delivered eleven witnesses and a map worth a war. The clerk stamped documents at a furious pace, and each strike of the stamp rang through the hall like a bell: the permit extension, the evidentiary filing, the emergency injunction against the Halvard Concession. *Nineteen days* had been the deadline, and they had made it with hours to spare, and now the stamp came down again and again, *received, received, received*, each blow driving a nail into the concession's coffin.

Through the tall windows Yarrow could see the steps, and the crowd on them, and Calloway at the top with the cores unwrapped at her feet, green stone drinking the morning light. She was talking to a judge now — Judge Amos Ferro, old and sharp-eyed, summoned from his chambers with his robe over his shirtsleeves. She spoke the way she surveyed: precisely, completely, leaving nothing out. The aquifer. Its extent, per Brandt's map. The order to falsify. The order after that. She named Okonjo. She named Vess. Her voice never rose and never shook, and Yarrow understood, watching her, that this was what authority actually looked like — not loudness, not rank, just a woman telling the truth in complete sentences while armed men tried to make it expensive.

Pell testified next, haltingly, about the green stone and the water where water could not be. Then the technicians. Then, near midday, a rider came in fast from the south with news that made the clerk drop his pen: Vess and four of his men had been taken at the ferry crossing, asleep in their bedrolls, by a posse Cray had sent out two days prior on nothing more than a hunch and a rumor of horses sold cheap. Vess had said nothing at all, Cray reported, except to ask whether the woman was dead. When told she was not, he had laughed, once, without any humor in it whatsoever.

Okonjo was not taken. Okonjo, it developed, had boarded a eastbound train eleven days ago and was by now beyond the territory's reach — but her letter was here, her seal was here, her signature was here, and the law, for once, moved faster than flight. By late afternoon the concession was suspended pending full inquiry, the cores were entered into evidence under seal, and Brandt's map hung framed in temporary custody on the courtroom wall, the old cartographer's careful lines glowing under lamplight, the buried river visible at last to everyone.

Yarrow watched all of it from his chair by the clerk's desk, hands ironed in his lap, and found that he felt — he searched for the word and it took him a moment — *light*. Not happy. Light. As if nine years of running had been a coat of lead and someone had finally cut it off him, and what remained underneath was thinner and colder and more easily wounded, but was, at least, himself.

Near evening Calloway came and sat beside him, on the bench, close enough that their shoulders touched. For a while neither spoke. Outside the light was going amber, then rose, then ash, and the courthouse windows held it the way a cup holds water.

"They're saying commutation," she said finally. "Service instead of prison. Cray's arguing for it. He says a man who delivers himself *and* the evidence ought to count for something." She paused. "I told the judge what you did. All of it. The Sere Basin. The sips. What you carried for us."

"It was Brandt who carried most of it."

"She said you would." Calloway smiled, briefly, tiredly. "She said the ranger would know what to do with a map, because he was the kind of man who stops for other people's dead."

Yarrow looked down at his ironed wrists. "I killed a man," he said. It was the first time he had said it aloud in nine years, and it came out smaller than he expected, almost conversational. "Out past Pinal. He drew on me first — that part was true, it's all true, that's what I've been running from, that it's all *true* — but I didn't have to shoot twice. Nobody makes you shoot twice. That was mine. That was all mine." He flexed his hands. "His name was Tomas. I never said it out loud either. Seems like I should have."

Calloway was quiet a long moment. When she answered, her voice had changed — had gone careful, the way a voice goes when it's stepping onto ice.

"Yarrow," she said. "Elias. What was his family name?"

"Why?"

"Because—" She stopped, started again. "Because Ada Okonjo had a brother. He worked territorial enforcement, out of Pinal, nine years back. He died out there. She never talked about it, but Vess knew, and that's part of why she picked Vess, and—" Calloway exhaled. "Her brother's name was Tomas Okonjo."

The courthouse clock ticked. Somewhere a door closed. The lamplight on Brandt's map wavered as someone passed in the hall, and the buried river seemed to move, to run, dark and cool beneath the paper.

Yarrow sat very still. Nine years of desert poured through him, every mile of it suddenly rerouted, every mercy and every cruelty finding its source at last — the thief he'd caught and released, the team he'd saved, the woman who'd died trusting him, all of it flowing backward through time to a hot room in Pinal County and a man he'd shot twice. He had spent nine years running from a name, and the name had been waiting for him at the end of the trail, wearing the face of the woman who'd hired him, carrying the letter that had ordered the people he'd saved to be erased.

"Tomas Okonjo," he said aloud, testing the weight of it.

"Yes," Calloway said softly.

Outside, the last light left the steps where he had surrendered, and the lamps of Rivergate came on one by one, and under the town, under the territory, unseen and undeniable, the water ran.
