## 22. Rivergate

The rain came in the third week of October, which was itself a kind of miracle, and Yarrow stood on the porch of the Rivergate station with his face turned up into it like a man greeting an old friend he had wronged.

It didn't last long. Desert rain never did — twenty minutes of gray veils marching across the flats, the smell of creosote rising sharp and clean as if the whole basin had opened a bottle of itself, water sheeting off the tin roof and pooling in the wheel ruts until the ruts became rivers and the rivers became mirrors. Then the clouds tore open at the western edge and the sun came through sideways and every drop still falling caught fire.

Behind him, inside the station, someone laughed. That was the other miracle.

He had lived alone in this building for nine years, or near enough to it that the sound of laughter under its roof still startled him, the way a man who has gone deaf is startled by thunder. He turned and looked through the doorway at the room: Pell asleep in the bunk by the stove with a geology text open on his chest, rising and falling; the thief — whose name, it had turned out, was Amos Teague, though everyone called him Thief anyway because names were slow things and habits slower — sat whittling at the table with his boots up, whittling nothing in particular, the way he did when he was happy, which was most of the time now, which Yarrow was still learning not to resent.

"You're dripping," Teague said without looking up.

"It's rain."

"It's *water*. You're standing in it like it's free."

Yarrow looked down at himself. Water ran off the brim of his hat and dripped from his coat hem onto the boards, darkening them one drop at a time. "It is free," he said. "That's rather the point of everything we did."

Teague grinned and went back to his whittling. Outside, the last of the rain moved east toward the Sere Basin, dragging its silver curtain across country that Yarrow knew now would hold that water for years — decades, maybe a century — in the dark sandstone vaults beneath it, the aquifer sleeping under the Halvard lease land like a heart under a ribcage, beating whether anyone believed in it or not.

The concession was voided in August. He had been there for it, or part of him had — the part that could sit still in a courtroom while lawyers said words like *fraudulent misrepresentation* and *suppressed findings* and *permanent injunction*, words that fell over the whole ugly business like a tarp thrown over a body. The judge had read Brandt's map for a long time. Longer than he read anything else. His finger had traced the blue lines she'd drawn showing the aquifer's extent, all two hundred square miles of it, drawn by a dying woman by lamplight in a place where men were hunting her, and when he finished reading he took off his glasses and looked out the window for a while at nothing, and then he said, quietly, as if to himself: "She drew this knowing she wouldn't see it used."

Nobody answered him. There wasn't anything to say. But Yarrow had thought, sitting in the gallery in leg irons he no longer minded wearing: *You're wrong, Your Honor. She sees it fine.*

* * *

His sentence had been commuted before the trial even ended. That was Calloway's doing, mostly — her testimony, and the testimony of ten others, and the sealed letter with Okonjo's signature on it, and the core samples, and the simple unbearable weight of eleven living witnesses against a company that had planned to make them dead ones. Okonjo herself had pled to obstruction and gotten eighteen months; Vess had never come to trial at all. He'd walked out of the hospital in Bitter Wells with his arm in a sling and Yarrow's mercy riding on his shoulder like a debt he couldn't discharge, and he'd vanished somewhere south, and the marshals said they'd find him, and Yarrow supposed they might. It didn't keep him up nights anymore. That was new too.

What kept him up nights, insofar as anything did, was simpler: the fact that he slept at all. That he could lie down in a room with another man breathing in it and not lie awake cataloguing exits. Nine years of running had built him like a wire fence, all tension and no rest, and it was taking a long time to unbend. Some mornings he woke reaching for a rifle that wasn't there, and lay listening to Pell snore, and slowly remembered that nobody was coming. Nobody had been coming for months.

The marshal from chapter two — Hask, his name was, a dry stick of a man with a mustache like a broom — had processed the surrender himself, back on the courthouse steps, and had done it correctly, by the book, cuffs and all, but he'd also done something Yarrow hadn't expected. Walking him down the steps past the crowd, past Calloway already climbing toward the witness stand, Hask had leaned close and said, low: "Nine years you made us look the other way, Ranger. Least I can do is make the paperwork honest." And the paperwork *was* honest, eventually — manslaughter, mitigating circumstances, self-defense that came three minutes too late to count — and the territorial board, embarrassed by the whole affair and eager to be seen doing right by the man who'd saved the water, had commuted the balance to service. Ranger service. The same district. The same desert.

Under his own name.

Marguerite Yarrow. He'd signed it on the commutation order and his hand had shaken, just slightly, the way a hand shakes after carrying something heavy a very long way and finally setting it down. Not tired. Unburdened. There was a difference, and his arm was still learning it.

* * *

In November the survey team scattered, the way good teams do — not broken, but released, each of them carrying a piece of the thing they'd made together out into the world.

Pell stayed first, to everyone's surprise including his own. The heat-madness had burned something out of him and left something quieter behind, and he'd taken a room above the Rivergate store and started writing a monograph on the aquifer that nobody had asked for and everybody would eventually need. He came down to the station some evenings and talked at Yarrow about recharge rates and confining layers until Yarrow's eyes glazed, and Yarrow understood, watching him, that this was Pell's way of burying Brandt properly — not with dirt but with data, every number a stone laid on her grave.

Ilsa Brandt's grave itself was unmarked, out in the Sere Basin where they'd left her, because Vess had been hunting them then and a marked grave was a confession. But in December, once the injunction was final and there was nothing left to hide, Yarrow rode out alone with a hammer and a chisel and a piece of sandstone he'd cut from the rim of Split Rock, and he set it up over the place where they'd dug, and cut into it:

**ILSA BRANDT — CARTOGRAPHER — HER MAPS OUTLIVE US**

Then he sat beside it while the light went long and gold, and told her how it ended. The voided lease. The protected water. Pell's numbers. He didn't cry. He'd compressed the grief so far down during those six days across the Basin that it had become something geological, a seam of ore running through him, and he suspected it would always be there, dense and valuable and buried. He found he didn't mind. Some things you don't spend. You carry them, and they make you heavier, and being heavier makes you harder to blow off the trail.

Calloway went east in the spring. The agency, in its infinite institutional embarrassment, had offered her a promotion — head of field operations, a desk, a title — and she'd declined it with a courtesy so glacial that the director reportedly needed a coat. She took a position instead with a private water commission forming in the capital, one of the reform commissions the scandal had spawned, and her last letter to Yarrow was four lines long and perfectly Ruth:

*The commission will need reliable men in the field. Men who read sign. Men who have been lied to by their own agencies and survived it. I told them I know exactly one.*

*Don't make me a liar.*

*R.C.*

He'd written back two lines: *I'll be here. Same name as before.* Which was, he reflected, the strangest sentence he had ever composed, and the truest.

Okonjo wrote once from prison — a single page, no apology, because he supposed an apology from Ada Okonjo would have been a forgery and she was many things but not that. She wrote only this: *The letter was sealed because I hoped you wouldn't open it. I hired you because I hoped you would fail. Both hopes were unworthy of the work. You were better than the job I gave you. A.O.* He kept it in the drawer with his warrant — the old warrant, the yellowed one, nine years dog-eared — because it seemed right that those two documents should live together, the crime and the confession, in the dark, where he didn't have to look at them but knew they were there.

And the thief stayed. That was the strangest arithmetic of all.

* * *

They rode the eastern line together on a morning in late spring, a year almost to the day since Yarrow had caught Teague leading a stolen bay across the flats and let him go, and the memory rose up between them now the way landmarks rise out of morning haze.

"I've been thinking," Teague said. They were walking the horses along the rim above the Rivergate wash, where the water from October's rain still pooled green in the stone tanks, and a herd of desert bighorn stood drinking below them, unbothered, unhurried, lords of a country that finally belonged to them more than to anyone. "About that day. When you caught me."

"You mean when I failed to catch you."

"I mean when you caught me and then did the other thing." Teague squinted at the bighorn. "I've been trying to figure out why. You had me cold. Rope, rifle, everything. One word from you and I go to Yuma for five years. And you let me ride." He shook his head. "I've been on the wrong end of a lot of law, Yarrow. Never once had any of it be *kind* by accident. So I keep waiting to find out what it cost you."

Yarrow was quiet for a while. Below them the bighorn finished drinking and began to pick their way up the far slope, unhurried, certain of their ground.

"What it cost," he said finally, "was nine years of believing that stopping was dying. That if I ever stood still — if I ever let myself be caught, or held, or known — everything I was running from would catch up and kill me." He watched the last sheep crest the ridge and vanish. "So I couldn't afford mercy. Mercy means stopping. Mercy means turning around. You beg, and I let you go, and for about a mile afterward I hated myself for it, because I'd stopped, and stopping was dying." He turned in the saddle and looked at Teague straight on. "Turns out stopping was the only thing that was ever going to save me. Took a survey team, a dead woman's map, and two hundred miles of hell to teach me that. But you were the first lesson. I just didn't know how to read it yet."

Teague absorbed this with the expression of a man doing sums he doesn't fully trust. Then he nodded, once, and spat over his horse's shoulder, and said: "Well. Good thing I'm a slow learner too, or we'd never have got along."

They rode on along the rim, two riders under a wide sky, and the desert opened ahead of them the way it always did — vast, indifferent, and lately, secretly, full of water.

* * *

Near noon they came down into the Rivergate wash proper, following the line of the old survey stakes, most of them leaning now, weathered gray, half-swallowed by saltbush. Yarrow reined in at one of them — stake fourteen, he knew them all now, he'd ridden this ground so often — and swung down to check the water tank at its base. Not a government tank. A new one. The commission had sunk test wells through the fall, and where the wells came in sweet, they'd built small stone basins, and where the basins filled, wildlife came, and where wildlife came, the country changed. Already the change was visible: more doves, more quail, a mule deer track in the mud that hadn't been there two years ago, printed delicate as lacework.

He crouched and put his palm flat on the water's surface, feeling the cool come up through his skin from two hundred feet down, from the dark vaults where the river ran that no one had known about — the river under everything, older than the leases and the lies and the warrants, patient as geology, keeping its own time.

"Still gives me the shivers," Teague said behind him. "All that water down there. Whole ocean, and the sky doesn't even know."

"The sky knows," Yarrow said. He stood, wiping his hand on his thigh. "That's what rain is. Just the sky paying it back."

They were mounting up when he saw it — or felt it first, the way you feel a change in pressure before you hear the wind: a dust plume far to the north, moving fast, cutting the angle toward the station. One rider. Coming hard.

Yarrow shaded his eyes. The rider carried no visible escort, no pack animals, and rode with the forward-hunched urgency of a man carrying news rather than fleeing trouble. Twenty minutes brought him close enough to resolve into a stranger — young, dusty, a commission courier's brass badge pinned crooked to his vest — and he hauled his horse to a lathered stop in front of them and fumbled a dispatch case open with shaking hands.

"Ranger Yarrow?" the boy said. "Marguerite Yarrow?"

"That's me." Saying it still gave him a small electric jolt, even now. Probably always would. He'd decided that was all right.

The boy handed over a telegram, sealed, urgent-franked. "From Miss Calloway. Commission office. She said — she said to tell you it's about the Sere Basin. About—" he swallowed, checking a memorized line, "—about water where the maps say there isn't any. And she said you'd understand. And she said to tell you—" the boy hesitated, apologetic, "—she said, 'Bring the thief.'"

Yarrow broke the seal and read it twice, the desert wind fluttering the paper, the hidden river humming its one long note beneath his boots. Beside him, Teague leaned over to look, and whistled low, and reached for his canteen like a man settling in for a long ride.

"Well," Teague said, grinning. "Stopping again?"

Yarrow folded the telegram into his coat, over his heart, next to nothing — because he carried nothing hidden there anymore — and swung up into the saddle, and turned his horse north.

"Only long enough to saddle up," he said. "Let's ride."
