## 1. Debt Weather

The storm came in off the flats like something owed.

Petra Vasquez had learned to read weather the way other people read ledgers — every cloud a column of figures, every wind shift an entry in a book that never balanced. This one was a fat gray line on the southern horizon, dragging its skirt of rain across the salt crust, and it would pass over the old shipping channel in forty minutes if she pushed the *Cormorant* hard enough to make her hull complain.

So she pushed her.

The airboat's propeller howled and the flat white world blurred beneath her, salt crystals kicking up in the slipstream like thrown rice. Her hands knew the work without her: throttle at three-quarters, rudder trimmed against the crosswind, eyes flicking between the sky and the scar of deeper water ahead where the channel cut its dark vein through the crust. Three years of this had worn grooves into her the way water wears stone. She didn't think anymore when she chased weather. She simply *was* the chase, and everything else — the debt, the name, the brother-shaped hole with no body in it — fell away behind her like spray.

That was the trick of it. That was the whole trick. Keep moving and you don't have to be anyone.

The storm hit at 4:17 in the afternoon, and it hit like a fistful of gravel. Rain came sideways, stinging, turning the world to static. Petra killed the prop and let the *Cormorant* drift while the squall wall walked past her, and in the sudden hush under the storm's belly she could hear it — the sound she'd come two hundred miles to hear. Metal. Groaning metal, down in the drowned channel, shifting as the pressure changed.

Old wrecks sang before they gave up their dead. She'd learned that from Tomas.

"Stop," she said aloud, to herself, to the memory, to nobody. Her voice was swallowed by rain.

She dove twice. The first dive was reconnaissance: a freight barge, sunk maybe fifteen years, sitting canted in thirty feet of green-brown murk with its cargo hold split open like a ripe fruit. The second dive was the money. She worked by feel mostly, visibility being what it was, her gloved hands walking along rusted ribs while her regulator rasped and her pulse counted seconds she couldn't afford to waste. Copper ingots, six of them, wedged in the silt of the hold where the looters hadn't thought to look because looters never look down. Salvage law on the flats was simple and brutal: finders keepers, if the finder survives the finding.

She surfaced with the last ingot clutched to her chest and her lungs burning, and the storm had moved off eastward, trailing veils of rain, and the light over the salt was the color of old pewter. Six ingots. Maybe four hundred dollars' worth after the smelter took his cut. Enough for a payment. Not enough for the *principal*. There was never enough for the principal; she suspected the principal was theological, like the number of angels, unknowable by design.

She laughed at that, alone on the deck, wringing salt water out of her braid. It came out sounding like coughing.

* * *

The fuel dock at Perdido Bend sat at the edge of the flats like a tooth left standing in a ruined jaw — one pump, one shack, one proprietor who charged triple and asked nothing, which was why Petra liked him. Night had come down soft and warm, moths orbiting the dock lights in dumb devotion. She fueled, she bought coffee that tasted like hot cardboard, she counted her cash twice at the little table outside where the boards were silvered smooth by weather.

Four hundred and ten dollars. Payment-sized. Survival-sized. Never both.

"You look tired, Ms. Vasquez."

Petra's hand went still over the bills. She knew the voice before she turned around, the way you know a song that played at a funeral.

Merritt stood at the head of the dock with his hands in his pockets and his smile already deployed, wide and warm as a heat lamp. He was a big man gone soft at the edges, dressed in clothes too clean for the flats, and behind him at the rail of the fuel shack leaned two others from the crew — she never learned their real names, only their functions: the one who watched, and the one who wrote things down. The watcher was already watching. The writer had a ledger open against his knee, pen moving with the calm of a man doing arithmetic he'd done a thousand times.

"Merritt." She put the money away slowly, deliberately, letting him see her do it. "You're a long way from anywhere."

"So are you." He came closer, boots thumping the dock boards, and settled himself across the table from her uninvited, the way he always did, occupying space like it had been reserved for him. "We've been looking everywhere for you, you know. Coahuila. Bitterwell. That sad little marina down at Horseshoe. You move around a lot."

"I like the weather."

"The weather's been good to us too." His smile widened a fraction. "Every place you run, we arrive a few days later and collect interest on the delay. It's remarkable, really. You're the most productive asset we have. All that running about — it compounds beautifully."

Petra drank her terrible coffee and made her face do nothing. Exhaustion lived in her body like ballast, low and permanent, and she'd gotten good at disguising it as competence: steady hands, level voice, eyes that gave back exactly as much as they received. Tired people got offers. Competent people got terms.

"What's today's number?" she said.

Merritt spread his hands, delighted, a showman presenting the evening's entertainment. The writer cleared his throat and read from the ledger in a voice like dry paper.

"Outstanding principal, eleven thousand, two hundred and forty. Accrued interest since the Horseshoe payment, six hundred and twelve. Late assessment for the missed window, four hundred. Travel costs incurred by our collection efforts—" Merritt waved modestly at himself, at the dock, at the whole absurd distance between here and wherever the *Optimist's Wake* was moored, "—eight hundred and fifty, amortized. Your new balance is thirteen thousand, one hundred and two dollars and some cents."

"Cents," Petra repeated.

"We're precise people." Merritt leaned forward, elbows on the table, and for a moment the warmth in his face thinned and she could see the machinery underneath, the patient gears of an operation that had never once needed to raise its voice. "Here's the thing, Petra. We like you. Genuinely. You're honest in your way — you always pay. Most of our clients, we have to *find* things. You just need finding. It's almost restful." He tapped the table once. "But eleven months at this rate? You'll never clear it. You know that. I know that. The arithmetic knows that. So we've adjusted your terms. Interest goes to eight percent monthly, effective tonight."

"Eight percent." She kept her voice flat. Inside, something small and exhausted lay down and refused to get up, and she stepped over it. "You raised it in March."

"And business has been good since March. Costs rise. The Wake is a generous ship but she isn't a charity." He stood, buttoning his jacket, and the smile came back up like a shopfront shutter. "You want my advice? Free of charge, because I like you. Stop chasing storms. Come work the deck for us. Steady wage, applied directly against principal. You'd be free in — oh, four years, five. Instead of never."

Working the deck of the Optimist's Wake. Being owned in daylight instead of hunted in the dark. She looked at him and felt the trap's shape so clearly she could almost hear it click.

"I'll keep chasing storms," she said.

"Of course you will." He beamed, as if she'd agreed to something. "You're so *good* at it."

They went down the dock together, the three of them, unhurried, and the writer paused beside her long enough to stamp something in the ledger — a date, a figure, a small red mark like a wax seal — and then they were gone into the dark toward whatever boat had brought them, and the moths resumed their orbiting, and Petra sat very still with her cold coffee until her hands stopped wanting to shake.

* * *

Later, in the bunk of the *Cormorant*, she did the arithmetic she swore every month she'd stop doing. Eight percent of thirteen thousand was a thousand dollars a month. A good month of salvage — a great month, diving wrecks in weather that killed divers — might gross nine hundred.

The numbers weren't a wall. They were a tide. They came in a little further every time, and they never went out, and somewhere ahead of her was the day they'd reach the high-water mark of everything she owned, everything she earned, everything she was, and simply stay there.

She lay listening to the flats tick and settle around her, salt contracting in the cooling dark, and she thought — not for the first time, but with new weight — that stopping cost more than moving. Stopping meant the Wake caught up. Moving meant the Wake caught up *slower*. That was the entire difference between her options, measured in days.

Her radio crackled. Static, mostly — the flats full of ghosts at night, dead channels whispering to each other. She reached to switch it off and then a voice came through, broken, wet-sounding, half-drowned in interference:

"...any station... Meridian expedition... we're in the canyon, taking wate— ...only pilot... Vasquez. Tell Vasquez she—"

Static. Then, faintly, impossibly, a second voice beneath the first, or the same voice stretched wrong, saying something that might have been her name again, or might have been another name entirely — a name she hadn't heard spoken aloud in three years.

Petra sat up in the dark, radio in both hands, heart going like a bilge pump.

The channel hissed. The voice did not come back.
