## 2. The Silence Upstream

The radio woke her the way bad news always did — not with a bang but with a voice.

Petra had been sleeping in the Vireo's cabin, one hand curled around a wrench out of three years' habit, when the set on the bulkhead crackled to life at 3:14 in the morning. Static first, the long hiss of a dead channel, and then — threaded through it like a hair caught in amber — a human voice.

*"...any station, any station, this is Meridian Star expedition, we are—"*

She was upright before she knew she'd moved, bare feet on cold deck plating, one hand slapping the gain knob up until the speaker buzzed like a hornet in a jar.

*"—flooded canyon section, hull breach amidships, we are taking water faster than—"* A burst of interference swallowed whole seconds. When the voice came back it was thinner, stretched, a man shouting into wind from very far away. *"—only pilot who knows this route is Vasquez. Petra Vasquez. If anyone hears this, tell her the markers are still good. Tell her I kept them painted. Tell her—"*

Static. Long static. The kind that goes on so long you understand it isn't interference anymore. It's absence.

Petra stood in the dark with her hand still on the radio, listening to the empty channel breathe, and felt the river rise in her chest without any water anywhere near her.

*Tell her I kept them painted.*

Elias. It had to be Elias. Three years since he'd gone upriver with the Meridian Star and its cargo of optimists and instruments, three years since the last postcard with a smudge of orange paint in the corner where he'd tested a marker color against the paper — *still good, sis, brightest damn ochre you ever saw* — and now his voice had come out of the night sky to name her like a summons.

Or someone who sounded like him. Or someone who wanted her to think so.

That was the thought that finally made her sit down on the bench seat, cold seeping through her clothes. Because there were people in this world who knew exactly what her brother's voice was worth to her, and every one of them would spend it gladly if it got her somewhere they wanted her to go.

She keyed the transmit button anyway. "Meridian Star, this is Vasquez. Say again your position. Meridian Star, come in."

Nothing. The channel hissed like rain on hot metal. She tried for an hour, working down through the bands, calling into the dark the way you'd call into a well. Somewhere past four she stopped, because stopping was its own kind of answer.

Dawn came up gray over the fuel dock at Halfway Light, and Petra did what she always did with unbearable things: she worked. She greased the Vireo's shaft bearing. She re-spliced a chafed halyard. She scrubbed the salt haze off the windows until they were clear enough to see the flats shimmering beyond the breakwater, heat already standing up in columns off the mud. Her hands knew the work. Her hands didn't have to think about brothers or radios or the specific weight of a sentence cut off mid-word.

It was almost noon when the woman found her.

*

Petra saw her coming down the dock before she heard her — which was unusual, because most people announced themselves on those old boards like drummers. This one walked quiet, deliberate, a tall figure in a dust-colored coat with a canvas satchel riding her hip and dark hair pinned back so severely it looked like discipline rather than style. She stopped at the edge of Petra's work light and stood there, not hovering, just present, the way weather stands offshore before it commits.

"You're Vasquez," the woman said. Not a question.

"Depends who's asking." Petra didn't look up from the bearing housing. "And whether they're carrying money or trouble."

"Both, probably." The woman crouched, unhurried, bringing herself into Petra's eyeline. Up close she was younger than the coat suggested — late twenties, maybe — with a sunburn peeling across the bridge of her nose and eyes that had the flat shine of someone who hadn't slept in days. "My name is Noor Haddad. My father is Sami Haddad. He's the expedition lead on the Meridian Star."

Petra's wrench went still in her hand.

"The Meridian Star went silent eleven days ago," Noor said. "Last confirmed position was above the second narrows. The river authority says they can't mount a search until the survey season opens. The insurance company says the same thing in nicer words." She reached into the satchel and pulled out a battered field recorder, set it on the deck between them. "Then last night somebody picked up this."

She pressed play.

The voice came out thin and full of static, and hearing it a second time in daylight didn't make it smaller. *"—only pilot who knows this route is Vasquez. Petra Vasquez."* Petra watched Noor watch her face while it played, cataloguing whatever crossed it, and made herself keep her expression as blank as a chart of open water.

"Tell her the markers are still good," the recording said, and died.

Noor stopped it. The dock creaked under their combined silence.

"So you know my name," Petra said finally. "Half the coast knows my name. It's on a debt ledger somewhere with a smiley face next to it."

"I know more than your name." Noor's voice stayed level, but something underneath it vibrated like a taut line. "I spent nine days in the archives at Cordero Station going through the Meridian's planning files. Route surveys, hydrographic charts, tide models. Do you know what I found?"

"A lot of paper."

"One map." Noor drew out a folded sheet, opened it on the deck plating, weighted the corners with two bolts from Petra's own toolbox. It was a chart of the flooded canyon country above the second narrows — hand-drawn, hand-annotated, depths penciled in margins that changed with the tide tables printed along the bottom edge. And in the lower right corner, in a looping, impatient hand Petra would have known anywhere, even underwater, even burned: *E. Vasquez, surveyor.*

The dock tilted. Or Petra did. She put her palm flat on the planking and waited for the world to settle.

"My brother drew that," she said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "Three years ago. He signed a contract with the Meridian expedition to run their hydrography. Then the expedition went quiet and he went quiet with it, and everyone told me to stop asking questions because asking questions upriver gets you drowned."

"And you stopped?"

"I got very busy." Petra folded the chart along its original creases, precise as surgery, and handed it back. "Whatever you're selling, Ms. Haddad, I'm not buying. I chase storms and salvage scrap. I don't do rescue missions. Rescue missions are how people end up owing money to cheerful men in clean boots."

"I'm not selling." Noor took the chart back, but she didn't put it away. She held it, folded, against her chest like something with a heartbeat. "I'm hiring. One job. You pilot me upriver through the canyon before the salvage fleet reaches the wreck. Whatever the Meridian was carrying — equipment, cargo, data, anything recoverable — we split it. Fifty-fifty. Enough to pay off whatever ledger has your name on it twice over."

Petra laughed, short and ugly. "You know about the ledger too. You've done your homework."

"I know the Optimist's Wake holds paper on half the pilots on this coast. I know they always find people." Noor's eyes didn't waver. "And I know the Corvid Fleet left harbor three days ago with six boats and a charter to 'recover assets' from the Meridian site. Assets meaning everything. Including, if my father is alive, nothing at all. Salvage law says a wreck abandoned eleven days belongs to whoever raises it. That includes people."

"There it is." Petra stood, wiping her hands on a rag that only spread the grease around. "You want a race. You want me to outrun six professional boats through a canyon that drowns twice a day, on a route I haven't run since my brother disappeared on it, for a share of a wreck that's probably holding corpses. And your pitch is money."

"My pitch is that you listened to that recording forty times last night. I could hear the replay clicks on the counter when I played it back." Noor rose too, unhurried, and shouldered her satchel. "You counted. I could tell. Somebody who doesn't care doesn't count."

The afternoon heat stood in columns off the flats. Somewhere behind the breakwater a gull argued with the silence. Petra looked at the woman — this stranger with her father's stubborn jaw and her nine days of archives and her terrible, perfect arithmetic — and understood that she had already lost the argument she was having with herself. Had lost it at 3:14 in the morning, probably. Maybe three years ago.

"The canyon route on that chart," Petra said slowly. "There are maybe four people alive who can read it. Two of them are dead. One of them is me." She paused. "And the fourth drew it."

Something flickered across Noor's face — hope, quick and painful, like sun off water. "You think he's alive."

"I think a man who keeps painting markers for three years is a man planning to be followed." Petra turned away, toward the Vireo, toward the long list of things that needed doing before any boat went upriver. "Tide windows start closing in two days. If we're going, we load tonight."

"That's a yes?"

"That's a *maybe* shaped like a yes." She glanced back. "One condition. Whatever we find on that wreck — logs, records, bodies, ghosts — I see it first. Before the fleet, before the authorities, before you. That's not negotiable."

Noor studied her for a long moment, and whatever she saw made her nod once, slow. "First look. Agreed."

She walked back up the dock the way she'd come, quiet as weather, and Petra stood in the work light watching her go, the folded chart-shaped absence still burning in her hands.

*Tell her I kept them painted.*

She went below, keyed the radio, and called the channel one more time — habit, hope, superstition, all three wearing the same coat.

Static answered.

But far upriver, past the second narrows, past the drowned gates of the canyon, she could feel it waiting: ochre-bright markers on black stone, a brother's handwriting on a chart, and six tides' worth of time to find out which of them was still true.
