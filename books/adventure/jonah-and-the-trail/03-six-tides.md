## 3. Six Tides

Petra

The tide tables at the fuel dock were printed on paper gone soft as cloth, and Petra read them the way other people read scripture — with her thumb, with her lips moving, with a small cold prayer she didn't believe in.

Six windows. That was what the canyon gave them. Six openings in the next nine days when the water would drop low enough to let a boat through the flooded gorge without peeling its hull open on the drowned town's rooftops, and high enough afterward to float it out the far side before the walls closed like a throat swallowing.

She counted them twice. Counted again a third time because the number refused to sit still in her head.

Six windows for one boat.

Four, if you were a fleet.

That was the arithmetic that had woken her at three in the morning, staring at the underside of the Optimist's Wake's berth ceiling while the harbor slapped against the pilings like something impatient to be let in. A fleet didn't need six chances. A fleet could afford to lose two tides to bad luck, to a fouled prop, to a hull scraped and patched and sent through anyway. The Meridian Star's expedition had needed every window they had, and someone upstream had made sure they got none.

Now Noor Haddad stood on the dock beside the *Saltglass*, watching Petra load her with the particular patience of a woman who had decided not to ask questions until the answers were unavoidable.

"You're counting," Noor said.

"I'm always counting." Petra heaved a drum of diesel across the gunwale and felt the old ache light up along her spine like a fuse. "Fuel first. Water second. Everything else is decoration."

Noor picked up a coil of line without being asked and flaked it down properly, which surprised Petra more than she wanted to admit. Most money came aboard boats like weather — loud, wet, and useless below decks. This one coiled.

"How long have you been up?" Noor asked.

"Define up."

"Fair."

The harbor was waking around them in its usual order: gulls first, then the fish buyers, then the smell of diesel and brine and old rope cooking in the early heat. And then, third or fourth in the sequence, the way it always came — the sound of boots on the harbor master's stairs that were too clean for a working pier.

Petra didn't turn around. She'd heard those boots before, in a dozen harbors, wearing a dozen uniforms. Clean boots meant a man who hadn't walked his own deck in years. Clean boots meant bought.

"Morning, Vasquez." Harbor Master Ellery stood at the top of the ramp with his clipboard held against his chest like a shield he expected to need. He was a soft man going softer, jowls and sweat rings, and he couldn't look at her left eye. Everyone who lied to her couldn't look at her left eye. "Got some paperwork for you."

"Paperwork comes after fuel."

"This paperwork comes now." He descended the ramp one careful step at a time. "Your transit permit for the upper river. There's been a — revision."

Petra set down the drum. Slowly. The way you set things down when your hands want to do something else. "A revision."

"Channel closure. Canyon section. Hazardous conditions." He said the words like a man reciting lines he'd been given, not ones he understood. "Salvage traffic prohibited pending survey."

"Whose survey?"

"Pending survey," Ellery repeated, with the serene stubbornness of a door.

Behind him, at the end of the pier, a boat sat at the fuel berth that hadn't been there yesterday. White hull, navy trim, three men in matching coveralls hosing salt off her rails. On her transom, in gold leaf elegant enough to be an insult: *Cormorant*. And behind the Cormorant, rafted two deep at the commercial dock, four more hulls of the same bloodline, all flying the same pennant — a white bird on a black field, wings folded.

Not spread. Folded. Like something patient.

"That's a lot of boats for a closed channel," Noor said quietly.

Ellery's gaze skittered off her like water off wax. "Miss Haddad. I'd hoped you'd reconsidered."

"You'd hoped my father's money had run out of daughters." Noor stepped forward, and Petra watched the harbor master take an involuntary half-step back, which told her everything about how this conversation usually went. "You took fleet money, Mr. Ellery. I can see it in your boots."

The color went out of his face in stages, like a tide going out over mudflats.

"Permit's denied," he said, and fled up his stairs.

* * *

Noor

Noor had grown up watching her father refuse things. Refuse investors, refuse shortcuts, refuse the salvage fleets' offers year after year, standing in doorways with his big careful hands folded, saying *no thank you* in four languages. She had thought refusal was a kind of weakness, back then — the family trait she'd inherited and hated. Now, watching Petra Vasquez stand in the denied shadow of a closed channel and simply begin loading her boat anyway, Noor understood that refusal was a muscle, and some people were built like rigging.

"We can't go," she said, mostly to hear herself say it, mostly to watch what Petra did with the sentence.

Petra kept loading. "We can't get a permit," she corrected. "Different animal. Permits are paper. Paper floats worse than we will."

"They'll stop us at the mouth of the canyon."

"They'll try." Petra cinched a strap with her teeth and her hands at once, an economy of motion that looked rehearsed because it was. "Here's what I know about the Cormorant fleet, since you're new to the water. Voss runs five hulls and buys every official between here and the headwaters, cheap, because cheap officials are loyal officials — nobody else wants them. He doesn't race. Racing costs hulls. He closes doors. Then he walks through the one that's left open, which is his."

"And ours?"

"Our door has a tide schedule." Petra finally turned and looked at her, and Noor made herself hold the look, both eyes, even the ruined one with its pale scar through the brow like a lightning fossil. "Six windows in nine days. His fleet needs four minimum to run five boats through safe. Which means he'll burn two windows just getting positioned, and he knows it, and it's making him stupid, and stupid men with money are the most dangerous weather on this river."

"So we're faster."

"We're lighter. Faster is a promise. Lighter is a fact." Petra patted the Saltglass's rail the way you'd pat a horse. "He's got tonnage, permits, and the harbor master. I've got a boat that draws less than his dinghy and a route in my head that isn't written down anywhere he can buy."

"The markers," Noor said. "Your brother's markers."

Something moved behind Petra's face then — quick, submerged, gone — like a fish turning under ice. "Somebody's been talking."

"You said it yourself. At the fuel dock. You said only one person ever mapped the gorge, and you learned it from him." Noor kept her voice level, though her heart was doing something arrhythmic and embarrassing. "I've spent three weeks reading every log, letter, and bar tab my father left behind. Your brother's name is in four of them. Jonah Vasquez, pilot and surveyor. My father trusted him with the route. Trusted him with—" She stopped herself. Some sentences were too heavy to lift alone.

Petra went very still, the way water goes still right before it goes over the edge.

"With what?" she asked.

"With the reason he was really going upriver," Noor said. "Which I think you already know. And which I think is why you said yes to me when you've said no to everyone else for three years."

The gulls screamed about something. Diesel smoke drifted. Between them lay the loaded boat and the closed channel and the whole bright hostile morning, and neither woman picked the sentence back up, because both of them knew it would still be there tonight, heavier, waiting.

"Six tides," Petra said instead. Her voice had changed — lower, flatter, the voice of someone stepping onto ice and testing it with their weight. "First window opens tomorrow, 0440, lasts ninety minutes if the wind stays down. We go dark, no lights, no radio except a scheduled check-in I'll fake if anyone's listening. We eat cold food. We don't stop."

"And if the fleet's already at the mouth?"

Petra looked past her, down the length of the harbor, to where the white bird pennants hung limp in the dead air. Folded wings. Waiting wings.

"Then we learn something important about how much time we actually have," she said, "and we spend it better than they expect."

* * *

Petra

Night came down on the harbor like a lid, and Petra sat in the Saltglass's cockpit with the tide tables under a red lamp, doing the arithmetic again because the arithmetic was the only thing in her life that obeyed her.

Window one: tomorrow, 0440. Window two: day after, 0530, shorter. Windows three through six strung out across the week like beads on a fraying string, each one thinner than the last as the spring tides died toward neaps. After window six, the canyon closed for eleven days. Eleven days was long enough for Voss to buy whatever remained unbought, long enough for the wreck to be stripped, long enough for anything.

She thought about the Wake crew's smiling faces at the fuel dock last month, the cheerful way they'd raised her interest, the patient menace of people who never had to hurry because they owned the clock. She'd spent three years learning that lesson: stopping costs more than moving. Every harbor she rested in, they found her. Every payment she made, they recalculated. Debt wasn't a hole, it was a current, and she'd been swimming against it so long she'd forgotten there was any other direction.

But sitting here, with the tide tables soft under her thumb and a sleeping woman wrapped in a tarp on her foredeck, Petra felt something she hadn't felt since Jonah's last postcard: the current changing.

Voss had offered to buy her debt. She hadn't told Noor yet. Hadn't told anyone. But the offer sat in her chest like swallowed ballast, and she understood now what she'd refused to understand at the fuel dock — that the Wake and the Cormorant fleet spoke the same language, wore different flags, and that somewhere behind both of them stood the same quiet room where men decided which rivers stayed open and which expeditions came home.

Jonah had mapped that gorge. Jonah had signed on with Haddad's expedition. Jonah had vanished into the same water she was about to enter, and the fleet had closed the channel behind him, and no one had ever asked why out loud.

Until now. Until a daughter with her father's stubborn hands and a debtor with nothing left to lose had decided to ask it with a boat.

Petra rolled the tide tables, stowed them in the chart tube lashed under the cockpit coaming, and killed the lamp. In the dark, the harbor breathed around her — halyards ticking, water sucking at the hull, the distant clank of the fuel dock. And beneath it all, faint but unmistakable, the sound of engines. Out past the breakwater. More than one. Running without lights, slow, holding position.

The fleet wasn't waiting for morning either.

Petra smiled in the darkness, and it wasn't a nice smile, and it was the first honest thing she'd worn in three years.

"Window opens at 0440," she murmured to the sleeping boat, to the sleeping river, to the brother who might be dead or worse. "Let's see who's lighter."

She started the engine low, slipped the mooring, and took the Saltglass out through the black harbor on memory alone — past the harbor master's dark window, past the folded-wing pennants, toward the mouth of the canyon that drowned twice a day and kept its dead.

Behind her, at the breakwater gap, a white hull began to move.
