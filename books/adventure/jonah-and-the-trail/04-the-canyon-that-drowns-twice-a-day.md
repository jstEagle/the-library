## 4. The Canyon That Drowns Twice a Day

The river mouth opened like a held breath.

Petra ran the Saltglass dark — no running lights, no cabin glow, just the low mutter of the engine and the green smear of the compass card. Behind them, a mile back and closing by inches, the white hull of Voss's lead boat stitched its own wake across the black water, patient as a debt. She could feel it there without looking, the way you feel a hand on the back of your neck.

"Tell me what I'm looking at," Noor said. She stood at the bow rail with her hands wrapped around it, her hair whipping loose from its braid, and she wasn't afraid. Petra kept checking for fear and kept not finding it, and it unsettled her more than the rocks did.

"You're looking at a door that opens twice a day," Petra said. "And closes on whoever's inside."

Ahead, the canyon walls rose out of the estuary like the ribs of something drowned and enormous. The tide was still three hours from full, which meant the gorge was half-drowned — black stone shoulders showing above the flood, and between them, channels of moving water that looked calm and were not. The surface lay smooth as poured glass over current that would take a swimmer down and fold her into the walls like laundry.

"The Meridian expedition went in here on a falling tide," Petra said. "Against my brother's advice."

"How do you know his advice?"

"Because he gave it to everyone. Free of charge." Her mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "It's why nobody listened to him."

* * *

The first marker appeared at 0447.

Petra saw it because she'd known where to look — a hand-span of faded blue paint on a rock face that every other eye in the world would have read as bare stone. A chevron, pointing left and slightly down. Below it, in her brother's cramped, stubborn handwriting, painted small enough to fit on a dinner plate: *SLACK +20. DO NOT ARGUE.*

Three years of rain and salt had thinned it to a ghost, but ghosts were her trade. She eased the throttle forward and took the channel the chevron promised, and the Saltglass slid between two black shoulders so close that the paint on her hull kissed the stone with a sound like a long exhale.

Noor didn't flinch. She leaned out instead, one arm hooked through the rail, and read the words upside down.

"He wrote instructions," she said. "On the canyon walls."

"He wrote them everywhere. He used to say paper drowns and stone doesn't." Petra fed the wheel a quarter-turn as the channel doglegged. "He painted markers up this whole river when he was nineteen. The fleet uses charts. We use him."

There were eleven chevrons between the mouth and the deep water, her brother had told her once, drunk on cheap wine and triumph, counting them off on his fingers like a rosary. Eleven prayers against drowning. Petra had laughed at him then. She was not laughing now. Every one of those blue marks was a life he'd spent on this river before he ever set foot on the Meridian Star, and she was spending them again, three years late, hoping they hadn't expired.

* * *

The fifth chevron nearly killed them.

It sat on a wall that only existed at low water — a fang of basalt that the flood swallowed whole, so that at any other point in the tide cycle the safe channel ran straight over the top of it and onto the shelf beyond. Her brother had painted the mark high, knowing that anyone reading it correctly would have to trust the arithmetic: slack water plus twenty minutes, no more, or the fang came awake beneath you and ate your keel.

They hit the mark at slack plus fourteen.

Six minutes early. Six minutes is nothing, on land. On this river, six minutes was the difference between glass and teeth.

Petra felt it before she saw it — a change in the boat's voice, the hull beginning to hum a note lower than the engine, the water under the transom going from silk to muscle. The current ahead was turning. It was supposed to be done turning. It was turning anyway, because rivers do not read tide tables, they write their own.

"Kill the engine," she said.

Noor's head came around. "We're in a narrows—"

"I know what we're in. Kill it."

To her credit — and Petra would think about this later, in the way you think about a stranger who turns out to be made of the same material as you — Noor reached down and cut the fuel line without another word. The Saltglass went silent. Silent, in a flooded canyon, drifting toward a submerged fang of rock with a current waking underneath it, is its own special kind of prayer.

"Listen," Petra said.

Under them, faint as a heartbeat through a wall, the river was singing. A low note, rising. The fang was breathing. When the note crested, the boil would show on the surface — a smooth black blister, the only warning the rock ever gave.

"Where do I look?" Noor asked. Not *what do I do*. Where do I look. As if she already understood that in this place, seeing was the whole job.

"Off the port bow. Small blister, don't blink."

The note climbed. Petra counted under her breath, an old habit, her brother's habit, borrowed without permission. At nine the blister broke the surface — a circle of disturbed water no wider than a kitchen table, opening and closing like something feeding.

"There." Petra pointed. "That's our rock. Now watch what the water does around it."

Around the blister, the current split — two smooth tongues peeling left and right, leaving a seam of dead water directly behind the fang, a pocket maybe two boat-lengths long where the river forgot itself.

"We drift into the seam," Petra said. "Engine off until it passes. Then we go through behind it, in its wake, while it's still confused."

"That's insane."

"Yes. Start us up on my word."

They drifted. The Saltglass moved like a leaf, slow and obedient, and the seam of dead water opened its arms and took them, and for ninety seconds Petra Vasquez sat in a silence so complete she could hear her own blood, holding a wheel that did nothing, trusting a river she did not believe in, because a dead-or-missing man had painted arithmetic on a wall six years ago and she had nothing else.

The boil passed them on the starboard side close enough to touch. Then the note began to fall, the current spent its tantrum, and Petra said, "Now," and Noor started the engine on the first try, and they slid through the gap behind the fang into deep green water that had decided, for the moment, to be kind.

Noor let out a breath that sounded like it had been held since the harbor.

"What was that?" she asked. "What did you listen for?"

"My brother called it the rock singing. He said every drowned thing in this canyon has a note, and if you're quiet, they'll tell you where they are." Petra kept her eyes on the channel. "I thought it was nonsense. I was twenty-two and knew everything."

"And now?"

"Now I know slightly less, and it keeps me alive."

* * *

The seventh chevron was different.

Petra saw it from forty meters and her hands went cold on the wheel, because this one wasn't blue. It was red — a color her brother never used, red being, in his private theology, the color of things that had gone wrong and stayed wrong. Beneath the chevron, the plate-sized lettering had been refreshed. Newer paint. Not six years old. Not even six months.

*CHANNEL MOVED. TRUST THE WALL, NOT THE CHART.*

Someone had been here recently. Someone who wrote in her brother's hand.

She didn't tell Noor. Not yet. Some information is cargo you carry alone until you know whether it floats.

Instead she did what the red mark asked — she abandoned the charted line and hugged the west wall, where the water ran darker and colder over deeper stone, and thirty meters past the old channel the bottom came up fast and shallow, a sandbar that hadn't been on any map, deposited by last spring's flood. Any boat following the official route would have opened its belly on it in the dark.

The fleet was following the official route.

Petra glanced back once, at the far white smudge of Voss's hull hanging outside the canyon mouth, waiting for the next window like a reasonable, chart-reading man. She thought about the sandbar. She thought about saying nothing, forever.

Then she thought about the radio call that had named her, garbled and dying, and about a daughter who had hired a stranger because no one else would come, and she keyed the handheld and sent, on open channel, in the clear:

"Canyon traffic advisory. Charted channel at marker seven is shoaled. Repeat, shoaled. Use the west wall."

Silence. Then, distant, a voice she recognized — smooth, unhurried, amused even through static.

"Appreciated, Captain Vasquez. See you at the top."

Voss. Who now knew she was ahead of him, and knew she'd warned him, and would spend the rest of the run wondering why.

Let him wonder. It cost him speed, wondering.

* * *

They cleared the eleventh chevron at 0631, into water so wide and flat it felt like being born. The canyon walls fell away astern, dripping, indifferent. Ahead the river unrolled toward the drowned terraces of the middle reach, gold-grey in the first real light, mist standing on it in columns.

Noor came aft and sat across from Petra at the wheel, close enough that their knees almost touched, and studied her with those unafraid eyes.

"The red mark," she said.

Petra's stomach dropped. "You saw it."

"I see everything. It's my only skill." Noor tilted her head. "New paint. His handwriting. You didn't tell me."

"No."

"Why?"

"Because I don't know yet whether it means he's alive or whether it means someone wants me to think he is." Petra heard her own voice go flat and careful, the voice she used at fuel docks when creditors smiled. "Both possibilities are worse than the one I've been living with."

Noor was quiet for a moment. The river ran. Somewhere behind them, faint, the white hull entered the canyon mouth and began, cautiously, to hug the west wall.

"When I was twelve," Noor said, "my father left a note in my lunch. Just his handwriting, nothing else, no explanation. I carried that note for four years because I couldn't decide if it meant he loved me or he was mocking me." She looked out at the mist. "Eventually I found out it was both. Handwriting is like that. It's the person, all of them, compressed."

"You think the red mark is both."

"I think whoever painted it wanted you to survive this canyon today. That part's certain — you'd be on that sandbar otherwise." Noor turned back to her, and something passed between them then that Petra would later recognize as the exact moment trust stopped being a loan and became a holding. "The rest we find at the wreck. So let's get there first."

Petra put the wheel over, gentle, toward the middle reach, and the Saltglass ran clean and fast on water the color of hammered tin.

Behind them, deep in the gorge, an engine note changed pitch — climbing, straining, wrong — followed by a sound like a giant tearing canvas, and then a shout, thin with distance, and then Voss's voice on the open channel, no longer amused:

"Mayday, mayday. Fleet vessel Corvus, aground, marker seven. Vasquez—" a pause, and when it came back the charm had burned off it entirely, leaving something older and hungrier, "—you knew."

Petra keyed the mic. She thought about the debt, and the Wake, and the smile at the fuel dock, and about how many tides were left.

"Advisory stands," she said. "Charted channel's shoaled. Should've trusted the wall."

She set the handset down. Ahead, the middle reach waited, and somewhere in it, a camp that someone had sabotaged, and a wreck that someone had made.

Noor watched her, and smiled slowly, like a door opening.

"You warned him," she said. "Why?"

"Because I want him at the wreck," Petra said. "I want him watching when the truth comes up out of the water. Some things you don't get to miss."

The Saltglass ran north, light on her feet, six tides burning down to five.
