## 5. What the Fleet Left Behind

I had imagined the camp a hundred times on the way upriver, and in every imagining it was empty in the ordinary way — tents collapsed by weather, cookfire long cold, the patient archaeology of abandonment. I had not imagined this.

We smelled it before we saw it. Diesel, sweet and wrong, soaking into the mud like something spilled on purpose. Then the bend opened and there it was: the Meridian expedition's forward camp, wedged into the shelf of gravel where the canyon widened just enough to hold a handful of human lives.

The tents still stood. That was the first thing that made my stomach turn. Three of them, guy-lines taut, rain flies cinched down against storms that had come and gone for weeks without anyone inside to feel the wind. My father's blue dome at the center, the one he'd carried on every expedition since I was nine, the one with the patched sleeve where I'd burned it with a camp stove at fourteen. Standing. Waiting. As if the people who slept in them had stepped out to look at the river and simply never come back.

"Petra." My voice came out smaller than I wanted it to. "They didn't leave."

She was already out of the boat, rope coiled over her shoulder, moving through the shallows with the flat economy of someone who had learned not to waste motion on hope or horror. "Stay behind me," she said, which was absurd — she'd known these people for three days and I'd known one of them my whole life — but I stayed anyway, because her back looked like the only solid thing in the world.

* * *

The sabotage announced itself slowly, the way rot does.

First the fuel drums, lined up neat as soldiers along the eastern edge of camp. Every one of them drained. Not spilled — drained, the bungs backed out and the drums tipped carefully into a pit dug for the purpose, so the diesel would seep down through the gravel and be gone by morning. Someone had taken the time to dig. Someone had stood here in the dark with a shovel and made sure.

Then the water line. The expedition had run polyethylene tubing from a spring seep half a mile up the talus slope, a gravity feed that had kept six people alive through two previous field seasons. Petra found the cut forty minutes after we landed, following the line like a thread through the brush. It wasn't torn. It wasn't chewed by animals. It was severed with a blade, cleanly, in three places — three, so that no single repair would fix it — and the ends had been tucked under rocks. Hidden. Whoever did it wanted the failure to look like accident, wanted it discovered only after thirst had done its work.

"Look at this," Petra said, crouching. She held the cut end up to the light. "Fresh blade. See how clean the wall of the tubing is?" She turned it. "This wasn't driftwood. This wasn't a bear."

I crouched beside her and the smell hit me again, fainter here, diesel ghosting off her gloves. "How long?"

She rubbed the cut between her fingers, and I watched her do the arithmetic she did with everything — tides, weather, other people's intentions. "Rain's been through twice since. Edges are soft." A pause. "Five weeks. Maybe six."

Six weeks. Six weeks since someone had knelt on this slope with a knife and quietly begun killing my father's crew.

* * *

It was the radio that broke me.

Not broken — sabotaged, the same careful way. The Meridian's long-range set sat in its case beneath the mess fly where my father always kept it, and when I lifted the lid I understood immediately what I was looking at, because I had grown up around radios the way other children grew up around pets. The antenna lead had been disconnected and the connection point corroded — saltwater, deliberately applied; I could smell the brine residue even now. The spare fuses were gone from their clips. And the battery, the big deep-cycle unit they hauled upriver every season, sat in its place with its caps loosened so the acid would slowly boil away on the first hot day.

My father hadn't gone silent. He'd been silenced. His last transmission — the garbled one, the one that said *Vasquez, tell Vasquez the markers hold, tell her—* before the channel dissolved into static — that transmission had been a scream through a throat someone else had already cut. He'd been shouting into a radio that could barely whisper, praying that enough signal would leak through the sabotage to reach the one pilot who knew the flooded route.

He'd been praying to you, I thought, looking at Petra across the dead radio. He was dying and he spent his last words on your name.

"What is it?" Petra asked, because my face must have done something terrible.

"He tried," I said. "He got a message out anyway. Through all this." I touched the corroded connector, gently, the way you touch a wound. "He knew they'd take the radio. So he used it before they finished."

Petra went very still. Stillness was her native language; I was learning to read it. This particular stillness meant a door had opened somewhere inside her and she was deciding whether to walk through.

"The fleet," she said finally. "Voss's people were here first. Before the silence."

"They weren't racing them." The words came out of me with a force that surprised us both, as if they'd been waiting behind my teeth for weeks. "They were hunting them. They cut the water. They killed the radio. They drained the fuel so no one could run the boats downstream for help." I stood up and turned in a slow circle, seeing the camp now not as a grave but as a crime scene, every detail rearranging itself. "Six people. Six weeks of dry camps and dead radios and no way out. And then the tide came up the canyon and—" I couldn't finish it. The river finished it for me, sliding past the gravel shelf, brown and patient, carrying whatever it carried.

* * *

We worked through the afternoon because grief needs hands or it turns inward and eats.

Petra catalogued. I read. My father's field journal was in the blue tent, wrapped in oilcloth inside his duffel — he'd taught me that trick when I was small, *oilcloth beats water, Noor, water beats everything else* — and the entries stopped mid-sentence eleven days after the cuts in the waterline. The last pages were about the wreck itself, coordinates and depth soundings in his cramped engineer's hand, and then, on the final page, in handwriting that had deteriorated from cramped to shaking:

*Someone is taking our stores at night. Watch rotations doubled. R. thinks it's the salvage fleet working the lower gorge. If we can reach the Star before they do—*

The sentence ended there. Not trailed off. Ended, the pen pressed hard enough to tear the paper, as if he'd heard something outside the tent.

There were other things. A boot with the sole split, dried in the shape of the foot that had worn it. A child's drawing — someone's daughter, mailed upriver months ago, pinned inside the mess tent: a stick-figure boat on a stick-figure river, and underneath, in crayon, COME HOME SOON PAPA. The paper had faded unevenly, darker where tears had fallen and dried and fallen again.

And under the third tent, the one belonging to the expedition's dive master, a woman named Okafor whose voice I recognized from old voicemails — under her cot, wrapped in a shirt, a pry bar bent nearly double and stained with paint the color of Voss's flagship hull. Petra turned it over twice, said nothing, and put it in the evidence bag she'd started keeping, which told me everything about which direction her mind had already gone.

By dusk we had the shape of it whole. The fleet had come upriver ahead of the Meridian crew, found their camp, and gutted it — not with violence, nothing so honest as violence, but with knife-work and patience, turning a functioning expedition into a trap and then walking away to let thirst and isolation and the tide do the killing. They hadn't needed four tides to beat my father to the wreck. They'd needed only to make sure he never reached it at all.

"They'll say it was the river," I said. We were sitting on the gravel shelf as the light died, the camp behind us, the six tents standing their silent watch. "Weather. Bad luck. Nobody's fault."

"The river doesn't cut waterlines in three places," Petra said.

"No. But rivers don't testify either. People do. And the people who could testify are at the bottom of the gorge."

She was quiet a long time. When she spoke, her voice had changed — lower, stripped of the ironic distance she wore like a rain jacket. "You should know something. About why I stopped diving wrecks. Why I chase storms instead, salvage what the weather breaks loose, never go down into anything with people still in it." She looked at the river. "Three years ago I came up from a dive and my brother wasn't on the boat. Same canyon. Same kind of contract. Same kind of charming client with clean hands and a fast hull." Her jaw worked. "I told myself it was the river. For three years I told myself it was the river."

"And now?"

"Now I'm sitting in a camp where the river didn't do any of this." She picked up a pebble and threw it far out into the current. "Your father sent my name up a dead radio because he knew I was the only one who could follow him in. Which means he knew what was happening to him. Which means—" She stopped. Started again. "Which means somebody owes six families an answer, and I'm done letting the river take the blame for men."

The moon came up over the canyon rim and laid a white road across the black water, straight toward the narrows, straight toward the wreck. Somewhere down that road, in a drowned ship, lay the proof we'd need — logs, manifests, maybe bodies wearing the evidence of what had been done to them. And somewhere behind us, coming upriver on the next tide with four windows to our six and a harbor master in his pocket, came Voss and his smiling fleet, expecting to find a salvage job.

They expected wreckage. They expected grief, if they expected anything at all — the passive kind, the kind that signs settlements and goes home.

I stood up and brushed the gravel from my knees and felt something settle in my chest, hard and bright and cold as a bearing. My father had taught me navigation, engineering, how to read water. He had also, without ever meaning to, taught me this: that the difference between mourning and purpose is only whether you decide to steer.

"We're not salvaging anything," I said. "Not first. We're going to get the truth out of that wreck and put it on every channel on this river until it can't be buried twice."

Petra looked up at me, and in the moonlight I saw that whatever she'd decided while walking through that door inside herself, we had arrived at the same place.

"Then we'd better beat the tide," she said. "And the man who smiles."

Far downstream, faint as a heartbeat, I thought I heard engines.
