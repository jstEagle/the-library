## 6. The Man Who Smiles

The trading post at Halfway Bend announced itself before you saw it: a smell of diesel and frying plantains and wet rope, a sound of generators coughing against the river's noise. It sat on a shelf of mud and pilings where the canyon widened just enough to let two boats pass without holding their breath, and it was the only place for forty miles where a person could buy fuel, ice, or lies.

Petra tied off the *Saltglass* with hands that wouldn't quite stop shaking. The fourth tide window had been ugly — a standing wave in the narrows that had come out of nowhere, or out of somewhere she'd forgotten to remember — and Noor had spent twenty minutes afterward quietly being sick over the rail before reappearing with her hair rebraided and her chin set like nothing had happened. Petra had decided not to mention it. There was a particular mercy in letting people keep their dignity on the river.

"Fuel," Noor said now, counting bills into her palm. "Ice if they have it. And I want to ask about the Meridian's last resupply. Someone here sold it to them."

"And someone here will sell that answer to whoever pays more," Petra said. "That's how trading posts work."

"Then I'll pay well."

"You always do." Petra looked at her sideway. Three days upriver had burned the city off Noor Haddad like varnish off a deck left in the sun. Underneath was something harder and stranger than the woman who'd walked into the fuel dock at Corvane with a satellite phone and a checkbook. She still flinched at sudden spray. But she'd learned to read water faster than anyone Petra had taught, which was nobody, because Petra didn't teach.

They went up the plank walk together. The post was built like something that had lost an argument with the river and kept rebuilding anyway — stilt legs wrapped in tar paper, a porch sagging under nets and rusted traps, a bar inside lit by one bulb and a television playing static. Behind the counter, a broad woman named Odile — Petra knew her, distantly, the way you know everyone who works the middle river — was weighing out rice with the expression of someone who had weighed everything twice in her life and found it short both times.

Odile saw Petra and her eyes did something complicated.

"What," Petra said.

"Man came through yesterday," Odile said. "Asked after a pilot named Vasquez. Paid me not to tell him you were coming."

"And?"

"I didn't take his money." Odile slid the rice aside. "I don't like men who smile before they say what they want."

Petra's stomach dropped in a way that had nothing to do with the tides.

* * *

He was waiting on the porch when they came back down, sitting on an overturned crate like he'd grown there, drinking from a bottle of warm beer he'd presumably paid too much for and hadn't minded. He was younger than Petra expected, or performed younger — sandy-haired, sunburned across the nose, wearing a salvage fleet jacket with the sleeves torn off as though sleeves were for people with something to hide. When he stood, he did it slowly, hands visible, a man announcing he was no threat the way other men announce they are.

"Petra Vasquez," he said. "I feel like I already know you. Voss."

"I know who you are."

"Of course you do. Reputation travels." He smiled. It was a good smile. That was the whole problem with it; it had clearly been engineered by professionals. "You've been beating my boats through the canyon for three days. Do you know what my helmsmen say about that? They say there's a ghost running the flooded route. Old markers, old lines, nobody alive who reads them." He tipped the bottle toward her. "But you're not a ghost, are you. You're just the only one left who can read them."

Noor stepped forward. "Whatever you're selling—"

"I'm not selling, Ms. Haddad. I'm buying." He said her name gently, familiarly, a man who had done his homework and wanted credit for it. "Your father's expedition. Terrible thing. I've followed the coverage. You have my condolences, for whatever those are worth this far upstream."

"They're worth nothing. Get to it."

"Straight to it, then." He turned back to Petra, and the smile adjusted itself, recalibrated, became something smaller and more sincere-seeming, which was how you knew you were looking at the real machinery underneath. "Here's my offer. I know about the Optimist's Wake. I know the number — don't look surprised, Ms. Vasquez, numbers are my business, I buy debts the way other men buy boats. Yours has changed hands four times since your brother disappeared. It's currently held by a consortium that would sell its own grandmother's teeth at nine percent interest." He paused, savoring. "I'll clear it. Today. Every cent, every lien, every claim on your hull and your name. You walk away free. In exchange, you turn the *Saltglass* around, leave Ms. Haddad at this charming establishment, and let my fleet run the last two windows unmolested."

The river filled the silence behind him. Somewhere downstream a bird screamed once and quit.

"Noor," Petra said carefully, "go ask Odile about fuel."

"I'm not leaving you alone with—"

"*Go.*"

Noor went, but she went badly, walking backward until the door swallowed her, and Petra understood that she'd just made an enemy of exactly no one and a witness of exactly everyone on the porch, because the two fishermen mending nets at the rail had stopped pretending to work.

Voss watched her go with something like approval. "Loyal. That's rare. That's actually the thing I admire most about you, if we're being honest. Everyone says you're the best pilot on this river, and maybe you are, but plenty of people can read water. Almost nobody keeps faith anymore. It's economically irrational." He took another pull of beer. "Which is why I'm offering you so much for it."

"How much is my debt, exactly?"

"Does it matter? You don't know the number. Nobody knows the number. That's the design. The Wake doesn't want you to pay it off; paying off means losing you. They want you chasing storms forever, salvaging just enough, paying just enough, staying hungry enough to keep moving and scared enough to never stop." He said it lightly, conversationally, like a weather report. "I've read their ledgers, Ms. Vasquez. Part of my due diligence. Fascinating reading. Did you know they financed two of my hulls? Small positions. Quiet ones. We're practically family."

Something cold moved down Petra's spine and settled in her chest, where it intended to stay.

"The Wake and your fleet."

"Are different expressions of the same appetite." He spread his hands, delighted, as if she'd solved a puzzle he'd planted for her. "There's no conspiracy, if that's what you're hoping for. Conspiracies require coordination. This is simpler. Money likes certain shapes, and it flows into them wherever they appear. A debt that owns a pilot. A fleet that owns a river. Same shape. Different water." He finished the beer and set the bottle down on the crate with a soft click that sounded, in the quiet, like a lock turning. "So. The offer stands. Freedom, today, cash-equivalent, no strings except one boat turning around. You'd be a fool to refuse. You'd also be a fool to accept, because you'd spend the rest of your life knowing what you bought it with, and I'm told that's a heavy coin to carry. I genuinely don't know which fool you are. It's why I came in person instead of sending money ahead."

"You talk a lot for a man who hasn't heard an answer."

"I haven't asked the question yet." And then the smile changed again — dropped away entirely, all at once, like a mask lifted rather than slipped, and what was underneath wasn't cruelty exactly. It was worse. It was patience. "Elias Vasquez."

The name landed in Petra's chest like ballast.

"He mapped this route for the Meridian," Voss said, softly, reasonably. "Everyone upstream knows the story — the pilot's brother who drew the drowned canyon and walked into it and never walked out. What the ledgers don't say is that Elias contacted the Wake eleven days before the Meridian went silent. Offered to buy out his sister's debt himself. Had almost enough. Was short by—" he consulted nothing, recited from memory, enjoying it, "—four thousand two hundred credits. The Wake declined to extend him terms. Eleven days later the expedition vanished, and with it, conveniently, the only man ever trying to free you." He tilted his head. "You've spent three years running from that river, Ms. Vasquez. Running fast, running deep, running rich-for-a-day and broke forever. And I'm the first person to tell you the truth: they never gave him the loan. They could have. Four thousand credits. They chose the chase over the payout, because the chase was worth more."

The porch boards creaked under Petra's weight as the world tilted very slightly and righted itself. She thought of every storm she'd chased, every salvage payment handed over with a smile from smiling creditors, every year of motion justified as competence, as necessity, as the only honest life available to a woman with her name on a ledger. She thought of Elias, eleven days from freedom, asking politely, and being refused by patient, cheerful men who had never once raised their voices.

She had hated the Wake for its interest rates. She had never once thought to hate it for its arithmetic.

"You're lying," she said. "Or you're telling the truth to hurt me. Either way, you're using him."

"Obviously," Voss agreed pleasantly. "Everything is usable. That's the whole of economics. The question isn't whether I'm using your brother, Ms. Vasquez. The question is whether the number I'm offering is bigger than what he's worth to you dead in a wreck you're about to dive anyway." He stood, brushed off his shorts, retrieved his bottle. "You have until the fifth tide. My fleet runs the narrows at slack water tomorrow morning. If the *Saltglass* is tied up here, the deal closes and you're free. If it's not—" he shrugged, sunny, magnanimous, "—then I'll assume you've chosen Ms. Haddad's money over your own life, and I'll plan accordingly. I do hope you choose well. I'd hate to lose the only pilot who reads the old markers. It would make the rest of this so much less interesting."

He walked down the plank walk toward his launch, whistling something cheerful and tuneless, and the fishermen watched him go the way you watch a shark cruise a swimming beach — without surprise, without hope, taking note of the direction.

* * *

Noor found her ten minutes later at the rail of the *Saltglass*, staring at the water. The girl sat down beside her without asking, which was new, and said nothing, which was newer.

"He offered to buy my debt," Petra said finally.

"I heard the shape of it through the door."

"He told me things about Elias." Petra's hands were flat on the rail, knuckles white. "Things I can't verify. Things designed to sit in my chest and rot. And the worst part, Noor — the worst part is that none of it changes anything. Even if every word is true, even if the Wake refused him four thousand credits and killed him with arithmetic — the answer is still at the bottom of this river. Not in his ledger line. In his log. In whatever he wrote last." She laughed, and it came out wrong. "Three years I've been running from this exact spot. Voss just charged me admission."

Noor was quiet for a long moment. Downriver, the light was starting to fail, gilding the canyon walls the color of old brass.

"My father used to say," she said at last, "that a man who smiles before he tells you the price is telling you the price is you." She looked at Petra sideways. "Fifth tide is at 4:40 tomorrow. His fleet runs the narrows at slack. So we run it first."

"We'd be cutting it inside the margin. Inside the *error* margin."

"Yes," Noor said. "I know. I've been doing the math since the door." She pulled the tide tables from her jacket, already soft with river damp, and smoothed them flat on the rail between them. "So the only question left is the one he actually asked. Not the debt. Not the money." Her voice was steady, but her hand on the tables wasn't. "Whether you're going to keep running, or whether you're going to dive."

Petra looked at the water, black now between the walls, holding the last of the light like a secret. Somewhere down in that drowned gorge, in a corridor her brother had drawn in grease pencil on a chart she still carried, a logbook waited with the truth in it — and possibly, impossibly, an empty berth where a body should have been.

"Fuel us at first light," she said. "We run the fifth tide before Voss does."

Above them, on the darkening bluff, a single lamp came on in the trading post — and below it, moored in the shadow of the bend, a second running light flickered awake that neither of them had noticed before, low and patient, watching the *Saltglass* the way the Wake had always watched her.

Not leaving. Waiting.
