## 7. Brother Water

Noor had learned to read Petra the way her father taught her to read water — by what moved beneath the surface, not what glittered on top. For six days upriver, the pilot had been a study in stillness: hands steady on the wheel, voice flat as slate, eyes doing all the work of a mouth that refused to open. But tonight, anchored in the lee of a drowned orchard where apple trees stood to their shoulders in black water, Noor watched the stillness crack.

It started with the radio. Petra had it open on her knees, soldering iron in hand, repairing a connection that didn't need repairing. She'd been at it for an hour. The smell of flux hung in the cabin like incense.

"You've fixed that board three times," Noor said from the doorway.

"It keeps breaking."

"Nothing keeps breaking three times unless someone wants it broken."

Petra's iron paused mid-stroke. Outside, the river breathed against the hull — that slow tidal exhalation, water pulling back toward the sea through the canyon they'd threaded at dawn. In four hours the tide would turn again and come hunting for them, and they would ride it or die of it, and between now and then there was only this: two women, one boat, and everything neither of them was saying.

"Sit down, Haddad," Petra said quietly. "I'm going to tell you something, and I'd rather do it before I lose my nerve."

Noor sat. The cabin lamp swung gently on its gimbal, throwing their shadows against the chart rack — against a map she now knew better than her own handwriting, because she'd traced it every night since the canyon. The route through the flooded gorge, marked in faded paint strokes reproduced in careful ink. A dead man's map, she'd assumed. Her father's expedition's last gift.

"I need to tell you about my brother," Petra said.

* * *

His name was Tomás. Everyone called him Teo, because when he was small he couldn't say Tomás, and because he liked being called something new — liked being anyone new. That was the whole of him, really, Noor. He was a man who kept becoming other men."

Petra set down the soldering iron with exaggerated care, as if it might go off.

"He grew up on this river. Our mother ran cargo; our father ran from us. Teo learned the water before he learned letters. By fifteen he could read the canyon like scripture — every ledge under the flood line, every eddy that lied. When the Meridian expedition came through looking for a pilot who knew the drowned route, there was exactly one name on everyone's lips, and it wasn't mine. It was his."

The lamp swung. Shadows tilted across her face like weather.

"He was twenty-three. I was nineteen and already in trouble — already owing people, already running. He told me he'd take the job, make real money, come back and buy me out of whatever hole I'd dug." A sound that wasn't quite a laugh. "He even shook on it. My brother shook on saving me. Three weeks later the expedition went silent, and the river gave back nothing. Not a body. Not a plank. Nothing."

Noor's throat tightened. "You never said. Six days, Petra. You piloted us through markers you—"

"Through markers *he painted.*" The words came out raw. "Every stroke of paint on that canyon wall is his hand. I've been following my brother's handwriting for six days, and I let you believe it was just navigation."

"Why hide it?"

"Because—" Petra stopped. Started again, slower, like someone wading into deep water feeling for the bottom. "Because for three years I've told myself a story. That he's dead, and the river took him, and there's nothing to find. Dead is clean, Noor. Dead means I grieved and it's over. But if the wreck of the Meridian Star is sitting in that flooded town the way your father's charts say it is — then his last logbook might be sitting with it. And a logbook isn't clean. A logbook has a final entry. A final entry has a truth in it, and I have spent three years running from every kind of truth, because truths are the one thing creditors can't repossess but somehow always collect."

The confession filled the cabin like floodwater, rising past things Noor hadn't known were submerged. She thought of the fuel dock story Petra had finally told her — the cheerful crew of the Optimist's Wake, the debt that grew like kelp. She thought of how Petra checked the horizon every eleven minutes, even at anchor. Even asleep. Some part of her always scanning for whoever was coming to collect.

"You think he's alive," Noor said. It wasn't a question. She'd heard it — the way Petra said *nothing*, the way her voice snagged on the word like a hull on a snag.

"I think the river gave back nothing," Petra corrected. "And nothing isn't a body. Nothing is a door left open." She pressed the heel of her hand against her eye. "God, I hate that I still hope. Hope is expensive. Hope is the worst interest rate on the river."

* * *

They came out on deck because the cabin had grown too small to hold it. Night had come down soft and total; the drowned orchard made its black lace against a sky salted with stars. Somewhere upstream, a night bird called three notes and got no answer.

Noor found herself talking before she meant to. "My father stopped writing to me two years before the expedition. Did I tell you that?"

"You mentioned."

"Not why." Noor gripped the rail. The wood was cold and damp and honest. "Because I know what you'll think. You'll think he cut me off the way people cut off debtors. But it was the opposite. He wanted me to come with them — upriver, into the canyon. He said I had the head for it, the patience. And I said no. I said I had a life. A flat in the city. A man I thought I loved." She laughed, and the laugh came out wrong. "So he went without me, and the last thing I ever sent him was a postcard saying *maybe next season.* And next season the river had him."

Petra said nothing, which was its own kind of answer. She leaned on the rail beside Noor, shoulder almost touching, and let the silence be shared instead of owned.

"My mother used to call the river Brother Water," Noor said after a while. "Old country phrase. It gives and it takes and it doesn't apologize, so you'd best love it like family — closely, and with your guard up." She turned to look at Petra's profile, sharp-edged against the stars. "You've been loving it that way your whole life. And it took your brother anyway."

"And yours," Petra said. "And forty tons of Meridian Star besides." She straightened. Something had shifted in her face — Noor saw it happen, the way a current shifts and the whole surface changes character. The exhaustion was still there, but underneath it something harder had surfaced. Ore, not foam.

"So here's what I've decided, standing here being honest with you, which I warn you I find physically painful." Petra's voice had gone dry and level, the pilot's voice, the one that meant the water ahead was bad and she intended to get them through it anyway. "Tomorrow's the fifth tide. Your father's charts put the Star in the old church basin, thirty feet down, inside the flood zone. If we take the fifth window we can reach it. If we take the sixth we can leave with whatever we find. Two windows. One shot each."

"And Voss's fleet?"

"Voss needs four windows to run the narrows with those barges of his. He's spent three. He'll burn the fifth trying to catch us, which means the sixth belongs to whoever's fastest and meanest." A ghost of the smile Noor had learned to distrust and then, somewhere in the spray of the canyon, to trust. "I'm both."

But Noor caught the tremor under it. "And if the log says he's dead?"

Petra was quiet long enough that the bird called again, three notes, no answer.

"Then I stop running," she said at last. "Not because I give up. Because you can't run from a grave you've never visited. I'm going to visit it, Noor. Whatever's written in that book — I'd rather stand in front of the truth than keep paying interest on the not-knowing. That's the whole disease, isn't it? The Wake, the fleet, all of it. They don't own me because of money. They own me because I'll pay anything to avoid finding out how the story ends."

She turned, and in the dark her eyes were bright and terrible and free.

"Well. Tomorrow I find out how it ends."

* * *

Noor didn't sleep. She sat in the cabin with her father's charts spread under the lamp, and she did the thing she'd been avoiding since the trading post: she read them like a daughter instead of a salvager. His annotations in the margins. His little jokes — *beware the polite eddy* — his habit of naming dangerous water after people he disliked. And there, in the corner of the final sheet, a note she'd skimmed a dozen times and never understood:

*T.V. confirmed the deep channel. Best pilot I ever hired. If anything happens, the Vasquez girl gets paid first.*

T.V. Tomás Vasquez. Her father had known. Had trusted him enough to write it into the margins of his life's work, and Noor had spent six days sailing beside the sister without seeing the thread that tied them — two families frayed by the same river, knotted together now in one small boat.

She heard Petra move on deck. Through the porthole she watched her check the anchor line, check the sky, check the dark water — the eleven-minute ritual, the vigil that never ended. Then Petra went still, head cocked, listening.

Noor listened too.

Downriver, faint but unmistakable, came the sound she'd been dreading since the trading post: engines. More than one. Running without lights, pushing hard against the ebb, coming up the canyon like wolves scenting a kill.

Voss had found the fifth tide early.

Noor was already reaching for the engine controls when Petra appeared in the doorway, soldering iron still in her hand like a weapon, and said the sentence Noor would remember for the rest of her life:

"They're not waiting for the tide. Get us moving — we take the narrows *now*, in the dark, on my brother's water."
