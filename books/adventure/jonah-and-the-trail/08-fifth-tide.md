## 8. Fifth Tide

The fifth tide came in mean.

Petra felt it before she saw it — a change in the way the water moved through the narrows, a stutter in the current that had run smooth and green all morning. The canyon walls here pinched close enough that she could have touched both sides with an oar, and the river between them went from glass to boil in the space of a breath. Somewhere below, the sea was pushing its way back in through channels no map had ever bothered to name, and it did not care what was floating on top of it.

"Two minutes," Noor said. She had the tide table open on her knee, one hand flat against the deck to keep her balance as the *Heron* yawed. "Window closes at 14:40. After that we're swimming."

"We're not swimming." Petra fed the throttle forward in increments, feeling for the engine's temper the way you'd feel for a bruise. "We're going to be through this gap and tied up above the flood line before the water even knows we're here."

"You always talk like that when you're scared?"

"I never get scared."

"Liar," Noor said, and there was something almost fond in it, which was worse.

The narrows ahead were a slot of shadow between two walls of wet black stone. Her brother's markers clung to them still — three years of rain and salt and nobody's maintenance, and yet there they were: a yellow chevron at head height, a second one lower, painted on a slab that only showed at half tide. He'd mixed his paint with pine resin so it would hold. She remembered him telling her that, remembered the smell of the resin, remembered being fourteen and bored and wishing he'd stop talking about rocks. She would give anything now to be bored in that kitchen again.

"Chevron high," she said aloud, because saying it made it real. "Slab shows at half. We hug the left wall until the bend, then we're in the green and it opens up."

"And if the fleet is waiting?"

"They won't wait in the narrows." She said it with more certainty than she owned. "Too shallow for their draft. They'll hit us above the bend where they can maneuver."

She was wrong about the first part and right about the second, and both mistakes cost her.

* * *

They came out of the slot into light so sudden it hurt, and the ambush was already unfolding.

Voss's boats were good. That was the thing she'd underestimated — she'd spent three years thinking of the salvage fleet as scavengers, gulls, opportunists, and she'd forgotten that men like Voss bought fast hulls and drilled their crews. Two boats came off the bank where they'd been tucked under cut brush, netting thrown over the rails, and a third held position downstream with its engine idling low, patient as a spider.

The first shot took the wheelhouse window. Glass went everywhere, a bright brief snowfall, and then the sound arrived — flat, hard, echoing off the canyon walls until it seemed to come from every direction at once.

"Down!" Petra hauled Noor by the collar and put them both behind the engine housing, and the *Heron* began to die.

It wasn't dramatic. That was the horror of it. A hole below the waterline somewhere aft made less noise than a dropped pan; the boat just started drinking, quietly, industriously, the way the Wake collected debts. Petra heard the bilge pump kick in and knew within ten seconds it was losing the argument.

"They want the boat stopped, not sunk," Noor shouted over the gunfire. "They want us alive."

"Then they've mispriced me," Petra said, and did the arithmetic she'd been avoiding since the first shot. The narrows behind them were closing with the tide. The fleet blocked the water ahead. The *Heron* was taking on the river by the gallon. There was no line through this that ended with her hands on the wheel.

There was only the bank.

"Grab the go-bag," she said. "Not the salvage gear. Not the charts. The bag."

Noor looked at her — one long second, water already sloshing around their boots — and then she moved without another question, and Petra understood that this was what trust looked like when it was finished being earned: someone doing exactly what you said while the world came apart.

She ran the *Heron* at the bank the way you'd run a horse at a fence. The hull ground up onto shale and gravel with a shriek that went through Petra's teeth, and she killed the engine, and she shoved Noor over the rail into the shallows, and the third boat was already coming, already turning, men on its deck with ropes and guns and Voss's terrible pleasant smile nowhere in sight but present anyway, somehow, in the whole arrangement of things.

"Noor—"

"I'm going," Noor said. "I'm fast. Go inland, find people, don't be stupid alone—" And then she was running, up the shale, into the scrub, quick and low, and for one full second Petra believed in the plan.

Then a rope went over Noor like a lasso going over a calf, and two men leaned back, and the daughter of the Meridian expedition came off her feet and into the water, and the last Petra saw of her was a fist, a splash, and a face turned toward the bank — not screaming, not begging, just looking, filing Petra away the way you file a debt.

The boats turned downstream with their catch. The narrows closed behind them with a sound like a door.

Petra stood in eighteen inches of freezing water beside her dying boat and discovered that she had no idea what to do next.

* * *

Here is what running teaches you: how to be alone efficiently. How to sleep in shifts, eat standing up, trust no harbor, owe no favors. Petra Vasquez had been very good at it for three years. Alone was a skill, and she'd sharpened it fine.

Alone was also useless.

She got the *Heron* beached properly above the flood line and stuffed the hole with a shirt and a length of rubber sheeting, mostly so her hands would have something to do while her mind refused to work. Noor was on a fleet boat heading downriver toward whatever Voss wanted from her — leverage, probably, or bait, or simply removal, because a Haddad with a claim on the wreck was a complication he hadn't priced. Petra could chase them. She had no boat that floated, no gun, no plan, and six miles of flooded canyon between her and anywhere.

Or she could do the thing she'd sworn off.

She sat on the shale with the river shoving past her knees and let herself say it honestly, once, the way you set a bone: *I need help.* Three words, and every one of them cost more than the interest the Wake ever charged.

Because help meant people, and people meant staying, and staying meant roots, and roots meant the Wake would find her again — except the Wake had found her in every port from here to the salt flats, hadn't they? Found her at fuel docks, found her in storm season, found her with cheerful patience and raised rates. Running hadn't saved her. It had just kept her too tired to notice she was paying either way.

And there was something else, something the river had been trying to tell her since the first marker. Her brother had known these people. Had painted their rocks and drunk their tea and mapped their drowning canyon because they'd asked him to, because he was the kind of man who got asked. When Jonah vanished, these banks had lost someone too.

Petra wrung out her sleeves and started walking upstream along the flood line, looking for smoke.

* * *

She smelled woodsmoke an hour later, threading down through the salt-cedar, and followed it to a house built on stilts above the high-tide line, all weathered gray timber and netting hung to dry, with a tin chimney breathing a thin blue ribbon into the evening air. An old woman was mending a net on the porch, and she looked up at Petra with the unhurried assessment of someone who had watched every kind of weather come up this river.

"You're the sister," the old woman said.

Petra stopped dead. "I'm sorry?"

"The painter's sister. The one who marked the narrows." She jerked her chin at Petra's soaked clothes, her salt-crusted hair, the direction of the canyon. "You've got his walk. Like the ground owes you money and you intend to collect." She set down the net. "Sit. You look like a drowned chart."

"I don't—" Petra's voice cracked, and she hated it, and she sat anyway, on the edge of the step, because her legs had made the decision for her. "My brother. You knew him?"

"Knew him? Boy ate at my table two seasons running. Taught my grandson to read tide tables." The old woman's eyes narrowed. "Marta. My name's Marta. Now tell me why you're dripping on my porch and who put that look on your face, because that's not grief. Grief I know. That's somebody planning something."

So Petra told her. All of it — the expedition, the silence, Noor, the fleet, the ambush, the rope going over Noor like she was livestock. She told it flat and fast, the way she reported damage, and Marta listened without interrupting, which was its own kind of mercy.

When she finished, Marta didn't say *I'm sorry*. She stood up, went inside, and came back with a brass bell, which she rang twice, hard, out over the water.

"What does that do?" Petra asked.

"Calls the crew."

"What crew?"

Mara gave her a look that was nearly identical to the one Noor gave her when Petra lied about being scared. "Child, you think my grandson learned tide tables for fun? This river has forty families on it, and every one of them owed your brother something, and every one of them has been waiting three years for somebody to come asking." She hung the bell back on its hook. "You spent all this time running from creditors. Well. Tonight you learn what it looks like when the debt runs the other way."

They came out of the dusk in ones and twos, as if the bell had been a rumor passed mouth to mouth: skiffs and punts and a flat-bottomed barge with a lantern swinging at its bow. Men and women with rope-scarred hands and faces like the canyon itself, weathered and patient and absolutely unimpressed by the idea of Voss or his fleet or his guns. Marta's grandson — sixteen, sharp-eyed, called Teo — arrived first and fastest and stared at Petra like she was a chapter from a story he'd grown up on.

"You're really her," he said. "Jonah's sister."

"I'm really her."

"He said you'd come someday. Said you ran, but runners always double back eventually." Teo grinned, and it was Jonah's grin, and it went through Petra like a harpoon. "He said you'd need a boat."

They gave her one before midnight — the barge, slow and ugly and armored in the only way river boats are armored, which is with people who refuse to sink. They gave her charts drawn in her brother's own hand, corrections and annotations in margins she'd never seen, including a route through the flooded back-channels that bypassed the narrows entirely. They gave her hot food, dry clothes, and a plan assembled in an hour by forty voices talking over each other, and somewhere in the middle of it Petra realized her face was wet and couldn't remember when that had started.

Voss had taken Noor downstream toward the deep pool above the wreck, where the fleet would anchor for the sixth and final tide. He thought he had everything: the girl, the route, the timing, the guns.

He didn't have the river.

At dawn, Petra stood at the tiller of the barge with Teo at the sheets and Marta's whole impossible crew spread across the decks behind her, and she watched the fifth tide turn and begin its long pull toward the sea.

"Hold on to something," she said. "We're going to introduce Mr. Voss to the family."

Behind her, forty strangers answered as one voice, and it sounded, for the first time in three years, like home.
