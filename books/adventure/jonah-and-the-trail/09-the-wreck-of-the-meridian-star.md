## 9. The Wreck of the Meridian Star

The fifth tide had taken Noor and left Petra a holed boat and a debt of a different kind — the kind you can't pay with salvage. The river people of the narrows had patched the *Optimist's Wake* hull with tarred canvas and stubbornness, fed her fish stew she didn't deserve, and pointed at the water when she asked where the Meridian Star lay.

"Past the second bend," said an old woman whose hands were stained blue from net-dye. "Where your brother's markers stop. He never painted past there. Said the canyon didn't need his help to kill you."

Now the sixth tide was coming in, the last window, and Petra stood alone on the foredeck watching the water rise up the gorge walls like something patient finally standing up from a long crouch. The sky was the color of wet slate. Somewhere upstream, Voss's fleet was holding Noor somewhere, and somewhere downstream, the river authority was either coming or not coming, depending on how cheaply Voss had bought them.

The tide table said ninety minutes. The water said hurry.

Petra stripped to her dive skin, checked her tanks twice, three times, the way her brother used to check knots — not because he doubted them but because the ritual was a prayer. She spat in her mask and rinsed it. The water below the boat was green-black, thick as tea, and it moved against the current in slow spirals, like it was thinking.

"You don't have to go down," she told herself out loud, because three years of running had taught her that silence was where the lies lived.

She went down anyway.

* * *

The cold hit first, then the dark, then the sound — the drowned canyon groaning around her like a ship settling, rock flexing under tons of incoming tide. Her lamp carved a cone through water full of drifting silt, motes turning in it like snow falling upward. She followed the drop-off down past the high-water line, past the old flood wrack caught in ledges, past a hand-painted marker on the wall at forty feet — her brother's work, the little laughing wave symbol he'd signed everything with, paint flaking but still grinning after all this time.

*You're close,* the marker said, in the language only she could read anymore.

The Meridian Star came out of the dark all at once, the way big things do underwater — one moment nothing, the next a wall of steel looming over her, keel-up along the canyon floor, masts snapped, hull split along the rocks like a fruit opened by a careless thumb. Fifty feet of expedition boat lying in sixty feet of green water, and Petra hung in front of it breathing hard through her regulator, feeling the wreck's silence press against her ears.

Three years. Three years of storms and salt flats and fuel docks and smiling creditors, and here it was: the answer, lying on its back at the bottom of the world.

She swam the length of the hull. The breach was forward of the midships — wrong place, wrong angle for a grounding. She'd seen enough wrecks to read them like weather. Groundings tore along the bottom, long gouges, honest wounds. This was a hole punched inward, edges bent *out*, plates peeled back from the inside.

Explosive. Small, placed, deliberate.

Her hands went still on the steel. The fleet hadn't raced the Meridian crew. They'd sunk them. And her brother had been aboard, or near aboard, or —

*Breathe.* The gauge said plenty of air. The tide said otherwise.

She found the breach and slipped inside, into the belly of the dead ship, her lamp beam swallowed by corridors furred with silt. The galley was a drift of cups and cutlery frozen mid-fall. A bunk room, mattresses bloated like drowned animals. In the chart room, tables bolted to the floor, charts fused into pulp — except one, laminated, pinned under a tank strap. She pulled it free. The flooded canyon route. Every marker annotated in her brother's cramped handwriting, and at the bottom, in fresh ink that had somehow survived in its plastic sleeve:

*If they're reading this — P.V. knows the way in. Trust her before anyone who smiles.*

Petra laughed underwater, a burst of bubbles, and it broke into something else, something that shook her shoulders and fogged the inside of her mask.

Then she saw the logbook.

It was wedged in the chart drawer, wrapped in oilcloth, tied with the double-loop knot he'd taught her when she was nine years old — *the knot that says I meant to come back*. Her fingers knew it before her eyes did. She untied it the way you unwrap a wound.

The pages were damp-swollen but legible. Entries in his hand, dated across the final week of the Meridian expedition. Fuel counts that didn't match. A stranger's boat shadowing them through the lower canyon. Then, three days before the sinking, the entry that stopped her heart mid-beat:

*Sabotage confirmed. Cut mooring lines, drained reserve tanks. Voss's people. They don't want us finding what's in the deep pool — they want us gone before anyone knows the Meridian ever made it this far. I've hidden the proof where only a diver would look. If I don't surface, tell my sister the route is hers. Tell her I'm sorry about the money. It was never about the money.*

And beneath that, the last entry, written in a different pressure, a different hour:

*Going into the water tonight. Not because of the fleet. Because of what the deep pool holds. If it's what I think it is, no one should own it — not them, not me, not anyone. P., if you're reading this: I walked away. Don't dig for a body. There isn't one.*

No body.

She swept the lamp through the wreck's drowned corridors, expecting — dreading — the pale drift of him, three years patient in the green. Nothing. Bunks empty. Galley empty. The crew manifest clipped to the bulkhead listed eleven souls; the deep pool beyond the wreck held whatever secrets it held, but the wreck itself was a house with everyone gone home.

He'd walked away. Swum out, maybe, through the breach before the charge blew, or never been aboard at all when it went down. Either way: alive, or alive enough. Alive the way a postcard is alive. Not ready. But reachable.

Petra pressed the logbook to her chest inside its oilcloth and felt the tide begin to turn around her — a change in the water's voice, a low pull starting far off toward the sea. The window was closing. Sixty feet of rising, reversing current between her and the light.

She kicked for the breach.

* * *

She surfaced in the last gray light with the log sealed in a waterproof bag lashed to her harness, and the first thing she saw was the muzzle of a rifle.

Voss stood on the deck of his lead boat ten feet away, and behind him two of his men, and behind them, tied to the rail with her wrists raw, Noor Haddad — alive, furious, one eye swollen shut, and grinning like a woman who'd already won something.

"There she is," Voss said warmly, the way he said everything, as if the whole world were a deal waiting to be closed. "The famous Vasquez. You know, I offered to buy your debt this morning. I'll sweeten it now. The logbook for your freedom — yours and hers. Clean ledgers. A boat that floats. You could be anywhere by dawn, and nobody would ever say the name Voss to you again."

The tide was pulling. Petra trod water, treading the line too, and looked at the gun, and the man, and the girl tied to the rail who had hired a stranger and trusted her with everything.

*Trust her before anyone who smiles.*

"The Wake owns me," Petra said. "Not you. And the Wake's paper isn't worth what it thinks it is — not once certain ledgers reach certain ears."

Voss's smile flickered, just at the corners. "You're not thinking clearly. That book is worth more than both your lives."

"That's exactly why nobody gets to keep it." And Petra reached for the radio clipped to her shoulder strap — the river people's gift, a battered handheld unit, channel open, transmitter hot since the moment she'd surfaced, because the old net-mender woman had wired it herself and said, *We remember your brother. We listen for our own.*

She keyed it live.

"This is Petra Vasquez, broadcasting open on all channels, river band and coastal band alike. I'm reading from the log of the *Meridian Star*, recovered from the wreck. Names, dates, fuel counts, sabotage orders — all of it. Copy that, Voss? All of it."

His men shifted. The rifle wavered. And out of the darkening gorge, from three directions at once, came engine noise — small boats, dozens of them, river people with nets and hooks and lanterns and no love for the fleet that had murdered an expedition in their canyon, closing in with the turning tide.

Noor was laughing, bloody-mouthed and bright. "Told you," she called to Voss. "Told you she wouldn't trade."

Petra kept reading, voice steady, while the water rose and the lights came on down the canyon like a constellation deciding, finally, to take sides — and the last thing she saw before the boats converged was Voss's face, unsmiling at last, doing arithmetic he was going to lose.

"*Entry, final day,*" she read. "*I walked away. Don't dig for a body.*"

Somewhere downcoast, a man with her brother's laugh was going to hear his own words come back up the river.

She intended to make sure of it.
