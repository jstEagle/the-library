## 10. Still Water

The broadcast went out at slack tide, when the river held its breath between the pull of the sea and the pull of the hills, and every ear on the water heard it.

Petra's voice came through first, roughened by salt and diesel and three years of running, reading her brother's log in a flat, steady tone that made it worse somehow — worse than shouting, worse than weeping. The words of a dead man describing his own murder. *They cut our lines at Anchor Nine. They drained the tanks while we slept. Voss says the insurance will cover what salvage won't. If you're hearing this, I loved you. Tell Petra she was right about the markers.*

She read it twice. Then she read the ledger pages Noor had photographed in the fleet's strongbox — the Wake's ledgers, the Optimist's own cheerful arithmetic, interest compounded on grief, debts bought and sold like weather. She read until the transmission looped and the river authority cut in with an emergency bulletin that was really just an admission: they had heard. Everyone had heard.

On the trading post's sagging porch, the radio operator — a woman named Ferro who'd known Jonah Vasquez well enough to have kept one of his hand-painted channel markers hanging above her door like a saint's icon — turned the volume down and looked at the crowd gathered in her yard.

"Well," Ferro said. "That's done."

* * *

Voss heard it too, forty miles downriver, aboard the *Gilded Hour*, with a cup of coffee going cold in his hand and his smile still fixed in place from some conversation that had ended without his noticing.

He had always believed the river could be bought the way harbors were bought — quietly, in back rooms, with numbers spoken softly. He had bribed the harbor master at Salt Landing. He had bribed two tide pilots and a customs clerk. He had even bribed a man in the river authority, a minor functionary named Ochoa whose signature could make inspections evaporate.

What he had never understood — what men like Voss never understand — is that the river keeps its own books, and everyone downstream reads them.

Ochoa arrived at dawn with four boats and no smiles. He stood on the *Gilded Hour*'s deck in a rain jacket too big for him and read the warrant in a voice that shook only a little. Voss listened with his head tilted, courteous to the end, as though the whole thing were a scheduling error he'd soon sort out. Then the crew began hauling up his hidden cargo — the cutting tools, the spare transponders keyed to the Meridian's frequency, the crate of sabotaged line marked with the fleet's own stencil — and something behind Voss's eyes finally closed, like a shop pulling its shutters.

"They'll forget," he said to Ochoa, as the cuffs went on. "The river forgets everything."

"The river remembers," Ochoa said. "That's rather the point of it."

The *Gilded Hour* was impounded where she sat. Her crew scattered into the delta towns like spilled grain, and not one of them, when questioned, said a word in Voss's defense. That was the truest verdict of all.

* * *

The Optimist's Wake came for Petra one last time, three days after the broadcast, and found nothing left to collect.

They arrived at the fuel dock at Salt Landing in their spotless boat, wearing their patient smiles, carrying their folder of cheerful paperwork — and found the dock crowded. Ferro was there. The river people who had pulled Petra's holed boat off the narrows were there. Old Sefa, who remembered Jonah teaching her grandsons to read the canyon walls, sat on a bollard with her arms crossed like a woman attending a funeral she intended to enjoy.

The Wake's lead creditor — a pleasant man called Merrick who had raised Petra's interest twice over the years with genuine warmth, as if doing her a kindness — opened his folder and stopped.

"You've seen the broadcast," Petra said. Not a question.

"We've seen the broadcast." Merrick closed the folder. For a moment the smile flickered, and underneath it was something tired and old and almost human. "The ledgers are public record now. Every debt we hold is under review. Including yours."

"And?"

"And," Merrick said, and here he did something none of them expected: he laughed, short and rueful, at himself, at the whole rotten architecture coming down around him, "and it appears the Optimist's Wake has been operating as an unlicensed collection concern for eleven years, which means most of what we've taken from people was never legally ours." He tucked the folder under his arm. "Your debt died with the Meridian, Ms. Vasquez. Consider it drowned."

He tipped his hat to Sefa on the way back to his boat, because manners were the last thing the Wake ever truly owned.

Petra stood on the dock for a long time after the sound of their engine faded. Noor found her there, hands in pockets, watching the water do nothing in particular.

"You okay?" Noor asked.

"I don't know what to do with my hands," Petra said. "I've spent three years holding them ready to run. There's nowhere to run to. There's nothing chasing me." She flexed her fingers, bewildered. "It turns out I don't know how to stand still. Nobody taught me."

"Jonah did," Noor said. "According to his log. He wrote that you were the only person he knew who could sit in a boat all day waiting for the tide to turn and call it work." She shrugged. "Maybe standing still is just another kind of piloting. You wait for the water to decide, and then you go where it goes."

Petra looked at her — this strange, brave woman who had hired a stranger with her inheritance and gotten back a sister instead — and felt the last knot inside her chest come loose, not all at once but the way a mooring line comes loose, in stages, with creaking.

* * *

The postcard came a week later, forwarded twice, salt-stained at the corners.

It showed a lighthouse downcoast, white against a sky the color of hammered tin. On the back, in handwriting Petra knew better than her own — handwriting she had traced in memory across three empty years — five lines:

*Petra. Heard the broadcast in a bar in Puerto Aliso and had to sit down on the floor. You read my terrible handwriting beautifully. I'm alive. I wasn't ready to be found before, and I'm still not entirely, but I'm closer than I was. The canyon route works both ways, if you ever want to come south. Take your time. I took mine.*

*— J.*

P.S. Tell the girl with the stubborn face thank you.

Petra read it standing in the doorway of the *Optimist's Wake*'s old office — no. Of *her* boat, the one the river people had helped patch, the one with the new name Noor had painted on the stern in letters the color of storm light: *Still Water*. She read it three times. Then she sat down on the deck planks, right there in the doorway, and laughed and cried in about equal measure, which is what people do when a wound that has been holding them together finally gets to heal.

Alive. Not ready. But reachable.

Downcoast. A lighthouse. A bar in Puerto Aliso. A route that worked both ways.

She didn't leave that week. She didn't leave that month. There were tides to learn again, now that she was staying for them; there was a boat to finish fitting out; there was Noor, who had decided, with the same terrifying calm she brought to flooded canyons, that she was staying too, at least until the river authority finished its hearings, at least until the Meridian families got their answers, at least — she said, looking out at the water — until further notice.

* * *

On the last evening of the season, Petra walked the dock at Salt Landing with her bags unpacked for the first time in three years, everything she owned folded into drawers and shelves aboard the *Still Water*, nothing left bundled for a fast exit. It felt like walking without shoes. It felt like breathing after a long dive.

Ferro was closing up the radio shack. She paused in the doorway, key ring in hand.

"Heard anything?" she asked. It had become their ritual. Every evening, the same question, gentle as a lamp being lit.

"Not yet." Petra leaned on the rail. The river lay flat and pewter-colored, holding the last light. Somewhere upstream, the canyon was flooding with the evening tide, Jonah's painted markers slipping beneath the surface the way they did twice a day, patient, certain of rising again. "But he said take my time. And I'm good at tides."

Ferro smiled and locked the door. "There'll be a window," she said. "There's always a window."

Petra stayed at the rail after she'd gone. Far downriver, past the last bend, a single light moved on the darkening water — a fishing boat, probably, or a patrol skiff, or nothing at all, just the eye playing tricks in the dusk. But she watched it anyway, the way you watch any small bright thing heading toward you out of the distance, and she found she wasn't afraid of what it might be.

For the first time in three years, whatever came up the river would find her home.
