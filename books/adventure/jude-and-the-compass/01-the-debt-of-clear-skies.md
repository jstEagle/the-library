## 1. The Debt of Clear Skies

The storm we'd chased for six days died somewhere over the Salt Flats without ever touching ground, and I stood in the truck bed watching it go like a man watching his paycheck drive over the horizon.

"Tell me you got something," Dex said from the cab, not looking up from the instruments. He'd stopped looking up at me around day four, when the fuel gauge started making decisions for us.

"I got a beautiful anvil cloud and a wind shear profile worth framing."

"We can't frame it. We can't eat it. And we sure as hell can't give it to Vane's people."

He had me there. The Vane Syndicate didn't deal in beauty. They dealt in numbers — specifically, in the number forty-one thousand, which was what I owed them after two seasons of chasing that had produced exactly one marketable dataset and a great deal of character. The collectors had started showing up at our stops with the cheerful patience of men who knew exactly where I slept. Last month one of them bought me dinner. That was worse than the threats.

I climbed down out of the bed and looked at my crew. Dex, who'd followed me out of loyalty or stupidity or some alloy of the two. Priya, our meteorologist, who could read a sky the way priests used to read scripture and who hadn't been paid in five weeks. The trucks needed new tires. The radar array needed a new gyro. I needed a miracle with a signature line on it.

That was when Mara Quill walked into the diner.

She did it the way she did everything — like the room had been reserved for her in advance. Quill Expeditions, three trucks deep, crewed by people with matching jackets. She sat down across from me without asking, which was either arrogance or intimacy, and with Mara it had always been both.

"Sebastian Coldharbour," she said. "You look like a man who's about to say yes to something."

"Mara."

"I hear you're in a little trouble with Vane."

"You hear a lot of things."

"I hear they're quoting interest now." She stirred my coffee like she owned it. "Here's the shape of things. I've got a contract. Survey crew needs escorting across the Kestrel Range before the snows close the passes. High-altitude route, weather window maybe ten days wide. My trucks are committed south. Yours are sitting here rusting into a monument to bad decisions."

"And your cut?"

"Ten percent finder's fee. Your fee is thirty thousand, paid half up front." She slid an envelope across the table, thick enough to stop a door. "Fifteen thousand today. The rest on delivery."

I looked at the envelope. Fifteen thousand dollars would clear the worst of Vane, buy tires, put food in my crew's stomachs. Fifteen thousand dollars was oxygen.

"What's the catch?" I asked, because there is always a catch, and with Mara there are usually several, arranged decoratively.

"No catch. Escort the surveyors, keep them alive, get them through. Sign here." She turned the contract toward me and set a pen on top of it with the tenderness of a woman laying flowers on a grave.

I signed. I want to be honest about this, because honesty is the only currency I have left: I signed without reading it. Not because I trusted Mara Quill — I have never trusted Mara Quill, and she has built a career on that — but because fifteen thousand dollars makes a man illiterate. The debt was screaming so loud in my ears that I couldn't hear anything else, including the small cold voice that always asks *where*.

Dex asked it anyway, back at the trucks, when he finally pried the contract out of my hands.

"Route says rendezvous at Kestrel Station, then west approach through—" He stopped. I watched him stop. I watched his face do the thing faces do when they're deciding whether to hand you something heavy.

"Through what?"

"Seb."

"*Through what*, Dex."

He handed it back. I read the route line twice, three times, as if the words might rearrange themselves out of mercy.

*Terminus: Coldharbour Hollow.*

The valley. My valley, once. The place where I learned to read storms off my brother's shoulder, where Tomas would stand on the fence rail with his arms out like the wind was going to carry him somewhere better. The place where the storm took him — took him and left me standing in the road holding nothing but a barometer and a broken engagement, because Jude Ashcombe looked at me that night and saw a man who loved the sky more than anything living under it, and she wasn't wrong.

I swore I'd never go back. I'd routed around that hollow for eleven years. Added four hundred miles to crossings rather than let my headlights touch the county line. There are roads you don't drive and graves you don't dig up, and I had kept both promises until tonight.

And now the money was already in my pocket.

That's the trap nobody warns you about. It isn't temptation that gets you — temptation you can refuse. It's relief. Fifteen thousand dollars sitting warm against my chest like a second heartbeat, Dex and Priya fed and paid, Vane's collectors suddenly just men instead of weather. All of it purchased with a signature, and the bill comes due in a valley I buried my brother behind.

"You knew," I said to Mara later, on the diner porch, breath fogging in the first real cold of the season.

"I suspected you wouldn't read it." She lit a cigarette against the wind, cupping the flame like it was something shy. "Doesn't matter. A job's a job. You need it, I need you gone from my rearview, and the surveyors need someone who knows high country. Everyone wins."

"Everyone except whoever lives in that hollow."

Something crossed her face then — quick, gone, like a fish under ice. "Nobody wins everything, Sebastian. That's what the range is for."

She left before dawn with her convoy heading south, and I stood in the parking lot holding a contract that ended at Coldharbour Hollow and a wad of cash that felt less like payment every time I touched it.

We rolled out two days later. Priya navigated, Dex drove lead, and I rode in the back seat of the second truck doing the arithmetic cowards do — how many days to the pass, how many ways to avoid the terminus, whether a man could deliver a survey crew to within sight of a valley and call the job done. The mountains came up green and then gray and then white-capped, patient as creditors, and every mile west the air got thinner and my chest got tighter, though I told myself it was altitude.

On the fifth morning we crested the last ridge before the station, and Priya pointed down into the long green throat of the valley below and said, "There it is. Coldharbour Hollow."

She said it like a place name. Just a place name, a dot on a map, a terminus in a contract I should have read.

But I heard it the way you hear your own name called from a dark house — and eleven years fell away between one heartbeat and the next, and I swear to God the wind coming up out of that valley smelled like my brother's jacket.

Smoke rose from chimneys at the far end of town. Small, steady, domestic. Somebody still lived there. Somebody had built a life there, brick by careful brick, on the foundation of the night I drove away.

"Boss?" Dex said. "You good?"

I watched the smoke rise. I thought about fifteen thousand dollars, and forty-one thousand more besides, and the ten-day window before the snows closed, and the fact that a door I had nailed shut with both hands was now swinging open with the whole weight of winter behind it.

"Yeah," I said, and reached for the door handle. "Let's go meet the locals."

* * *

The town came up slow, the way towns do when they're deciding whether to be glad to see you. Main street was one street. A feed store, a church with a lightning rod it clearly needed, a café called the Waypoint with a chalkboard menu and a porch that had been repainted since I left — blue now, instead of gray.

I was reading the blue paint, trying to decide if it meant anything, when the café door opened and she stepped out with a crate of jars balanced on her hip.

Jude Ashcombe.

Eleven years, and my first coherent thought was that she'd changed her hair. My second was that she hadn't changed at all — same level gray eyes, same way of standing like the ground owed her rent. She saw the trucks first, then the logo on the doors, then me, and I watched recognition move through her face like frost moving across a window: fast, total, irreversible.

The crate tilted. She caught it. She set it down on the porch rail with exaggerated care, the way you handle something when you'd rather throw it.

"Well," she said, in a voice that carried all the way across the street and into the eleven years between us. "They'll let anybody through the pass these days."

Behind me, Priya whispered, "Who's that?"

I picked up the contract, folded it, and put it in my pocket like it could protect me.

"That," I said, "is our guide."
