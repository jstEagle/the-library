## 2. What the Valley Remembers

The first thing I noticed about Sebastian Coldharbour after eleven years was that he still couldn't park a truck.

He'd nosed the lead vehicle up to the hollow's one crossroads at an angle that suggested he'd been aiming for the well and changed his mind halfway. Behind him came three more trucks, mud-splattered, riding low with survey gear, and a fifth that was mostly antenna. The whole procession idled there in the gray light like a funeral that had gotten lost on the way to the cemetery.

I stood in the doorway of the supply house with a clipboard I wasn't reading and watched him climb out. He'd gone gray at the temples, which felt like a personal insult — as if he'd spent those years aging while I'd been standing still, holding my breath.

"Jude." He said my name the way you'd test ice before stepping on it.

"Coldharbour." I said his surname because his given name had too much history in it to say out loud in front of the postmaster, who was already pretending not to listen from across the road. "You're blocking the well."

He looked back at his truck as if noticing it for the first time. "Right. Sorry." He didn't move it.

Eleven years, and the man still thought an apology was the same thing as an action.

* * *

Here is what you need to know about Coldharbour Hollow in late October: the light comes down the valley at an angle that makes everything look remembered rather than seen. The pines go dark. The river goes the color of old pewter. And every year around this time, the town holds its breath, because the pass above us — Kestrel Saddle, the notch in the range that our grandfathers named and our mothers feared — closes when it wants to, and it does not consult anyone's schedule.

I have built a life here the way you build a wall in flood country: stone by stone, each one chosen, each one placed with intent. I run the supply house. I sit on the winter council. I know which families need firewood by November and which ones will lie about needing it. I teach the Ashcombe route to two teenagers who think of the mountains as scenery, and I correct them gently, because the mountain is not scenery, it is a landlord, and rent is due whether or not you're home.

What I did not build into my careful life was Sebastian Coldharbour walking back into it with a survey crew and a smile like a man arriving at his own trial.

* * *

They wanted provisions, obviously. That part was simple. Forty people eat a lot, and Mara Quill — who had come down from the ridge line looking like she'd been carved out of the same granite — paid in cash without haggling, which in my experience means either wealth or desperation, and Quill struck me as both.

It was the guide request that complicated things.

"We need someone who knows the saddle," Sebastian said, evening of the second day, standing in my supply room among the crates and lamp oil like a ghost haunting its own grave. "Mara says you're the best mountaineer in the hollow."

"Mara says a lot of things."

"She said you mapped the high route yourself."

"I did." I kept stacking tins. My hands needed something honest to do. "When I was nineteen. Before."

"Before," he repeated, and the word sat between us like a third person.

"Before the storm took your brother and you took yourself off to chase weather for a living," I said. It came out flatter than I'd intended, which surprised me, because I'd rehearsed it sharp. Eleven years of rehearsal, and all I could produce was flat. Grief, it turns out, doesn't keep its edge. It just keeps its weight.

"Jude—"

"You want the route, I'll draw you the route. You want me to walk it with you?" I set down the tin harder than necessary. "Ask the mountain. See what it says."

He was quiet for a moment. Then: "I'm asking you."

And there it was — the thing I hadn't planned for, which was not his apology but his directness. Sebastian Coldharbour had never once in his life asked anyone directly for anything; he'd charmed, he'd bartered, he'd vanished. Standing there among my tins, he looked like a man who had finally learned what things cost, and the knowledge had not made him prettier, only more careful.

"The crew can't cross without a local," he said. "There are forty of them and half have never seen snow. If the saddle seals early, they die up there, and their deaths will be on this valley either way. I'm not asking you to forgive me. I'm asking you to keep them alive."

Which was, I will admit, a well-constructed sentence. He always could build a sentence.

"I'll do it for the crew," I told him. "Not for you."

"Understood."

"And if you say my name on the trail, I'll leave you at the treeline."

"Also understood."

He almost smiled. I turned away before I could find out why that annoyed me so much.

* * *

We left on a morning so clear it felt like a dare. Frost on the fence wire, breath hanging, the peaks above the hollow wearing their first white like a warning label. Forty-one of us went up: forty strangers and me, plus Sebastian at the front and Mara Quill somewhere behind with her instruments wrapped in oilcloth like something stolen, which I would later learn was because it was.

The first day was easy ground, and I hated how good it felt. The high country has always been where I think clearest, and having Sebastian Coldharbour breathing my air didn't change the fact that the trail was mine — every switchback, every cairn I'd rebuilt after washouts, every spring I knew by taste. He walked behind me and I could feel him cataloguing it, the way he used to catalogue storms, and some traitorous part of me liked being catalogued again.

We talked in barbs, because barbs were safe.

"Your council let you go?" he asked, second morning, as we crossed the scree field below the shoulder.

"The council trusts me. Novel concept."

"They used to trust me too."

"They trusted your driving, Coldharbour. There's a difference."

He laughed — actually laughed — and the sound did something to my chest that I chose not to examine. "You've gotten mean."

"You've gotten polite. One of us should worry about it."

But here is the thing about barbs: they're load-bearing. Take enough weight off them and they crack, and what's underneath comes through. It happened on the third night, camped under the shoulder of the saddle with the wind picking up and the temperature falling faster than the almanac promised. He found me checking the anchor lines, and instead of the usual fencing he just stood there in the lamplight looking older than his years and said:

"He'd be twenty-nine now."

I didn't ask who. We both knew who. Thomas, who used to follow us up this very trail with his ridiculous red jacket, shouting echoes off the walls.

"Yes," I said.

"I think about him every time the pressure drops. Every single time. Some kind of punishment mechanism, probably. The sky takes someone and then bills you forever."

"That's not how grief works, Sebastian."

"No," he said. "That's how guilt works. Grief is different. Grief I might have managed. But I left, Jude. Three weeks after the funeral I was gone, and I told myself it was chasing storms, but we both know I was running from a valley that had stopped being a home and started being a verdict."

The wind moved through the camp. Somewhere a guyline hummed.

"Why are you telling me this now?"

"Because you asked why I came back, and I never answered, and you deserve an answer even if it's a bad one." He looked at the peaks, black against the last violet of the sky. "I came back because everything I ran toward turned out to be the same thing I ran from. Weather doesn't care where you sleep, Jude. Debt doesn't care. Nothing out there cared, and it turns out the only place that ever held me accountable was the place I left."

I should have said something cruel. Cruelty was in the plan; cruelty was the wall. Instead I heard myself say, quietly, "Thomas would have laughed at your parking."

Sebastian's face did something complicated. "God, yes. He used to rate my parking on a scale of one to ten."

"He gave you a four. Once. You were so proud."

"I framed the memory."

And I laughed — a real one, ugly and sudden, grief and love coming out the same door — and I hated him for it, and I hated myself more, because eleven years of careful stonework and one conversation on a cold mountainside had put a crack clean through it.

* * *

I woke before dawn on the fourth day because the barometer had fallen like a stone off a cliff, and I stood outside my tent and looked north and did not like anything I saw.

The clouds over the saddle weren't weather clouds. They were freight. Stacked, bruised, moving against the wind at altitude — the signature of a storm arriving early and meaning business. By my reckoning we had hours, not days, to get forty greenhorns over the notch or find shelter, and the only shelter past the shoulder was the old mining hut, and the hut was two miles short of the pass.

Then I saw the lights.

Downslope, threading up the valley behind us — not headlamps, not lanterns. Vehicle lights, three sets, moving fast along the lower trail where no vehicles were supposed to be, closing the distance between us and the way home.

I woke Sebastian with a hand on his shoulder and no gentleness in it at all.

"Whoever taught you to run, Coldharbour," I said, "taught somebody else to follow. We've got company."
