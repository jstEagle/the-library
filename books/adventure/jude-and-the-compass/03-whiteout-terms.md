## 3. Whiteout Terms

The storm came in like a creditor — politely at first, then all at once.

I'd been watching the sky all morning, the way you watch a man across a poker table who's stopped blinking. The clouds over the Kestrel Range had that bruised, organized look that means business, and by noon the wind had picked up a whistle I didn't like. By one o'clock it was singing. By two, the world went white and stayed white.

"Seal up!" I shouted over it. "Camp here! Nobody moves without a rope!"

The survey crew moved with the practiced misery of people who'd been cold for three days and had stopped expecting otherwise. Okafor got the tents guyed out before the gusts could take them. Little Priya — twenty-two, sharp as a tack, hired because she was cheap and good, which is to say because she was me ten years ago — staked the instruments down and looked at me like she wanted an explanation for the weather personally.

Mara Quill stood at the edge of camp with her hood back, letting snow land on her face, wearing the expression of a woman checking her watch.

"You knew," I said, coming up beside her. "You checked the almanacs. You knew this front was early."

"I knew it was *possible*." She turned, and there was something in her face I couldn't read, which with Mara usually meant I didn't want to. "Sebastian. We need to talk."

"We're in a blizzard."

"Which is why we need to talk now, while everyone's too frozen to argue."

That's when I saw them.

Three figures coming up the trail behind us, moving against the storm like they had a grudge against physics. Big shapes, steady pace, no wasted motion. Anybody else would've turned back hours ago. These men hadn't turned back because turning back wasn't in their contract.

Vane Syndicate collectors. I knew the middle one — Corvus, they called him, on account of he showed up after something died. He'd sat across from me in a borrowed office eight months ago and explained, with great patience, what happened to men whose arithmetic didn't work out.

"Mara," I said slowly, "why are they here?"

She didn't answer right away, and the not-answering told me everything before she opened her mouth.

"The survey data," she said. "The full set. Every reading, every map layer, the deep-scans of the range. That's what Vane wants. That's what Vane gets."

The wind howled through the pass like it was laughing at me.

"I don't understand," I said, and I did understand, I understood completely, I just needed thirty more seconds of not understanding. "You hired me to escort a survey crew."

"I hired you because you're desperate, desperate men sign anything, and because your name opens doors in this valley that mine doesn't." She said it flatly, no apology in it, which was somehow worse than if she'd gloated. "The crew is real. The science is real. But the client isn't a mining consortium, Sebastian. It never was. Vane wants the Kestrel Range mapped — the passes, the water rights, the mineral seams under Coldharbour Hollow — and they want it done by someone the valley trusts enough to let close. You were the key. I just turned you in the lock."

"You used me."

"I *paid* you. There's a difference, and the difference is called a debt, and you of all people know how those work." She glanced toward the tents, where my crew was huddled, where Jude was — God help me — checking the rope lines like the storm was a personal insult. "Corvus takes the data tonight. Your ledger clears. Everyone walks away. That's the deal."

"And if I say no?"

Mara looked past me at the three shapes closing through the whiteout, and didn't say anything at all.

* * *

They made us gather in the big tent. All nine of us: me, Mara, the six crew, and Jude, who stood with her arms crossed like she was guarding the door even though the door was a flap of canvas and the enemy was already inside it.

Corvus was exactly as I remembered him — broad, gray-eyed, patient the way avalanches are patient. He had a leather case under one arm and a pistol on his hip he clearly considered decorative, because men like Corvus don't need guns when the weather does their work for free.

"Sebastian Coldharbour." He smiled like we were old friends. "Eight months. You've been hard to find. Though apparently not hard to hire."

"Corvus."

"Ms. Quill has been very helpful." He set the case on the instrument crate and opened it. Inside, padded like relics, were drives and folios — the survey's entire archive, copied and collated, ready to travel. Ready, I realized with a cold that had nothing to do with the storm, since before we ever left the lowlands. "Everything accounted for. Which means your account, Mr. Coldharbour, is settled. You may consider yourself a free man."

"Free," Priya repeated, from the back. Her voice cracked. "What about us? What about the town? Those are our readings. That's — those maps go to strip crews. To—"

"To development," Corvus agreed pleasantly. "The valley has value. Value wants extracting. That's not villainy, Miss, that's economics."

"That's my home," said Jude.

Everyone turned. She hadn't raised her voice. She didn't need to. In that tent, over that wind, she was the loudest thing in the Kestrels.

"Those scans," she said, stepping forward, "include the hollow. The aquifer. The old survey lines my father walked for thirty years. Somebody sold that."

And here is the thing about being stripped down to nothing in front of the person who once knew every honest bone in your body: there is nowhere left to stand.

"Jude," I said.

"Somebody sold it," she said again, quieter, which was worse. "Somebody who swore—" She stopped. Looked at me. And I watched it land, watched the arithmetic finish behind her eyes, watched eight years of grief reorganize itself around a new and better reason to hate me. "You sold the valley, Sebastian. You sold *my father's* lines. To pay for your running-away money."

"It was before," I said. "It was years ago, it was—"

"It was always going to be this." Her voice didn't shake. I almost wished it would. Shaking would have meant I could still reach her. "Every map you ever drew, every promise. You put a price on all of it and then you left before anyone could read the invoice."

"Jude—"

"Don't." She held up a hand, and I swear the storm outside went quiet out of respect. "Don't say my name like it's yours."

Corvus cleared his throat, gentle as a headsman. "Touching. Truly. But the weather's turning meaner, and we have a schedule. Ms. Quill — the case."

Mara reached for it.

And I heard myself say, "No."

The word came out small in all that wind, but it came out mine.

Corvus looked at me with something like curiosity. "Mr. Coldharbour. Your debt is forty thousand crowns. Your crew is unpaid. This storm has closed both passes — nobody goes anywhere for two days minimum, and we have food for one. Refuse, and I simply wait. The mountain will negotiate for me. It always does."

He was right. That was the terrible thing. He was right, and the math was right, and the math had been winning my whole life.

But Jude was standing there with her father's survey lines wrapped around her like a coat, and Priya was watching me the way I'd once watched older, braver fools, and somewhere under the wind was a valley where my brother's name was still spoken, and I thought: *you have spent eight years paying for leaving, and it hasn't covered a copper of it.*

So I did the only thing I'd never tried.

I stopped.

"No," I said again, louder. "The data stays. The crew stays whole. Take it up with the mountain."

Corvus sighed, and nodded to the man on his left, and the man began, very calmly, to douse the cook stove.

Outside, the blizzard screamed like it had been waiting all day for permission.

And Jude Ashcombe looked at me — furious, betrayed, snow-melt dripping off her jaw — and mouthed one word that I chose, in that moment, to hear as a question instead of a verdict:

"*Why?*"
