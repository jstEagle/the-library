## 1. Freight and Rumors

The fog came off the water at Kettle Landing like something being poured, slow and deliberate, and Odile Winterbourne watched it swallow the end of her runway while she counted crates.

Nine of them. Insulin in the foam-lined ones, penicillin and sutures in the rest, all of it stamped with the provincial health authority's seal and all of it three weeks late because the barge had turned back at Cape Merrow twice before anyone thought to ask a bush pilot who didn't mind weather. She'd flown it up from Ternwater that morning through a sky the color of dishwater, and now she stood on the gravel strip with her collar up and her breath making small ghosts, doing the arithmetic she always did: fuel burned against freight carried, hours logged against money owed, and underneath all of it, the older sum she never quite finished — another season gone, another winter coming, and still no reason to land anywhere for longer than it took to unload.

"Odile." Marta Kettle came down the boardwalk with her coat open despite the cold, a woman built like the pilings that held her town above the tide, gray and salt-cured and immovable. "You got my message about the extra crate?"

"You said nine."

"I said nine." Marta stopped beside the last crate and put her boot on it, not quite standing on the lid, just claiming it. "This one isn't mine. Came in on yesterday's mail boat with your name on the manifest."

"My name doesn't go on manifests. My name goes on invoices, after the money clears."

"Well, somebody knows the difference." Marta crouched and turned the crate so the stencil faced the light. O. WINTERBOURNE — C/O KETTLE LANDING AERODROME. Below that, no return address, only a single word stenciled smaller: *PASSENGER.*

Odile looked at it for a long moment. The fog moved behind her, filling in the runway, erasing the world one yard at a time.

"I don't take passengers," she said.

"You take whoever pays," Marta said. "That's different, and we both know it."

* * *

She left the crate in Marta's dry shed and walked into town to eat something hot and hear what there was to hear, which was the other reason pilots stopped in Kettle Landing, though nobody admitted it. The Landing had one café, the Tide Table, and the Tide Table had one rule posted over the counter in Marta's dead husband's handwriting: NO POLITICS BEFORE THE COFFEE'S ON. It was eleven o'clock, the coffee was on, and politics were in full flood.

"—third survey team this month," a trapper named Corrigan was saying as Odile took the corner table. He was a big man gone soft at the middle, with hands like cured leather. "Staking ground up the Sorel headwaters. Ground that's been trapped, fished, and buried on for sixty years, and now some outfit with a Vancouver letterhead says it's theirs."

"It's not an outfit," said the schoolteacher, a thin woman named Pryce who corrected everyone including herself. "It's the Halvard Consortium. And they're not saying it's theirs. They're filing mineral claims under the 1911 Act, which is worse, because it means they don't have to say anything at all until the drills arrive."

"The magistrate would've had something to say about it," Corrigan muttered, and the room did the thing it always did when someone mentioned the magistrate, which was to go quiet in a way that wasn't silence but a held breath, thirty people listening without appearing to listen.

Odile stirred her coffee. She'd been flying this range for eleven years and the magistrate was in every conversation somewhere beneath the surface, the way bedrock is beneath soil. Amos Vane, they called him, or just the Magistrate, a man who'd ridden circuit through these valleys forty years ago with a ledger instead of a gavel and settled disputes the courts wouldn't touch — water rights, stolen traplines, a mill owner who'd shorted his crews for a decade. Then he'd vanished, and the range had spent two generations arguing about whether he'd been a saint or a tyrant, which usually meant both.

"Magistrate's dead," said a young man Odile didn't know, sitting near the stove with the careful stillness of someone trying not to be noticed and failing at it. "Dead men don't stake claims either way."

Corrigan snorted. "And what would you know about it, boy?"

"More than you'd think." The young man turned then, and Odile saw him properly for the first time: mid-twenties maybe, city clothes worn wrong for the country, a canvas duffel at his feet that he kept one hand on the way other men keep a hand on a rifle. His eyes went around the room once, cataloguing exits, and snagged on hers for half a second too long.

She looked away first. That was a skill, looking away first, and she'd had a lot of practice.

* * *

He found her at the aerodrome an hour later, when the fog had thinned to rags and she was halfway through her preflight, wiping the moisture off the Beaver's struts with a chamois because water froze on them at altitude and ice on a strut was a small death waiting for a convenient moment.

"Ms. Winterbourne."

"Pilots don't get called miss. It's Odile or it's nothing."

"Odile, then." He stopped a polite distance from the wing, close enough to talk, far enough that she couldn't pretend she hadn't assessed him. Up close he looked tired in the specific way of a man who hadn't slept indoors in a while. "I'm told you fly anywhere in the range. Is that true?"

"It's true I fly where I'm paid to fly. Whether that's anywhere depends on your definition and my fuel."

"I need to go up the coast. North of Cape Merrow. There's—" He paused, and she watched him choose his words with visible effort, a man translating from a language he wasn't sure she spoke. "There's a place I'm looking for. An old sea cave. The charts don't show it."

"Charts don't show a lot of things."

"This one shows even less than most. It's only accessible at low tide, and only certain times of year. The locals used to say—" He caught himself. "Some people say it appears twice a century. That the tide opens it and closes it like a door."

Odile set down the chamois. The wind came off the water and moved the fog around them in slow ropes, and somewhere behind the fog the surf worked the beach with its patient sound, in and out, in and out, a door opening and closing.

"That's a story," she said. "Stories are free. Fuel is four dollars a gallon."

He reached into his coat and brought out a drawstring pouch of soft leather, old leather, the kind that comes from something that lived a long time. He loosened the string and tipped a little of the contents into his palm: gold dust, fine as flour, dull yellow in the flat light. Maybe two ounces of it, maybe more, sitting in his hand like something that had been buried a long time and not by him.

"Will that cover fuel?" he asked.

She looked at the gold, then at his face. He was watching her the way people watch a lock they're trying to pick — patiently, hopefully, with a plan already made if hope fails.

"Where'd you get that?"

"Around."

"*Around* is not an answer I can log."

"Then log it as payment received." He closed his fist over the dust. "Look. I'll be straight with you, because I think you're a woman who'd rather be warned than flattered. There are men looking for the same place I am. They'll be asking questions in towns along the coast, maybe already have. If they find me before I find it, that's my problem. But I need to be north of Cape Merrow by tomorrow's low tide, and there are exactly two ways to get there — a week's walk through country that wants you dead, or you."

"And why me? There are other pilots."

"There are three other pilots in the range, and two of them drink, and the third is Halvard's cousin." He said it flatly, without drama, and she believed him, because she'd done the same math years ago and arrived at the same number. "You're the only one who flies like the weather owes you money."

That almost got a laugh out of her, and the fact that it almost did unsettled her more than the gold.

* * *

She told him she'd think about it, which was a lie, because she'd already decided, and they both knew it, and the knowing sat between them on the walk back to the café like a third passenger. She bought him dinner at the Tide Table because he looked like a man who'd been eating out of cans, and over the food he gave her a name — Callum — and a story about being a historian, and she listened with the particular attention of a woman who has heard a great many stories from a great many passengers and learned that the truth is usually hiding inside the lie like a seed inside fruit.

A historian. Sure. Historians paid in gold dust. Historians who watched doors. Historians who needed to reach a cave that appeared twice a century by tomorrow's low tide, as if the cave had sent him an invitation.

But here was the thing she couldn't explain to Marta later, or to herself, or to the ceiling of whatever bunkhouse she slept in next: she wanted to go. Not for the fare. Not even for the mystery, though God knew the mystery was working on her like current works on a hull. She wanted to go because staying was the thing she'd never learned, because every town she landed in became a countdown, because the longest she'd ever lived anywhere was eight months in Ternwater and she'd left that place in the night with a note on the hangar door and a debt she'd paid off by flying freight through two bad winters.

Her father had been the same way. That was the ugly truth under it. Elias Winterbourne, who could not sit in a room with a closed door, who moved them eleven times before Odile turned fourteen, who taught her to fly and then flew away himself in every sense but the literal, and died literally doing it, up in the pass, in weather every other pilot had grounded through. She'd inherited his hands and his restlessness and his habit of calling flight freedom when any honest person would call it running.

Stopping costs more, she told herself. Stopping costs everything. You stop, you root, you build something, and then the world comes along and takes it, and you learn what it is to lose a thing with your name carved on it. Better to own nothing but a plane and a route. Better to be weather yourself, passing over everything, touching nothing.

Except that the gold dust in Callum's pouch had been old. Buried old. And the word *passenger* on that crate had been stenciled by someone who knew her name before she'd ever seen his face, and she had loaded it into her hold without asking a single question, because that was who she was now: a woman who ferried medicine and secrets across the interior and had stopped pretending there was much difference between them.

* * *

Dawn came gray and windless, the kind of false calm that on this coast meant the atmosphere was holding its breath. Odile stood at the open door of the hangar with a mug going cold in her hand and watched the barometer on the wall, the old brass one her father had given her, its needle sitting a hair lower than it had any business sitting in October.

Marta found her there. The old woman looked at the plane, at the young man already stowing his duffel in the rear seat with the efficiency of someone who'd loaded boats before, and then at Odile, and her face did something complicated.

"You're taking him," Marta said. Not a question.

"Fare's a fare."

"Fare's a fare," Marta agreed. "And that boy is trouble wearing a clean shirt. I've run this landing thirty years, Odile. I know what trouble looks like getting on a plane." She paused. "There's talk in town since yesterday. Men came through on the mail boat asking after pilots. Asking after *you*, specifically. Big fellows, company manners, paid for their coffee in crisp bills and left before the fish trucks came."

Odile felt the cold move through her that had nothing to do with the morning. "What did you tell them?"

"What you'd expect. That you come and go like weather and nobody keeps your schedule, least of all you." Marta's eyes were steady. "But they wrote down your tail number, honey. Off the side of your own plane, while it sat right there. So whatever you're carrying north — and I know it isn't just that boy, because nothing is ever just anything with you — carry it fast."

Odile drained the cold coffee and set the mug on the piling. Above the tree line to the northwest, past Cape Merrow, the sky had a bruised look to it, a green-gray band low on the horizon that her father would have called a *storm's promise* and every pilot on the coast would call what it actually was: an early winter, arriving ahead of schedule, the way trouble does.

She climbed into the cockpit, ran the checks, and brought the engine up to a roar that scattered gulls off the pilings. Callum settled into the rear seat and plugged in the headset, and through the intercom, over the engine noise, he said only one thing:

"Low tide's at midafternoon. We should hurry."

She swung the Beaver onto the strip, into the wind, and took the fog's remaining rag of visibility as a personal challenge. The wheels left the gravel. Kettle Landing fell away beneath her — the boardwalk, the Tide Table, Marta's raised hand, the whole small stubborn town holding itself above the tide on its pilings — and Odile banked north along the coast with a stranger's gold dust in her cash box and a storm building behind the horizon like a debt coming due.

Below her, the sea worked at the shore. In and out. In and out.

Somewhere ahead, a door was getting ready to open.
