## 2. The Tide's Toll

The storm came out of the northwest like something with a grudge.

Odile saw it first as a bruise on the horizon while she was still over the tree line, forty minutes out from Kettle Landing with an empty cargo bay and a fuel gauge that read half. By the time she'd turned the nose of the Cessna toward the coast, the bruise had grown teeth — a wall of cloud the color of wet slate, dragging its skirt of snow across the water in long gray veils. The radio gave her nothing but static and a weather advisory that had been recorded, judging by its tone, by someone who had already given up on the day.

"Early," she said to the plane, because saying things aloud was a habit she'd never managed to break, and the empty cockpit made it easy. "Nobody said anything about early."

The forecast had promised the front for Thursday. It was Tuesday. In the interior, weather kept appointments the way her passengers did — loosely, and usually with excuses — but this wasn't lateness. This was the season arriving ahead of itself, the first snows coming down off the high country like creditors who'd stopped waiting politely at the door.

She ran the numbers the way she always did, fast and without sentiment. Kettle Landing behind her, ninety minutes back through worsening air. The mining strip at Corben's Flats to the east, closed since October and probably drifted shut besides. And ahead, along the coast, the long gravel shelf below the old light station — wide enough, flat enough, if the wind would only quit shoving her sideways like a drunk in a doorway.

The wind did not quit. But it hesitated, once, for about eleven seconds, and Odile took those eleven seconds and spent them all at once, dropping the little plane through two hundred feet of turbulence and setting it down on the gravel with a bounce, a second bounce, and then a long grinding roll that ended with both wingtips trembling and her heart going like a sewing machine.

Then the snow arrived, and the world went white, and there was nothing left to do but sit in the shaking fuselage and listen to it come down.

* * *

She tied the plane down twice, using every strap she owned, and then stood in the driving white and looked at her work with the professional skepticism of someone who had once watched a storm peel a tied-down Beaver off a beach like a child opening a present. The Cessna was light. The Cessna was a kite with delusions of being a boat. If the wind swung around to the south and came off the water, no amount of strapping was going to matter.

Below the shelf, the sea was working itself into a fury. She could hear it even through the snow — a deep, rhythmic concussion, wave after wave hitting the base of the headland with the patience of something that had been doing this for ten thousand years and intended to continue.

There was a sea arch down there. She remembered it from summer runs, when the coast showed itself green and the water lay flat as poured glass: a natural bridge of gray stone thrusting out from the cliff, big enough that she'd once watched a fishing boat motor straight through it, the helmsman raising two fingers off the wheel in lazy salute. In summer it was a curiosity. In winter, with the tide running high and the swell up, it would be a place where the ocean went to break things.

But it was also shelter. Behind the arch, if she remembered the geometry right, the cliff hollowed back into a shallow overhang — dry-ish, out of the worst of the wind, close enough to the plane that she could check on it between squalls. Better than sitting in the fuselage waiting for the straps to fail, and better than trying to walk anywhere in this.

She packed a bag with the deliberation of habit: rope, tarp, stove, the dented thermos of coffee that had survived four years and two engine failures. Headlamp. And — after a moment, because the weight was nothing and the coast was empty and you never knew — the flare gun and three flares, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp.

The climb down took twenty minutes and cost her the skin on both palms. The snow turned to sleet halfway, then to cold rain, then back to snow, as though the storm couldn't decide what it wanted to throw at her and so threw everything. By the time she dropped the last six feet onto the wave-cut platform at the base of the cliff, she was soaked to the sternum and breathing hard, and the arch loomed above her like the ribcage of something enormous and long dead.

It was bigger than she remembered. That was the thing about the coast — everything shrank in memory until you came back and it reminded you how small you were. The arch rose a good sixty feet, black stone streaked white with bird lime, and through its span she could see the sea heaving, green-gray and furious, exploding against the far side in columns of spray.

Behind it, the overhang. She found it by feel more than sight, ducking under a lip of rock into a hollow maybe thirty feet deep, floored with coarse sand and old storm wrack — bleached logs, tangles of kelp, a fish crate with its slats gone soft as cloth. The wind still reached her here, but thinned, baffled, spending most of its anger on the stone above her head. She got the tarp strung, the stove lit inside a ring of stones some previous castaway had left, and sat with her boots steaming and the coffee going, and let the storm have its tantrum.

That was when the tide began to go out.

* * *

She noticed it the way you notice anything on a coast in bad weather — gradually, then all at once. The waves were still hitting the arch, but lower. The foam line crept seaward, foot by foot, uncovering first the dark apron of rock below the platform, then a shelf of cobbles, then something else.

A floor. A worked floor.

Odile set down the thermos and leaned forward into the gray light, frowning. At first she took it for a natural terrace — the coast here was all basalt and upheaval, stone laid down in sheets by lava and lifted by frost. But the lines were too regular. Long joints running parallel, crossed at intervals by shorter ones, the whole surface sloping gently down toward the retreating water like a road, or a ramp, or —

Steps. Three of them, shallow and broad, cut clean, their edges softened by centuries of salt but unmistakable. Steps did not happen by accident. Steps meant hands. Hands meant people, and people meant a reason, and a reason meant that somewhere under the headland, hidden behind the arch where no boat would ever think to look, there was a door.

No chart admitted any such thing. She'd flown this coast for four years; she'd studied the charts the way other people studied scripture, because out here the charts were scripture, the difference between the ink and the truth being the difference between landing and not landing. There was nothing here. The headland was marked as solid to the waterline. No cave, no inlet, no notation of any kind.

And yet the ebb kept pulling back, and the sea kept giving ground, and there at the base of the cliff, revealed inch by inch like a secret being pried loose, was a mouth.

It was low and wide, half-hidden behind a shoulder of rock so that you could stand fifty feet away and miss it entirely unless the light came at it from exactly the wrong angle — which, just now, it did, the storm tearing a hole in the cloud cover to the west and throwing a flat pewter gleam across the wet stone. The mouth was maybe eight feet tall, its lintel a single slab of granite that some hand had squared, and beyond it the darkness went back and back, drinking the light.

Odile sat very still and listened to the storm and told herself the sensible things.

You are alone on an uninhabited coast in the first storm of winter. Your plane is tied down with hope and nylon. You have food for two days and fuel for one night of stove. The sensible thing is to stay under the tarp, drink your coffee, and let the tide do whatever the tide does. The sensible thing is to remember that caves on forgotten coasts are not adventures; they are ways of dying that take longer than falling.

She drank her coffee anyway. She watched the mouth. The tide kept falling.

Here was the trouble, and she knew it, and had known it for years, and had stopped pretending otherwise somewhere over the Mackenzie country one bad night: Odile Winterbourne could not leave a door unopened. Not a literal door, not a figurative one, not a question or a rumor or a locked drawer in a dead man's desk. Stopping cost more — she'd said that to herself so often it had worn smooth as river stone — but it wasn't stopping that cost. It was the not-knowing. The knowing-that-she-could-have-known and didn't. Her whole life was built on movement, sure, but underneath the movement, under the ferrying and the flying and the perpetual leaving, there was this: a woman walking toward every closed door on the continent, unable to pass one by.

Her father had been the same way. That was the thought that surfaced, unwelcome, as she laced her boots. He'd been a quiet man, careful, a bookkeeper for the consortium in the years before anyone called them that, and everyone had assumed his silences were contentment. They weren't. They were doors, dozens of them, all locked from the inside, and one morning in April he had opened the last one and stepped through, and Odile had been running ever since — from that house, that silence, that particular way of leaving.

She stood up. The storm shouted. The cave mouth waited, patient as arithmetic.

"Oh, hell," she said, and reached for the flare gun.

* * *

The approach was worse than it looked. The cobbles were iced, and the last stretch of the worked floor ran slick with spray, and twice she went down to one knee and once nearly all the way over, catching herself on the granite lintel with fingers gone numb as meat. Up close the masonry was more impressive and more unsettling — the blocks fitted without mortar, tight as teeth, and along the top of the arch someone had cut a line of characters that the salt had half-eaten. She swept her glove across them. Shapes. Old ones. Not English, not anything she recognized, though the middle glyph had the look of a balance scale, or a bird with folded wings.

The darkness inside smelled of stone and salt and something older — a mineral coldness, like the air off a glacier, like the inside of the earth holding its breath.

She clicked on the headlamp. The beam jumped out, thin and white, and found a passage going back at a gentle downward slope, walls of dressed stone giving way after twenty feet to raw rock, the work of men ending where the mountain's own work began. Water dripped somewhere ahead, each drop landing with a sound like a struck bell.

"Hello?" she called, and heard the word come back to her doubled and tripled, fading down the throat of the passage, and heard nothing answer.

She should have turned around then. She knew that too. Every rule she lived by — and she lived by few, which made the few load-bearing — said you did not enter unknown ground alone, in a storm, at the turn of a tide, with no one knowing where you were. The tide would come back. The tide always came back. Whatever this passage was, however deep it went, the sea would reclaim its door within hours, and if she was inside when it did —

Against her better judgment. That was the phrase, and she held it up and examined it honestly, standing on the threshold with the wind moaning through the arch behind her and the dark breathing cold against her face. Against her better judgment was where she had made every decision that mattered in her life. Taking the fare in gold dust. Leaving Kettle Landing. Leaving home. Flying into weather that sensible pilots sat out, carrying medicine to people who might be dead before she arrived, because the alternative — the grounded, prudent, tethered life — was a kind of death she feared more.

She fired the first flare into the passage.

It went off with a crack and a hiss, and the red light leapt away from her, bouncing, painting the walls in pulses of blood-color, and for three full seconds she saw the shape of the place: a corridor widening into a chamber, the chamber opening into further dark, and on the walls — carved, hundreds of them, thousands, receding into the distance like a congregation — marks. Lines of them. Columns of them. Writing, or the ancestor of writing, covering the stone from floor to ceiling the way frost covers a window.

Then the flare died, and the dark came back, and Odile Winterbourne stood in a cave that did not exist, on a coast no chart admitted to, looking at a wall covered in somebody's record of something, and felt the feeling she knew best in all the world settle over her like a coat: the cold bright terror of having gone too far to turn around cheaply.

The tide was still going out. She checked her watch, did the arithmetic — four hours, maybe five, before the water started climbing toward the door — and told herself she'd go as far as the next bend. Just the next bend. Just to see.

The passage bent. Beyond the bend it went on.

Somewhere deep in the stone, faint as a memory, she heard a sound she would spend the rest of the night failing to explain: small, rhythmic, deliberate. Like a chisel. Like someone, far down in the dark, keeping count.
