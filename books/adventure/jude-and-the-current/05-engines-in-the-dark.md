## 5. Engines in the Dark

The sound came up the beach like a rumor of wolves.

Odile was halfway through the chest's second drawer when she heard it — a thin, two-stroke whine riding under the crash of surf, rising and falling in that particular way engines rise and fall when they're hunting for a way through. She'd spent eleven years listening to machines across open country. She knew the difference between an engine going somewhere and an engine looking.

These were looking.

"Kill your light," she whispered.

Jude didn't move at first. He knelt where she'd left him, one hand flat on the ledger chest as if it were a sleeping animal he could calm by touch, and his face in the spill of her headlamp was all cheekbone and watchfulness — a boy built out of patience and salt air. Then the lamp clicked off and the dark came down so complete it felt like water closing over her head.

She counted her breathing until her eyes gave up nothing and started inventing things instead: gray ghosts, drifting motes, the suggestion of stone ribs overhead. The cave breathed around them. Somewhere behind the walls, water moved through channels older than any map she'd ever flown with, and the sound of it made the whole chamber feel like the inside of a lung.

The engine note swelled. Fell away. Swelled again — closer now, or maybe just clearer, carrying off the wet sand the way sound carries off a lake at dawn.

"They've found the Cub," she said quietly. It wasn't a question. A downed plane on an empty beach was a signature written large; there was no missing it, not even in the dark, not even in the rain still ticking down from the storm's ragged tail.

"You know them?" Jude asked. His voice was low and even, and that steadiness frightened her more than the engines did. Boys who sounded like that had been afraid before, often enough to learn how to hold it.

"I know what pays them." She kept her hand on the chest's edge, orienting herself. "Consortium runs security out of Cold Harbour. Contract men, mostly ex-constabulary, mostly the kind who washed out for cause." She thought of the gold dust in the tin cup back at Kettle Landing, the passenger with the soft voice and the questions about caves that appear twice a century. *You were the bait,* she realized, and the thought landed cold in her stomach. Or the boy was. Either way, someone had drawn a line from Kettle Landing to this coast faster than weather should have allowed. "They tracked my emergency beacon, most likely. Or they were already coming."

"For me."

"For whatever's in that box. You happen to be attached to it."

He almost smiled. She heard it rather than saw it — a small exhale through the nose, the ghost of humor in a place that had no business hosting any. "It's been attached to me longer."

* * *

The engines stopped.

That was worse. Idling engines meant deliberation. Silence meant decision. Odile pictured them out there on the black sand: three, maybe four sleds, headlamps sweeping the tide line, finding the skid gouges where her wheels had kissed too hard, following them to the Cub's hunched silhouette against the storm-scoured sky. Finding the door hanging open because she'd been in too much of a hurry to latch it. Finding—

Her flare gun was in her coat pocket. One cartridge. Her rifle was in the plane, which might as well have been on the moon.

"Is there another way out of here?" she asked.

Jude was quiet long enough that she thought he hadn't heard. Then: "Why do you ask like you already know there is?"

"Because caves that hide ledgers don't have one door. Keepers don't build their lives into a trap." She said it with more confidence than she felt, but she'd learned long ago that confidence was a tool like any other — useless if you waited until you were sure you needed it.

Another silence. Water dripped somewhere deep in the dark, patient as a clock. Out on the beach, faintly, voices — two of them, flattened by wind, the words stripped away but the tone unmistakable. Men giving orders. Men who expected to be obeyed.

"There's a passage," Jude said finally. "The old keepers called it the Eel Run. It goes up and back through the headland and comes out above the high-tide line on the far side of the point. My keeper made me swim it when I was nine." A pause. "In winter."

"And it floods?"

"When the tide comes back, the low sections fill first. We have—" He stopped. She heard him doing arithmetic in the dark, the same arithmetic she was doing. The storm surge had been ebbing for six hours, maybe seven. Which meant the turn was close. An hour, perhaps less, before the sea began reclaiming everything it had lent the land. "We have time," he said, in the tone of a boy who had been taught never to lie about tides. "If we go now."

"Then we go now." She lifted the chest. It was heavier than she'd guessed — forty pounds, fifty, waterproofed wood and iron fittings worn smooth by generations of hands. "I'll carry—"

"No." His hands closed over hers on the handles, small hands, rope-hard. For a moment they stood like that, two strangers gripping the same box in absolute darkness, and she felt the whole strange architecture of the night settle between them: her plane wrecked on a beach, his life's work between her fingers, engines cooling somewhere in the rain. "You don't know the ground. I can't run it blind. We carry it together or we lose it."

Together, then. They lifted, and the weight distributed itself into something bearable, something shared, and Odile thought — absurdly, in the teeth of everything — that it had been years since she'd carried anything with anyone.

* * *

Jude moved through the dark like it was a room he'd furnished himself.

He'd taken her spare headlamp — she had two, because a pilot who ran the interior alone learned redundancy or didn't stay alive to learn anything else — and set it to its lowest red glow, a coal of light barely strong enough to make shadows. He kept it aimed at the floor. "Light draws eyes," he said. "Even in here. Especially in here." And he walked.

She followed the red coal and the sound of his feet, the chest swinging between them on improvised carries — her jacket rolled through the handles, gripped fist over fist. The main chamber fell away behind them and the cave narrowed, the ceiling dropping until she felt it press at the crown of her head, then opening again into a corridor of wet stone that bent back into the headland like a swallowed breath. The air changed. Salt gave way to something colder, mineral, ancient — the smell of rock that had never seen sun.

"How far?" she managed.

"Half a mile. Maybe more. The keepers measured it in candles." A dry note entered his voice. "Forty candles to the first sump. Sixty to the exit. I never understood why they counted in candles until I tried carrying a lamp through the narrows and understood exactly how much light you can afford to spend."

Behind them, far back through the stone, a new sound: boots on rock. Echoing wrong, multiplying, so that four men sounded like twelve and twelve sounded like a landslide. A beam of white light stabbed past the mouth of the corridor they'd left — she caught it only as a brief bloom on the wet wall ahead of them, there and gone, like heat lightning.

"They're in," she breathed.

"They'll find the main chamber. They'll find the hearth. They'll waste twenty minutes deciding whether the smoke stains mean anyone lives here." Jude said it without pride, but the pride was there anyway, buried in the certainty. "My keeper drilled the timing into me. Men in caves are slow. Caves make them careful, and careful is slow, and slow is time."

"And after the twenty minutes?"

"After the twenty minutes they find the Eel Run, and then it's a race." He glanced back at her, the red lamp catching the edge of his jaw. "So let's not be in a race."

* * *

They ran when the floor allowed it and climbed when it didn't, and Odile discovered that fourteen-year-olds raised in sea caves possessed a vocabulary of movement she had never learned. Jude read the stone the way she read sky. Where she saw uniform black rock he saw footholds, hand-lines worn into the limestone by a century of keepers' palms, polished hollows where knees had braced ten thousand times. "Left foot here," he'd say, tapping. "There's a lip. Trust the lip, not the wall — the wall crumbles." And the lip held. It always held. Generations of dead keepers holding her weight from beyond the grave, and something about that steadied her more than any prayer would have.

Twice the passage dropped into sumps — low tunnels where the ebb had drained the water out but left the floors glazed with wet sand and stranded kelp. In the first, the ceiling came down to eighteen inches of air and they floated the chest, pushing it ahead of them while they half-swam, half-crawled, the cold punching the breath out of her, her broken-in flight boots filling with seawater that felt like needles. In the second the water was deeper and the air pocket smaller, and she had to duck fully under for three strokes with her lungs locked and the dark pressing on her eyeballs, and when she surfaced on the far side gasping, Jude's hand was there on her collar, hauling her onto the shelf.

"Breathe," he said. "Slow. You breathe fast down here, you use the air twice as quick."

"That," she wheezed, "is the worst advice—"

"It's the best advice. Panic spends oxygen like a drunk spends wages." He was already moving, hauling the chest up beside her. "My keeper said that too. He said most people who die in caves die of hurry."

Somewhere behind them — muffled now, bent through a mile of stone — a shout. Then another. Then the unmistakable crack of a flare being fired indoors, and a harsh white glare came washing up the passage behind them, throwing their shadows huge and trembling down the tunnel ahead.

"They found the Run," Jude said. All the calm had gone out of his voice, replaced by something tighter. "Move. Move *now.*"

* * *

What followed, Odile would remember afterward in fragments, the way pilots remember bad weather — not as a continuous story but as a series of instants burned separately into the film.

The chest banging against limestone as they rounded a bend too fast. Her shoulder hitting the wall and the pain arriving late, politely, like a guest. Jude ahead of her, red lamp bouncing, shouting depths and distances in a language of numbers — *thirty candles, twenty-five, the sump's next, air's good, air's good* — and her body obeying him without consulting her, because her body had decided somewhere in the last hour that this feral boy knew things she didn't, and bodies are wiser than pride.

The sound of the sea growing. Not the muffled heartbeat of it inside the cave but the real thing, close and enormous, and she understood before he said it: the tide had turned. Somewhere behind them and below them, the ocean was walking back up its own throat, filling the low country inch by inch, erasing the road behind them at the exact speed of their escape.

"The sump will be flooded!" she shouted.

"It floods to the roof for six minutes at peak! We beat the peak or we drown — those are the terms!" He wasn't scared anymore. That was the terrible thing, the thing she turned over later: he sounded *alive*, lit through, a boy running the only race he'd ever been trained for. "Drop the chest!"

"What?"

"We can't float it through a full sump — it'll catch on the ceiling and pin us! We push it through ahead of the water, wedge it in the high pocket past the narrows, come back for it if we have to!" Even in the red glow she could see the cost of the words on his face. This was the ledger. This was every injustice the law refused to touch, every name, every debt, every crime written down by dead men so the living couldn't pretend. And he was telling her to leave it in a flooding hole in the earth.

"Do you trust the pocket?"

"I've slept in it."

"Then we wedge it and we run."

They wedged it — jammed into a limestone hollow eight feet above the sump's low point, chocked with driftwood that some long-dead keeper had left there for exactly this purpose, because keepers left things everywhere, a hundred years of foresight stacked in the dark like provisions. Jude's hands shook as he drove the last stick home. Hers didn't, and she noticed that, filed it away, wondered briefly what it said about her that the boy guarded the past and the woman guarded the exit.

Then the water reached them.

It came up the tunnel fast and silent, faster than any river she'd ever run, a black tongue licking over their boots, their knees, and the temperature of it closed her throat. Jude grabbed her wrist. "Air pocket's at the far end — thirty feet, ceiling drops to a foot, you go under and *keep going*, don't stop, don't come up early, there's a shelf on the left and if you surface anywhere else you'll break your skull. Do you hear me? Do you *hear* me?"

"Thirty feet. Left shelf. Don't stop."

"Go on three. One—"

He went on two. She went half a stroke behind him, and the water took them both.

Thirty feet underwater in absolute darkness, in cold that felt like a hand squeezing her heart into a smaller and smaller shape, following the pale ghost of a boy's heels and the memory of his voice — *left shelf, don't stop* — and somewhere in the middle of it her lungs began screaming and every animal instinct she owned voted to surface, and she overruled them all, one by one, because a fourteen-year-old had told her the truth and she had chosen, in the oldest part of herself, to believe him.

The shelf scraped her shoulder. She surfaced into a bell of air the size of a closet, coughing, alive, and a moment later Jude broke the surface beside her, dragging the chest — he'd gone back for it, wedged it loose and pushed it through behind him in the flooded narrows, and she wanted to scream at him and hug him and had no breath for either.

The air pocket connected to a chimney. The chimney climbed. And climbing it, hand over hand up wet stone with the sea booming below and enforcers' lights flickering in the flooded tunnel beneath them — too late, always too late, the cave giving its secrets only to those who'd paid for them in years — Odile started, helplessly, to laugh.

Jude looked at her like she'd gone mad.

"Sixty candles," she gasped. "Your keeper counted in candles. I count in fuel reserves. We're the same kind of stupid, you and I."

Something cracked in his face — not breaking, opening — and he laughed too, once, sharp and startled, a sound that had probably not been used in this cave since his keeper died. Then he pointed up the chimney, where a thread of gray light showed between stones. Real light. Sky light.

"That's the exit," he said. "Above the high-tide line. Past the point there's an old trapper trail that climbs to the alder bench, and from the bench—" He stopped. Listened. Far below and behind, muffled by water and stone, an engine turned over, turned again, and died. Men regrouping. Men thinking.

"From the bench?" Odile prompted.

"From the bench, we're ghosts till morning." He looked at her, and the laughter was gone but something else remained in its place, something settled and deliberate. "You have a plane on a beach and men with guns around it. I have a chest full of paper and nowhere in the world that's safe. I think we need to talk terms, pilot."

Above them the gray thread widened, and the first clean cold air of the outside world came down the chimney smelling of snow.

"*Terms,*" Odile repeated, and pulled herself up toward the light.
