## 6. Terms of Passage

The secondary passage let them out a mile up the coast, through a crack in the limestone no wider than a coffin lid, and Jude came out of it into grey daylight with the chest strapped to his back and salt water in his boots. Behind them, the sea was already climbing back into its cave. He could hear it — that low swallowing sound the tide made when it took the entrance back, like a door closing in a house where someone had just died.

Odile Winterbourne stood at the lip of the rocks with her headlamp still on, though the light was useless now, a pale coin floating in all that grey. She was watching the water come back over the arch. Her plane sat on the shingle two hundred yards south, nose-down in the gravel where the storm had shoved it, one wingtip bent like a finger hooked over the edge of the world.

"They'll find the plane," she said. Not a question.

"They'll find where the plane *was*," Jude said. "The tide takes the rest."

She turned and looked at him properly for the first time since the passage — looked at him the way people look at things they've pulled out of a wreck, half relief and half suspicion. Fourteen years old, soaked to the ribs, carrying a chest older than the province. He knew what he looked like. The keeper had told him once: *You'll always look like something the mountain coughed up, boy. Make them work past it.*

"Your name's Jude."

"You knew that already." He'd heard her say it down there in the dark, reading it off the flyleaf of the keeper's copy-book before he'd put the flare between them. "You read my name before you asked it. That's not asking. That's checking."

Something moved across her face — not quite a smile. "Fair. Jude, then. I'm Odile, but you know that too, because I introduced myself like a normal person while you were pointing a flare gun at me."

"It wasn't loaded."

"I didn't know that either."

* * *

They walked south along the shingle because standing still on an open beach with snowmobile engines somewhere behind the headland was a way of dying slowly instead of quickly. Jude set the pace — not fast, but never stopping, the way the keeper had taught him to move in broken country: *the land forgives motion, it punishes hesitation.* Odile kept up without complaint, which surprised him. Bush pilots, he'd been told, were either patient as stone or restless as water, and he'd assumed she was water. But she matched his rhythm over the slick cobbles like she'd grown up on them.

The chest rode his spine and he felt every step of it. Forty pounds of oilcloth and cedar and iron corners, and inside it, eleven hundred pages of everything the law had refused to touch for ninety years.

She tried twice more before they stopped.

The first time was at the mouth of a gully where a creek came down off the range, brown with glacier flour, loud enough to cover voices. She crouched beside it, filled the dented kettle from her survival kit, and said, casually, the way you'd ask about weather: "How much does it weigh? The ledger."

"Forty pounds."

"And how much of it is copies?"

"All of it is copies." He drank when she handed him the kettle. "The originals went into the ground with Keeper Aldous in 'sixty-one. What's in the chest is the working record. Every entry copied out by hand by whoever kept the cave. Three hands, mostly. Mine makes four."

"So if I took the chest—"

"You'd have paper." He said it flat, no challenge in it, because the keeper had taught him that too: *never argue from anger, boy; argue from arithmetic.* "Paper burns. Paper sinks. Paper tells a magistrate nothing about where the next copy sleeps. You'd be carrying forty pounds of leverage that stops being leverage the moment you use it wrong."

Her eyes narrowed. Not angry. Recalculating. He was learning her face the way he'd learned weather — the small fronts moving across it.

The second time was worse. They'd climbed out of the gully into scrub pine, and the wind had come around from the northeast with a wet iron smell in it, and she stopped under a leaning spruce and said, quietly, without any casualness left at all:

"Jude. Those men are going to kill you for that box. You understand that. Whatever's in it, whatever your old keeper told you it meant — they will shoot a fourteen-year-old boy on a beach and sleep fine afterward. Give it to me. I'll fly it out. You can't outrun this on foot."

And here was the thing he hadn't expected: she wasn't wrong. That was what made it hard. Every word of it was true, and he stood there under the spruce with the rain starting to tick through the needles and let himself feel the whole weight of it — the engines behind them, the winter ahead, the forty pounds on his back, the fact that he was fourteen and alone and the last hand of four.

Then he thought about what the keeper would have done, and the answer was: *he'd have made the pilot do the math.*

"No," Jude said.

"Jude—"

"No. Listen." He shrugged the chest off his shoulders and set it down between them on the needle-fall, deliberate, so she could see he wasn't clutching it. "You want me to trust you with ninety years of record. Fine. Here's why I won't, and here's what I'll trade instead. Both. In order."

She crossed her arms. Waiting. Good. She was someone who could wait.

"One: I don't know you. Two days ago you were a stranger flying medicine into Kettle Landing, and yesterday you walked into my cave because the tide showed you a door. That's luck, not loyalty. Luck doesn't get custody. Two: everyone who ever wanted the ledger wanted it for a reason of their own. The consortium wants it burned. Magistrates want it buried. Towns want it opened only far enough to settle their own debts and no further. Nobody has ever wanted it just *read*. Until somebody does, it stays with the person whose whole job is wanting nothing except that it survives." He tapped his own sternum. "That's me. That's the job. My keeper died six weeks ago and the job didn't die with him, no matter how much I wish it had."

The rain ticked. Somewhere behind them, faint, the sea worked at the coast.

"And the trade?" Odile said.

"The trade is this." He made himself breathe, because the next part was the part he'd been turning over since the moment he'd seen her plane on the shingle and understood what it meant — wings, distance, a way off this coast that didn't end in a snowmobile's headlights. "I can get us over the range before the snows close the passes. Every trail, every shelter cabin, every crossing the survey maps don't show. The keepers mapped them for ninety years precisely so the record could move when it had to. I can take you and that chest anywhere in these mountains faster than those men can drive a machine around the roads. That's my half."

"Your half of what?"

"A flight." He held her eyes. "There's a judge. Amara Sorel. Provincial court, seat at Graywater, in the valley east of the Col. Twenty-two years ago — before you flew, maybe before you were born — she cited the ledger. Entry four hundred and nine. A water-rights case, two ranches, and she ruled against the man with the better lawyer because the ledger proved the stream had been diverted in 1934 and everybody upstream had built their claims on top of a lie. She wrote the citation into the public record. First official in ninety years to treat the thing as evidence instead of rumor." His voice did something on the last words and he hated it and pushed through anyway. "She's the only one. Everyone else who ever touched it honestly ended up dead or ruined or gone. She's still sitting. Still honest. Still *there*."

Odile had gone very still. It was a particular kind of stillness he recognized from animals — the stillness of something deciding whether the thing in front of it was threat or kin.

"You want me to fly you to a judge."

"I want you to fly the ledger to a judge. Me and it together. I don't leave the chest and the chest doesn't leave me, and neither of us goes anywhere without the other, so it's the same sentence however you build it."

"And if I say no? If I just take the chest while you sleep tonight?"

He'd known she'd ask. He'd rehearsed the answer on the climb out of the gully, and now that it was time, he found he didn't need the rehearsal at all.

"Then you take it," he said. "And I follow you. However far. To Graywater, to the coast, to wherever you land. I don't fight you and I don't report you and I don't need to catch you — I just need to be standing next to you when you finally open it and realize you're holding other people's lives and no way to know which page belongs to whom. There's a reason it took four generations of keepers, ma'am. It's not the walking. It's the knowing."

The silence stretched long enough that he heard a raven somewhere upslope, one dry note, like a stick breaking.

"You called me ma'am," Odile said finally. "I'm thirty-four."

"The keeper said respect costs nothing and confuses people, and confused people negotiate slower."

This time the smile actually arrived — brief, crooked, gone again, but real. She crouched down next to the chest, not touching it, and studied the iron corners, the oilcloth, the wax-sealed seams the way she'd probably study an engine: looking for what it cost to run and where it would fail.

"Entry four hundred and nine," she said. "Sorel cited it by number?"

"In the ruling. Page sixty-one of the published decision. The consortium bought the law reports from that year out of three courthouses afterward, but the Graywater library kept theirs. I've read it. She wrote—" he closed his eyes, because he'd made himself memorize it, the way you memorize the one spring that doesn't freeze, "—she wrote: *'A community that cannot remember its own injuries cannot heal them, and a court that refuses to look at memory is not neutral; it is merely deaf.'*"

When he opened his eyes, Odile Winterbourne was looking at him differently. Not softer, exactly. More like the way you look at a river you'd dismissed as a creek right before it takes out your bridge.

"My father," she said, and stopped. Started again. "My father came from these mountains. Left before I was born. I've spent my whole life going the other direction as fast as an engine would carry me, and I don't fully know why, and I'm telling you that because you just asked me to bet your life on my judgment, and it seems like you should have the full inventory." She exhaled. "One condition."

"One."

"We stop at Kettle Landing first. Resupply. My plane needs parts I can't fabricate out of driftwood, and we need food for a traverse, and I need—" she glanced north, toward the headland, toward the sound of engines that might or might not still be hunting the tide line, "—I need to know how much those men know about me. You can't navigate a hunt if you don't know whether you're the hunter or the fox."

Jude considered it. Kettle Landing was a town of two hundred people, one fuel pump, and ears everywhere. It was also the only strip within a hundred miles that could lift a damaged plane with cargo.

"Two days," he said. "False names. We're in and out before anyone finishes being curious."

"False names," she agreed. Then, standing, brushing the needles off her knees: "For the record, kid — and I mean this kindly — you negotiate like a fifty-year-old ferry clerk."

"The keeper said I was advanced."

"He sounds like he was a piece of work."

"He was the best man I'll ever know," Jude said, "and the consortium burned his grave marker to ash last spring, so let's get moving."

He saw it land on her — the small flinch, the recalculation, the new arithmetic of exactly what kind of men were behind them on that coast. She picked up her end of the world and settled it, and pointed east, up into the timberline, where the range stood waiting under its first thin veil of cloud.

"Graywater via Kettle Landing," she said. "Before the snows. Deal."

Jude hoisted the chest onto his back and fell into step beside her, and for the first time since the keeper died, the forty pounds on his spine felt less like a burial and more like a message that had finally found itself a courier.

They were two miles up the trail, deep in the pines with the sea gone quiet behind them, when Odile stopped so suddenly he nearly walked into her. She was staring back the way they'd come, down through the trees at the grey ribbon of coast.

Smoke. Thin, deliberate, three columns rising off the beach in a line — signal fires, spaced like men standing shoulder to shoulder.

Not searching, Jude thought. *Announcing.*

And the middle column had just turned black, which meant they'd thrown something on it that wasn't wood.

"That's not for each other," Odile said quietly. "Somebody's telling the rest of their team exactly where we came ashore."

Jude looked at the smoke, then at the sky, measuring the light left in the day against the miles left in the route, and gave her the only answer the mountains had ever taught him.

"Then we walk in the dark."
