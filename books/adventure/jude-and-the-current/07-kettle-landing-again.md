## 7. Kettle Landing Again

Kettle Landing smelled the same as it always had — diesel and spruce smoke and the brackish sweetness of the tideflats at low water — but to Odile it smelled different now, because she was arriving in it as a liar.

She'd done it before, of course. Every pilot who worked the interior lied somewhere: on a weight manifest, on a fuel receipt, to a customs man with soft hands and hard eyes. But those were small lies, lies of convenience, the kind you told the way you wiped grease off your hands before shaking one. This was different. She had walked into the Kettle Mercantile with a boy she'd known four days and given the clerk a name that wasn't hers, and the lie sat in her chest like a stone she'd swallowed.

"Winter," she said. "Odile Winter. And my nephew."

The clerk — a round-faced young man named Pell who'd been bagging flour when he still had baby fat on him — looked at her for exactly as long as it took to not recognize her, which was the whole trick of it. She'd worn her hair down under a wool cap, traded her flight jacket for a canvas coat she kept in the plane's survival kit, and dirtied her face with a thumb-smear of crankcase oil before they came down the boardwalk. It wasn't much. It didn't have to be. Nobody in Kettle Landing looked twice at anyone; the town ran on the principle that whatever you were doing was your own business until it cost somebody money.

"Room's eight a night, bathhouse is behind the stable, no cooking in the rooms," Pell recited, sliding the register across. "You want supper, kitchen closes at seven."

Jude signed after her. He'd practiced it on the walk over — *Jude Winter* — his letters cramped and careful, a boy's handwriting pretending to be somebody's nephew. She watched him do it and felt something twist in her that she refused to name. Fourteen years old, signing false names in a town where men were already asking questions about him. She'd flown supply runs into places where the mining consortium had opinions, and she knew what happened to boys who got asked questions by people with money behind them.

They took the room. It had one bed, a washstand, and a window that looked out on the alley behind the mercantile, which Odile chose deliberately — back doors mattered more than views.

"We eat fast, we buy what we need, we're gone before first light," she said, keeping her voice low even though the walls were log-thick. "Fuel, food, oil for the engine. Nothing else."

Jude stood at the window with the curtain pulled to a two-inch gap, watching the street the way he seemed to watch everything — like a heron standing in shallows, motionless, all attention. "There's a man across the street," he said. "He's been there since we came up the boardwalk. He hasn't gone into any shop. He's just leaning on the rail by the dock office."

"Dockhands lean."

"He's wearing boots worth more than that building."

She came to the gap and looked. The man was fortyish, heavy through the shoulders, dressed better than anyone leaned on that particular rail on a Tuesday. He had the settled patience of a man being paid by the hour to stand somewhere. Odile had seen that patience before, in Yellowknife, in Whitehorse, outside courthouses and union halls. It was consortium patience. It was the patience of money waiting for something to be worth buying.

"Maybe," she said. "Stay away from the windows anyway."

* * *

Supper was stew and flatbread in the hotel dining room, and Jude ate the way he did everything — carefully, completely, wasting nothing, a boy whose body had learned early that food was not a guarantee. Odile found herself cataloguing him again, the way she'd been doing since the cave: the way he held his spoon, the way he listened to conversations three tables away without appearing to, the way he'd folded the ledger's oilskin wrapping into his coat lining so it wouldn't rustle. He was learning to move through the world as someone hunted, and he was learning it fast, and every hour of it made her angrier at people she hadn't met yet.

"The reward's posted," he said quietly, not looking up from his bowl.

Her spoon stopped. "Where?"

"Tacked inside the door of the assay office. I saw it when we passed." He tore his bread and dragged it through the gravy. "It doesn't use my name. It says *juvenile male, approximately fourteen years, dark-haired, may be traveling with a female pilot.*"

"A female pilot." She set her spoon down. "That narrows it right down to half the sky."

"It says the aircraft is a de Havilland Beaver, high-wing, blue and white, registration ending in four-seven."

Four-seven. Her tail number. She sat very still while the dining room went on around her — the clink of cutlery, the cook shouting something cheerful toward the kitchen, a dog scratching itself under the stove. Somewhere between the cave and here, someone had described her airplane to a printer.

"That means they talked to someone who saw us land," she said. "On the coast. After the storm."

"Or before it." Jude finally looked at her. His eyes were gray-green, sea-colored, and older than fourteen had any right to make them. "The keeper taught me that when you find a trail, you look for where it starts, not where it crosses yours. Someone knew you were flying that coast looking for shelter before the weather ever turned. Maybe they put the storm and you together."

"You think they forced me down?"

"I think," he said, with the terrible gentleness of the very young, "that I've been alone in that cave for five months, and in five months nobody found me. You were there for one night, and by morning there were engines on the beach."

She wanted to argue. She was good at arguing; it was most of what she had instead of a family. But the arithmetic was simple and ugly, and she couldn't make it come out any other way. Five months against one night. Either the enforcers had gotten lucky, or someone had drawn them a map, and the only new mark on that map was a blue-and-white Beaver riding out a storm on a beach that no chart admitted existed.

"I need to know who's talking," she said. "And I think I know where to ask."

* * *

Marta Halloran kept the radio shack at the north end of the boardwalk, a squat cedar building with a forest of antennas on its roof and a woodstove that had been burning since before Odile could legally fly. Marta was sixty, built like a dock piling, and had been relaying messages across the range for thirty years — birth announcements, death notices, price lists, and every secret in between, because nothing moved through this country without passing through her headphones first. She was also, in the loose and unspoken way of the interior, Odile's friend. One of maybe three people alive who could claim it.

Odile left Jude in the room with instructions that amounted to *don't exist*, and walked the four blocks alone, counting the ways out of town as she went. Old habit. New necessity.

Marta was on the air when Odile let herself in, finishing a relay to a trapline cabin up the Sustut. She held up one finger — *wait* — logged the transmission, and swung around in her chair with the particular expression of a woman who had been expecting this visit for two days and had rehearsed the opening.

"Well," Marta said. "Look who's dead."

"Marta—"

"Don't." She said it flat, without heat, which was worse than heat. She reached under the operating desk and came up with a folded sheet of paper, official stationery, the consortium's tree-and-pick logo stamped in the corner. She slapped it down on the desk between them like a card she was tired of holding. "Came through on the wire yesterday morning. Priority distribution to every post office, radio shack, and trading post from here to the divide. *Notice of inquiry. Persons with information regarding the following individuals should contact the nearest consortium office. Substantial reward.*" She tapped the paper. "Substantial, Odile. That's five figures. That's a man's lifetime wages for pointing at a kid on a street."

Odile picked it up. There were two descriptions. The first was Jude — age, build, coloring, *last seen in company of the below-named party*. The second was her own name, spelled correctly, followed by her correct license class, her correct home base, and a physical description accurate enough to have come from an old employer or a government file. Underneath both, in smaller type: *Miss Vasquez is believed to be unaware of the nature of her passenger. No action will be taken against her provided she presents herself for interview.*

"They're calling me a dupe," Odile said.

"They're calling you bait." Marta leaned back, and the chair creaked under her. "Think about it. They don't want you scared off. Scared pilots fly wrong directions. They want you comfortable. They want you to read that notice and think, *I'm fine, I just hand over the boy and walk away.* They've made it easy for you, sweetheart. That's how you know it's a trap. Nothing about the consortium is ever easy unless they want something."

Odile set the paper down. Through the shack's front window she could see the boardwalk, the dock office, the man in the expensive boots — gone now, she noticed, and that absence frightened her more than his presence had. "Who reported me, Marta? Somebody described my plane to whoever prints these things. Somebody on the coast."

Marta was quiet for a moment. Outside, the wind worried the guy-wires of the antenna mast, a low harmonic hum, the sound Odile associated with every important conversation of her adult life.

"You know I don't carry gossip," Marta said finally. "I carry traffic. And I'm going to tell you some traffic now, and then you're going to leave, and we're never going to speak of it again, because if I speak of it again I'll have to decide whether I reported it, and I'd rather not find out what I'd do." She pulled a logbook toward her but didn't open it. "Two days ago, before the wire came through, I handled a personal message out of here. Paid cash, sent priority, addressed to the consortium's regional office at Alder Creek. Sender's name was logged as T. Brannock."

The name landed in Odile's stomach like ballast dropping.

Tommy Brannock. Who'd run charters out of Kettle Landing when she was learning mountain flying, who'd taught her how to read a glacier's surface for crevasse shadows, who'd slept on her floor half a winter the year his marriage came apart, who'd fixed her carburetor ice problem with a baling wire and a curse and never let her forget either. Tommy, who owed her — or whom she'd thought he owed, or she'd owed him, the debts so tangled after fifteen years that neither of them could have said which direction they ran.

"He saw your plane on the coast," Marta said. "He was running a survey charter down the shoreline when the weather closed in. Landed at the same cove an hour after you did. Saw the Beaver on the beach, saw two figures go into the arch, and then — this is the part I want you to hear clearly, Odile — he saw the snowmobiles coming along the shore trail, and he made a choice about which side of that beach he wanted to be standing on. He keyed his radio and told Alder Creek exactly where you were. Then he flew out ahead of the weather and filed his message from here."

Odile stood very still. The stove ticked. The antenna wires sang their one thin note.

"Did he say why?" she asked, and heard how small her voice came out.

"He didn't explain himself to me." Marta's voice softened by perhaps a degree, which from Marta was a flood of tenderness. "But I'll tell you what I think, since you're going to fly out of here either way and I'd rather you flew angry than blind. Tommy's got a lease payment due on his Cub in the spring, and the consortium bought the paper on it last year when the bank called it. They own his debt, Odile. They own a piece of him the way they own a piece of everybody in this town, and when a man's owned, he tells himself the thing he did was small. He tells himself you'd have been fine. He tells himself the boy's probably a runaway and no great loss." She shrugged, huge shoulders rising and falling. "Men are very good at telling themselves things. It's why I charge cash."

Odile looked at the notice on the desk, at her own name sitting there in cold type between *believed unaware* and *no action taken*, and felt something shift in her that she recognized distantly, the way you feel a glacier calve ten miles off — a deep structural movement, a change in the shape of the world you'd been standing on.

She had spent her whole life believing that moving was safety. Keep the circle wide, keep the names few, keep the debts paid and the favors balanced, and nothing can corner you. She'd built her entire existence on the theory that if you never belonged anywhere too firmly, nothing you loved could be used against you. And here was the proof of the theory's opposite, delivered by a friend over a woodstove: Tommy Brannock had belonged to her once, as much as she let anyone belong, and the consortium had simply purchased the connection and pulled the lever. Being known hadn't protected her. Being known was the handle they'd grabbed.

"Does the boy know yet?" Marta asked.

"Not about Tommy."

"And you're going to tell him?"

Odile looked out the window. Down the boardwalk, small and deliberate, Jude was crossing the street toward the general store, hood up, moving with that heron-still watchfulness, carrying a canvas sack for supplies and a chest full of other people's sins. A reward notice with his description was tacked inside a door fifty feet from where he was walking, and he knew it, and he was going anyway, because the keeper had raised him to believe that memory was worth more than safety, and Odile was beginning to suspect the boy was right and everyone else — herself included, especially herself — had been wrong for a very long time.

"Yeah," she said. "I'm going to tell him. He deserves to know what knowing me costs."

"Odile." Marta stood, and for a moment her hand rested on Odile's shoulder, heavy and warm as a spar of timber. "The wire says *present yourself for interview*. That's not an invitation, that's a countdown. They gave you a window because they figure you'll use it to do their work for them. When the window closes—" She didn't finish it. She didn't have to. "Fly careful. And Odile — I never saw you come in here."

"You never do," Odile said, and meant it as thanks, and heard it land as apology, and couldn't fix the difference.

* * *

She found Jude at the mouth of the alley behind the store, crouched beside a stack of empty crates, reading the reward notice he must have lifted off the assay office door. He'd folded it open on his knee with the care of a boy handling scripture, and when he looked up at her, his face had the terrible calm she was starting to understand was his version of screaming.

"You knew," he said. Not accusing. Checking.

"I know now." She crouched down to his level, crates biting into her knees. "There's more. Sit still for a minute."

She told him all of it — the notice, the terms, the countdown, and then Tommy, the whole sorry shape of it, because she'd decided on the walk over that she would not manage this boy the way the world managed him, feeding him partial truths and calling it protection. He listened without interrupting, eyes fixed on the middle distance, and when she finished he was quiet for a long time.

"My keeper said a thing once," he said finally. "He said, *the ledger doesn't make men honest, Odile's kind of flying doesn't make her safe, and the snow doesn't care which of you is sorry.*" He glanced up. "He liked sayings that ended badly. He said hope that survives bad endings is the only kind worth carrying."

"Your keeper sounds like he'd have gotten along with Marta."

"Everyone gets along with the person who owns the truth." Jude folded the notice along its original creases and handed it back to her. "What do we do about Tommy?"

Odile took the paper. Somewhere across town, a man who'd taught her to fly was sitting in a kitchen he might lose in the spring, telling himself a story about a small thing he'd done, and the consortium was counting down the hours until she walked into an office and made their problem simple. She could hate him for it. She found, examining herself honestly, that she mostly didn't — and that frightened her more than the enforcers did, because hatred was simple and this was not.

"Nothing," she said. "Not tonight. Tonight we load the plane, and tomorrow we're wheels-up before the frost's off the windshield, and we fly the route nobody expects because nobody in their right mind flies it this late in the season." She stood, knees cracking, and looked east toward the black wall of the range where the pass waited, already rimed white above the timberline. "Sorel's courthouse is six days' hard travel from the far side. If the snow holds off."

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then we get very good at walking."

Jude shouldered the supply sack, and for a moment, in the lamplight spilling from the store's back door, he looked less like a boy and more like what the keepers had made him — a vessel for something heavy, walking steadily uphill. Then he blinked, and he was fourteen again, tired and hungry and far from the only home he'd ever known.

"Odile," he said. "The man in the good boots. He wasn't watching the hotel. He was watching the radio shack. He left right after you went in."

The cold went through her coat like the coat wasn't there. Marta. Thirty years of traffic, one logbook, and a name — *T. Brannock* — written in her own hand, timed and dated, sitting on a shelf in a cedar building that any consortium man with a warrant or a bribe could walk into by morning.

They broke cover at a dead run.
