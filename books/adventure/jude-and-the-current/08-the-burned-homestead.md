## 8. The Burned Homestead

Jude smelled the smoke before he saw the valley.

That was the first wrong thing. Woodsmoke in the high country meant someone burning brush, or a stove left careless, and both of those smells were thin, domestic, apologetic. This was different. This was heavy and chemical and old — two days old at least, maybe three — the smell of creosote and pitch and something underneath it that might have been lamp oil. It came down off the ridge on the morning wind like a confession nobody had asked for.

He stopped on the trail and stood with his face into the wind until Odile caught up.

"What is it?" she said. She was favoring her pack shoulder, the one that had taken the worst of the river crossing, but she'd stopped complaining about it somewhere above Cold Fork, which he understood to be her way of apologizing without having to say anything out loud.

"Smoke," he said. "Old smoke."

"From where?"

He didn't answer right away because he wasn't ready to say it, and saying it would make it true in front of another person. The trail bent east around a granite shoulder and dropped through a stand of black spruce, and beyond the spruce was the valley he had been born in, or as close to born as a boy raised by keepers ever got to being. The keeper's cabin sat at the head of it where the creek flattened out. He had lived there from the time he could walk until eleven days ago, when he'd walked away with a chest of paper on his back and an old man's last instruction ringing in his ears: *Take it to Sorel. Don't look back. Looking back is how they find you.*

He had looked back anyway, once, from the col. The cabin's chimney had been smoking. That was all he'd seen — a thread of gray against the pines, ordinary, home-shaped. He'd let himself believe it was the keeper's neighbor, Marta Ellingboe, come up with bread, the way she did some Sundays even though the keeper never answered his door for anyone but Jude.

Now the wind was carrying the truth of that chimney smoke down to him, and it was not bread.

"It's my valley," he said finally.

Odile went still in the way she went still, which was not stillness at all but a kind of coiled readiness, like water deciding whether to freeze. "The consortium?"

"I don't know."

"You do know," she said gently. "You just don't want to."

* * *

They came down through the spruce single file, Odile behind him because she'd learned by now that he knew this ground the way she knew weather fronts, and the trees opened, and there it was.

The cabin was gone.

Not damaged. Not burned down to its frame, which would have been bad enough, would have been grief enough. *Gone.* The stones of its foundation had been pulled apart and rolled into the creek bed. The well had been filled with rock and capped with a slab of timber. Even the outhouse pit had been dug open and backfilled. And the ground itself — the ground where the cabin had stood for longer than anyone living could remember, ground that had held three generations of keepers, their garden beds, their drying racks, the flat stone where the keeper had taught Jude his letters by scratching them in frost — the ground had been scraped. Dozed, probably. A blade dragged across it, peeling away the topsoil and the moss and every trace of habitation down to raw mineral dirt, and then left like that, naked and scoured, so that the valley floor looked less like a homestead than like a wound that hadn't decided whether to heal.

At the center of the scrape, driven straight into the earth with a hydraulic driver by the look of the collar around it, stood a survey stake. Orange paint. Aluminum cap. A number stenciled on the cap that meant nothing to Jude except that it belonged to no one he had ever known.

Odile walked the perimeter while he stood at the edge of the scrape and couldn't make himself cross it. She moved the way she did everything, efficiently, without ceremony, crouching here and there, reading tracks in the mud the way he read entries in the ledger. When she came back she didn't soften it.

"Crew of six, maybe eight. Came in on machines from the north cut. They brought fuel." She nodded toward a blackened ring near the creek where something large had been doused and torched. "They burned what wouldn't fit in a truck and buried what wouldn't burn. Then they graded the site so the next people who fly over see nothing but a clearing."

"The next people who fly over will be them," Jude said. His voice came out flat and strange, like someone else's. "That's who flies over valleys now."

He crossed the scrape after all. He didn't know why. Maybe because the stake wanted him to stay on the far side of it, and he had spent fourteen years learning that whatever a powerful man drew a line around, you walked to the middle of and stood there until you understood it.

The heat was still in the ground. He could feel it coming up through his boot soles, faint, banked, the way a stove holds warmth hours after the fire dies. Under the mineral dirt, here and there, the scraping blade had missed things. A hinge. A scrap of tin roofing folded like a leaf. The iron trivet from the hearth, warped into a shape like a claw. He picked up each piece and put it down again, because none of it was what he was looking for, and he was afraid to name what he was looking for even inside his own skull.

Then he found the chest.

Or rather, he found where the chest had been. There was a rectangular scar in the subsoil, deeper than the rest of the disturbance, and around its edges the dirt was scorched almost to ceramic. They had found the strongroom under the floor — of course they had; the keeper had built it before Jude was born and the consortium had had forty years to wonder about the cabin's foundations — and they had burned it hot, hotter than a cabin fire, deliberately, with fuel poured down into it, because paper needs a great deal of heat and a long time and plenty of air to be sure it's dead.

But the chest itself had been iron-banded cedar, and cedar does not surrender easily, and whoever ran the crew had been in a hurry. Snow was coming. Everyone in the range could feel it coming. So they had raked the ashes, loaded the truck, planted their stake, and gone, and they had missed the corner of the strongroom where the blade's reach fell short, and in that corner, half-fused into a lump of charcoal and melted band-iron, lay a single page.

Jude knelt over it for a long time before he touched it.

It was the bottom third of a leaf. The upper portion was char gone past saving, but the lower edge had been protected — folded under, tucked against the chest wall, kissed by heat instead of consumed by it. The paper was brown and brittle as autumn birch, and along its burnt margin the handwriting of the second keeper — the magistrate's own hand, the hand Jude had spent ten years learning to copy stroke by stroke — broke off mid-word and curled away into blackness.

What remained was four lines.

*Item the eleventh. In the matter of the Aldous Creek claims, the company men came to the widow Harkness on the 4th of March and told her the mountain under her house was theirs by paper she had never signed. She asked to see the paper. They showed her a rifle instead.*

Below that, in the same ink, slightly smaller, the way the magistrate's writing always shrank when he was angry:

*I could do nothing. I record it so that the doing-nothing is also written down.*

Jude read it twice. Then a third time, aloud, barely voicing it, because the keeper had taught him that an entry read aloud was an entry alive, and this one needed to be alive more than any entry he had ever carried. Widow Harkness. Aldous Creek. March the fourth. A rifle shown where a signature should have been.

Fifty years ago. And the men driving stakes into this valley today were the grandsons of the method, if not the blood, and the only difference between then and now was that then, a man in a cave had written it down.

* * *

Odile gave him the time. He noticed that about her — she was the most impatient person he had ever traveled with and she had developed, without either of them discussing it, an instinct for when to leave him alone with a thing. She busied herself at the creek line, filling bottles, checking the sky over the western peaks with the narrowed eyes she used for weather, and when she finally spoke it was practical, which he understood was her dialect of kindness.

"We can't stay. If a crew comes back to check their work—"

"They won't." He was still kneeling. "Nobody checks work they believe is finished."

"Somebody checks work worth eight figures." She crouched beside him, careful of the ash. "Jude. Look at me."

He looked at her.

"I'm sorry," she said. "I know that's a small word for this."

"It's not small." He surprised himself by meaning it. "Most people don't say it at all."

She glanced at the stake, and her expression did something complicated — anger and recognition tangled together. "I've seen this before," she said. "Different mountains. Same stake. Down south, a mining outfit scraped a village off a map because the map was the only place it existed. No papers, no witnesses, no village. It took the courts nine years to agree it had ever been there, and by then everyone who remembered it was dead or bought." She paused. "Paper is the only thing that beats them. You know that better than anyone."

"That's why they burn it first."

"That's why we carry it out." She stood, offered him her good hand. "We're losing light. We should be over the saddle before—"

"Marta Ellingboe lives four miles down-valley." He didn't take her hand yet. "If the crew came through the valley, they passed her place."

Odile was quiet for a moment. "And if they passed her place, she may have seen which way they went. Which direction. How many. Whether Rell was with them." She exhaled through her nose. "You understand that asking those questions makes us slower and easier to find."

"I understand that Marta taught me to snare rabbits when I was seven and I'm not walking past her door without knocking."

Something shifted in Odile's face — not softening exactly, more like a decision arriving. "All right," she said. "One hour. Then we climb."

* * *

Marta Ellingboe's cabin sat below the treeline where the valley widened into hay meadows, and unlike the keeper's homestead it still stood, shutters closed, woodpile unbroken, a sled turned upside down by the door the way you store it when you mean to stay through winter. But there was a paper nailed to the doorframe, and Jude's stomach dropped before he was close enough to read it.

It was a notice. Printed, official-looking, stamped with a logo he now knew the way he knew the taste of ash: the consortium's twin peaks over crossed hammers. The text declared the parcel — the whole valley, every quarter-section, meadow and creek and timber — subject to mineral lease survey under provincial statute, and warned all persons *in occupation or possession* to remove themselves within thirty days or be considered trespass.

"In occupation or possession," Odile read over his shoulder. Her voice had gone quiet and dangerous. "Marta's family has worked this valley since before the province existed."

"They have a paper too," Jude said. "A printed one. Ours is handwritten. They're betting the province believes ink by the barrel."

He pulled the notice off the nails. Odile raised an eyebrow. "Evidence?" she said.

"Habit," he said, and folded it into the chest pocket where the charred page rode against his ribs. "Everything gets kept. That's the whole religion."

He knocked at Marta's door with the pattern she'd taught him — two, pause, three — the knock that meant *it's Jude* and not a bill collector or worse. Nothing. He tried the latch. Unlocked.

Inside, the cabin was cold but orderly. Dishes washed and racked. Beds made. The stove swept clean and empty, banked for absence, not abandonment. On the table, weighted under a stone, a note in Marta's blunt carpenter's pencil:

*Men came Tuesday. Machines. Asked questions about the old man and the boy. I told them nothing because I know nothing, which is the only lie I tell well. They took the road north after. Went to your valley, Jude. I watched the smoke from the hay barn and I am ashamed I watched. Gone to my sister's on the coast until this blows over or I don't. Feed the cat if she comes back. She won't.*

Underneath, in fresher pencil, added later, the handwriting hurried:

*The tall one with the gray coat asked me twice if the boy had the book. Not papers. The book. He knows what it's called.*

Jude stood holding the note while the cold of the cabin worked its way through his jacket, and it wasn't the cold that made his hands unsteady. *The book.* Not documents, not records, not contraband — *the book*, the word the keepers used, the word that had never been written anywhere outside the cave and the cabin and the heads of two dead men and one living boy. Which meant someone had talked. Which meant somewhere behind the enforcers and the survey crews and the printed notices there was a person who had known the cave's secrets, and that person had sold them, and the hunt was no longer a hunt for a runaway boy carrying rumors.

It was a hunt for the ledger, by name, by people who understood exactly what it was.

Odile read the note twice. When she looked up, her eyes had the flat bright quality he'd learned to recognize as the engine turning over beneath her calm.

"Gray coat," she said. "Tall. That's not Rell's crew. Rell works alone and dresses like a funeral director. This is the second team." She folded the note and handed it back to him. "The ones without a price list. The ones who don't offer deals."

Jude put the note in his pocket beside the charred page, beside the notice, beside the little tin of the keeper's ashes he'd carried since Cold Fork, and he felt the weight of all that paper against his chest and understood, with a clarity that felt almost like calm, what the rage in him was for.

For eleven days he had been running. Running was the keeper's last instruction and he'd honored it, mostly, though running had never sat right in him the way it seemed to sit naturally in Odile, who had made an art and a livelihood of it. He had run because the chest must reach Sorel, and the chest must reach Sorel because the keeper said so, and obedience had been enough fuel to get him over the col and down the river and through the whiteout.

But standing in Marta Ellingboe's cold kitchen, with the smell of his burned home still in his hair and a stranger's pencil telling him the enemy knew the word *book*, obedience stopped being enough. Something else lit where obedience had been, older and hotter and much harder to extinguish.

They hadn't burned the cabin to hide a crime. They'd burned it because they were still committing it — today, this season, with orange-painted stakes — and the ledger was not history to them. It was a mirror, and mirrors are the one object tyrants cannot abide.

He was not carrying the past to a courthouse anymore. He was carrying evidence of a crime in progress, and every mile they climbed out of this valley was a mile the crime continued unrecorded.

"Odile," he said. "We need to move faster."

She was already shouldering her pack. "I was hoping you'd say that."

They stepped out into the last of the afternoon light. Behind them the valley lay scoured and silent, the stake catching the sun like a nail driven into the land's palm. Ahead, the saddle waited, and beyond the saddle the high country, and beyond that a courthouse and a judge and — if the snow held off, if the gray coat didn't find them first — a reckoning fifty years overdue.

Jude touched the charred page through the fabric of his shirt, the way the keeper had taught him to touch the chest before opening it: once, lightly, as a promise.

*Item the eleventh,* he thought. *I record it so that the doing-nothing is also written down.*

There would be a twelfth item. He would write it himself.
