## 9. River of Small Betrayals

The guide's name was Teodor, and Odile didn't trust him from the moment he spat over the side of the raft and said the Kestrel was running "fat and friendly" this time of year.

Rivers that ran fat and friendly in late autumn were rivers that had already eaten people. She'd grown up on water like this — brown, muscular, carrying a season's worth of snowmelt and secrets down out of the range — and she knew that friendliness in a river was just hunger moving slowly.

But there was no bridge over the Kestrel for forty miles either direction, no road, no way across except by raft or by swimming, and swimming meant losing the chest to the current or losing Jude with it. So she paid Teodor his outrageous fee without haggling, because haggling made you memorable, and being memorable was the one luxury they couldn't afford anymore.

Not since Kettle Landing. Not since Marta had gripped her wrist behind the counter of the trading post and whispered, *They already know your name, Ollie. Somebody sold it before you even landed.*

They'd come down out of the high country on the sixth morning after the burned homestead, following a game trail that smelled of frost and rotting rowan berries, and found Teodor's camp in a bend of willows: a canvas lean-to, a raft of lashed spruce logs, a kettle of fish stew, and a man who looked at them exactly one second too long before smiling.

"Two travelers," Teodor said. His eyes went to the chest strapped to Jude's back — the waterproof box, scarred green, heavy as sin — then away again, quick as a pickpocket. "Going where?"

"Downriver," Odile said. "Then west."

"West is a big place." He ladled stew into tin bowls without asking if they wanted any. "I take people to the ferry landing at Stony Mouth. From there, you're on your own."

Jude sat close to her, knees drawn up, eating with the careful mechanical hunger of a boy who'd learned never to leave food uneaten but never to trust the hand that offered it. He hadn't spoken since they'd arrived. He didn't need to. She could feel him reading Teodor the way the old keeper had apparently taught him to read everything — looking for what was written underneath.

Later, while Teodor checked the lashings, Jude leaned against her shoulder and murmured, so low it was almost breath: "He has new rope. Coiled by the fire. Nobody buys new rope for a raft that old."

"So?"

"So the keeper said you can tell what a man expects by what he's bought. New rope means cargo he doesn't want to lose." A pause. "Or passengers he means to deliver somewhere specific."

Odile turned that over while the light died through the willows. It might mean nothing. Rope rotted; guides replaced it. But it might also mean that Teodor had been told to expect them — told to expect two travelers and a green chest — and had prepared accordingly.

She slept in shifts that night, her pistol loose under her jacket, and woke once to see Teodor standing at the edge of the willows looking north up the trail they'd come by, listening to something she couldn't hear. Snowmobiles carried a long way on cold air. So did voices.

By dawn she'd made her decision, and the decision was simple: get on the raft, get across, and be gone into the trees before Teodor could finish whatever transaction he was planning. The river was the danger. The river was also the exit.

* * *

The Kestrel at first light was the color of strong tea and moving faster than it looked, which was the oldest lie rivers told. Mist stood on the water in ragged columns. Teodor poled them off the gravel bar with an ease that reminded her, grudgingly, that a man could be untrustworthy and still be very good at his work.

The chest went amidships, wrapped in oilcloth. Jude sat behind it with his arms around it, and Odile took the forward position where she could watch both the water and the man.

"Stony Mouth by midday," Teodor said, poling them into the current. "If the river agrees."

The first hour agreed. The second hour began to argue.

The valley narrowed, the banks stepping up into grey scree, and the current gathered itself the way water does when the walls start closing in. Odile felt the change in her hands before her head caught up — the pole vibrations coming through the hull, the raft's lazy drift tightening into intent. She'd flown rivers like this from above a hundred times, silver threads stitched through the range, and had always thought of them as scenery. Up close, at the speed of the thing, a river was not scenery. It was a schedule.

It was Teodor who broke it.

He stopped poling. Just — stopped, letting the current take them, and turned his head north toward a cut in the eastern bank where a tributary slid in quiet and dark between walls of alder. And Odile saw him raise one hand, not dramatically, not waving, just lifting it off the pole and holding it there, palm out, the way a man signals *here* to someone waiting.

She followed his look upstream along the tributary and saw nothing. That was the problem. There should have been nothing to see — no reason to signal, no reason to stop — unless something was waiting in those alders that didn't want to be seen yet.

"Keep poling," she said.

Teodor smiled at her, and it was a kind smile, almost apologetic. "Current does the work here, miss. I'm just steering."

"You raised your hand."

"Stretching." He rolled his shoulders, demonstrating, all innocence. "Old man's arms."

Jude had gone very still behind the chest. When Odile glanced back, he gave her the smallest shake of the head — *don't* — and she understood. Whatever was in the alders, confronting it here, mid-river, in moving water, with a boy who couldn't swim well and a chest that couldn't float, was how they all died. The play now was distance. Get past the tributary mouth. Get to the far bank. Get into the trees.

The raft swept past the confluence. For three heartbeats the alders slid by, close enough to touch, and Odile counted what she could see through them: nothing, nothing, nothing —

— and then the sun flashed off glass. Once. Deep in the green. A lens, or a scope, or a phone screen. Something that caught light and shouldn't have been there.

Her stomach dropped the way it did in downdrafts.

They came around the next bend and the river dropped too — a long brawling chute of white water where the valley floor tilted and the Kestrel remembered it was a mountain river wearing a lowland costume. Teodor started poling in earnest now, genuinely working, threading them through the first waves with real skill, and Odile realized with cold clarity that he wasn't running the rapids for their sake. He was running them because the plan required them to be somewhere specific at a specific time, and rapids were how you moved cargo fast.

"Teodor." She kept her voice level. "Who's at the tributary?"

"Nobody, miss."

"How much are they paying you?"

His pole hesitated — half a stroke, no more — and that half-stroke told her everything. He sighed, and some of the performance fell off him like a coat. "Enough to buy a new boat. Enough to stop doing this." He looked at her with something that wasn't quite apology and wasn't quite shame. "You're worth more than a season of fares, the pair of you. I've got a daughter in Stony Mouth with bad lungs. Medicine costs what medicine costs."

"And us?"

"They said don't hurt you. Said just slow you down, hold you at the landing till evening." He shrugged. "What happens after isn't mine."

*Nothing is ever anyone's,* Odile thought. *That's how the whole range works. Everyone holds a small piece of the betrayal and nobody holds the whole knife.*

Ahead, the rapids braided out around a mid-channel island of stacked driftwood, and beyond it the river bent hard west into a gorge whose walls rose sheer and wet. She read the water the way she read weather — instinctively, hungrily, the way Jude had been teaching her to read cloud over the last week, *look at what it's doing, not what it's supposed to do.* The main channel ran left of the island, deep and fast, straight toward the gorge. Toward wherever Teodor's employers waited downstream.

But right of the island, between the driftwood pile and the right-hand bank, ran a secondary channel. Narrower. Shallower. Ugly — she could see the white water stacked up in it, a steep churning drop — but it bent northwest instead of west, away from the gorge, and at its bottom the river spread wide and shallow over a gravel fan before gathering itself again.

An exit. A bad one. Which made it the only one.

"Teodor," she said, and drew the pistol, and held it low against her thigh where the alder shadows would keep it private. "New plan."

* * *

What happened next happened in pieces, the way emergencies do, each piece complete and separate and insane.

Piece one: she told Teodor to run the right channel, and he refused, and she put the muzzle of the pistol against the gunwale beside his hand and fired once into the wood. The crack went up the gorge like the sky splitting. Teodor swore in three languages and put the pole over.

Piece two: the driftwood island came at them faster than anything that size should move, and Jude — feral, silent Jude, who hadn't volunteered a full sentence in days — suddenly shouted, "*Left! Left oar, hard!*" and she realized he'd been reading the water too, the whole time, the way keepers read tides, and that the main channel's smooth surface was lying about a strainer — a deadfall pinned under the surface that would wrap their raft like thread around a spool. Teodor saw it a half-second later and hauled left, and they missed the strainer by less than the length of an arm, close enough that Odile could have counted the rings in the drowned timber.

Piece three: the right channel took them.

It was like going over a staircase in a barrel. The raft climbed, hung, slammed down; spray came aboard in sheets, ice-cold, the melt of some glacier weeks and miles back reaching them at last. The chest tried to slide. Jude threw himself across it and held on with everything he had, cheek pressed to the wet oilcloth, eyes wide open, watching the water come. Odile lost the pistol overboard — she felt it go, a small final tug at her jacket hem, and let it, because grief was a luxury too — and grabbed a spare paddle lashed at the stern and fought.

Teodor fought too. That was the strange part, the part she'd think about later. Whatever deal he'd made, whatever daughter with bad lungs, when the river decided to kill them all equally he worked like a man saving his own life, because it was, and skill is honest even when its owner isn't. He called the drops before they hit them — "*Big one, center-left, brace!*" — and she braced, and they survived the big one, and the next one, and the one after that.

Then the channel narrowed to half its width between two leaning slabs of granite, and the whole river funneled through the gap standing up, a single green wave taller than the mast of her plane, and Teodor yelled "*Paddle, don't fight it, ride it!*" and Odile rode it.

The world became noise and white. Somewhere in it Jude was shouting something that might have been her name. The raft surfaced sideways, spun, scraped stone with a sound like a scream, righted. And then — abruptly, absurdly — it was over. The gradient eased, the water spread out brown and broad over the gravel fan, and they drifted in the sudden quiet with steam rising off their shoulders, all three of them breathing like landed fish.

Behind them, up the gorge, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of engines. Not snowmobiles — outboards. Two of them, maybe three, working upstream against the current, hunting the main channel. Looking for a raft that wasn't there anymore.

Teodor sat down heavily on his own gear and put his face in his hands.

* * *

They put ashore on the gravel fan a mile below the rapids, where the willows grew thick enough to hide a raft and everyone in it. Odile kept the spare paddle as a weapon and didn't apologize for it. Teodor didn't ask her to.

"They'll figure it out," he said. He was wringing water from his cap, and he looked ten years older than he had at dawn. "When they don't find you at the landing, they'll come back up. They know my raft. They know me."

"Then you'd better be gone when they do."

"I've run this river twenty years." He said it flatly, not bragging — stating a fact the way a man states the depth of a hole he's fallen into. "Twenty years, and I never once sold anybody. You want to know what changed?" He looked at her, and then at Jude, and then at the chest, and his voice dropped. "Word came down the river about what's in that box. About what the magistrate wrote. About names." He tied the cap onto his knee with unnecessary force. "Everybody in these valleys is in that book somehow, girl. Or wishes they were. Or is terrified somebody else is. You're not carrying papers. You're carrying every grudge and every debt from here to the divide, and men will drown each other for the chance to tear out their own page."

Odile said nothing. Because he was describing her father, and he didn't know it, and she wasn't ready to say it out loud yet, maybe wasn't ready to say it at all.

Teodor pushed off alone, poling his empty raft back toward the main channel and whatever reckoning waited for him at Stony Mouth. At the last moment he paused, one foot on the gravel, and spoke without turning around.

"There's a trapper's cache two miles northwest, where the fan meets the timberline. Old blaze on a larch, red paint. Blanket, dried fish, maybe a stove." A pause. "Consider it a fare refund."

Then he was gone into the mist, and the river closed over the sound of his pole, and Odile stood on the gravel with her boots full of meltwater and her pistol at the bottom of the Kestrel and understood that she had just been betrayed, saved, and forgiven by the same man inside four hours.

"That's the third one," Jude said quietly.

She looked at him. He was wringing out his sleeves, hair plastered to his skull, fourteen years old and soaked to the bone and utterly calm.

"The guide, the woman in Kettle Landing who marked your map, the ferryman at Cold Fork who asked too many questions." He ticked them off on wet fingers. "Small betrayals. Everybody sells a little." He met her eyes, and there was no accusation in it, only the terrible fairness of a boy raised on records. "The ledger taught me that. People aren't good or bad. They're mostly scared, and mostly for sale, and mostly sorry after."

"Doesn't that make you angry?"

"It used to." He shouldered the chest, staggering slightly under it, refusing help. "Now I just write it down."

They walked northwest through the willows, two miles of wet gravel and alder scratch, and found the blaze exactly where a guilty man's conscience had promised: a red slash on a grey larch, old paint gone pink, above a cache barrel sunk in the moss. Inside — blanket, matches, a tin of dried whitefish, a rust-spotted stove, and, inexplicably, a child's wool mitten, left by some trapper years ago and never reclaimed.

Jude held the mitten for a long moment before putting it back.

They ate. They dried what could be dried. And when the light failed, Odile laid out the route on the scrap of map Marta had given her, and the decision made itself the way the best ones do — obviously, terribly, with no alternative anywhere in sight.

The enforcers had boats on the main channel and men at Stony Mouth. The east bank trails were watched. But the Kestrel itself, below the rapids, ran northwest through country so broken no one patrolled it — and she had spent eleven years flying over that country, memorizing its bends from above, the braids and the gravel bars and the canyon where the river cut through the spine of the range itself.

"We go down the river," she said. "At night. On foot along the bank, or on the water if we can build anything that floats. We follow the Kestrel into the gorge country, and we don't stop until we're through the range."

Jude studied the map upside down, corrected her on one bend, and nodded once.

"They'll expect us to hide," he said.

"They will."

"So we do the loud thing." Almost — almost — a smile. "The keeper used to say the tide doesn't hide. It just keeps arriving."

They set out as the last light drained off the water, the chest between them, the river roaring softly ahead like something enormous sleeping. Behind them, far back up the valley, an engine coughed and caught and began to hunt.

Odile walked faster.
