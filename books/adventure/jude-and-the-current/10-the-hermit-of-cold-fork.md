## 10. The Hermit of Cold Fork

The hermit's cabin sat where Cold Fork split around a granite tooth, smoke rising from a chimney built of river stones so old the mortar had gone the color of bone. Jude smelled woodsmoke and something else — tallow, maybe, or the particular staleness of a place where a man had lived alone long enough that his own smell had become the smell of the house.

Odile stopped at the treeline and put a hand on his shoulder. Not gentle. Warning.

"You're sure about this?"

"He said to come." Jude shifted the chest higher on his back. Three days of carrying it had taught him its weight in a new way — not the weight of paper but the weight of every name inside it, pressing down through the canvas and into his spine. "He said if I ever needed walls that weren't mine, come to Cold Fork and knock twice."

"Knock twice," Odile repeated. She looked at the cabin the way she looked at weather — reading it, deciding what it would do. "And you trust him because?"

"I don't." Jude started down through the alder. "That's why we knock twice instead of walking in."

She made a sound that might have been a laugh in a different life. Behind them the range stood up white to the shoulders, the first snows already chewing at the high country, and ahead of them the valley floor held that particular northern stillness that Jude had learned meant *someone is listening*.

He knocked twice on a door that had never once been painted.

The man who opened it was small and bent like a birch in wind, with a face seamed deep enough to plant potatoes in, and eyes that went straight past Jude to the chest on his back and stayed there.

"Well," the old man said. His voice was dry as August grass. "So the last one died after all."

* * *

His name was Emeric Voss, though he told them not to use it. He fed them barley soup and dark bread and asked nothing for three hours, which was its own kind of interrogation — Jude had been raised by a keeper who believed silence pulled truth out of people the way gravity pulled water downhill. Odile slept by the stove with her boots off and her pistol under her hand, and even asleep she looked like someone keeping watch.

It was only when the light through the single window went from gold to iron that Voss finally spoke to the thing that mattered.

"Open it."

Jude didn't move. "You know what's in it."

"I know what's *supposed* to be in it." Voss set down his cup. "I carried pages of it myself, boy, forty years ago, when my knees worked and the magistrate was alive and angry. I want to see whether the ones who came after me kept faith."

Jude looked at Odile. She was awake now; he could tell without looking directly, the way he'd learned to tell everything about her — by the change in her breathing, the small tension in the hand near the gun. He knelt and worked the chest open.

The ledger came out wrapped in oilcloth, and unwrapped it looked like what it was: a book too big for any lap, bound in hide gone stiff and dark, its spine repaired twice with sinew and once, badly, with copper wire. Beside it lay the loose pages, the copies, generations of keepers' hands copying entries so no single fire or flood could kill them all.

Voss reached out and stopped, fingers trembling an inch above the cover, as if touching it might cost him something.

"May I?"

"It's not mine to refuse," Jude said. "That's rather the point of it."

The old man made a sound — half cough, half something older — and opened the book.

What followed, Jude watched from the outside, the way he'd watched the keeper do it a hundred times with pilgrims and petitioners and once, memorably, a consortium lawyer who'd come upriver under a false name and left pale as milk. Voss read the way other men prayed. His lips moved. His thumb traced lines of ink. And page by page, entry by entry, the bent little hermit straightened, until he was sitting upright like a man called before a bench.

"The Meridian strike," he murmured. "Eighty-one men docked a month's pay for a cave-in the company caused itself. It's here. Names, amounts, the foreman who signed the order." He turned a page. "The Hollow Creek feud. Both families' grievances written side by side, and the settlement the magistrate forced them to sign — grain debts forgiven, a marriage between the houses, and both fathers' signatures at the bottom." He looked up, and his eyes were wet and furious at once. "Whole towns paid their debts by this book. Whole towns settled blood feuds over it, because everyone knew the magistrate wrote down what actually happened, and once it was written, it couldn't be unwritten. You could lie to a judge. You could bribe one. You couldn't lie to ink."

"I know," Jude said quietly.

"Do you?" Voss closed the book gently, the way you'd close a coffin. "You're fourteen. You know what your keeper taught you. But do you know why the consortium wants it burned? Not because it shames dead men. Because half the land claims in this range rest on documents those men forged — and the ledger is the only record anywhere that says what they did to get them. Burn the ledger and the forgeries become history. Keep it, and every claim they've staked since Kettle Landing is a lie waiting for a courtroom."

"That's why we're taking it to Sorel."

"Amara Sorel." Voss said the name carefully, tasting it. "The one judge who cited it. Yes. If she'll take custody — and taking custody will cost her, understand, cost her dearly — then the contents become evidence, and evidence can't be burned without burning the law itself. Clever. Your keeper taught you well."

"My keeper taught me everything," Jude said, and heard the crack in his own voice, and hated it.

Voss studied him for a long moment. Then he did something unexpected: he reached across the table and laid his spotted hand over Jude's wrist.

"I served him eleven years," the old man said. "I know what losing a keeper feels like. It feels like losing the ground under your feet and being expected to walk anyway." He squeezed once, hard, and let go. "Now. There's something else. There always is, with this book. Ask me, boy. Ask me what else I know."

Jude felt it coming the way he'd learned to feel weather coming — pressure behind the ears, a wrongness in the light. Across the room, Odile had gone very still.

"What else do you know?"

Voss turned back to the ledger, licked his thumb, and began working through the pages with the certainty of a man returning to a house he'd lived in. "Entries are numbered by year and grievance. The early ones are the magistrate's own hand. Then the war years, then the copies. Somewhere past the middle there's an entry the keepers argued about for a generation — whether it should stand, whether it was fair, whether grief excused anything. It stands. The magistrate ruled it stood, and the magistrate's rulings about his own book were final." He found the page. Stopped. Looked at Odile.

"Her father," Jude said. It wasn't a guess. He'd seen her stillness. He'd known, some part of him, since Cold Fork's smoke first rose above the trees — known that Odile Winterbourne, who never asked questions about the ledger, who flew them through weather that grounded every other pilot in the range without once asking what was in the chest, had a reason of her own.

"Ellis Winterbourne," Voss read. "Year thirty-one. Grievance number two hundred and six." He hesitated — and then, because the book did not permit hesitation, read on. "*Paid the sum of four hundred dollars by Consolidated Mining to locate and destroy the secondary copy of this record held at the Kettle Landing mission. Confirmed by two witnesses. The copy was burned in the mission stove on the night of the fourteenth. Mr. Winterbourne returned the payment untouched to the magistrate's office on the morning of the fifteenth, and was found on the sixteenth.*"

The stove ticked. Snow ticked against the window. Odile had not moved, and her stillness was the worst thing Jude had ever seen, worse than engines on a beach, worse than the keeper's cold hand — because it was the stillness of a woman holding a door shut against something enormous.

"Found how," she said. Her voice came out level. That was the terrible part. It came out completely level.

Voss's eyes dropped. "Hanged," he said. "In the mission bell tower. The entry records it as the magistrate recorded it. He recorded everything, ma'am. Even the things that shamed the book itself." He turned the page toward her. "There's more. Read the whole entry. All of it."

Odile crossed the room like a sleepwalker. She leaned over the page, and Jude watched her lips move through the magistrate's cramped, relentless script, and watched her face do nothing at all, and understood that whatever was happening behind it was too large to show.

"*Note appended,*" Voss read softly, when she didn't. "*Mr. Winterbourne took the money intending, by his own account left in writing, to buy medicine for his daughter, who was sick that winter. He burned the copy. He could not live with having done it. The magistrate notes that the daughter recovered. The magistrate further notes that he does not know what justice looks like here, and has written this entry anyway, because the book does not exist to make men comfortable. It exists so that what happened, happened on the record.*"

Odile straightened. She looked at her hands. When she spoke again, her voice was still level, and now Jude understood that the levelness was not calm. It was a woman walking a ledge.

"I had fever when I was six," she said. "I remember the doctor coming. I remember my mother crying with relief. I never knew where the money came from." A pause. "He hanged himself in a bell tower and I've spent my whole life running away from a town I thought had failed him."

"No," Voss said. "You've spent your whole life running from a story you were never told. That's different. That's fixable."

"Is it?" She laughed, and it was the worst sound Jude had ever heard her make. "He burned a copy of that book to save my life. Do you understand? Everything I am, I am because he—" She stopped. Pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "Every entry in that box ruined somebody. That's what it does. It finds the worst thing a person ever did and writes it down forever."

"And the best things," Jude said.

She lowered her hand and looked at him.

"The same entry," Jude said. "He gave the money back. He couldn't live with it — that's not nothing, that's the opposite of nothing. Most men who take consortium money keep it. The book doesn't just find the worst thing. It keeps the whole of it. That's why it's dangerous. That's why Rell wants it. Because nobody comes out of those pages clean, and nobody comes out of them all bad either." He heard himself, and was surprised. The keeper's voice was coming out of his mouth. It happened more often lately, and each time it hurt less. "Your father's in there whole. Not just the burning. The giving back. The sickness he was trying to cure. You."

Odile stared at him for a long time. Outside, the light failed entirely, and snow began to come down thick and silent, filling the space between the trees like static between stations.

"I need air," she said, and took the lantern, and went out into it.

* * *

Jude stood in the doorway and watched the lantern move down toward the river, small and yellow and stubborn, and did not follow her. Some doors you had to let people open alone.

"She'll come back," Voss said behind him. "Women like that always come back. It's the ones who run clean that you lose." He poured two cups of something amber and pushed one into Jude's hands. It smelled like a struck match. "Drink. You look like your keeper. Did anyone ever tell you that? Same way of standing. Like the ground owes you money."

"Everyone says it."

"They say it because it's true." Voss settled onto his bench with a groan, the ledger open on his knees again, his thumb resting on the page as if it were warm. "Boy. I need to tell you something, and you need to hear it tonight, because tomorrow you'll be gone and the snow will close the pass and I may never see another living soul who knows what this book is."

Jude drank. The liquor burned all the way down and lit a small fire in his stomach.

"When I carried pages for the magistrate, there were others. Six of us, at the height of it. Runners. We moved copies between the cave and the mission and the judge's chambers in the provincial seat, back when the seat had judges worth the name." He turned his cup slowly. "Two died natural. One drowned. One took consortium money and told us where the mission copy was hidden — that's how Ellis Winterbourne knew where to look, though he never knew who sold it, and I never told him, and God forgive me I never will, because the man's dead and ashamed enough."

Jude set down his cup. "And the sixth?"

"The sixth," Voss said, "was a young man named Corwin Rell. Good runner. Fast in the mountains. His grandfather's grievances are in that book — third generation, timber claims stolen up the Ash River, the whole family ground down to nothing by exactly the kind of men he works for now." The old man's voice dropped. "Rell didn't sell us out, boy. Rell just stopped believing the book could save anyone, including himself. He decided it was heavier than a man could carry, and he put it down, and the consortium picked it up and handed him a wage and a snowmobile and a list of names."

The fire ticked. Down by the river, the lantern had stopped moving.

"Captain Rell," Jude said. "The one who's hunting us."

"The same. And here's what you carry up that mountain besides paper, boy: when you meet him — and you will meet him, men like Rell are drawn to that book like frost to glass — remember that he isn't your enemy because he's evil. He's your enemy because he read those pages once and concluded that memory is a debt no one can ever finish paying. Your job is to prove him wrong." Voss closed the ledger and pressed both hands flat on its cover, like a man taking an oath. "Because if you can't convince Rell, you'll never convince the range. And if you can't convince the range, Judge Sorel can enter that book into record until kingdom come, and it'll be a museum piece. Dead paper. The book only lives if people believe remembering is worth the price."

The door opened. Cold poured in, and Odile with it, snow in her hair, her face raw and scrubbed and somehow rearranged — not healed, nothing so fast as healing, but aimed. She looked at Jude first, then at the ledger, then at Voss.

"How far to Sorel's court?" she asked.

"Four days, if the col stays open," Voss said. "Three, if you're mad. Five, if the storm—"

"Then we leave at first light." She crouched beside Jude and, to his astonishment, touched the chest — lightly, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal. "All of it goes in the record. Every page. Including my father's." Her jaw tightened. "Especially my father's."

Voss smiled without any joy in it at all. "Then you'd best sleep," he said. "Because I walked that col forty years ago in fair weather, and I'm telling you both — the storm that's coming down behind you is moving faster than any storm I've ever seen. Whatever drove it, it isn't weather."

Jude looked at the window, where the snow fell thick and soundless, and thought of engines on a beach, and did not sleep for a long time.
