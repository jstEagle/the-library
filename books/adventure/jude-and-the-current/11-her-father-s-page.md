## 11. Her Father's Page

The hermit's cabin smelled of spruce smoke and old paper, and Odile stood in the doorway of it for a long moment after he said the words, as if the cold outside might still be argued with.

*There's a page in there with your father's name on it.*

Jude had the chest open on the hermit's table. The ledger lay in its oilcloth wrapping like something surgical — which, she was beginning to understand, it was. It cut. It had been cutting for a hundred years.

"I don't want to see it," she said, and heard how thin that sounded in a room with two strangers who had both already lost everything to this box of paper.

The hermit — Corliss, he'd said, though he hadn't offered a hand to shake — banked the stove without looking at her. "Most people say that," he told the fire. "Then they look anyway. It's not a thing you can un-know, girl, but it's a thing you can carry. I've watched men carry worse."

"I've carried plenty."

"Sure." He settled onto his stool, joints cracking like green wood. "But not your own name."

Jude hadn't touched the ledger since Corliss spoke. His hands rested flat on either side of it, the way he'd rested them when they first met in the cave — a boy standing guard over a thing older than his grief. Now he looked up at her across the table, and there was no pity in his face, which she was grateful for, and no hesitation either, which she wasn't.

"It's yours to read or not read," he said. "That's the rule. Nobody reads another person's page aloud. Not even me. Not even if it would save my life." A beat. "It nearly did once, with Rell. But that was different. Rell's page is about him. Yours is about you."

*My page.* As if she'd been in the ledger all along, waiting, filed away between a drowned fisherman and a cheated widow, patient as tide.

Odile pulled out the second chair. It scraped loud on the plank floor. She sat down the way she'd learned to sit down before hard flying — straps tight, hands where they belonged, no wasted motion — because the alternative was not sitting at all.

"Show me."

Jude unwrapped the oilcloth. The ledger was thicker than she'd imagined, bound in hide gone dark as peat, its spine stitched twice where someone had repaired it. Pages of varying ages, various hands, some copied in a careful schoolroom script, some scrawled by lantern light. Jude turned through them with the reverence of a man handling dynamite, and stopped.

He rotated the book to face her and took his hands off it entirely, stepping back to the wall, giving her the room the way you give a pilot the controls.

She read.

---

*Entry 214. Winter of the year the Kettle froze early.*

*Complaint entered by Maren Ostergaard, weaver, of Cold Fork: that a copy of this record, being kept in the house of Tomas Winterbourne, freighter, was demanded of him by agents of the Consolidated Range & Mining Company, and that he accepted payment for its destruction.*

*The sum was forty dollars and a promise of continued hauling contracts.*

*The copy was burned in his own stove, witnessed by his daughter, aged nine.*

*Tomas Winterbourne came to me in the spring. He could not live under what he had done. He asked me whether a man could put back what he had burned. I told him the truth: that memory survives in more places than paper, but that a man must choose to be one of those places, and that choosing costs everything he has left.*

*He chose. He is recorded here not as a thief but as a witness, because he gave testimony against the company agents before he died, and his testimony stands.*

*He hanged himself from the beam of his own barn on the third of May. He left a note. It said: Let the record show I knew what it cost and paid it, and let no one tell my girl her father was a coward, because a coward burns quietly and keeps the money.*

*Let the record so show.*

---

The cabin went very far away. The stove ticked. Somewhere outside a raven said one flat syllable and quit. Odile became aware that she had read the entry three times and that her eyes were dry, which surprised her, because she could feel something enormous moving underneath the dryness, the way you feel a river under ice.

*Aged nine.* She had been nine. She remembered the stove now — God help her, she remembered it — the orange light on her father's face, the smell of scorched vellum, the way he'd fed the pages in one at a time like a man dosing medicine he didn't believe in. She had thought it was just papers. Old papers. She remembered asking if she could warm her hands, and him saying yes, *yes, sweetheart, warm your hands,* in a voice she had spent thirty years forgetting and had never actually forgotten at all.

Forty dollars and hauling contracts. Her whole childhood — the moving, the new towns, the mother going quiet then going gone, the winters of borrowed bread — all of it priced at forty dollars and a route she'd spent her life re-creating in the air.

"You knew," she said. Her voice came out level, which frightened her more than screaming would have. "Corliss. You knew before you said it."

"I served the magistrate twenty years." The old man didn't flinch. "I copied half those entries myself. I knew the name Winterbourne the way you know a scar. When you walked in behind the boy, I knew the face too. You have his jaw."

She did have his jaw. She'd been told so her whole life, usually by people in towns she was leaving.

Jude spoke from the wall, quiet. "The last part. Did you read the last part?"

"The note."

"Read it again. Out loud, if you can."

"I can't—"

"You can." He pushed off the wall and came to the table, and he didn't touch the book or her, but he stood close enough that she could feel him willing her upright. "That's the whole point of the ledger. It doesn't just write down what people did wrong. It writes down what they did after. My keeper made me memorize that rule before he taught me anything else. *The ledger is not a list of sins, Jude. It's a list of what people are worth.* Read it again."

Odile looked down at the page. At her father's handwriting in the magistrate's transcription — no, wait. She looked closer. The final lines weren't in the magistrate's neat script at all. They were pasted in, a strip of paper glued below the entry, browned with age, the ink faded to the color of weak tea. Her father's actual hand. She knew it instantly, the way you know a voice on the radio. The slanted capitals. The way the *g*s looped low. She had seen that handwriting on grocery lists, on notes pinned to doors in six different houses.

*Let no one tell my girl her father was a coward.*

Her vision blurred and she let it. She put her palm flat on the table beside the book, steadying herself the way she'd steady a wing in crosswind, and she cried without sound, because sound would have meant letting go of the table, and she wasn't ready to let go of anything ever again.

Corliss got up without ceremony and poured three cups of spruce tea and set one at her elbow, and neither man said a word until she'd finished, because they were cave people and ledger people and they understood that some readings take as long as they take.

* * *

Later — an hour, maybe two; time had gone soft — she sat by the stove with the tea going cold in her hands and tried to do what she always did with unbearable cargo: strap it down and get airborne.

It wouldn't strap.

Thirty years of running, and she finally saw the shape of the runway. Every town she'd left before the leaving got comfortable. Every contract she'd finished early. Every man she'd liked and then vanished on — poor Devlin at Kettle Landing, who still probably thought she was coming back for that dinner. She'd told herself it was restlessness, wanderlust, the bush pilot's disease, something in the blood, something free. And all of it, every mile, had been one long attempt to outrun a stove in a house in Cold Fork, to stay ahead of a fire that had happened when she was nine years old and watching her hands get warm.

She hadn't even known what was burning. That was the part that doubled her over. They'd never told her. Her mother had packed them out of Cold Fork within the month and never once said the word *why*, and Odile had built a whole life on top of the silence like a strip built over muskeg, and now she understood why the landing gear had never quite felt solid anywhere.

"He testified," she said at last. "Before he died. He gave testimony against the agents."

"He did." Corliss nodded slowly. "Spring assizes, the year after. I sat in the gallery. Skinny man, freckled, hands shaking so bad he had to grip the rail. But he stood up there and named names — Harrow and Pike, the two company men — and he described the payment to the dollar. The case collapsed anyway. Company lawyers, bought jury, the usual weather. But the testimony went into the record, and the record went into the ledger, and here we are." He tapped ash into the stove. "Your father lost everything that spring except the truth, and he spent the truth like his last coin, and then he was done. That's not a coward, girl. That's a man paying retail."

"And nobody told me." Her voice cracked on it finally. "Nobody ever told me."

"Your mother thought she was protecting you."

"She was burying me." Odile stood up. The motion surprised her; she hadn't decided to stand. "There's a difference. There's a — thirty years of difference."

Jude was watching her from across the room with that feral-polite attention of his, and she realized he was seeing something in her face that she couldn't. "What?" she asked him.

"You're not running anymore," he said simply. "I've been watching you since the cave. You're always halfway to the door. Even asleep, you're angled toward it. You're not now."

She looked at the door. It was right there, four steps, and for the first time in her adult life it held no pull whatsoever. The storm outside was real and cold and full of snow, and none of it mattered, because the thing she'd been fleeing had caught her in a hermit's cabin over spruce tea, and it turned out the catching didn't kill you. It just stood there and let you look at it.

"My father's name is in that book as a witness," she said. "Not a thief. The magistrate wrote it down so it would hold."

"That's what it's for."

"Then it holds." She crossed to the chest and stood over the ledger, and her hand hovered above it the way it used to hover over the throttle before a short-field takeoff — that suspended moment where commitment becomes irreversible. "And here's what else holds. Those agents, Harrow and Pike. Are their names in here too?"

Corliss smiled without any warmth at all. "Pages sixty-one through sixty-four. Harrow died rich. Pike's grandson sits on the consortium board. The company your friends with the snowmobiles work for was built on the backs of entries one through ninety, and they know it, and that's why there's a price on that boy's head, and that's why keepers die, and that's why a fourteen-year-old has been living in a hole in a cliff eating gull eggs."

"Then we leave tonight."

Jude straightened. "Storm's still—"

"I've flown into worse than this. I've *landed* in worse than this." She was pacing now, three steps each way, the small cabin becoming a cockpit around her. "I spent thirty years running from a fire. You know what I never did? I never once went back and looked at what burned. Well, I'm looking now. And what I see is a company that paid forty dollars to blind a whole range of people, and has been paying ever since, and thinks it's about to pay off the last page too." She stopped in front of Jude. "They're not getting your ledger, kid. And they're not getting Sorel's courthouse. We're crossing that range if we have to walk it on our teeth."

"That's the spirit I was hoping for," Corliss said mildly, "though I'd have settled for crying and a nap."

"I did the crying. The nap's cancelled."

Jude was already repacking the chest, quick and precise, but he paused with the oilcloth half-folded and looked at her with an expression she'd learn to know well in the weeks ahead — the look of a boy deciding to trust someone with the only thing he had. "Your father's page," he said. "When we get to Sorel — it goes into the public record with all the others. Everyone will know. Is that what you want?"

Odile thought about it honestly, the way she'd check a fuel gauge twice.

"Yes," she said. "Because a coward burns quietly and keeps the money. And my father wasn't one. And I'm done letting other people decide what my family's story says." She picked up her parka. "Besides. Somebody in this partnership has to model good behavior, and it's clearly not going to be the fourteen-year-old."

Something that was almost a grin crossed Jude's face, there and gone like a fish rising.

Outside, the wind had backed around to the northwest and was carrying the smell of heavy snow. Corliss walked them as far as the treeline, pressed a sack of dried whitefish and a tin of matches into Jude's pack, and gripped Odile's forearm hard enough to leave marks.

"Harrow and Pike," he said. "Sixty-one through sixty-four. When it gets hard — and it will — remember that your father's testimony is still standing in there after eighty years. Paper burns. Testimony doesn't. Not while somebody carries it."

They reached the edge of his clearing, and Odile stopped and looked back once at the little cabin with its smoking chimney, and then she didn't look back again, because looking back was a habit belonging to a woman who no longer existed.

Ahead, the range stood up black and white against the bruised sky, and somewhere beyond it a judge was holding a courtroom against the weight of money, and somewhere behind them engines were surely climbing the coast road.

"Which pass?" she asked.

Jude consulted the sky the way other boys consulted watches. "Sorel's seat is northeast. But that storm's coming down the main col like it's got a grudge. If we move tonight and move fast, we make the timber shelter below the Col before it closes." He shouldered the chest and settled it like it weighed nothing, like it wasn't the most dangerous object in the province. "Two days' hard walking. Three if the weather argues."

"Then we argue back," Odile said, and stepped off the hermit's clearing into the trees, toward the col, toward the courthouse, toward the first ground in thirty years she had any intention of standing on.
