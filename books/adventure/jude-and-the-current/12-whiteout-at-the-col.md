## 12. Whiteout at the Col

The storm came a day early, the way debts do.

Odile felt it before she saw it — a pressure behind her ears, a taste like tin on the wind, the light going flat and sourceless over the high country. Jude had been teaching her to read weather the way keepers read tides, and she was starting to understand that the sky, like the sea, gave warnings only to people who already believed it would kill them.

"Smell that?" Jude said. He'd stopped on the trail ahead of her, nose lifted, and for a moment he looked less like a fourteen-year-old boy in a coat two sizes too big and more like something older wearing him.

"Tin," Odile said. "I know."

"That's not tin." He crouched, pressed his bare hand flat against a boulder, and held it there like he was taking its pulse. "That's cold coming down off the icefield faster than it should. The col's going to close tonight. Maybe sooner."

They had been three days out from Cold Fork, climbing through larch country gone gold and then bare, following a route Jude knew from maps he'd memorized rather than carried. The ledger rode against his chest in its oilskin wrap, strapped under his coat where he swore he could feel its weight even when he slept. Behind them, somewhere down in the river valleys, Rell's silence was holding — or not. There was no way to know. That was the trouble with mercy: you couldn't bank it.

"We could drop low," Odile said, though she already knew the answer. "Wait it out below treeline."

"If we drop low, we lose two days. Two days is enough for them to get ahead of us through the pass road." Jude stood, brushing snow grit from his knees. "We make the col today. We shelter above treeline."

"Above treeline." Odile looked up at the notch between the peaks, a gray seam in a gray sky, still hours away. "You want to sleep in a whiteout with no trees, no fuel, and enforcers climbing behind us."

"I want to be alive when the storm ends," Jude said. "The col has a wind scoop on the north side. Old keeper route. There's a bivvy site my teacher used — he called it the Post Office, because letters got left there. Rock overhang, dry floor if you dig right."

"And if we dig wrong?"

"Then we dig again."

She almost laughed. That was the thing about the boy that kept catching her off guard — he treated catastrophe as a scheduling problem. She'd flown twenty years in the interior and she still met every storm with her stomach knotted around itself. Jude met them the way water meets rock: without argument, just redirection.

"All right," she said. "Post Office. But we move fast."

They moved fast. Or they moved as fast as Odile's body allowed, which was the honest arithmetic of it. Her lungs burned at this altitude in a way they hadn't ten years ago, and her left boot — the one she'd patched with duct tape in Kettle Landing and never replaced — was letting wet in at the toe. She didn't mention it. Mentioning it would mean stopping, and stopping meant the men behind them closing distance, and so she filed the wet toe away in the same drawer where she kept everything else she refused to look at directly.

By early afternoon the light had gone wrong entirely. Not dark — worse than dark, because dark has edges. This was a whiteness without floor or ceiling, the world reduced to the six feet of ground visible underfoot and nothing else. Odile had flown into whiteouts; she knew what they did to instruments. She was learning what they did to legs.

Jude stopped so suddenly she walked into him.

"What?"

He pointed. She saw nothing. Then, following his finger, she saw the faintest interruption in the white — a shadow with no object casting it, a suggestion of slope where there should have been none.

"Cornice," he said quietly. "The whole ridge edge is loaded. If we walk out there, we ride it down."

"How can you possibly see that in this?"

"You don't see it. You feel where the wind's been." He turned them ninety degrees, away from the invisible edge, and began traversing on a bearing he held in his head, counting paces under his breath. Odile followed the sound of his counting the way she'd once followed a compass needle, and hated how much comfort she took in it.

* * *

They found the Post Office an hour before full dark, which by then meant nothing, because there was no full dark — only a deepening of the white. It was exactly what Jude had promised: a slab of overhanging granite leaning off the mountainside like a door ajar, with a pocket of shelter beneath it maybe eight feet deep and four high. Someone had been here before them, long ago. A ring of stones sat against one wall, blackened. Scratched into the rock above the fire ring, in letters worn nearly smooth, someone had carved: LEAVE WHAT YOU CAN. TAKE WHAT YOU NEED.

"Letters," Odile said, running her fingers over the carving.

"Keepers' route ran through here for sixty years. Magistrate's couriers, then just keepers after him." Jude was already moving with a purpose that shamed her exhaustion — dropping his pack, unrolling the shovel-blade he wore strapped to it, testing the floor with the blade's edge. "There'll be a cache hole. There always is."

"There always is," Odile repeated. "You say that like it's scripture."

"It kind of is."

He found it under a flat stone near the back wall: a cavity lined with birch bark, bone dry. Empty except for half a candle stub and a tin box containing three matches, a fishhook, and a note written in faded pencil on paper gone soft as cloth. Jude read it by headlamp, and his face did something complicated.

"What does it say?"

"'Last entry, 1971. Cache emptied by party of four, nothing returned. Shame on you, whoever you are.'" Jude folded the note carefully and put it back in the tin. "My teacher wrote that. He was young then. He checked this site every year of his life hoping somebody would pay it back."

Odile thought about that — a man carrying a grudge across fifty years over a handful of matches — and found she couldn't smile at it. She understood it completely. Some accounts you keep because no one else will.

They dug in. Jude showed her how: dig down into the slope until you hit the harder old snow, wall the entrance with cut blocks, leave a vent gap no wider than your fist. The work was brutal and she was clumsy at it, her hands going stupid with cold between rounds of digging, and twice Jude took the shovel from her without comment and finished what she'd started. She let him. Twenty years ago she would have bristled. Now she understood that letting someone help was its own kind of honesty.

By the time the walls were up, the storm had arrived in earnest. The wind hit the col like a freight train taking a curve, screaming across the notch, and inside the shelter the sound became a pressure, a living thing pressing on the snow blocks, testing them. Odile wedged herself against the back wall beside Jude and tried to calculate their supplies without wanting to.

One day of food, if they split it thin. Half a stove bottle of fuel. Her left boot soaked through. Jude's gloves worn to lace at the fingertips.

"And they're behind us," she said.

"They were behind us this morning." Jude fed a pinch of stove flame to melt snow, the blue light making his face look carved. "In this, nobody climbs. Even Rell's people aren't stupid."

"People who burn cabins aren't famous for their judgment."

He didn't answer that. The stove hissed. Outside, the wind found a new register, higher, and something far off boomed — an avalanche running somewhere in the invisible dark, the mountain clearing its throat.

"Jude." She waited until he looked at her. In the stove light his eyes were very steady, and very tired, and she realized with a small shock that he was scared. He'd been scared the whole time, probably, and had simply decided fear wasn't useful enough to show. "If the walls come down tonight. If something happens. You need to know where I stand."

"Okay."

"The ledger matters. I believe that now — I've seen what's in it, I've seen what it costs people. But it's paper. You're not." She heard her own voice go rough and was surprised. "If it comes to saving one or the other, I save you. I want you to hear me say it while I still can."

For a long moment the boy said nothing. The stove ticked. The mountain roared.

"My teacher told me something the last week he was alive," Jude said finally. "He said the chest isn't the ledger. The chest is just wood and oilskin. The ledger is whoever remembers it." He pulled his coat tighter. "So if you save me, you save both."

Odile turned her face away, because there was no reason for her eyes to be burning and she refused to call it anything else. Above treeline, pinned in a whiteout, hunted, half-starved, and the boy was handing her a theology that made her the keeper too.

"Eat," she said, dividing the food. "And then we check each other's feet. Frostbite doesn't care about our feelings."

* * *

They took turns through the night. That was the arrangement, unspoken and absolute: one slept, one watched the vent gap and the walls and the other person's color. When Odile woke from her first shift of sleep she found Jude watching her instead of the wall, and when she woke from the second, her boots were off and her socks were drying against his chest inside his coat, and he pretended to be asleep when she stirred.

Her toes were bad. Not black, not yet, but the two smallest ones on the left foot stayed white and waxy no matter how she warmed them, and the pain when feeling came back was a bright animal thing that made her bite down on her sleeve. Jude worked them the way his teacher must have taught him — firm pressure, rewarming, never rubbing — murmuring instructions like a prayer. "Tell me if it goes numb again. Numb is worse than pain. Pain means it's arguing with you."

"It's arguing," she managed. "Loudly."

"Good. Keep it loud."

Somewhere in the deepest part of the night the wind changed key, dropped, and for twenty minutes there was a silence so total it rang. In that silence Odile heard, faint and impossible, a sound she knew better than her own heartbeat: engines. Snow machines, throttling up and down, searching, somewhere below the col on the south approach.

She crawled to the vent gap. Nothing — pure white, the world erased. But the sound was real, moving along the contour of the mountain like a shark circling a reef, patient, methodical.

"Jude."

He was awake instantly, of course. He listened for ten seconds and his face went grim. "South side. They're running the summer route."

"In this?"

"Somebody's paying them enough to try." He was already repacking, movements quick and economical. "When the wind comes back it'll cover the sound. They'll lose the trail. But if it drops again—"

"It won't stay down. Storms like this breathe."

"Then we have a problem," Jude said, "because we can't move, and they can."

* * *

Dawn, if it came, came as a marginally less terrible shade of white. The wind had settled into a sustained scream rather than a shriek, and the temperature had fallen through some floor Odile didn't have a name for. Their fuel was down to fumes. The food was gone except for a strip of pemmican Jude insisted she take and a spoonful of sugar he claimed not to want.

She watched him sit very still against the wall, conserving, and she understood with terrible clarity what she was looking at: a boy running a caloric deficit through his second night of survival arithmetic, giving himself the smaller share and lying about it badly. His lips were cracked. His hands, when he pulled them from his sleeves, were slow.

"When we get down," she said, "I'm buying you breakfast. Whatever you want. Eggs, bacon, all of it."

"Pancakes," he said immediately, and then, embarrassed, "with the lemon. My teacher made them on solstice mornings. He said sugar was a keeper's privilege."

"Sugar is a keeper's privilege," Odile agreed solemnly. "Pancakes with lemon at the first town past the pass. That's a contract."

"That's a contract." And he smiled — the first true smile she'd seen on him since Cold Fork, brief and crooked, and it did more for her morale than any amount of food could have.

Then the wind gusted, shifted, and through the vent gap came a sound that froze them both: voices. Human voices, shredded by wind but unmistakable, close — closer than the engines had been, much closer. On the ridge above them, or beside them, or somewhere in the annihilating white where distance had stopped meaning anything.

Jude snuffed the stove with his bare fingers. In the sudden dimness they looked at each other, and Odile saw him reach slowly, silently, for the ice axe.

"Post Office rules," he breathed. "Leave what you can. Take what you need."

Outside, gravel crunched under a boot. One boot. Then another. Someone was walking the ledge above the overhang, kicking at the snow blocks of their wall, and a voice called out — not Rell's voice, rougher, younger, uncertain:

"Anyone in there? We've got a man down! Do you copy? Anyone!"

Odile looked at Jude. Jude looked back, and mouthed one word:

*Bait.*
