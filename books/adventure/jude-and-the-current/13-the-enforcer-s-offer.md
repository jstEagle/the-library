## 13. The Enforcer's Offer

The storm had passed in the night, and the world it left behind was a country made of glass.

Jude woke first, as he always did, and lay still inside the bivouac sack listening to the forest tick and settle under its new weight of ice. Every branch wore a sheath of frozen rain. When the wind moved through the spruce, the whole slope rang like a chandelier in an empty house. Somewhere below them, a limb gave way with a crack like a rifle shot, and he counted to ten out of habit before deciding no one was shooting.

Odile slept with her broken wrist bound against her chest, her breath fogging and fading, fogging and fading. Three days since the timberline. Her face had gone thin in a way that frightened him more than any engine noise ever had — the face of someone spending money she didn't have. They had half a tin of pemmican, a handful of tea, and by his reckoning two days' walk to the valley road that led down to the provincial seat.

He wormed out of the sack without waking her and stood among the trees, working feeling back into his fingers. The sky above the ridge line was the color of old pewter, clean at the edges, which meant cold and meant clear and meant they could move. He was scooping snow into the pot for meltwater when he heard it.

Not engines. Boots.

The sound came from downstream, where the creek dropped through a narrow gully toward the river flats — the crunch-crunch-slide of a man walking on glazed snow who knew how to do it without hurrying. Jude froze with the pot in his hands. One set of boots. That was wrong. Rell's teams traveled in fours; he'd heard them enough nights on the traverse to know their arithmetic. One set of boots meant either a scout — or something else.

He looked back at Odile. She hadn't stirred. He thought about waking her and decided, in the half-second the boots gave him, not to. If it was trouble, she'd need every minute of sleep she could bank. If it wasn't —

He set the pot down, took the ledger chest's strap in his fist, and walked upstream into the trees, away from her, drawing whatever it was after him.

* * *

Rell was waiting for him at the bend of the creek, standing in the open with his hands visible at his sides, which was its own kind of message. A man with a rifle doesn't show you his hands.

He looked worse than the last time Jude had seen him, back on the river flats before the rapids — gray at the temples now that the hood was down, a cut healing crooked over one eyebrow from the whiteout climb. But he stood the way he'd always stood, planted, patient, like a fence post that had learned to watch things.

"Morning," Rell said.

"You're alone."

"I am."

"Your men don't like being alone with me." It wasn't bravado. It was bookkeeping. Jude had read aloud to three of them already — Voss in the pass, the brothers at Cold Fork, the young one whose name might have been Tam. Memory did things to enforcers that bullets couldn't.

"My men are two miles back at the timber camp," Rell said. "I told them I was running a patrol line east." He turned his palms up, a small shrug. "I wanted to talk without an audience. So did you, or you'd have woken the pilot."

Jude said nothing. The strap of the chest bit into his palm. Above them the iced branches chimed.

"I'm going to make you an offer," Rell went on. "And before you spit at it, I want you to understand that I know exactly what you're carrying. Not what the consortium thinks it is — a box of paper, leverage, dirt. I mean what it actually is. I've been reading your family's handwriting my whole career, boy, one way or another. Every report I've ever filed has been an attempt to keep people from finding out what's in that chest."

"Then you know I won't sell it."

"I know you won't *sell* it." Rell's voice stayed level, almost gentle, and that gentleness was the most frightening thing about him. "I'm not asking you to sell it. I'm asking you to *stop*. There's a difference, and the difference is worth more than you think."

He began to walk, slowly, along the creek bank, and Jude — hating himself for it — fell into step, because a man walking away is easier to listen to than a man bearing down on you.

"The consortium will pay you a keeper's wage for life," Rell said. "Real wage, not company scrip. A house in the valley, any valley you name, with your name on the deed. Schooling, if you want it — and you should, because you've got a head on you that's been fed nothing but grievance for fourteen years and it deserves better food. In exchange, the chest comes to me. Not burned. Not dumped in the sea. To *me*, sealed, held in trust. And here's the part you'll want to call a lie, so I'll say it slow: nothing in it gets destroyed while I'm alive. It just stops moving. Stops being a weapon anyone can pick up."

"And when you die?"

"Then it's somebody else's problem, same as it was your keeper's, same as it was yours." Rell stopped and faced him. Steam rose off both of them in the bitter air. "You think I don't know how that sounds? You think I haven't lain awake doing the arithmetic? One boy's freedom against every settlement in this range getting dragged through twenty years of hearings and feuds and lawyers, every old debt dug up and waved around, neighbor testifying against neighbor because some dead magistrate wrote down what their grandfather did in 1891. You've seen what that book does to people. You've *used* what that book does to people. Don't tell me you haven't noticed."

Jude had noticed. He thought of Rell's own men going quiet when he read to them. He thought of Odile's face in the hermit's cabin, the night the entry with her father's name surfaced — the way a page could reach out of a hundred years ago and close its hand around a living throat.

"That's not my decision to make," he said. "It isn't mine to bury. The keepers kept it so it would be *everybody's* someday. That's the whole point. You're offering me the one thing I have no right to give."

"I'm offering you the only thing that keeps you alive past Thursday." For the first time Rell's composure cracked, just a hairline, just enough to see the tiredness underneath. "There's a second team on the range, boy. Kessler's. Six men, and Kessler doesn't read. Doesn't care what's in the chest. His orders are to take it and leave no witnesses, and those orders come from people who sign his pay, same as mine, except nobody signs mine anymore because I've stopped being useful. I can slow him. I can't stop him. Take the deal, and there's nothing left on this mountain worth killing you for."

The wind moved through the ice-laden spruce and the whole slope rang, long and silver, like the cave breathing.

Jude stood very still. And the terrible thing — the thing he would carry afterward, the temptation with teeth — was that part of him *wanted* it. Not the house, not the wage. The stopping. Fourteen years of watching, copying, memorizing, hauling a dead man's duty through blizzards while men hunted him for the crime of remembering. He was so tired. He was fourteen years old and he was so tired, and here was a man offering, in a level voice, permission to put it all down.

His hand found the chest strap. Under his mittens, through canvas and oilcloth, he could feel the edge of the pages. Ten thousand entries. Eleven keepers. A hundred and sixty years of everything the law refused to touch.

"No," he said.

Rell closed his eyes briefly, like a man hearing a diagnosis he'd expected.

"Then hear my counter-offer," Jude said.

* * *

He set the chest down on a flat stone beside the creek, unstrapped it, and lifted out the ledger with both hands, the way he'd been taught — spine supported, never by the boards alone. The cold had stiffened the leather. He opened it against his forearm to the ribbon his keeper had tied there years ago, the marker for the R section, because a good keeper knew his ledger the way a pilot knew her charts, and because he had known, since the moment he'd heard the single set of boots, that if Rell ever caught him alone, this was the page he'd fight with.

Not a rifle. A page.

"Reuben Rell," Jude read. "Timber faller, Cold Fork district. Entered spring of the magistrate's ninth year."

"Don't," Rell said quietly.

"Complaint entered by Reuben Rell, age nineteen. States that the Alder Creek Mining and Timber Company docked his father's wages eleven months running for 'cabin rent, tool wear, and store credit,' leaving the family four dollars for the year. States his sister Maren, age six, died in February of the cough, and that the company doctor charged two dollars for the visit and wrote *exposure due to parental neglect* on the certificate. States his father walked up the flume road that night and did not come back, and that the company posted notice the next morning that wages lost to desertion would be recovered from kin."

The creek ran under its lid of ice. Rell had not moved. His face had gone to something carved and gray, but he had not moved, and he had not told him to stop again.

"Magistrate's disposition," Jude continued, and here his voice steadied, because this was the part, this was always the part, the part the keepers died for: "Company practice of wage docking found to be debt bondage under color of contract. Ordered discontinued throughout the district. Ordered the Rell account cleared and two years' back wages paid. Ordered the words *parental neglect* struck from the child's record and corrected in the district register. Noted for the permanent record: the law of the province declined jurisdiction in this matter. This office does not decline."

He looked up.

"He paid your father," Jude said. "He cleared your family's debt. He made the company write the truth about your sister into the register, where it still is, because registers get copied and copied and copied. Your grandfather signed a receipt for those back wages. It's in here. His mark, next to the magistrate's hand. That's why you know this book is real, Captain. That's why you've never once tried to take it by force yourself, in all these months of hunting us. You could have rushed us at the river with six men and taken it. You never did. Because somewhere in your house growing up there was a story about a winter when a man with a ledger made the company pay, and you have never been able to make yourself be the one who burns that story down."

"Stop," Rell said, and this time it came out rough.

"No," Jude said. "You got to make your offer. I get to make mine. Here it is." He closed the ledger gently and laid his hand flat on the cover. "Kessler's team comes for us, and you stand between. Not for the consortium. Not for me. For Maren Rell, age six, whose death certificate the magistrate corrected a hundred and fifty years ago, because somebody had to, because *somebody always has to*. You stand between, and when this reaches Judge Sorel — and it will, Captain, whether I'm alive to carry it or not, because Odile knows the contents and so do you now, and memory doesn't freeze to death — you testify. On the record. What the consortium ordered. What Kessler's orders were. And when it's done, the ledger goes into the public record where anybody can read your grandfather's receipt, and nobody — not the consortium, not me, not God — can ever use it against your family again, because it belongs to everyone, which means it belongs to no one's enemies."

Rell stared at him for a long time. The wind had died. The only sound was the creek, ticking under its ice, counting something out.

"You're fourteen years old," Rell said at last, and there was wonder in it, and disgust, and something that might have been grief.

"I'm a keeper," Jude said. "We start early. We don't get to choose."

Rell looked away, up the slope, toward the timber camp where his men waited two miles off believing he was walking a patrol line east. When he spoke again his voice had gone flat and professional, the voice of a man folding a map.

"Kessler crossed the divide yesterday. He'll hit the valley road ahead of you — he knows the low route, he helped survey it. You can't go down the way you're planning." He pulled a folded paper from inside his coat and held it out. "Old fire road, west fork, marked on here. It adds a day. It puts you into the seat from the north, past the mill yards, and it stinks of creosote the whole way, but Kessler won't look for you on a road he thinks he owns the memory of." A pause. "That's not standing between. That's just pointing. Don't mistake it."

"I won't," Jude said. "But I'll write it down."

Something moved across Rell's face then — not a smile, nothing near a smile. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man seeing the trap he'd been in all along close gently around him, and finding, strangely, that it fit.

"Your pilot," he said, turning to go. "Her wrist is bad, and she's hiding how bad. I've carried men with wrists like that. Get her down the mountain or she loses the hand." He took three steps, stopped, and said, without turning around: "Maren. Nobody ever said her name right since. Everybody says *Marren*. It's *Mah-ren*. My grandmother used to correct people until the day she died."

"Mah-ren," Jude repeated.

Rell nodded once and walked away downstream, crunch-crunch-slide, unhurried, hands loose at his sides, until the ice-hung trees took him.

Jude stood a long while with the ledger in his arms. Then he repacked the chest, cinched the straps, and went back up the slope to wake Odile and show her a fire road drawn on consortium paper — and to tell her that the man who'd hunted them across the whole range had just become, whether he liked it or not, the newest name in the book.
