## 14. Rell's Silence

Rell stood in the falling snow with his hands loose at his sides and did not draw his weapon.

Odile had the flare gun up before her mind caught up to her arm — she'd carried it since the coast, a habit of a woman who had learned that the world's mercy was mostly weather — and she held it level at his chest while Jude stood beside her with the ledger chest between his feet like something he'd sooner die defending than surrender. The wind came down off the col in long white gusts, erasing them all by turns.

"You read to me," Rell said. His voice was flat, pitched under the wind so it wouldn't carry to his men below the ridge. "Out of that book. My grandmother's name."

"Anya Rell," Jude said. "Fined forty days' wages for timber she never cut. The magistrate wrote it down because no court would hear her. She walked eleven miles to file a grievance and was turned away twice."

"I know what she did." Rell's jaw worked. Snow gathered on the shoulders of his parka and he didn't brush it off. He looked, for a moment, less like an enforcer than like a man standing at his own grave. "I know what she did. I've known since I was nine years old. My father told me the story every winter like it was scripture." He exhaled, and the breath tore away sideways. "You think I don't know why you're carrying that box?"

"Then let us pass," Odile said.

The flare gun trembled. She hated that it trembled. She'd held it on men before — once on a drunk with a rifle outside Kettle Landing, once, years ago, on a man who'd tried to take her cargo and her plane both — and her hands had been stone then. But this was different. This wasn't a threat she could end with one bright burning second. This was a man deciding something, slowly, in front of them, and no amount of powder in the world could hurry him.

Rell looked at the ledger. Then at Jude. Then, last and longest, at her.

"The consortium pays me to bring back the boy and burn the book," he said. "That's the job. That's always been the job." A pause, filled with wind. "I'm not doing the job today."

He turned and started back down the slope, boots punching through the crust, and then stopped without turning around.

"They won't stop coming," he said. "Voss has a second team on the east side of the range. They don't negotiate. They don't read. If you're smart, you'll be over the col before they close the high route — and if you're not smart, none of this mattered." He walked three more steps. "And woman — the offer stands. It'll always stand. That's the trouble with money. It doesn't get bored."

Then he was gone into the white, and Odile lowered the flare gun only when the sound of his descent had faded entirely, swallowed by the storm.

* * *

They didn't speak for an hour.

They moved instead — down off the exposed shoulder of the col, along a shelf of rock Jude seemed to know from maps he'd memorized rather than walked, until they found a hollow beneath an overhang where the wind couldn't reach and the snow fell straight instead of sideways. Jude got a fire going with a skill that still unsettled her, feeding it slivers of dry wood from a bundle he'd carried since Cold Fork, cupping the flame like it was a living thing he'd been entrusted with. Odile melted snow in the dented pot and watched the water shiver toward boiling and thought about nothing, deliberately, the way you hold your hand over a wound.

It was Jude who broke the silence, because he was fourteen and fourteen cannot hold silence longer than an hour.

"He let us go," Jude said. "Why would he let us go?"

"Because you read him his own family out of that box," Odile said. "Because for about ninety seconds, you weren't a boy with a price on your head. You were the voice of a dead woman named Anya, and he couldn't shoot his own grandmother."

Jude frowned at the fire. "I didn't do it to manipulate him. I did it because it was true."

"I know you did." She poured hot water over the last of their tea, weak as ditchwater, and handed him a cup. "That's what makes it worse."

"Worse?" He looked genuinely baffled, and she remembered — not for the first time — how young he was, how the cave had kept him innocent in some ways even as it armed him in others. "How is it worse? We're alive. He's letting us go. The book saved us."

"The book bought us," Odile said. "There's a difference, and you'd better learn it before Voss's second team finds us, because they won't be buying."

She wrapped her hands around her own cup and stared into the steam, and the thing she'd been refusing to think about all afternoon finally arrived, the way weather arrives — all at once, from behind.

"Think about what just happened," she said. "A man whose job is to destroy that ledger let two strangers walk away because of what's written inside it. Not because we were right. Not because the law is on our side. Because the ledger gave *him* something. A grievance. A story where his family are the victims. You handed a mercenary a mirror and told him he was beautiful, and he believed it, and here we sit."

"So?"

"So ask yourself what happens when the next man looks in that mirror and sees something else." She set the cup down. "What if someone reads that ledger and finds their enemy's name in it? What if they decide the magistrate's judgment is a license — that the record says this man wronged my family, therefore I'm owed, therefore anything I take back is justice? What if a whole town decides that? What if a mine owner buys a page, or burns a page, or adds a page?" She shook her head. "That box isn't justice, Jude. It's power. And power doesn't care who holds it. It corrupts as easy as it redeems. Maybe easier. Redemption takes work. Corruption just takes wanting."

Jude set his cup down with exaggerated care, the way he did when he was angry and trying not to show it. She'd learned that about him on the traverse: the politeness was armor, and underneath it was a temper like a deep lake — slow to move, but enormous once it did.

"That book is the only reason any of those people were ever counted," he said. "The miners. The homesteaders. The people the courts wouldn't touch because the consortium owned the courts. Nobody wrote them down anywhere else. Nobody *remembered* them anywhere else. And now you want to tell me remembering is dangerous?"

"I'm telling you everything is dangerous. That's not the same as saying throw it in the sea."

"But you'd want to. If you could." His eyes came up, gray and steady and older than fourteen. "You've wanted to since Cold Fork. Since you read your father's page. Don't think I didn't see it."

The fire popped. Somewhere above them the wind changed its mind and came around from the north, colder, carrying the smell of deeper winter.

"Yes," Odile said. "Part of me wanted to burn it. There. Now you know." She held his gaze because looking away would have been a lie of its own. "My father took consortium money to destroy an earlier copy of that book. You know what it cost him. Everything. And every mile of this range I've flown for twenty years, I was running from a page in a box. So yes — there's a part of me that thinks the world would be kinder if no one could write anyone else's sins down and carry them around like a weapon." She paused. "But it's a small part, and it's the coward's part, and I know that. Because the alternative to your ledger isn't a clean world. It's the consortium's world, where the only records kept are invoices."

Jude's shoulders came down half an inch. "Then what are you arguing?"

"I'm arguing that you think that book is a sword," Odile said, "and I'm telling you it's a fire. Same heat. Same light. Burns the hand that holds it wrong, and everyone believes they're holding it right." She poked the coals, sending up a scatter of orange. "Rell held it tonight and became a good man for ten minutes. Give him a week and consortium payroll, and he'll hold it again and become a monster who thinks he's avenging his grandmother. That's the fault line, boy. Right down the middle of the thing you've guarded your whole life. Everyone who touches that ledger wants to be its hero or its victim. Nobody wants to be its reader."

* * *

They argued until the tea was gone and the fire had burned down to a red eye in the dark.

It was not a shouting argument. Jude didn't shout; Odile had stopped shouting somewhere over the years, the way pilots stop flinching at thunder. It was worse than shouting — it was two people circling the same truth from opposite sides and neither willing to land.

Jude said the ledger was memory, and memory was the only court the poor had ever been granted, and she of all people should know what happened when the powerful got to decide what was remembered. Odile said fine — then who guards the guarders? Who copies the copyists? The keepers chose what to preserve and what to leave out; even the old keeper in the cave had been a man with preferences, grudges, blind spots. Jude said the entries were witnessed, countersigned, cross-checked against survey marks and wage books. Odile said so were church records, and churches had burned people. Jude said, quietly, that she sounded like someone who had been hurt by the truth and wanted the truth to apologize. Odile said nothing to that, because it was true, and they sat in the quiet after it like two people sharing a bench with a ghost.

Finally Jude said, "What would you do? If it were yours. If Sorel takes it and the hearings start and somebody puts it in your hands and says, you decide."

Odile was quiet a long time.

"I'd put it in the open," she said at last. "All of it. Every page, copied and scattered, so no one person owns it and no one person can be bought or burned out of it. Sunlight, not custody. Let it be a public record instead of a relic. Because a relic makes priests, and priests make politics, and politics make men like Rell." She looked at him across the coals. "Your keeper raised you to be a priest, Jude. I'm telling you the world needs librarians."

"That's not what the keepers were."

"No," she agreed. "They were braver than librarians. But brave isn't the same as right, and you can't build a country out of brave. Brave runs out. Systems don't."

Jude pulled his knees up and rested his chin on them, staring into the fire, and for a while he looked exactly his age — a boy too tired to fight, holding an inheritance too heavy for anyone. When he spoke again his voice had gone small.

"If everyone has it, then anyone can use it. That's what you're saying. The bad versions too."

"That's what I'm saying."

"And you'd take that risk."

"I already took it," Odile said. "Twenty years ago, in a way. I flew medicine to towns that used it to save people and towns that used it to survive things they shouldn't have survived. You don't get to hand out help and then audit what they do with it. You just hand it out. That's the whole terrible bargain of doing good." She fed the fire its last slivers of wood. "Your ledger is medicine, boy. Bitter medicine. And bitter medicine in one man's chest is poison waiting for a buyer."

Jude was silent for so long she thought he'd gone to sleep sitting up. Then:

"Sorel," he said. "When we get there. I was going to give it to her and walk away. That was the plan. Keeper delivers, keeper's done." He turned his head and looked at her, and in the firelight his face was stripped of everything feral and armored, and what was left was just a kid asking an adult a question he should have been able to ask years ago. "Is that enough? Just handing it over? Or do I have to stay and watch what they do with it?"

Odile felt something turn over in her chest — the same thing that had turned over at Cold Fork, at the burned homestead, at every mile of this godforsaken traverse where the boy kept giving her pieces of himself and she kept pretending she wasn't collecting them.

"That," she said, "is the first honest question anyone's asked me in twenty years. And I don't know the answer. But I know you don't answer it alone."

She banked the fire. Above the overhang the snow had thickened, falling soft and endless, sealing the high country behind them and the low country ahead, and somewhere out in that white dark, Voss's second team was moving without negotiating and without reading.

"We sleep in shifts," she said. "First light, we move. The col closes tomorrow whether we're through it or not."

Jude nodded and took first watch, the ledger chest drawn against his side, and Odile lay back on the cold rock and stared at the underside of the stone and understood, with the clarity that comes only at the edge of exhaustion, that whatever waited for them in the valley below — Sorel, the courthouse, the hearings, the whole machinery of justice they were hauling this fire toward — the hardest part of the journey hadn't been the mountains at all.

It was going to be deciding, together, what the fire was *for*.
