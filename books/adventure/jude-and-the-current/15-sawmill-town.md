## 15. Sawmill Town

The town announced itself before we saw it — a smell like wet iron and cut pine, then the sound, a sawblade's endless complaint rising out of the valley like something the mountain itself was grinding its teeth on. Odile stopped on the trail where the cedars broke open onto the ridge and looked down at Grayling Falls the way a pilot reads weather: not with hope, but with calculation.

"Company town," she said. "Sawmill, bunkhouses, company store, company church. One road in, one road out, and both owned by the same men we're running from."

"We could go around," I said.

"We could. It's two extra days over the divide, and your hands are still cracked from the col, and we're down to oats and the rabbit I shot yesterday." She shifted the pack on her shoulders, the one with the chest wrapped inside it in oilcloth and my spare shirt. "Besides, we need salt, bandages, and a map that isn't forty years old. Towns like this don't ask questions if you've got coin. They only ask questions if you've got none."

I had learned by then that when Odile made a list of reasons, the real reason was usually the one she left off. I didn't ask what it was. I was fourteen and had been alone in a cave for a season, and the truth was I wanted to see a town — wanted lights, wanted the noise of people, wanted bread that hadn't been cooked over a fire I'd built myself. I told myself the ledger needed salt too.

We went down.

* * *

Grayling Falls was built the way company towns are always built, which is to say like a sentence someone else wrote and you have to live inside. The mill sat on the river where the falls made power cheap, and the houses climbed the slope in rows graded by importance — the manager's house at the top with its glass windows and painted shutters, the foremen below him, and at the river's edge, in the mud and sawdust fog, the bunkhouses where the men who fed the blades lived four to a room.

The store stood in the middle of it all, two stories, with a false front painted to look like brick. Scribbles of the company's name ran across it in letters that were already peeling. Above the door, someone had hung a sign that read FAIR PRICES FOR FAIR MEN, and beneath that, in smaller letters, CREDIT EXTENDED TO EMPLOYEES ONLY.

I stood in the street and felt the ledger wake in my pack.

That sounds like a thing a keeper would say, and I was the keeper, so I'll say it plainly: I had carried the chest long enough that it had stopped being a thing and become a sense, the way you learn to feel weather in a healed bone. And what I felt standing in that street was the same thing the old keeper had made me feel in the pages — the particular weight of a town that was owed. You could read it in the men coming off shift: the way they walked past the store without looking at it, the way they didn't look at it too carefully, the way a man doesn't look at a dog that has bitten him before. You could read it in the women's coats, mended past mending. You could read it in the children, who were barefoot in a town that produced a thousand board-feet of lumber a day.

"They pay in scrip," Odile said quietly. She'd seen me looking. "Company money. Only spends at the company store, where the prices are set so the wage comes back to the company by Friday. It's the oldest theft there is. No one even calls it theft anymore. They call it the system."

"The magistrate would have called it theft," I said.

"The magistrate is dead, and his book is in oilcloth on my back, and we are here to buy salt." She touched my elbow, light, the way you steady something you're afraid of tipping. "Eyes down, Jude. We're a pilot and her nephew going through to the coast. That's all."

Inside the store it was warm and dim and smelled of kerosene and molasses. Odile did the talking, because Odile did all the talking that involved money, and she had a way of haggling that made it sound like friendship. I drifted along the aisles, and that was where I saw the board.

It was nailed up by the door, a plank painted black, and pinned to it were two papers. The first was a wage notice, dated the first of the month. The second was a notice of adjustment, dated the fifteenth, informing all hands that due to "market conditions and the costs of recent disruptions," wages would be paid at four-fifths the posted rate, the difference to be credited against outstanding store accounts.

I read it twice. I had been trained to read things twice. The old keeper used to rap my knuckles with his pipe stem if I read an entry once and thought I knew it. *Read it again, boy. The first reading tells you what they want you to know. The second tells you what they want you to miss.*

What I missed the first time was the word *disruptions*. The second time, I went and stood at the window, and I saw what the word meant.

There were men on the mill steps. Maybe thirty of them, sitting on their gear, not working, and the mill was running anyway, half its blades silent, a skeleton crew of men who wouldn't meet anyone's eyes. The sitting men weren't shouting. That was the thing that got into me. They weren't shouting at all. They were just sitting, quiet as a congregation, with a banner laid across their knees that read EIGHTY CENTS ON THE DOLLAR IS NOT A WAGE, and they were waiting the way people wait when they have already waited too long and know the waiting might be the only weapon they have.

And along the fence line, at intervals, stood the other men. Big coats. New boots. Rifles slung casual, muzzles down, the way you carry a gun when you want everyone to see it but want to pretend you don't. Consortium grey on the shoulder patches. I had seen that grey at a river crossing with a raft guide's betrayal in it, and in a whiteout on the col, and once through the sight-gap of a snowmobile's headlight. My body knew it before my mind did. My hands went cold around the salt tin I was holding.

"Jude." Odile's voice, low, right at my ear. "We're leaving. Now."

"One minute."

"No."

"Odile." I turned and kept my voice down even though everything in me wanted to lift it. "The notice. They cut the wages because of the strike, and the cut goes into the store accounts, which means the men are paying for their own strike out of their own stomachs. That's not market conditions. That's a trap with a ledger's face on it. The magistrate wrote down things exactly like this. Page after page. *Company of Grayling, deducts from wage what it fears from the strike.* It's the same theft with a new date on it."

"I know what it is."

"Do you?" I heard my own voice go sharp, and I didn't stop it. "Because I've read two hundred years of this, Odile. Two hundred years of men writing down what the law wouldn't touch, and every entry starts the same way — *they said it was nothing, they said it was the system, they said look away.* The whole reason the ledger exists is so that some boy like me, someday, stands in a street like this one and doesn't look away."

The store had gone quiet around us. Not all quiet — the kind of quiet where people are listening while pretending to weigh flour. Odile saw it too. She paid for the salt and the bandages and a battered survey map without another word, took my sleeve, and walked me out into the sawdust fog.

* * *

She didn't speak until we were two streets up, in the shadow of the company church, where a bell with no rope hung silent in its tower.

"You almost did it," she said. "In there. You almost stood on a flour barrel and started reading."

"I was going to show them the notice. Read it against the wage board. That's all. That's what the keeper trained me for — you read the entry aloud, in front of everyone, and the town knows its own grievance has a record. That's how it worked in the magistrate's time. Half the settlements on the range paid their debts because someone stood up and read."

"And what happened to the men who stood up?" Odile asked. She wasn't mocking me. That was the worst part. She was asking the way you ask a question you already know the answer to, because the other person has to say it.

I didn't want to say it. I said it anyway. "Some of them are in the ledger under a different heading."

"What heading?"

"*Deaths arising from public readings.*" The words tasted like the cave, like stone water. "Eleven of them. The keeper made me memorize the number before he let me memorize anything else. He said a keeper who forgets the cost isn't a keeper, he's a collector."

"Eleven," Odile said. "And that was with a magistrate who had the law's ear, in towns where the ledger had standing. What do you think happens to a fourteen-year-old boy with a box, in a town full of rifles, where the consortium has posted a reward for his face?" She held up her hand before I could answer. "And before you say it — yes. I know what happens to the men on those mill steps if nobody ever reads anything aloud to them. I grew up in towns like this one. I'm not blind, Jude. I'm counting."

"Counting what?"

"Costs. The thing you do before you spend something you can't get back." She looked down the street toward the mill, at the thin smoke rising from the half-dead stacks, at the sitting men who had become, in the last hour, less a crowd than a wound. "You want to read them their grievance. Fine. I even believe it would work — for an hour. For a day. They'd stand up, and it would feel like the magistrate's time, and then Rell's grey coats would come down that road, and there'd be twelve entries in your ledger instead of eleven, and the mill would run, and the store would keep its accounts, and nothing would be different except that I'd have gotten you killed to make myself feel like the story was moving fast enough."

"That's not what I'd be doing it for."

"I know." She said it gently, and the gentleness was worse. "You'd be doing it because it's right. And I'm telling you that justice delivered by a boy with a box isn't justice at all. It's a performance with a body count. The whole reason we're walking to Sorel — the whole reason we broke our hands on the col and crossed the river at night and buried the raft guide's betrayal behind us — is so that the reading happens in a courtroom, under a judge, where it can't be undone by whoever has the most rifles on Monday morning. You don't light a fire in a sawmill, Jude. You carry the match to where there's a hearth."

"And if the hearth is too far?" My voice cracked on it, and I hated that, fourteen years old and my voice cracking in a company town. "Those men are sitting on those steps *now*. The children are barefoot *now*. The ledger waited two hundred years in a cave — it can wait, I know it can wait, the keeper taught me that too. But they can't. And I'm standing right here with the only record in the world that says what's being done to them has a name."

For a long moment she didn't answer. The sawblade sound rose out of the valley and fell, rose and fell, like the town breathing in its sleep.

"Come with me," she said finally. "Not to the mill. To the church."

* * *

The church was unlocked, the way company churches always are — the company likes a place where men can be sad on Sundays. Inside it was cold and smelled of pine resin and floor polish, and the only decoration was a banner someone had hung behind the pulpit that read THE TRUTH SHALL MAKE YOU FREE, in the company's own paint, on the company's own cloth.

Odile sat down in the last pew and unwrapped the chest, and for the first time since the cave, she took the ledger out herself. She held it in both hands, this woman who had crossed a range with it strapped to her back, and she looked at it the way you look at a loaded thing.

"I'm not saying the book is wrong," she said. "I've read my father's page. I know what's in this book is truer than anything the consortium ever filed. What I'm saying is that a truth that only exists when one boy stands in one street and reads it aloud isn't a truth yet. It's a hostage. And everyone who wants it — Rell, the consortium, the men on the steps, you — everyone wants to be the one holding it. That's what power is, Jude. And I have watched power like this eat every person who touched it." She set the ledger on the pew between us, open to nothing, its pages breathing. "So here's what we do instead. Not nothing. Not reading on a flour barrel. We buy the salt, and we walk out the coast road tomorrow like a pilot and her nephew, and when we put this book in Amara Sorel's hands, we tell her about Grayling Falls. Names, dates, the wage notice, the grey coats. We make this town the first entry in the new record — the record that comes with a judge attached. That's the hearth. That's the only reading that can't be shot down on a Monday."

"And the men on the steps?"

"Will still be sitting there when Sorel's writ comes down the valley. Sitting is what they've got. It's more than the eleven had." She closed the ledger. "I'm sorry. I know what it costs you to walk past. I know because I spent twenty years walking past things, and I can tell you exactly what it costs, and I can also tell you it costs less than the other thing."

I sat in the company's cold church with the company's banner over my head, and I hated her, a little, and I knew she was right, and I knew that both of those things could live in me at once, the way the ledger held both the grievances and the deaths that came of speaking them. The old keeper had a saying for that. *A keeper holds the book so the book doesn't have to be opened in the street.* I had thought it meant cowardice, once. Sitting in that pew, I understood it meant the opposite. It meant that the reading only counts when the reader can't be silenced — and a boy can always be silenced. Eleven entries proved it.

"Tomorrow, then," I said. "The coast road."

"Tomorrow," Odile said. "And Jude — when Sorel asks us what we saw, you'll tell her everything. Every detail. That's your reading. That's the one you get to do."

I nodded. I picked up the chest. And that was when the church door opened.

A man came in fast and shut it behind him, and I knew his coat before I knew his face — grey, consortium grey, but with the patches torn off, and under the coat a mill worker's hands, scarred with pitch. He was one of the sitting men from the steps; I'd seen him lay the banner across his knees. He looked at the ledger on the pew between us, and his face did something complicated, and when he spoke his voice was low and shaking.

"You're them," he said. "The ones the reward's for. The boy with the book." He swallowed. "I'm not here to turn you in. I'm here because my brother's name is on a page in that thing — 1911, the Grayling entry, the man they drowned for asking about his wages. And because tomorrow morning at six, the company's bringing in strikebreakers on the morning train, and the foreman's got orders, and there are men on those steps who are going to die for eighty cents on the dollar." His eyes went from me to Odile and back. "So before you two walk out that coast road — somebody in this town is going to read something aloud tonight, at the bunkhouses, whether it's you or me. And I'd rather it was the boy who's done it before."

Odile and I looked at each other across the closed ledger, and outside, far down the valley, the night train whistled — early, six hours early, already coming.
