## 16. The Long Traverse

The first morning above the timberline, Odile learned that Jude checked her hands the way she checked her engine.

He did it without asking, the way he did everything — crouched beside her in the blue half-light before dawn, took her right hand in both of his, and pressed the pads of her fingers one at a time, watching her face for the flinch that meant numbness had gone past feeling into damage. His own hands were cracked and red at the knuckles, a boy's hands aged by weather and work, and they were warm as banked coals.

"Capillary refill," he said, pressing her thumbnail until it went white and counting under his breath. "Two seconds or less, you're fine. Longer than that, we stop and warm up."

"Where'd you learn that?"

"The keeper lost two fingertips the winter of '09. He said it was because he was proud, not cold. Pride makes you stupid at altitude." He released her hand and looked up, and for a moment his eyes were the old man's eyes, or what she imagined them to be — patient, appraising, faintly amused at the sight of her. "Your turn."

She checked his hands, his ears, the pale tip of his nose. He was fine. He was always fine. Fourteen years old and he moved through this country like water moving downhill, certain of every route, unbothered by any obstacle, while she — a woman who had landed planes on gravel bars, glacier shelves, frozen lakes no wider than a road — picked her steps like a tourist on wet stone.

They were three hours out of the col's shadow camp, climbing the long shoulder of granite the maps called the Widow's Back and Jude called nothing at all, because keepers didn't name things, they read them. The sky overhead was a hard, rinsed blue with a smear of cirrus in the west, thin as breath on glass.

Jude stopped, squinted at it, and frowned.

"What?" Odile said.

"Cloud's wrong."

"It's ten miles off and thinner than my patience."

He kept frowning. "It's got a hook in it. See? The tail curls down toward us." He traced the shape against the sky with one finger, the way another boy might have traced a picture in a book. "The keeper called those fishhooks. They come ahead of the real weather by a day, sometimes less. Up here, less."

Odile looked at the cloud again. She had flown beneath ten thousand of them, had read ceilings and winds aloft off instruments and instinct both, and she had never once thought of a cirrus veil as having a *hook*, but now that he'd said it she couldn't unsee it — the whole western sky curling down toward them like something reaching.

"All right," she said. "Fishhook. What do we do about it?"

"We make the saddle before dark. And tomorrow we don't sleep late." He shouldered his pack — the chest rode inside it now, wrapped in oilcloth, riding against his spine like a second heart — and started uphill without waiting for her, because that was also how he did everything.

* * *

The first day was the hardest and neither of them said so. The trail — if it could be called that — was a game track widened by old prospectors, switchbacking up scree that shifted and sang under their boots. By noon the wind had found them, a dry, sawing wind out of the northwest that made conversation impossible and thought nearly so. They walked in single file, Jude ahead, Odile counting her own footsteps to keep rhythm, watching the small of his back rise and fall beneath the pack straps.

At the saddle they dropped into the lee of a granite fin and made camp in a hollow the wind couldn't reach. Jude built the stove from parts he carried in a tin — a spirit burner, a windscreen of hammered aluminum — and melted snow in a pot older than either of them. While it heated he pulled off his boots and inspected his feet with clinical detachment, then hers, pressing each toe, counting capillary refill.

"You do this every night?" she asked.

"Every night and every morning. The keeper said frostbite is a thief. It doesn't break in. It waits until you're tired and then it takes a little, and you don't miss it until spring." He flexed her toes one at a time. "You've got good feet. Pilot's feet."

"There's no such thing as pilot's feet."

"There are mountain feet and valley feet. Yours are somewhere between. You walk like you're waiting for the ground to move."

"I fly," she said. "The ground is mostly a rumor."

Something happened to his mouth then — not quite a smile, but the place where a smile would go if he ever decided to build one there. She was learning the geography of his face the way she'd once learned the geography of the range: slowly, in bad light, by repeated passes.

They ate oatmeal with raisins and a crumbled strip of dried meat, and drank tea brewed from spruce tips Jude had stripped from a stunted tree at the treeline, hoarded in a waxed paper bag like treasure. The tea tasted like Christmas and turpentine. It was the best thing she'd had in weeks.

"Tell me a story," Jude said.

She looked at him over the steam. "What kind of story?"

"Any kind. The keeper told stories at night. He said a person who walks all day and doesn't talk at night starts to believe they're alone in the world, and that's how people die up here — not from cold, from believing that." He wrapped both hands around his tin cup. "I don't want to believe it."

So she told him about her first solo flight, seventeen years old, a rented Cub with a fuel gauge that lied, and how she'd navigated forty miles of fog by following the sound of a river she couldn't see, listening through the open window, correcting left when the water noise faded, right when it grew too loud, until the fog tore open over Kettle Landing and the runway was there beneath her like a promise kept.

Jude listened the way he did everything — completely, without interruption, his eyes fixed on her face as though the story were cargo he was taking personal custody of.

"The river talked you home," he said when she finished.

"I suppose it did."

"That's how the keeper read tides. Not with his eyes. He'd stand at the cave mouth at night with the lamp out and listen to the water change its mind. Said the sea tells you everything if you quit talking long enough to hear it." He turned his cup slowly. "Weather's the same. It talks. Most people just answer instead of listening."

* * *

The second day the country opened up and broke her heart.

They topped the saddle at first light and the range unfolded northward, ridge behind ridge behind ridge, blue and violet and bone-white, the snowfields glowing even in shade, the valleys between them holding rivers of fog like slow smoke. Somewhere out there was the provincial seat, two days' walk if the weather held. Somewhere behind them, Rell and whatever remained of his team were either dead, scattered, or regrouping — but the mountains had gone quiet, and quiet, Odile knew, could mean many things.

They walked. The air was so clear it felt engineered, every peak sharp enough to cut. Jude set a pace that was relentless without being fast, and taught as he went, pointing with his chin because his hands were full of trekking poles he'd cut from birch at the last trees.

"Lenticular," he said, nodding at a smooth white lens parked over the far summit like a hat. "That means wind up high. Don't care how pretty it is. Pretty clouds kill more people than ugly ones, because ugly ones make you turn around."

"Plume," he said an hour later, at a thread of snow blowing off a ridgeline. "Wind's strong enough to carry snow off the crest. That ridge—" he pointed at the one they'd have to cross tomorrow— "if it's pluming tomorrow, we go around, low. Under it. Even if it costs half a day."

"Why?"

"Because the wind loads the lee slope with snow that fell last week and last month and last year, all stacked up, waiting. You cross a loaded slope in daylight and you're fine. Cross it after a storm and the whole slope decides at once." He glanced back at her. "The keeper buried two surveyors in '11. Pulled one out alive. The other one he wrote into the ledger — death by avalanche, cause listed as impatience."

"He wrote everything in that book?"

"Everything the law wouldn't touch. Avalanches. Drowned fishermen. Wages stolen. A woman beaten in Sawmill Town while the constable watched from the porch." His voice didn't change when he said it; that was the frightening thing. It came out level, rehearsed, the way a priest recites. "People think the ledger is a list of crimes. It isn't. It's a list of *answers*. Every entry has what happened, who let it happen, and what was owed. The magistrate believed nothing was truly forgotten until it was paid or forgiven, and he wrote down which one it was."

"And your family? Are you in it?"

He walked a dozen steps before answering. "My mother's in it. Entry four hundred and six. She came to the cave pregnant and alone, and the keeper before my keeper gave her shelter, and the magistrate's record says she was owed protection and received it." A pause. "Then she died anyway, of fever, and the record says grief is not a debt, it's a weight, and weights get carried, not collected." He looked at Odile sideways. "The keeper raised me on that page. Read it to me every year on her day. So I'd know I wasn't an accident. I was an *entry*. Somebody decided I mattered and wrote it down where it couldn't be erased."

Odile said nothing for a long time. The wind filled the silence with its dry sawing. When she finally spoke, her voice came out rougher than she intended.

"My father's in that book too."

Jude stopped walking.

"I haven't read the page yet," she said quickly. "I know. I know what you're going to say — I could have read it any night by the stove. But I haven't." She looked north, at the plume rising off tomorrow's ridge, thin and white and constant. "There's a version of me that still gets to not know. Once I read it, that's gone forever. I've spent my whole life running from things before they could catch me. Let me have this one day where it hasn't caught me yet."

Jude considered this with the gravity he brought to everything, and then he nodded once, and started walking again, and said only: "Tonight, then. Or tomorrow. Whenever you're ready. The page keeps."

*The page keeps.* She turned the phrase over all afternoon as they crossed the high bench, as they forded a stream running shallow and glassy over stones the color of iron, as the light went long and gold and the shadows of the peaks stretched east across the snowfields like fingers reaching for the valleys. *The page keeps.* As if the ledger weren't a weapon or a treasure or a target, but simply a room where things waited, patiently, to be seen.

* * *

The second night they camped in a hollow below the loaded ridge, and the temperature fell so hard the fabric of the tent crackled when she brushed it. They cooked by headlamp, huddled close, their breath freezing on the nylon in a rime of tiny crystals that glittered when the beam swept past.

"Check," Jude said, and took her hands.

"Check," she said, and took his.

It had become liturgy already, two nights in — the word passed between them like a password, the examination performed with a seriousness that bordered on tenderness. Her fingers were fine. His ears were pink but warm. She pressed his thumbnail, counted. Two seconds. Fine.

"Your turn to tell a story," she said.

He was quiet for a while, melting snow for the morning's water, feeding chips of snow into the pot one at a time like offerings.

"The keeper used to tell one about the tide," he said finally. "How it goes out twice a day and everyone thinks it's leaving. But it isn't leaving. It's just gathering itself somewhere else. He said people are like that. They go away and everyone thinks they're gone, but they're only gathered somewhere you can't see, getting ready to come back stronger." He poured the hot water over the spruce tips. "I used to think he meant himself. Now I think he meant the ledger. Everyone thought it was gone. It was just waiting for the tide to bring someone to the cave."

"Is that what I am? The tide?"

"You're the storm that opened the door," he said, matter-of-fact, and handed her the tea, and Odile Winterbourne — who had not cried since she was nine years old and had sworn off it as a waste of water at altitude — had to look away at the tent wall for a moment and pretend to study the frost.

They slept close that night, closer than the first, backs together inside the double bag arrangement Jude insisted on, sharing heat the way mountaineers have always shared it, without embarrassment. She felt his breathing slow against her shoulders, felt the small weight of him settle, and understood with a clarity that frightened her more than any enforcer ever had: she would not give this boy up. Whatever waited at the courthouse, whatever the ledger cost, whatever page bore her father's name — she would see him through it. Not because of the bargain. Because of the sound of his breathing.

Somewhere in the dark she heard him murmur, half asleep, in the cadence of a lesson repeated ten thousand times: *"Two seconds or less. Fishhooks in the west. The page keeps."*

* * *

The third day dawned clear and cold and utterly still — the stillness itself a warning, though Jude read it as opportunity.

"No wind," he said, standing outside the tent in the gray light, his breath hanging around him like a scarf. "Ridge won't be pluming. We cross today, high route, save ourselves half a day. Storm's still a day out — maybe less. Fishhook's thicker." He pointed west, where yesterday's wisp had grown into a hooked arm dragging a horizon of gray behind it. "We move."

They climbed. The ridge was exactly as he'd promised — wind-scoured on the crest, the snow hard and squeaking underfoot, the views opening on both sides like a book held wide. From the top Odile could see the whole northern range, and beyond it, impossibly far, a smudge of woodsmoke haze that Jude said was the valley of the provincial seat. Two days' walk. One, if they pushed.

They stood a moment on the summit, side by side, saying nothing. The world below them was silent and enormous and indifferent, and Odile felt smaller and larger than she had ever felt, both at once.

"Keeper would've liked you," Jude said.

"He never met me."

"He met everyone eventually. That was his whole theory. The tide brings everyone to the cave sooner or later." He smiled — actually smiled, brief and crooked and startling, the first true one she'd seen — and turned to start down.

And that was when Odile, looking south along the back trail with a pilot's habit of checking six, saw the movement.

Far below, at the edge of the timberline they'd left behind that morning, at the very limit of resolution — figures. Small, dark, deliberate. Three of them. Maybe four. Moving up their back trail in a spaced line, the way men move who have done this before, who are not hurrying because they do not need to hurry, because they know exactly where the trail leads and exactly who is on it.

Not Rell. Rell had let them go. This was the other kind — the kind the hermit had warned about, the kind with no interest in deals.

Jude followed her gaze. The smile died on his face by degrees, like a lamp being turned down.

"How long?" he asked quietly.

Odile measured the distance, the grade, the spacing of the figures, the angle of the sun. She had spent her life computing such things without knowing she was doing it.

"They'll make the summit by dark," she said. "If they push."
