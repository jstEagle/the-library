## 17. Ambush at the Timberline

The weather turned kind on the third morning above the river, and that was the first thing that should have warned them.

Jude had been taught to read the sky the way other children learned to read their mothers' faces. The old keeper used to say a mountain never gives you anything for free — not light, not warmth, not a single easy mile — so when the range handed them three days of blue and stillness after the whiteout at the col, Jude kept waiting for the bill to come due. Odile didn't wait. She moved like a woman who'd made peace with debt.

"Timberline by noon," she said, crouching over the map with her mittened hand flat against the contour lines. "There's an old fire road down the east shoulder. If we make it before dark, we're below the snow line, below their machines, and every step after that is just walking."

"And if they're waiting at the timberline?"

She looked up at him. Windburn had cracked her lips and she hadn't complained once about it, which told him more about her than any of the stories she'd let slip around the cook fire. "Then we find out how good you are at running downhill."

He was good at running downhill. He'd been good at running in every direction since the day he'd buried the old keeper under the cairn of grey stones and understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water, that there was no one left between him and the world except himself. But running was one thing. What he felt now, watching Odile fold the map with her frost-stiffened fingers, was something heavier. Somewhere along the traverse — somewhere between the col and the frozen waterfall they'd climbed in the dark — the ledger had stopped being his alone to carry. It had become theirs. And theirs could be lost.

They broke camp fast. No smoke, no noise, the tent shaken out and rolled before the sun cleared the eastern ridge. Jude took point because his eyes were younger and because the keeper had trained him to move through high country the way water moves through rock: patiently, always finding the line of least resistance, never fighting the mountain when the mountain would win.

The trees came up to meet them through the morning — first stunted pines bent like old men into the wind, then proper spruce, dark and dense, and the smell of them hit Jude like a memory he hadn't known he was carrying. Green. Living things. For six days the world had been white and grey and the color of his own breath, and now the forest rose up around them and he wanted, absurdly, to put his hand against the nearest trunk and thank it.

Odile stopped so suddenly he walked into her.

"What," he started.

Her hand came up. Below them, maybe four hundred meters down the slope, the fire road cut across the mountainside — a pale scar of packed snow and gravel, exactly where the map promised. And parked on it, casual as a wagon outside a church, sat two snowmobiles and a sled.

Jude went flat behind a fallen spruce without being told. Odile dropped beside him, and for a long moment neither of them breathed hard enough to fog the air.

"Count them," she whispered.

"Two machines. One sled." He squinted through the branches. Figures moved near the treeline — three, no, four men, spread out in a loose arc, facing upslope. Toward them. "They're not traveling. They're set."

"Rell's team?"

"Rell deals." Jude's mouth had gone dry. He thought of the man's face in the whiteout, the strange arithmetic of a killer who'd read his own family's grievances aloud from a stranger's box and then simply walked away. "This isn't Rell."

"How do you know?"

"Because Rell counts." He watched the figures below again. One of them stood apart from the others, hands loose at his sides, head bare despite the cold — a big man built like something quarried rather than born, standing with the patience of a person who has never once needed to hurry. "That one doesn't count. That one waits."

Odile exhaled slowly through her teeth. "Voss," she said. "Consortium security chief. I've seen him at the Kettle Landing freight office twice a year, collecting manifests nobody's allowed to read. They say he once walked a strike organizer forty miles into the bush in winter and came back alone."

"Then we go around."

"There's no around. Look at the slope." She was right, and Jude hated that she was right. To the west the mountainside steepened into a wall of ice-glazed rock no sane person would descend loaded; to the east the ridge fell away into avalanche country, the snow sitting fat and unstable above a convex roll of terrain. The fire road was the only door out of the high country, and Voss was standing in it.

Below them, the bare-headed man did something small and terrible: he checked his watch. Then he looked up the slope — not searching, just confirming — and said something to his men that made them fan wider.

"He knows our line," Jude breathed. "He's not guessing. Someone gave him our route."

"The raft guide." Odile's voice had gone flat and cold, the way it went when she talked about the betrayal on the river. "Or the hermit. Or—" She stopped herself. It didn't matter anymore. Names were a luxury for people with time.

"We split," Jude said. "I take the ledger east, through the slide country, draw them off. You cut down to the road and take a machine—"

"No."

"You don't even know what I was going to say."

"I know exactly what you were going to say, because it's what I would say, and I'm telling you no." She grabbed his sleeve, and her grip was stronger than her size suggested, strong enough that he felt it through the parka. "Listen to me. You are fourteen years old and carrying a box that half the province wants burned and the other half wants owned. If they catch you alone, you die in a ditch and the ledger dies with you. If they catch us together, we die in the same ditch. Neither of those is acceptable. So we do the thing they're not expecting."

"Which is?"

Odile looked down the slope at the sled — the big cargo toboggan hitched behind one of the snowmobiles, riding empty on its runners — and Jude watched an idea arrive in her face the way weather arrives over a ridge, all at once and from nowhere good.

* * *

The plan, such as it was, took four minutes to build and had perhaps a one-in-five chance of working, which Odile pronounced "better odds than most of my landings."

The slope above the fire road was open timber for its last hundred meters — spaced trunks, thin snow cover over scree, a grade steep enough to make a body want to sit down and let gravity have its opinion. Odile would come down that slope loud and visible, straight toward the gap between two of Voss's men, and every eye on that road would track her, because a lone woman stumbling out of the high country was exactly what a waiting ambush expected to see eventually.

And while they tracked her, Jude would come down the western edge, through the thick timber, quiet as the keeper had taught him to be, and reach the sled.

"Not to steal it," she'd said. "To kill it. There'll be a tow hitch, a pin or a clevis connecting the sled to the machine. Pull it and the sled runs free. Then get behind the second machine and do the same to whatever keeps its track tensioned, or smash the belt, or pour snow in the intake — improvise. Two dead machines means they walk back to wherever they came from, and men on foot don't catch anybody."

"And you?"

"I'm the loudest decoy in the interior." Something flickered across her face that might have been a grin. "Story of my life."

It went wrong in the first ten seconds, and it went wrong because Voss did not count, did not guess, and did not watch the thing he was supposed to watch.

Odile went down the open slope on a slant, arms windmilling, kicking up a glorious banner of powder, shouting wordless alarm like a woman fleeing a bear. It was, Jude would think later, a genuinely magnificent performance. Four heads turned. Four rifles swung. And Voss — Voss didn't turn at all. He looked at the place where the timber thickened on the west edge, the place Jude was already moving through, and raised one hand, and two of his men peeled off toward Jude's line as if they'd rehearsed it.

Because they had rehearsed it. Because Voss had stood at the bottom of that slope doing arithmetic of his own: *one woman, one boy, one box, and only one of them worth the bullets.*

Jude heard them coming — boots punching crust, the wet rasp of breath — and did the only thing the mountain offered him. He went downhill faster than they could safely follow, letting the slope carry him, using the trunks as gates, and burst out of the treeline onto the fire road twenty meters short of the sled with two enforcers crashing through the brush behind him and Voss turning, unhurried, rifle rising.

The sled sat between them. Between Jude and everything.

He reached it. His hands found the tow hitch — a steel clevis pin through a forged ring, crusted with ice — and he wrenched it, and it held, and behind him Voss fired and the bullet took a chunk out of the sled's gunwale close enough to sting his cheek with splinters.

"Boy." Voss's voice carried up the road without effort, deep and level, a voice built for giving orders in bad weather. "You can pull that pin, and my men will shoot you before the sled stops rolling. Or you can step away from it, and we can talk about the box. I'm told I'm more reasonable than my colleagues."

Jude pulled the pin anyway. The clevis dropped, the tow rope slithered free, and the sled — heavy, runner-shod, sitting on a grade — began to move, slow and majestic, gathering speed down the fire road with nothing attached to it at all.

What happened next happened in pieces, and for years afterward Jude would keep reassembling the pieces and finding new ones.

Voss swung his rifle toward Odile — still descending the open slope, still playing her part, now exposed and alone and suddenly the only target left. Jude saw the muzzle track her. He saw the man's finger settle. And he understood, with the same cold-water clarity as the day of the burial, that Voss had never intended to take prisoners, that *reasonable* was a word men like this used the way fishermen use bait, and that in approximately one second Odile Winterbourne was going to stop existing.

He shouted her name. It wasn't enough. Nothing he could do was going to be enough — he was too far, too slow, too young, too unarmed —

Except she'd already seen it. He knew that later, from the angle of her body: she'd seen the muzzle swing, and she'd done the arithmetic faster than any of them, faster than Voss, faster than the mountain itself.

Odile threw herself sideways off the open slope and onto the fire road, directly into the path of the runaway sled.

The impact made a sound Jude felt in his teeth. The sled caught her at the hip and shoulder and spun her — he saw her grab the gunwale, deliberately, *deliberately*, hauling herself onto the moving mass instead of under it — and then man and machine and woman went down the road together in a storm of thrown snow, and Voss's shot went high and wide and struck a spruce cone off a branch thirty feet above her head.

The sled ran fifty meters, sixty, shedding speed on the flat, and Odile rolled off it into the ditch and did not get up.

Everything after that, Jude did without deciding to do it. He was simply moving, down the road, past Voss — who did not fire again, who stood watching with his head tilted like a man studying an unexpected result — past the two enforcers floundering in the treeline, down to the ditch where Odile lay on her back in the churned snow with her eyes open and her right arm held against her chest at an angle arms don't naturally hold.

"Hey," she said, before he even knelt. Her voice was steady. Her face was the color of the snow. "Sled's disabled. You're welcome."

"Your arm—"

"Is broken, yeah. Wrist, maybe both bones. I heard it, which was honestly the worst part." She tried to flex the fingers and the sound she made was small and furious and she bit it off immediately. "Listen. Listen to me. Machines?"

Jude looked up the road. In the chaos, in the seconds he'd spent running, someone — he genuinely could not remember doing it — had smashed the drive belt on the nearer snowmobile; its housing hung open, rubber shreds scattered like entrails. The second machine sat untouched, but Voss's men were regrouping around it, and Voss was walking toward them down the road at his unhurried quarry-man's pace, rifle slung now, which was somehow worse than the opposite.

"They're mostly dead," Jude said. "One of them. The other one's theirs."

"Then here's the last part of the plan I didn't tell you, because you'd have argued." She rolled her eyes toward the slope below the road, where the forest plunged away toward the valley — steep, dense, brutal, and utterly untrackable by anything on wheels. "Down. Both of us. Now. Before he decides to stop being reasonable."

"He won't follow into that."

"He doesn't need to follow. He needs to wait at the bottom, and he knows exactly where the bottom is, because there's only one valley and only one town in it." Odile was already trying to stand, using her left arm, and failing, and hating it. "But he can't wait forever either. Not with one machine and a report to write about how a girl and a boy wrecked his whole operation. Go. Jude. *Go.*"

Jude looked at her — at this woman who had dragged him out of a cave, argued with him across a province, taught him that stopping costs more than moving — lying broken in a ditch because she had chosen, in half a second, to put her body between him and a bullet. The keeper had died quietly, in a bed, of age and weather. This was different. This was someone spending themselves on purpose, for him, and he found he had no training for it at all.

So he did the only thing he could think of that matched it.

He crouched, got his good shoulder under her left arm, and lifted. She was lighter than the chest, barely. He'd carried the chest over a mountain; he could carry her down one.

"This is humiliating," Odile said.

"Save your breath."

"For arguing? Never." But she settled against him, and her weight became real, and Jude turned his back on Voss and the road and the ruined machines and started down.

The forest took them the way the sea takes a swimmer — completely. Snow to the knee, then the thigh; deadfall to climb over and hollows to fall into; spruce branches raking his hood like impatient hands. Twice Odile blacked out against his neck, brief and shallow, and twice the pain brought her back with a curse. He navigated by the keeper's rules — fall line, spacing, rest before you need it — and by one rule that was entirely his own invention: *she carried me out of the cave, and I will not be the reason this ends in a ditch.*

Behind and above them, faint through the trees, he heard the surviving snowmobile start, run thirty seconds, and die. Heard Voss's voice, once, giving some order he couldn't parse. Heard nothing else. The silence was worse than pursuit. A patient man at the bottom of a valley was a trap with a very long spring.

Near dusk they broke out of the steep stuff onto gentler ground, and Jude smelled woodsmoke — real woodsmoke, domestic, someone's stove — and saw, far down the darkening valley, a scatter of lights. A mill town. Company housing in neat rows. The consortium's own front porch.

Odile stirred against his shoulder and followed his gaze, and laughed — a raw, cracked sound with no humor in it anywhere.

"Well," she said. "Of course. Of course that's where we'd end up. Straight into the wolf's parlor." Her broken hand twitched against his collarbone. "Get me to those lights, kid. And then find me a doctor who doesn't ask names — because tomorrow we're walking into that town, and I need to know which of us they're hunting harder."

Jude adjusted her weight and started down toward the lights, counting the hours until dawn, knowing Voss was counting them too.
