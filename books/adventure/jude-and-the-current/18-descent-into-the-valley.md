## 18. Descent into the Valley

The snow came down like a verdict.

Odile felt it before she saw it — a change in the air's weight, the way the wind quit moaning and went flat and silent, the way sound stopped traveling. Above them, at the col where they'd spent the worst night of her life pressed together in a snow cave with Jude's boot against her broken wrist to keep her from shivering apart, the storm had sealed the pass behind them. She'd watched it happen at first light: the notch between the peaks filling, white on white, until there was no pass anymore. Just a wall.

"There it is," Jude said beside her. He didn't say *there goes our way back*. He was fourteen, not stupid.

She flexed the fingers of her splinted hand. The wrist had swollen overnight despite the willow bark and the wrap of torn shirt, and the pain had settled into something constant and conversational, like a companion who wouldn't stop talking. Her right hand. Her flying hand. The thought of it made her want to laugh — except that laughing would cost breath, and breath was fuel now, and they were running on almost nothing.

"Food check," she said.

Jude opened his pack without ceremony. He'd learned her habits by now; he did the inventory aloud because she'd told him once that saying numbers out loud kept them honest. "Half a strip of dried fish. Six crackers. The last of the pemmican." A pause. "That's it."

"That's it," she agreed.

Below them the valley fell away in long white shelves, timber giving way to scrub, scrub to the grey-brown smear of the lower country where the provincial seat sat like a stone in a riverbed. Greyrock, they called it. Odile had flown over it a hundred times without landing — a courthouse, a rail spur, a town that existed because the government needed somewhere to put its stamp. She had never once wanted to go there. Now it was the only direction left in the world.

"How far?" Jude asked.

"Two days if we're careful. Three if we're smart." She looked at the slope, reading it the way he'd taught her to read weather — not for what it was but for what it intended. "One day if we're desperate."

"Which are we?"

Odile considered the question honestly, because he deserved honesty and because lying to him had never once worked. "We're out of food, my wrist is broke, the passes are shut, and there are men behind us who want what you're carrying." She shouldered her pack with her good arm, teeth gritted. "I'd say we're desperate with a day's margin."

Jude slung the chest strap across his chest — the ledger rode against his back now, wrapped in oilcloth, tied down like cargo. He'd refused to let her carry it even after her wrist went, and she hadn't argued. Some weights belonged where they belonged.

"Then we're careful and smart," he said, "and fast anyway."

They started down.

* * *

The mountain did not make it easy.

By midmorning they were moving through terrain that punished every mistake — wind-scoured ice under the powder, hidden rock, slopes that groaned faintly when the wind gusted, as if the whole face were considering letting go. Odile led where she could read the snow and followed where Jude could. His mountain craft still astonished her. He moved the way water moved, finding the line of least resistance without seeming to look for it, probing cornices with a ski pole cut from birch, testing each step before committing his weight. Twice he stopped her with a raised hand and made her wait while he circled something she couldn't see — a hollow, a crevasse bridge, a place where the snow held its breath.

The second time, she asked him how he knew.

"The keeper taught me," he said. "He said the mountain talks all the time. Most people just don't listen till it's shouting." He kicked a test hole, studied the layers, moved on. "He said tides and avalanches are the same thing. They don't hate you. They just don't know you're there."

"He sounds like he'd have liked flying."

"He said pilots were people who listened to weather from the wrong side." Almost a smile. Then it faded, and she saw him glance back up the slope, toward the col, toward whatever might be climbing down after them.

Nothing was. Not yet. Rell's silence had bought them two days, and the second team — the one with no interest in deals, the one whose leader Odile had crashed a sled to stop — was scattered or dead or digging themselves out of their own bad morning. But *not yet* was not *never*, and both of them knew it. The consortium's money didn't sleep. It hired new hands.

At noon they crossed a frozen waterfall, moving one at a time along the rock shelf while the ice boomed softly somewhere inside itself, like a heart in a chest too big. Odile's wrist screamed on every reach, and she stopped pretending otherwise. By the time they reached the treeline she was sweating and grey-faced, and Jude made her sit against a spruce and eat half a cracker soaked in snowmelt while he rewrapped the splint with hands far steadier than any fourteen-year-old's had a right to be.

"You're favoring it," he said.

"I'm aware of it."

"Favoring it makes the other shoulder do the work. Other shoulder gets tired, you fall. You fall up here, that's the end of the arithmetic." He tied off the wrap. "Keeper used to say an injury doesn't kill you. The lie you tell about it does."

"Your keeper," Odile said, "said a lot of things."

"Yeah." Jude looked down at his hands. "Wish I'd written more of them down."

They sat with that a moment. Below them, through the thinning trees, the valley floor showed itself in patches — a river running dark between white banks, a thread of smoke rising far off where some homestead or hunting camp burned its stove against the cold. Smoke meant people. People meant risk. People also meant, possibly, food.

"We could ask at the camp," Jude said, following her gaze.

"We could get reported at the camp. Reward's up to four hundred now. That's a year's wages to a trapper."

"That's a lot of fish."

"It's a lot of fish," Odile agreed. She got up, using the spruce trunk, refusing the hand he offered mostly out of pride. "But we've still got the fish we have, and Greyrock's got a hotel, and Judge Sorel's got a kitchen, if judges have kitchens. One more day of hunger is cheaper than one wrong name spoken around a stove."

Jude nodded slowly. "You think like a keeper."

"I think like somebody who's been sold out twice this month." She smiled so it wouldn't land as bitter. It landed anyway. "Come on. Daylight's spending faster than we are."

* * *

They walked until the light went blue and then they walked past that, because stopping meant a fire and a fire meant smoke and smoke meant being found, and because every hour of descent was an hour the storm worked in their favor, burying their backtrail under fresh white. Odile counted her steps when the pain got loud. She thought about her father, which she had been doing more lately — not the shame of it, exactly, but the shape of it, the way a man could take money and burn paper and then find that the burning didn't burn out of him. She had run the range for fifteen years telling herself motion was freedom. Motion was just motion. It went nowhere unless you aimed it.

She aimed it downhill.

Full dark caught them in a stand of old growth where the canopy held off the snowfall and the going turned easier, softer. They made camp without fire — spruce boughs laid thick, backs to a fallen log, packs between them, sharing body heat the way they'd learned to at the col. Odile split the last of the pemmican unevenly, giving Jude the bigger piece, and he noticed, and pushed half of it back, and she let him win because arguing cost more than calories.

"Tell me about her," Jude said, after a while. "Sorel."

Odile chewed slowly. "I met her once, years ago. Flew in a witness for a mining claim hearing — a widow whose husband died in a shaft collapse the company called an act of God." She remembered the woman's hands, cracked and quiet in her lap. "Sorel took testimony for six hours. Asked questions nobody else would ask. Ruled for the widow and cited precedent from a case file nobody could produce afterward." Odile had wondered about that missing file for years. Now she knew where it had gone — or at least where its shadow lived. "The consortium's been after her seat ever since. Recusal petitions, ethics complaints, funding her opponent. She's still sitting. Stubbornest woman in the province."

"And you think she'll take the ledger."

"I think she'll have no choice," Odile said. "People like Sorel always have no choice. That's what makes them what they are." She looked at him in the dark, at the outline of the boy and the box that had been his whole inheritance. "You understand what happens tomorrow? Once it's in her hands, it stops being yours. Stops being your keeper's. It becomes evidence. Evidence belongs to everybody and nobody. There'll be hearings. Men named in it will deny it, and some of them will be believed. Your magistrate's handwriting will be argued over by lawyers who bill by the hour."

Jude was quiet a long time. Snow ticked down through the branches above them, soft as ash.

"The keeper told me about that too," he said finally. "Near the end. He said a ledger in a cave protects nothing. He said memory only works in daylight, among people." His voice caught slightly and steadied. "He said keeping it was just carrying it toward the day someone else could carry it better."

"Tomorrow's that day?"

"Tomorrow's the closest we can walk to it." He lay back against the log. "Get some sleep, Odile. You listen to weather from the wrong side. I'll listen tonight."

She slept, and dreamed of tide lines, and woke once to find him awake, watching the dark, the chest strapped to him even in sleep.

* * *

The last day came down out of the mountains with them.

By dawn they were below the worst of it, following the river's frozen margin through country that grew gradually domestic — a fence post, a rusted trap, a blazed trail. Odile's world narrowed to rhythm: step, breathe, step, breathe, wrist throbbing in time. Jude set the pace and she hated him gently for how good it was, and loved him plainly for how he slowed whenever her breathing changed, without ever making it a thing, without ever once suggesting she couldn't make it.

They ate the last of the fish at noon. They ate the last crackers at three. Hunger stopped being an event and became a landscape they traveled through.

And then, late in the afternoon, the trees opened, and there it was.

Greyrock sat in the valley mouth where the river bent, smoke rising from a hundred chimneys, the rail spur a black scar across the white, and above it all — visible from miles, built to be visible — the courthouse. Stone, grey as its namesake, with a copper dome gone green, and lights already coming on in its tall windows against the early dark. Odile had flown over it a hundred times. From the ground, from the bottom of everything, exhausted and starving and one-handed, it looked different than it ever had from the air. It looked like the far shore.

Behind them, far up the valley, the storm still stood over the passes — a white column reaching into the clouds, sealing the high country until spring. No way back. No way around. The mountains had closed the book behind them, and the only page left was forward.

Jude stood beside her, breathing hard, looking at the courthouse lights the way a man looks at water in a desert.

"They'll know we're coming," he said. "Word travels faster than feet. The consortium will have people in that town."

"They will," Odile agreed. "And the judge has bailiffs, and the town has a newspaper, and daylight is coming whether they like it or not." She shifted the pack on her good shoulder and started walking. "Besides. We didn't come all this way to knock politely."

They came down the river road as full dark settled, two thin figures against the snow, and passed the first lit window of the outskirts just as a bell somewhere in the town began to ring the hour — six slow strokes rolling across the frozen valley, counting them in.

In the courthouse's tallest window, a light moved. Someone was working late.

Odile picked up the pace.
