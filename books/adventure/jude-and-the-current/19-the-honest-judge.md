## 19. The Honest Judge

The valley opened below them like a held breath.

Jude had never seen a town built by law before. Kettle Landing was built by tide and timber, Cold Fork by stubbornness, the sawmill towns by greed — but Meridian Falls had been laid out on paper first, streets ruled straight as ledger lines, and at the head of them, on a granite rise above the river, stood the courthouse with its clock tower and its copper roof gone green with weather. It looked, from the ridge where they stopped to rest, like something that had been placed there on purpose. Something meant to last.

Odile sat down hard on a boulder and cradled her splinted wrist against her chest. Six days since the timberline. The break had set crooked — he'd done his best with two spruce splints and a strip of her own shirt, and she'd bitten down on a leather strap and not made a sound, which was the bravest thing he'd ever watched a person do — but the hand beyond the splint was swollen dark, and her lips had a gray tinge he didn't like.

"How far?" she asked.

"Half a day. Maybe less, if we follow the river road."

"River road means people."

"People is the point." He crouched in front of her, opened the pack, and checked the chest one more time — the oilcloth, the wax seals, the dry interior. Habit now, older than he was. "We can't come this far and leave it in the trees."

She looked at him for a long moment. Snowmelt dripped off the rocks around them. Somewhere below, faint on the wind, a church bell rang the hour, and the sound of it — ordinary, civic, sane — seemed to move through her like warmth through frozen hands.

"All right," she said. "But we walk in like nobody special. We're cousins. My name's Marta. Yours is—"

"Jude."

"You can't be Jude."

"Why not? It's my name."

"Because it's been on a reward poster from here to the coast for three weeks, boy. *Fifteen hundred dollars.* I could buy a new plane."

He almost smiled. Almost. "Then I'm Eli," he said, after the keeper's father, whose name was carved into the third shelf of the cave and nowhere else. "Eli Winterbourne. If I'm going to lie, I'll at least keep it in the family."

Something crossed Odile's face when he said *family* — quick, like a fish under ice — and then she shouldered her end of the pack and got up without help, because that was who she was.

* * *

They came down the river road in the long gold light of late afternoon, and Meridian Falls received them the way towns do: without noticing. A dray horse clopped past hauling beer kegs. Two boys fished off a footbridge. A woman beat a rug over a railing and gave them the flat once-over of someone who has seen every kind of traveler and trusts none of them. Jude kept his hood up and his eyes forward and felt the chest's weight against his spine like a second heartbeat.

But the town was wrong in a way he couldn't name until they reached the square below the courthouse steps, and then he could name it all at once.

There were men on the corners. Not constables — constables wore badges and slouched. These men stood straight, wore good coats, and did not talk to each other. One leaned against the pump. One pretended to read a notice board. One simply stood at the base of the courthouse steps with his hands folded, facing the doors, the way a man stands when standing *is* the message.

"Consortium?" Odile murmured, not turning her head.

"Some of them. Look at their boots." Jude had learned boots during three weeks of being hunted; Rell's men wore government-issue laced boots no matter what coat covered them. "Those two are company. That one—" he tipped his chin at the man by the notice board, "—is paid by somebody else. Lawyers, maybe. Or Sorel's own opposition."

"They're not here for us."

"No." His mouth went dry anyway. "They're here for her."

The notice board told the rest. Pinned dead center, under a proclamation about spring road repairs, was a printed petition — dozens of signatures — demanding the recusal of *the Honorable Amara Sorel* from the matter of *Graywater Range Consolidated Mining et al. v. The Settlers of the Upper Valley*, citing conflict of interest, citing partiality, citing, in language so polished it had clearly been bought by the word, "a documented pattern of prejudice adverse to the interests of lawful enterprise."

Documented pattern. Jude read the phrase twice and felt something cold settle behind his ribs. They weren't just pressuring her. They were building a *record*. Paper against paper. If you couldn't burn a thing, you buried it in filings.

"She's still sitting, though," Odile said quietly. "Court's still in session. See the docket nailed to the door?"

He saw. Tomorrow morning, nine o'clock: *In re petitions for recusal*, followed by the land case itself. She hadn't stepped aside. Whatever they'd brought against her, she'd answered by putting it on the public docket where everyone could watch her refuse.

"Come on," Jude said. "Before I lose my nerve."

"You don't have nerve to lose. You have a chest full of other people's courage. It weighs more." She said it lightly, but her good hand found his shoulder for half a step, one squeeze, and let go.

* * *

The clerk's office smelled of ink and coal smoke and old varnish. Behind the counter sat a small round woman with spectacles on a chain and the unhurried air of someone who had outlasted four judges and expected to outlast a fifth.

"We need to see Judge Sorel," Odile said. "Tonight. It concerns the Graywater case."

"The judge sees petitioners during posted hours—"

"It concerns the ledger of Magistrate Corvane."

Jude had wondered how they'd say it — whether there was some password, some ritual. There wasn't. There was only the name, spoken plainly in a quiet room, and the effect it had: the clerk went absolutely still, spectacles halfway to her nose, and for three full seconds the only sound in the building was the clock in the hall outside, ticking like something counting down.

Then she set the spectacles down, rose, and said, "Wait here. Touch nothing," and disappeared through the inner door at a pace considerably faster than posted hours.

Odile exhaled. "Well," she said. "That's either very good or very bad."

"Keeper always said the name would open one of two doors."

"Which other one?"

"The one with men behind it."

She snorted despite herself, then winced, pressing her wrist. Jude looked around the office while they waited — the shelves of bound volumes, the map of the range on the wall with its survey lines drawn in red, creeping up every river valley like veins of something diseased. On the corner of the clerk's desk sat a framed photograph: a younger woman in judicial robes standing on these same courthouse steps beside an old man with a white beard and a walking stick, both of them squinting into sun. Jude leaned closer. He knew the face from the frontispiece of the oldest volume in the chest. He'd copied those features a hundred times in practice, learning letters by tracing the magistrate's engraved stare.

"That's him," he breathed. "That's Corvane. With her."

The inner door opened before Odile could answer.

Judge Amara Sorel was not what Jude had expected, though he couldn't have said what he had expected — robes, maybe, or thunder. What stood in the doorway was a tall woman in her sixties with iron-gray hair pinned back carelessly, wearing a cardigan with a hole at one elbow and holding a cup of tea she'd evidently abandoned mid-pour. Her eyes went past Odile entirely and fixed on him, on the pack, on the shape of it, and Jude watched sixty years of discipline fight something rawer and lose by a fraction.

"Close the door," she said to the clerk. "Lock it. Then go home by the back stairs and don't come back tonight."

"Your Honor—"

"*Don't come back tonight,* Miriam."

The lock clicked. The outer door thumped shut. And then Amara Sorel crossed her own office, took Jude gently by the shoulders, turned him around, and lifted the chest's lid herself with hands that trembled just slightly, the way his had trembled the first time he'd opened it alone.

For a long time she said nothing. The lamplight moved across the oilcloth, the wax seals, the spines of volumes written in five different hands across eighty years. When she finally spoke, her voice was low and roughened.

"I was nine years old," she said. "He carried me up the courthouse steps himself when my mother died, because there was no one else and he didn't believe in leaving a child to the wind. Everyone hated him for what he wrote down. Half the range wanted him burned as a tyrant and the other half wanted him crowned as a king, and he refused both, and when he died they came for the books, and my father hid one page under our floorboards for thirty years." She touched the topmost spine with one finger, barely, the way you touch a sleeping animal. "I cited that page in my first ruling. A water rights dispute. Small matter. I thought I'd buried it safely in precedent." Her jaw tightened. "It's why they want me recused. They've known for years what I read as a girl. They've just never had cause to use it until now."

"Then don't recuse," Jude said.

She looked at him then — really looked, taking in the hollow cheeks, the frost-split lips, the clothes that had been mended too many times, the fourteen-year-old architecture of him holding up under everything — and whatever she saw made her close the lid softly and turn to Odile.

"Who is this child?"

"His name's Jude. He's the last keeper. And he walked that ledger over the range through the first snows of winter to bring it to you, Your Honor, so I'd mind how you talk about him." Odile's voice was steady but Jude heard the weariness underneath it, the six days and the broken wrist and the burned homestead all packed flat beneath the words. "Also his name is Eli right now, if anyone asks."

Sorel almost smiled. It flickered and died. "Sit down, both of you. There's tea, and bread if Miriam left any, and you're going to eat before we discuss anything, because you look like the famine illustrations in my grandfather's Bible." She was already moving, already pouring, and Jude understood suddenly that this was how she fought — by making order, by feeding people, by refusing to be hurried even with wolves at the door. The keeper had been the same way. Grief caught him sideways, unexpectedly, and he had to look hard at the floor until it passed.

When they'd eaten — thick bread, salt butter, tea sweet enough to make his teeth ache — Sorel sat across from them, laced her fingers, and became a different person. The softness banked itself. What remained was precise.

"Now. Tell me exactly what's in the chest, in what condition, and who besides you knows you have it."

Jude told her. All of it — the cave, the keeper's death, the enforcers, Rell, the burned cabin, the raft guide, the ambush at the timberline, Odile's wrist, the pass closing behind them. He told it in the order things happened because that was how the keeper had taught him to record: facts in sequence, no decoration, no complaint. Sorel listened without interrupting once, which was its own kind of skill, and when he finished she was silent for a moment, looking at the window, where the last light was failing over the square and the man at the bottom of the courthouse steps was lighting a cigarette.

"And Rell?" she said. "Where does Rell stand?"

"He let us go at the col. I read him his grandmother's entry — the mill seizure, the fine she paid for eleven years. He didn't say anything. He just walked back down the mountain." Jude hesitated. "I think he's still deciding which side of the ledger he's on."

"Then he's wiser than most men, because most men decided years ago without knowing it." Sorel stood and paced to the window, hands clasped behind her back. "Understand what happens tomorrow. At nine o'clock I hear argument on the petition for my recusal. If I deny it — and I intend to deny it — the consortium will appeal immediately, and the appeal will buy them weeks. Weeks in which that chest can be stolen, subpoenaed, sealed, or made to disappear in a fire that no one will ever be charged for. Unless." She turned. "Unless the contents are already part of the public record before their lawyers finish their first sentence tomorrow morning."

Odile straightened. "You mean enter it into record. All of it. Tomorrow. At the hearing."

"I mean that a document entered into the official record of an open proceeding becomes evidence in a matter already before the court — and evidence cannot be destroyed by private parties without committing a crime against the administration of justice itself, in front of witnesses, in a courthouse full of reporters I intend to invite personally tonight." Sorel's voice was quiet and absolutely level. "They've spent forty years keeping this history contraband. Contraband can be seized. *Evidence* has to be answered."

The room went very still. Jude felt the words land somewhere deep, in the place where the keeper's lessons lived. *Memory is contraband,* the old man had told him, hunched over candlelight, teaching him letters. *That's why they fear it. But write it down plain enough, in the right room, in front of enough eyes, and it stops being contraband and becomes the truth — and truth is the one thing they can't carry away.*

He'd thought the keeper meant someday, vaguely, in some future he wouldn't see.

He hadn't known it would be a woman in a holey cardigan, tomorrow at nine o'clock.

"There's a risk you should hear from me plainly," Sorel continued, looking at Jude now, and her voice gentled without softening. "If I take custody of this ledger and enter it, your life does not return to normal afterward. There is no normal to return to. Men have killed for less than what's in that chest, and tomorrow they'll know it's beyond their reach, which makes some of them desperate and some of them clever. You'll be famous in the worst way a boy can be famous. Are you prepared for that?"

Jude thought about the cave. About the keeper's chair by the tide pool, empty now. About the shelf where his own name would have been carved, someday, if there had been anyone left to carve it. He had spent his whole life guarding a secret for people who were dead, against men who wanted it buried, waiting for a door he wasn't sure existed.

The door was a woman with a chipped teacup, and it was open.

"It was never mine to protect," he said. "It was mine to deliver. That's done. Do it."

Sorel studied him for a long moment. Then she nodded, once, the way judges nod — a verdict in miniature — and extended her hand, and he shook it, and her grip was dry and strong and did not let go quite immediately.

"Then it's accepted into my custody, as of this hour, witnessed—" she glanced at Odile, "—by whom, may I ask, am I addressing?"

"Marta," Odile said. Then, after a breath, with a tired shrug that cost her something: "Odile Winterbourne. Pilot. Formerly of Kettle Landing. Currently of nowhere in particular."

"Winterbourne." Sorel's eyebrows rose a fraction. "There's a Winterbourne in the 1897 volume. A ferryman who testified against the smelter interests and lost his license for it."

Odile went very still. "My great-grandfather," she said finally. "I never knew that."

"Read it sometime," Sorel said. "It's a good page. Your family has a habit of showing up when the record needs carrying." She turned to the window again, and the lamplight caught the green of the courthouse clock through the glass, and her voice dropped into something almost private. "Nine o'clock tomorrow. Sleep here — there are blankets in the jury room, and no one gets past Miriam's back stairs without me hearing them. And children—" she paused, and for a moment the judge was just an old woman looking at two exhausted young people, "—whatever happens in that courtroom tomorrow, know that you got it here. Through snow and blood and a broken wrist and God knows what else. No ruling of mine creates that. It only receives it."

* * *

The jury room had two benches and a window overlooking the square. Jude lay on one bench under a wool blanket, listening to the building tick and settle, watching the lamps below go out one by one. Odile slept on the other bench, her splinted arm propped on her chest, breathing slow and even for the first time in days.

At some hour past midnight, Jude gave up on sleep and went to the window.

The square was empty except for one figure. The man who had stood at the foot of the courthouse steps all evening was there still, collar up against the cold, smoking, looking up at the dark windows of the building — at this window, Jude realized, though he couldn't possibly be seen. Just looking. Taking its measure.

And as Jude watched, a second man emerged from the shadow of the clock tower's base and joined the first. They spoke briefly. The second man handed the first something small — papers, maybe, or money — and then both of them looked up at the courthouse together, unhurried, unafraid, like men checking the weather before a job they'd already been paid for.

Jude's hand found the windowsill and gripped it.

Tomorrow at nine o'clock, hope was going to walk up these steps in broad daylight, in front of everyone, and be entered into the record where no fire could reach it.

But between now and nine o'clock, the night was long, the doors were locked, and the men below had stopped pretending to be strangers.

He went and woke Odile.
