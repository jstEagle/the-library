## 20. Reading Into Record

The provincial seat smelled of coal smoke and wet stone, and Odile hated how much it looked like every other town they'd run through — the same company boarding houses, the same saloon with the same painted woman peeling off its false front. Except this town had a courthouse. A real one, granite-faced, with a clock tower that had kept honest time through three changes of government and didn't care who knew it.

Jude walked beside her with the chest strapped to his back like a pack mule's burden, and she could feel him vibrating, a wire pulled to singing. His eyes went everywhere — rooftops, alleys, the faces of men loitering near the feed store. He'd learned that much from her in six weeks on the trail: never look at the thing you're protecting; look at everything else.

"Steps," he said quietly.

"I see them."

The courthouse steps were wide and shallow, built for processions, for farmers in their Sunday coats, for the slow machinery of a justice that mostly worked by being too public to corrupt. There were eleven of them. She counted them the way she counted runway markers before a short-field landing, because counting was what her hands did when her mind wanted to scream.

Her left arm hung in a sling made from a flour sack and two bootlaces, the wrist beneath it swollen purple and hot. Six days since the timberline. Six days of Jude carrying her pack, feeding her, splinting her straight when the bone tried to set crooked, and never once saying what they both knew: that if it came to running, she was dead weight, and they both knew there would be no running today anyway.

"You could wait in the alley," Jude said. It wasn't charity. She'd taught him better than that. It was tactics, offered flat, and it still stung like a slap.

"No."

"They'll come for you first. You're the one who crashed the sled. Rell told me—"

"Rell talks too much." She stopped at the base of the steps and turned to face him, and up close she could see what six weeks had done to him — filled him out, hardened his hands, put a man's calculation behind the boy's eyes without quite killing the boy. "Listen to me. The ledger goes into record this morning or it doesn't. If it doesn't, we're just two more bodies in a snowmelt ditch by June. So we do this in front of as many people as God and the weather will give us. You understand? We are not sneaking in the side door. We are walking up those steps like we own them."

He swallowed. Nodded.

"And if it goes wrong," she said, "you don't stop. You don't come back for me. You get that chest to Sorel if you have to crawl the last hundred yards on your belly."

"That's not—"

"That's the deal. That's been the deal since Cold Fork." She held out her good hand. "Shake on it, keeper."

He took it. His grip was stronger than hers now, and neither of them mentioned it.

* * *

The clerk's office opened at eight. They were on the steps at seven-forty, and the square was already filling — market day, carts and crates, women with baskets, a knot of sawmill men in canvas coats who watched them climb with the flat curiosity of people who'd heard rumors. Word traveled ahead of them the way weather did. Somewhere between the valley and here, *the boy with the box* had become a story, and stories moved faster than snowmobiles.

Judge Amara Sorel met them in the rotunda. She was sixty, gray-braided, with a face like a cliff that had decided long ago which way it would erode. She looked at Jude, then at the chest, then at Odile's arm, and something in her jaw tightened.

"You're late," she said. "The land case convenes at nine. The consortium's counsel arrived an hour ago with four associates and a very expensive haircut."

"We're not here about the land case," Jude said. "We're here to make one."

Sorel's eyebrows rose a fraction. "You'd better come upstairs."

But they didn't make it upstairs.

Odile heard it first — she always heard engines first, a bush pilot's curse, the ear tuned to anything mechanical where it shouldn't be — and she turned toward the doors as three trucks swung into the square and parked in a line across its mouth, deliberate, theatrical, blocking the market traffic. Men got out. Eight of them. Nine. Not Rell's crew — Rell had let them go at the col and hadn't followed, and whatever that silence had cost him, he was paying it somewhere else. These were the second team. The ones led by the man with no interest in deals. The one called Voss, who wore his gun the way other men wore wedding rings.

The market noise died in a wave, stalls going quiet vendor by vendor, the way birds go quiet before a storm.

"Courthouse," Voss said, loud enough to carry. Not to them. To the square. "Nothing happens in there today that concerns any of you. Go home."

Nobody went home. That was the thing about company towns and mining camps and river settlements — people knew exactly what a consortium enforcer looked like, and they knew what it meant when nine of them blocked a courthouse square on market morning. The sawmill men drifted closer instead of away. An old woman with a basket of eggs stood her ground directly in front of the steps, arms crossed, like a hen defending a nest.

Voss started up the steps.

Odile planted herself in the middle of them, sling and all, and raised her voice to the register she used over engine noise.

"This is a courthouse," she said. "Not a company office. Whatever you think you're doing, you're doing it in front of forty witnesses and a clock tower, and I promise you the clock tower has outlasted better men than you."

Voss stopped one step below her. Up close he was ordinary — mid-forties, sandy, forgettable, the kind of face you'd trust with your luggage. That was the horror of him.

"Ma'am," he said pleasantly, "you're injured. You should sit down."

"I'm standing fine."

"There's a warrant for the boy. Theft of corporate property." He said it loudly, for the square. "The contents of that chest belong to Meridian Consolidated under claim filed fourteen years ago. We're simply recovering our assets."

"Is that what it is?" Odile let her voice crack across the square like a whip. "Because I've read what's in that chest, mister, and I can tell you what it is. It's names. It's dates. It's a magistrate who wrote down every man your company paid to look the other way, every homestead burned, every wage stolen, every grave dug shallow — for fifty years, in his own hand, witnessed and dated. And you want it back because paper burns but memory doesn't, and you've finally figured out the difference."

The square had gone absolutely silent. Even the clock seemed to hold its tick.

Voss smiled. It was the worst thing she'd seen in six weeks, worse than the whiteout, worse than the broken wrist — because it was patient. "Last chance, ma'am. Step aside."

"No."

"Then we'll step around you."

And that was when the old woman with the eggs moved left, closing the gap. Then one of the sawmill men. Then another. Nobody touched anybody. Nobody said a word. They just filled the steps, forty bodies deep, the way water fills a channel, and Voss stood on the fourth stair with his men at the bottom looking up at a wall of ordinary people who had suddenly remembered they outnumbered everybody.

Odile's heart was slamming so hard she could hear it in her teeth. She had stalled them. She had maybe a minute, maybe less, before patience ran out and this became the kind of morning that ended in blood — and above her, through the tall windows of the rotunda, she could see movement. Sorel. Sorel moving fast.

She turned her back on Voss. It was the hardest physical act of her life, harder than the crash, harder than the rapids — turning her back on a armed man on purpose, in front of everyone, because the script required it.

"Your Honor!" she shouted at the windows. "The record's waiting!"

* * *

What happened next, she would remember all her life in fragments, the way pilots remember crashes — not as continuous time but as a series of frames.

Sorel appearing in the great doors, robed, holding a leather-bound book of court procedure like a shield. Her voice, pitched to carry to the back of the square: *"By authority vested in me as circuit judge of this province, I am entering into the public record of this court the complete registry of the office of Magistrate Eland Corvane, deceased, comprising some four hundred and twelve entries, as presented by its lawful custodian—"*

"—Jude," the boy said, and his voice broke on his own name, and he said it again, louder. "Jude. Last name Corvane. I'm his grandson twice over, on both sides, and I'm the last keeper."

*"—as presented by its lawful custodian, Jude Corvane, who surrenders it not to any party but to the public itself, where no claim, lien, or writ may attach to it, for the record of this court belongs to the people of this province and may not be bought, burned, or buried—"*

Voss moved. Of course he moved. Odile saw him signal with two fingers, saw his men start up the steps into the crowd, and she did the only thing left in her inventory: she stepped sideways into the first enforcer's path, one-armed, off-balance, furious, and let him bounce off her like she was a gate post. He caught her bad wrist by reflex and the pain went through her like a bolt of lightning, white and total, and she screamed — not from weakness but because screaming was loud, and loud was the whole point, and forty witnesses heard a wounded woman cry out as a consortium man grabbed her.

The square detonated.

It wasn't a riot. It was worse than a riot, from Voss's point of view. It was a wall. Sawmill men, cart drivers, the egg woman swinging her basket like a flail, dockworkers, two off-duty constables who suddenly remembered their oaths — bodies packing the stairs shoulder to shoulder, not fighting, just *being there*, immovable, while inside the rotunda Sorel's clerk read entry after entry aloud through the open doors at full bureaucratic volume, the way the law required, the way the law had always required, the way nobody had ever dared make it matter until now:

*"...Entry ninety-one. The twelfth of March, thirty-one years past. At the Kettle Landing mill, wages withheld from forty-one men for a period of seven months. Witnessed. Entry ninety-two. The burning of the Halloran homestead, Upper Valley, with livestock confined. The fire was set by hired men in the pay of the predecessor concern. Witnessed. Entry ninety-three..."*

Names. Dates. Places. Fifty years of it, read into the record, transcribed by two clerks working in shifts, copied even as it was spoken, so that by noon there would be five copies and by evening twenty, and no truck, no torch, and no amount of gold dust in the world could call it back.

Odile sat down on the cold granite, cradling her wrist, and watched Voss understand it. That was the frame she kept. Not the pain, not the crowd, not Sorel's voice rolling through the rotunda like a bell — but Voss, standing on the fifth step, turning slowly in place, taking the measure of the square and finding it closed to him, and the exact moment when the arithmetic finished behind his eyes and he lowered his hand and walked back down through the crowd, which parted for him the way water parts for a drowned thing, and got in his truck, and drove away.

The enforcers' trucks pulled out one by one. The market noise came back like a tide. And through the open doors, the clerk's voice went on, steady as a metronome:

*"...Entry one hundred and six. The pilot D. Winterbourne, engaged to destroy a duplicate registry at Cold Fork, accepted payment and did not perform the task. Disposition of the duplicate unknown. Entered without judgment. The magistrate records what is done. What a man does with his own page is between him and the sky."*

Odile closed her eyes.

Her father's page. Read aloud, in public, on the steps of the highest court in the range — and the old dead magistrate, who had never met her father, had somehow understood the thing she'd needed six weeks and a broken wrist to learn: that the record keeps what happened, and lets the living decide what it means.

A shadow fell over her. Jude. He crouched down, the chest empty now, light as a shell, held against his chest like something he couldn't quite believe he was allowed to put down.

"It's done," he said. "She says it can't be sealed, amended, or expunged. Not by her. Not by anyone. It's *in the record*."

Odile looked up at him — this boy the cave raised, this boy with her blood type of stubbornness and none of her scars yet — and found she was laughing, or crying, or both, in the undignified way of the exhausted.

"Well," she managed. "I guess we're done running."

Jude opened his mouth to answer — and stopped, because Judge Amara Sorel had come down the steps herself, robes snapping in the wind, and she was looking at the two of them with an expression Odile had seen once before, years ago, on the face of a doctor delivering news too big for one sitting.

"Not quite done," Sorel said. "Mr. Corvane. The hearings begin Monday. And there's someone testifying who asked specifically whether you'd be in the room." She paused. "It's Rell."
