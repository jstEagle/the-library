## 22. Where Odile Lands

The ice went out of the Kettle River on a Tuesday, and by Thursday Odile was standing in the mud outside her hangar with a wrench in one hand and a parts catalog she'd memorized twice over in the other, looking at the Cessna like a woman deciding whether to forgive someone.

The plane had not forgiven easily. The enforcers' second team had found it at the Kettle Landing strip before the snow closed everything down — had slashed the tires, pulled every panel loose they could reach, and poured something into the fuel that smelled like a chemistry experiment gone to war. She'd spent the winter rebuilding it in a borrowed hangar with a wood stove that smoked and a radio that only caught the provincial station after dark. Her wrist had healed crooked enough that cold mornings made it sing, but her hands remembered everything else: the torque sequence, the safety-wire twist, the particular way a cylinder head seated when it was true.

Jude sat on an upturned crate near the hangar door, reading. He'd been reading all winter — court transcripts mostly, photocopied pages of the hearings Judge Sorel had set in motion, which he consumed the way other boys ate, steadily and without much visible pleasure but with real necessity. He was taller now. Not dramatically, but enough that his coat sleeves had surrendered their last inch of cuff, and Odile had stopped noticing the feral-polite boy from the cave and started noticing something more stubborn and more hopeful: a young man learning what it cost to be believed.

"You're going to fly it today," he said without looking up.

"I'm going to taxi it today."

"You said that three days ago."

"Three days ago the carburetor wasn't back from Fairbanks." She wiped her hands on a rag that had been white once, in some previous geological era. "You want to be useful, hand me the cowling pins."

He did, and they worked in the easy silence of two people who had spent four months sharing a wood stove and a bathroom schedule and the particular intimacy of watching each other grieve things neither of them could name. The consortium's collapse had been slow and public and unsatisfying in the way most justice is — no dramatic arrests, just subpoenas, frozen assets, survey crews quietly pulling stakes out of valleys while the news moved on to something louder. Rell testified for eleven days. Odile had watched some of it on the courthouse television, the enforcer captain's face gray and careful, and had felt nothing she could use.

* * *

The route filing happened at the small regional aviation office above the Kettle Landing general store, where a woman named Petra Okonkwo kept the charts and the weather logs and a running list of every pilot who owed her money. Odile filled out the form at the counter with a pencil stub, printing carefully because her left hand still tired fast.

MEDICAL COURIER & MAIL SERVICE — PROPOSED ROUTE: KETTLE LANDING / COLD FORK / SAWMILL TOWN / UPPER VALLEY SETTLEMENTS (ALL) / SEASONAL: ICE-OUT TO FREEZE-UP.

Petra read it upside down, as was her habit and her right. "That's a lot of stops for one plane."

"That's why I bought the cargo pod."

"And this line here." Petra tapped the form. "'Printed materials, public record.' You're hauling paper?"

"Copies of the ledger," Odile said. "Sorel's office printed five hundred sets. Every settlement in the range gets one. Bound, indexed, translated where it needs translating." She shrugged, or tried to; the shoulder on the broken side still complained. "People fought hard enough for it. Least I can do is deliver it."

Petra looked at her for a long moment over the counter. "You know you could've gone anywhere by now. Big courier contracts out of the city. I heard from two outfits asking after you."

"I heard too."

"They pay better than medicine runs and mail."

"Medicine doesn't wait," Odile said. "And mail—" She stopped, because the true answer was sitting in her chest where the restlessness used to live, and it didn't fit in a sentence yet. "Mail matters. That's all."

Petra stamped the form. "Route's provisional till inspection. Inspection's Friday. Don't crash before then, I hate the paperwork."

* * *

Friday she flew.

Not far — a shakedown loop up the valley and back, the new engine coughing once at altitude and then settling into the steady drone she'd missed all winter the way you miss a voice. Below her the range unrolled in its spring colors: brown water, gray rock, the first green insistence of willow along the creeks. Somewhere under that green were valleys she'd crossed on foot with a fourteen-year-old boy and a box of contraband memory, passes now open, rivers now fordable, the whole country unclenched and breathing.

She landed at Cold Fork on impulse, because the strip was there and the hermit's cabin smoke was rising and because some debts you pay in person. The old man came out squinting, recognized the plane before the pilot, and laughed until he had to hold his ribs.

"Thought you'd be long gone," he said. "Whole range had money on it. I lost twenty dollars."

"Sorry about your twenty dollars."

"Don't be. Best bet I ever lost." He fed her tea and told her the Sawmill Town union had won its back wages — the ledger entries had given the arbitrators dates and names nobody else had kept — and that a schoolteacher in Upper Valley was using the magistrate's records to teach local history, and that half the range now argued politics by citing page numbers. "Your boy start that?" he asked.

"He's not my boy."

"No," the hermit agreed mildly. "But he sits in your hangar reading your transcripts, so."

She flew home in the long gold light of a spring evening, and somewhere over the col — the same col where they'd bivouaced in the whiteout, where Jude had dug them into the snow with his bare hands and kept her alive through the worst night of either of their lives — she understood what had changed.

It wasn't that the wandering had stopped feeling good. It was that it had stopped being necessary. For fifteen years she'd flown away from a page in a burned book, from a father who'd taken consortium money and hanged himself with the shame of it, and every takeoff had been a small escape and every landing a small defeat. Now the page was public record. Anyone could read it. There was nowhere left to fly *from*.

Which meant everywhere she flew, she was flying *to*.

* * *

The first official run of the Winterbourne Medical & Mail Service went out on the first of June, and Jude rode along as cargo handler, address-checker, and — though the paperwork said otherwise — keeper emeritus of the record he'd carried out of a cave.

They hit six settlements that day. At each one the ritual was the same: medicine to whoever needed it, mail to everyone, and then the moment Odile had come to think of as the handing-over — a bound copy of the ledger passed across a counter or a porch rail or a tailgate into hands that took it with a weight people usually reserve for infants and rifles. An old woman at Sawmill Town opened hers on the spot and found her grandmother's name in it, entered by the magistrate himself in 1911, a debt recorded and never paid until now. She cried. Jude stood very straight and very still beside the plane until Odile put a hand on his shoulder, and then he breathed again.

At the last stop, a homestead up a creek with no name on any chart, a man came out to meet them who looked at Jude a long time before he spoke.

"I knew your keeper," he said. "Forty years back. He taught me my letters, right there in the cave mouth, tide coming in under our feet." He held the ledger copy against his chest like a shield. "Tell me the cave's still there."

"The tide still opens it," Jude said. "Twice a century, like always. Somebody'll keep it."

"Somebody will," the man agreed, and looked at him meaningfully, and Jude didn't look away.

On the flight home, in the dusk, Jude broke the silence.

"Odile. When the hearings end — when there's nothing left to testify to — what happens to keepers?"

"Keepers of what? It's public record now. Libraries have it. Schools."

"Someone should still carry it. Not the paper. The knowing. Why it mattered. What it cost." He turned the transcript he'd been reading face-down on his knee. "I've been thinking maybe the route needs a second person. Someone who goes into the settlements, talks to people, writes down what's happening now — the new injustices, the ones Sorel can't fix from a bench. A living ledger. And someone would need to fly him places."

Odile watched the ridgeline slide past, black against the last orange band of sky, and felt the old itch — the one that had kept her moving since she was nineteen — stir once, test its chains, and lie back down.

"There's a spare seat," she said. "Cargo pod's got room for a writing desk, if you build it light."

"You'd have to teach me to land it, eventually. In case."

"In case of what?"

"In case you're ever the one who can't."

She laughed — really laughed, the first time in months — and brought the plane around final over Kettle Landing, over the general store and the hangar and the wood stove and the crooked wrist and the whole small anchored life she'd built without noticing she was building it.

The wheels touched. She taxied in, shut down, and sat for a moment in the ticking quiet, Jude already climbing out to chock the tires, the evening smell of river and cut grass coming through the vents.

Fifteen years of leaving, and here she was, home before dark, with tomorrow's route already written on the clipboard on her knee:

COLD FORK. UPPER VALLEY. SAWMILL TOWN. AND ALL POINTS THE RECORD HASN'T REACHED YET.

She clipped the pen to the board, killed the lights, and went inside.
