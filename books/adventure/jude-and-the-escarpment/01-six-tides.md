## 1. Six Tides

The trench fought back.

Jude Galloway had learned to read ground the way other men read weather, and the ground on the west side of the Hebridean peninsula was telling him, in no uncertain terms, that it did not want a fibre-optic conduit laid through its spine. The peat gave way to clay, the clay to a shelf of Lewisian gneiss that had been old when the Atlantic was young, and every strike of the mini-excavator's bucket rang up through his arms like a bell tolling for his patience. Rain came sideways off the Minch, fine as needle-points, finding the gap between his collar and his neck with the persistence of something that had taken a personal interest in his discomfort.

He killed the engine and sat for a moment in the cab, watching the water bead and run down the glass. Through the grey wash he could see the rest of the crew: Callum at the spoil heap, cigarette cupped against the wind; young Sefa hauling cable drums with a rhythm too steady for the conditions, as if she'd decided the weather was beneath her notice. Good crew. Small crew. The kind that got hired for jobs nobody else wanted, at rates nobody else would stomach, by contractors who knew exactly how much a man with debts would endure.

Six weeks. That was what the letter from the debt collectors had said, though it hadn't said it kindly. Six weeks to clear what Petra Galloway had left behind when she walked out of his life — or rather, when she'd been walked out of it by bankruptcy proceedings, an academic disgrace, and whatever private catastrophe she had never once explained to her son. Forty-three thousand pounds, accrued across three creditors, compounding monthly like something biological. The solicitors used phrases like *structured repayment* and *asset recovery*. What they meant was: dig, Jude. Dig until your hands are ruined and your back is a question mark, and maybe, if the ground is generous, you'll be free of her before you're fifty.

He was thirty-one. Some mornings he felt the arithmetic of it like a stone in his boot.

The rain thickened. He started the excavator again and went back to war with the gneiss.

* * *

They broke for lunch in the site hut at half twelve, which on Harris in October was less a meal than a ritual of endurance. Callum boiled water on a battered camping stove while Sefa spread her sandwiches on the table with the care of someone laying out surgical instruments. Jude sat near the window, peeling off gloves that had gone from wet to cold to some third state beyond both, and that was when Callum's phone buzzed against the tin surface of the table.

Callum read the message twice. Then he looked up, and there was something in his face that Jude had learned to distrust over two years of working together — the particular blankness of a man deciding how much to say.

"That'll be it, then," Callum said. "Confirmation came through."

"Confirmation of what?"

"The next contract." He turned the phone face-down on the table, which was unlike him. "Big one. Marine salvage. Survey vessel out of Stornoway, then a working fleet. Six weeks' minimum, hazard rates, the lot."

Jude felt something shift under his ribs, the way ground shifts before it slumps. "Salvage doesn't come looking for a land crew with a mini-dig and a transit van. Who's the contractor?"

And Callum, who had faced down angry farmers and one memorable incident with a bull, looked away first.

"It's Vane," Sefa said quietly, not looking up from her sandwiches. She had a way of arriving at the centre of things without seeming to move toward them. "Marcus Vane. Meridian Recovery Group. We're contracted as shore support and diver handling. The job is the *Meridian*."

The name landed in the hut like a dropped tool.

Jude had grown up with that word the way other children grew up with prayers. The *Meridian* — a cargo steamer lost off the northwest coast in 1934, forty souls aboard, sitting in sixty metres of cold Atlantic dark. His mother had spent four years of her life on it. Four years of expeditions, permits, sonar surveys, funding applications written in the small hours while he slept in the cab of a Land Rover with his coat over his head. And then, in the space of a single season, all of it ended. A diver died — Tomas Reyes, a name spoken in their house afterward only in the past tense, and never above a whisper — and Petra Galloway signed off the project, surrendered the survey data, and began the long retreat that ended with her reputation in pieces and her son wondering, for twenty years, what he had done to make her choose the sea's silence over him.

She'd told him once, the last time they'd spoken properly, standing in the doorway of a rented flat in Oban with her bags already packed: *"Some wrecks keep what they keep for a reason, Jude. Promise me you'll never go down to her."*

He had been nineteen. He had promised nothing, because promising would have meant admitting he understood.

Now the promise was being sold back to him at market rate.

"When does she sail?" he asked. His voice came out level, and he was grateful for that, because inside the levelness something was screaming.

"Sunday's tide," Callum said. "Vane wants full complement aboard. There's a signing bonus." He named the figure, and Jude made himself do the arithmetic he'd been avoiding since the word *salvage*: the bonus alone covered nearly a fifth of the debt. Six weeks of hazard pay covered most of the rest. It wasn't fortune. But it was the difference between six weeks and six months, between freedom and another winter of trenches.

"You don't have to take it," Sefa said. Still not looking at him. "I can tell them we're short-handed. I can tell them anything."

"And lose the contract? Lose the rates?" He shook his head, and heard how reasonable he sounded, and hated how reasonable he sounded. "It's a job, Sefa. Wrecks are just wrecks. Steel and silt and whatever the lawyers say belongs to whom. My mother's ghosts aren't on the payroll."

But even as he said it, he saw her — not Petra as she was in the photographs, squinting into Atlantic light with a survey map pinned under one elbow, but Petra as she'd been that last morning in Oban, gripping the doorframe as if the building might otherwise carry her back to him. *Some wrecks keep what they keep.*

He was about to find out what she'd meant. For money. For Marcus Vane, of all people — the rival whose name had curdled every dinner-table conversation of his childhood, whose ambition his mother had described once, in a rare unguarded moment, as *"a man who mistakes the seabed for a bank vault."*

"Sunday's tide," Jude repeated. He pulled his gloves back on. Outside, the rain had thinned to a silver drifting haze, and far out past the headland the sea lay flat and pewter-coloured, keeping its counsel. "Then I'd better get the trench finished."

* * *

He worked the rest of the day harder than he needed to, driving the machine until his shoulders burned, because work was the one argument he'd never learned to lose. By the time the light failed — early now, the days folding themselves away like letters nobody wanted to read — the conduit trench had reached the headland's shoulder, and below it, visible for the first time through a break in the cloud, the open Atlantic stretched black and enormous toward where the *Meridian* lay.

He stood at the edge of the cut with a thermos of tea going cold in his hands and let himself think the thought he'd been refusing all afternoon:

*She swore off that wreck. She walked away from everything rather than go back to it. And the first real money I've been offered in two years comes from the man who helped drive her out, asking me to do exactly the thing she begged me never to do.*

A pragmatic man would take the job. A pragmatic man would pocket the bonus, keep his head down, handle his divers, and never once ask why Marcus Vane — after twenty years of leaving the *Meridian* alone — suddenly wanted her raised now, this season, with this urgency. Pragmatism was a coat that fit him well. He'd worn it so long he'd forgotten it was a costume.

His phone buzzed. A text from the debt collector's office, automated, cheerful in the way only machines managing human ruin can be: *Reminder: 42 days remaining on agreed settlement window.*

Forty-two days. Six tides, give or take, if you counted the way sailors counted.

Jude looked out at the water for a long moment. Somewhere under that pewter skin lay a ship, and a dead man's family, and whatever truth his mother had carried out of the deep and buried so thoroughly that she'd buried herself along with it.

"All right," he said aloud, to the sea, to the trench, to the ghost in the doorway in Oban. "You win. I'm coming down."

He drained the cold tea, climbed back into the cab, and turned the excavator toward the last stretch of rock — telling himself, in the voice of a man who has already lost the argument but hasn't admitted it yet, that it was only ever about the money.

It was the first lie of the voyage, and he knew it.
