## 2. The Compass That Lies

The *Corvina* smelled of diesel and new money. Jude felt the difference the moment he came aboard — everything on Marcus Vane's survey vessel was either gleaming or wrapped in plastic waiting to be gleaming, and both conditions struck him as a kind of lie. The salvage crews he'd worked with in the Hebrides wore their rust like scars. Vane's fleet wore cologne.

His bunk was amidships, a narrow coffin of varnished pine with a reading lamp bolted to the bulkhead and a folded towel laid across the mattress like an offering. Someone had vacuumed. Someone had left a bottle of water with the label facing out. Jude set his duffel down on it and did not unpack for a long moment, standing instead with his hand flat against the hull, feeling the engine's idle come up through the wood like a pulse.

Six weeks. That was what the collectors had given him, and six tides was what Vane had promised, and the arithmetic between those two numbers was the whole shape of his life now. He'd signed the papers in the crew office at Stornoway without reading past the indemnity clause — he knew how those clauses worked; they were designed by people who expected men like him not to read. His name was on a manifest bound for the Meridian site, forty fathoms down off the shoulder of the Sound, and somewhere below that gray-green ceiling of water lay whatever Vane believed was worth three vessels, a dive team, and the patience to hire a trench-digger who had once been somebody's son.

*Once been.* He caught himself doing it already — thinking of Petra Galloway in the past tense, though no one had told him she was dead. She was worse than dead. She was absent, which meant she could still be imagined alive, which meant every silence from her carried the specific cruelty of a door left open.

He unpacked slowly, because there was almost nothing to unpack: two shirts, oilskins, a photograph he kept face-down because looking at it aboard this ship felt like treason against himself. And at the bottom of the duffel, wrapped in a wool sock inside a tin that had once held tobacco, the compass.

Brass, palm-sized, its glass clouded at the edges where twenty years of salt air had gotten under the bezel. The hinge was stiff. It had always been stiff; even as a boy he'd needed both hands to open it, and Petra would watch him struggle and say, *Good. Things that open easily aren't worth opening.* Her voice arrived in his head unbidden, in that low Hebridean burr she'd never quite sanded off, and he shut it away the way you shut a drawer on something that might bite.

He opened it anyway.

The needle swung free and did what needles do — or should have done. Instead it shivered, hesitated, and settled pointing not north but east-southeast, off by nearly a hundred degrees, steady as a churchgoer. Jude turned the compass a quarter-turn in his hand. The needle stayed fixed on its bearing, indifferent to the case, indifferent to him.

"Local anomaly," he said aloud, to hear something ordinary in the room. Iron in the hull. Cargo steel in the hold. There were a dozen mundane explanations and he reached for each one and found it thin as gauze. They were still tied up at the quay, engines idling but not running hot, and the *Corvina*'s own magnetism wouldn't reach through a porthole and seize a needle like this. He walked the compass to the far bulkhead. Same bearing. He climbed the ladder to the deck, into the wind and the flat pewter light of the Minch, and held it out over the rail like a man offering feed to a bird.

East-southeast. Toward the Meridian.

He stood there until his knuckles ached from the cold, watching the needle tremble with something that looked less like magnetism than eagerness. His mother's compass, the one she'd carried on every dig he could remember, the one she'd let him hold once on the cliff path above Aird Uig and said, *This doesn't tell you where you are, Jude. It tells you what you're looking for.* He'd thought it a joke then, the kind of thing mothers said to make mysteries out of hardware. Now the joke sat in his palm pointing at a grave.

Because that was what the Meridian was. Not a treasure. Not a prize. A grave with a serial number. Everyone on this fleet knew the story the way fishermen know weather — the expedition twenty years back, the diver who went down and didn't come up, the inquiry that found nothing to find, and Petra Galloway walking off the site mid-contract and never taking another marine job as long as she lived. Vane had built his fleet on the wreckage of her reputation. And now Vane was going back, and he'd hired her son without — Jude was almost certain — knowing whose son he'd hired.

Almost.

* * *

Vane found him at dusk on the aft deck, and Jude understood immediately that *found* was the accurate verb. The man moved quietly for someone so large; he had the stillness of a heron, all the violence stored and waiting. He was holding two mugs of coffee and offered one with the easy generosity of a landlord.

"Galloway." Just the surname. "You cut a clean trench at Borve. My foreman says you read sediment like a ledger."

"I dig where I'm told."

"That's rarer than skill." Vane leaned on the rail beside him, close enough that their sleeves nearly touched, and looked out at the water with the expression of a man appraising property. "Your mother taught at Glasgow for a while. Stratigraphy. Sediment records. I followed her work before she—" He made a small gesture, a door closing. "Before she stopped publishing."

"She stopped digging," Jude said. "Publishing requires something to say."

Something flickered behind Vane's eyes — not anger. Interest. The interest of a man who has heard a floorboard creak in a house he thought empty. "You don't speak of her kindly."

"You don't know me well enough to expect otherwise."

"No," Vane agreed pleasantly. "But I know her methods when I see them. You flagged the contractor's core samples at Stornoway before anyone asked you to. You re-ordered the dive rotation by tidal lag, not seniority — that was in your file notes, did you know? Very Petra. She used to sequence her trenches by the tide tables when everyone else was working by the clock." He sipped his coffee. "It's why I hired you. Sentiment, if you like. Or the belief that good habits survive estrangement."

Jude kept his face still and his coffee untasted. *Estrangement.* So Vane knew. Had known when he signed him, probably — had maybe chosen him *because*, the way you'd choose a knife for its history. The compass sat in his jacket pocket like a coal. He became aware of it the way you become aware of a wound, by its heat.

"The Meridian," Jude said. "Why now? Twenty years is a long time to leave money on the seabed."

Vane smiled, and it was a good smile, practiced in mirrors. "Permits lapsed. Claims reverted. The sea keeps its own registry, Mr. Galloway — it just needed someone patient enough to read it." He pushed off the rail and straightened, and for a moment his shadow fell across Jude entire. "Get some sleep. First dive tomorrow, slack water at oh-seven-forty. I want my best hands rested."

"I dig," Jude said. "I don't dive."

"Not yet." Vane said it lightly, already turning away, and somehow that was the most frightening thing he'd said all evening. At the companionway he paused, half-turned, silhouetted against the warm light below decks. "One more thing. Your mother kept a brass compass. Everyone who worked with her remembers it — she treated it like scripture. If you ever come across it, I'd take it as a professional courtesy to see it again. Nostalgia." Another smile, thinner. "Objects like that have a way of pointing people wrong."

Then he was gone, and the hatch closed, and Jude stood alone in the dark with the wind coming off the Minch and the needle in his pocket straining east-southeast toward forty fathoms of black water.

He took it out one last time. In the darkness he couldn't read the card, only feel the needle's tremble through the brass, faint and constant, like something knocking.

Whatever his mother had sworn off, it wasn't finished with her. And now, it seemed, it had sent for her son.

Below, in the galley, the dive team laughed over cards, and the tide began to turn.
