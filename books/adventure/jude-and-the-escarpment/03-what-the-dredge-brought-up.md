## 3. What the Dredge Brought Up

The Meridian came up in pieces, the way all drowned things do.

Jude stood at the rail of the *Corvus* and watched the dredge claw its first bite out of the seabed, forty fathoms down, and felt something turn over in his chest that he refused to name. The wreck lay on a shelf of rock off the Sound of Harris, half-buried in sand the color of old bone, and the sonar images Vane's people had shown him made it look like a sleeping animal — a cargo steamer from the last century, gone down with holds full of brass fittings, copper ingots, and whatever else the manifest had never admitted to. Salvage law said what came up belonged to whoever held the permit. Vane held the permit. Jude held a shovel and a debt.

"First lift in twenty minutes," called the dive marshal, a broad Norwegian named Halvard whose voice carried over the wind like a foghorn. "Stand clear of the A-frame."

Jude didn't stand clear. He never did. It was a habit from the contractor trenches — you learned where the danger actually lived, and it was never where they told you to stand. He watched the winch cable come taut, singing a low note through the morning, and then the crane swung its burden up out of the grey water in a sheeting cascade of weed and silt.

It was not copper. It was not brass.

It was a strongbox, iron-banded, crusted white with twenty years of sea, and it hit the deck with a sound like a coffin lid.

Beside it, tangled in the same netting, came something smaller. Jude saw it before anyone else did, because he knew its shape the way he knew his own hands. A dive helmet. Old pattern, hard-hat brass, the kind his mother had trained in when he was a boy and she'd let him polish the fittings until they shone like suns. This one was furred with barnacle, the copper gone green as verdigris, but the faceplate —

The faceplate was cracked. A single star of fracture radiating from the center, the glass gone opaque with age, and behind it nothing but darkness and the sea's slow chemistry.

Nobody spoke. Even the gulls seemed to go quiet, hanging on the wind above the mast.

Vane appeared at the rail's end the way he always did, without seeming to cross the distance between one place and another. He was a lean man gone silver at the temples, with the particular stillness of someone who had spent decades making other people move first. His eyes went to the strongbox, and something hungry moved behind them; then they went to the helmet, and everything behind them closed like a shop shutter.

"Box goes to my cabin," he said. "The rest is scrap. Dump it."

"It's a helmet," Jude said. Before he could stop himself. "Hard-hat rig. That's an artifact, not scrap."

Vane looked at him then, and for a moment the sea wind was the only thing moving between them. "Everything that comes off this wreck is mine by permit, Mr. Galloway. Including the scrap." He turned to Halvard. "Over the side. Now."

The helmet went back into the water with less ceremony than it deserved. Jude watched it sink, turning slowly, the cracked faceplate catching the light once — a flash, like a signal, like an eye opening — and then the grey took it. He stood at the rail long after the water closed, and the feeling in his chest finally found its name, and the name was dread.

* * *

Sefa had counted the serial numbers on eleven hundred pieces of salvage in her three years aboard Vane's fleet, and she had never once miscounted.

She was in the galley when the helmet came up, scrubbing pots because it was her watch and the fleet didn't care about weather or wonders, only watches. She heard the crane, heard the crowd at the rail, and by the time she got there the deck was already emptying and there was a wet ring on the planking where something heavy had lain.

But Sefa had grown up on her uncle's stories, and her uncle had died on a wreck, and she had spent nineteen years learning every number attached to his name. Tomas Reyes, dive ticket 4471-C, registered rig: Siebe Gorman, six-bolt, serial prefix TR-2. She'd traced the prefix through salvage registries the way other girls traced pop stars. When the incident report finally reached the family — two years late, three pages thin — she had memorized the equipment list the way you memorize a psalm.

TR-2044. That was the number stamped inside the faceplate frame of her uncle's helmet.

She hadn't seen the helmet itself. She'd only seen the deck after, and the wet ring, and Halvard the Norwegian scrubbing something green off his gloves with a look on his face she'd never seen a man wear before. But she'd heard Galloway say *hard-hat rig*, and she'd heard Vane say *dump it*, and she had spent nineteen years learning what men sounded like when they were hiding something.

That night she climbed to the wheelhouse under the pretense of returning the log book, and while the mate's back was turned she read the day's recovery manifest. One item listed. One iron strongbox, condition sealed. No helmet. No mention of any helmet at all.

The sea outside the wheelhouse glass was black and patient. Sefa stood very still, holding the log book against her chest like a shield, and thought about her mother receiving that two-years-late report, and how her mother had read it standing up, and then sat down on the floor, and how no one had ever explained why the report described a regulator failure when her uncle's rig had been a surface-supplied helmet with no regulator to fail.

Nineteen years of small wrongnesses, and tonight one of them had surfaced wearing barnacles.

She went down to the crew quarters and found Galloway sitting on his bunk in the dark, not sleeping, just sitting, the way men sit when they're arguing with themselves.

"You saw it," she said. Not a question.

Galloway's head came up. In the dim light his eyes were wary. "Saw what?"

"The helmet." She kept her voice flat, deckhand-flat, the voice that carried no consequences. "Brass, six-bolt, cracked faceplate. You told Vane it was an artifact. He dumped it anyway."

Something moved behind Galloway's eyes — recognition, maybe, or the beginning of fear. "You know the rig?"

"I know the serial number." She let that sit in the dark between them. "TR-2044. It belonged to Tomas Reyes. He died on this wreck twenty years ago, on the first survey expedition, and the official report says his air failed." She paused. "His rig didn't have air to fail. It was fed from the surface."

Galloway was quiet for a long time. When he finally spoke, his voice was rough. "My mother was on that expedition."

"I know," Sefa said. "Petra Galloway. Her name's in the report. Witness statement, page two." She had read that page so many times the paper had gone soft. "You want to know what else is in the report? Nothing about a strongbox. Nothing about why the survey stopped. They pulled everyone off the site in nine days and the permits went cold, and then Marcus Vane bought the rights five years later from a shell company nobody can trace."

The ship rolled beneath them, gentle as a hand rocking a cradle. Somewhere above, Vane's cabin light burned behind frosted glass.

"So why," Sefa said quietly, "does a man dump a dead diver's helmet overboard within four minutes of it coming up — unless he already knows exactly whose it is?"

Galloway didn't answer. But he didn't look away either, and Sefa had spent nineteen years learning the difference between men who lied and men who were afraid of the truth, and Marcus Vane's newest hire was the second kind.

* * *

Jude lay awake past midnight, listening to the engines turn over toward the wreck site, and did the arithmetic he'd been avoiding since the helmet sank.

His mother had left this wreck. Left the profession, left the islands, eventually left him — and he had spent fifteen years telling himself a story about ambition, about a woman who loved old stones more than her own son. But Sefa's questions had put hairline cracks through that story, and through the cracks he could see another shape entirely: a woman who had seen something down there, or known something, and been made to leave.

And now here was her son, digging the same seabed for the man who might have made her.

He got up quietly and took the brass compass from his kit — the one thing of hers he carried, the one thing the debt collectors hadn't touched because it was worthless. Its needle lay still in the lamplight, pointing not north but down and east, down and east, faithful as a dog at a grave.

Tomorrow the dredge would open the strongbox. He'd seen Vane's hands on the rail today, and he understood now that whatever was in that box, Vane had crossed an ocean expecting it — and would drown anyone who stood between him and keeping it buried.

Jude set the compass on the sill, needle trembling toward the deep, and made himself a promise shaped like a question: *What did you find, Mum? What did they make you walk away from?*

Above him, faint through the deck plates, he heard Vane's cabin door open and close, and footsteps going aft — toward the dive locker, where the manifests were kept.

Someone was rewriting history tonight. And Jude intended to read the original.
