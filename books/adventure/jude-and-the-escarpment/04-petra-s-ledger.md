## 4. Petra's Ledger

The strongbox came up in the second lift bag of the day, and it was heavier than it had any right to be.

Jude felt the weight before he saw it — a dead pull on the line as he fed slack to the winch operator above, the kind of drag that meant iron, not coral. He'd been diving wrecks long enough to know the difference between a box that held ballast and a box that held something someone had bothered to lock. This one had a hasp gone green with two decades of salt, a keyhole furred with barnacle, and stamped into the lid, half-eaten by rust but still legible: **M/V MERIDIAN — CHARTERED SURVEY — 1998**.

He'd known the name since before he could read properly. It had been a lullaby in his house once, back when his mother still sang anything at all.

Now he crouched over it on the deck of Vane's survey vessel, water pooling off his wetsuit into the scuppers, and let the crew crowd around him without really seeing them. Sefa was there, arms crossed against the wind. Two of Vane's divers. The bosun with his pry bar already out, looking to Vane's deck boss for permission like a dog waiting on a whistle.

"Open it," the deck boss said.

The hasp sheared on the third swing. Inside, packed in tarred canvas that had done its job better than anyone deserved: a dive slate with Petra Galloway's initials scratched into the corner. A waterproof document case, swollen but sealed. And beneath both, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, a ledger bound in grey board, its spine cracked from years of use before it ever touched the sea.

Jude knew the handwriting on the cover before he knew what the book was.

*P.G. — Site Records — Do Not Remove.*

His hands were shaking. He told himself it was the cold.

* * *

He was nine years old the first time he understood that his mother's work was a kind of war.

It was a kitchen table in Oban, rain hammering the window, and Petra had spread charts across the wood like a general laying out a campaign. She'd been arguing with a man on the phone — Jude remembered only fragments of his mother's side of it, the way you remember lightning rather than the storm whole. *"Permits don't make history, Marcus. They just decide who gets to sell it."*

Marcus. He hadn't known then who Marcus was. He'd thought it was a woman's name, because the only Marcuses he knew were in books.

After she hung up she'd caught him watching and softened, the way she always did for him alone, and pulled him onto her knee among the charts. "Do you know what this is?" she'd said, tapping a contour line. "That's where a ship died. Everything under that line is somebody's last afternoon. You don't get to dig that up because a piece of paper says you can." She'd kissed the top of his head, smelling of coffee and diesel and the particular cold-air smell she carried home from every survey. "Remember that, Jude. The paper isn't the truth. The wreck is the truth."

He had remembered it. He'd remembered it right up until the debt collectors started calling, at which point paper became the only truth that mattered — paper debts, paper claims, paper names on paper contracts. He'd taken this job telling himself the wreck didn't care who raised it. Telling himself his mother's principles were a luxury he couldn't inherit along with her name and her ruin.

Now her handwriting sat in his hands, dredged up from the exact grave she'd sworn off, and every one of those comfortable lies began to rot.

* * *

He waited until the evening watch to open the ledger properly. Vane had ordered the strongbox contents catalogued and locked in the chart room — standard procedure, the deck boss said, everything logged for the salvage master's review — but nobody had counted the items twice, and Jude had spent four years on salvage crews learning which pockets went unwatched between tides. The ledger rode inside his jacket to the fo'c'sle, flat against his chest like a second heartbeat.

Sefa found him there an hour later. She didn't ask what he was reading. She'd seen his face when the box opened; whatever was in it had reached up through twenty years of water and grabbed him by the throat, and she was Reyes' niece besides, and people who carried drowned family in their mouths recognized the weight in others.

She sat down across from him with her tea and said nothing, which was its own kind of permission.

The first pages were routine. Petra's site records, meticulous as scripture: tide tables, visibility logs, artefact inventories in columns so straight they looked ruled. Dated spring 1998. The Meridian, chartered survey, west coast shelf. His mother's abbreviations everywhere — *vis. poor, surge mod., rec. halted — perms. in question.*

Permits in question. He turned the page and the war began.

There was a photostat, folded twice, of a salvage permit issued by the regional maritime authority. The signature block read *M. Vane, Salvage Master.* But beside it, in Petra's hand — small, furious, pressed so hard the pen had nearly torn through — was a list. Dates. Filing numbers. Cross-references to registry offices that, Jude could see even without checking, did not match the dates claimed. The permit had been signed three weeks before the application it supposedly answered existed. A younger Marcus Vane had put his name to a document authorizing a claim that had not yet been made, on a wreck whose coordinates had been obtained — the next page made clear how — from Petra's own confidential survey data, sold or stolen, she hadn't determined which, though the margin note *ask R. if he copied my files* suggested she'd had her suspicions about someone close.

And below all of it, one paragraph in Petra's hand that stopped Jude's breath:

*T. Reyes flagged the discrepancy on 14 June. Tomas being Tomas, he wanted to report it. I told him to wait until we surfaced — no point burning bridges from forty metres down. He agreed to hold. God forgive me, I should have let him shout it from the masthead.*

Jude read the date twice. Fourteen June. The dive where Tomas Reyes died was the sixteenth.

Two days. His mother had asked a good man to keep quiet for two days, and the sea had collected the debt before she could repay it.

* * *

He had spent eleven years being angry at her for walking away.

That anger had a shape he knew better than his own handwriting: the empty chair at graduation, the postcards with no return address, the cottage in the Hebrides he'd visited exactly once, at nineteen, standing in her garden while she watched him from the doorway as if he were a stranger who'd wandered in from the road. *You chose the sea over me,* he'd told her once, the night before he signed on with his first contractor crew, and she'd looked at him with an expression he'd filed away as guilt and never examined again.

But the ledger's later pages told a different story, and they told it in the language of a woman documenting her own destruction with the same precision she gave to tide tables. Page after page of correspondence copies. Letters to the maritime authority, returned unopened or answered with form rejections. A lawyer's letter advising her that pursuing the forgery claim would be *costly and unlikely to succeed given the passage of time and the absence of the primary witness.* The primary witness. Tomas Reyes, dead, unable to testify that he'd found the fraud — or perhaps unwilling, in the end, to testify to anything at all.

And then a final letter, undated, never sent, tucked into the back cover like a confession too heavy to carry further:

*M.,*

*I know what you did. I have known since June. I could prove it once; now I can only know it, and knowing is worth nothing in any court you can't buy.*

*I'm not writing to threaten you. I'm writing so that somewhere outside your head there exists a record that you were caught. That a diver died carrying your secret, and I let him carry it because I was a coward for two days, and you made sure those two days cost everything.*

*I am leaving the profession. Not because you won — because staying means working in the house you built. Every permit I sign, every claim I stake, will sit on top of yours like frost on rotten ground. I won't do it. Take the sea. It was never mine anyway. It was only ever the truth's, and you've buried that deeper than any wreck.*

*— P.*

Jude set the ledger face-down on his knees and stared at the bulkhead for a long time.

She hadn't abandoned him. That was the thing arriving in him slowly, painfully, like blood returning to a numb limb. She hadn't chosen the sea over her son, or principle over family, or ghosts over the living. She had been driven out — professionally executed by a rival young enough and hungry enough to forge his way to a fleet, and she had swallowed it whole rather than spend her son's childhood in courtrooms she'd lose. The silence, the distance, the cottage with its locked shed and its single cold visit — none of it was abandonment. It was exile. Self-imposed, yes. Chosen, in the way drowning men choose water. But exile nonetheless, and the man who'd decreed it was sleeping thirty feet above him in the master cabin of a ship named for the very crime.

Grief reframed itself around the old anger the way water reframes around a new obstruction. He wasn't angry at her anymore. He was angry *for* her, and the anger had a direction now, and the direction was up, toward the bridge, toward the cabin where Marcus Vane kept his charts and his forged legacy and his fleet.

"Incoming," Sefa said quietly, nodding at the porthole. On deck, lamplight moved — the deck boss making rounds, or worse, someone sent to check on the Galloway boy who'd gone pale over a dead box.

Jude wrapped the ledger back in its oilcloth. But his hands slowed at the last page, because there was one more thing: a slip of newer paper, laid in like a bookmark, in Petra's hand but shakier, older. Coordinates. The Meridian's true position — not the published one, the *true* one, offset by half a mile. And beneath them, five words:

*If you're reading this, finish it.*

She had hidden the ledger where only someone diving her wreck would find it. She had written it to whoever came after. To him, maybe. To anyone. To the truth itself.

Above, boots crossed the deck. The hatch handle turned.

Jude slid the ledger into the space behind the fo'c'sle's loose panel — the same trick, he realized with a strange cold clarity, that his mother would have used — and had his tea in hand before the door swung open and Marcus Vane filled it, silhouetted against the lamps, smiling the smile of a man who suspected nothing and checked everything.

"Galloway," Vane said. "A word about tomorrow's dive."
