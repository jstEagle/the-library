## 5. Storm Glass

The barometer began its fall at dawn, and by noon the sky over the Minch had turned the color of wet slate. Sefa felt it first in her teeth — a pressure, a wrongness — before the wind arrived to confirm it. She had grown up reading weather the way other people read faces, and this face was saying something ugly.

"Glass is dropping fast," she told the mate on watch, tapping the brass instrument mounted by the wheel. "Faster than any spring blow I've seen."

The mate, a Shetlander named Guthrie with forearms like mooring rope, squinted southward where the horizon had gone soft and bruised. "Vane knows. He's already signaled the fleet."

By mid-afternoon the order came down: all three vessels of Vane's salvage flotilla were to run for the shelter of Loch Erisort, a long finger of sea cutting deep into Lewis's eastern coast. The Meridian job would wait out the storm. Sefa stood at the rail as they came about and watched the survey vessel *Corvus* heel into the swell ahead of them, her wake unspooling white behind her like thread pulled from a wound.

They anchored in the loch as the light failed, three ships riding at their cables in the gathering dark, close enough that Sefa could see lamplight moving behind the *Corvus*'s windows. Rain came in horizontal sheets. The wind found every gap in the superstructure and played it like a badly tuned instrument, and below decks the whole ship groaned and shifted, hull plates ticking against each other as if counting.

Sefa didn't sleep. She lay in her bunk listening to the storm interrogate the vessel, and she thought about a serial number stamped into a dive helmet's collar: **TR-4471**. She thought about her uncle's hands, which had been beautiful hands, quick and sure, the hands of a man who could tie a bowline blindfolded in the dark. She was nine years old when those hands stopped existing. Twenty years, and the sea had kept him, and Marcus Vane had kept the secret of how.

She rose near midnight, dressed against the cold, and went up.

* * *

The galley was empty at that hour except for the kettle's low simmer and the smell of old coffee. Sefa poured herself a mug more for warmth than thirst and settled onto the bench beneath the porthole, where the glass showed nothing but rain and the distant, wavering lights of the sister ships.

That was when she heard voices on the companionway stairs.

She knew the first voice immediately. Vane's voice carried the way his money did — effortlessly, expecting space to be made for it. The second voice took her longer to place: Captain Aldous Rennick, who commanded the *Kestrel*, the smallest and oldest of the three vessels, a man Sefa had always taken for Vane's most reliable lieutenant. He sounded different now. He sounded like a man being handed something heavy and being asked not to look at it.

"—delay works in our favor, actually," Vane was saying. Footsteps crossed the galley doorway without entering; they paused just beyond it, in the passage where the chart table sat under a swivel lamp. "Rennick, sit down."

A creak of a stool. Sefa eased off the bench and moved to the galley's back corner, behind the stacked crates of provisions, where the shadow was deepest. She left her mug on the bench. A mistake like that could be forgiven; a person standing in an empty galley at one in the morning could not.

"The Reyes matter," Vane continued. His voice had dropped into the register he used for figures and settlements, smooth as ballast. "It's resurfaced. Literally." A dry sound that might have been a laugh. "The helmet. You saw what came up."

"I saw you order it thrown back," Rennick said.

"And you did it, because you're a sensible man. Now I need you to be a sensible man once more. There's a family. Niece, mostly. The parents are dead, the brother's a drunk in Cádiz. The niece works — " a pause, and Sefa felt her own pulse hammering against her ribs — "somewhere in the fleet, actually. Deckhand. Good worker, by all accounts."

The silence that followed lasted four full seconds. Sefa counted them against her heartbeat.

"You're telling me," Rennick said slowly, "that the girl on my deck crew is Tomas Reyes' blood."

"I'm telling you it doesn't matter, provided it stays a matter of arithmetic rather than sentiment. I have an intermediary in Stornoway — a solicitor, respectable, no connection to me or to the company. Tomorrow, when the weather eases enough to launch the tender, you'll take ashore a sealed envelope. Inside is an offer: a settlement, generous, twenty years of interest compounded into it. In exchange, the family signs a release affirming the death was accidental, waives all claims, and — this is the important part — agrees not to cooperate with any inquiry, maritime or journalistic, past or future."

"And if she refuses?"

Another pause. When Vane spoke again, his voice had cooled several degrees, the way the loch water had cooled outside the hull. "Then the offer becomes a warning. The release gets signed eventually, Rennick. The only variable is whether it's signed gratefully or bitterly. People sign gratefully when the number is right. Make sure the number is right."

"And if she's already talked to someone? The Galloway boy's been asking questions. I've seen him and the girl talking on the afterdeck."

"Galloway is contained. Galloway is a debt with legs, and debts can be bought." Papers rustled — the envelope being passed, Sefa guessed, or instructions written. "You'll go ashore yourself. No radio traffic, nothing in the log. This conversation never happened, and tomorrow never happened, and by the sixth tide we raise what we came for and everyone retires comfortable."

A long exhale from Rennick. Then, quieter: "Twenty years ago, Vane. I was there. I helped load the gear. I've spent two decades telling myself the hatch failure was the hatch failing."

"The hatch failed," Vane said, "because I needed it to fail. There's a difference between lying to yourself and finally understanding yourself. Take the envelope. Go to bed."

Footsteps receded. A door closed somewhere forward. The galley's small universe resumed its sounds — kettle, wind, rain — as though nothing had occurred inside it but weather.

Sefa stayed in the dark behind the crates for a long time, her breath shallow, her hands pressed flat against the cold steel of the bulkhead as if she needed proof of something solid.

He had said it aloud. *Because I needed it to fail.* Fourteen words, spoken to a witness, in a room she had heard through a wall thinner than conscience.

Her uncle had not drowned. Her uncle had been *spent* — written off the way a captain writes off damaged gear, entered in some ledger as a cost of doing business. And now the same man was pricing her grief, calculating the exact sum at which her family's dead could be purchased and silenced, and calling it generosity.

She should feel shattered. What she felt instead, rising through her like the tide coming into the loch, was something colder and far more useful.

Quiet fury has a long memory. It does not shout. It takes notes.

* * *

She waited until the watch changed at four, until Guthrie came below stamping rain from his boots, until the ship settled into its deepest hour of quiet. Then she moved.

The chart room sat amidships, its door kept unlocked because Vane trusted locks less than he trusted surveillance of habit. Sefa slipped inside and closed the door behind her, leaving the lamp off. Rain streaked the windows. Through them she could see the *Corvus* riding her anchor light a hundred yards off, and beyond her, barely, the *Kestrel*, dark and patient as a waiting animal.

The envelope was where men like Vane always put things they wanted found by exactly one person: in the chart drawer beneath the current working charts of the Meridian site, weighted under a spare dividers case. Sefa lifted it out. Heavy paper, wax-sealed, addressed in a clerk's careful hand to *J. MacAulay, Solicitor, 14 Cromwell Street, Stornoway*. But tucked against it was a second sheet — the draft of the settlement itself, and beside the blank line reserved for the signature, typed in small capitals:

**RELEASE EXECUTED BY: M. REYES (BROTHER OF THE DECEASED) AND T. REYES-FERRAN (NIECE).**

Her name. Typed there in black ink, in a document she had never seen, consenting to a lie she had never agreed to. They had spelled her name correctly. That was somehow the worst of it — that Vane's clerks knew her name, had known it all along, had filed her aboard his own fleet like a loose thread waiting to be stitched shut.

She stood in the dark chart room with the paper trembling very slightly in her hands, and she let herself feel all of it at once: the nine-year-old at the funeral with no body to bury, the teenager who'd stopped asking questions because the questions made her mother cry, the deckhand who'd signed onto Vane's fleet because the pay was good and the work was honest and she had never once suspected she was sailing on the wreck of her own family.

Then she folded the settlement draft along its original creases, slid it inside her oilskin, and put the sealed envelope back exactly where it had lain.

Let the tender go ashore tomorrow. Let the solicitor receive his letter. Let Vane believe his machinery was turning smoothly. A message intercepted was worth ten messages blocked, because a message intercepted taught you the shape of the whole network — who carried, who paid, who signed.

And there was one more thing she needed, and the chart room gave it to her: the fleet's radio schedule, pinned above the set. Two days of storm meant two days of enforced idleness, and idleness made men careless with frequencies. She memorized the channels, the scheduled check-ins with the shore agent, the emergency band. If Jude Galloway was half as serious as he seemed on the afterdeck — if the ledger in that strongbox said what his face said it said — then sooner or later they would need a way to make noise that Vane couldn't buy back.

She returned the drawer to true, checked the corridor, and went out.

In the passageway she nearly walked into him. Jude Galloway, wrapped in a blanket against the cold, standing with a mug of something steaming, looking at her with the wary intelligence of a man who also couldn't sleep and also had reasons.

"You're up late," he said.

"So are you."

His eyes went briefly to the front of her oilskin, where the folded paper made a faint rectangular shadow against the fabric. He said nothing about it. Neither did she. But something passed between them in the dimness of that rocking corridor — an acknowledgment, wordless, the way sailors acknowledge weather: *it's coming, and we'd better decide which side of it we're standing on.*

"The storm breaks tomorrow night," Sefa said quietly. "Guthrie says it's the worst of the season."

"Then we've got two days of water," Jude said, "and nothing to do but think."

"Yes," Sefa said, moving past him toward her bunk, the paper flat and secret against her heart. "Think carefully, Galloway. Some of us are done thinking alone."
