## 6. Two Days of Water

The knock came at dusk, two days out from the Meridian site, and it was not the polite knock of a man asking permission.

Jude had spent the afternoon in the survey cabin with the ledger open beneath a clipboard of tide charts, transcribing nothing, reading everything. He had learned the trick of it in the contractor trenches — how to look busy in a way that invited no questions, how to keep his eyes moving across paper while his mind moved elsewhere. But the ledger was not busywork. It was a confession written in his mother's hand, and every time he read the entry for March of twenty years ago — *Permit 4471-B, countersigned M. Vane, coordinates altered from original survey, see attached* — he felt the floor of the world tilt a few degrees, like a deck taking water.

His mother had not failed. That was the thing he kept returning to, turning it over like a stone in his pocket. All those years he had believed she'd cracked — that the death of a diver on some routine wreck had broken something in her and she'd walked away from the profession out of cowardice or grief or both, leaving him to grow up in the shadow of a name that meant *quitter* to everyone who'd ever said it. She hadn't quit. She had been pushed, and the man pushing her was two decks above him right now, drinking something amber and expensive, waiting for his fleet to bring him a fortune built on a forgery and a drowned man.

The knock came again. Harder.

Jude closed the ledger, slid it into the oilskin bag beneath his bunk, and said, "It's open."

Marcus Vane filled the doorway the way weather fills a horizon. He was not a large man, but he had the particular presence of someone who had never once been told no by the sea or by anyone standing between him and it. He wore a grey sweater that probably cost more than Jude's annual debt, and he carried two glasses, which was somehow more menacing than a weapon would have been.

"Galloway." He said the name like he was testing its weight. "Your mother's name. Did you know she and I started the same season, same year, same first survey? She found a brooch off Islay in our second month that I still think about." He set the glasses on the desk, poured two fingers of whisky into each, and pushed one across. "Sit down. This isn't an interrogation. I don't like interrogations. They make people lie badly."

Jude didn't sit. He didn't touch the glass. "You've read the manifest error report I filed."

"I've read everything you've filed. That's rather the point of you." Vane smiled, and it was almost a warm smile, which was the coldest thing about him. "You file beautifully. Your mother filed beautifully. It's in the handwriting, actually — the way you cross your sevens. I noticed it the first week."

The cabin felt smaller. Jude kept his voice level. "If you have something to say, say it."

"Permit 4471-B." Vane said it lightly, the way a man might name a horse. "You went through the strongbox before it was logged. Sloppy of the deck crew, but I understand — curiosity is a family trait. The question is what you intend to do with what you found." He picked up his own glass, turned it, watching the whisky climb the sides. "And before you perform indignation, consider that I'm not accusing you. I'm employing you. There's a difference, and the difference is money."

Jude said nothing. Outside the porthole, the grey water ran backwards past them, the fleet's three vessels holding formation through the long Atlantic swell. Somewhere below all that grey, the Meridian sat on her shelf of rock with her dead, and with a helmet whose faceplate had been cracked from a direction cracks don't come from.

"Here are the numbers," Vane went on, as if Jude had asked. "Your mother's debts — and they are creative, Galloway, I'll grant her that; she owed money to people I'd have thought were myths — total, with the collectors' interest, a figure I'll call forty thousand for the sake of round arithmetic. My offer is a hundred. A hundred thousand, wired before we surface, to whoever holds the paper on you, and the balance in an account with your name on it." He drank. "In exchange, the ledger burns. Tonight. In that sink, with that lighter, while I watch. And you forget you ever learned to cross your sevens the way she does."

The number landed in Jude's chest like a dropped anchor. A hundred thousand. He had spent three years in the trenches doing arithmetic of survival — how many metres of pipe at what rate to cover the interest, the interest on the interest, the letters that came with red borders now. Forty thousand had been the wall he couldn't see over. Vane had just offered him the sky, and the sky was priced at one fire, one small fire, in one small sink.

And that was the obscenity of it, he thought. Not the size of the number. The ease. Vane had done this arithmetic twenty years ago with a man's life and had evidently found it came out clean.

"You killed Tomas Reyes," Jude said.

Something moved behind Vane's eyes — not guilt, nothing so useful as guilt. Recalculation. "I was twenty-six years old and I countersigned a permit that moved a survey line forty metres," Vane said. "A diver went down on a wreck that permit said was ours. The sea killed him. The sea kills someone every week, and no one burns the sea." He set the glass down with a small, precise click. "What I am guilty of, if we're being Victorian about it, is ambition. Petra couldn't live with ambition. She took her ledger and her grief and she disappeared into whatever she disappeared into, and the world called it conscience, and I called it what it was — a woman who preferred being right to being rich." He smiled again. "You, I think, are more practical. You dig trenches for strangers to pay a debt that was never yours. That's not a conscience. That's a man who understands arithmetic."

Jude looked at the whisky. He thought about the debt collectors' last letter, the one that had named a date. He thought about walking into the bank in Oban with a receipt, whole, the name Galloway scrubbed back to neutral. He thought about his mother — wherever she was, if she was anywhere — and what she would say if she could see him standing in Vane's cabin weighing a dead man against a number.

He found, to his own surprise, that the answer arrived without struggle. That was the strange mercy of it. Somewhere between the trench and the strongbox, the arithmetic had changed on him, and no amount of money could price it back.

"I'll need until we make the site," Jude said. "Two days. I'll give you your answer at the site."

Vane studied him for a long moment. Jude had the sensation of being surveyed — of being a seabed, and Vane's attention the sonar sweep, reading the contours, finding the anomaly.

"Two days," Vane said at last. "You're stalling. It's competently done, so I'll respect it. But understand what stalling costs." He drained his glass and stood. "Your mother taught me that too — that everything on the water costs, and the bill always comes on the last tide."

He paused at the door, hand on the frame, and did not turn around.

"Oh — and the compass. The brass one you keep in your kit and check when you think no one's watching. The one that doesn't point north." Now he did turn, and his face was pleasant and terrible. "It points to my wreck, doesn't it? She built it to do that. I always wondered what happened to it. Bring it to the bridge. Tonight. Consider it a condition of your two days."

* * *

They came for the compass at midnight, and they were not gentle about the rest.

Jude had considered running — the oilskin bag, the ledger, the rail, a life raft, the black water. He had stood at the porthole for an hour doing the arithmetic of it: three vessels, night vision on the lead ship, water at nine degrees, land forty miles off the beam. The numbers came out the way numbers had been coming out all voyage, and he had learned what that meant. There were no good numbers left. There was only the site, and whatever he could do when he reached it.

So when the knock came — three men this time, the bosun and two of the Filipino deck crew who wouldn't meet his eyes — he handed the compass over himself. It felt like handing over a bone. The brass was warm from his pocket, and the needle inside swung in its small blind way toward a wreck forty fathoms down and two days out, faithful to a dead woman's secret and useless for anything else in the world.

"Mr. Vane says you'll have it back when the job's done," the bosun said, and there was something in his voice — not quite apology, not quite warning — that Jude filed away with the other small data points he'd been collecting: the deck crew's silence at meals, the way Sefa had looked at him across the galley the night the helmet came up, the way she'd looked away. *There are alliances shifting below decks*, his mother would have written in the ledger, in that small fierce hand. *There always are. Find them before the last tide.*

They locked him in the survey cabin — officially a "safety hold" pending a review of his manifest filings, which was Vane's kind of joke. The door had a new hasp on the outside, bright and unweathered, installed sometime in the last day while Jude had been reading. They had planned this before the confrontation. The whisky, the offer, the arithmetic — all of it theatre staged for an audience of one, with the exit bricked up in advance.

Jude sat on the bunk and listened to the ship.

That was the one thing the trenches had given him that no one could confiscate: he could hear a vessel the way other men heard music. He heard the engine note hold steady through the evening, then change at some black hour — not the surge of weather, but the deliberate, staggered rhythm of a fleet making its final run, all three ships pushing to arrive on the sixth tide with the light. He heard the bosun's boots pass his door twice, then a third time, then a pause outside that lasted four breaths and moved on. He heard, near dawn, a low exchange in Tagalog in the passageway, and one word repeated twice — *Reyes* — and then silence, and then footsteps going not toward the bridge but away from it.

Two days of water between him and the wreck. Two days of a locked door and a confiscated compass and a hundred thousand pounds sitting in some account like bait in a trap that had already sprung.

Jude lay back and stared at the overhead, at the pipes and cables running their blind routes through the ship, and he made himself do the last piece of arithmetic, the honest kind. Vane would not let him surface with what he knew. The offer had been the mercy; the hasp on the door was the truth. Whatever happened on the sixth tide, Jude Galloway was not meant to be on the deck for it.

He turned his head toward the porthole. Far off to starboard, past the black running water, a single green light rode the swell — Sefa's watch, or someone's, keeping its slow orbit at the stern rail. It vanished behind the superstructure. Appeared again. Vanished.

Like a signal. Like a door, opening and closing, opening and closing, waiting for him to decide whether he was the kind of man who watched doors or walked through them.

Jude got up off the bunk and began, quietly, to take apart the hasp's screws with the only tool they hadn't thought to take from him — the one he'd kept in his boot since the trenches, since a boy who dug for strangers learned that every lock is just a promise, and promises, on the water, are made to be broken.
