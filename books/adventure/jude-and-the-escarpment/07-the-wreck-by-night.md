## 7. The Wreck by Night

Sefa had stolen worse things than a key. She'd stolen her uncle's name back from an incident report once, copying it out in her own hand until the official version stopped being the only one that existed. So when the second mate hung the quarters key on the board by the galley and went below to count chain, she took it the way she took everything Vane's fleet thought belonged to them — quietly, and without apology.

Jude Galloway was sitting on the bunk with his back to the bulkhead when she unlocked the door. He looked up at the sound of the lock, and for a moment neither of them spoke. The lamp swung gently overhead with the swell, dragging his shadow back and forth across the cabin like something pacing.

"You're either getting me out," he said, "or you're his."

"I'm nobody's." She held up the key between two fingers, then pocketed it. "But if you're going to be his, tell me now, because I've got about forty minutes before the watch changes and I'd rather not waste them on a man who's already sold."

He stood slowly. Three days locked in a cabin the size of a coffin turned upright had bent him, but hadn't broken anything that mattered; she could see that in how he moved, testing his own weight like a man stepping onto ice.

"Vane offered me enough to erase every debt I have," Jude said. "I told him I needed to think about it. That was three days ago. He decided thinking was the same as no."

"It usually is, with him." Sefa glanced down the passageway, then back. "The fleet runs for the site tonight. Final approach. His divers go down at slack water with cutting charges — he's not raising cargo anymore, Galloway. He's gutting the wreck before anyone can look inside it properly. Before anyone finds what your mother found."

Something moved through his face — not surprise. Confirmation arriving late, wearing the expression of a man who has been carrying a weight so long he'd forgotten it wasn't normal.

"The ledger," he said.

"Is safe. Where he'll never find it." She let that sit. "But paper burns, Galloway. Paper gets lost. Paper gets *disputed*. There's one thing on that wreck that can't be disputed, and it's twenty years old and it's still down there holding its breath."

His eyes narrowed. "What are you talking about?"

"Tomas Reyes' last tank." Her voice stayed level, which cost her. It always cost her. "The one he was wearing when they pulled him off the Meridian. The incident report says equipment failure. My aunt signed it because they told her signing it was the only way to get his body home." She swallowed. "I was nine years old. I watched her sign it."

Jude looked at her for a long moment, and whatever he saw in her face made him stop asking questions and start moving.

* * *

They went over the side of the survey vessel at half past eleven, from the shadow of the tender davits where the floodlight didn't reach. The sea was black glass under a moon three days shy of full — bright enough to dive by, dim enough to hide two swimmers kicking away from a fleet of lights.

Sefa had spent the afternoon doing the small treasons: charging both sets of tanks on the quiet rack behind the compressor shed, taping a slate and a dive light to the ladder rail, noting the watch rotation the way she noted everything. The fleet lay anchored in a loose crescent a quarter mile off the site buoy, engines idling low, waiting for slack water around two in the morning. That gave them better than two hours underwater and a margin that would feel thin as paper by the end of it.

They swam out past the buoy without lights until the wreck's silhouette rose beneath them — the Meridian lying on her port side in thirty-one meters, a coastal freighter gone down in '89 with a hold full of machine parts and, according to every manifest anyone had ever produced, nothing else worth mentioning. Petra Galloway had known better. Tomas Reyes had died proving it.

At fifteen meters Sefa clicked her light on, and the beam opened the dark like a knife opening a letter.

The hull came up at them, furred with twenty years of plumose anemones, white and ghostly in the torchlight, waving in the slow pulse of the surge. Fish scattered from the beam in silver explosions. Jude swam beside her, close enough that she could see his eyes above the mask rim — wide, fixed, drinking it in. She understood. This was his mother's wreck. He was swimming through the shape of everything she'd refused to talk about.

They entered through the break in the starboard plating where the hull had torn on the way down, into the corridor that ran aft toward the engine room. Their bubbles went up ahead of them in broken strings, silvering through the beams of their lights. The interior was a cathedral of rust and rope — collapsed shelving, a tool cabinet standing absurdly upright, a coffee mug fused to a bulkhead by two decades of mineral scale. Everything wore the same shroud of fine silt that swallowed sound and motion alike.

Sefa led them aft and down, following the route from the original expedition's dive logs — logs she had read more times than the maritime authority ever had, because her aunt had kept copies, because her aunt had never believed the report she'd signed.

The engine room door was on the deck below, a watertight hatch hanging open at an angle, jammed against its frame by a bent section of ladder rail.

Not bent by the sinking. She'd known that since she was fourteen, when she'd finally asked her aunt why a ladder rail inside a compartment would deform *outward*, toward the door, as if something had been driven against it from the passage side. Her aunt had gone very quiet and very grey and said, *Some questions you inherit, Sefa. I'm sorry I gave you mine.*

Sefa put her light on the hatch now and held it there, steady, letting Jude see what she saw: the deformation, the fresh scrape-marks where salvage crews had worried at it over the years, and beneath all of it, the old score in the steel where something had been levered hard enough to bite metal into metal.

"He couldn't get out," she said. Her voice sounded strange in her own ears, flattened by the regulator, carried to Jude through the water itself as much as through the comms line they didn't have. "The report said he panicked. Got disoriented. Ran out of air trying to find his way through the ship." She shook her head, and the motion sent her light swinging across the jammed hatch. "You don't panic your way into bending a ladder rail from the wrong side."

Jude had gone still in the water. Then he eased himself down through the hatch, into the engine room, and his light swept the space — catwalks, gauges, the great dead block of the main engine furred green and white — until it found what they'd come for.

The tank lay wedged beneath the lower catwalk where the recovery divers had missed it, or been told to miss it. Aluminum, eighty-cubic, its paint long gone to bare oxide. But the valve was closed — closed *neatly*, the way a trained diver closes a valve, not the way a dying man flails — and when Sefa took it in both hands and turned it into her beam, she saw the scratches.

They covered the cylinder from shoulder to boot. Long gouges, short frantic ticks, arcs and jabs and one place near the crown where the marks went deep and repeated, over and over, a rhythm like a fist on a door.

She had prepared herself for this. She had told herself for six days, since the helmet came up in the dredge net with its cracked faceplate and Tomas Reyes' serial number, that she was ready to touch the thing that had killed him.

She was not ready. Her breath hitched loud in her own ears, and the regulator roared with it, and for a moment the whole cathedral of the wreck seemed to contract around her, silt and dark and the pressure of thirty meters pressing down like a hand.

Because the scratches were on the *inside* of the story too. Equipment failure meant a tank that failed. A tank that failed did not get shut off cleanly by a man who then had time to beat on it — not in fear, but in message. *I am here. I am alive. Someone shut this door.*

"He was alive," Jude said. His voice came through the water strangely calm, the calm of a man standing very close to a fire. "When the hatch was jammed. He was alive and he knew someone had done it, and he used the last of his air to write it on the tank because it was the only surface that would survive." He looked up at her through the murk. "Petra knew. That's why she walked away. That's why she swore off this wreck. She found this, or she found what led to it, and Vane—"

Above them, faint as a heartbeat through thirty meters of water, came a sound.

*Thud.* A pause. *Thud.*

Both their lights snapped upward at once.

Through the open hatch, through the torn plating far above, the surface of the sea glowed — a soft, shifting bloom of white light descending, growing brighter, resolving into shapes. Divers. Four of them, maybe five, coming down the shot line in a loose string, work lights blazing, and slung along their harnesses, unmistakable even at distance to anyone who'd grown up around salvage decks, the heavy canvas-wrapped shapes of cutting charges.

Vane hadn't waited for slack water. Or slack water had come early, or he simply didn't care anymore whether the fleet saw what he did. The lights sank toward the wreck's upper structure, toward the cargo hold, toward the sections of the Meridian that still held whatever Petra Galloway's compass had been pointing at for twenty years.

"They're going to cut her open tonight," Sefa said. "Before the tide turns. Before anyone official is anywhere near this water. And when they're done there won't be a hold left to search — just scrap and silt and a story about unstable wreckage."

Jude was already moving, gathering the tank against his chest, checking the straps of the mesh bag she'd brought for exactly this. His movements had changed. All the hesitation of the past week — the stalling, the weighing, the man caught between debts and truth — had burned off him like fog off morning water.

"We take it up," he said. "We take it up and we don't stop at the fleet. We go past them."

"Past them to where?"

"To whoever's listening." He kicked up through the hatch, the tank cradled like something rescued from a fire, and his light swept once more across the jammed door, the bent rail, the whole sealed argument of the engine room. "Vane thinks evidence is something you sink. He's about to learn it's something you *broadcast*."

Above them the cutting charges began to flash — dull orange pulses bleeding down through the water, each one a small thunderclap felt in the teeth rather than heard. The Meridian groaned, a long iron lament rolling through the hull and up into their bones.

Sefa followed him toward the light, the dead man's tank strapped tight between them, climbing out of the dark while the wreck that had kept its secret for twenty years began, at last, to scream.
