## 8. Sixth Tide

The water gave him up like a confession.

Jude broke the surface into the black hour before dawn, and the first thing he heard was the fleet — engines idling in a loose ring around the Meridian's buoy, deck floods cutting cones of white through the drizzle, and above it all the flat, urgent voice of Marcus Vane crackling from every radio at once. *All hands to stations. All hands.* The words came apart in the wind and reassembled, and Jude trod water between them, the evidence bag strapped tight across his chest like a second heart.

He had maybe ninety seconds before someone in the floodlights noticed the ripple where a man should not be.

The Meridian's tender was moored off her stern quarter, riding low, unattended. Jude swam for it with long, economy strokes, the cold closing over his shoulders like a hand that meant to keep him. His fingers had gone past numb an hour ago; they moved now on memory and spite. He hauled himself over the gunwale, spat seawater, and looked down at his hands shaking against the thwart — Reyes' air tank wedged under one arm, the ledger sealed in its waterproof sleeve pressed to his ribs by the strap.

Scratched from the inside. He could still feel it through the neoprene, that frantic geometry of fingernails on steel. *Alive when the hatch was jammed.* The sentence had been living in him since the bottom, growing teeth.

* * *

The tender's radio was an old set, VHF, its housing crusted with salt but its guts sound — Sefa had seen to that two nights ago, when she'd loosened every screw on this boat just enough. Jude keyed the transmit toggle and felt it click home with a satisfaction that bordered on religious.

"Mayday, mayday, mayday." His voice came out steadier than he deserved. "This is Jude Galloway, diving without permit off the wreck of the Meridian, position five-eight degrees north. I am declaring a crime scene. I say again — a crime scene. Twenty years ago a diver named Tomas Reyes was murdered on this wreck. I have his tank. I have the ledger."

Static answered him, then silence, then — worse than either — Vane's voice, smooth as bilge oil. "Unidentified vessel, you are transmitting on a restricted working channel. Cease transmission."

"I'm transmitting on *your* channel, Marcus." Jude let the name sit there, naked, in front of forty crewmen who all pretended they weren't listening. "Everyone with a handset can hear me. Everyone with a phone is recording. Petra Galloway kept a ledger. It documents salvage permits forged in your name twenty years ago — permits that made this wreck yours on paper and a graveyard in fact. And Tomas Reyes didn't drown. Listen to the tank if you don't believe me. Listen to what he scratched into it trying to get out while somebody jammed the hatch above him."

He let go of the transmit key and the channel bloomed with voices — deckhands, cooks, a mate on the far tender asking someone, twice, *is that true?* The fleet's discipline was a held breath, and Jude had just put a pin in it.

Across the water, on the bridge of the flagship, a door slammed. Figures moved behind glass. Floodlights swung, hunting, and found the empty tender a half-second after Jude slipped back over the side and submerged, because staying aboard now was dying stupidly, and he had not come this far to die stupidly.

* * *

Underwater the world went quiet in the way that always undid him — the pressure of it, the blue-black patience. Above his fins the hulls of the fleet glowed like a ceiling of dim lanterns. Below him the Meridian waited, and below her, somewhere in the silt he'd disturbed an hour ago, lay the compass.

Petra's compass. Confiscated from his cabin on the third day, logged as "ship's property," and then — Sefa had told him this in a whisper through the bulkhead, her voice shaking with something between fury and awe — carried personally by Vane onto the dive barge. Not thrown overboard. Not locked away. *Carried down.* As if Vane needed it near him. As if after twenty years the needle still frightened him.

That was why Jude had gone down a second time. Not for glory. For the compass — because it was the one thing on this ocean that pointed at the truth, and because Vane's divers, working by floodlight with cutting charges already racked on their frames, were about to turn the whole site into slag and bury the evidence under a thousand tons of collapsing steel. Scuttle the claim, scuttle the proof, scuttle the dead man twice.

The charges went off while Jude was still descending.

He felt them in his teeth first, then his chest — three flat concussions rolling through the water like the sea clearing its throat. A rain of sediment sifted down past his mask. Somewhere ahead, the Meridian groaned, a long iron lament, and a shudder ran through the dark that had nothing to do with tide.

*Get the compass. Get out.* He said it to himself the way you'd say a prayer to a god you didn't believe in but couldn't afford to insult.

* * *

The dive barge sat on the seabed like a stranded beetle, its lights still burning, abandoned in the panic after the first charge. The compass was where Sefa said it would be: clipped inside Vane's own equipment case, wrapped in a chamois cloth, treated with more tenderness than Vane had ever shown a human being. Jude took it with both hands because his fingers wouldn't close properly anymore, and the brass was warm — no, not warm, he corrected himself, *less cold*, there was no warmth left in him to be fooled — and through the cracked crystal the needle swung once, twice, and settled.

Not north. Toward the wreck. Toward the hole in the world where Tomas Reyes had spent his last hour learning exactly how much water weighs.

Then the second wave of charges fired, closer, and the Meridian began to die in earnest.

Jude had heard wrecks settle before. This was different. This was a ship being *murdered*, plates peeling, rivets letting go in stuttering volleys, and the silt around him lifting in a brown wall that erased the floodlights one by one. Visibility went from ten meters to three to nothing. His guideline — the line Sefa had rigged from the tender's anchor — was somewhere behind him, or wasn't. The current turned with the tide, right on schedule, sixth tide of six, and it shoved him sideways like a door swinging shut.

His gauge read sixty bar. Enough for ten minutes on the surface. Down here, fighting current and panic, it was a coin flip wearing a diving suit.

He swam anyway. Up, and up, and the wall of silt swallowed him and the compass knocked against his sternum and he thought — absurdly, in the last clear moment — of his mother standing in a kitchen twenty years ago saying *some things you don't bring up, Jude*, and how he'd hated her for it, and how he understood it now with his lungs full of fire: she hadn't been hiding the truth. She'd been carrying it somewhere it couldn't be sold.

His head broke surface into chaos.

* * *

Rain, floodlight, diesel smoke. The Meridian's superstructure was going down by the bow, and half the fleet's crews stood along their rails watching it happen with the expressions of men witnessing either a crime or a miracle and unable to decide which. The tender was thirty meters off — Sefa had brought it around, God bless her, running dark with its engine coughing, and she was leaning over the rail screaming his name into the wind.

Jude raised the tank above his head with the last of his arms, so the floodlights would find it, so the phones filming from every deck would see it — scratched steel catching the light like a signature — and then the current took his legs and the swell closed over him once, twice, and the third time it did not give him back.

Sixty bar became fifty. Fifty became the taste of rubber and copper. He clawed toward the tender's running light, a smeared green star in the murk, and the sea pulled at him with the patient strength of something that had already taken Reyes, already taken the Meridian, already taken his mother's whole life, and saw no reason to stop at him.

*Not tonight.*

He got a hand on the tender's anchor line. Then the other. Then Sefa's grip closed on his wrist — small, ferocious, Reyes' blood in it somewhere, though neither of them knew yet how literally — and hauled, and the world tilted, and he came over the gunwale in a rush of seawater and vomit and alive, improbably, criminally alive.

Behind them the Meridian's mast went under with a sound like a struck bell, and the fleet's radios, still open on the working channel, carried the noise to every deck, every phone, every recording device within ten miles. Vane's voice cut across it one last time, ordering the charges secured, ordering silence, ordering the impossible — and the channel answered him with static, because nobody aboard those ships was willing to be the last man loyal to him.

Sefa knelt over Jude on the deck plating, pressing the evidence bag flat against his chest as if she could keep his heart beating through sheer contact. "You got it?" she demanded. "Tell me you got it."

He opened his fist. The compass lay in his palm, streaming seawater, needle trembling — and then, slowly, as the tide finished turning beneath them, it swung wide, wavered, and settled.

North. For the first time in twenty years, north.

Jude laughed, or sobbed, or both, and pressed the little brass instrument to his forehead. But out across the water, on the flagship's bridge, a silhouette stood motionless against the floodlights — Marcus Vane, watching his fortune settle into the sea, and even at this distance Jude could see that the man was not looking at the wreck.

He was looking at the tender. At them. And he was reaching, very calmly, for the ship's phone.
