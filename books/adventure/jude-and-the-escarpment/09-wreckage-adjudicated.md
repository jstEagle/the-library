## 9. Wreckage, Adjudicated

The hearing room in Stornoway smelled of wet wool and old varnish, and Sefa Reyes sat in the third row with her hands folded like a woman at a funeral, which in every way that mattered she was.

The Maritime Adjudication Board had given the case a number — MA-2214, *In re Salvage Vessel Meridian Claim* — and numbers, Sefa had learned, were how institutions buried things politely. Her uncle had been a number once. Incident Report 88-114, *fatality, diving operations, cause: equipment failure*. Twenty years of that line sitting in a filing cabinet on the mainland while Tomas Reyes rotted into a footnote. She had come ashore to make sure the number got a name this time, or to burn the building down trying.

Jude Galloway sat two rows ahead of her, still salt-crusted at the seams, his hands wrapped around a paper cup of tea he never drank. He hadn't spoken much since the sixth tide. She'd watched him surface that morning with the compass strapped inside his jacket like a second heartbeat, watched him cough half the Hebrides out of his lungs on the deck of the *Corvid*, and when he could finally stand he hadn't celebrated. He'd just looked back down at the water where the charges had torn the old hull open, and his face had been the face of a man who had paid for something and wasn't sure yet what he'd bought.

Now the board's chairman, a dry stick of a man named Callum Frazer, read the findings aloud in a voice like gravel poured slowly.

"—the salvage permits held by Vane Marine Recovery, reference VMR-77 through VMR-81, are hereby declared void ab initio. The signature of authorisation attributed to Inspector D. Halloran has been determined fraudulent. The original documentation, recovered from the wreck site aboard the vessel *Meridian*, bears handwriting analysis consistent with one Marcus A. Vane, then aged twenty-four—"

Sefa let the words wash over her without drinking them. Fraud was clean. Fraud was paperwork. Fraud was the kind of crime that came with a fine and a forfeiture order and a paragraph in the shipping gazette. What fraud did not cover — what no one in this room would say out loud — was the tank.

She had seen it herself, before they brought it up. The final air tank from the dive station, fished off the seabed by Jude's hands in the dark, its valve housing scored with long parallel scratches. Scratches made from the inside of the jammed hatch. Scratches made by a man who was alive, who had air, who had spent his last hour clawing at metal while someone above him decided he was worth more dead than rescued. The metallurgist's report called it *post-mortem tooling consistent with entrapment*. Sefa called it what it was. But there was no statute for it anymore — not after twenty years, not with the man who'd jammed the hatch dead himself, lost in the same storm that took Tomas, and not with Marcus Vane's lawyers already circling the word *proximate* like gulls over a fish gut pile.

* * *

Vane arrived on the third day, and he arrived the way weather arrives — inevitable, grey, and impossible to argue with.

He wore a good coat. That was the thing Sefa noticed first, the thing that made her jaw ache from clenching. After everything — after the fleet impounded at anchor under court seal, after the *Corvid's* crew giving statements until their voices went hoarse — the man wore a good coat and sat down at the respondent's table and folded his hands as though he were there to discuss mooring fees.

His counsel was a silver-haired woman named Ingrid Solheim, and she was very good. Sefa hated how good she was. She didn't deny the ledger. She didn't deny the forged permits. She walked the board through them almost tenderly, a tour guide of her own client's ruin, and then she pivoted.

"My client acknowledges the documentary irregularities," Solheim said, "and does not contest the forfeiture of the salvage claim or the vessels. What my client contests is characterisation. Mr. Vane was twenty-four years old. The permit forgery was an act of desperate ambition committed under the direction of senior partners since deceased. As to the death of Mr. Tomas Reyes—" and here her voice dropped into something almost gentle, "—the incident occurred during a force-nine storm. Equipment failure. The original inquiry found no evidence of foul play, and nothing recovered from the site alters that finding. Scratches on a tank valve prove a man struggled. They do not prove anyone helped him drown."

Sefa stood up before she knew she was standing. The room turned. Frazer's gavel hovered.

"Twenty minutes," she said. Her voice cracked and she hated that too, but she kept going. "He had twenty minutes of air left when the hatch jammed. We know because the gauge was recovered intact. He wasn't struggling because he was panicked. He was struggling because someone welded the dogging clamp shut, and the weld was fresh, and your own survey photographs show it." She pointed at the evidence table, at the plastic-sleeved photographs Jude had risked his life for. "You want to call it equipment failure? Fine. Then tell me whose equipment. Tell me whose hands."

"Sit down, Ms. Reyes," Frazer said, not unkindly.

She sat. But she saw it land — saw it move through the room like a current, the board members exchanging looks, Solheim's pen pausing for half a second too long. It wouldn't be enough. She already knew it wouldn't be enough. But it would be *on the record*, in the transcript, in the permanent file, and some days that was the only kind of justice the sea ever paid out.

In the end, the board gave exactly what Sefa had predicted it would give. The permits: void. The fleet: impounded, four vessels, seized against forfeited bond. The criminal referrals: manslaughter — *declined*, Crown Office citing insufficient evidence of identification and the intervening deaths of all principal actors. Vane himself: walking out of the building on a technicality so thin you could read a newspaper through it — the acts charged fell outside the limitation period, and the man who'd done the welding couldn't be prosecuted posthumously, and Marcus Vane, it turned out, had only ever signed papers. Papers didn't drown people. Hands drowned people, and the hands were at the bottom of the Minch.

Sefa watched him leave. At the door, Vane paused, and for one moment his eyes found hers across the room — not triumphant, not sorry. Something worse than either. *Acknowledging.* As if to say: *you know what I am, and I know you know, and neither of us can do a thing about it.*

Then the door closed, and Stornoway's rain took him.

* * *

The journalist was waiting outside, a wiry mainland woman named Aoife Bran with a recorder already running, and Sefa almost walked past her. She was tired of talking. Tired of rooms.

"You're Tomas Reyes' niece," Bran said. Not a question. "The incident report lists him as 'T. Reyes, contracted diver.' No first name in the finding. No photograph. Twenty years, and the official record still doesn't say who he was."

Sefa stopped. The rain came down fine and cold between them.

"What do you want?" she said.

"A correction," Bran said. "Not mine. Yours. If you give me the name, on record, I'll run it above the fold. *Tomas Elias Reyes, diver, murdered.* Not 'died.' Not 'lost.' Murdered — your word, your name attached. The lawyers will hate it. Vane will hate it. It won't convict anyone." She shrugged, rain dripping off her notebook. "But records are strange things. They outlast technicalities."

Sefa thought about her uncle's hands. He'd taught her to tie a bowline when she was six, standing on the dock at Mallaig, his big fingers guiding hers through the rabbit-and-the-hole. He'd laughed when she got it wrong the fourth time. She could not remember his face perfectly anymore — twenty years will do that — but she remembered the hands, and the laugh, and the way he'd said *a knot either holds or it doesn't, cariño, there's no in-between.*

There was no in-between.

"Tomas Elias Reyes," she said to the recorder. "Born Oban, 1951. Diver. My uncle. And he was murdered — I'm saying that word, and I'll sign whatever you need me to sign. Put my name next to his. Let them come find me if they don't like it."

Bran wrote it down longhand even though the recorder was running, the way old reporters do, as if ink made it more true. Above them the gulls wheeled over the harbour where Vane's fleet sat dark and sealed, navy tape fluttering on the rails, the *Corvid* riding low and confiscated beside ships that would never sail for him again.

* * *

That evening Sefa walked out to the point past the harbour wall, where the water goes from green to iron, and stood a long time doing nothing. Jude Galloway found her there eventually — she heard his boots on the shingle before she saw him, and didn't turn around.

"They're saying he'll appeal the forfeiture," Jude said. "Solheim filed notice within the hour. He'll get some of the fleet back. Maybe most of it."

"He'll get boats," Sefa said. "Boats aren't the point."

"No," Jude agreed. "They're not."

They stood together in the wind. Down the coast, the buoy marking the Meridian site rocked on the sixth tide's leftover swell, and beneath it, forty fathoms down, the old ship lay open to the sea at last — her holds breached by Vane's own charges, her dead finally unguarded, the hatch with its welded clamp rusting in full view of whatever God watched over the Minch. They'd never raise her now. The board had designated the site a protected maritime grave pending review, which meant, in practice, forever. Sefa found she was glad. Some things should keep their dead.

"My mother used to say the sea keeps better books than any court," Jude said quietly. "I thought she was being bitter." He looked out at the water. "She was being precise."

Sefa almost smiled. Almost.

"Bran's piece runs tomorrow," she said. "Front page. His name, my name, the word *murdered* in print where no lawyer can redact it." She turned to look at Jude at last. "It's not enough. You understand that. He walks. He keeps his money, most of it. Twenty years from now he'll be some grand old man of the industry telling stories at dinners."

"I know."

"And you're all right with that?"

Jude was quiet for a moment. Then he reached into his jacket and brought out the compass — brass worn to gold at the edges, dented, alive — and held it flat on his palm between them. The needle swung, trembled, settled. Pointing, Sefa noticed, not north. Not toward the wreck. Somewhere inland, steady as a heartbeat, toward the east and the hills beyond the island.

"No," Jude said. "But I'm done letting him decide what enough looks like." He closed his hand around the compass. "I've got somewhere to be. There's a cottage on Skye, and a letter I haven't opened yet, and a debt I intend to pay with his bond money just to spite him."

He started up the shingle, then paused.

"Sefa. When it runs tomorrow — when your uncle's name is in print — I hope it does something. I do. But if it doesn't fix anything—" he glanced back, rain flattening his hair, "—know that it fixed one thing. Everyone who reads it will know he fought. Twenty minutes, scratching at the metal. Nobody drowns quiet. Not him."

He walked on toward the ferry landing, getting smaller against the grey. Sefa stayed at the point until the light went, and when she finally turned back toward the town, the harbour lights were coming on one by one across the black water — and below them, sealed and silent, the fleet that had been built on a lie rocked gently at anchor, waiting for courts, while the real verdict lay forty fathoms down with a man named Tomas Elias Reyes, who had held, in the end, the way a knot holds or doesn't.

There was no in-between.
