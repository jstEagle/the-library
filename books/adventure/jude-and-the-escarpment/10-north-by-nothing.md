## 10. North by Nothing

The cottage sat at the end of a track the council had stopped maintaining years ago, and Jude walked the last half mile because the hire car couldn't. Gorse had taken the verges whole, yellow and furious in the late light, and beyond it the sea ran grey-green against black rock with the patient violence of something that had never once lost an argument. He had grown up on this coast. He had forgotten how loud it was.

The door wasn't locked. It hadn't been locked in twenty years; Petra used to say locks were for people who believed their things were worth more than their neighbours' need. He pushed it open into air that smelled of peat smoke gone cold, of dust and old paper and, beneath it all, faintly, of her — the lanolin-and-ink smell he'd carried out of childhood like a splinter he'd never worked free.

She had died in February. The solicitor's letter had reached him in March, forwarded twice, arriving like everything else from that side of his life: late, water-damaged, impossible to ignore. *The estate is small,* it said. *There is a cottage. There are debts.* As if those two sentences could hold a whole estrangement between them.

He stood in the doorway a long time before going in.

* * *

Inside, the cottage kept her order. Charts rolled and banded on the shelf above the hearth. Boots by the door, worn through at the heel, waiting as if she'd only stepped out to check the tide. On the table under the window, a single lamp with a green glass shade, and beside it — he stopped breathing for a moment — the strongbox, empty now, its lid up, the ledger gone where he'd sent it: to the maritime court at Inverness, into the hands of a journalist named Ailsa Craig who wrote like a scalpel and asked questions like one too.

Six weeks ago he had owed men with flat voices forty-three thousand pounds. Six weeks ago he had signed onto Marcus Vane's fleet because the debt collectors gave him six weeks and the Meridian job paid enough to make the arithmetic almost work. Now Vane's permits were ash, his flagship impounded at Stornoway, his name dragged through the shipping pages in type usually reserved for disasters. And Jude still owed the money — less than before, but real, and real debts do not care whose villain fed you.

But there was the bond.

Vane had posted a salvage bond — standard practice, insurance against exactly the kind of fraud he'd committed. When the adjudication came down, the bond forfeited to the claimants. Sefa had fought for that word, *claimants*, in three hearings, standing in a corridor outside Ullapool with Reyes' incident report folded in her coat pocket like a weapon. Tomas Reyes had no grave. His family had twenty years of silence bought with hush money that had never quite silenced them. The bond would not bring him back. But part of it, the tribunal ruled, flowed to those damaged by the fraudulent claim — and Petra Galloway's estate, defrauded first and longest, stood at the head of that list.

Which meant, in the strangest inversion of his whole life, that Marcus Vane was about to pay off Jude's mother's debts.

Jude sat down at her table and laughed until it hurt, and then it hurt, and then he let it.

* * *

The compass had come back to him the way nothing else in this story had come back clean.

Vane had confiscated it aboard the *Meridian Star*, locked it in the chartroom safe with the arrogance of a man who believed other people's inheritances were inventory. When the fleet was seized, the safe was opened by a customs officer with a clipboard, and the brass compass — dented, salt-bloomed, its glass clouded at the edges — went into an evidence bag with a number on it. Sefa had testified it was his. She'd stood in that room and said, *It belonged to Petra Galloway, and it belongs to her son,* and the officer had looked at her, and at Jude's split lip and salt-stiff clothes, and handed it over without a word.

For six days it had sat in his jacket pocket, dead weight, pointing nowhere honest. For six days he hadn't opened it. He told himself it was because of the hearings, the statements, the endless small machinery of vindication. That was a lie. He hadn't opened it because the last time he'd held it, he'd been treading water in the sixth tide with his lungs full of fire and Vane's charges cracking the hull below him, and the compass had been the only thing between him and joining Tomas Reyes in the dark — and some part of him believed, superstitiously, completely, that if he opened it now, on dry land, in his mother's kitchen, it would finally tell him what it had been trying to say all along.

On the seventh evening, with the money transferred and the debt collectors paid and the number zero sitting in his account like a held breath released, he set the compass on the green-shaded lamp's circle of light and opened the housing.

It was meant to be simple. A bezel, a card, a pivot. But the card wouldn't sit level. It rocked, a hair's breadth, a hesitation — and Jude, who had spent six weeks learning to read hesitation in men's faces, reached for the fine screwdriver in Petra's tool drawer without deciding to.

The retaining ring came away. The card lifted free on its pivot, and beneath it, where a manufacturer would seal wax or nothing at all, lay a fold of oilcloth, tied with thread, thin as a wafer. Twenty years it had lain there, under the needle, under every false north the compass had ever shown. He understood then why it drifted — why it had pulled toward the Meridian across two hundred miles of open water. Not magnetism. Weight. A letter, folded small, shifting the balance of the card the way grief shifts the balance of a life.

His hands were steady until they weren't.

* * *

The paper was ship's log stock, the kind that survives salt. Her handwriting had changed in twenty years — harder now, the loops burned off it, a hand that had learned to write under scrutiny — but he knew it the way he knew the sound of this sea.

*Jude.*

*If you're reading this, you found the compass, which means you found the wreck, which means Vane found you, which means I'm sorry. I'm sorry four times over, and each apology is true, so read them all.*

*I'm sorry I left. You were nineteen and furious and I let you believe the worst version because the truth required me to say aloud that I watched a man die and did nothing. Tomas Reyes went down on the Meridian because the hatch was jammed — jammed from outside, jammed on orders, orders I heard given and did not stop. I was thirty-one and afraid of a man named Marcus Vane, and my fear cost a family their son. When I surfaced and said nothing, I stopped being an archaeologist. Everything after that — the silence, the exile, the years of letting you think I chose ambition over you — was penance, and penance is just cowardice wearing better clothes.*

*I didn't take the compass when I left because I couldn't carry it. It pointed at the truth and I couldn't bear to watch it do it. So I hid the truth inside it, where only someone looking for north would ever find it, and I waited twenty years for someone brave enough to go looking. I hoped it wouldn't be you. I prayed it would be you. Both prayers at once — that's what love does to a person.*

*The ledger is yours. The truth is yours. Do with them what I could not. And Jude — the fortune you were chasing was never in the wreck. It was in the walking away. I know you'll find that out the hard way, because you're mine.*

*Point yourself north. Whatever else happens, point yourself north.*

*Your mother,*
*Petra*

He read it three times. The third time, his vision blurred so badly the words swam like fish, and he set it down on her table, in her lamplight, in her house, and put his face in his hands and wept the way he hadn't wept at the funeral, hadn't wept in the water, hadn't wept when Sefa took his arm on the courthouse steps — great ugly sobs that scattered into the cold cottage air and came back at him off the stone walls like the sea coming back at the rocks, patient, certain, returning everything eventually.

When it was done, the silence felt different. Occupied. Forgiveness, he thought, isn't a thing you're given. It's a thing you find hidden in the mechanism, under the needle, where only the looking can reach it.

* * *

He stayed nine days.

He repaired the gate hinge. He burned the gorse back from the track. He read her charts at the kitchen table in the evenings and found, pencilled faintly in the margin of the Minch survey, a note in her young hand — *T.R. was better than any of us* — and he left it there, sacred, unamended.

The money he handled the way she'd have wanted, which took him three days to work out and one phone call to confirm. The debt: paid, in full, from Vane's forfeited bond, every pound of it laundered through the tribunal's ruling until it came to him clean as rain. What remained — eleven thousand, four hundred pounds — he divided in an afternoon. Two thousand to the Reyes family fund that Sefa administered with a fierceness that made lawyers flinch. Two thousand to the coastal rescue station at Ullapool, whose boat had plucked him from the sixth tide. The rest to a diving scholarship in Tomas Reyes' name, administered through the university Petra had once been expelled from defending, with a letter that said only: *He was better than any of us.*

Sefa came up on the ninth day, driving a borrowed van full of her own belongings and no explanation of why. She stood in the doorway surveying the cottage the way she'd surveyed the Meridian's deck — reading it, claiming it.

"Rent-free," Jude said. "In exchange for keeping me honest."

"Honest," she said, testing the word. "That's a full-time post."

"I've seen your work ethic."

She almost smiled. Behind her, the sea kept up its long argument with the shore, and the gorse burned yellow in the last of the light, and somewhere down the coast a rescue boat ran its engine warm against the evening.

That night, alone at the table, Jude took up the compass one final time. He seated the card back on its pivot, light now, balanced, the letter moved to the tin box where Petra kept her mother's rings. He held the brass in both palms, feeling the cold of it, the history of it, the weight of everything it had carried and everything it had refused to say.

He set it on the table and stepped back.

The needle swung — a slow, wide arc, shedding twenty years of drift — and settled.

North. Only north. Steady as a heartbeat, pointing out past the gorse and the black rocks and the grey-green water, toward nothing buried, nothing owed, nothing drowned.

Jude watched it for a long time. Then he turned off the lamp, and in the dark the little glow of the compass card held like a star, and outside the tide turned, and turned, and turned.
