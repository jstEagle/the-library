## 1. The Weight of a Compass

The pawnbroker's name was Mr. Chai, and he had the hands of a man who had weighed ten thousand lives and found most of them wanting.

Petra laid her gear out on his glass counter in the order she'd decided on the walk over, which was the order of what hurt least to lose. Sleeping bag. Stove. The titanium pot set she'd carried through three ranges and two expulsions. A rope she trusted more than most people, sixty meters of it, still supple, still honest.

Mr. Chai picked up each item, turned it over, and named a price in the flat tone of a man reading a weather report for somewhere else.

"Two hundred," he said, for the lot.

"It's worth eight."

"It's worth what I give you." He set down the stove and looked at her over his spectacles. "You want more, go to Bangkok. You have time to go to Bangkok?"

She didn't. That was the whole architecture of her life these days: no time, no margin, no slack anywhere in the line. The debt to Vuthy was eleven days past due, and Vuthy was not a man who charged interest so much as he collected other things — favors, information, the occasional dignity. She'd seen what happened to people who let him collect long enough. They became furniture in his operation. Useful, quiet, owned.

"Three hundred," Petra said.

"Two-fifty." He was already sweeping the gear into a canvas sack behind the counter, which meant the negotiation had been theater and they'd both known it. "Passport too?"

"No."

He glanced up at that. Everyone in this town knew what she was. The farang woman who'd come off the river three weeks ago with a duffel bag and nothing else, who sat in the Night Market drinking one beer for two hours, who asked questions about work the way hungry people ask about food — carefully, without hope. Blacklisted from Chile. Blacklisted from Nepal. The story had traveled faster than she had, the way stories do, shedding nuance at every border.

"Everyone has a price for the passport eventually," Mr. Chai said, not unkindly.

"Not that one."

It wasn't the passport he meant and they both knew it. Her father's compass sat in her chest pocket, brass-cased, lid scarred where a rock had kissed it on the Cordillera Huayhuash in 1991, back when her father was alive and the world still made a certain kind of sense. It weighed almost nothing. It weighed everything. There were nights in the last three weeks when she'd lain awake doing arithmetic with it — what a pawnshop would give her for it, what a collector might pay, what it would mean to be a person who had sold the last thing her father ever put in her hands.

The answer to the last question was: nothing. It would mean nothing. It would mean she was the kind of person who arrived at the end of herself and kept selling.

"Two-fifty," she said. "And you hold the gear thirty days. If I come back with cash, I buy it all back at the same rate."

"Thirty days." Mr. Chai wrote the ticket in careful script. "You are optimistic."

"I'm desperate. Optimism is just desperation wearing better shoes."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth that might have been respect, or indigestion. He counted out the bills — worn notes, mostly hundreds, smelling faintly of someone else's sweat — and slid them across the glass.

Outside, the border town was doing its afternoon impression of a place with a future. Motorbikes buzzed across the friendship bridge like iron mosquitoes. Cargo barges idled against the current, stacked with teak and gravel and things nobody declared. Down the strip of guesthouses and noodle shops, a tuk-tuk driver spat red betel juice and called out to her in three languages before accepting that she wasn't buying anything today.

The hostel was called the Golden Lotus, which was optimistic naming, since the lotus motif extended to a faded wall decal and nothing else. Her room was four dollars a night and came with a fan that worked on two speeds: off, and haunted. She climbed the stairs two at a time out of habit, though there was nothing upstairs worth hurrying to except the arithmetic waiting on her bunk.

Vuthy's debt: four thousand US, plus the eleven days of whatever Vuthy decided those days were worth. The pawn money: two hundred fifty. The gap between those numbers was a canyon, and at the bottom of the canyon was the thing she refused to look at directly, which was the family in Denver — the widow and the son of Daniel Marsh, the client who'd died on her rope in the Andes six years ago. The debt to Vuthy was business. The debt to the Marshes was the kind that didn't appear on any ledger, the kind that had followed her through two blacklists and half a hemisphere, whispering the same sentence every time she closed her eyes: *You were the guide. It was your call.*

She counted the money twice, then a third time, as if the third count might change the total. Two hundred fifty. Against four thousand. She'd been a mountain guide for fifteen years; she could read terrain, weather, rockfall, human fear. None of those skills priced out at anything in this town.

The knock came at seven.

She knew who it was before she opened the door, because nobody else knocked on her door. Vuthy's men didn't knock; they simply appeared in doorways or beside motorbikes, materializing with the patience of people paid by the hour. Vuthy himself knocked. He considered courtesy an investment.

"Miss Lockridge." He stood in the corridor in a linen shirt too clean for the climate, holding a paper bag of mango sticky rice as if it were a diplomatic gift. "You eat? You look like you don't eat."

"I ate."

"You look like the food was theoretical." He stepped past her into the room without being invited, which was also part of the investment, and settled onto the one chair like a man visiting property he technically owned. He set the rice on the desk between them. "Eleven days."

"I know."

"Do you?" He opened the paper bag, released the smell of coconut and warm sugar into the room, and did not offer her any. "I wonder. Because a person who knew would have found four thousand dollars in eleven days, and instead I hear you sold a sleeping bag." He smiled. He had a good smile, wide and gold-capped on one side, deployed the way other men deployed handshakes. "Chai tells me things. Chai tells everyone things. It is why I use him."

"I'll have your money."

"When?"

"Soon."

"Soon." He tasted the word like the sticky rice, chewing it slowly. "You know what soon means in my business? Soon means never, wearing a watch." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and for a moment the showman's polish dropped and she saw the ledger underneath, cold and balanced. "I like you, Miss Lockridge. This is true. You are competent, you are discreet, and you are broke, which makes you honest in a way rich people never manage. So I am going to do something I almost never do. I'm going to give you a way to pay me that isn't selling your organs or your father's compass."

Her hand went to her chest pocket before she could stop it. Vuthy's eyes tracked the movement and filed it away; she watched him file it, watched the small click of information being stored for later use, and hated him a little for how good he was.

"There's a job," he said.

"I'm blacklisted in this country too if anyone checks too hard. You know that."

"I know many things. That is my profession." He produced a folded newspaper page from inside his jacket — a Thai-language broadsheet, three weeks old — and smoothed it on the desk. He tapped a small item halfway down the page. "Read it. Or I can summarize. A government survey team, six people, went into the Khao Sen ranges five weeks ago to audit mining concessions along the upper river. They did not come out. Search parties found their camp intact. Tents pitched, food cached, radios silent. No bodies. No tracks leaving." He looked up. "No tracks, Miss Lockridge. Six adults do not walk out of karst country without leaving tracks. Not even in rain."

Petra said nothing. But something in her chest had already shifted weight, the way a snowpack shifts before it moves — a settling, a listening.

"The government search was called off after nine days," Vuthy continued. "Officially: presumed lost, monsoon approaching, tragic. Unofficially—" he spread his hands, "—nobody official wants to say out loud what everybody unofficial knows, which is that teams do not vanish. Teams are vanished. And there are parties who very much want to know which, and who, and where."

"What parties?"

"The paying kind." He stood, brushed invisible dust from his trousers, and named a number.

She made him repeat it. Not because she hadn't heard it — because hearing it twice was the only way to make sure her face didn't do anything stupid. Four thousand. Exactly the debt. Which meant either coincidence, which she didn't believe in, or Vuthy had known the size of her hole before he'd walked up the stairs, and had priced the ladder accordingly.

"That's not a fee," she said. "That's a leash."

"It's both. Most useful things are." He retrieved the newspaper, refolded it along its old creases. "You find the team, or what's left of them, or what happened to them. You bring me a report I can sell. You get four thousand, your debt dies, and Chai holds your gear until you're ready. Thirty days." He paused at the door, and the gold tooth caught the light of the bare bulb. "One condition. You leave tomorrow, before the rain. And Miss Lockridge — the last man I sent up that river came back with nothing and a story he wouldn't repeat. Whatever is up there, it isn't lost. Lost things can be found. This is something else."

The door clicked shut behind him.

Petra sat on the bunk for a long time. The fan turned its haunted circles overhead. The mango sticky rice sat untouched on the desk, going cool, sweet-smelling, patient — a gift she hadn't earned and wouldn't take, because Vuthy's gifts were always invoices.

Four thousand dollars. Her father's compass pressed against her sternum, light as a lie. Somewhere north, in country she didn't know yet, six people had stepped out of the world without disturbing it, and the first honest question — the one she was already asking, the one that had gotten her blacklisted twice, because she'd never learned to stop asking it — was not *where did they go* but *who needed them gone*.

She took out the compass anyway. Opened the lid. The needle swung, wobbled, and settled north with the calm of a thing that had never once lied to her.

"Okay, Dad," she said to the empty room. "One more river."

She started packing what was left to pack, which was almost nothing, and outside the first fat drops of the early monsoon began ticking against the tin roof like a countdown.

* * *

She was on the water before dawn.
