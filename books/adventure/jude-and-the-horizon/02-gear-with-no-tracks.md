## 2. Gear With No Tracks

The trail died two days before the camp did.

Petra knew it the way she knew weather coming — not by seeing it but by feeling the absence of it. For six hours she'd followed a survey team's spoor up the eastern fork of the Nam Kha: boot prints in the clay, machete blazes on the soft-barked trees, a cigarette butt here, a torn corner of ration packaging there. Seven people moving at survey pace, unhurried, well-fed, doing their jobs. Then, somewhere below the second waterfall, the prints simply stopped — as if seven adults had walked into the river and evaporated.

She'd told herself it was the rain. A squall had come through the night before, heavy enough to scrub softer ground bare. It happened. She'd seen it happen.

But the prints didn't fade gradually, the way rain-erased tracks did. They ended clean, mid-stride, on a stretch of exposed clay that the squall would have had to hit like a knife blade and miss everywhere else. And the machete blazes kept going.

She'd stood over that last print for a long time, letting the forest breathe around her, and made herself say the thing her gut was already saying: *This isn't where they vanished. This is where someone wanted me to think they vanished.*

Then she'd kept walking, because the contract said find them, and because Vuthy's money was real, and because turning around meant going back to the hostel in Ban Sok with nothing but a pawn ticket and a compass.

* * *

She smelled the camp before she saw it — woodsmoke gone cold and sour, wet canvas, the faint chemical bite of kerosene. Then the trees opened onto a shelf of flat ground above the river bend, and there it was.

Two dome tents, guyed out properly, flysheets tensioned against rain that hadn't come yet. A cook tarp strung between two saplings. A fire pit ringed with stones, ash still holding the shape of last night's logs. Gear laid out with the tidy discipline of people who expected to come back to it: dry bags stacked under the tarp's drip line, boots paired outside the tent doors, a mess kit drying on a flat rock.

Petra stood at the tree line for a full minute and didn't move.

Twenty years of mountains had taught her that camps talked. A camp was a sentence written in gear, and you learned to read it or you buried clients. This one was saying all the right things — *we stepped out for a morning survey, we'll be back by dark* — and that was exactly what bothered her. Camps left behind in emergencies never looked like this. People who fled at night took their boots. People who ran from something knocked over the water pots, scattered the food, grabbed whatever was closest. Panic was messy. Even discipline broke along specific seams.

Nobody — not one person out of seven — had taken their boots.

She walked the perimeter first, slow, hands loose, reading the ground the way other people read documents. The camp sat on a bench of compacted earth above the high-water mark, and the monsoon hadn't reached this elevation yet, which meant the surface held everything. She circled it twice.

No footprints leaving.

There were prints arriving — dozens of them, overlapping, weeks old by the weathering, a working camp's worth of coming and going. There were prints around the fire, around the latrine trench fifty meters east, down to the water point at the river. All of it consistent, all of it organic, all of it ending in a rough circle maybe thirty meters across.

Beyond that circle, the forest floor was unbroken. Leaf litter lay undisturbed in every direction except the trail she'd come in on. No exit path worn toward the survey grids she'd seen marked on the map. No prints heading downstream, upstream, uphill. Seven people had arrived at this camp over the course of days, and then, apparently, sunk into the earth.

Petra crouched at the edge of the traffic zone and touched the litter. Dry on top, damp underneath, no compression lines crossing it. She worked her way around the whole perimeter on her knees in places, checking the way a tracker checks — not looking for tracks but proving their absence, which was harder and more honest work.

At the north end, where the ground dropped to the river, she found the only anomaly: a patch of litter that looked *almost* right. Almost. The leaves were the right species, the right density, laid at the right angle. But they'd been laid. She could see it once she let herself see it — a hand-width strip where the overlap pattern ran against the slope, leaves resting *on* the stems of neighboring leaves instead of tucked beneath them. Rain does a lot of things. Rain doesn't re-stack leaf litter against gravity.

Someone had brushed out a path here. Recently enough that the leaves hadn't fully settled into their natural lie.

She sat back on her heels and let the suspicion finish forming, cold and clean, the way ice forms on a canteen — slowly, then all at once.

*They walked out this way,* she thought. *Or they were carried. And someone swept the ground behind them.*

* * *

By late afternoon she'd turned the camp inside out, and it kept telling her the same beautiful lie.

The food cache was the worst of it. Under a ground sheet weighted with stones: rice in sealed drums, oil, dried fish, a case of condensed milk, noodles, sugar, salt. Enough for seven people for three weeks. She counted it twice. Nothing missing. Not even opened. You don't abandon three weeks of food unless you're leaving in a hurry — and hurried people leave evidence of the hurry. There was none. The drums were arranged in date-order, oldest on top, the way a careful quartermaster stacks them for a rotation that never happened.

The tents were worse. She crawled into both. Sleeping pads rolled. Sleeping bags stuffed into their compression sacks. One mosquito net folded into a neat square. On the floor of the larger tent, a paperback novel lay face-down, open, spine cracked at page 212 — someone's bookmark made of a moment, mid-read. Beside it, a pair of reading glasses folded closed.

You don't fold your glasses closed if you're being dragged out of your tent at gunpoint. You don't stop reading at a chapter break either. Page 212 ended on a scene break; she checked. Whoever had put that book down had done it the way you do when you're stepping out for five minutes.

The radios were the part that turned unease into something with edges.

There were two — a base unit under the cook tarp and a handheld in a dry bag. Both dead, but not broken-dead. She pulled the battery from the handheld and tested the contacts: corroded, deliberately, a thin film of something applied to the terminals. She scraped it with her thumbnail and sniffed. Petroleum jelly mixed with something saline. Saltwater paste eats contacts in days. Someone had killed these radios on purpose, gently, so they'd look like equipment failure rather than sabotage.

The base unit's antenna was still extended. Its channel dial was set to the provincial emergency frequency — the correct one, the one any competent team lead would monitor. It had been set there to be found that way.

Petra sat back on the cook tarp's bench and ate half a ration bar without tasting it, running the arithmetic she didn't want to run.

A team of seven vanishes. Their government employer waits eleven days — she'd checked the timeline Vuthy gave her — before anyone comes looking, and then doesn't send police or soldiers, sends a blacklisted freelance guide through a fixer. The camp is intact, provisioned, orderly. The radios are sabotaged to look dead. The ground is swept. The story writes itself: flash flood, or landslide, or the forest just swallowed them, tragic, regrettable, close the file.

Except floods take gear. Landslides bury it. Forests don't fold eyeglasses.

*This isn't a disappearance,* she thought. *This is a set dressing. Somebody built a stage and walked the cast off it.*

The question that mattered wasn't *where did they go.* People who leave camps without boots go where they're told, and people are told things at knifepoint or gunpoint, and neither leaves much sign on hard ground. The question was *who benefits.* Seven surveyors don't vanish for free. Somewhere downstream of this camp there was an accounting, and somewhere above it there was an author.

She'd met authors before. In the Andes, the author had been weather — indifferent, enormous, blameless. She'd spent four years paying for a storm as if storms could be billed. But this — this had fingerprints. Human scale. Which meant human accountability, and human accountability was a currency she'd learned to distrust, because everyone she'd ever watched spend it had spent someone else's life to do it.

*Not your problem,* said the voice that sounded like Vuthy, smooth as bank paper. *Find the team, collect the fee, clear the debt. What happened here happened before you got here.*

But another voice, older, her father's, said nothing at all — just turned the compass over in its case, the way he used to when a route didn't read right. He'd taught her that. When the map and the ground disagree, believe the ground. And the ground here was lying to her, which meant somebody had made it lie, which meant—

Which meant she was already in it. That was the thing she couldn't dodge. You didn't get to discover a staged crime scene and then un-discover it. Whatever came next started the moment she'd knelt in the leaf litter and known.

* * *

Dusk came down through the canopy in layers, green to grey to black, and Petra made camp forty meters from the survey camp because she didn't trust it. Superstition, maybe. Or the simpler animal logic that said *the people who staged this might come back to check their work.*

She built a small fire in a hollow she dug herself, low-flame, screened. Boiled water. While it cooled she went back one more time, headlamp off, moving by feel and moonlight, because darkness tells different truths than daylight and she wanted both testimonies.

That was when she found the third thing.

It was under the base radio, taped to the underside of the field table — a flat packet wrapped in plastic and duct tape, positioned so that only someone crawling under the table in the dark would find it. Her first thought was *cache*, her second was *trap*, and she handled it accordingly, fingertips only, turning it in the moonlight.

Inside the plastic: a notebook. Cheap school exercise book, water-swollen at the corners, filled with dense handwriting in two languages. The script she recognized as Khmer; the other was English, blocky and hurried, the writing of someone taking notes they intended to survive them.

She read by red light, cupped close, heart ticking faster than she liked.

Survey data. Grid references. Water sample numbers — dozens of them, listed with dates and sites, many annotated in English with words like *retest*, *anomalous*, *off-scale*, and once, underlined twice: **DO NOT LOG THIS IN THE FIELD BOOK.**

Below that, a single paragraph, pressed hard enough into the paper to emboss the next three pages:

*If anything happens to us, the numbers are real. Check the discharge points below the plant. Tell my sister in Phnom Penh I'm sorry about her wedding.*

No name. She flipped to the front cover. Inside, in the same hand: a string of characters that wasn't a name at all — a sample designation, or a code, or a dead drop address. And beneath it, smaller:

*Case went downstream with A. If you're reading this, find A.*

Petra closed the notebook and sat very still in the dark, listening to the river and the insects and her own pulse.

*A.* Someone had been sent away before the end — sent downstream, carrying samples, carrying the proof of whatever those numbers measured. Everyone else had walked north out of this camp on swept ground, and one person had gone south with the truth in a case.

The camp had been built for her to find. The notebook had been hidden so she wouldn't — or so that only the right finder would. Either way, the geometry was the same: whoever wrote this had known the end was coming, had prepared for it with the calm of the already-doomed, and had bet everything on a stranger following the river down.

She looked at the neat tents, the ordered food, the folded glasses. Seven people had eaten dinner here on their last ordinary night, and one of them had spent that evening taping a confession under a table.

Petra zipped the notebook into her jacket, banked her fire, and lay down facing the treeline.

Tomorrow she'd go downstream. Tomorrow she'd start asking who *A* was, and what the water below the plant carried that someone had been willing to kill seven people to keep quiet.

Sleep came slow and shallow, and just before it took her, her headlamp caught movement at the far edge of the abandoned camp — a shape, low and quick, slipping between the tents. Not an animal. An animal wouldn't move like that, upright, deliberate, pausing at the cook tarp.

Gone before she could raise the light. But not gone far. She could feel it the way she felt weather coming: someone else was out there in the dark, and they hadn't known she was watching.

Petra slid the knife from its sheath and waited for dawn.
