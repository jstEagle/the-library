## 3. The Concession Clock

The rain started an hour before dawn, a fine grey mist that turned the jungle into something breathing. Petra lay in the survey team's largest tent with her boots on and the client's compass open on her chest, listening to water find every seam in the flysheet she'd patched twice since arriving.

She had spent two days cataloguing the camp, and the catalogue made no sense.

That was the thing about staged scenes — they were never too empty. They were too full. Real abandonment shed weight. People who walked out of a camp took things: batteries, medicine, the good knife, the photographs. What they left behind was junk, and even the junk told you which way they'd gone, because nobody carried out what they couldn't lift and nobody left their boots if they expected to walk.

This camp had been curated.

She'd gone through it the way her father taught her to read a river — not looking at what was there, but at what the water did around it. The tents were pitched properly, guy-lines tensioned, groundsheets tucked. Nobody packed up a camp and then re-pitched it unless they wanted it to look lived-in. The food cache under the tarpaulin held rice, oil, canned fish, dried chilies — enough for six people for three weeks, arranged in neat rows like a shop display. The radio sat on the folding table with its antenna coiled beside it, battery door closed, batteries inside. Dead batteries. She'd tested them against her headlamp's spares. Not drained — dead, flat, chemically finished, which took months of sitting. Someone had swapped them in.

And there were no tracks leaving.

There were tracks arriving — boot prints, dozens of them, days old, hardened by one rain into little plaster casts. They came up from the river landing and they ended at the tents. After that, nothing. Six men did not levitate out of a jungle camp. Either they'd left on the water — no boat missing, the longboat still tied at the landing with half a tank of fuel — or they hadn't left at all.

Petra had sat with that thought through most of the night, watching the mist move through the trees, and by the time the light came up grey and sourceless she had stopped thinking of this as a search.

It was a scene. And scenes had authors.

* * *

The document was in the third place she looked, because documents that matter are never in the first place anyone looks.

The team leader — Vannak, according to the laundry-marker initials on the mess kit and the handwriting on the duty roster pinned inside the cook tent — had kept his personal effects in a dry bag inside a duffel inside a footlocker, three layers deep, which was either paranoia or habit. Inside the dry bag: a passport, a paperback swollen with damp, a zip pouch of chargers and cables, a leather folder with a government crest stamped in gold that had begun to flake.

Petra put on gloves. Not because of fingerprints — after nine days of tropical humidity, forensics was a joke — but because handling paper felt different when you understood you were holding the only honest witness left.

The folder held a work schedule, a chain-of-custody log for sample containers, a hand-drawn map of sampling sites along forty kilometers of river, and — clipped to the back, separate, as if it had been added late and meant to be removed early — a concession permit.

She read it twice.

The permit was issued to the Kiriwat Mineral Development Group, a name she didn't know but whose letterhead knew how to spend money: heavy stock, embossed seal, bilingual text with the English version slightly better than the Khmer, which told her which language it had been drafted in first. It granted exclusive extraction rights to a defined corridor along the upper Sen watershed — the exact country she'd spent two days walking — for a term of years she skimmed past, because what stopped her was the date block at the bottom.

*Term expires:* thirty-eight days from today.

She sat down on Vannak's footlocker and did the arithmetic again, slower, the way you re-count money that comes out wrong.

Thirty-eight days. The fixer Vuthy had told her the team went missing eleven days ago. Which meant the survey team had walked into this valley twenty-six days before their own government's lease on the land beneath their feet came up for renewal.

Petra had worked enough contracts on enough continents to know what that window was. It wasn't a coincidence. It was a calendar. Concessions got renewed when the paperwork said the holder had complied — environmental standards met, water quality maintained, community obligations discharged. And compliance reviews were done by survey teams. Teams like this one. Teams whose findings went into a file that someone in a ministry office read with a pen in his hand.

If the survey came back clean, the lease rolled over for another term, and Kiriwat kept its mountain.

If the survey came back dirty —

She turned to the sampling map. Forty kilometers of river, marked with numbered sites in Vannak's small, disciplined hand. She'd seen the sample bottles themselves yesterday, racked in a padded case in the equipment tent, sealed, labeled, dated — and *still here*. A survey team that finished its fieldwork carried its samples out. Samples were the whole point. Samples were the product. You could lose the tents, the radios, the boots; you did not lose the data.

Unless the samples weren't supposed to reach a lab.

Unless the survey wasn't supposed to finish.

Petra set the folder down on her knees and let the mist drip off the tent fly for a while, because there was a shape assembling itself in her head and she wanted to look at it straight before she committed to it.

Six men come upriver to audit a mining lease that dies in under six weeks. Their camp is pitched carefully and then preserved, like a museum exhibit of a working survey. Their radios are killed with old batteries so no one asks why no distress call ever went out. Their boat stays behind so the story writes itself: flood season, swept away, tragic, regrettable, the river takes whom the river takes. No bodies needed. Rivers are their own alibi.

And the samples stay behind too — because samples in a lab are evidence, and evidence was the one thing somebody could not afford.

Someone hadn't made six men disappear. Someone had made an *audit* disappear, and used the men as the packaging.

Her hands were steady. That surprised her a little. In the Andes, in the white room in Quito where they'd taken her statement, her hands had shaken for a week. But that fear had been about what she'd done. This was about what someone else had done, and somehow that sat easier in her body, the way righteous anger always does until it costs you something.

She thought about Vuthy's face across the hostel table, the finder's fee quoted in crisp American hundreds, the way he'd said *gear intact, no tracks, strange business, yes?* Strange business. He'd known. Maybe not all of it — maybe he was just a broker selling a search to whoever paid — but he'd known this wasn't a rescue job. You don't hire Petra Lockridge, blacklisted twice, cheap and discreet and unmissed, to find living people. You hire her to walk into a valley, confirm a story everyone has already agreed on, and come back with a report that closes a file.

She was not the searcher. She was the rubber stamp.

The realization should have made her pack up. That was the professional read: contract compromised, client misrepresentation, withdraw. Every guide code she'd ever sworn to said the same thing — when the mountain stops being a mountain, when the job stops being the job, you go home.

But home was a debt. Home was a number with too many zeros owed to a family in Colorado whose son she'd tied to a rope that failed, and every month she didn't pay it down was another month she woke up hearing his voice on the radio saying *I'm clear of the ice, Lior, I'm moving* right before the serac came down. Vuthy's fee cleared half of it. Half was not nothing. Half was the difference between owing a ghost and owing a ghost *and* being broke.

So she stayed. She told herself it was for the money, and the telling was almost convincing, and she was honest enough with herself to notice how almost it was.

* * *

By mid-morning the mist had burned off into proper heat and she went back over the camp with new eyes, and the camp confessed everything.

The fire pit: burned down to ash that had been raked flat. Raked. Nobody rakes a fire pit they're abandoning; you rake a fire pit you're leaving for visitors.

The latrine trench: filled and capped with cut sod. Same logic. Tidiness as stagecraft.

The trail camera — she found it at noon, strapped to a trunk overlooking the landing, lens wiped clean, memory card still in it. Nine days of footage. She scrolled through it on her own screen, hunched under the tarpaulin while rain ticked on the leaves above her. Day one: the six-man team arriving, cheerful, hauling gear, Vannak recognizable from the duty roster photo, a big man laughing at something off-frame. Days two through five: ordinary camp life, cooking, sampling runs down to the water, a man washing socks. Day six, 4:17 in the afternoon: two boats coming upriver, unmarked, aluminum, six men aboard in matching dark jackets, and none of them looking like any government survey crew she'd ever seen, because government crews wore whatever they owned and these men wore the same boots.

The timestamp jumped. 4:31 to 9:52 that night. Ninety minutes of footage, gone — not deleted clumsily, but excised, the card edited by someone with software and patience. When it resumed, the camp was empty of the survey team and the two aluminum boats were pulling away downstream, riding low.

Riding low. Petra froze the frame and zoomed until the pixels broke apart, and even broken she could read it: the way the hulls sat in the water, the way the men spaced themselves. Boats carrying cargo that didn't help paddle.

Six men, bound or drugged or worse, taken out by water on the sixth day. Then nine days of silence while someone waited to see who came asking. And now the clock: thirty-eight days on the lease, and somewhere in a ministry a renewal decision waiting on a survey report that would never arrive, unless the disappearance stayed quiet long enough to be ruled an accident and the file closed on a default finding — *no adverse data received.*

No adverse data received. She wrote the phrase in her notebook because it was the whole crime in four words. Not murder, officially. Just an absence. Bureaucracies ran on absences the way rivers ran on gradient.

She checked the last frames of footage. Day eight, mid-morning: a single figure at the tree line, far side of the landing, standing very still among the trunks. Small. Dark clothing. Watching the camp for a long time — the timestamp showed four minutes — before withdrawing. Too slight for any of the six men. Too careful for a villager.

Not her. She'd arrived day nine.

Somebody else was watching this camp. Somebody who also didn't believe the river's story.

Petra crouched there with the camera warm in her hands and felt the job turn over underneath her like a boat in current. An hour ago she'd been a hired stamp. Now she was a witness holding footage of an abduction, alone, unpaid in any currency she trusted, thirty-eight days from a deadline she hadn't signed up to enforce.

Vuthy's fee said: report the camp intact, say the trail ends at the water, take the money, pay the debt, sleep.

The compass on its lanyard against her sternum said nothing at all. It never did. Her father had bought it secondhand in a market in Ubon and it pointed north the same as any compass, and the only reason she carried it instead of a GPS was that it had been his, and the only reason that mattered was a question she'd been avoiding since Quito: *what would he have done?*

He'd have read the sampling map. So she did.

Forty kilometers of river, nineteen sites. She overlaid it mentally against the concession corridor in the permit folder and the two matched almost exactly — Vannak had sampled every kilometer of Kiriwat's lease and a few points beyond its boundaries, upstream and downstream, control sites, the kind a careful man takes so nobody can accuse him of cherry-picking. Site fourteen sat just below a tributary confluence. Site fifteen just above it. A man comparing those two numbers would know exactly what the mine was putting into the water, and how much, and whether it was getting better or worse as the lease ran down.

A man with those two numbers, in the last weeks of a lease, was the most dangerous man in the province.

Petra repacked the dry bag exactly as she'd found it, wiped her prints off the footlocker, and stood in the center of the murdered camp with rain starting again on the canopy, a sound like slow applause.

Thirty-eight days. Somewhere downstream, six men were either dead or wishing they were, and somewhere in a ministry office a pen was hovering over a renewal clause, and the only things standing between Kiriwat and another decade of its mountain were a folder of paper, a rack of sealed bottles, and whatever the figure at the tree line knew.

She should call it in. Satellite phone, coded check-in, Vuthy's voice smooth as river stone: *camp confirmed, no survivors evident, returning.* She had the phone in her hand, thumb on the power, before she registered that she'd already decided not to say any of that — because the moment she reported what she'd actually found, she stopped being disposable, and people who stopped being disposable in this valley had a way of boarding unmarked aluminum boats.

The phone stayed dark.

Behind her, back toward the landing, a stick cracked. One crack, deliberate in its carelessness — the sound of someone who wanted to be heard and not seen.

Petra slid the camera into her jacket, dropped her hand to the knife at her hip, and turned slowly to face the tree line.

"Whoever you are," she said to the green dark between the trunks, "you've been watching this camp for two days. So let's talk about what you know."

Nothing answered. But nothing left, either — and in Petra's experience, that was its own kind of reply.
