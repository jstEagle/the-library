## 5. The Man Who Wasn't on the Manifest

Petra came back to the camp on the fourth day because the camp kept bothering her.

She'd told herself she was done with it. She'd walked the perimeter twice, measured the tent lines against the prevailing wind, checked the fire pit for ash depth, and concluded what she'd concluded on the first morning: nobody left this camp. People who leave camps leave signs. They leave a kicked-over water bottle, a latrine trench filled in badly because they're in a hurry, a boot print in the clay where the ground stays soft for a week after rain. This camp had none of it. Six tents, three of them still staked taut, two collapsed neatly with the guy-lines coiled on top like presents being wrapped. The food cache was lashed to a platform three meters up a strangler fig, sealed against monkeys, still more than half full. The radios were dead, but dead in the way radios die from a pulled battery, not dead the way radios die from weather.

And the manifest — the one Vuthy had given her, eight names in a neat government typeface — listed six men and two women, and the camp had sleeping space for exactly eight, and eight mess kits hung from the drying line, and eight pairs of sandals were lined up outside the command tent in two tidy rows of four.

Nobody leaves a camp with their sandals on.

So she came back. Not because she expected to find anything. Because a camp that bothered her this much was a camp that hadn't finished telling her what it was.

She approached from downstream this time, off the trail, through the bamboo, moving the way her father had taught her to move when he still had a reason to move that way: weight on the outside of the foot, hands loose, eyes doing the work the ears couldn't. The river was up a hand's width since yesterday. The monsoon was thinking about it. You could feel the season leaning on the far hills like a man leaning on a door.

She smelled the smoke before she saw the fire.

Not campfire smoke. Cooking smoke — rice, and something with garlic in it, and under it the sour chemical tang of a spirit stove run too hot. Her stomach tightened with an instinct older than thought. Someone was in the camp. Someone was *living* in the camp, casually enough to cook, and they had been there long enough that the smoke had become a habit.

Petra stood in the bamboo for a long time and let the anger at herself pass through her and out. Four days. She had been within two kilometers of this place for four days, sleeping in a hammock, drinking filtered river water, and someone had been cooking rice in the camp she was being paid to read like a book.

She checked the wind. It came off the river toward the bamboo, which meant her smell was going to them and not the reverse, which meant she'd already lost the first exchange. Fine. She'd lost first exchanges before and won the fights.

She went in low along the drainage cut, past the collapsed mess tent, and stopped at the edge of the clearing with her knife out and her weight on her back foot.

A woman was crouched at the command tent's entrance, working through the document case with the focused economy of someone who'd done it before. Mid-thirties maybe, hard to tell, brown skin going weathered at the cheekbones, hair cut short and practical and going grey early at the temples. Field clothes, sun-faded, mended at both knees with machine stitching — not field-expedient mending, actual machine stitching, which meant she'd had a base somewhere. A headlamp pushed up on her forehead. A canvas sample satchel slung across her body with the strap worn shiny.

She hadn't heard Petra. She was reading a page, lips moving slightly, and whatever was on the page had all of her.

Petra took two steps into the clearing and snapped a dead branch under her heel — deliberately, because the alternative was crossing the last four meters of open ground with a knife in her hand and that was how people got shot or stabbed by startled strangers.

The woman came up off her heels and turned, and to her credit she didn't scream and she didn't run. She went still the way animals go still, and her hand moved — not toward Petra, toward the satchel — and Petra read that movement the way she read rockfall: fast, total, final.

"Touch that bag and I'll pin your sleeve to the tent pole," Petra said. Her Thai was functional, her English was better, and the woman's face said English was fine. "Slowly. Hands where I can see them."

"You're the one who's been sleeping downriver." The woman's voice was low, unhurried, with an accent that put her education somewhere expensive and her nerves somewhere unshakeable. "Hammock. Blue tarp. You filter your water twice."

"You watched me."

"I watch everyone. It's kept me alive for nineteen days." The woman's eyes went to the knife and back to Petra's face, cataloguing her with a professional's efficiency. "You're not consortium. They don't hire women for this work, and they don't creep through bamboo — they come by boat, in threes, and they're rude about it. You're not police. Police don't filter water twice." A pause. "You're the finder Vuthy hired. The farang guide. Lockridge."

The name in a stranger's mouth, out here, in a camp that was supposed to be empty. Petra felt the ground tilt a degree, the way it did when she realized a slope was steeper than it looked.

"Everyone keeps saying that name like I should be flattered."

"You were blacklisted out of Chile and Peru. The Andes thing. Four years ago." The woman said it without cruelty, the way you'd note a river gauge reading. "Vuthy hires people with nothing left to lose. It's cheaper."

"And what are you?" Petra didn't lower the knife. "Because the manifest says eight, and you're not one of the eight. I've checked. Twice."

Something moved behind the woman's eyes — not fear, something more tired than fear. She looked at the document case, at the page still open on the ground, and Petra watched her make a decision with the same deliberate weight she'd use to place a foot on rotten rock.

"Anong Suriyawong," she said. "Hydrologist. Water quality, heavy metals, sediment transport. I was attached to this survey."

"Attached. The manifest doesn't have you."

"The manifest is the government's list of people the government admits were here." Anong's chin lifted a fraction. "I wasn't admitted. Dr. Chai — the team leader, Chai Sonklin — brought me in off the books because what he suspected was in this river was something his ministry didn't want measured, and he wanted it measured by someone whose name wasn't on anything. I have been hiding in this camp for nineteen days eating the survey's cached rice and waiting to find out if the people who took them come back for the paperwork."

"Took them." Petra let the words sit. "You said took. Not lost. Not dead."

"I said what I said."

Petra circled left, keeping the fire between them, keeping Anong's hands in her sightline. The woman tracked her without turning her whole body, which was the movement of someone who'd been watched by professionals and had learned the grammar of it.

"Here's my problem," Petra said. "Six days ago I found this camp staged like a museum exhibit. Tents struck or kept, sandals lined up, food cached, no tracks out. I've stood in a hundred camps and I have never stood in one that read like this one. Somebody wanted the camp to say *we stepped out and never came back*. And now I find a ninth person living in it who isn't on any list, who watched me for four days and didn't make herself known, and who just told me my own blacklist from memory." She tilted the knife a degree, not a threat, a punctuation mark. "So you understand why I'm not putting it away."

"I'd think less of you if you did." Anong crouched, slowly, and set the open page on the document case, and turned both palms out. "Look at it. Then decide what I am."

Petra didn't move for a moment. Then she crossed, keeping her angle, and picked up the page.

It was a water sampling log. Dates, coordinates, and a column of numbers that meant nothing to her except that several of the late entries had been circled in red ink, twice, hard enough to tear the paper slightly. At the bottom, in a cramped hand: *Re-run site 7. Confirm. If this is right we cannot file it through the ministry.*

"Site seven is four kilometers upstream," Anong said. "Where the Khao Sen headwaters come off the consortium's lease block. Chai ran it three times because he didn't believe it the first time. Arsenic, cadmium, and something in the tailings seepage he couldn't identify without a better lab. At concentrations that would put this river's fish on a list and the villages downstream on a worse one." She was still crouched, palms out, but her voice had changed — the flat professional register cracking open to show the thing underneath. "He sent me downstream with half the samples before the rest of the team —"

She stopped. The sentence hung there, unfinished, and Petra watched a woman decide in real time how much of a stranger to trust, and watched the decision go the wrong way.

"Before the rest of the team what," Petra said.

"Before the rest of the team stopped answering the radio." Anong rose slowly, knees stiff from nineteen days of crouching in a camp she was afraid to leave. "That's what I have. That's everything. A man I worked with for two years stopped answering a radio, and I ran, because he told me to run, because he said the data was the only thing that couldn't be replaced and I was the only thing carrying it." Her jaw worked. "I have told myself for nineteen days that following orders was the same as being right."

Petra lowered the knife. Not all the way — she wasn't a fool — but to her side, blade back along her forearm.

"Chai Sonklin," she said. "Describe him."

"Sixty-one. Bad knee, left. Smoked even when he told his team not to. Kept a photograph of his daughter in the map case." Anong's voice went quiet. "Why."

"Because there's a man's watch in the command tent, on the table, not on a wrist, and it's a cheap one, and the strap's been replaced twice, and there's a photograph folded behind the face of a girl of maybe twelve." Petra looked at the tent. "I've been sitting with that watch for six days wondering why a man who left a camp would leave the one thing he'd take."

Anong made a sound that wasn't quite a word. She put her hand against the tent pole to steady herself, and for the first time since Petra had walked out of the bamboo, she looked her age and then some.

"They didn't leave," she said. "I knew they didn't leave. I told myself maybe — maybe there was a boat, maybe the ministry pulled them, maybe —" She pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "You looked at a watch for six days. I've been eating their rice for nineteen."

"That's not the part that matters."

"No?" Anong dropped her hand. Her eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely level. "What's the part that matters?"

"The part that matters is that whoever staged this camp knew how a real camp reads. That's not soldiers. Soldiers leave tracks and trash. That's someone who has spent years making scenes look natural." Petra crouched by the cold fire pit, turning over a half-burned stick without touching it. "And the part that matters is that the survey was auditing the consortium's lease, and the lease expires in thirty-eight days, and a contamination finding at site seven doesn't just fail the audit — it ends the concession, triggers the remediation clauses, maybe criminal referral. That's not a fine. That's the whole game."

She looked up. Anong was staring at her.

"You read the permits."

"I read everything. It's cheaper than being surprised." Petra stood, sheathed the knife, and held out her hand — not warmly, but honestly, which was the only currency she had left. "Petra Lockridge. You already know my sins. Here's where I am: I'm being paid a finder's fee by a fixer I don't trust to find a team I now believe was disappeared by a company with thirty-eight days of motive. I have no radio, no extraction, and a monsoon coming that's going to close every trail in these ranges for six weeks." She let the hand hang there. "You've spent nineteen days hiding in a dead camp with half a sample set and a log that says *we cannot file this through the ministry*. I don't know yet if you're a witness, a survivor, or the best actor I've ever stood across a knife from. But I know you're the only other person in this valley who thinks the same thing I think when I look at this camp."

Anong looked at the hand. Then at the sample satchel across her body, and her arm came around it — not defensively, Petra noticed. Possessively. The way you hold the one thing you were trusted with.

"If I take your hand," Anong said, "you should know what I'm deciding. I decided once already, nineteen days ago, to run with the data instead of staying with the people. I've been doing the arithmetic on that ever since. Two years of work, eight lives, one satchel." Her grip found Petra's. Her hand was dry and very strong. "I'm not doing that arithmetic again. So if we walk out of here, we walk out together, and we don't split up, and you don't hand me to anyone — not Vuthy, not the ministry, not the police — until I've decided who in this country can be told the truth without the truth dying of it. Agreed?"

"Agreed."

"Then we have a problem, and you're not going to like it."

Petra almost laughed. "I never like anything anymore. Say it."

"The samples I carried downstream — half the set, the ones Chai trusted me with —" Anong's hand tightened on hers. "They're not here. I hid them the night I ran, and I've spent nineteen days not going back for them, because going back for them is the one thing that would tell whoever's watching this valley exactly where the last piece of evidence is." She nodded upstream, toward the headwaters, toward the lease block, toward site seven and everything the red ink was trying to say. "They're in a place I can find and you can't. And the monsoon closes that route in —"

She stopped. Both of them felt it at the same moment: the pressure drop, the way the light went thin and green through the canopy, the river's voice lifting a half-tone like a string being tuned.

The first fat drops hit the tent canvas above them like knuckles on a door.

"— in about a day," Anong finished. "Less, if it's early this year. And it is early this year."

Petra looked up at the sky through the bamboo, at the wall of grey coming down the far valley with the unhurried certainty of something that had never once been late, and thought about a leaking boat she'd seen tied at the last village, and a fixer whose price doubled every time he said her name, and a debt that had followed her across two borders and an ocean.

"Then we don't wait for morning," she said, and went to strike a camp that had been waiting nineteen days for someone to finally leave it.
