## 6. What the Water Knows

The knife is still between us. Petra Lockridge hasn't put it away, and I haven't asked her to, and there is a strange comfort in that — in a woman who doesn't pretend the danger has passed just because we've stopped shouting.

We are sitting on opposite sides of the collapsed survey tent, her back against an equipment crate, mine against the aluminum frame of a cot that nobody slept in the night this camp was emptied. The rain has started again, that thin pre-monsoon drizzle that isn't real rain yet, just the sky clearing its throat. It drums on the torn flysheet above us like fingers tapping out a question neither of us wants to answer.

"You were on the manifest," she says. "I checked it twice."

"I was never on any manifest."

"Then you weren't here."

"I was here." I hold up my hands, palms out, the way you do with frightened animals and armed guides. "Just not officially. There's a difference between not being somewhere and not being written down."

Her eyes narrow. She has the kind of face that does its thinking in public — every suspicion arriving like weather across it. I've watched her circle this camp for two days now, reading the ground the way I read water, and I know exactly what she's concluded: that nothing about this place is accidental except the absence of people.

"Start talking," she says. "Or I walk back downriver and let whoever emptied this camp finish the job."

"They'd pay you for that," I say. "Whoever they are. They're paying everyone."

Something moves behind her eyes at that — recognition, maybe, or the flinch of a woman who has already been offered money she shouldn't take. Interesting. That tells me more about her than the knife does.

* * *

Let me tell you who I am, since no one wrote me down either.

My name is Anong Suriyawet. I am thirty-four years old, and for eleven years I was a hydrologist with the Provincial Water Authority until I made the mistake of being right in public. I tested the groundwater below the Sen Khao concession in 2019 and found arsenic at nine times the safe limit, and I wrote a report saying so, and the report was revised by someone above me until it said the opposite of what my data said, and when I refused to sign the revision I was reassigned to a district office with no laboratory, no equipment, and no work. A desk facing a wall. They didn't fire me. Firing creates paperwork. They just made sure I had nothing left to be wrong about.

So when Dr. Wei Lin contacted me eight months ago — quietly, through a former colleague, through two more hands before it reached mine — asking if I would consult unofficially on an independent environmental audit of the same concession, I said yes before he finished the sentence.

Wei Lin was the team leader. You should understand what kind of man he was, because everything that happened afterward only makes sense if you understand him first. He was Taiwanese, sixty-one years old, a geologist who had spent forty years auditing mines on four continents and been thrown out of three countries for telling the truth about them. He collected enemies the way other men collect stamps. He drank his coffee black enough to stand a spoon in and he had a habit, when he was thinking, of drawing contour lines on anything within reach — napkins, dust, the back of your hand if you sat still too long.

He hired me off the books because he knew what I knew: that a hydrologist with an official title becomes an official problem, and an unofficial one can simply vanish into the hills with her instruments and her opinions.

"We're going to find what you found in 2019," he told me, the first night, over instant noodles in this very camp. "Only this time it will be documented properly. Chain of custody. Sealed samples. Independent lab. The kind of evidence that survives lawyers."

"Why now?" I asked him. "The permit expires in weeks. Why audit a mine that's already dying?"

He smiled at me over his cup, and I remember thinking he looked like a man watching a clock he could see and I couldn't.

"Because dying things are most dangerous right at the end," he said. "And because the renewal application is already filed. Forty more years, Anong. They want to double the tailings capacity. Which means whatever they buried in that valley needs to stay buried for another generation." He drew a contour line in the spilled coffee with one finger. "An audit that finds nothing renews clean. An audit that finds something—" He shrugged. "Well. We'll see what it finds."

* * *

What it found was worse than 2019.

I ran the water sampling myself — thirty-two sites across the concession boundary and downstream, following the seeps and the springs the way you follow veins on the back of a hand. And the water told me everything, because water always tells you everything; it's the one witness that cannot be coached. Arsenic in the eastern seeps at eleven times limit. Lead in the streambed sediments below the old tailings pond at concentrations that made me recalibrate twice because I didn't believe my own instrument. And at the spring the villagers call Nam Phrik — the chili spring, because it burns — cadmium, which wasn't even in the picture five years ago, which means the contamination plume is moving, growing, reaching.

Children drink from Nam Phrik.

I know that because I grew up forty kilometers downstream of it, in a village where my mother still lives and my brother still fishes, and when I was a girl that spring ran clear and sweet and cold enough to make your teeth ache. I stood beside it in March with a sample vial in my hand and watched a woman fill a kettle from it without a second thought, and I had to stand there and say nothing, because saying something — saying anything — would have ended the audit that day.

That is the arithmetic I have been carrying since March. Every day I stayed silent bought the evidence one more day of credibility. Every day of silence was a day those children kept drinking.

Wei Lin understood. That was the terrible thing about him — he understood completely, and he asked me to do it anyway, and I did.

"The report means nothing if it dies with us," he said. "Evidence that exists in one place doesn't exist. So we make it exist everywhere. Copies in three countries. Samples split between two labs. And the originals—" he tapped the aluminum case, silver, the size of a small suitcase, with customs seals and tamper-evident tape and a chain-of-custody log zip-tied to the handle, "—the originals go somewhere no one thinks to look until it's time."

"When is it time?"

"When the permit decision lands. Thirty-eight days after expiry, the ministry rules on renewal. If we file our findings before the ruling, the renewal dies in committee. If we file after—" he shrugged again, that shrug that meant *the world*, "—it's just history. Old news about old poison."

"So we wait."

"We wait. And we stay boring. Boring men don't get visited." He looked at me then, longer than usual, and I saw something in his face I hadn't seen before. Not fear. Preparation. "Anong. If anything happens — if anyone comes to this camp who isn't us — you take the case and you go downstream and you don't stop. Not for me. Not for anyone. Do you understand? The case is the mission. We are not the mission."

"I'm not leaving you."

"You are if I tell you to." He smiled, tired. "I've been escorted out of countries by men with guns four times. I know how these conversations go. The data walks out the front door while the men with guns are watching the back."

* * *

They came on a Tuesday night. I know it was Tuesday because I had just finished the weekly calibration log, and the last entry is dated, and I have read that date so many times the ink feels personal.

It was raining — real rain, the first proper storm of the season, the kind that erases everything. I was in the sampling tent at the river bend, four hundred meters downstream of camp, running a final duplicate series on the Nam Phrik samples because Wei Lin wanted redundancy in everything. My radio was on the table. At 21:40 it crackled once — Wei Lin's voice, half a word, cut off mid-syllable — and then nothing but static shaped like breathing.

I called for six minutes. I took the duplicate samples, sealed them, logged them — my hands doing their trained work while the rest of me went cold — and I walked upstream toward camp with a headlamp and a heart like a fist.

I stopped at the tree line. That's the part I can't forgive, the part I rehearse differently every night: I stopped. Because through the rain I heard engines — boat engines, more than one, idling low against the current below the camp landing — and voices, several, unhurried, the voices of men doing a job they expected to complete. And I saw lights moving inside the camp, methodical, tent to tent, and I understood with total clarity that whatever was happening in there had been arranged, scheduled, and resourced, and that I was looking at the difference between a missing-persons mystery and a mass grave with witnesses.

And I turned around. I picked up the sample case and Wei Lin's backup drive and I walked downstream into the rain, four hundred meters past my own courage, and I did not look back, and I have looked back every night since.

By morning the camp was empty. Tents pitched, food cached, radios dead, boots gone but no tracks leaving — because the boats came up in the storm and left in the storm, and the rain did the rest. Whoever planned it knew this valley. Knew the monsoon's first week would wash the landing clean. Knew that a vanished survey team in flood season reads as misadventure, as incompetence, as *the river took them*.

Except the river didn't take them. I know where the river goes. I've spent my whole life learning where the river goes.

* * *

Petra hasn't moved. The knife rests across her knee now rather than in her grip, which I choose to read as progress. Rain works its way through the flysheet and drips onto the cot between us, patient as testimony.

"Say it plainly," she says. "What's in the case."

"Thirty-eight sealed water and sediment samples. Duplicates of all critical sites. Field logs, calibration records, chain-of-custody documentation signed and dated. Photographs. Wei Lin's preliminary analysis with his own annotations." I keep my voice level, the way I was taught to deliver results — facts first, feeling later, feeling possibly never. "Enough to kill the permit renewal. Enough to open an inquiry. Enough, if it reaches the right lab and the right journalist, to end the consortium's license to operate in this country entirely."

"And they killed nine people for it."

"They killed nine people for the *absence* of it. There's a difference. Killing nine auditors buys you silence only if the evidence dies with them." I look at her steadily. "Which is why I'm still alive. And why you found me instead of finding them. They think the case burned with the camp or sank with the team. As long as they believe that, I'm not worth hunting."

"But you came back." Her chin lifts toward the camp, the tents, the whole staged emptiness of it. "To a place they emptied. Why?"

Because I couldn't breathe downstream anymore. Because guilt is a current and it flows one direction. Because I needed to see whether the rain had taken the last of it, and whether I could live with what remained.

None of those are answers I'm willing to hand a woman holding a knife.

"To recover the backup drive," I say. "Wei Lin cached a second copy. If the case is lost, the drive is the fallback."

"Is it here?"

"It's here."

She studies me for a long moment, and I watch her decide something — watch the decision move through her face like weather, suspicion hardening into something else. Not trust. Nothing so generous. More like a professional acknowledging another professional's gear check: *this woman's kit is real, her knots are real, her fear is real.*

"The consortium," she says slowly. "Sen Khao Mining. Their lease dies in thirty-eight days."

"Thirty-six now."

"And the survey was auditing them."

"The survey was the only thing standing between them and forty more years."

She exhales through her nose, almost a laugh, without any humor in it. "I was hired to find a missing team. Vuthy — the fixer who hired me — he said the government wanted closure before the permit decision. Said it was administrative." Her jaw tightens. "He never said the audit was the motive."

"He wouldn't." I lean forward. "Listen to me, guide. Ask yourself who profits from a search that finds nothing. A body recovered closes a file. A team never found leaves the audit incomplete forever — no report filed, no findings entered, renewal proceeds uncontested. Your employer may not be looking for the truth. He may be paid to make sure the looking stops."

The rain thickens around us, drumming harder now, the sky committing to the season. Somewhere downriver, thunder rolls along the valley like furniture being moved in a room above.

Petra is quiet for a long time. Then: "Where's the case now?"

"Downstream. Where it's been since Tuesday night. Somewhere no one thinks to look."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only answer I have. Not because I don't trust you—" and here I almost smile, because we both know trust is a currency neither of us is holding, "—but because the fewer people who know, the longer I stay uninteresting. Right now I'm a scavenger on a dead camp. The moment I become a courier, I become a target. And so does anyone standing next to me."

Something crosses her face at that — she hears it too, what I'm really saying. *You're already standing next to me.* Whatever brought her up this river, whatever debt or desperation Vuthy dangled in front of her, she is in this now. The consortium emptied a camp of nine people. She has seen the staging with her own expert eyes. She knows, the way I know, that this was never misadventure.

"The monsoon's coming early," she says finally, glancing at the sky. "Two days, maybe three, if the pressure keeps dropping. When it comes, the river owns this valley."

"Then we leave before it does."

"*We*." She raises an eyebrow.

"You have a boat. I have evidence. You were hired to find the truth about this team—" I nod at the empty tents, the dead radios, the absence with footprints leading nowhere, "—and I'm the closest thing to it that's still breathing. Take me downstream, guide. Take the case to the capital, to the ministry, to anyone with a stamp and a conscience, before the permit renews and all of this—" I gesture at the rain, the river, the graves that haven't been dug yet, "—becomes permanent."

She looks at me for a long moment. Then she stands, slides the knife back into its sheath, and holds out her hand — not to shake, but to pull me up off the cot, brisk and businesslike, the negotiation concluded without ever being spoken.

"First light," she says. "And Anong — if that case is a bomb, or bait, or anything other than what you say it is, I will leave you on a sandbar with your boots tied together."

"Fair," I say. "And if you sell me to the highest bidder, I hope the money clears your debts, because it won't clear anything else."

For the first time, she almost smiles. It's not warmth. It's recognition — two people who have each done the arithmetic of survival and found the sum shameful, standing on opposite sides of the same equation.

At first light we break camp. I take one last look at the tents, at Wei Lin's coffee cup still sitting on the crate where he left it, ringed with dried contour lines drawn by a dead man's finger, and I promise him what I promised in the rain: *the data walks out the front door.*

But as we push the longboat off the mud and the current takes us, I feel it against my spine where the case rides wrapped in my sleeping mat, thirty-eight sealed samples in their silver shell, heavier than aluminum has any right to be.

The truth has a weight now. A shape. A handle.

And downstream, somewhere in the rising brown water, I'd swear I can hear engines idling against the current — patient, unhurried, more than one.
