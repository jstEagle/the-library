## 7. Downstream

The longboat was called the *Sokha*, which meant happiness, and it was leaking from three places.

Petra found it tied to a rickety dock forty minutes downstream of the camp, half-hidden under palm fronds by someone who'd meant to come back for it and never did. The owner was a question she chose not to ask. The hull was fiberglass over foam, older than her boots, patched in places with what looked like epoxy and prayer.

"It floats," Anong said.

"So does a corpse."

"Then we have something in common with it." She swung her pack — the sample case wrapped inside a rice sack — over the gunwale and climbed in without waiting for an answer. That was the thing Petra was learning about her: Anong didn't argue. She simply proceeded as though agreement were a formality that would catch up eventually.

Petra checked the boat properly because nobody else would. Bilge dry-ish. Fuel tank two-thirds full, cap crusted with salt bloom. A bail bucket wedged under the thwart, green with algae. A long-tail engine bolted to a pivot frame, pull cord frayed but intact. Three leaks: one at the transom seam weeping slow, one forward where something had gouged a star-shaped crack, and one she only found when she pressed her thumb against the floorboard and watched water rise through it like a bruise surfacing.

She'd run worse. She'd run worse last month, and the month before that, on rivers with names that didn't make it onto maps because the people who named rivers had never been paid enough to care.

"Get in," she said. "Sit forward. Weight forward keeps the transom crack above waterline."

Anong sat. Petra cast off, pulled the cord twice, and the engine caught on the third with a sound like a chainsaw arguing with a beehive. The *Sokha* slid out into the current and the river took them the way rivers take everything — without asking.

* * *

The Khao Sen headwaters ran brown-green and fast, braided around gravel bars, the banks a wall of rainforest so dense it looked poured rather than grown. Mist clung to the ridgelines upstream. Behind them, already shrinking, the valley where six men had pitched their tents and cooked their food and then, according to every physical trace, evaporated.

Anong rode in the bow with her arms around the sample case, watching the banks slide past. She hadn't let go of it since the camp. Not once. Petra had watched her sleep curled around it like a child around a dog.

"How far?" Anong shouted over the engine.

"Two days if the river behaves. Three if it doesn't."

"Will it behave?"

Petra looked at the sky. High cloud moving east to west, wrong direction for this time of year, stacked up over the mountains like traffic. "No," she said, and left it there.

They ran the first hour in silence, which suited her. Trust was a currency she'd stopped carrying years ago, and the little she had left was spoken for. But silence on a boat is different from silence on land. On land you can walk away from a person. On water you're welded to them by circumstance, and after an hour the silence starts to feel less like a wall and more like a room.

It was Anong who broke it, shouting back over her shoulder: "You counted the footprints."

"What?"

"At the camp. When I came back for my kit. You were crouched at the treeline counting."

"I wasn't counting footprints. There weren't any. That was the point." Petra eased the throttle down as the channel narrowed around a rock garden, reading the water the way other people read sentences. "I was checking the soil. Six days since the team was last logged on the radio. In six days, rain or no rain, six men leaving a camp leave sign. Broken ferns heal in four days, but they don't heal clean. Mud holds prints for weeks here. There was nothing. Not even a game trail worn through the grass where they'd have walked to shit in the woods."

"And your conclusion?"

"Either they walked out barefoot in single file sweeping behind themselves, which nobody does, or they never left on foot at all." She threaded the needle between two submerged rocks, felt the hull kiss one, heard Anong inhale. "Boats came up this river at night. Quiet ones. They put the team aboard and they took everything that mattered and they staged the rest. Tents up, food cached, radios dead. A camp frozen mid-meal reads as tragedy. It's supposed to look like they wandered off, or the river took them."

"That's what I told the police," Anong said quietly. "In the provincial capital. Before I stopped being someone the police would listen to."

The current picked up as the valley widened. Petra let the engine idle and thought about how much to give away. Giving things away was how you got robbed; she'd learned that in two countries' worth of hard ways. But there was another arithmetic running underneath, the one she hated: a woman alone on this river was cargo. Two women on a boat, even strangers, even liars — that was a crew.

"The document," Petra said. "The permit I found in the survey tent. Thirty-eight days left on the consortium's concession. Your audit was the thing standing between them and a renewal."

"You read fast."

"I read everything. It's cheaper than trusting anyone." She glanced at Anong's back, the rigid line of her shoulders. "Who else knows about the contamination data?"

"The consortium knows. They've known for two years — I sent them a preliminary report through proper channels, stamped, signed, the whole liturgy. It went into a drawer. Then the survey got funded, officially, to do it again with government weight behind it. Dr. Vireak believed the second time would stick." A pause. The engine buzzed. Water ticked against the hull. "He was wrong about the sticking part. He may have been right about the rest."

"The rest?"

"He told me to go downstream with the samples before the end. He said, 'Data survives people.' I thought he was being dramatic. He was being precise." Anong turned around finally, and her face in the flat light was older than her hands suggested. "You want to know why I was scavenging the camp. I was looking for his field logs. The samples prove the water is poisoned. His logs prove he knew, when he knew, and who he told. Without them it's my word and some vials. With them it's a chain."

"Did you find them?"

"No."

Petra said nothing. She had found a locked aluminum document case in the leader's tent, small enough to hold field notebooks, too heavy to be empty, and she had put it in her own pack without mentioning it to anyone, including herself, really — a professional reflex dressed up as caution. She wasn't ready to hand it over. She wasn't sure what that made her.

* * *

They stopped at midday on a sandbar below a fish weir — bamboo stakes driven into the shallows in a chevron, abandoned for the season. Petra beached the bow, bailed the bilge while Anong stretched her legs, and ate cold rice out of a shared tin without discussing sharing it.

That was when Anong pointed upstream and said, very calmly, "Boat."

Petra's mouth was full. She didn't spit it out; spitting takes time. She looked. Two kilometers upriver, maybe less in that light, a longboat with a canopy was coming down the center channel at cruising speed. Canopied boats on this stretch meant money. Money on this river meant the mine, the road crews, or the government, and those three categories overlapped like a Venn diagram drawn by a drunk.

"Not locals," Anong said. "Local boats hug the banks. That one's running the middle like it owns it."

"Maybe it does."

"We could wait. Let them pass."

"They'll see us on the bar. Open ground, no cover." Petra was already moving, kicking sand off the anchor line, shoving the bow into the water. "We run. If they're curious, they'll follow. If they follow, we learn something. If they don't—" she pulled the cord, and the engine snarled awake, "—then we lost nothing but lunch."

The *Sokha* bit into the current. Petra ran the throttle high enough to throw spray, low enough not to crack the old hull on the chop, and kept one eye on the mirror she'd rigged — a truck side mirror zip-tied to the tiller frame, bought in a border town in another life. The canopied boat grew. Slowly. It wasn't gaining the way a boat gains when it's chasing. It was gaining the way a boat gains when it's bigger and newer and doesn't care.

"Faster?" Anong asked.

"Faster burns fuel. Fuel is the whole plan. We don't have a plan B, C, or a map." Petra fed it a little more anyway. The transom leak woke up, a thin silver thread of water finding the lowest board. "Bail."

Anong bailed. She was good at it — steady rhythm, no wasted motion, dumping windward — and Petra filed that away with the knife work and the unflappable sitting. This was a woman who had spent time on water recently, and alone.

Ten minutes. Fifteen. The mirror showed the canopy holding its distance, neither closing nor falling back. A shadow, keeping pace.

"They're not trying to catch us," Anong said.

"No."

"They're herding."

Petra's hands went still on the tiller for exactly one second, which on moving water is a long time. Because that was the word. Herding. She'd been on the wrong end of it once, in the Andes, guiding a client group through a valley while men on horseback funneled them toward a checkpoint they couldn't see yet, and the memory arrived whole: the specific patience of it, the way power moves slowly when it's certain.

Ahead, the river split. Left channel wide and shallow, braided through gravel, the obvious route. Right channel narrow, tree-shadowed, deeper, dropping into a gorge the maps marked with a single dismissive line.

"Left looks easier," Anong said.

"Left is where they want us. Wide water, one way in, one way out, and a landing beach halfway down where a truck could wait." She was already turning the tiller. "Right."

"Right has rapids."

"Right has trees. Trees mean shade, shade means we disappear, and I know how to read drops." She didn't say *and I've run worse*, because she'd already said it once today and repeating yourself was a luxury of people who expected to be believed.

The *Sokha* swung into the right channel and the world closed in like a door. Overhead canopy stitched itself shut; the light went green and underwater-colored. The current doubled, then tripled, funneling between limestone walls furred with moss. Petra dropped the throttle to a whisper and let the river do the driving, steering only enough to keep the hull off the seams where the eddy lines folded. Behind them, faint through the trees, the drone of the big engine continued down the left channel — patient, unhurried, going to check the beach.

Anong sat very straight in the bow, hugging the case, water flicking her face, and started, absurdly, to laugh. It was a small sound, quickly strangled, but real.

"You enjoy this," she said.

"No."

"You're smiling."

"I'm concentrating. My face does that." And God help her, it was true — the smile was there, involuntary, the old animal pleasure of solving a river. She killed it. Pleasure was how guides died; she'd written that sentence in a report once, about herself, in the third person, because the truth wouldn't fit in the first.

* * *

They made the gorge by late afternoon and camped on a ledge of bedrock where a side stream tumbled in cold and clear. Petra dragged the bow up until the transom crack sat in air, bailed the boat dry, and stuffed the star-shaped crack forward with a bar of epoxy putty she'd carried for three countries, kneading it gray and pressing it in like packing a wound.

Anong built a fire no bigger than a cupped hand, using dry bamboo shavings, feeding it twig by twig. Smokeless, nearly. Another skill filed away.

They ate. The dark came down through the trees all at once, the way it does near the equator, no dusk worth the name. Somewhere across the river a nightbird called in threes. The sample case sat between them like a third person at the fire.

"Tell me about the debt," Anong said.

Petra looked at her.

"You talk in your sleep. A little. Names." Anong held up a hand before Petra could decide whether to be angry. "I'm not prying. I'm doing arithmetic. You could have sold me to Serey's people today — don't look surprised, I saw the boat too, and I know Serey's boats. You could have taken the case and the finder's fee and been on a plane. Instead you're eating cold rice on a rock with me. People do that for money or for guilt, and you count money too carefully to spend it on kindness."

For a long moment the fire was the loudest thing in the world.

"A client died," Petra said finally. "Three years ago. Cordillera rescue, whiteout, my call to push for the summit ridge instead of digging in. He fell. I didn't. Both of those facts are true and only one of them matters to his family." She turned the tin plate in her hands. "His sister runs a foundation now. Every month I send what I earn and it disappears into a number that never gets smaller, because interest is a machine that eats effort. Two countries blacklisted me for it. The report said errors of judgment. The report was generous and it was also correct, and both of those are true too."

"And the fee Vuthy offered you clears it."

"Closes it. Ends it. I'd be a person again, on paper." Petra fed a twig to the fire and watched it catch. "So yes. Guilt. You found me out. What's yours?"

She expected deflection. She'd earned deflection; she'd given the story grudgingly, in the flattest voice she owned. But Anong just looked into the flames for a while, and when she spoke her voice had gone somewhere else, somewhere with children in it.

"I left," she said. "Vireak told me to take the samples downstream and I went. I didn't argue. I didn't ask what would happen to them. I did the math he gave me — data survives people — and I let him do the dying part alone." Her thumb moved along the edge of the case, back and forth, a metronome. "Six days I've been telling myself I followed orders. Orders is what soldiers say. I'm a scientist. We're supposed to say *the evidence required preservation*. It sounds better. It means the same thing."

The nightbird called again. Downstream, far off, the river made its continuous argument with the rocks.

"Tomorrow," Petra said, because someone had to say something practical or they'd sit in this all night and drown in it, "we hit the confluence by noon. After that there's traffic — fishing boats, ferries, eyes. We travel as boring as possible. Two women, one boat, going to market."

"And the case?"

Petra reached into her pack, and felt Anong go tense beside her — felt it, the whole body tightening like a rope taking load — and set the aluminum document case down between them. Locked. Dented at one corner. Heavy the way paper is heavy.

"This was in Vireak's tent," she said. "I should have mentioned it at knifepoint. I'm mentioning it now."

Anong stared at it. Then at her. Something moved across her face that wasn't quite forgiveness and wasn't quite suspicion but lived in the neighborhood of both.

"Do you know what's in it?"

"No. And I didn't open it, which you can verify, because I don't carry picks." She stood, joints reporting in one by one, and nodded downstream into the dark. "Open it if you want. It's yours. But open it tomorrow, in daylight, on moving water — because whatever's in there, the moment we know, we stop being two women going to market."

Anong pulled the case into her lap. Her hands rested on the latches and didn't move.

"Petra." Her voice was quiet under the river noise. "There's a light on the water behind us."

Petra turned. Far downstream, at the mouth of the gorge where it opened back into the main channel, a single white light hung in the blackness. Not moving. Not searching. Just waiting, the way a light waits when it already knows which way you have to come.

She looked at the fuel gauge. Half. She looked at the dark water between them and the light, and at the sky stacking its wrong-direction clouds over the ridge, and she thought: *thirty-seven days on the permit clock, and the river's about to get much higher.*

"Sleep in shifts," she said.
