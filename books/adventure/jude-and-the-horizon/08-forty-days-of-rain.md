## 8. Forty Days of Rain

The first warning was the birds.

Petra had been watching them all morning without knowing why — swiftlets that had been working the river in loose spirals since dawn suddenly climbing, climbing, then streaming south over the ridge in a single dark ribbon, like smoke pulled sideways by a draft. Anong sat in the bow of the longboat with her arms wrapped around the sample case, dozing against a coil of rope, and Petra decided not to wake her.

She decided wrong.

By early afternoon the light had gone the color of old tin. The air pressure dropped so fast her ears popped twice on the same bend, and the river — which had been running green and slow for two days — turned the color of milk tea and began to move with a purpose it hadn't shown before. Debris appeared: leaves, twigs, half a bamboo fence, a dead pig rotating slowly in an eddy with its legs stiff toward the sky.

"That's not normal," Anong said, awake now, standing to look upstream. "The monsoon isn't due for—"

"Nine days." Petra had the almanac page folded in her pocket, torn from a guesthouse wall calendar. "I know."

"Then this is—"

"Early." Petra cut the engine to idle and listened. Under the two-stroke chatter she could hear it now, faint but unmistakable: a low continuous roar from upstream, too steady to be rapids, too deep to be wind. Water moving through country that had no room for it. "Flash flood coming down the tributaries. The whole upper catchment got hit at once."

Anong looked at her. In three days on the river together they had developed a shorthand — not trust, exactly, but a working agreement about who knew what. Petra knew water. Anong knew everything else.

"How bad?"

"Depends on how much rain fell up there." Petra scanned the banks as she spoke, reading them the way other people read a page. The river here ran between low terraces of red clay, farmland mostly, abandoned for the season. Six meters of bank above the current waterline, maybe eight. "If the whole system dumped, we'll see a wall come down this valley inside two hours. If it was scattered, we get a fast rise instead. Either way we want to be tied off somewhere high before dark."

"And if we're mid-river when it hits?"

"We won't be."

* * *

They weren't. But it was close.

Petra pushed the boat hard through the afternoon, running water she would normally have treated with more respect, threading debris fields by eye, using the main channel's deeper water where the current was fastest because fast meant predictable. Twice she had to kill the engine and paddle through logjams, Anong hauling on the second paddle without being asked, matching her stroke count within minutes. The woman was a hydrologist; she understood rivers the way Petra did, just from the other side of the surface.

At 4:40, with the sky black as a bruise and the first fat drops hitting the river like thrown gravel, Petra saw what she needed: a side channel on the left bank, choked with vegetation, leading back toward a rise of limestone with a stand of fig trees on top. She nosed the bow in, gunned through the shallows until the hull ground against mud, and jumped.

"Tie off high! Two lines, one spare!" She was already hauling the bowline up through the roots. "Everything out of the boat that can't swim!"

"What can't swim?" Anong shouted over the rising wind.

"The fuel. The case. Us."

They got the boat winched six meters up the bank on rollers of dead bamboo, engine and all, with the rain arriving in earnest halfway through the job — not drops anymore but a solid gray curtain, water falling so hard and so straight that the river ten meters away vanished behind it. They dragged the gear under an overhang of rock, wedged the sample case in a dry crack lined with banana leaves, and Petra lashed the boat to two fig trunks with every knot her father had ever taught her, doubled, then tripled.

Then there was nothing left to do, so they sat under the overhang and watched the world end.

* * *

It didn't end dramatically. That was the thing about monsoon rain that nobody who hadn't lived through one could understand. It wasn't violence. It was patience. It fell with the flat, total commitment of something that intended to keep going until you stopped believing in anything else. Within twenty minutes the temperature dropped eight degrees. Within forty, the side channel they'd entered by was a brown torrent wide enough to float a truck. Within two hours, the river they'd been navigating all day was gone — not lower or higher, but *gone*, replaced by something broader, faster, and carrying entire trees upright like masts.

"The gauge station at Ban Tha Len recorded eleven hundred millimeters in the 2019 event," Anong said. She was shouting to be heard over the rain drumming the rock overhang. "In seventy-two hours. This looks worse."

"You have gauge stations out here?"

"I *was* a gauge station," Anong said. "For nine years. Before I became a fugitive."

Petra almost smiled. Almost. Then a sound came down the valley that killed whatever humor she'd found — a deep, grinding boom, repeated, rhythmic, like something enormous walking.

"Landslides," Anong said.

"On the karst slopes. Whole hillsides letting go." Petra watched the far bank, where a section of forest simply detached itself and slid into the flood, trees cartwheeling, the water swallowing them without a ripple of effort. "Every one of those is going to dam a tributary for a while, then burst. We could get surges all night."

"Is the boat safe up there?"

"It's safe from the water." Petra looked up through the rain at the fig trees, at the clay bank dissolving around their roots. "It's the bank I'm worried about."

* * *

At some point full dark arrived, though dark barely mattered; the rain made its own dim gray noon. They took turns sleeping, which meant taking turns lying rigid on a bed of banana leaves listening to the river eat the valley. Around midnight Petra woke to Anong shaking her shoulder and a sound she'd been dreading: cracking, wet and fibrous, like a giant biting celery.

The bank below the boat had slumped. Not collapsed — slumped, a meter-wide crescent of clay hanging away from the slope, the boat's stern line now anchored partly in soil that was actively leaving.

They worked by headlamp in sheets of rain, re-rigging both lines to a single massive fig root that grew straight out of living rock, hauling the boat another four meters up a slope that fought them the whole way, mud sucking at their knees, Petra falling once and catching herself on a root hard enough to wrench her shoulder. When it was done they stood gasping in each other's faces, soaked to the skin, and Anong started laughing — a wild, exhausted bark of laughter that Petra recognized because she'd heard it come out of her own mouth on mountains, in the hour after you survive something stupid.

"It's a boat," Anong said. "We are defending a *boat*. From a *river*. It's like defending a fish from the ocean."

"It's the only boat," Petra said, and that ended the laughter, because it was true, and they both knew what the boat was. It was the samples. It was the lab in the provincial capital. It was thirty-eight days on a permit clock and a chain of custody that died if they did.

They went back under the overhang. Nobody slept again.

* * *

Dawn came the color of dishwater and showed them a new planet.

The river had risen four meters overnight and spread beyond its banks into the farmland, so that where there had been terraces and fences and a distant temple roof there was now a single unbroken lake dotted with treetops, moving at walking pace but with a deep, muscular pull underneath that Petra could read in the way the debris moved — not drifting, *steering*, everything drawn toward the main channel with the inevitability of iron filings to a magnet.

Their side channel was part of the lake now. The boat sat on its mud island like a monument.

"We can't launch in this," Anong said. "Look at the flow. Anything without an engine gets taken downstream sideways, and anything *with* an engine spends all its power just holding position."

"We're not launching today." Petra was studying the boat itself, and what she saw tightened her chest. During the night evacuation, in the dark, with the rain hammering, the hull had taken damage — she could see it now in the way the boat sat, listing slightly to port, the gunwale riding lower than it should. She waded out and put her hand inside. Her fingers came away wet.

"Anong."

Anong came. Together they found it: a split along a seam below the waterline, maybe forty centimeters, the caulking gone soft and the planks sprung just enough to weep. Not catastrophic. Not yet. But a leaking boat on a rising river was a countdown of its own.

"How long has it been taking water?"

"Since last night, probably. Maybe longer — I wouldn't have noticed in the chop." Petra pressed the seam. A thin thread of water welled up through it, patient as the rain. "It's slow now. It won't be slow when we're loaded and moving. Every hour on this river it works wider."

"So we fix it."

"With what? I've got epoxy putty for a *hole*, not a seam. Seams need to be dried, cleaned, re-caulked, and that takes a day of sun we don't have and materials I don't have." Petra sat back on her heels and made herself think like a guide instead of a drowning woman. Improvise. Guides improvised; that was most of the job. "Actually. Wait. Maybe not."

She went to the gear pile and dug out the roll of heavy-duty plastic sheeting they'd been using as a rain fly, a spool of nylon cord, and the tin of two-part epoxy she'd bought in the border town along with the boat, back when the boat was someone else's problem.

"Here's what we do. We can't re-caulk, so we don't try. We laminate. Plastic sheeting bonded over the seam with epoxy, clamped with cord tension, three layers, extending well past the split on both sides. It'll flex with the hull instead of fighting it. Ugly. Temporary. Good for—" she calculated, hating the number she arrived at, "—maybe five days of hard use. Less if we hit debris."

"Five days to the capital?"

"Three, if the river behaves." Petra looked out at the lake that used to be a river and laughed shortly. "Which it won't. Four, realistically. Five if we're lucky and careful, and we haven't been either so far."

They worked through the morning. It was miserable, precise work — sanding the seam with a stone, mixing epoxy in a shell casing Anong produced from her pack with a look that invited no questions, laying the plastic down in overlapping shingles while the rain tried to wash it all away under a shelter they built from the boat's own cover. Petra held, Anong wrapped, Anong held, Petra wrapped. The cord went on in a spiral pattern, tensioned by levering with a paddle, until the patch looked less like a repair than like a bandage on a wound — which, Petra thought, was exactly what it was.

By midday the leak had slowed to a seep. By evening, when they tested it by floating the boat in the flooded side channel with fifty kilos of ballast aboard, it shipped perhaps a liter an hour.

"Bailable," Anong said.

"Barely." Petra bailed the test water out and looked at the sky, which showed no interest whatsoever in stopping. "One bailer each, bailing in shifts, any time we're under way. And we don't run at night anymore. Night running is how boats find logs."

"Petra." Anong was looking at her across the boat, rain dripping off her chin, and there was something in her face that Petra had learned to recognize over the past days — the expression of a scientist about to say a thing she'd already measured three times. "How much food do we have?"

Petra counted without looking, because she'd counted already, twice, hoping the answer would change. "Four days. Five if we stretch it."

"And the nearest town with a market is—"

"Nakhon Chai. Three days downstream in good conditions." Petra let the sentence sit. "These aren't good conditions."

* * *

They launched at first light on the third morning, because waiting cost food and food was now the scarcest thing on the river.

The flooded valley had merged with the main channel into a single inland sea, and navigating it was less like boating than like reading a manuscript written in disappearing ink. Petra steered by submerged fence lines, by the lean of drowned trees, by the direction the surface debris curved — always toward the buried channel, which was the only place the current had teeth. Twice she felt the keel kiss something soft and steered away before it became something hard. Anong bailed in steady rhythm, a liter can passing water over the side in a metronome beat that Petra used to time her turns.

They made nineteen kilometers the first day, which should have been forty. They camped on the roof of a submerged farmhouse, the boat tied to the chimney, eating half rations while the rain kept up its endless gray arithmetic.

On the fourth day the river took an interest in them.

It started as a vibration in the hull, a tremor Petra felt through her boots before she heard anything — and then the sound arrived, a roar from ahead, and she saw it: a section of the far bank giving way, a whole hillside of red clay and forest sliding into the flood, and the displaced water coming back at them as a traveling wave, a meter-high step of brown water moving faster than anything that size had a right to move.

"Wave!" she shouted. "Head into it! Bail like your life is the case!"

Anong understood instantly — the case *was* her life, had been since the day she'd carried it out of a camp full of men who were never coming back — and threw herself at the bailer while Petra swung the bow square and opened the throttle. The longboat climbed the wave's face, hung for a moment with the propeller singing in thin water, and slammed down the back side hard enough to crack the seat Petra was sitting on. Water came aboard in tons. The patch held. The patch *held*, and Petra had one bright, absurd flash of pride in a rectangle of plastic sheeting and shell-casing epoxy before the next swell hit.

They surfed the tail of the surge for two kilometers, broadside-rolling, shipping water, bailing, until the wave spent itself against the drowned farmland and the river went back to its patient work of rising.

When it was over they drifted in the lee of a floating mat of vegetation, engine off, both of them breathing like bellows. The bilge had thirty centimeters in it. Anong bailed it out in silence, then kept bailing water that wasn't there, her hands still going, until Petra reached over and stilled them.

"We're okay," Petra said.

"We're not okay." Anong's voice was flat and precise, the voice she used for data. "That was a minor bank failure. There are dozens of unstable slopes above us. The river is still rising. Our boat leaks, our food runs out tomorrow, and we are—" she stopped, recalibrated, started again. "I'm telling you this not because you don't know it. I'm telling you because I need to hear someone say what we do next."

Petra looked downstream. Somewhere down there was Nakhon Chai, and past it the capital, and past that a laboratory and a permit clock ticking down from thirty-four days. Somewhere behind them was Vuthy, who had a satellite phone and a price list and, possibly, a conscience — the odds on the conscience had shortened every day she'd known him.

What they needed was information. Rainfall data, river stages, which channels were open, where the checkpoints had washed out. And there was exactly one person who might sell it to them, if selling it to them paid better than selling *them*.

Petra dug the battered handset out of the dry bag, checked the battery — two bars, maybe three calls' worth — and looked at Anong.

"I'm going to call Vuthy," she said. "And I need you to remember that whatever he offers us, the answer starts with listening."

The rain hammered the river, patient as ever, as if it had all the time in the world.

Maybe it did. They had thirty-four days.
