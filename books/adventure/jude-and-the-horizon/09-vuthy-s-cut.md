## 9. Vuthy's Cut

The satellite phone lived in a waterproof pouch zippered inside Petra's jacket, against her left ribs, where she could feel its weight like a second heartbeat. She hadn't touched it in four days. Four days of river, of bailing, of Anong's silence and the rain's less patient kind, and now they were tied up under a fisherman's tarpaulin stilt-house forty minutes downstream from the checkpoint they'd bluffed through, and the phone felt heavier than it had in Vuthy's office, heavier than it had ever felt, because calling him meant admitting she needed something.

She waited until Anong went down to the water to wash. The girl — woman, Petra corrected herself; Anong was thirty-four and had published papers Petra couldn't read — moved with a careful economy that Petra had learned to read as exhaustion held together by discipline. She washed her clothes every evening whether they needed it or not. It was the only ritual she'd kept from before, and Petra understood rituals like that. You kept them because everything else had been taken.

The phone took ninety seconds to find satellites through the cloud deck. Then it found Vuthy on the first ring, which told her he'd been waiting.

"Petra." His voice came through warm and unhurried, the voice of a man who never had to raise it to be heard. "You're alive. I had money on three more days."

"You bet against me?"

"I hedge. It's why I'm still rich." A pause, and she could hear ice moving in a glass. "Where are you?"

"Downstream of the Kham checkpoint."

"Mm." He let the syllable sit there. "Past the police post. Bold."

"We had permits."

"You had paper," Vuthy said. "There's a difference, and you know it better than most. Tell me about the case."

So he already knew about the case. Of course he did. Anong had gone into hiding for eleven days, and in eleven days a man like Vuthy could learn what color socks a person wore. Petra watched the river slide past under the stilt-house, brown and swollen, carrying whole trees sideways. Somewhere upstream were six people who had been made to disappear, and somewhere downstream was a lab, and between those two points was this phone call, and she had known since she pulled the phone from its pouch that it would go badly.

"The case is intact," she said. "Chain of custody documented. Field logs, sampling coordinates, sealed vials. Everything your missing survey team died carrying."

"They're not dead."

"No?"

"I'd know," Vuthy said, and the terrible thing was that he probably would. "Dead men generate paperwork. Missing men generate invoices. This country runs on the second kind."

"That's a hell of a thing to say about people I'm being paid to find."

"And how is that going?" He sounded genuinely curious, which was worse than mockery. "Your finder's fee requires bodies, Petra. Or at least signatures. What do you actually have?"

She had a staged camp and a permit expiring in thirty-one days and a hydrologist who did arithmetic with human lives and hated herself for it. She had a body she hadn't reported, upstream, drowned out of season. She said none of it.

"I have enough to finish the job," she said. "Which is why I'm calling. I need extraction. Two seats, provincial capital, quiet. Your usual route."

"Two." Another pause. The ice clinked again. "The manifest I have says one."

"The manifest can be amended."

"It can. For a price. And here we arrive at my favorite part of any conversation, Petra, so pour yourself something if you have it." She heard him settle back into whatever chair he owned that cost more than her father's compass had ever been worth. "The situation has developed while you've been playing river guide. The consortium has posted a new number. Not for the team — nobody cares about them anymore, the audit failed on paper weeks ago. For you. For your location specifically. Alive preferred, but the preference has a discount attached."

"How much?"

"Double what they were paying when you started. Which was already obscene." He recited the figure, and it landed in Petra's chest like a dropped anchor. It was more than the finder's fee. It was more than the finder's fee and the debt combined. It was, she realized with a cold professional clarity, roughly the price of a life in this part of the world, priced by people who had done this math before and found it agreeable.

"They want me stopped."

"They want the case stopped. You're just where the case lives." Vuthy's voice softened into something almost paternal, which was his most dangerous register. "Which brings me to my offer. Sit down if you're standing."

"I'm sitting."

"Liar. Here it is. You give me the case. Tonight, tomorrow, whenever you can reach one of my couriers — there's a fuel depot at Ban Tha Rua, third pier, ask for the man who sells ice. You hand over the samples and the logs, I wire you the full finder's fee plus twenty percent hazard bonus, plus I arrange your extraction alone, clean passport, no questions, and I make one phone call that takes the consortium's price off your head. Everyone eats. The team stays missing, the permit expires quietly, the mine keeps employing nine hundred people, and you walk away solvent for the first time in three years." He let it breathe. "Petra. You could go home."

Home. The word did what he'd built it to do. She saw the client's family's lawyer's face, the number on the settlement letter, the way her own name looked in the blacklist notices — LOCKRIDGE, P., suspended privileges, both jurisdictions, pending restitution. She thought about a debt measured in a dead man's years, and a payout that would erase it, and a door opening after three years of walls.

Then she thought about Anong washing her clothes in brown water every evening because it was the only thing left she controlled. About the village children Anong hadn't talked about yet but carried like ballast. About a survey leader floating in a river that hadn't been high enough to drown him.

"What happens to the data?" she asked.

"The consortium buries it. That's the entire transaction."

"And the contamination?"

"Continues," Vuthy said, without cruelty, which somehow made it crueler. "It has continued for six years. It will continue whether you take my money or not. That's the part you need to understand, Petra. You are not choosing between saving these people and not saving them. They were never yours to save. You are choosing between getting paid and getting nothing. Between a future and a gesture." A sip, audible across four hundred kilometers of storm. "I've watched a lot of people make this choice. The ones who pick the gesture always describe it as freedom afterward. I've never been sure if that's conviction or cope."

"Which do you think?"

"I think you're stalling because you already know what you want to do and you hate it." He laughed, low and genuine. "That's why I like you. You're honest even when you lie."

* * *

Anong came back up the ladder while Petra was still holding the dead phone. She had a plastic basin of river water and a bar of gray soap, and she set them down and looked at Petra's face and read it the way she read gauge data — quickly, accurately, without flinching.

"He offered to buy it."

"Not buy. Acquire. He used softer words." Petra pocketed the phone. "He also told me there's a price on my head that would embarrass a cartel, and that he can make it go away if I hand over the samples and leave you on this river."

Anong sat down on the crate opposite her. Rain drummed the tarpaulin overhead, a sound Petra was beginning to think of as the country's native language. "What did you say?"

"I said I'd think about it."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only answer that keeps him talking. The moment he knows I'm refusing, I stop being a client and start being inventory." Petra rubbed her eyes. Her hands were cracked from bailing, salt-raw despite fresh water, and they ached in a way that felt earned. "He knew about the case, Anong. Before I mentioned it. Which means someone told him, and the list of someones is short."

Anong was quiet for a long moment. Rain filled the silence. "My brother," she said finally. "I called him from the karst camp, before the storm. Told him I was alive, told him to tell no one. He wouldn't sell me. But his wife's family leases land to the consortium's contractor. He may have said something without knowing what it was." Her jaw tightened. "Or maybe I'm wrong and I'm the one who led them to you. Both possibilities are mine."

"Stop doing that."

"Doing what?"

"Collecting blame like it's a wage. I've seen it before. It looks like humility. It's just another way of staying busy instead of acting." Petra stood, joints complaining, and looked down the river toward the capital she could not see. "Here's what matters. Vuthy made me an offer that solves every problem I walked into this country with. Debt, blacklist, the family, all of it. One phone call, one courier, one case." She turned. "And I'm not going to take it. So now you know, and now I know, and neither of us gets to pretend later that the choice was complicated."

Anong looked at her for a long time. Something moved behind her eyes — not gratitude, Petra noted. Recalculation. The look of a scientist updating a model.

"Why not?" Anong asked. "Honestly. Not the noble version."

Petra considered lying, decided she was too tired, and gave the true answer, which surprised her by existing. "Because my father ran these rivers for a mining company too, and he kept notes nobody ever read, and I spent thirty years thinking he was just a drunk with a compass. Then I found out he was mapping things other people wanted unmapped, and he never got paid for it, and it didn't matter, because the maps survived him." She shrugged. "I guess I'd rather be a map than a payout."

"That's the noble version wearing work clothes."

"Everything honest is." Petra zipped the phone back into its pouch, against her ribs, where it beat its small dead weight. "We move at first light. There's a sandbar eight kilometers down where we can patch the hull — she's taking water faster than I like, and I don't like running the lower gorge with a bilge full of hope."

Anong nodded and began folding her washed clothes with that same careful economy. Then, without looking up: "The man who sells ice at Ban Tha Rua. If we pass it, we pass it fast."

"If we pass it at all."

* * *

They cast off before dawn, into a rain that had lost its rhythm and settled into permanence. The longboat wallowed low in the stern, and Petra spent the first hour learning exactly how much water she shipped per minute and hating the number. By midmorning the sandbar appeared — a pale tongue of silt midstream, maybe sixty meters long, already shrinking as the river climbed. She grounded the bow, and they dragged gear above the waterline, and she turned the hull on its side to find the leak.

She found three.

One was a seam crack, manageable. One was around a drain plug someone had stripped, fixable with epoxy and a prayer. The third was a gouge along the keel line, old wood bruised dark around a fresh split, the kind of damage that announced itself politely now and catastrophically later. She sat back on her heels in the rain and did the math she'd been avoiding since the checkpoint.

"We can't run the gorge in this," she said.

Anong crouched beside her, hair plastered flat, watching the river rise around their island of silt with the detached attention of someone reading a chart of her own decline. "How long to fix it?"

"Six hours for the seams. The keel needs glass and resin and time to cure, and the humidity's wrong for it. Call it a day, if the river gives us a day." Petra looked upstream, then down. The water had come up a hand's width since they landed. "It won't give us a day."

"Then what?"

"Then we bail and we run and we pray to whatever god handles fiberglass." She was already unpacking the repair kit, laying out tools in the order her hands would need them, because order was the only prayer she trusted. "Get the food dry. Get the case dry. If the boat dies, the case swims with me, not with you — you're the chain of custody, I'm just the hull."

Anong started to argue, then caught Petra's expression and didn't. Instead she gathered the sample case against her chest and carried it up the sandbar to the highest point, a rise of silt barely wider than a grave, and set it down there, and stood over it, and Petra watched her and understood that she was looking at the exact center of both their lives now: a metal box on a drowning island, worth double a bounty and triple a conscience.

The rain thickened. The river climbed. And far downstream, in an office with ice in his glass, a man who hedged his bets was making a second phone call — this one not to Petra, and not about her rescue, and the fact that she would spend the next thirty hours bailing a dying boat without knowing it was the last mercy anyone would show her that week.
