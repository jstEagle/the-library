## 10. The Leaking Boat

The sandbar appeared out of the rain like a rumor — a pale crescent of mud and gravel maybe forty meters long, already shrinking as the river climbed its edges. Petra saw it first because she'd been watching for it for two hours, reading the water the way other people read faces. The current split around something shallow downstream, and where current splits, ground rises.

"There," she said. "Left of the dead tree."

Anong didn't argue. She hadn't argued about anything since the boat started taking on more water than they could bail, which was four hours ago, which was also roughly when the monsoon had stopped being weather and started being a fact of geography.

The longboat grounded with a grinding shudder that Petra felt in her back teeth. She was over the side before it settled, hauling the bow up onto the gravel by main strength while Anong bailed the last few inches out with a plastic rice scoop that had become, over two days, the most important object either of them owned.

"Higher," Anong said.

"I know it needs to be higher."

"It's still raining."

"I noticed." Petra dug her heels into the muck and walked the boat another three meters up the bar, until the keel sat on wet gravel above the wrack line of the last flood. Then she stood there a moment, hands on her hips, breathing hard, letting the rain hammer the back of her neck. "Okay. Okay. We're here."

"Where is here?"

Petra looked upstream, then down. The river ran wide and brown and fast, carrying whole trees past them at walking speed. On the far bank, jungle came down to the water like a crowd pressing against a barrier. Somewhere behind the rain, thunder moved around without committing to anything.

"Here," she said finally, "is dry enough."

* * *

They got the tarp rigged between an oar and a driftwood stump, a lean-to facing away from the wind, and dragged their packs under it. Petra built a fire from wood she'd stashed under the forward thwart all day — bone-dry teak offcuts scavenged from a logging landing two days back, wrapped in plastic, hoarded like ammunition. It caught on the third match. Anong watched it catch with an expression Petra could only describe as religious.

"We should look at the hull," Anong said, after she'd drunk half a liter of boiled river water and eaten most of a tin of sardines.

"After dark?"

"Sooner we look, sooner we fix it."

Petra tipped her head toward the boat. "Sooner we fix it, sooner we're back on that." She nodded at the river, at the brown freight-train current rolling past. "I'm not eager."

"You're scared."

"I'm *respectful*," Petra said. "There's a difference. Scared people make bad decisions on moving water. Respectful people wait for morning."

Anong almost smiled. It was the closest thing to humor Petra had gotten out of her since the checkpoint, and it lasted less than a second before the woman's face closed again, the way it always did, like a door pulled shut by someone standing inside it.

They turned the boat anyway. Better to do it in the last grey light than by firelight, and better to know.

The leak wasn't hard to find. The longboat was old construction — single trunk, adzed flat, patched in three places with fiberglass that some previous owner had applied with more faith than skill. One of those patches, aft of the midship thwart, had lifted along its lower edge, and beneath it the wood itself had gone soft, punky, drinking water through a crack that ran maybe thirty centimeters along a grain line. Every time they hit chop, the flex opened the crack and the river came in like it had been invited.

"How bad?" Anong asked.

Petra pressed her thumb into the soft wood. It gave like stale bread. "Bad enough that I wouldn't run rapids on it. Bad enough that I want glass and resin and six hours of dry weather, and I've got epoxy and a tarp."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only answer there is." She sat back on her heels. "We patch it tonight, we baby it tomorrow, we make the next town or we don't. If we don't, we walk."

"Walk where?"

"Out. That's what walking means."

Anong looked at her for a long moment, rain streaming off her chin, and said nothing, and went to get the epoxy kit from the pack.

* * *

The repair took two hours and both of them. Petra scraped and dried the seam with the camp stove held close, working in sections because the drizzle kept finding new ways to reach the wood. Anong mixed epoxy in a tin lid with the focused precision of someone who had spent a career measuring things — parts per million, milligrams per liter, thresholds of contamination below which children stopped getting sick and above which they didn't. Her batches were perfect. Petra laid the fiberglass cloth and worked the resin in with a spatula made from a cut-up fuel bottle, and the patch went down smooth and white over the scarred wood, and when it cured it would hold.

Probably.

They ate under the tarp after that. Rice and the rest of the sardines and a handful of dried chilies Anong produced from somewhere, because apparently hydrologists traveling incognito carried chilies the way other people carried rosaries. The fire hissed and spat in the rain. The river talked to itself all night long, that deep continuous conversation of a big waterway in flood, and every so often something large went by in the dark with a sound like a body turning over in bed.

Petra was on first watch. She sat at the edge of the tarp with the compass open on her knee, not checking anything, just holding it, and it was Anong who broke the silence, an hour in, without any preamble at all.

"I did the math," she said.

Petra waited. In her experience, when a person started a sentence that way at night, by a fire, after two days of silence, you let them finish it at their own speed.

"The night the team disappeared. Before it. That afternoon." Anong was looking at the fire, not at her. "Dr. Wichai — the team leader — he called me in at four o'clock. He had the sample case packed and sealed. Chain-of-custody forms signed, everything done properly, because Wichai was proper the way only a man who knows he's going to be disobeyed can be proper. And he said, *you take these downstream tonight. You go alone. You don't tell anyone.*"

"And you went."

"And I went." Anong turned her hands over, palms up, as if presenting evidence. "He told me the consortium's security chief had come to the camp that morning. Not threatening. Worse than threatening — friendly. Offering to help with logistics. Offering boats. Wichai understood what that meant faster than I did. He understood they were going to make the survey disappear, and that the only thing that mattered — the only thing — was that the data survive. So he gave me the case and he sent me downriver in the dark, alone, in a boat I barely knew how to handle, and I went."

She stopped. The fire popped. Downstream, far off, something fell into the water — a bank giving way, a tree, the river eating its own edges.

"Eleven people," Anong said. "There were eleven of them. Wichai and ten others. Surveyors, a geologist, two field techs, a cook, a young man named Kiet who carried the drill casings and sang Khmer pop songs badly to annoy everyone. Eleven. And I took the samples and I paddled away in the dark and I did not look back, because looking back would have been — " She searched for the word and didn't find one she liked. "Wasteful. That's what I told myself. Looking back would have been wasteful."

Petra said nothing. There was nothing yet to say.

"I got the case to the capital. I hid it. I hid myself. And every day since, I have done the arithmetic." Anong's voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "One case of water samples against eleven lives. Data against people. And every day the arithmetic comes out the same way, and every day I hate the answer, and every day I check it again hoping I made a mistake. Because if the data were worth eleven lives, then I'm the kind of person who believes data are worth eleven lives. And if they weren't, then I left them to die for nothing."

The rain thickened against the tarp. Petra fed a stick to the fire and watched it catch.

"Wichai made the choice," she said. "Not you."

"He made the choice about the case. I made the choice about me." Anong finally looked at her, and her eyes in the firelight were dry and very tired. "You weren't there. You don't get to hand me absolution. Nobody does. I've been carrying this for months and I will carry it for the rest of my life, and the only thing worse than having done it would be pretending I didn't choose it. I chose. I heard him say *go* and I went, and part of me — the honest part, the part I hate — was relieved. He was telling me I could live. He was making my cowardice sound like duty. And I let him."

* * *

Petra turned the compass over in her hands. Brass casing, worn to shine at the hinges, her father's initials scratched inside the lid where no one else would ever see them. She thought about the Andes. She thought about the rope.

"You want to hear my arithmetic?" she said.

Anong said nothing, which was answer enough.

"Four years ago I had a client on a fixed line above a col in the Cordillera Blanca. Name doesn't matter anymore; the family knows it better than I do. He froze up at altitude — happens to strong people, happens to the best of them — and we were behind schedule, and weather was coming, and I made the call to keep moving instead of turning back. My call. My schedule. My ego dressed up as judgment." She kept her eyes on the fire. "He fell. I held the rope. For a while. Long enough to feel it. Not long enough."

The words came out flatter than she'd intended, worn smooth from repetition, but underneath the flatness the old weight sat exactly where it always sat.

"They barred me from Peru. Then Bolivia, when I tried to work there under a different company's banner. His family sued, and the settlement broke me, and I've been paying it off ever since — which is why I'm sitting on a sandbar in the monsoon chasing a finder's fee instead of guiding rich tourists up things that don't fall down." She paused. "But here's the arithmetic part. Here's the part I've never said out loud, because saying it makes it real."

Anong waited.

"In the moment. When he fell. There was a half-second — less than a second — where I calculated. Where some cold little accountant in the back of my skull said: if you let go of this rope, you can arrest your own slide. If you let go, you live. And I didn't let go. I want that on the record, I need that on the record, I have built my entire remaining self out of the fact that I didn't let go." Petra's jaw tightened. "But the accountant spoke. The calculation happened. And I can't un-know that it happened. I can't un-know that there's a version of me one impulse thinner than the one that held on. So when you say you did the math — I know. I know exactly what you mean. The math isn't the crime. The math is just the thing that tells you what you're made of, and sometimes you don't like the answer, and you spend the rest of your life re-running it."

The fire burned low. Anong reached over and fed it two sticks, carefully, the way she did everything.

"So which is it?" she asked quietly. "Are we the calculation, or are we the moment after? The half-second, or the grip?"

Petra considered the question honestly, because the woman deserved honesty and because lying to yourself on a sandbar in the rain was a waste of good rain.

"I think," she said, "we're whatever we do next. That's the only arithmetic that hasn't been run yet."

Anong looked at her for a long time. Something in her face shifted — not softening exactly, more like a load being redistributed, weight moving from one shoulder to the other where it could be borne a little longer.

"Wichai trusted me with the truth," she said. "That's why he sent me. Not because I was fast, or quiet. Because I was the only one who understood what the numbers meant. What they meant for the villages downstream. For the kids." Her voice caught on the word and steadied. "So the case matters. The data matters. But I've decided something, out here, listening to this river. If it comes down to it — if it comes down to the case or a person, a real person, in front of me — I'm not doing that math again. I'm not choosing the file over the flesh. Never again. Whatever it costs."

"Even if it costs the whole reason we're here?"

"Especially then." She said it simply, without drama, the way you state a law of nature. "You can rebuild a case. You can resample a river. You can't resample a person."

Petra nodded slowly. Outside the tarp, the river kept rising — she could hear it, could hear the bar getting smaller, the water climbing the gravel with small patient sounds, the way it had climbed everything else in this country for ten thousand years.

"Sleep," she said. "I'll wake you at two. We run at first light, easy water only, and we make the provincial landing by tomorrow night if the boat holds."

"And if it doesn't hold?"

"Then we swim," Petra said, "and we're glad the water's warm."

Anong almost smiled again. Then she rolled into her sleeping bag, and within minutes her breathing slowed into the heavy rhythm of a person who had finally set something down, even if only for a few hours.

Petra took the watch. The fire sank to coals. Around midnight, between one roll of thunder and the next, she heard something she didn't like: an engine. Far off, upstream, running slow with the throttle feathered — not the sound of a fisherman, who runs wide open and doesn't care who hears, but the sound of a boat searching a bank in the dark.

It passed. Faded. Went wherever it was going.

Petra sat very still with the compass in her lap and watched the black water, and did not wake Anong, and did not sleep either.
