## 11. Checkpoint

The rain came in sheets now, not showers, and the river had gone the color of milky tea. Petra crouched under the tarp they'd rigged over the longboat's midsection, a survey document flattened against her knee, and studied the letterhead like it was a lock she intended to pick.

"You can't be serious," Anong said.

Petra didn't look up. "Which part?"

"Any of it. You're going to forge government permits. On a boat. In a monsoon."

"I'm going to *adapt* existing documents." Petra turned the page. The survey team's paperwork was beautiful — real stamps, real signatures, a provincial authorization for water sampling along forty kilometers of the Nam Theun tributaries, valid through the end of the concession audit window. Whoever had staged that camp's disappearance hadn't thought to burn the filing cabinet. Bureaucrats always signed everything. It was the one universal law of expeditions.

"The names are wrong," Anong said. "The team is dead or disappeared. If anyone checks—"

"Nobody checks in the rain." Petra fished the ballpoint out of her jacket, clicked it, and held it over the signature line. Her hand was steady. Her hands were always steady; it was the rest of her that shook. "But if someone does check, we're two contractors doing follow-up sampling under an authorized survey. Which is true, more or less. I'm following up. You're sampling."

"Under a dead man's authority."

"Under a missing man's authority," Petra said. "There's a difference, and right now it's the only difference we've got."

Anong pulled her knees up under the tarp and watched the river slide past, brown and swollen, carrying whole trees downstream at walking pace. She'd been quiet since the sandbar, since the confession about the arithmetic of leaving her team behind to save the data. Petra recognized the silence. It was the same one she'd carried out of the Andes — the sound of a person renegotiating terms with themselves.

"The checkpoint's at the confluence," Anong said finally. "Ban Houayxay side. There's a military post there during flood season. They stop every boat going downriver."

"How do you know?"

"Because I helped write the flood protocol that put it there." A bitter half-smile. "Before I understood what the protocol was actually protecting."

Petra looked at her. Rain drummed on the tarp. Somewhere upstream, thunder rolled along the valley floor like a barrel being dragged over stone.

"What else did you help write?"

"Contingency plans. Emergency response frameworks. A whole architecture of paperwork designed so that when something went wrong in those mountains, the response would be slow enough and confused enough that nothing would ever be confirmed." Anong's voice was flat, reciting. "I thought I was building safety systems. I was building alibis with a budget line."

Petra went back to the document. She worked quickly: corrected the crew manifest, added two names in handwriting she made deliberately worse than the original clerk's, adjusted the dates forward. Forgery, done properly, wasn't about artistry. It was about plausibility. Documents got forged by people in a hurry, by clerks working late, by guides whose clients changed plans mid-river. Perfect documents were suspicious. Tired documents were believed.

She paused over one blank field — purpose of travel — and wrote *follow-up sampling, sites 7 through 12* in careful Lao script she'd copied from the original. Then she sat back.

"There," she said. "We're boring."

"Boring gets people waved through."

"Boring keeps people alive." Petra folded the papers into a waterproof sleeve and tucked it inside her jacket, against her ribs. "How far to the confluence?"

"Two hours, maybe less with this current." Anong glanced at the sky, then at the water lapping over the gunwale. "The boat's taking on again."

"I'll bail. You steer."

They traded places without discussion, and that was new too — three days ago Petra would have insisted on the tiller, on controlling the thing that controlled their lives. But Anong read water differently than she did. Where Petra saw obstacles, Anong saw behavior. She'd spent fifteen years learning what rivers did with contamination, with sediment, with time, and she steered the way a doctor moves through a crowded ward: knowing exactly which body to avoid.

Petra bailed with a cut-down jerry can and watched the banks go by. The forest had drowned at its edges, trees standing knee-deep in their own reflection. A village roofline slid past, then another, all of them quiet, all of them pulled back from the water like animals scenting something wrong.

It was wrong. She could feel it the way she felt weather changes in her teeth. Three days since the sandbar. Two days since Vuthy's voice on the satellite phone had gone carefully neutral, telling her the consortium had doubled the price on her location, offering to buy the sample case himself before the line crackled and died. Since then: nothing. No extraction arranged. No callback. And a silence from a man like Vuthy wasn't silence. It was a transaction completing somewhere out of sight.

"They'll have radios at the checkpoint," Anong said over the engine noise. "If Serey's people have called ahead—"

"Then we're boring contractors with legitimate papers."

"If they've called ahead with a *description*—"

"Then I'll do the talking, and you'll cough and look official and foreign-adjacent, and we'll find out how good my paperwork is." Petra dumped another canful of river over the side. "One more thing. If it goes bad — if they separate us, if anyone puts hands on you — you give them the case."

Anong's head snapped around. "What?"

"The case. You give it to them."

"It's the entire point of—"

"It's a decoy." Petra met her eyes. "I split the samples last night while you slept. Half are in the case. Half are in the repair kit, wrapped in plastic, inside the hull patching. If they take the case, they take half a truth and we're still holding the other half. If they don't take it, great. Either way, nobody dies for a box today."

Anong stared at her for a long moment. Rain ran off the brim of her hat.

"You planned to fail," she said slowly.

"I planned to survive failing. It's a different skill." Petra went back to bailing. "My father taught me both."

* * *

The confluence announced itself as a widening, a place where the tributary gave itself up to the main river and the current went slack and confused, eddying in great slow circles. The checkpoint sat on the near bank where the road came down to meet the water: a concrete platform, a corrugated shelter, a wooden barrier arm across the boat landing, and four figures under the shelter wearing the dark green of river police.

Two boats ahead of them were already stopped. Petra watched through the rain as a policeman climbed aboard the first, poked through tarps, exchanged words with the boatman, and waved it through. Routine. Slow. Thorough enough.

"Kill the engine," she said quietly. "Drift in. We look like we're arriving, not running."

Anong throttled down and the longboat wallowed into the eddy, riding the lazy current toward the barrier. Petra took position amidships, papers in hand, and arranged her face into the expression she'd worn through a hundred border posts on three continents: tired, cooperative, faintly annoyed by procedure. The face of a woman who had been rained on for days and wanted it to be over.

The barrier arm stayed down.

A young policeman stepped onto the landing, boots splashing, and raised a hand. He couldn't have been more than twenty-two, wet to the shoulders, miserable. Behind him, an older officer — heavier, unhurried, with the particular stillness of a man who had learned that patience was a weapon — stood under the shelter and watched.

"Papers," the young one said.

Petra handed up the sleeve. "Follow-up sampling, sites seven through twelve. Survey authorization's in front."

He opened the folder. Water dripped off his cap onto the pages. He frowned at them, and Petra watched his eyes move and catalogued what he was seeing: the real stamp, the forged additions, the deliberately clumsy ink. He looked at the older officer. The older officer didn't move.

"This survey," the young policeman said. "The mineral concession survey?"

"The environmental audit attached to it," Petra said. "Water quality. We're downstream contractors."

"Downstream." He said the word like he was tasting it. "From where?"

"Upstream." She let a beat pass. "You want me to point at it, or can we skip geography?"

Something flickered behind his eyes — not suspicion exactly, more the discomfort of a young man caught between a script he'd been given and a woman who wouldn't perform her part in it. He flipped to the second page. The manifest. Two names. He looked up at Anong, hunched at the tiller under her hat, and then back at the papers.

"This name here. Dr. Anong Srisuk. There was a hydrologist by that name attached to the Sen survey."

"Yes," Petra said.

"She's listed as missing."

"Then your list needs updating." Petra kept her voice level, bored, just short of rude. "She was evacuated downstream with samples before the camp closed. Chain-of-custody requirement. Check page three — the custody transfer is documented."

There was no custody transfer on page three. There was a page three, and it had writing on it, and Petra had written it herself at five that morning in handwriting modeled on the team leader's — a man she'd never met, dead in the water upstream, staged as a drowning. She felt the lie sit in her chest like swallowed stone.

The young policeman turned to page three.

Behind him, the older officer finally moved. He came down the platform steps without hurry, took the folder from his subordinate's hands, and read it himself. Rain fell on the pages. He read for a long time. Long enough that Petra counted her own heartbeats and got to thirty-one.

"You're the guide," he said. Not a question.

"Contracted guide and boat operator."

"Name?"

"Lockridge. Petra Lockridge."

He looked at her then, fully, and she saw it happen — the small recalibration, the flicker of recognition, the mental cross-reference of a foreign name against some list he'd either been given or remembered. His face gave away almost nothing. Almost. There was a tightening at the corner of his mouth, the way a man's mouth tightens when he's decided something and is deciding whether to say it.

"Lockridge," he repeated. "Like the guide who lost the climbers in Peru."

The rain filled the silence. Anong had gone very still at the tiller. Petra felt the moment tilt beneath her like a boat taking water — the exact weight of it, the exact direction it wanted to roll.

"That guide's record was sealed," she said. "So either you know something you shouldn't, or you read the same rumors I did."

The older officer smiled. It didn't reach anywhere near his eyes. He handed the folder back to the young policeman.

"The papers are in order," he said. "Raise the barrier."

"Sir—" the young one started.

"The papers are *in order*, Kham." He turned back to Petra, and for one moment the stillness in him sharpened into something that was almost communication, almost warning, a message passed between two people standing on opposite banks of the same river. "Flood season is dangerous. So many things wash downstream this time of year. Bodies. Documents. Old mistakes." He inclined his head slightly. "I would get off the water soon, if I were you. Before the next checkpoint. There won't be a next checkpoint that lets you through."

Petra held his gaze. "That sounds like advice."

"It sounds like weather." He stepped back up onto the platform. "Go."

The barrier arm rose. The young policeman — Kham — stepped aside, and his eyes followed Petra as she returned to the boat, and there was something in them she couldn't file neatly: not hostility, not pity. Recognition, maybe. One person in uniform looking at another person who used to wear one, metaphorically speaking, and understanding exactly how uniforms get taken away.

Anong pushed the throttle and the longboat swung out into the main current, and the checkpoint shrank behind them into the rain.

Neither of them spoke for a full kilometer. The river carried them fast now, wide and brown and relentless, and the engine's drone filled the space where words should have been.

"He knew," Anong said finally.

"He knew."

"Not from our papers. From a list. Someone called ahead with a list, and he let us through anyway."

Petra bailed once, twice, mechanically, thinking. The older officer's words replayed themselves: *so many things wash downstream this time of year.* It hadn't been a threat. Threats were for people you intended to stop. That had been a man showing her the edge of a map — telling her, in the only language available to a policeman inside a machine he couldn't fight, exactly where the machine ended and the wilderness began.

"No," she said slowly. "Someone called ahead with a description, and he decided we weren't his problem. Or—" she paused, the thought assembling itself, unwelcome and cold, "—or someone higher than him decided stopping us here was messier than letting us run. Let us come to them downstream, where there aren't witnesses. Where there's a compound on the river with a private dock and no barrier arm at all."

Anong's hands tightened on the tiller. "Serey's territory starts below the gorge."

"Then we have until the gorge." Petra looked upstream, at the gray wall of rain moving down the valley toward them, thicker than any weather she'd seen yet, swallowing the ridgelines whole. The monsoon wasn't coming anymore. It was here, and it was early, and it was angry. "And I don't think we're going to like what's waiting at the bottom of it."

The first drops of the real storm hit the tarp like gravel, and somewhere behind them, faint under the engine noise, Petra thought she heard the low growl of a second engine — bigger, faster, and gaining.
