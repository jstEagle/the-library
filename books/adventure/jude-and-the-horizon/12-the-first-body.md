## 12. The First Body

They found the boat first.

It was wedged in the roots of a fallen fig tree a kilometer above the confluence, hull split along one seam, half-full of black water and leaves. A survey boat — white polyethylene, government stencil faded to a ghost. PETRA read the registration number twice before it meant anything. It matched the manifest she'd photographed in the abandoned camp, the one Anong had recited from memory without looking up from her bail bucket.

"Kamol's," Anong said. She'd gone very still in the bow, one hand on the gunwale, the other still holding the coffee can they used to scoop water. "That's Kamol's boat."

"You're sure."

"I patched that seam myself in March." Her voice was flat, careful, the voice of someone holding a door shut against something heavy. "There was a rock at Ban Hat Ngio. He laughed about it. Said the river had taken its tax."

Petra eased the longboat alongside the wreck and killed the engine. The river here ran slow and brown-green under the canopy, the kind of water that looked calm and wasn't, the kind that ate boats and kept them. Rain dripped off the fig leaves in fat, irregular drops. Somewhere upstream a bird made a sound like a hinge.

"Boats don't drift upstream," Petra said quietly.

Anong didn't answer. She was looking at the bank.

The ground rose steeply from the waterline, terraced by roots, and forty meters up, half-hidden behind a curtain of fern, there was a shape that didn't belong to the forest. Petra saw it the way she saw everything wrong with terrain — not as an object but as a break in pattern. Too pale. Too symmetrical. Too still.

"Stay with the boat," she said.

"No," Anong said, and climbed out.

* * *

The man had been laid out on his back.

Not dumped — *laid*. Arms at his sides, palms down, legs straight, head turned slightly toward the river as if he'd been watching it when he went. Someone had arranged him with care, or with the kind of care that passes for it in people who have done this before and want it over quickly. His shirt was buttoned. His boots were on. His glasses were folded in his breast pocket.

He'd been dead for weeks. The monsoon humidity had done what heat does — accelerated everything, softened the lines of him, sunk the face back toward the skull. But the bones of him were still legible: a big man once, broad through the shoulders, weathered hands, a surveyor's calluses. A laminated ID card on a lanyard around his neck had fused to his skin. Anong knelt and worked it free with two fingers and did not flinch, though her breath came out of her in a long controlled stream through her nose.

"Kamol Srisawat," she said. "Team leader. Fifty-one years old. Two daughters in Khon Kaen." She held the card so Petra could see the photo — a round-faced man squinting into sun, smiling like someone who liked his work. "He taught me how to calibrate the turbidity sensors. He brought mangoes to every site visit. He used to say data without context is just noise, so you have to know the river like a neighbor."

Petra crouched on the other side of the body and let herself look properly, because that was the job now, and because Kamol Srisawat deserved someone who would look.

The staging was good. That was the first thing she registered, professionally, coldly, before the grief could get its hooks in. Good enough to pass at a glance. Drowned men wash downstream; this man lay near the river; ergo, drowned. The clothes were wet-rotted in the way clothes get after weeks of rain. There was silt in the creases of his sleeves. Whoever had done it had even dragged him partway through shallow water — there were scrape marks in the mud below the bank, a furrow leading up from the waterline to where he lay.

But staging is a story, and stories have seams.

"Look at his hands," Petra said.

Anong looked. The fingers were intact, unmarked. No abrasions. No broken nails. No bark or grit driven under them.

"If he came down that bank alive — even dead, if he was rolled down it — he'd have grabbed something. Instinct. Everyone grabs." Petra pointed at the furrow in the mud. "And that drag mark goes uphill. Water doesn't carry things uphill. They pulled him out of the shallows and up here to pose him."

Anong's jaw tightened. "Maybe he drowned somewhere else and they moved him."

"Then why move him at all? If he drowned, leave him in the river. Rivers do the work for free. You don't haul a corpse forty meters into the ferns and lay him out straight unless you need him found — found *here*, found looking like an accident, found close enough to the last known camp to close the file." Petra stood, knees cracking, and scanned the ground around the body in slow arcs, reading it the way she'd read a snowfield for avalanche sign. "And there's something else. Something worse."

She walked a tight spiral, eyes down. The leaf litter was deep and matted, months of monsoon compressed into a wet brown carpet, but underneath it the soil told its story to anyone who spoke it. She found what she was looking for six meters from the body: a depression in the litter, old, collapsed at the edges, with the particular geometry of a tent floor. And beside it, half-buried, a rusted ring pull from a ration tin. And beside *that* —

"Fire pit," she said. "Old fire pit. Stones stacked. Look at the scorch under them."

"So he camped here."

"He camped here *before the rains*. This litter fell after the fire was built — see how the needles lie over the ash, undisturbed? That fire hasn't been touched since the dry season. Weeks before the monsoon broke. Maybe two months." Petra turned and looked at Anong, and made herself say it plainly, because plainness was a mercy. "The flood season started eleven days ago. Everything upstream has been running high since then. If Kamol drowned in this river during the floods, his body would have been carried kilometers downstream, snagged, buried in silt, shredded. Instead he's lying on dry ground next to a campsite he made before the rains ever came."

The silence between them filled up with rain-drip and river-hiss.

"He died before the flood season," Anong said. Her voice cracked on the second word and she steadied it. "He died before the water could have killed him."

"And somebody dressed him in a drowning and left him for the river to take the blame." Petra crouched again, closer, and gently turned Kamol's head with a gloved hand. Under the matted hair at the base of the skull there was a depression in the bone, a single blunt concavity, the size and shape of the business end of a pry bar or a heavy flashlight. One strike. Clean. From behind, from above — from someone standing while he sat, maybe, at that fire, eating, talking about turbidity sensors and mangoes.

"There," she said, and showed Anong, because Anong needed to see it, needed it to be undeniable, needed the door she'd been holding shut to finally swing open so the weight behind it could come out.

Anong made a sound. Not a scream. Something smaller and worse — a short exhalation, almost a laugh, the sound a person makes when arithmetic finishes and the total is unbearable. She sat back on her heels in the wet ferns and pressed the heels of both hands against her eyes.

"I sent the samples downstream," she said. "He told me to go. He said — he said *one of us should live to file it*. He put the case in my hands and he said the rest of them would follow on the morning boat, and I got on that boat, and I—" She stopped. Started again, lower. "I did the math. I've been doing the math for two months. Six lives against one dataset. I keep telling myself it was his decision, not mine. But I was the one who paddled away."

Petra had nothing to offer that. She knew that country. She lived there. Every guide who'd ever lost someone on a rope she tied knew the arithmetic of hindsight, the endless re-running of a moment already spent. So she did the only thing that helped: she stayed. She knelt in the ferns beside a dead stranger and a weeping woman and she stayed, and the rain came harder and she put her shell over both their heads and let the minutes be long.

When Anong finally lowered her hands, her face was finished — not calm, but resolved, the way ore looks after smelting.

"We bury him," Anong said.

"Not yet." Petra hated saying it. "I'm sorry. But if we dig here, we disturb the scene, and this scene is evidence. This is the proof, Anong. This is the thing that turns *missing persons* into *murder*. The wound, the pre-flood campsite, the staged position — a forensic team reads this ground like I read a snowfield. We document everything, we photograph everything, we mark the location, and then we bring people back here who can do this properly. His daughters deserve a real answer, not a hole in the jungle."

Anong stared at her for a long moment. Then she nodded, once, and took out her phone — the cheap waterproof one, the one she guarded like a relic — and began to photograph. Petra watched the treeline while she worked, because whoever had staged this body had been thorough, and thorough people sometimes came back to check their work.

* * *

They documented for two hours in the rain.

Petra shot the site from eight angles with the camp camera she'd salvaged from the survey tents — the same camera, she realized with a small cold jolt, that had probably hung around Kamol's neck. She measured the skull wound against a pencil for scale. She flagged the drag furrow, the fire pit, the collapsed tent depression, the tin pull. Anong logged GPS coordinates three times and wrote them in her field book in pencil, then traced the pencil in ink, then copied them onto a strip of tape inside her shirt collar, which struck Petra as exactly the behavior of someone who had learned, recently and painfully, that things get taken away from you.

They wrapped Kamol in his own tent fly, which they found stuffed in a root hollow twenty meters on — another seam in the story, Petra noted, because killers rarely bother to pack away the victim's shelter. Anong tucked the folded glasses back into his pocket. She put the mango pit she found near the fire pit into her sample bag, which made no sense and complete sense at once.

"He'd have thought that was funny," she said. "Evidence-wise."

They carried him down to the boat together, taking the weight on a pole through the fly's loops, and the going was brutal — slick clay, root ladders, the dead man heavier than living men are, the way the dead always are. At the steepest pitch Petra's boot skidded and she dropped to one knee with the pole across her shoulders and felt, absurdly, her father's compass digging into her chest through her jacket, hard as a knuckle, and heard his voice from thirty years and another continent away: *the dead don't need you to hurry, Lior. They need you to be exact.*

They loaded him amidships, under the tarp, weighted with stones from the bank so he wouldn't shift. Anong sat beside the tarp the whole way downriver with one hand resting on it, not praying, or maybe praying, or maybe just keeping watch, which is its own kind of prayer.

Petra ran the boat. The river had come up overnight — another half meter of brown muscle, whole trees riding the current like battering rams — and she worked the eddy lines with more attention than the water strictly required, because attention was easier than thinking. But thinking happened anyway, in the background, the way it always did.

Someone had killed Kamol Srisawat before the monsoon. Before the flood season. Which meant the timeline everyone was working from — the consortium's timeline, the police timeline, probably Vuthy's timeline — was a fiction. The team hadn't vanished during some disaster. They'd been murdered in the dry season, methodically, and the river had been recruited afterward to launder the deaths. Which meant the others — the four still unaccounted for — were likely dead too, and had been all along, and every day she'd spent chasing tracks that didn't exist, she'd been walking through a landscape where the only tracks left were the ones the killers wanted found.

Her hand found the compass through her jacket. Held it.

*You're being paid to find them,* she reminded herself. *Found is found. Dead is an answer. Take the answer, take the fee, clear the debt.* That was the contract. That was the clean line.

But the contract didn't cover the fact that she now knew where a murder weapon had met a murder victim, and that knowledge was not property. It couldn't be sold. It could only be carried, or buried, and she had buried enough.

At the confluence they pulled into a side slough, a quiet green channel walled in by bamboo, and buried Kamol Srisawat after all — high on the bank, above any flood line Petra could read, under a fig tree with his boots on and his glasses in his pocket, because Anong insisted and because Petra, who had argued for procedure all morning, discovered she had no argument left against dignity. Anong spoke in Thai, low and fast, and then translated the last line for Petra without being asked:

"I told him we're going to finish the survey."

They marked the grave with a cut stake and recorded the coordinates, and then they stood in the rain for a minute, two women who owed the dead different debts, and got back in the boat.

* * *

It was nearly dark when they reached the stilt hamlet below the rapids where they'd planned to buy fuel, and it was there, in the blue hour, with the river roaring past the pilings, that the hamlet's headman came down to the water with a satellite phone cupped in both hands like something rescued from a fire.

"For the farang lady," he said. "From the capital. A woman calling. She says she knows your sister's number, so you will know she is true."

Petra took the phone with rain-cold fingers. "This is Lockridge."

"Lior?" The voice was older than she expected, worn smooth, careful. Margaret Chen's mother. The mother of the man who had fallen on the mountain in Peru while Petra held the other end of the rope. "Lior, I'm sorry to reach you this way. The lawyer gave me the number. I hope — I hope it's all right."

"It's all right," Petra said, and found she meant it, and found that meaning it hurt.

"I'll be quick, because I know calls cost." A breath. "We've been sent an offer. Through the lawyer. A foundation — a private settlement fund, they called it. They'll clear everything. The debt, the funeral costs, the compensation we never got. Everything, Lior. Twenty years of it, gone tomorrow." A pause, and in the pause Petra could hear the whole shape of what was coming, could feel it arriving like weather. "There's one condition. They said you're involved in something overseas. Some dispute. They asked us to ask you to come home. To stop whatever it is. That's all. Just — come home, and let it go."

Downriver, thunder walked along the ridgeline. Anong stood at the rail watching her, rain streaming off her hood, waiting.

"Mrs. Chen," Petra said carefully, "who gave your lawyer this offer? Did they say?"

"They said a company. A resources group." A small, apologetic laugh. "Isn't that funny? All these years nobody wanted to help us, and now suddenly a mining company wants to pay your debts."

Petra closed her eyes. The compass pressed against her sternum like a second heartbeat.

"Tell me what you want to do," she said. "Not them. You."

The line hummed. Somewhere in the capital, an old woman who had every right to hate her weighed mercy against a stranger's war.

"That's just it," Margaret Chen's mother said. "I called to ask you the same question."
