## 13. Duty Calls Home

The message came through the satellite phone at 4:17 in the morning, which was how Petra knew it was serious. Nobody called at 4:17 in the morning to say something kind.

She'd been sleeping on the sandbar with the phone zipped into her jacket, a habit from years of being the person other people's emergencies woke up. The screen's glow lit the underside of the tarp like a small blue moon. Anong slept three meters away, curled against her sample case the way other people slept against a lover, and Petra lay still for a long moment, watching the rain tick against the plastic, deciding whether she wanted to know.

She wanted to know. That had always been her problem.

The number was American. A Colorado area code. She knew it before she answered it, the way you know a scar by touch in the dark.

"Petra." Margaret Hale's voice. The mother of Daniel Hale, who had come to the Cordillera Blanca with a guidebook, a wedding ring he'd stopped wearing, and a body that was thirty pounds too heavy for what he'd asked his guide to do with it. "I hope I haven't woken you."

"You did."

"Good. Then you're not lying to me about where you are." A pause, the sound of ice shifting in a glass, ten thousand kilometers away. "I'm going to say something once, and then we're both going to pretend this conversation happened at a reasonable hour. Are you listening?"

Petra sat up under the tarp. Rain drummed. Downriver, somewhere in the dark, the consortium's boats were presumably doing whatever consortium boats did, but here there was only the sandbar, the river talking to itself, and a voice from a life she'd spent six years trying to pay off.

"I'm listening."

"The debt is forgiven."

The words didn't parse at first. They were English words, arranged correctly, and they made no sense. Sixty-two thousand dollars. Fourteen months of her Andes season fees, garnished for two years after that, then the private contracts she'd taken because no outfitter would touch her name — every dollar routed through a lawyer in Denver to a family that had never once cashed a check without sending back a note saying *this doesn't bring him back*. The debt wasn't money. It was the only structure left standing between Petra Lockridge and the woman who'd tied off the anchor incorrectly, or checked the forecast lazily, or whatever the inquiry had finally decided, in its gentle institutional way, had killed Daniel Hale.

"Say that again," Petra said.

"We're forgiving it. All of it. Effective today." Ice clinked. "There are conditions."

And there it was. Petra felt something in her chest settle — not relief, nothing so clean as relief, but the grim recognition of a shape she knew. Every kindness she'd ever been offered had arrived wearing a harness.

"Margaret—"

"He would have been forty-one this year." The voice didn't waver. It had the flatness of someone who had rehearsed this in front of a mirror until the mirror stopped working. "I've done the arithmetic, Petra. Fourteen years of payments, however long you live, however much you earn — you'll die owing us. That was never the point. I understand that now. What I want—" the ice shifted again, and Petra heard, beneath the flatness, the effort of a woman holding herself together with procedure, "—what I want is for my son's death to stop being a thing that happens to strangers in jungles."

The rain came harder. Somewhere down the sandbar, Anong stirred but didn't wake.

"There's a man named Vuthy," Margaret continued. "He contacted us. Through the lawyer. He told us where you are, what you're carrying, what it's worth, and who wants it buried. He told us you're sitting on evidence that could get you killed, and that the only reason you're still in that country is that someone is paying you to find dead men who were made dead."

Petra closed her eyes. *Vuthy.* Of course. The fixer's reach had always been longer than his arm; she'd known that when she pawned her watch to him, when she took his finder's fee, when she told herself the fee was just money and money was just a tool. He hadn't sold her out yet. He'd done something worse. He'd sold her *leverage* — handed the Hales the one currency that could buy her out of the game entirely.

"What do you want?" Petra asked. Her voice came out level. She was almost proud of it.

"Come home. Walk away from the case, from the samples, from the hydrologist, from all of it. Let the lawyers handle whatever happens over there — it isn't yours. You've carried enough. Come home, and the ledger closes. Not paused. Closed. You'll be free, Petra. Actually free, for the first time since the mountain."

*Free.* The word landed like a piton driven into bad rock. It held weight, and she hated how badly she wanted to trust it.

"And if I don't come home?"

The pause this time was longer. When Margaret spoke again, the flatness cracked, just once, just enough to show the wound underneath.

"Then I'll know that my son died so you could go on being brave, and I will spend what's left of my life making sure everyone knows it. The outfitters. The boards of inquiry. The journalists who like a redemption story." A breath. "I don't want to do that, Petra. God help me, I don't want to be that woman. But I have buried a child, and I have learned that grief makes you capable of anything, and right now I am asking you — as the last person who saw my son alive — to give me the only thing I have ever asked you for."

Which wasn't true, and they both knew it. She'd asked for the truth once, at the inquiry, and Petra had given her the version that kept her own license revocable rather than revoked. That version had cost sixty-two thousand dollars and fourteen years. This call was the interest coming due.

"I need time," Petra said.

"You have until the permits expire. Forty days, Vuthy says. Thirty-eight now." The glass clinked a final time. "You always were good with a countdown. It's why I hired you."

The line went dead.

* * *

Petra didn't move for a long time. The rain settled into its monsoon rhythm, patient and total, and the river rose around the sandbar with the slow confidence of something that intended to win.

Thirty-eight days. The same clock the consortium ran on. Vuthy had synced them — the Hales' ultimatum and the miners' deadline, two hands on one dial. That wasn't coincidence. Vuthy didn't deal in coincidences; he dealt in pressure, applied at joints, and he'd found hers.

Because here was the thing she couldn't say to Margaret Hale: the debt wasn't a chain. It was an *excuse*. For six years, every choice had been pre-made for her — take the contract, run the river, keep moving, don't look at what you are without the paying-off to organize your days. If the debt vanished, she'd have to find out who Petra Lockridge was when nobody was owed anything. And if she walked away from Anong now, she'd learn the answer to that question, and the answer would be the same person who'd given the Hales the softened truth at the inquiry. The person who survived by rounding down.

Anong shifted in her sleep and murmured something in Khmer, her hand finding the case strap without waking. On the sandbar across from her, the sample case sat under its rain fly like a small ark. Half-degraded samples. Field logs. A chain of custody that expired with the concession, that expired with the lab window, that expired with everything — including, now, Petra's forgiveness.

She thought about Daniel Hale on the moraine, the morning it happened, laughing about the coffee. She thought about the team leader upstream, drowned in a dry season. She thought about the children in Anong's village, the ones whose arms bent wrong, and Serey's reasonable voice offering triple the fee and a clean passport, and how she'd already refused that once, freely, and how refusing twice shouldn't be harder than refusing once but was, because this offer came wrapped in absolution instead of money.

Love against duty. Except nobody had told her which one was which.

The sky was graying at the edges when Anong woke. She looked at Petra's face and read it the way she read water levels — quickly, accurately, without flattery.

"Bad news," Anong said. Not a question.

"Family of a client I lost. Six years ago. They've forgiven my debt." Petra watched the river. "If I walk away. Today."

Anong was quiet for a moment. Then: "Will you?"

And Petra found that the honest answer was the only one she could give, because she'd spent this whole journey learning that rounded-down truths were how rivers like this one got poisoned in the first place.

"I don't know," she said. "Ask me again in thirty-eight days."

Anong nodded slowly, and began breaking camp, and neither of them mentioned it again — but Petra caught her, twice, looking at the sample case the way you look at something you've started guarding with your life, and Petra understood that the question hadn't been idle. Anong was doing her own arithmetic. Deciding whether the woman bailing water beside her was cargo or crew.

By midmorning they were on the water, the leaking boat riding low, the rain steady, the current faster than yesterday. Petra took the first bail shift and let her hands work while her mind ran the numbers it always ran: distance to the capital, hours of daylight, liters of bilge, days remaining.

Thirty-eight days to clear her name. Thirty-eight days to lose it forever.

The river bent east, toward the checkpoint she'd have to bluff past next, and Petra put her shoulder into the bail bucket and did not look back at the sandbar, where a phone call had offered her everything she'd spent six years drowning for — and where she'd left it sitting in the rain.
