## 14. Anong's Village

The river narrowed after the checkpoint, and the rain came in sheets so dense that Petra steered by sound — the slap of water on rock to starboard, the hollow knock of submerged logs she could feel through the hull before she heard them. Anong sat amidships with the sample case strapped across her chest like a child, one hand bailing when her arm remembered to.

"We should stop," Anong said around midday. "There's a village."

Petra didn't look up from the bilge. "Whose village?"

"Mine."

"You said your family wouldn't take you back."

"I said they might not." Anong wrung out the bail rag over the side. "There's a difference. My mother keeps a house on the left bank, past the fish traps. If anyone asks, we're traders running salt downstream. Don't say my name where the neighbors can hear it."

"Why not?"

"Because I'm the daughter who left," Anong said, "and the daughter who came back is a different question entirely."

* * *

The village announced itself before it appeared: a smell of woodsmoke and drying fish, then the creak of a footbridge, then dogs. The left bank rose into a shelf of red clay terraced with vegetable plots gone muddy and half-drowned. Stilt houses stood in rows along the high ground, their ladders slick with rain, laundry strung between them like signal flags nobody was reading.

Anong killed the engine two hundred meters out and let them drift in, which told Petra everything about how welcome an arriving motor would be. They poled the last stretch through the fish traps — bamboo weirs standing in the brown current like the ribs of drowned animals — and tied off under a house whose stilts were wrapped in old tires and prayer cloth.

A woman came down the ladder before they'd finished tying. Small, gray-haired, built like a hook, moving with the economy of someone who had never once wasted a step. She looked at Anong for a long moment without speaking, and Petra watched something pass between them that had no words in any language she knew: recognition, and grief, and a kind of guarded relief, all at once.

"Ma," Anong said.

"Your hair is short," the woman said. It wasn't a greeting. It was an inventory.

"It was easier underground."

The woman — Somchai, though everyone called her Mae Yai, Grandmother, because half the village had been raised in her kitchen — turned to Petra and studied her the way she'd studied her daughter, but faster, the way you study weather. "You eat?" she said finally.

"Yes."

"You lie. Come up."

* * *

The house was one large room above a storage floor, and it smelled of lemongrass, kerosene, and damp that no amount of burning charcoal would ever fully beat. A loom occupied one corner. Photographs hung crooked on the wall behind plastic sheeting, and Anong went still in front of them, staring at a picture of herself at maybe fifteen, school uniform, awkward smile, standing next to a brother Petra hadn't heard mentioned yet.

"He's downriver," Anong said quietly, catching Petra looking. "Kiet. He runs boats."

"You didn't mention him."

"There's a lot I haven't mentioned."

Mae Yai set out rice, dried fish, a sour soup heavy with tamarind that tasted like medicine in the best way. She ate standing up, watching them eat sitting down, which was its own kind of statement. When Anong reached for a third helping, the old woman said, "You were always hungry when you were hiding from something," and Anong put the bowl down and didn't pick it up again.

Petra ate slowly and listened. This was the part of the job nobody trained you for — being a guest inside someone else's unfinished argument. She kept her hands visible, kept her mouth full, and learned more in forty minutes than she had in four days of reading survey documents.

What she learned was this: Anong had left the village eleven years ago on a government scholarship, the first person from this bank of the river ever to get one. The village had held a feast. Her mother had sold a buffalo. And Anong had come back exactly twice since — once for her father's funeral, once for a week three years ago, when she'd arrived unannounced with testing kits and stayed nine days taking water samples from every well, every stream, every jar of stored rainwater in the district, and then left without explaining what she'd found.

"She took our water away in little bottles," Mae Yai said, still standing, watching. "And she did not tell us what was in them."

"I couldn't," Anong said. "If I'd said it out loud, the company would have known I was looking. They already had people here buying land."

"What did you find?"

Anong set down her spoon. For the first time since Petra had met her — at knifepoint, in a dead camp, in the dark — she looked directly at her mother and answered a hard question without hedging.

"Arsenic," she said. "And something else, a processing chemical, cadmium compounds. In the shallows below the mine tailings. In the wells on this side of the river. Not everywhere. Not yet. But in the places children drink from, because children drink from the shallow places."

Mae Yai didn't react visibly. Old women in river villages don't perform shock; they file things. But she stopped eating, and she didn't start again.

"And now?" she asked.

"Now I have proof with dates and seals on it," Anong said. "Now there are men upstream who want me dead for carrying it. Now I've come home because home is the only place on this river where nobody has been paid to look at me sideways."

Her mother looked at her for a long time. Then she said, "You will sleep in the back room. You will not go out after dark. And you will tell me, before you leave this house again, what you are going to do about it."

"That's why I'm here," Anong said.

"No," Mae Yai said. "You're here because it's raining. What you're going to *do* is a different thing. We'll see if they're the same thing."

* * *

It stopped raining near dusk, the way monsoon rain does — not ending but pausing, like a sentence with a comma in it. Petra walked the village path with Anong while the light went amber and the mud steamed. Dogs trotted ahead of them. Kids ran alongside for thirty meters, lost interest, ran back.

That's when Petra saw the first one.

A boy, maybe seven, standing in the doorway of the third house down, holding himself very straight, very deliberately, the way children hold themselves when their bodies have betrayed them and they're compensating. His legs were thin in a way that wasn't hunger — or wasn't only hunger. His skin had the gray-brown stippling of chronic arsenic exposure, the pattern Petra had seen once in a medical pamphlet Anong had shoved across a sandbar table during an argument about chain of custody. Keratotic patches on the palms. She'd thought, at the time, *clinical*. Standing in front of the boy, she understood what clinical meant. It meant a child's hands looking sixty years old.

He didn't run with the other kids. He watched them.

"His name is Noi," Anong said, and her voice had changed — flattened, careful, the voice of someone carrying something fragile. "His mother won't let him swim anymore. The other mothers followed. There hasn't been swimming in this bend for two years. Do you understand what that means, on a river like this? Children who don't touch the water. It's like teaching them not to breathe."

Petra watched the boy watch the river. The current moved past the bank, brown and fast and indifferent, and the boy stood at the exact distance his mother had drawn for him, an invisible line in the mud he clearly knew to the centimeter, because he'd tested it and been pulled back enough times.

"How many?" Petra asked.

"In this village? Eleven that I know of with visible symptoms. Probably more early-stage. Downstream, in the three districts past the confluence — hundreds. Maybe thousands. Nobody's counting, because counting requires a number somebody would have to explain."

They kept walking. At the end of the row, a girl sat on a porch doing homework by the last daylight, propped against her grandmother, and Petra noticed the girl's posture — leaning, always leaning, some deep fatigue baked into her frame that no eight-year-old should own. Anong stopped. Didn't speak. Just stood there for a moment, looking at her, and Petra realized she was looking at data. This was what happened to a scientist when the spreadsheet got up and walked around wearing a school uniform.

"The samples I sent downstream with the team leader," Anong said finally, as they walked back toward the stilt house in the blue dark, "were from these wells. From this bend. That's why I carried them instead of running clean. Every vial in that case is a child on this bank."

"You said half degraded in the heat."

"The ones in the outer tray. The core set survived. I packed them like they were going to war, because I knew they were." She paused at the ladder. "I need to show you something tomorrow. Upstream of the village, where the creek comes down. You should see it with your eyes, not my words."

"Is it dangerous?"

"Not to stand there. To know it exists and do nothing — that's the dangerous part."

* * *

Night in the house was a ledger of small sounds: rain starting again on the tin roof, the loom ticking as Mae Yai worked late, a gecko somewhere in the rafters making its territorial claim in loud punctuation. Petra slept on a mat in the main room, near the door, out of habit — first out, first to hear trouble. Anong slept in the back room, and Petra heard her mother close the door behind her, softly, the way you close a door on something you're not sure you can protect.

Around two in the morning, Petra woke to voices. Low, urgent, coming from the back room. She lay still and listened, not eavesdropping so much as standing watch — it was the same skill, and she'd never learned to turn it off.

Anong and her mother were arguing. Not shouting. Arguing the way people argue who love each other and have run out of polite moves: sentences getting shorter, silences getting longer, each silence a blow.

"—you knew," Mae Yai was saying. "Three years ago you knew, and you drove past this village on your way to the capital and you said nothing."

"If I'd said something—"

"You had a mouth. You had proof. You had a name that opened doors mine never will."

"I had a career I was trying not to burn down before the data was bulletproof. I needed the full survey. I needed the official audit to confirm—" 

"And the official audit disappeared." The old woman's voice cracked on the word, just slightly, the way dry bamboo cracks. "And my granddaughter's hair fell out in patches last year, and the doctor in the provincial town charged me two days' wages to tell me it was 'a common condition,' and I sat there thinking: my daughter bottles our water and sends it to Bangkok, and Bangkok tells me nothing is wrong with our water. Who do I believe?"

There was a long silence. Then Anong's voice, quieter, stripped of all its professional armor:

"You believe the vials. You believe what's in the ground. Ma — I was wrong. Not about the science. About waiting. I thought the system would work if I fed it correctly. I thought if the numbers were perfect, someone would act. I traded years for rigor, and the rigor died in a camp with six people, and the years cost—" her voice caught, steadied, "—cost whatever that is on your wall. Whatever Noi's hands look like now. I know. I've done the arithmetic every night for three years and it never comes out in my favor."

Another silence, longer. Then Mae Yai, and her voice had shifted into something Petra recognized from her own father, decades ago — the register adults use when the fight is over and the truth underneath it is finally allowed to stand up:

"Then don't come home to hide," she said. "Come home to finish. This house has buried two elders and a buffalo to the company's promises. If you carry that case out of here, carry it like it belongs to everyone on this bank. Because it does."

Petra heard the door open once, later, and footsteps cross the main room — Anong, unable to sleep, standing at the window looking downriver toward the checkpoint they'd bluffed through, and beyond it the provincial capital, and beyond that the lab where the surviving vials were either going to matter or weren't. She stood there a long time. Petra pretended to sleep and watched her silhouette against the rain-light, and understood that she was looking at the moment a woman decided something.

In the morning, Anong was different. Petra had seen it before in herself, on the mountain after the rescue that broke her — the specific recalibration of a person who has stopped negotiating with their own guilt and started spending it. Guilt as fuel. It burns dirty, but it burns hot, and it doesn't run out the way money does.

* * *

After breakfast — rice, dried fish, and tea brewed from something bitter that Mae Yai claimed prevented fever — Anong led Petra upstream on foot, along a path the villagers used to reach the far gardens. Twenty minutes through wet secondary forest and the canopy opened over a ravine, and the creek came down through it.

Petra had read the survey reports. She'd read Anong's field notes. She knew the numbers: turbidity, metal concentrations, exceedance factors. None of it prepared her for the color.

Below the point where a side channel drained down from the direction of the concession — from the mine, though you couldn't see the mine, only the road cut and the tailings pond's distant gleam — the creek ran a pale, milky ochre, braiding around rocks furred with orange sediment. Above the confluence, twenty meters upstream, the same creek ran clear over gray stone, cold and ordinary and alive. The line between the two was so sharp it looked deliberate, like someone had drawn it.

"Every rain event," Anong said, standing at the margin, not quite touching the water, "it flushes. The tailings pond has a liner with a tear in it — I documented it in year two, they patched it with a cheap membrane, it failed again within eighteen months. The permit says zero discharge. The reality is a slow IV drip into this watershed, dosed by rainfall." She pointed downstream, where the creek bent toward the village wells. "Twenty minutes at walking speed. That's the delivery route. That's the whole crime, right there. No conspiracy required for this part. Just gravity and a torn liner and a company that did the math on a patch versus a lawsuit."

Petra crouched at the clear water upstream and cupped her hands and drank, deliberately, ostentatiously, and then walked to the ochre stretch and didn't touch it, and made sure Anong saw her do both. It was a small theater. It was also the fastest way she knew to make a fact physical in her own body — the same reason she'd once eaten snow off a suspect avalanche slope to prove it was safe to a frightened client. Some truths you don't understand until your mouth has tasted both sides of them.

The contaminated water smelled faintly of iron and something industrial, something that didn't belong to any forest she'd ever worked. She thought about the boy, Noi, and his invisible line in the mud. She thought about a whole generation of kids on this bank learning the river as a threat before they learned it as a home.

"This is what the survey team was documenting when they vanished," Anong said. "This site. These readings. The leader wanted to sample the seep itself, at the source, with the consortium's own discharge numbers beside ours. That trip was scheduled for the week they disappeared." She looked down at the poisoned water running quietly toward the children. "They didn't vanish. They were removed from the schedule of history. And the creek kept flowing the entire time, like nothing happened, because creeks don't file reports."

Petra stood there longer than she needed to. Long enough for the anger to arrive — not the transactional anger of a guide protecting a contract, but the older kind, the kind that comes up from somewhere below the debt and the blacklist and the finder's fee, from the place where she was still, despite everything, her father's daughter, a person who believed rivers shouldn't be used as sewers and children shouldn't be used as margins in someone's quarterly spreadsheet.

She didn't say anything to Anong. She didn't need to. Something had shifted in the geometry between them, and they both felt it: yesterday they'd been allies of circumstance, two compromised women sharing a leaking boat. Today they were witnesses of the same thing, and witnesses have obligations that alliances don't.

On the walk back, Anong spoke once, quietly, without stopping.

"When we reach the capital, I'm not just delivering samples," she said. "I'm delivering names. Mine first."

"Your mother said something like that last night."

"My mother has been saying it for three years," Anong said. "Last night was the first time I heard it."

* * *

They spent the rest of the day at the house, and it was the strangest day of the trip so far, because nothing happened and everything changed. Rain, rice, mending — Anong re-caulked the boat's worst seam with resin Mae Yai kept for the fishing craft, working with a competence that surprised Petra until she remembered hydrologists live on boats too. Petra sharpened her knife on the doorstep and answered questions from village kids about the mountains she'd climbed, exaggerating nothing, letting the altitude figures do the impressing.

Late afternoon, Kiet came up from downriver — Anong's brother, mid-thirties, boatman's shoulders, a face like his sister's rearranged into someone harder to read. He tied off below the house, came up the ladder, and the room tensed the way rooms tense when a family member arrives who is also a variable.

He looked at Anong first. Then at the sample case, which sat against the wall where it always sat, and which he clearly knew the significance of, because he looked at it second and longest.

"So it's true," he said. "You're actually doing it."

"I'm actually doing it."

"The men from the company were in Ban Tha Nam last week," Kiet said. "Two of them, staying at the guesthouse, paying cash, asking about you. Asking which house is your mother's. The guesthouse owner told them nothing — he's Mae Yai's cousin. But they'll ask again, and not everyone on this river is related to us."

Anong nodded slowly. "I assumed."

"Assuming isn't planning." Kiet set down his bag. "Here's what I can give you. I know every channel, every cutoff, every log jam from here to the confluence. I can move you fast when you need to move, and I can lose anyone who follows, because I grew up losing fisheries officers in these channels. What I can't do is be seen helping you before you're past the confluence. After that, it doesn't matter what they know about me."

"Why not?" Petra asked.

"Because after the confluence," Kiet said, "the story stops being about a village and starts being about a province, and provinces are too big for the company to buy everyone who looks at them sideways. Down there, strangers help strangers all the time. Up here, everybody knows everybody, and knowing is a currency." He glanced at his mother, who was pretending to sort beans and listening to every word. "Also, I have a wife and two daughters in Ban Tha Nam, and one of them is eight, and I've seen what the water did to the eight-year-olds on this bank. So understand me correctly: I'm not helping you because I forgive my sister. I'm helping you because I want those men's bosses in a courtroom, and I want it while my girls still drink from a well I can fix."

Mae Yai sorted beans and said nothing, which from her was a benediction.

Petra looked around the room — the old woman, the brother, the returning daughter, the case against the wall — and felt the shape of the thing they were all standing inside. A village that had been paying, in children's skin and children's fatigue, for a concession signed in a capital none of them would ever visit. A crime so distributed it had become weather. And in the middle of it, a waterproof case holding the only arithmetic that could undo it.

That night, packing to leave at first light, Petra found a note tucked into her pack. Anong's handwriting, small and even, the handwriting of someone who labels vials for a living:

*If we don't make it, the case goes to Kiet. He knows a monk in the provincial town who owes Mae Yai a debt from 1998. Monks don't get bought.*

Petra read it twice and put it back without telling Anong she'd found it. Some preparations you honor by not acknowledging them.

At dawn the river was up another hand's width, brown and muscular, carrying branches past the fish traps like a crowd moving somewhere in a hurry. Mae Yai came down the ladder to see them off and pressed a bundle into Petra's hands — sticky rice wrapped in banana leaf, enough for two days, plus a small amulet she didn't explain and Petra didn't ask about.

"Go past the confluence," the old woman said. "Past it, you're a story. Before it, you're a target."

Anong hugged her mother briefly, stiffly, the way people hug when the hug contains an apology too large for the gesture. Then she stepped into the boat, and Petra pushed off, and the current took them.

Halfway across the bend, Petra glanced back once. On the bank, small against the rising water, stood the boy named Noi, watching them go — watching two strangers carry his well water away in glass vials toward a city he'd never seen, standing exactly at the edge of the line his mother had drawn for him, at the exact boundary of the water that had already gotten into him.

Petra looked at him until the bend closed over the view, and then she put her back into the oars and did not look at the river ahead the way she usually did — scanning, calculating, reading the water for profit and passage.

She looked at it the way Anong had taught her to, in one day, in one village.

As something worth saving.
