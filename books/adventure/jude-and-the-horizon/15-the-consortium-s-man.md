## 15. The Consortium's Man

He arrived the way bad news always arrived in the village: by boat, unhurried, with more politeness than the river deserved.

Petra saw him first from the stilt house where she'd spent three nights sleeping badly on a woven mat, her body still cataloguing its complaints — the ribs the flood had bruised, the shoulder that clicked now when she reached above her head. Down at the landing, a longboat the color of wet slate slid out of the morning mist with an outboard running so quietly it was more suggestion than sound. The man at the bow wore a linen shirt the color of nothing, pressed despite the humidity, and he stepped onto the mud like a man stepping into a hotel lobby.

"He's early," Anong said behind her.

"You know him?"

"I know the type." Anong set down the bowl of rice she'd been carrying and looked at it as if it had betrayed her. "That's how they come. Never with guns first. Guns are what they send when the polite one fails."

Petra watched the boat tie off. Two crew stayed aboard — big men in dry shirts, which was itself a kind of uniform out here, where everyone else's clothes had surrendered to the rain weeks ago. The polite man climbed the ladder to the stilt house alone, and he climbed it well, no wasted motion, no fear of the slick rungs. Someone who'd grown up around water, or trained hard enough to fake it.

"He'll want to talk to you," Anong said. "Not me. I'm the problem. You're the price."

"And you're going to hide?"

"I'm going to sit with my niece." Anong's voice did something Petra hadn't heard from her before — went flat and old. "If he sees me, he'll know the samples are real. If he doesn't, you can tell him I'm a rumor. Tell him whatever buys us an hour."

Petra wanted to argue and didn't have time. The polite man was already at the top of the ladder, and an old woman — Anong's mother, or her sister, the family architecture was still a maze to Petra — was bowing him toward the shaded platform where guests were received, because hospitality was a debt this village paid even to men it should have drowned.

He saw Petra and smiled like they were old friends who'd missed each other by a season.

"Ms. Lockridge." He pronounced it correctly, which meant he'd done his homework. "Serey. I represent some people with an interest in the upper river. May I sit?"

"It's your village's platform," Petra said. "I'm the guest."

"Then we're both guests." He sat anyway, crossing his legs with the ease of a man who'd sat on a hundred platforms like this one and would sit on a hundred more. Up close he was maybe fifty, silver at the temples, a face built for trust — open, lined, faintly amused, the face of a favorite uncle. He set a small insulated bag on the boards between them. "Coffee? Real coffee. I flew it in from the capital yesterday. I find that conversations go better when nobody's drinking the instant stuff."

Petra looked at the bag. Looked at him.

"You flew coffee into a flood zone," she said, "to talk to a guide who's been blacklisted twice."

"I flew coffee into a flood zone," Serey said, "because I respect you, and I wanted to demonstrate that respect before I opened my mouth." He poured two cups without waiting for permission, and the smell hit Petra like a memory of another life — hotel lobbies, airports, a world where water came out of taps and nobody asked what was in it. She took the cup. Refusing it would have been a move in a game, and she was tired of games she didn't know the rules to.

"Serey," she said. "That's Khmer."

"My mother's side. My father's people were further south." He sipped, closed his eyes briefly, savoring it. "I was born two provinces from here, Ms. Lockridge. Whatever you think you know about the people I work for, I grew up on this water. I swam in it. I have opinions about what's been done to it."

"Then you're in the wrong line of work."

"I'm in exactly the right line of work. That's what I came to talk about." He set down his cup and folded his hands, and just like that the uncle was gone and something more careful sat in his place. "You have a case. Aluminum, blue latches, roughly this size." He sketched it in the air. "It contains water samples, field logs, and chain-of-custody documentation from a government survey that officially never collected anything. The consortium I represent would very much like that case to stop existing. I've been authorized to make that worth your while."

"And if I say I don't have any case?"

Serey smiled again, gently, the way you'd smile at a child who'd lied about the cookies. "Ms. Lockridge. I paid a man in a river town two days' travel downstream for the contents of a satellite phone call. I know what boat you left on. I know it leaks. I know you bailed it for eleven hours on the night of the twenty-third. I know you stopped here." He gestured, taking in the stilt house, the brown water sliding past, the children who had stopped playing to stare at the strangers. "I'm not your enemy. I'm the first person in this entire affair who's been honest with you. That should count for something."

Petra drank her coffee. It was, infuriatingly, excellent.

"Vuthy offered me a finder's fee," she said.

"Vuthy," Serey said, and something flickered behind the uncle's face — fast, unreadable, gone. "Yes. Vuthy. A broker of modest imagination. He offered you money to find the survey team. My employers are prepared to offer you considerably more to stop looking."

"How much?"

"Triple his fee. Cash, in the capital, within a week." Serey said it the way other men said good morning. "Plus this."

He reached into the linen jacket and produced a passport. Not her passport — hers was in her boot, warped with damp, stamped with the two bans that had ended her career in two countries. This one was navy, uncreased, and when he flipped it open there was her face, her name, and a birthdate, and a nationality, and a decade of blank pages waiting.

Petra didn't reach for it. She made herself not reach for it, and she knew he saw the effort, and she hated him a little for the kindness of not smiling about it.

"That's a forgery," she said.

"That's a *document*," Serey said. "Issued through channels that don't ask questions, registered in databases that do exist. It will survive any border check on three continents. It will survive the inquiry that's coming, when this all comes apart — and it will come apart, Ms. Lockridge, one way or the other. The permits expire in a few weeks. The consortium's lease dies with them. Everything downstream of that is noise." He turned the passport so it caught the light. "With this, you walk away from all of it. The bans, the debt, the name that follows you through every border post from here to Patagonia. A woman with a clean book and money in the bank. Mountains don't check passports. You could be guiding again by spring."

Petra set her cup down. Her hands were steady. She was proud of that, and ashamed of how much effort the pride was costing.

"You know about the debt," she said.

"I know about everything. That's my job." Serey's voice softened, and the worst part was that it didn't sound like a performance. "The Delgado family. The rescue in the Cordillera Blanca. The rope that was old, the storm that was early, the client who died, and the guide who was blamed — fairly or not, I don't know, I wasn't there, and I'd guess you're the only one who knows, and I'd guess you don't fully know yourself." He let that sit. Petra let it sit, because arguing with it would have confirmed it. "You owe them money you will never earn back guiding, because no one will hire you. You took Vuthy's contract to dig out of that hole. I am offering you the same hole, filled in, with grass planted on top. No debt. No blacklist. No Anong."

"There it is," Petra said.

"There it is," Serey agreed, without apology. "I'll say the quiet part plainly, because I respect you and I think you can hear it. The hydrologist is the danger. Not the samples — samples can be challenged, degraded, dismissed. The woman. A scientist with a name, a face, a village full of sick children, and nothing left to lose. If she reaches the capital, or a journalist, or a court, this stops being a business matter and becomes a story, and stories are the one thing my employers can't buy." He picked up his coffee again, unhurried. "You hand me the case and tell me where she's going next, and none of the rest of it ever touches you. She was never on the manifest, Ms. Lockridge. Officially, she doesn't exist. Unofficially — well. Unofficially, neither do you, anymore. That's the symmetry of it. Two ghosts. Only one of you has to stay one."

Down at the landing, one of the crewmen shifted his weight, and Petra noted it the way she noted loose rock on a traverse — automatically, without letting her eyes move. Two on the boat. One on the platform. The village had maybe thirty adults, a dozen of them men, none of them armed with anything better than machetes and the particular courage of people who had nowhere else to go.

Somewhere behind the house, she could hear Anong's niece laughing at something. A small, bright, ordinary sound.

"Can I ask you something?" Petra said.

"You can ask me anything. I may not answer, but you can ask."

"You said you swam in this water. You grew up here." She turned to face him fully. "Do you know what's in it now?"

Serey held her gaze, and for a moment — a real moment, she was almost sure of it — something moved behind the uncle's face that looked like grief. Then it was packed away, neat as the passport in his jacket.

"I know the numbers," he said quietly. "I've known them longer than the survey team did. I told you I have opinions about what's been done to this river." He set the passport down on the boards between them, face up, an open hand. "And I'll tell you the other thing I know, the thing that keeps men like me employed. Being right changes nothing. Being *useful* changes everything. The contamination will be in that water whether you testify or not. The children will be sick either way. The only variable in this entire equation, Ms. Lockridge, is you — whether you end this year broke and barred from a third country, or whole, solvent, and free. That's not a moral question. That's arithmetic."

"Arithmetic," Petra said. "Anong has a word for that too."

"I imagine she does. She's better at it than either of us. It's why she's dangerous." He rose, smoothing his shirt, and the visit was suddenly, obviously over — he'd gotten what he came for, which wasn't an answer. It was the sight of her looking at the passport. "The offer stands for seventy-two hours. There's a radio frequency on the back of the document. One call, any time, day or night. No pressure. No consequences for declining — I want you to believe that, and it's true. My employers are reasonable people. They'd much rather pay you than—" He stopped, delicately. "Than the alternative. I'd much rather pay you. I've read your file, Ms. Lockridge. I think you're the most competent person involved in this entire mess, including me. It would be a waste to see you drown in it."

He descended the ladder as gracefully as he'd come up, and the slate-colored boat pulled away into the mist with its silent motor, and the river closed behind it like a page.

* * *

Petra sat on the platform for a long time with the passport in front of her and didn't touch it.

The coffee had gone cold. The rain had started again, that fine vertical drizzle the monsoon used to fill silence, and the children had gone back to their games, and the river kept doing what rivers do, which is carry everything somewhere else.

She thought about the Delgados. About the phone call she would eventually have to make, the one she'd been rehearsing for three years and would botch anyway. About the number in her head — the debt, the exact figure, the one that woke her up at 3 a.m. in hostels in four countries. Triple Vuthy's fee erased it. The passport erased the rest. She could be in Kathmandu by February. She could be *nobody*, which after these years felt less like an insult than like a vacation.

She thought about the arithmetic, and how Serey was right, and how being right changed nothing.

That was the trap, she realized. Not the money. The money was just the bait on the hook. The trap was that everything he'd said was true — the samples could be challenged, the children would be sick either way, her refusal would buy the river nothing. Corruption at its best didn't ask you to do evil. It asked you to do nothing, and made doing nothing feel like wisdom.

"Did you hear all of it?" she said, without turning around.

Anong came out from behind the house wall, where she'd been standing long enough for the drizzle to darken her shoulders. She sat down across from Petra, looked at the passport, and didn't touch it either.

"Triple," Anong said.

"Triple. Plus a clean passport. Plus they leave you alone." Petra turned it over. A radio frequency was written on the back in neat ballpoint. "Seventy-two hours to decide."

"They'll give you seventy-two hours," Anong said. "They won't give *me* seventy-two hours. The polite one came alone so you'd talk. The next boat won't be polite, and it won't be alone, and it won't come for you." She looked out at the water, at the mist where the slate boat had gone. "My brother's arm is still bandaged from their last message. The next message will be worse."

"So we run."

"We run," Anong agreed. "But Petra." She turned, and her eyes in the grey light were the flattest Petra had ever seen them. "Before we run — I need to know. When he put that passport on the boards. For how long did you want it?"

Petra thought about lying. Anong had earned better, and besides, lying to a hydrologist was like lying to a river; it always found you out.

"About four seconds," Petra said.

"Four seconds," Anong repeated. "That's honest. I had the samples in my hands the night they took the team, and I had a seat on a boat, and I did the arithmetic in about two." She stood, and held out her hand — not for the passport, Petra understood, but for what came after it. "Burn it or throw it in the river. Then pack. We leave tonight, upstream, before they expect movement. There's something at the last sampling site I should have gone back for weeks ago, and now we have no choice."

"What's at the site?"

"The team leader's backup logs," Anong said. "And the man who hid them there is the same man whose body you haven't found yet — because he isn't dead. He's been hiding worse than I have."

Petra looked at her. "You've known that the whole time."

"I've known it since the night it all happened," Anong said. "And I let you believe he was dead, because a dead man's evidence is safe and a live man's evidence is a target. I'm sorry. It was arithmetic." She walked toward the ladder, then stopped. "You had four seconds. I've had six weeks. That's the difference between us, and it's not a good one."

Petra picked up the passport. It was lighter than it should have been, the way all lies are lighter than the truth. She carried it to the edge of the platform, held it over the brown water where the children had been swimming all summer, and let it go. It turned once in the drizzle, white pages flashing, and then the river took it the way the river took everything, somewhere else, and kept it.

Behind her, Anong was already packing.
