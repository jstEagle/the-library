## 16. Refusal

Serey poured the tea himself.

That was the thing about him Petra kept circling back to later, in the long dark hours on the river, when she took the conversation apart bolt by bolt to see if she could have handled it differently. He poured the tea himself. No servant, no assistant. He'd carried the tray out to the veranda of the stilt house with his own hands, sleeves rolled to the forearm, a man in his fifties with soft palms and a watch that cost more than everything Petra had pawned in her life combined. He poured three cups — one for her, one for Anong, who stood at the far rail with her arms crossed like the tea might be evidence against her — and set the pot on the table between them, within everyone's reach, as if to say: we are all reasonable people here.

"Your reputation precedes you," Serey said. "The Andes. Two countries. I've read the incident reports. Both of them." He smiled, and it was a good smile, unhurried, the smile of a man who had never once needed to raise his voice. "The second report blames you. The first one blames the weather. In my experience the truth is usually somewhere in the weather."

"Flattering," Petra said.

"It's not flattery. It's diligence. I don't make offers to people I haven't understood." He turned his cup a quarter turn on its saucer, precise as a man setting a bearing. "You are in debt to a family in another hemisphere. You are owed money by a fixer who, I suspect, has already sold your name to at least two parties. You are blacklisted twice, and the second blacklist is the kind that follows you. You are sitting in a village that is not yours, next to a woman who is not your client, holding a case that is not your problem." He lifted the cup, drank, set it down. "And in forty days, none of it will matter, because the permits expire and the whole question dissolves like sugar in the river."

Anong's knuckles whitened on the rail.

"So here is my offer," Serey said. "Triple Vuthy's fee. Paid today, in the currency of your choice, before you leave this veranda. A clean passport — a real one, not a forgery, with a name you choose and a history that holds up at any border on earth. And passage. Anywhere. The debt to the family in the Andes, settled quietly, through channels they'll never trace back to you." He paused, letting the shape of it settle in the humid air between them. "In exchange: the case. The samples, the logs, whatever the hydrologist is carrying. And you forget you ever met her. You take the money, you take the passport, and you walk out of this story the way you walked into it — alone, which is how you've always preferred it, isn't it?"

The rain had stopped an hour before dawn and the village was steaming. Somewhere below the stilt house a woman was calling children in from the water. A dog scratched itself against a post. The river ran brown and heavy past the pilings, carrying last night's rain toward the coast, toward everything downstream, toward the children Anong hadn't been able to look at that morning.

Petra looked at the tea. It was good tea — she could tell by the smell, jasmine and something smokier underneath, the kind of thing that got served to men who mattered. She thought about the arithmetic of her life. The debt. The blacklists. The way every door she'd knocked on for six years had opened onto another corridor with a locked door at the end of it. Triple Vuthy's fee. A clean passport. The debt, settled. Her father's voice, if he'd been sitting there: *Take it, girl. Dead men don't need rivers.*

"Can I ask you something?" she said.

"Of course."

"The team. The survey team. Eight people." She'd counted the tents. Eight tents, eight bedrolls, eight sets of eating kit. "Did you know any of them?"

Serey's expression didn't change, but something behind it did — a shutter moving, very slightly, like a man adjusting his weight. "I knew their names," he said.

"Did you know what they found?"

"I knew what they would have found, if they'd been permitted to finish." He said it plainly, without apology, and that was somehow worse than a lie. "Petra. May I call you Petra? I've read your file, so it seems only fair." He leaned forward, elbows on his knees, and for a moment the polish dropped away and she saw the actual machinery underneath, and it was not cruelty. It was something colder and more efficient than cruelty. It was bookkeeping. "You think I'm a monster. I'm not. I'm a man who understands that the world runs on concessions — leases, permits, permissions — and that every one of them expires, and that what happens in the gap between one lease and the next is nobody's story. The water in this river has been the color it is for thirty years. It will be the color it is for thirty more. Eight people, six years ago, walked into that range knowing the risks of the monsoon. The river took them. That is the official finding, and it is, in every way that matters to you, the true one."

"The river took them before the monsoon," Petra said.

"The river takes people all year," Serey said gently. "That's what rivers do."

Down at the water a boy pushed off from the bank in a dugout, poling into the current, and Anong made a small sound, almost inaudible, and Petra understood that the boy was one of the ones with the lesions, one of the ones whose bloodwork Anong had seen, and that Anong was standing at that rail holding herself together with her arms because if she let go she was going to cross this veranda and do something that would get them both killed.

Petra picked up her tea and drank it. It was, as she'd expected, very good.

"No," she said.

Serey nodded slowly, as if she'd confirmed something for him. "May I ask why? I'm genuinely curious. The money is real. The passport is real. You will not get a better offer in this life, and I say that with respect."

"Because you poured the tea yourself," Petra said.

He waited.

"Men like you have people for everything. You have people to burn villages and people to write reports and people to drown survey teams in rivers that haven't flooded yet. But you poured the tea yourself, because you wanted me to see your hands." She set the cup down. "You wanted me to think that underneath the work there's a person. There isn't. The work is the person. And I've spent six years being the kind of person who takes the money and walks out of the story, and every time I do, somebody ends up dead and I end up holding the compass." She stood. "The answer's no. It was no before you opened your mouth. I just wanted to see the hands."

Serey looked at her for a long moment. Then he did something she hadn't expected. He smiled — a different smile this time, smaller, almost private, the smile of a man watching a card land exactly where he'd predicted — and he gathered the cups back onto the tray with those soft, careful hands.

"I'll have the boat ready for you at the north landing," he said. "It would be a shame if something happened to you here, in a village with so many children in it. Accidents travel badly in places like this. They spread." He carried the tray inside, and paused at the door. "For what it's worth, I hope you're fast. I've read your file. You're not as fast as you think you are."

The door slid shut behind him.

* * *

Anong didn't speak until they were down the ladder and into the alley between the stilt houses, the boards slick underfoot, the smell of fish and woodsmoke and the river's permanent brown breath.

"You should have taken it," she said.

"Probably."

"Triple the fee. A passport. The debt." She stopped in the shade of a drying rack, nets hanging around them like the ribs of something dead. "Petra. I've done the arithmetic. I'm a scientist, arithmetic is what I do. One woman, one case of samples, against a consortium with boats, money, and a government ministry on a leash. The probability that we reach the lab — that the lab accepts the chain of custody, that anything survives the forty days, that anyone is left to testify — you want me to calculate it? Because I can. It's not a number that justifies a village burning."

"It's not your village burning," Petra said. "That's the point. That's always the point. Somebody else pays for your conscience." She heard her own voice come out flat and bitter and recognized it — it was the voice from the Andes, from the incident report, from the second inquiry where she'd sat with her hands folded and said *I made the call* and watched a family's faces across a table. "I know exactly what I just did. I just signed a bill and handed it to whoever's standing closest. That's what refusing men like Serey costs. It's never you who pays."

Anong looked at her for a long moment. Then, quietly: "The children. You saw them this morning."

"Yes."

"Then you did the arithmetic too." Anong pushed off the drying rack and kept walking. "You just didn't show your work."

They packed in silence. Petra's kit had been reduced to its skeleton — rope, tarp, the knife, the water filter, the compass in its case against her sternum where it rode like a second heart. Anong's pack was heavier: the case, wrapped in oilcloth, and her field logs in plastic sleeves, and the backup drives she'd been carrying against her spine for six years like a tumor she refused to have removed. The old woman from the next house — the one whose granddaughter's arms were mapped with the rash that didn't heal — came to the door with rice wrapped in banana leaf and wouldn't take no for an answer, and pressed it into Anong's hands and said something fast and low in the village dialect, and Anong answered in the same dialect, and whatever she said made the old woman's face crumple and then set, hard, like mud drying in the sun.

"What did she say?" Petra asked when they were moving.

"She said her husband worked the mine road for nine years. He died at forty-one. She said, *make them write it down somewhere it can't be burned.*" Anong's voice was steady. Her hands weren't. "I told her I'd try."

The north landing was where Serey said it would be, and the boat was where he said it would be — a longboat, fueled, provisioned, its tank full, which was the most menacing thing Petra had seen in weeks. A gift with a receipt attached. She checked it anyway, all of it, the fuel line and the plug and the bailer and the spare, because paranoia was free and everything else cost.

They were a kilometer downstream before Anong spoke again.

"He'll burn it," she said. "The village. Maybe not today. Maybe he waits until we're far enough away that it reads as coincidence, or bandits, or a cooking fire in the dry season. But he'll do something. That's what the boat means. That's what *accidents travel badly* means. He told us what he's going to do, politely, over tea, and we're leaving anyway, and every house on that bank is a number in his ledger now because of a choice you made on a veranda." She wasn't accusing. That was the terrible thing. She was stating it the way she'd state a flow rate, a contamination level, a parts-per-million figure. "Do you regret it?"

Petra ran the boat through the bend where the current stacked up against the cut bank, feeling it answer her hands, and thought about the question honestly, because Anong deserved honesty and because she was going to ask herself the same question at three in the morning for the rest of her life and she might as well start now.

"Nothing," she said. "And everything. Both at once. I regret the cost. I'd pay the cost again. I don't know how to make those two things fit in the same head, but they're both true." She looked at the brown water sliding past, the same brown it had been for thirty years, the brown that was about to become the most expensive color in the country if Anong's samples said what Anong said they said. "You asked me once why I couldn't walk away from this. I told you it was the money. That was a lie. It's because I've been the person who takes the deal. I know exactly what she looks like. I've seen her in the mirror for six years, and I am not doing it again. Not for a passport. Not for the debt. Not for anything." She throttled up. "So we're fast. We're faster than he thinks. We run day and night, we don't stop, we don't trust anyone Vuthy ever shook hands with, and we get to the lab before the permits die."

"And if we don't?"

"Then at least the ledger has our names in it on the right side."

Anong almost smiled. It was the first one in two days. "That's not science."

"No," Petra said. "It's navigation. You pick a bearing and you hold it and you stop asking whether the river agrees."

Behind them, far upstream, the village shrank into the treeline, and the air above it was clear, and there was no smoke yet, and Petra found herself watching the horizon anyway, the way you watch a sky that has gone too quiet, and she thought: *he gave us the boat so we'd be gone when it started, so we'd know, wherever we are, whatever river we're on, exactly what our refusal bought.*

The first smoke went up an hour before dusk.

It was behind them, a gray thread standing straight up in the still air off the eastern bank — not the village, not yet, but the mine road, the fuel depot, the treeline above the old workings, a burn line being laid like a signature. And then, as the light failed, a second thread, closer, and Anong stood up so fast the boat rocked, and said, in a voice Petra had never heard her use, "That's the school."

Petra opened the throttle all the way, and the longboat climbed up onto its bow wave and ran into the dark, toward the fire, because there was no longer anywhere in the world to run to that wasn't on fire — and because the children were still on the bank.
