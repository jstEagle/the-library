## 17. Burned Bridge

The smoke woke her before the smell did.

Petra came up off the sleeping mat with her hand already on the knife, the way she'd woken for eleven years in a hundred bad rooms, and through the slatted window of Anong's mother's house she saw the orange glow climbing over the stilt roofs at the far end of the village. Not sunrise. Sunrise didn't flicker. Sunrise didn't roar.

"Anong." She was moving before she finished saying it, barefoot across the cool boards, grabbing the sample case from under the bed where they'd slept in shifts since Serey's visit three days ago. "Anong, wake up. Fire."

Anong was already awake — she'd been sleeping badly since the village, since the children, since the arithmetic of what she'd done and hadn't done — and she took one look out the window and said a word in Khmer that Petra didn't know but understood completely.

"The north end." Anong was pulling on her shirt, her hands steady in the way Petra had learned meant the opposite of calm. "That's where the drying racks are. That's where—"

She stopped.

Petra watched the name arrive in her like a stone dropping into still water. *Vireak.* The brother who'd taken them in when their own family wouldn't speak to Anong. The brother who'd carried their gear across the flooded fields without being asked, who'd laughed at Petra's terrible Khmer, who'd told her last night over river fish and rice whiskey that his sister had always been too stubborn to drown.

"He's down there," Anong said. Flat. Already past fear into something colder.

They went out into the rain.

* * *

The monsoon rain should have killed the fire. That was Petra's first coherent thought as they ran through the mud between the houses — it was raining hard enough to hurt, fat warm drops hammering the tin roofs, and still the fire climbed the north end of the village like it had been paid to. Which meant accelerant. Diesel, probably, poured along the walkways and up the stilts while the village slept, lit and left. This wasn't an accident or a cooking fire gone wrong. This was punctuation.

This was a sentence written in a language Petra had read before, in other countries, other mountains: *We can reach you anywhere. This is what refusal costs.*

The message wasn't for the village. The message was for her.

The north end was chaos. Villagers formed bucket lines from the river, passing dented aluminum pots hand to hand, but the fire had already eaten two houses whole and was working on the third, and the heat pushed back against the rain in a shimmering wall that made the whole scene warp like something seen through bad glass. A woman was screaming a name over and over. Dogs ran in circles. Somewhere a child cried in the thin, rhythmic way of a child too exhausted to do anything else.

Anong went straight into it.

Petra grabbed for her arm and missed. "Anong—"

"Vireak's house!" And she was gone, into the smoke, small and fast and utterly certain, and Petra stood for exactly one second — one second in which every survival instinct she owned, instincts that had kept her alive in the Andes and the Karakoram and a dozen places with no names, screamed at her to stay out, to protect the case, to protect herself — and then she followed.

Because that was the other thing those instincts had taught her. You could survive anything except the moment after you didn't go in.

* * *

Vireak's house was burning from the bottom up. Someone had piled diesel-soaked palm fronds against the stilts, and the flames had found the dry storage beneath the floor and were eating upward through the heart of the structure. The ladder was already alight.

"VIREAK." Anong's voice cracked against the roar. "VIREAK, ANSWER ME."

A cough. From inside. Above them, through the smoke boiling out of the windows, something moved.

"He's up there." Anong was already reaching for the ladder, and Petra caught her wrist and hauled her back.

"It'll take your weight."

"So will staying here!"

Petra looked at the burning ladder, at the flames crawling the stilts, doing the math she hated — load, time, structural failure, the way fire turns wood into a schedule you can't negotiate with. Then she looked at the big water jar beside the house, half-full, and the long bamboo pole leaning against the neighbor's fence.

"Ladder's gone. We go up the pole side." She slammed the pole against the jar, cracked it, dumped the water over both of them until they were soaked enough to buy maybe thirty seconds of skin. "You climb behind me. If I say drop, you drop. Understood?"

Anong's eyes were streaming — smoke or something else — and she nodded once.

The pole was green bamboo, slick with rain, and it bent under their combined weight like it was considering whether to betray them. Petra went up it hand over hand with her shoulders screaming, the heat pressing against her left side like a physical hand, and at the top she swung onto the veranda rail into air so thick she couldn't see her own hands. The floorboards were hot through her soaked clothes. Somewhere below, the fire made a sound like a crowd.

"VIREAK."

Here. A shape by the interior wall, low, dragging himself toward the window. He'd gone out the wrong door first, toward the main stairs, and found them already gone, and now he was crawling through smoke that would kill him in another minute whether the fire reached him or not.

Petra got him under the arms. Fifty kilos if he was one, dead weight plus smoke-stupidity, and the veranda rail was four meters above black mud and rising floodwater. No time for good decisions.

"Drop," she said to Anong, and dropped.

* * *

The water caught them — shallow, filthy, knee-deep at best, so the landing was less a dive than a controlled collapse — and Petra rolled Vireak face-up and got her ear to his mouth while Anong tore at his shirt. Breathing. Wet, ragged, rattling, but breathing. His forearms were burned where he'd shielded his face from a wall of flame; the skin there had gone tight and shiny, blisters already rising.

"We need—" Anong started.

"I know." Petra got Vireak's arm over her shoulders. "Your mother's house. Move."

They carried him through the rain while the fire behind them found the roof beams and let go, collapsing inward in a geyser of sparks that drew a collective moan from the bucket line. Nobody had died. Petra counted heads later, twice, because she didn't believe it the first time — two houses lost, the drying racks, three boats including the one they'd been repairing for the run downstream — but no bodies. Whoever set this had wanted a message, not a massacre. Or wanted a massacre and gotten sloppy. She honestly didn't know which possibility frightened her more.

At the house, Anong's mother — a small, iron-spined woman named Sokha who had not spoken more than ten words to her disgraced daughter in the entire week they'd sheltered there — worked over Vireak's burns with boiled water and banana leaf and a competence that needed no words. Anong sat beside her brother holding his good hand and saying nothing, and Petra stood in the doorway watching the rain come down on the smoking ruin of the north end, and felt the thing settle into her chest that she'd known was coming since the night she refused Serey.

*This is what it costs,* she thought. *Not you. Never you. The people around you.*

In the Andes it had been Daniel Cho's family waiting for a body. Here it was a burned man who'd laughed at her Khmer, in a village that had fed her for free.

Same ledger. Different page.

* * *

Vireak woke properly near dawn, gray-faced, fever already building in the burns. The village medic — an old man with a leather bag and no formal training and forty years of practice — cleaned the wounds again and said, in the unhurried way of people delivering verdicts: infected if not watched, needs real medicine, real medicine is in the provincial capital, capital is two days downstream, and the boat that could have taken them downstream was ash.

Petra did the math and didn't like any column of it.

Anong came out onto the veranda where Petra sat sharpening the knife she hadn't needed last night, because last night hadn't been about fighting, it had been about demonstrating range. Anong sat down next to her. For a while neither of them spoke. The rain had thinned to a gray drizzle, and the village moved quietly among the wreckage, salvaging nails, righting a scorched canoe, the particular industry of people who have been punished before and know the work doesn't wait for grief.

"They used diesel," Anong said finally. "In monsoon season. They wanted us to know it was them."

"I know."

"Sokha hasn't spoken to me in six years. Six years, Petra. Because I left. Because I chose the data over—" Her voice snagged. She smoothed it flat. "And last night she stitched my brother's arms like it was nothing, like she does this every day, and then she brought me tea. Tea, Petra. First thing she's handed me in six years." Anong stared out at the black skeleton of the drying racks. "It took a fire."

Petra turned the knife over in her hands. "I'm sorry."

"Don't." Anong's voice came out with an edge on it, sudden and bright. "Don't apologize. You didn't pour the diesel."

"No. But I'm the reason it got poured." She said it evenly, because it was simply true, and she'd learned the hard way that flinching from true things only charged interest. "Serey offered me money to walk away from you. I said no. That's mine. I own that. But the bill didn't come to me, Anong. It came to Vireak's arms and those families' houses. That's how these people work. They never send the invoice to the person who signed."

Anong was quiet for a long moment. When she spoke again the edge was gone, replaced by something harder and more durable.

"Then we make sure the invoice goes back." She looked at Petra, and in her face was the same transformation Petra had seen in the cave, in the boat, at every point where grief might have broken her and instead had been forged into direction. "The samples. The logs. The lab. Thirty-one days left on that permit, and every day we sit here, they get to keep burning things. My brother needs a hospital downstream. The evidence needs a lab downstream. Same river. Same direction."

Petra almost smiled. Almost. "You've been reading my route notes."

"I've been reading everything you do. It's terrifying." Anong stood, and winced, and straightened. "There's a fisherman, Chamroeun. His boat survived — it was moored at the south mooring, away from the fire. He owes my family. He'll take us as far as the junction, but he won't go near the capital, not with consortium eyes on the river. After the junction we're alone."

"Alone is fine. Alone is where I do my best work."

Down the slope, Vireak stirred and called his sister's name, weak and smoke-roughened, and Anong went to him, and Petra stayed on the veranda a moment longer, looking north at the ruin.

Serey had miscalculated, she realized. He'd sent a message meant to break her — *look what your principles cost* — and instead he'd spent his leverage buying her something she hadn't had since the Andes: a debt she could actually pay. Not money. Not forgiveness. Just forward motion. Every kilometer downstream was a payment against a bill with Vireak's name on it.

She stood, sheathed the knife, and went to help pack.

They left within the hour, before the fever could decide what it wanted to be, before the consortium's watchers could regroup. Chamroeun's boat was small and smelled of fish and had an engine that started on the fourth pull, and as the village fell away behind them — the two blackened houses standing over the water like burnt teeth — Anong stood at the stern watching it shrink, her brother's bandaged arm resting against her side where he lay propped on nets.

Petra watched the river ahead instead. That was the division of labor they'd fallen into without discussing it: Anong guarded what was behind, Petra what was in front.

Which was why Petra saw the boat first.

It was a kilometer back, maybe more, tucked into the tree shadow on the far bank where the current slowed — a long, low silhouette with an outboard idling, not moving, not fishing, just *waiting*. Holding position the way only someone with fuel to burn holds position.

It had been there since they cleared the bend. It would be there at every bend.

"They're not attacking," Petra said quietly, not turning her head. "They're herding. Making sure we go where they want, or making sure whoever's ahead of us knows we're coming."

Anong looked back once, briefly, a professional assessing data. "Then we don't go where they want."

"No," Petra agreed, and put her hand in her pocket, and closed it around her father's compass, its brass worn smooth, its needle swinging toward the falls country upstream of the junction — the unmapped water, the water only two people had ever run, one of whom had left his notes folded in the case lining. "We go where nobody wants."

The idling boat behind them held its patient shadow. Ahead, the river widened toward the junction where Serey's men would be waiting — unless the junction wasn't where they were going at all.

Petra turned the tiller toward the far channel, where the water ran fast and brown and unmarked on every chart she'd ever seen.
