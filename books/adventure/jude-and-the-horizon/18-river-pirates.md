## 18. River Pirates

The rain had thinned to a fine gray mist by the time they passed the burned village behind them, and Petra allowed herself the first stupid thought in three days: *maybe we're through it.*

She should have known better. Rivers don't forgive optimism. They just wait for you to relax your grip.

Anong was bailing again — a rhythm they'd settled into over two days of open water, the plastic bowl dipping and flinging, dipping and flinging, while Petra stood at the stern with one hand on the tiller extension and the other shading her eyes against the flat silver light. The river here was wide as a lake, braided around sandbars that shifted with every flood pulse, and the current ran lazy and deep. Too deep to read. That was what bothered her. A river this wide should have shown her its channels — the darker threads where the water moved fast, the pale stains of shallows, the V-wakes of submerged bars. Instead the surface lay smooth and opaque as poured tin, hiding everything.

"Left side," Anong said, without looking up from her bailing. "There's a boat."

Petra saw it a half-second later. Then two more.

They came out of a drowned forest on the eastern bank — three longboats running in echelon, engines wide open, cutting white furrows across the still water. Not consortium boats. Consortium boats were new, fiberglass, painted corporate blue with registration numbers stenciled neat as scripture. These were old wooden hulls patched with sheet metal and tar, engines that smoked blue, men standing in them with the loose-kneed balance of people who'd grown up on moving water.

Pirates was the word the provincial capital used for them, said with a curl of the lip, as if they were a species of river vermin. The word Petra's father had used, thirty years ago, charting these same waters, was *toll-takers*. Men who lived off the traffic the government pretended didn't exist — fuel runners, timber poachers, cargo that never met a customs stamp. In a monsoon year when the legitimate trade dried up, they got hungry like everyone else.

"Run?" Anong asked. She'd stopped bailing. The bowl hung from her hand, dripping.

"Outboard's ours is twenty horsepower and half-flooded." Petra did the arithmetic without wanting to. "They're doing forty. Three boats. We don't run."

"Then we fight."

"We don't fight either." She eased the throttle back, letting their boat settle into the current, presenting herself as slow and unafraid — or too tired to care, which was closer to true. "We talk. Get the bailer ready anyway."

The lead boat swung wide and came alongside in a long curving approach, professional, no wasted motion. There were five men in it. The one at the bow held an old shotgun broken open over his forearm, casual as a farmer carrying a rake, which somehow made it worse. The man at the helm was younger than Petra expected — mid-thirties, lean, a scar splitting one eyebrow, and eyes that went over their boat the way she'd go over a mountain face: reading it, pricing it, deciding what it could yield.

He brought his boat parallel, ten feet off, close enough that Petra could see the salt crust on his engine housing and the machete lashed under his gunwale. He said something in Khmer. Then, seeing her face, he tried Thai. Then Lao. Then, with visible reluctance, English.

"You are going where?"

"Downstream," Petra said. "Provincial capital."

"What do you carry?"

"Bad luck and a leaking boat."

His mouth twitched. He didn't laugh. Behind him, the second boat was circling to cut off their downstream escape, and the third held position near the bank, guarding the route back. Textbook. Whoever ran this crew had done this before, many times, and gotten bored enough with easy takings to do it well.

The scarred man's gaze moved across their gear — the tarp, the fuel cans, the dry bag with the sample case nested inside it, wrapped in Anong's spare shirt. It rested there a beat too long. Petra watched him price it and knew he'd decided it was worth taking.

"The case," he said. "Open it."

"It's water samples," Anong said quickly, in Khmer. "River testing. Government work. There's nothing—"

"I know what river testing looks like." His voice flattened. "I also know what government boats look like, and yours has been repainted. Twice. So." He lifted the shotgun, closed it with a snap that carried clean across the water. "Open it, or I open your boat, and then we see what floats."

* * *

Here was the thing about being robbed on open water: there was nowhere to be robbed *to*. On land you could run into trees, into ravines, into the thousand small mercies of broken terrain. On a river you were cargo on a conveyor, and every direction was the same direction.

Petra had learned this at nineteen, working her father's charter runs, the summer before everything. They'd been stopped twice in one season by toll-takers on the upper reaches, and both times her father had done the same thing, and it had worked both times, and she'd asked him afterward why they never just shot us, and he'd said something she hadn't understood until years later:

*"Because a dead guide is worth one boat. A scared guide is worth one boat. But a useful guide — a guide who knows where your hull will open up on a sandbar at low water — that's worth a partnership."*

Her father had never met a problem he couldn't reframe as a transaction. It was his best quality and the thing that eventually killed him, but right now, with a shotgun leveled at Anong's chest, it was the inheritance that mattered.

"Before you take anything," Petra said, loud enough to carry to all three boats, "you should know what I know."

The scarred man's eyebrows rose a fraction. Genuinely curious, or performing curiosity. Either worked.

"There's a bar two kilometers downstream," she went on, "where the east channel shoals out to less than a meter at this stage. Your draft, loaded, you'd ground hard enough to crack a plank. The safe channel is west, tight against the far bank, past the dead kapok tree — but only until the next bend, because after the bend there's a wrecked barge under the surface and the channel dog-legs left around it. If you're running this stretch regularly, you already know some of that." She let it hang. "You don't know all of it. Nobody does, this season. The flood moved everything."

Silence, except the rain ticking on the water and the idle rumble of three engines.

"You're a pilot," the scarred man said.

"I'm a guide. Mountains mostly. Rivers when the mountains are closed." She jerked her chin at the gray sky. "Mountains are closed."

"And if I take your knowledge and your case?"

"Then you get one boatload of stolen samples and a river full of holes you'll find the hard way, one cracked hull at a time, all season." She kept her voice level, almost bored, though her heart was going like a trip-hammer. "Or you keep me talking and I keep you floating. Your call. You're the businessman."

One of the other men shouted something from the second boat — impatient, greedy. The scarred man raised a hand without turning, and the voice shut off. Command, Petra noted. Real command, not just seniority. That made him more dangerous and more negotiable at the same time.

"Samples," he said. "Government work. What government wants with river water in a flood year?"

"That," Anong said, "is a very good question."

Something passed between them — hydrologist and toll-taker, two people who'd both spent their lives reading water and both been lied to about it. Petra saw it happen and didn't interrupt.

"My village," the man said finally, "is upstream. Near the mine roads." He said *the mine* the way people say a disease. "Our children have sores that don't heal. Our fish taste of metal. For six years we told the province, and for six years the province told us the tests came back clean." His eyes flicked to the dry bag. "So. Whose tests are those?"

"Ours," Anong said. "And they won't come back clean."

The rain thickened, thinned. Downstream, the second boat's driver gunned his engine once, restlessly, and the scarred man silenced him with a look this time.

"You understand my problem," he said to Petra. "My crew is hungry. The flood killed the trade. If I let you pass with nothing, I look weak, and weak captains feed their crews last." He turned the shotgun over in his hands, thoughtful rather than threatening, and Petra understood she'd crossed some line from mark to counterpart. "So we don't do nothing. We do a toll."

"A toll," Petra repeated.

"Small. Fuel — I know you have extra, nobody runs this stretch without extra. And the woman's shirt." He nodded at the dry bag wrapping. "Not the case. The shirt. My crew sees cargo taken. My crew eats the story even if they don't eat much else." A thin smile. "And you give me one thing free. One answer, when I ask for it, before the bend with the wrecked barge. Deal?"

It was extortion with the volume turned down, and it was the best offer she'd get today, and every instinct honed in two decades of bad terrain told her to take it before he remembered he had the shotgun.

"Fuel and the shirt," Petra said. "Deal."

* * *

The transfer took four minutes. Two jerry cans passed hand to hand across the gap between hulls, both boats rising and falling on the same swell, and then Anong unwrapped the case, pulled out her spare shirt, and rewrapped it, her movements precise and unhurried, daring anyone to comment. Nobody commented. The crew of the lead boat watched the case go back into the dry bag with the particular hunger of men watching food they've agreed not to eat, and Petra filed that away: discipline like that came from the top, and wouldn't hold forever.

The scarred man brought his boat alongside one last time as they parted.

"The bend," he reminded her. "One answer."

"Ask."

He studied her for a moment, and whatever he saw — the blacklisted guide, the disgraced scientist, two women in a dying boat with nothing left to lose — seemed to satisfy some private audit of his own.

"The consortium," he said. "Their security boats. Blue hulls, twin engines, they run at night with no lights. Where do they go? Everyone on this river asks and no one answers."

Petra felt Anong go very still beside her.

"They run north of the confluence," Petra said. "Past the third checkpoint. There's a compound on the east bank where the karst hills come down to the water — you can't see it from the main channel, it's screened by the flooded forest, but there's a cut channel through it, dredged. Deep enough for loaded boats at any stage." She paused. "That's where they took the survey team's equipment. And that's where they're holding everything they don't want found."

The scarred man absorbed this without expression, but his knuckles whitened on the gunwale.

"Why tell me this?"

"Because you asked. Because your kids have sores." She shrugged, and it cost her more than the shrug showed. "And because in about thirty days, that information is going to be worth something. To somebody. Maybe to you."

He looked at her a long moment. Then he laughed — short, genuine, surprised out of him — and shouted something to his crews, and the three boats peeled away in their practiced echelon, engines climbing, white wakes unspooling toward the western channel and the dead kapok tree.

At the last moment the scarred man raised one hand, not quite a wave, not quite a salute. Something a captain gives another captain.

Then the rain folded them away, and Petra was alone on the water with Anong and a boat riding noticeably higher for the loss of two jerry cans.

* * *

They ran the east channel anyway — the shallow one, the one she'd warned the pirates off — because the pirates would expect them to take the safe route, and because Petra wanted the shoal water at her back, not ahead of her. Her hull drew less than theirs. It was the kind of petty tactical vanity that kept her feeling like a professional instead of a fugitive.

For an hour neither of them spoke. The bailing resumed. The mist came and went. Somewhere behind them, thunder stacked up along the mountains in slow gray towers, building toward evening.

"He knew about the contamination," Anong said at last. "His whole village knew. Six years."

"People always know. That's the part nobody believes." Petra adjusted the tiller, holding the dark thread of the channel. "Governments don't hide things from people. They hide things *about* people, from each other. The villagers aren't the audience. The investors are."

"You gave him the compound."

"I gave him a location he probably already suspected. What I bought was a story — 'we paid the toll' — that keeps his crew from coming back for the case tomorrow when they're hungrier." She glanced at Anong. "Also, I bought us an hour of feeling clever. Worth every liter of fuel."

Anong almost smiled. It was the first almost-smile since the village burned, and Petra counted it like salvage.

"You were different back there," Anong said. "When they came alongside. You weren't afraid."

"I was terrified."

"No. You were *working*. I watched you. The fear went somewhere and the work came out, like turning a tap." Anong wrung out the bailer, watched the water fall back into the river it had come from. "I want to learn that. Where the fear goes."

"It doesn't go anywhere. You just stop letting it drive." Petra looked downstream, where the light was failing and the first real rain of the evening was stitching the horizon to the water. "You put it in the seat next to you and you tell it to shut up and read the map."

"And if the map's wrong?"

"Then you make a new one." She heard her father's voice in it and didn't flinch, which was new. "That's the whole job. Everything else is weather."

Anong was quiet a moment longer. Then she reached down into the dry bag, past the sample case, and pulled out something Petra hadn't seen her handle in days: her field logbook, swollen with damp, its cover soft as cloth. She opened it on her knees and began, in the fading light, to write — recording the encounter, the toll, the compound confirmation, the scarred man's village and its six years of clean test results. Building the record. Building the chain.

Petra watched her write and felt something loosen in her chest that had been clenched since the village fires. They were still broke, still hunted, still floating on a hull held together by tar and stubbornness, with Serey somewhere behind them and Vuthy silent somewhere ahead. But they'd just talked their way past five guns with a geography lesson and a truth, and the truth had landed like a hook set deep.

The compass rode in her breast pocket, a small hard weight against her ribs. Her father's weight. She found herself thinking, not for the first time, about the strange fit of the case — how the lid sat a hair proud on one side, how the hinge had been resoldered sometime before she'd inherited it, how her father had been a man who hid things in plain sight and called it navigation.

Tonight, on the sandbar where they'd beach, she decided, she'd open it properly. All the way. Take the liner out and see what her father had thought was worth hiding inside a gift he'd pressed into her hands the last time she'd seen him alive.

The rain arrived for real then, drumming on the tarp, and Petra opened the throttle as far as the sick little engine would give, and ran for the light going out of the west.

Behind them, far back in the murk, a blue-hulled boat with twin engines slid out of the flooded forest and turned to follow — running dark, running quiet, exactly where she'd told the pirate captain it would be.
