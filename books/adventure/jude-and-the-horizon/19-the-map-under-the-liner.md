## 19. The Map Under the Liner

The pirates' boat fell away behind them an hour before dark, and by then the rain had started again, soft and businesslike, as if it had only stepped out for a moment and was resuming its shift.

Petra ran the longboat up a side channel where the water braided through drowned cane, found a shelf of mud high enough to sleep on, and killed the engine. The silence afterward was enormous. Her hands were still shaking — not from fear, she told herself, but from the effort of talking six armed men out of shooting them, which was a kind of labor, and labor made you tremble.

Anong sat in the bow with her arms around her knees, watching the channel the way they'd come.

"You gave them the river," she said finally.

"I gave them a shortcut."

"It was a good shortcut." A pause. "You didn't even look at the chart."

Petra almost laughed. "There is no chart for that stretch. There's me, and there's what my father put in my head when I was fourteen, and there's hoping those two things still agree."

Anong looked at her for a long moment, and something in her face shifted — not warmth exactly, but recognition. The look of one professional measuring another and finding the same wear in the joints.

They ate cold rice and dried fish from the pirates' own gift — the leader had pressed a bag of provisions on them at parting, half courtesy, half payment for the navigation lesson, and Petra was pragmatic enough to take it. Then they rigged the tarp between two cane stalks and lay down listening to the rain work the leaves.

Petra did not sleep. She never slept well after a close call; her body treated survival as a debt to be audited. So she sat up under the tarp with the compass in her lap, turning it over, running her thumb along the brass case the way she had every night since Vuthy's hostel, the way she had since she was old enough to be trusted with it.

Her father's compass. The only thing she hadn't pawned.

It was a surveyor's instrument, older than she was — a liquid-damped needle in a brass housing, the bezel worn to bare metal on the east side because her father had been left-handed and checked his bearings against the rising sun more than any sane man needed to. He'd carried it up the Andes and down the Mekong and through a dozen ranges that didn't have names on maps, and he'd given it to her at a bus station in Chiang Rai with both hands, like handing over a child.

She opened the lid. The needle swung, settled, pointed home to magnetic north with the same dumb faithfulness it always had.

And then, because there was nothing else to do with her hands, she picked at the corner of the felt liner inside the lid.

It had always been there. She'd assumed it was padding, or repair — some shop's fix for a cracked hinge, felt glued over the damage. She'd run her thumb past it ten thousand times without once thinking about why a man as precise as Elias Lockridge would let a sloppy patch of glue sit inside a precision instrument.

The corner lifted. The glue was old and had gone brittle, and the felt came away in one piece, and underneath it, written directly on the inside of the brass lid in pencil so fine it was almost engraving, was her father's handwriting.

* * *

Petra held the compass up to the dim light of the covered lantern and read.

Not words, at first. Lines. A hand-drawn reach of river, compressed into three inches of brass: a bend, a confluence, a dotted line leaving the main channel at a place marked with two short strokes. Coordinates in an old datum, six figures, the kind of shorthand field men used when paper was scarce and memory was the real map. Below it, smaller:

*K.S. — sample line. Do not log.*

K.S.

Khao Sen.

The rain got louder, or maybe she just stopped hearing anything else. She turned the lid toward Anong, who had come awake without seeming to move, the way river people do.

"Is this—" Anong began.

"Wait." Petra's voice came out flat and strange. "Look at the coordinates."

Anong took the compass with both hands, careful, the way you handle evidence or a wounded bird. She pulled her waterproof notebook from her shirt, opened it to the page she'd shown Petra a week ago outside the dead camp — the page listing the survey team's off-book sampling sites, the ones that weren't in any official log, the ones the consortium couldn't have known about unless someone talked.

She set the notebook beside the compass lid.

The coordinates matched. All four of them. To the second.

"That's impossible," Anong said, but her voice said she already knew it wasn't. "Your father died before the survey existed. Before the concession was even drafted. How does he have our secret sites?"

"He doesn't have your sites." Petra heard herself speaking slowly, the way she did when the ground was moving. "You have his."

* * *

They sat up the rest of the night, and the story assembled itself piece by piece, the way a wreck assembles itself when you dive it slow.

Elias Lockridge had worked these rivers. Not the tourist stretches, not the border runs — the interior. In the years before Petra was born, and again during the years she'd spent at boarding school resenting him for being gone, he'd taken contracts doing baseline hydrology for companies scouting concessions. It was the family shame nobody named: the man who taught her to read water clean had spent seasons helping the people who planned to dirty it. He'd quit, eventually. She'd known that much. She'd assumed he quit out of conscience.

Now she understood he'd quit out of discovery.

"Do not log," Anong read aloud, tracing the pencil. "He sampled the same tributaries we did. Thirty years earlier." She looked up. "Petra. If he found contamination back then—"

"Then it wasn't new when your team found it." Petra's mouth was dry. "It was old. It was *known*. Somebody buried his data first, and then buried yours on top of it."

Anong went very still. Petra had learned to read her stillness now — it wasn't calm, it was pressure finding a container. "The consortium's parent company," she said. "The one holding the lease. I pulled their registration filings when I still thought paperwork could protect anyone. They've operated in this country under different names for decades. Mining, timber, dredging." Her finger stopped on the compass lid. "Long enough."

"Long enough to hire him."

"Or to employ him. Or to—" Anong stopped herself, which was its own kind of mercy.

But Petra finished it anyway, because she was her father's daughter and she'd never once flinched from an ugly number. "Or to catch him sampling where he shouldn't. Off-log. *Do not log.*" She stared at the tiny river drawn in brass. "He wasn't working for them when he drew this. He was working against them. Quietly. The way you went downstream with samples while your team stayed behind."

The comparison landed between them and neither tried to pick it up. Some inheritances you don't discuss until you've survived them.

* * *

Dawn came gray and dripping. Petra pried the rest of the liner free and found more beneath it — not just the one site but a whole hidden atlas, penciled into the lid and the case's underside and even, impossibly, along the flat of the needle housing itself, in letters so small they might have been machining marks. Seven sites total. Notes on access: which channels ran shallow in dry season, which village headmen could be trusted, which stretch of bank had a rock overhang deep enough to cache a case.

At the bottom of the case, folded into a space she'd never known existed because the felt had been glued over its seam, was a strip of oilcloth. Inside the oilcloth, a single page torn from a surveyor's logbook, soft as cloth with handling.

Her father's handwriting again, but hurried now, the pencil pressed hard:

*If you are reading this, you opened the case, which means you needed it, which means I am not there to explain. The water in the Khao Sen headwaters carries things it should not carry. I measured it twice, ten years apart, and both times the numbers were worse, and both times the men who paid for the measurements explained to me, politely, that the measurements were wrong. They were not wrong. I am putting this where no auditor will look and only family will find. If the rivers ever need a witness, start here.*

He'd signed it with a date. Eleven days before he died.

Petra read it four times. On the fourth reading she noticed her vision had blurred, and she wiped her face with the heel of her hand, annoyed, and kept reading.

Anong watched her without comment. Then she said, quietly: "You thought he was just a guide."

"I thought he was a guide who drank too much and loved rivers more than he loved staying anywhere." Petra folded the page along its original creases, precisely, and put it back in the oilcloth. "Turns out he was hiding evidence inside a gift for me. For years. Waiting for me to pick at a piece of felt."

"Why would he hide it from you?"

"Because knowing this gets you killed, and he'd already lost enough to these rivers." She closed the compass. "Because he wanted me to have the tool, not the target. You don't hand a kid a reason to be brave. You wait until she's stupid enough to find it herself."

Anong smiled — the first full smile Petra had seen from her since the camp. "You're not stupid."

"I'm blacklisted in two countries and floating a leaking boat through pirate water. Don't flatter me."

* * *

They pushed off mid-morning into a river gone the color of strong tea, swollen another half meter overnight. The leak in the hull had worsened — the pirates' wake had worked the seams loose — and they bailed in shifts, Anong with the cut-down jerrican, Petra steering and watching the water level with the grim attention of a woman who has already calculated how far it is to the next high bank.

But her mind wasn't on the bailer. It was on the seven sites.

"Your team's samples," she said, over the drum of rain on the tarp. "The ones you carried downstream. Are they from the same reaches? His reaches?"

"Four of the seven match exactly." Anong bailed, steady, mechanical. "The other three, we chose ourselves. But the four—" She paused. "The four are the ones with the worst numbers. Arsenic, cadmium, things I won't name until a lab confirms them. We thought we'd discovered hot spots. We were walking a trail someone else cut thirty years ago."

"And the chain of custody—"

"Starts with us. Starts with *this*." Anong tapped her sternum. "Which means if we die on this river, the chain dies with us. Which means whoever owns the consortium doesn't need to destroy the samples. They just need to destroy the two women carrying them."

Petra steered around a submerged fence post — a farm's boundary marker standing in what had been a field last month. "Then we should probably stop dying."

"We should probably stop being findable." Anong wrung out the jerrican and sat back on her heels. "Vuthy knows the river. Serey knows the river. Every fixer between here and the capital has a cousin with a boat. We've been lucky twice. Luck is not a plan."

"No," Petra agreed. "Luck is what you spend when you can't afford a plan."

But even as she said it, she was thinking about the notes. *Which channels run shallow in dry season. Which headmen can be trusted.* Her father had moved through this country invisible, carrying worse secrets than theirs, with less money and fewer friends. He'd left instructions. He'd left a route.

Seven sites. Seven safe passages, maybe. Seven doors only a Lockridge would know to knock on.

* * *

Near noon the rain thickened into the vertical, hammering kind, and the river rose visibly while they watched it — brown water climbing the banks in slow licks, whole trees riding the current root-first. The monsoon wasn't coming anymore. It was here, early and angry, and every hour it ate another sandbar, another landing, another option.

They tied up under a rock overhang — one of her father's, though she hadn't known to look for it until yesterday — and ate, and studied the compass lid by lantern light, copying the seven sites into Anong's notebook in case the brass ever left her.

On the fourth site, Anong's pencil stopped.

"This one." She turned the notebook around. "This coordinate. That's not a sampling point."

Petra leaned in. Her father's notation beside the coordinate wasn't depth or flow rate. It was two words, underlined twice:

*The vault.*

"What vault?" Anong asked.

"I don't know." Petra took the compass back and looked at the tiny penciled river one more time — the bend, the confluence, the dotted line leaving the main channel at a place marked with two short strokes. A place her father had considered important enough to hide inside a gift, under a layer of felt, behind eleven days of remaining life.

Somewhere upstream, thunder rolled down the valley like furniture being moved.

"But I think," Petra said, "we just found out where we're going."

Anong looked at the rising river, then at her, and began rolling up the tarp.
