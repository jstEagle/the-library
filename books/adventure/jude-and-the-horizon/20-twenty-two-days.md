## 20. Twenty-Two Days

The permit was a single sheet of paper, and Petra had read it so many times the creases had gone soft as cloth.

She sat in the stern of the longboat, moored in a backwater slough where the river widened into brown stillness and the trees leaned over them like conspirators. Rain came down in that steady, indifferent way it had for three days now — not a storm, just the monsoon settling in for the long haul, as if it had all the time in the world. Which, she supposed, it did.

Thirty-eight days had been the number when she'd found the document in the abandoned survey camp. She'd counted backward from it like a woman counting down to an execution. Thirty-eight days until the consortium's concession expired. Thirty-eight days until the audit data Anong carried in a battered aluminum case became either a weapon or a curiosity. Thirty-eight days until everything they were doing meant something, or stopped meaning anything at all.

Sixteen days had burned since then. Sixteen days of rain, of bailing, of sandbars and checkpoints and a dead man upstream with river water in lungs that had never touched the river.

Twenty-two left.

"You're doing the math again," Anong said from under the tarp they'd rigged over the midsection. She didn't look up from her field logs, which she copied and recopied by pencil light every evening, duplicating everything in case the originals drowned or burned or were taken off her corpse. "I can hear it. It sounds like grinding teeth."

"Twenty-two days," Petra said.

"I know."

"And we've covered maybe half the distance to the capital. Less, if the river stays up like this. The current's fast but the channels are flooded out — I can't run the main stem at night anymore, there's debris in the water. Whole trees coming down. Yesterday we passed a floating gas station roof."

Anong did look up then. Her face was thinner than it had been at knifepoint, ten days and a lifetime ago. "So we need eleven days of travel and we have twenty-two. That leaves margin."

"Margin for what? Another checkpoint? Another ambush?" Petra turned the permit sheet over, as if something new might be written on the back. There wasn't. There never was. "We lost two days to that flooded karst system. We'll lose more. Rivers don't care about our schedule."

"No," Anong agreed mildly. "But deadlines are useful anyway. They tell you what you're willing to sacrifice." She went back to her logs.

Petra wanted to throw the permit into the water. Instead she folded it along its softening creases and put it inside her jacket, against the compass, where she kept everything she couldn't afford to lose.

* * *

They ran the river through the morning because running was better than waiting. Petra took them down channels she only half-remembered from her father's route notes, reading the water the way other people read newspapers — flood lines on the banks, the color changes where a tributary dumped its silt load, the standing waves that marked a submerged fence line nobody had bothered to remove. The longboat leaked the way it always leaked, a slow seep around the transom fitting that no amount of epoxy seemed to cure, and they bailed in shifts without discussing it, the rhythm of it worn into them now like a shared language.

It was past noon when they pulled into a river trading post called Ban Hat Kham — a strip of stilted shacks and fuel drums and a satellite dish bolted crookedly to a roof, the kind of place where the whole economy ran on diesel, dried fish, and information. Petra needed all three. Mostly the third.

There was one phone at Ban Hat Kham, a landline hanging behind the counter of the general store on a cord that had been spliced so many times it looked like a rope bridge. The store owner, a heavy man with a transistor radio murmuring Thai pop beside him, wanted five hundred baht for a call to the provincial capital. Petra paid eight hundred and asked him to leave the room.

Vuthy answered on the fourth ring.

"It's me," Petra said.

A pause. On the line she could hear the capital behind him — traffic, a generator, the particular acoustic hum of a place with too many people and not enough drainage. Then: "Where are you calling from?"

"Does it matter?"

"It matters to me." His voice was flat in a way she'd never heard from him before. Vuthy was a man who performed every emotion at volume — his outrage, his generosity, his wounded pride. Flatness wasn't in his repertoire. Flatness was what happened when the performance ended.

"The money," Petra said. "I need to know about the extraction. We're moving slower than planned. The monsoon—"

"I know about the monsoon."

Another silence. Longer. Somewhere behind him a door closed.

"Vuthy." She made her voice careful, level, the voice she used with clients on bad mountains when panic was contagious. "Talk to me. What's happening?"

What he said was: "Don't call this number again."

Then the line went dead. Not hung up abruptly — set down gently, deliberately, the click of a receiver placed in its cradle by someone who wanted her to know it was final.

Petra stood holding the phone for a long moment. The store owner had come back and was watching her with the frank, incurious stare of a man who had watched a thousand hard conversations end badly on his telephone.

"Bad news?" he asked, in the tone of a man who didn't actually care.

"Yeah," Petra said. She put another bill on the counter. "You got a radio?"

* * *

She tried Vuthy twice more that day, once from the store and once from a boat mechanic who let her use his handheld set in exchange for helping him re-seat a propeller shaft. Both times, nothing. Not even the flat voice. Just static, and the sense of a door already closed being locked behind her.

By evening she had worked through the possibilities, and none of them were good.

One: Vuthy was dead. Men like Vuthy had a shelf life, and brokers who played both ends of disappearing acts had shorter shelves than most. But if he were dead, someone would likely have used his phone to find out who was calling. The gentle hang-up suggested otherwise.

Two: Vuthy had been bought. Serey's people had gotten to him — money, threats, or some arithmetic of both — and the flat voice on the phone was a man who had already decided which side of the ledger she stood on. This was the possibility that sat in Petra's stomach like swallowed river water. Because Vuthy hadn't brokered her contract out of kindness. He'd brokered it because finding the survey team served someone's interest, and she had never asked hard enough whose.

Three: Vuthy had sold her. Not bought — sold. Actively, for a price, with a location attached. And if that was true, then every hour they spent on this river was an hour someone else spent triangulating.

"You think he sold us," Anong said. They were moored again, upstream of the trading post, hidden in a channel choked with water hyacinth where the boat would be invisible from the main stem unless someone came looking — and someone coming looking was precisely what Petra was afraid of.

"I think he went quiet," Petra said. "Quiet is worse than angry. Angry means you still matter to him."

"He brokered your contract. He found you in a border-town hostel with nothing but a compass and debt. Why would he do that if he was already inside it?"

That was the question, and Petra had been chewing it since the phone went dead. "Because whoever hired him wanted the team found," she said slowly, working it through aloud. "Or wanted *me* to find them. Or—" She stopped. Started again. "Or the finder's fee was bait. A way to flush out whatever survived the disappearance. The samples. A witness. You."

Anong was quiet for a while. The hyacinth knocked softly against the hull. Downriver, thunder muttered — not close, but getting closer, the monsoon gathering itself for another act.

"If that's true," Anong said finally, "then everything we've done since the camp has been visible. Every call. Every checkpoint. Every trading post." She looked at Petra across the small dark space of the boat. "He knew where we'd call from. He knows how you think. He built the route you're following."

Petra's hands had gone cold, and it wasn't the rain.

* * *

They didn't sleep much. Around two in the morning Petra gave up pretending and climbed out under the tarp to stand watch, and found Anong already awake, sitting with the sample case between her knees like a woman holding a sleeping child.

"Tell me honestly," Anong said. "If you were alone, would you keep going?"

Petra considered lying and decided the night was too dark for it. "I don't know. Two weeks ago, yes. Now I've seen what they do to villages. To rivers. To survey teams." She paused. "Yes. I'd keep going. But I'd be lying if I said the payout wasn't part of why I started."

"At least you're honest about it." Anong rested her chin on her knees. "My brother sent word through a fisherman today. He says men have been asking about us in the villages downstream. Two of them. Polite ones. One carries a leather folder."

Serey carried a leather folder. Petra had seen it, had watched him lay documents on a table like a priest laying out communion — clean passport photos, bank drafts, a life laundered and gift-wrapped. If he was downstream, he wasn't chasing anymore. He was waiting. Waiting men with folders didn't need to chase; they just closed the exits one by one.

"Which villages?"

"Ours is the fourth one down. He won't stop there. He knows my family's name."

Petra looked out at the black water, at the rain beginning again, softer now, almost apologetic. Twenty-two days. Half a river to cover. No extraction, no money, no fixer. A leaking boat, a stranger who had become something else — she didn't have a word for what yet — and an aluminum case full of dying samples that constituted the only truth anyone would believe.

"We cut south at the next junction," she said. "The main stem's watched. My father's notes show an old logging canal — narrow, shallow, ugly, but it bypasses forty kilometers of river and every village on it. It puts us near the coast road. From there we can move faster than any folder-carrying man expects."

"Can the boat take it?"

"The boat can take anything for eleven days." She glanced at the transom, where the seep had begun its patient work again. "Whether it takes twelve is another question."

Anong almost smiled. Almost. "And after the canal?"

"After the canal," Petra said, "we find out whether Vuthy sold us, or whether somebody just wants us to think he did. Either way, we stop trusting phones, we stop trusting posts, and we stop trusting anyone who knows our names."

"That's a short list of people left."

"It's the right length," Petra said. "It's got you in it. Me in it. That's enough crew for a boat this size."

Thunder rolled closer, and the first real gust of the storm hit the hyacinth beds and set them hissing. In the east, behind the rain, the sky had gone the color of old tin. Petra checked the compass out of habit, though there was nothing yet to navigate toward — just the number in her head, ticking down like water into a bilge.

Twenty-two days. And somewhere behind them, in the dark, the river was carrying news of their position faster than any current could.

At dawn, as she coiled the mooring line, Petra noticed it: fresh boot prints in the mud of the bank, two sets, heading toward their hiding place from the direction of the trading post — and stopping, just short, as if whoever had made them had heard them talking, and turned back to report.
