## 21. The Sample Case

The rain had found a rhythm by the third day of the countdown, a steady percussion on the tin roof of the stilt house where they'd holed up above the ferry landing. Petra sat on the floor with her back against the wall, cleaning the compass with a rag that was more grease than cloth, and watched Anong do something she hadn't done in two weeks of traveling together.

Anong was opening the sample case.

She'd been carrying it since the camp, since the night at knifepoint, since everything — a hard-shell Pelican-style box, scuffed silver, heavy as a sleeping child. She'd slept with it strapped to her wrist. She'd refused to let it out of arm's reach even when they were bailing the boat in chest-deep water, even when Petra had asked — once, carefully, only once — what was actually inside.

*Water,* Anong had said. *And proof.*

Now she laid it flat on the table under the single bulb, wiped her palms on her trousers like a surgeon prepping, and released the latches one at a time. The hinges gave a small, tired sound. Petra set down the compass without being asked and came to stand beside her.

Inside, rows of small glass vials sat nested in foam, each one labeled in cramped handwriting, each one sealed with paraffin wax over the cap. Maybe sixty of them. Petra had imagined something more dramatic — gold bars, documents, a ledger of sins. What she saw was a box of muddy water.

"This is it," she said. Not a question. She kept her voice level because she could hear what was underneath her own words: *this is what we nearly died for, this is what Serey offered me a passport for, this is a box of river.*

"This is it," Anong said. "Every sampling site on the upper Sen. Tailings seepage from the consortium's processing ponds. Heavy metals — arsenic, cadmium, mercury at levels I still don't fully believe." Her finger hovered above the vials without touching them. "Chain of custody starts here. My initials, date, site code, sealed in the field, witnessed by Dr. Vannak before—" She stopped. Started again. "Before."

Petra waited. She'd learned that about Anong: there were rooms in her she locked mid-sentence, and you didn't pick those locks. You waited by the door.

"Vannak," Petra said instead. "The team leader. The one we found upstream."

"The one they put in the river." Anong's jaw worked. "He signed every seal. He's the accredited lab liaison. Without his signature on the transfer forms, the samples are just my word in glass."

"They have his signature," Petra said slowly. "On the seals. That's not nothing."

"It's not enough." Anong lifted one vial to the light. The liquid inside was the color of weak tea, a fine sediment hanging in it like weather. "Here's the problem nobody tells you about field chemistry. These aren't preserved. We didn't have fixative for the full run — Vannak sent most of our reagent budget downstream with me and kept the cheap stuff at camp, because he thought he'd be back at the provincial lab within a week." A pause. "It's been nine weeks."

Petra felt it before Anong said it, the way you feel a slope change under your boots before your eyes confirm it.

"How many are still good?"

Anong turned the case toward her and began moving down the rows, tilting each vial, reading the sediment line against a mark she'd scratched into the glass herself. Her lips moved as she counted. The rain filled the silence. Somewhere below the stilts, the river muttered against its banks, higher than yesterday. Petra could smell it — green rot and silt, the monsoon doing its work.

"Twenty-six," Anong said finally. "Maybe twenty-eight if I'm wrong about two of these, and I don't think I am." She set the vial down with exaggerated care, the way you handle something already broken. "Half the run is degraded past evidentiary value. The metals have adsorbed onto the sediment or precipitated out. I can't prove what was dissolved in that water anymore. A defense lab will shred us. They'll say improper storage, thermal degradation, contamination after collection — and they'll be right."

"So half the evidence is dead."

"Half the evidence never lived long enough." Anong closed her eyes briefly. "I did the math on the boat, weeks ago. I've been carrying the number around like a stone. Twenty-six viable samples out of fifty-four collected. And every one of the twenty-six has an expiration now, too. The wax seals aren't sterile forever. Evaporation through the cap threads. Microbial action changing the speciation. In another month, maybe six weeks, even the good ones start becoming arguable."

Petra looked at the case differently then. Not a treasure. A fuse.

* * *

They spread it all out on the table that night while the storm walked circles around the roof: the survey documents Petra had copied at the camp, the permit with its expiry date, Anong's field logs in their oilcloth wrapping, and the map — the real one, the one from inside her father's compass liner, with his route notes running along the same rivers the survey had sampled twenty years apart.

Anong had gone very quiet when Petra first showed her the map, three nights ago. She'd touched her father's handwriting — a stranger's handwriting — and said, *Your father sampled site fourteen. His note says 'water tastes sweet here, don't trust it.' Site fourteen is my worst arsenic reading.* Some inheritances arrive like that: a dead man's warning reaching across decades to confirm a living woman's data.

Now Anong laid the viable vials out in a row, twenty-six small candles at a wake, and matched each to its log entry.

"Chain of custody," she said, tapping the log. "Collection, sealing, transport, analysis. Every hand the evidence passes through has to be documented, or opposing counsel calls it broken chain and the whole thing dies. Right now the chain reads: collected by the survey team, sealed by Vannak, carried downstream by me, held in unspecified conditions for nine weeks." She grimaced. "'Unspecified conditions' includes a leaking longboat, a flooded cave, and three days inside my shirt at body temperature during the worst storm of the year."

"You're saying a lawyer would laugh."

"I'm saying a lawyer would bill us for the laughing." Anong straightened. "But here's what survives. If the samples reach an accredited laboratory — not a government lab, the government is leased to the consortium until the permit expires, I mean a genuinely independent one — and if they're analyzed *before* degradation advances further, and if the analysis matches my field readings within tolerance, then the field logs plus the lab results plus Vannak's seals become a documented chain. Imperfect. Attackable. But real. Real enough that burying it costs more than admitting it."

"And if we miss the window?"

"Then in forty days the permit expires, the consortium restructures under a new name, the ponds get capped or abandoned, and every vial in this case becomes a souvenir." Anong's voice didn't rise. It got flatter, which Petra had learned was worse. "There's no second collection. The sites are underwater right now. Even after the monsoon, the tailings will have flushed downstream and diluted. This is the only evidence that will ever exist that the water was poisoned *while they held the lease.*"

Petra stood and walked to the window. Below, the river had climbed the bank and taken the lower steps of the landing. Two weeks ago it had been a brown ribbon; now it was a brown country. She thought about distances. The provincial capital was four days downstream in good water. In this water, with this boat, with checkpoints and Serey's men somewhere behind them and Vuthy silent for eleven days —

Eleven days. She kept coming back to that number. Fixers didn't go silent. Fixers went quiet when they were counting someone else's money.

"There's a lab at the university," Anong said behind her. "Independent enough. Dr. Chenda runs the analytical chemistry unit — she trained me, she hates the consortium, and she owes me nothing, which makes her trustworthy. But she leaves for a conference in Singapore in nineteen days. After that, no accredited analyst touches these samples for a month."

"Nineteen days." Petra turned around. "Four days' travel if the river behaves. It won't behave. Call it seven. Checkpoints, call it eight."

"That leaves eleven days of margin."

"Margin shrinks." Petra came back to the table and put her finger on the map, on the stretch of river below the karst country where the channel braided and drowned forests swallowed the banks. "Everything shrinks. The river rises, the boat gets slower, every stop costs a day we don't have. And Vuthy's silence means somebody's paying for information about where we sleep." She looked up. "We need to know exactly which vials matter most. If we lose the case — if it goes in the river, if it's taken from us — what's the minimum that convicts?"

Anong was quiet for a long moment. Then she began sorting the row of vials into groups, quick and certain, a woman who had spent her career making decisions about water.

"These five." She lined them apart. "Sites twelve, fourteen, sixteen, nineteen, twenty-two. The direct seepage points below the processing ponds. Highest concentrations, cleanest provenance, Vannak personally witnessed the collection at all five. If only five survive, these five convict them." She pushed a second group forward. "These nine are the downstream gradient — they show the plume spreading toward the villages. They prove harm, not just violation. And these twelve corroborate. Redundancy. If we can save all twenty-six, the case is strong. Five, it's alive. Fewer than five—"

She didn't finish. She didn't need to.

Petra looked at the five vials. Small enough to fit in a shirt pocket. Worth more than the finder's fee, more than the debt, more than the passport Serey had promised. She thought about how easy it would be to carry them separately from the case — split the risk, the way you split a rope party on a glacier so one crevasse doesn't take everyone.

"We split them," she said. "The decoy case stays whole — all the degraded ones, the paperwork, the logs that look complete. Anyone who takes the case takes thirty vials of mud and a paper trail we can afford to burn. The live five travel on your body. The other twenty-one stay with me, wrapped, close to skin, warm but not hot."

Anong stared at her. "You'd carry contaminated water against your chest for four hundred kilometers."

"I've carried worse ideas closer than that." Petra allowed herself half a smile. "Besides, it's not contaminated. It's *documented.*"

That earned her the first real laugh in days, short and startled, gone almost before it arrived. But something in the room changed with it — the way a rope comes taut. They were no longer two people fleeing with a box. They were a team with a plan and a deadline, and deadlines, Petra knew, were the only honest things left in this country.

* * *

They repacked the case by lamplight, working in silence, each movement deliberate as a ritual. Anong re-waxed the five critical seals herself, breathing steadily, hands steady, murmuring site codes under her breath like prayers. Petra wrapped the twenty-one in strips of cotton padding and a waterproof groundsheet, then sewed them into a padded bandolier harness cut from the boat's spare fuel-line cover — ugly, functional, tight against the ribs. When she shrugged it on, she looked like a woman wearing a rib cage of glass.

Anong checked the fit, tugged a strap, and stepped back. Her face did something complicated.

"When Vannak handed me the case at the camp," she said, "he told me: *Don't be brave. Be exact.* I thought it was a strange thing to say. He was about to stay behind with men coming up the trail, and his last instruction to me was about precision." She looked down at the case. "I hated him a little, for that. For sending me away with the water while he stayed with the people. I did the arithmetic every night since — my life for his, my life for theirs, and the numbers never come out clean."

"They never do," Petra said. She thought of the Andes, of a client's name she still couldn't say aloud, of a rescue that went wrong in ways that were partly her fault and entirely her burden. "The arithmetic never comes out clean. You just keep carrying it."

"Is that what you do? Carry it?"

"Every day." Petra met her eyes. "But I'll tell you what I've learned carrying it. Guilt is a bad navigator. It walks you in circles and calls the circles penance. What works better is a bearing." She tapped the case. "This is a bearing. Capital. Nineteen days. Lab before expiry. Everything else is terrain."

Anong nodded slowly. Outside, the rain thickened, and beneath the rain they both heard it — the river, still rising, patient as arithmetic of its own kind.

"One more thing," Anong said as she latched the case shut. "Dr. Chenda will need the transfer forms countersigned. Chain of custody. The moment these samples leave my custody, the documentation has to show who took them, when, where, and why." She pulled a blank form from the oilcloth and smoothed it on the table, then hesitated, pen hovering. "If you carry them, part of the chain is yours. Your name, Petra. Written down. In a document that could end up in front of an inquiry, a court, a consortium's lawyers."

Petra thought about her record. Blacklisted twice. A debt she couldn't pay. A name that opened doors nowhere and closed them everywhere else. The smart move — the move the old Petra, the paid-out Petra, the woman who'd taken Vuthy's contract to make rent — would be to stay off the paper. Stay deniable. Let the hydrologist own the evidence and the risk.

She picked up the pen.

"Print or cursive?" she said.

Anong wrote the date, the time, the place, the count: *twenty-six viable specimens, transferred in part.* Then she slid the form across, and Petra signed beneath it — her full name, legible, unhidden, the first time in years she'd put it on anything that mattered.

The ink needed a moment to dry. The rain hammered. Downriver, four hundred kilometers of floodwater lay between them and a woman named Chenda who would leave in nineteen days, and somewhere in the dark between here and there, whatever Vuthy had sold and whoever Serey had hired was already moving.

Petra folded the transfer form into the oilcloth with the logs, shouldered the glass against her ribs, and looked at the map one last time.

"We leave at first light," she said. "Before the river gets any bigger ideas."

Anong blew out the lamp. In the dark, the five critical vials rode against Anong's heartbeat, and twenty-one more rode against Petra's, and neither of them slept much — but for the first time since the camp, the fear had a shape, and shapes, unlike fogs, can be navigated.

At dawn the river had risen another half meter overnight, brown and muscular, tearing at the landing steps. The old boatman who'd agreed to take them took one look at the water and doubled his price.

Petra paid it without haggling, which frightened her more than the river did.
