## 22. Monsoon Peak

The storm came at them sideways off the ridge like something with intent.

Petra had watched weather her whole life — read it the way other people read faces — and she had never seen a sky organize itself this fast. One hour the monsoon was the usual gray ceiling, steady and businesslike. The next, the clouds over the karst towers went black-green and began to rotate, slow as a thought, and Anong said quietly, "That's not rain anymore."

"No," Petra said. "It isn't."

They were on the river between two limestone massifs, the longboat riding high and light because they'd eaten most of the food, and there was nowhere to run to. The banks were vertical jungle. The nearest village was half a day downstream in water that was about to become a weapon. Behind them, somewhere upriver, Serey's people were still looking for them, which meant the only direction worse than forward was back.

Then Petra saw it: a black seam in the cliff face on the left bank, maybe four meters above the current, half-hidden by a curtain of vines. A cave mouth. She'd been watching for one since noon, because you always watch for one when the karst starts closing in — every guide who'd worked these rivers knew the limestone country hid refuges the way a fist hides a coin.

"There." She put the boat across the current before Anong could ask.

The climb up the wet rock with the sample case strapped to Petra's chest and the fuel can in one hand was forty seconds that felt like an hour. The river grabbed at their ankles the whole way, pulling with a strength that hadn't existed twenty minutes ago. Then they were inside, and the world outside turned into noise.

* * *

The cave went back farther than the light did.

Petra got the headlamp going and walked in far enough to understand what they'd found: not a pocket but a system, a throat of white limestone opening into chambers where the water had been carving for a million years. Stalactites hung in ranks like organ pipes. The floor sloped gently upward, away from the entrance, and thirty meters in, the air was dry and smelled of stone and bat guano and nothing else.

"High enough?" Anong asked. She'd stopped shivering, which Petra knew was the wrong sign. You wanted people to shiver. Shivering meant the body still believed it could fix things.

"We'll see." Petra set the sample case on a dry ledge above a tide line of old flood debris — silt, driftwood, a plastic sandal bleached gray. "This mark is from years ago. But the river doesn't care about averages."

The first night was just loud. Rain like gravel thrown against the mountain, thunder rolling around inside the karst so the whole cave hummed, water roaring past the entrance in a brown ribbon wider than the river had any right to be. They ate half a bag of rice cooked over a whisper of flame, because even smoke was a risk if anyone was watching the banks, and lay side by side under the tarp with their backs to the stone and listened to the world try to drown itself.

Sometime in the dark, Anong said, "My grandmother used to say the monsoon is the year holding its breath."

"Your grandmother sounds smarter than most hydrologists."

"She was. She just didn't have a degree to prove it." A pause. "Neither do I anymore, technically."

"You have the data."

"I have the data." Anong's voice was flat in the dark. "Some days I think that's all I am now. A container."

Petra didn't answer. Outside, the thunder cracked so close it wasn't sound anymore, just pressure, a fist closing around the mountain. She found Anong's hand without deciding to. Anong let her.

* * *

On the second day the water came in.

Petra woke to a sound she'd spent her whole career learning to hate: the pitch of the river changing, dropping, going deep. She crawled to the entrance in the gray light and saw that the brown ribbon had climbed six meters overnight and was now lapping at the base of their ledge, swirling with whole trees, a dead pig, half a roof. The cave had become a grotto. The entrance was a waterfall's window.

"It's going to come up the slope," she said.

"How far?"

"Depends how long the peak holds." She marked the waterline on the wall with a knife scratch and checked her watch. "We measure. Every hour. If it climbs more than a meter an hour, we go higher and we don't stop to argue about it."

They went higher twice that day. The second time, the water followed them into the third chamber, cold and fast around their knees, carrying silt that stank of drowned fields. The sample case rode on Petra's chest again, dry under its wrap, and she caught herself thinking about it the way she used to think about clients in trouble — as a life she was responsible for, heavier than its weight.

By evening the rise had stalled. The water stood a meter below their camp, breathing — advancing, retreating, advancing — and Petra sat on the highest dry rock with the knife in her hand and made herself do the arithmetic she'd been avoiding.

Food: a handful of rice, three protein bars, a tin of fish, some salt. Two days, carefully. Three, uncomfortably.

Water: infinite, and undrinkable. The river was a chemical soup of topsoil and whatever the consortium's mines bled into it, and drinking it was a slower way of dying than starving. They had the filter, barely functioning, clogging hourly with silt. Each liter cost them twenty minutes of pumping.

Light: one headlamp, a candle stub, half a bottle of fuel.

And the storm showed no sign of ending. It had settled in over the karst like a tenant, raining with the patience of something that owned the lease.

Anong sat down beside her on the rock. Her feet were cut from the climb, the cuts going soft at the edges. Petra cleaned them with the last of the alcohol wipes without asking, and Anong watched her work and said nothing until she was done.

"You're good at this part," Anong said.

"This part's easy. This part has rules."

"And the rest?"

"The rest is just this part with worse lighting."

Anong laughed — a real laugh, surprised out of her, brief and rough — and then pressed her sleeve to her eyes as if the laugh had opened something else. "I keep calculating," she said. "I can't stop. Probability we get out. Probability the samples are still viable. Probability anyone will ever care. My whole career I reduced rivers to numbers and now I've done it to us."

"What's the number?"

"For surviving?"

"For whether it was worth it. The team. Your brother's village. All of it."

Anong was quiet for a long time. The water breathed below them. Somewhere deeper in the cave, bats shifted and resettled, a dry rustle like old paper.

"I'll tell you when I know if we live," she said finally.

"That's the honest answer," Petra said. "Most people would have made one up."

* * *

The third day, the storm broke something loose in the mountain.

It started as a vibration in the rock, low and wrong, felt in the teeth before the ears. Petra was awake instantly, hand on the knife, listening. The vibration built, and then from somewhere behind the fourth chamber came a boom like a door slamming in the earth, and dust breathed out of the dark, fine and white, tasting of chalk.

"Rockfall," Anong whispered. "Deep in?"

"Deep enough. But it means the system's shifting." Petra played the headlamp along the waterline, which had crept up another half meter during the night. "And the water's still rising. Slower. But rising."

They spent the morning moving everything up to a shelf near the ceiling of the fifth chamber — a natural balcony of flowstone wide enough for two bodies and a case, reached by a scramble up slick rock. From there they could watch the floodwater pool in the chamber below like a brown lake filling a bowl. It was, Petra admitted to herself, a beautiful place to be trapped, if you had to pick one. The stalactites came down in frozen cascades. The lamplight turned the calcite to amber and honey. If you ignored the lake, the cold, the hunger, and the fact that the exit was underwater, it was almost a cathedral.

Almost.

They rationed the rice into two spoonfuls each at midday. Anong gave Petra half of her protein bar and lied about having already eaten hers, and Petra let her believe the lie had worked, and understood that this was also a kind of intimacy — two people lying to each other kindly, in the dark, to keep each other alive.

In the afternoon, the candle between them, Anong talked. Not about the data or the consortium or the team. About small things. A dog she'd had as a girl that swam the river every day to follow her to school. The taste of the sticky rice in her mother's kitchen. The way her father had looked at her when she'd left for university — proud and bereaved at once, like a man watching a bird he'd raised decide the cage was optional.

"He died two years ago," she said. "Before the survey. Before all this. He never had to see what I became."

"A disgraced hydrologist hiding in a cave?"

"Someone who chose numbers over people." She turned the candle stub in her fingers. "When Kiet sent me downstream with the samples, I didn't argue. Do you understand? Eleven people were about to be erased and my first feeling was relief. Relief that it was him making the call and not me. That's what I've been carrying. Not guilt about leaving. Guilt about being glad."

Petra looked at her a long moment. The candlelight moved on both their faces.

"In the Andes," Petra said, "there were eleven seconds. Maybe twelve. Between when I heard the rope team call and when the serac came down. Twelve seconds to choose who to reach." She heard her own voice go flat and distant, the voice she used for altitudes. "I chose the client who paid. Tomás, the local porter who was also on the rope, died. The family doesn't blame me for the ice. They blame me for the arithmetic. So do I."

"Do you think it gets lighter?"

"No." Petra said it without cruelty. "I think you get stronger shoulders."

Anong reached over and took her hand again, and this time neither of them pretended it was for warmth.

* * *

The water peaked on the third night.

Petra knew it the way she knew a summit — not by seeing it but by the absence of the thing she'd been braced against. The lake below stopped climbing. For an hour it held, trembling at its high-water mark, licking the base of their shelf, close enough that spray misted the sample case. Then, near dawn, it dropped a centimeter. Then another.

"Turning," Petra said hoarsely. She'd been awake thirty hours. "It's turning."

Anong surfaced from sleep like a diver. "How long till the entrance clears?"

"Hours. Maybe a day. And then the river out there won't be a river, it'll be a landslide with a current." Petra worked her jaw, thinking. "But it'll be moving our direction. And nobody pursues anything in this."

They slept in shifts after that, properly, for the first time in three days, and when Petra woke the light at the cave mouth had gone from black to gray to a washed, exhausted silver, and the roar had softened into something merely enormous.

She stood at the entrance and looked out at the new world. The valley was a single brown sea from cliff to cliff, unrecognizable, wearing the forest like a hat. Trees moved in it, whole and sailing. The air smelled scoured. Somewhere under all that water were the banks, the channel, every landmark she'd been navigating by — gone, redrawn, unmapped.

Her father's compass hung at her neck. She pulled it out and opened it, an old habit, and stared at the needle swinging lazy circles in the flooded valley's magnetic silence, and thought: *he ran water like this once. It's in his notes. It's in my blood.*

Behind her, Anong said, "Petra."

Something in her voice. Petra turned.

Anong was standing by the sample case, and she was holding the wrap open, and her face had gone the color of the limestone.

"There's a problem," she said. "With the samples. I need to show you something."
