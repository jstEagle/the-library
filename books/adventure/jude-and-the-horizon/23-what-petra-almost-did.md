## 23. What Petra Almost Did

On the third day, Petra caught herself doing arithmetic with the sample case.

Not consciously. That was the thing about hunger — it worked on you below the waterline, the way floodwater worked on a bank, silent and patient until the whole structure shifted. She'd been sitting on the limestone shelf with her back against the cave wall, watching the brown water climb another finger's width up the rock, and when she came back to herself she was holding the aluminum case in her lap, weighing it, thinking: *sixteen kilos. A man could carry that. A man could trade that.*

She set it down like it had bitten her.

Across the flooded channel, Anong lay on a flat of dry stone with her knees drawn up, conserving everything — movement, words, heat. Three days in the cave system and she'd stopped talking somewhere in the middle of the second one, not from anger but from the same cold economy that had made her a good field scientist. Calories in, calories out. Every sentence was a withdrawal.

The cave was a cathedral the river had been building for a million years, and right now it was a cathedral with a congregation of two and a congregation of nothing else. The monsoon had driven them in off the river on the ninth day of the flood, a wall of rain so dense it had made a sound like static, and the karst had swallowed them the way karst swallows everything — one moment a channel between drowned hills, the next a black throat opening in the limestone, and then the storm had closed the door behind them.

The water had risen since. It was still rising.

Petra did the inventory again, because the inventory was the only thing she could control. Two energy bars, saved for emergencies, and the question of what constituted an emergency had gotten philosophical around noon. Half a bottle of water purification tablets. Petra's knife. The compass, which was not food but which she found herself holding anyway, the way you'd hold a hand. Anong's field logs, wrapped in plastic. The sample case: eleven vials that had survived the heat and the water and the weeks, the chain of custody written in Anong's cramped hand, the only physical proof on earth that the Khao Sen rivers were carrying the mining consortium's poison into a hundred villages.

Sixteen kilos. The most valuable sixteen kilos in the province, and worth exactly nothing to the two women starving in the dark.

*Everything is tradeable,* her father used to say. *The trick is knowing what you're trading it for.* He'd said it about gear, mostly. About the time he'd traded a sleeping bag for a mule crossing in the north and slept cold for a week and called it a fair deal. He'd never meant it the way it was meaning itself now, in Petra's head, in the dark, with her stomach eating itself.

She heard the engine before she understood what she was hearing.

* * *

It came down the flooded channel outside the cave mouth — a long, low, diesel idle, the sound of a boat moving slow and careful in high water. Not the consortium's boats; those ran big outboard engines, aggressive, fast. This was a trader's engine, the kind of puttering single-cylinder workhorse that moved rice and fuel and cooking oil up every river in the country during monsoon, when the water was too high for the roads and the traders were the only supply line left.

Petra was on her feet before she'd decided to stand. The cave mouth was a ragged arch maybe four meters across, and through it she could see the channel running brown and fast between drowned trees, and then — a flash of blue tarp, a man in a poncho at the tiller, a boat piled with covered cargo riding low and heavy.

She didn't think. That was the honest version, the version she would tell later, and the version she would tell herself at three in the morning for the rest of her life: she didn't think. She stepped to the mouth of the cave and she shouted.

"Hey! *Hey!*"

The boat didn't stop. Of course it didn't — a woman screaming from a cave in flood season was a bad omen or a ghost or a trap, and traders survived by not stopping. But the man turned his head, and she saw him see her, and she saw the moment his hand moved toward the throttle.

"Please!" She waded out onto the lip of stone, water to her knees, arms up. "I have something to trade! Fuel! Medicine! I'll pay —"

The boat slowed. It shouldn't have. She knew it shouldn't have, and later she would understand that the reason it slowed was the same reason everything in this country slowed for the right words, and the word was *trade*, and the man was already doing his own arithmetic.

He brought the boat in against the current with real skill, nosing it into an eddy behind a limestone fin, and let it drift toward the cave mouth with the engine ticking over. He was fifty, maybe, weathered down to sinew, with a plastic poncho over a work shirt and the flat, unfooled eyes of a man who had spent thirty years on rivers that occasionally lied to him.

"You are stuck," he said. Not a question. His eyes went past her, into the dark, counting. "Two of you. How many days?"

"Three."

He looked at them — at her, and at Anong, who had sat up and was watching with her arms wrapped around her knees — and he looked at the water, which was still rising, and Petra watched him arrive at the same number she'd been avoiding. The cave mouth was four meters above the current flood line. The banks of the karst system funneled the whole catchment through this channel. Two more days of this rain and the water wouldn't be at the cave mouth. It would be through it, and then it would be at the shelf, and then it would be at the high gallery, and then there would be nowhere left that was dry, and two women who hadn't eaten in three days were not going to swim out of a flooded cave system in monsoon current.

"Two more days," the trader said, "maybe three, this water drops. Maybe it doesn't." He shrugged, and the shrug contained the entire hydrology of the province. "I can take you down to Ban Sok. Two hours. There's food there, a road."

"What do you want for it?"

He smiled without any humor in it at all. "You have money?"

"A little."

"A little buys a little. A little buys you standing on the bank at Ban Sok with wet feet." He looked past her again, into the cave, and this time his eyes found the sample case, because Petra had carried it out with her without deciding to, because her hands had decided, and it sat on the stone beside her boot, sixteen kilos of aluminum with a survey-team stencil on the lid.

The trader looked at it for a long time.

"Government case," he said.

"It's mine."

"Government case," he said again, gently, the way you'd correct a child. "I know the stencil. I carried those men's fuel up the river in the spring." He turned back to her, and now his face had changed — not hostile, nothing so simple. Interested. A man who had spent his life on the water had heard things, the way everyone on the water had heard things, and the things he'd heard had made him careful, and careful men did not carry government cases out of flooded caves without asking what they were.

Petra heard herself say: "Name your price."

* * *

Later — much later, on other rivers, in other lives — she would try to reconstruct the exact sequence of the next ninety seconds, and she would never quite get it straight, because the truth was that all of it happened at once, the way a rockfall happens at once.

He named a price. That was the first thing. He said: "The case. The case and I take you both to Ban Sok, feed you, put you on the road, and I never saw you." He said it reasonably, the way Serey had been reasonable, the way Vuthy was reasonable, because in her experience the devil had stopped bothering with contracts signed in blood a long time ago and had moved on to fair market value. "That case is worth more than my boat. Whatever's in it, somebody will pay me more than you can, and I won't have to know what it is. That's a good trade. That's the best trade you're going to get in this cave."

And the second thing was that Petra said nothing, and her silence was a yes. She knew it was a yes. She stood there with the flood roaring past the cave mouth and her stomach clenched around nothing and her mind — the professional mind, the guide's mind, the mind that had gotten her blacklisted twice because it never stopped calculating — laid it out in clean columns like a route plan.

*The case buys two hours downstream, food, a road. The case buys Anong out of this cave alive. The data is half-degraded anyway; the chain of custody is already broken; a lab report from a dead survey means nothing without a witness, and witnesses can be found other ways. Sixteen kilos of aluminum against two lives, and one of the lives is the only person who can testify to what the water knows. This is not even a hard calculation.*

That was the horror of it. It wasn't a hard calculation. It was the kind of calculation she'd been making her whole career — weight against margin, risk against reward, the client's summit fever against the weather window — and every time before, the arithmetic had been about a mountain, and mountains didn't care, and neither did she, and that had been the whole architecture of her life: competent, unattached, unaccountable.

The case sat there. The trader waited, patient as water.

And the third thing — the thing that happened at once with the other two, the thing she'd never be able to sequence — was Anong's voice from the dark of the cave, quiet, hoarse from three days of disuse, not angry, not pleading, just *present*:

"Petra."

Just her name. That was all. And it was enough, because Petra turned and looked back into the cave and saw Anong sitting up on the stone with her knees drawn in, and behind Anong, in the black, the flooded channel they'd come in by, and beyond that, downstream, a village where the children had lesions on their arms from the water, and a dead man named Chai who'd been staged as a drowning in a river that hadn't flooded yet, and a team of seven people who had been made to disappear so cleanly that even their footprints had been erased, and Petra understood — with the clarity that hunger and fear and the smell of limestone water give you, a clarity she would spend the rest of her life trying to get back — that the case was not sixteen kilos of aluminum.

The case was the only reason any of those people had died for anything.

If she traded it, they'd died for nothing. Not for evil, even. For a trader's boat fare. For two hours downstream and a plate of rice. The pettiness of it was what stopped her — not the morality, which she could have argued with, but the *arithmetic*, which she couldn't. Her father's voice, or her memory of it, or whatever the hunger had made of it: *The trick is knowing what you're trading it for.*

She was not trading seven people for a boat ride.

"Get out," Petra said.

The trader blinked. "You're not thinking clearly. You're starving. In two days —"

"I know exactly what I'm trading it for." She picked up the case and held it against her chest, and it was heavier than sixteen kilos, it was the heaviest thing she'd ever carried, and her arms shook around it and she didn't care. "Get out of my cave."

He studied her for a moment — the flat river eyes going over her face, her shaking arms, the case — and whatever he saw there, he'd seen it before, on other rivers, in other people, in the mirror maybe. He put the tiller over.

"Ban Sok," he said, backing the boat out into the current. "There's a road from Ban Sok. There's food in Ban Sok. When the water drops, walk." He paused, one hand on the throttle, and the rain hissed down around him, and he said, not unkindly: "The men who took those survey people. They have boats faster than mine, and they are not stopping for ghosts." Then he opened the throttle and was gone, a blue smear in the brown, and the channel was empty, and the sound of his engine faded into the sound of the flood, and Petra stood in the cave mouth with the case in her arms and her whole body trembling with something that wasn't hunger.

Anong had come to the edge of the shelf. She was looking at Petra with an expression Petra couldn't read — not gratitude; gratitude would have been easier.

"You almost did it," Anong said.

"Yeah."

"I would have." She said it flatly, without self-pity, the way she said everything. "If you'd traded it, I'd have let you. I was going to let you. I said your name because I wanted you to carry it, not because I wanted to stop you."

"I know," Petra said. And she did know. And that was the thing she would examine for years afterward, honestly, the way the chapter of her life demanded honesty: not the moment she refused, which had felt clean, but the ninety seconds before it, when her silence had been a yes, when the columns had balanced, when the best version of herself and the worst version had stood in the same body in the same floodlit cave mouth and the difference between them had been one word, one name, said quietly in the dark.

The price of survival, it turned out, was not what you'd pay the trader. The price was what you'd almost become on the way to the counter.

* * *

They ate one of the energy bars that night, split down the middle, and Petra made Anong take the bigger half by the simple expedient of handing it to her already divided and lying about which half was bigger. The rain kept coming. The water kept climbing. By midnight it had swallowed the stone lip where the trader's boat had sat, and Petra sat up all night with the compass in one hand and the knife in the other, watching the black water rise through the arch of the cave mouth, doing the arithmetic she'd refused to do with the case: two women, no food, one day of bar left, water rising.

There was a way out. There was always a way out; that was the whole of her profession. The karst system drained somewhere — water always went somewhere — and somewhere upstream of the flood channel there had to be a higher gallery, a dry passage, a chimney, a sinkhole. Her father's compass couldn't show her that. But her father's route notes, the ones she'd found under the liner, the ones that matched the survey's secret sampling sites — he'd worked these rivers. He'd mapped karst on the far side of the range. And somewhere in the tangle of his handwriting, in the margins of a route that was thirty years old, there might be a line that said *dry cave, high entrance, west bank* — or there might not, and then the arithmetic would finish itself.

She unrolled the notes by the light of the last match she was willing to spend, and Anong crawled over to look, and they were both still bent over the paper when Petra heard it.

Not the rain. Not the river. Not the trader's engine coming back.

A different engine. Bigger. More than one. Coming up the flooded channel from downstream, against the current, running fast, running like boats that knew where they were going — and the only thing downstream of this cave was Ban Sok, and the only thing between here and Ban Sok was a trader who had seen two women and a government case, and had told them, kindly, that the men with the fast boats were not stopping for ghosts.

Petra killed the match.

In the dark, Anong's hand found her wrist and gripped it, and out in the flood, still distant, still minutes away, the engines got louder.
