## 24. Serey Closes In

The junction announced itself before they saw it — a widening in the river, a slackening of the current, the smell of open water where two channels met and argued. Petra killed the engine and let the longboat drift, listening.

Rain, steady on the tarp. Water slapping the hull. Somewhere ahead, the low murmur of the main channel where the Nam Theun forked around a forested island, one branch running fast toward the falls country, the other curling south toward the provincial road.

And under all of it, faint but unmistakable: an outboard idling.

"Kill your light," Anong whispered.

"It's been off for an hour."

"I know. I'm telling you again anyway."

Petra almost smiled. Three days since the cave, since the storm had spat them out bruised and half-starved onto the flood plain, and Anong had developed this habit of repeating instructions back to her as if repetition were a form of prayer. Maybe it was. They'd bailed and patched and run dark through forty kilometers of drowned forest, and they were still afloat, still together, still carrying the case.

The case sat between them now, wrapped in the plastic groundsheet, lashed under Petra's knee. Half its samples degraded to uselessness in the heat of the cave, but the other half — the ones Anong had double-bagged, the ones with the chain-of-custody forms filled out in her small, furious handwriting — those were still good. Still irrefutable. Still the only thing in the world that could kill the concession.

Forty days had become nineteen.

Petra eased the boat toward the eastern bank, into the shadow of a fig tree whose roots went down into the water like the ribs of something drowned. Through the rain she could make out the junction itself: the island splitting the current, and beyond it, on the far bank, a cluster of lights that shouldn't have been there. A fuel depot, maybe. Or a fishing camp.

Or a net.

"Serey's not stupid," she said quietly. "He knows we have to take the south branch eventually. There's no other way to the capital that doesn't go through his compound."

"He doesn't know which night."

"He doesn't have to. He has boats and we don't. He can wait at every junction between here and the city." She scanned the far lights, counting. Four. Maybe five. "What he doesn't know is whether we're desperate enough to run the north channel."

Anong followed her gaze north, to where the river narrowed and the sound changed — a deep, continuous grinding, like millstones. Even over the rain, even at this distance, you could hear what the flood was doing to the rocks up there.

"That's not a channel," Anong said. "That's a chute."

"It's a chute with water in it."

"Petra."

"We ran worse in the Andes."

"You ran worse in the Andes and it got you blacklisted from two countries."

Petra turned and looked at her. In the dark, with the rain coming down, Anong's face was just an outline — sharp jaw, wet hair plastered flat, eyes catching what little light the sky gave back. She'd stopped being a stranger somewhere in the flooded karst, in the three days of dark when they'd shared body heat and the last of the dried fish and the particular intimacy of being afraid together. Petra couldn't pinpoint when it happened. She only knew that the thought of the north chute scared her less than the thought of losing the woman sitting across from her, and that was new information about herself, and she didn't have time to do anything with it.

"We drift past the junction quiet," Petra said. "We look at both channels with our own eyes. If the south is clean, we take the south. If it's not—"

"If it's not, we take the chute and die like professionals."

"If it's not, we take the chute and live like them."

Anong exhaled through her nose — the closest thing to a laugh she'd managed in three days. "My brother used to say you could tell everything about a person by how they read water."

"What did he say about me?"

"He hasn't met you yet. He's still deciding." She paused. "He liked you better before we burned half his village's roofline."

That landed where it always landed, in the place below Petra's sternum where the guilt lived rent-free. The fires at the village were four days old now. The boy — Anong's brother, Kiet, seventeen, all elbows and fury — had a bandaged arm and a silence that he aimed at Petra like a weapon whenever he thought she wasn't looking. She'd chosen refusal in front of Serey's polite smile, and the bill for that choice had been delivered to someone else's house.

She pushed the thought down with the others. Later. Everything got pushed to later.

"Engine on," she said. "Quarter throttle. We hug the island's west side and come at the junction slow."

* * *

They came at the junction slow, and it didn't matter.

Later, running it back — and she would run it back a thousand times, on the sandbar, in the cell they'd eventually put her in, in the years after — Petra would find the exact moment it went wrong. It wasn't the lights on the far bank, which were a decoy, cheap and obvious. It wasn't the fishing boat mid-channel, nets down, which was also theater. It was the third thing, the thing she never saw at all: the second boat tucked into the cut on the island's downstream tip, engine off, riding the eddy with its lights doused, exactly where a boat would be if whoever placed it understood rivers the way she did.

Serey hadn't hired muscle. He'd hired a guide.

They were fifty meters from the island's tip when the eddy betrayed them — the same recirculating water Petra would have used herself, the dead spot where current curled back on itself and anything floating there stalled, hung, waited. Their hull slid into it and the river simply stopped carrying them, and for three full seconds they sat motionless in the rain, exposed, a lit match in a dark room.

The spotlight came off the island like a thrown spear.

White light, total, erasing the night. Petra's hand went to the engine and her mind went to the map — south branch, wide, open, running fast toward the road and whatever waited on it; north chute, narrow, screaming, running toward the falls country and maybe freedom. And directly downstream of them, wedged across the south channel with its running lights blinking lazy and official, a barge. Not a fishing boat. A barge with a winch and a work deck and men standing along its rail in ponchos, spaced evenly, like fence posts.

A gate. He'd built a gate out of the flood itself.

"Petra." Anong's voice, very calm. "The north."

"I see it."

"The case stays with us or it stays with nobody."

"I know."

The spotlight found them fully then, pinned them, and a voice came across the water amplified and courteous, the voice of a man who had never once needed to shout: *"Friends! You are drifting! The river is dangerous tonight — come alongside, please, for your safety!"*

Serey himself. Of course. He'd come personally, because Petra Lockridge was worth a personal trip now, because triple fees and clean passports had failed and the polite phase of the war was over.

Petra made her decision the way she made all of them: fast, whole-body, without looking back at it.

She gunned the engine and broke left.

The longboat came out of the eddy like a fish off a hook, nose swinging hard toward the north channel, and behind her she heard the amplified voice lose its courtesy — heard it sharpen into clipped commands in Khmer, and then the second engine, the one from the hidden boat, opening up.

The first shot hit the water a meter short. The second hit the transom.

* * *

The north chute took them the way a fist takes a fly.

There was no reading it, no line, none of the craft her father had taught her at ten years old on these same brown waters. There was only speed and noise and the absolute democracy of floodwater, which treats skill and stupidity identically. The longboat surfed down the first drop sideways, and Petra fought it straight with the tiller and her whole weight, and for eleven seconds — she counted, some cold clerk in her head counted — they were actually winning.

Then the wave.

It came off the second ledge taller than the engine cowling, a standing wall of brown water fed by three days of monsoon, and it hit them broadside and lifted the entire boat like a toy and threw it. Petra felt Anong's hand close on her wrist — felt it grip, slip, grip again — and then the world inverted and the river reached into the boat and took everything that wasn't bolted down, and Anong was not bolted down.

The rope did its job. That was the thing she'd remember, the detail that would keep her sane later: the safety line, the one she'd rigged in the cave, the one Anong had laughed at as paranoia — it held. For four seconds, five, six, Petra dragged her through the water by one arm while the chute hammered them both, and Anong's face surfaced once, close, streaming, mouth open around words the water shredded before they arrived.

Then the rope snapped.

Not dramatically. No crack, no moment. One instant there was tension and a living weight at the end of her arm, and the next instant there was only river, and Petra was alone in the white noise with a tiller that steered nothing and a case lashed under her knee and a hole in the world shaped exactly like a hydrologist who repeated instructions back to her like prayers.

She screamed her name into the flood. The flood didn't care. Nothing cared. The chute carried her down through two more ledges and a mile of standing waves and finally dumped her, gasping, into the long slack pool below the falls country, clinging to half a boat.

Half a boat, and the case.

Her hands found the lashing by pure animal reflex, checked it, found it intact. Nineteen days. Half the evidence. Everything Anong had bled for, still sealed in plastic, still pressed against her thigh.

Everything except Anong.

Petra hauled herself onto the broken hull section and knelt there in the rain, shaking, scanning the black water upstream, downstream, everywhere. The flashlight was gone. Her voice was gone — she'd screamed it out in the chute. The current here ran soft and confused, back-eddying along the banks, and anything — anyone — could be held in any of twenty eddies within a kilometer.

Or taken. Serey's men had boats. Serey's men had seen where the rope broke. If they fished her out of the pool below the chute—

No. Think. The chute was suicide for their heavier boats too; they wouldn't follow her down it tonight. Which meant they'd run the south channel and come around, which meant hours, which meant—

Which meant if Anong was alive, she was alive right now, in this hour, in one of these eddies, and every minute Petra spent calculating was a minute of river between them.

She cupped her hands and shouted until her throat tore. She paddled the wreck section along every slack line, every foam raft, every snagged log that could hold a body. The rain kept falling with the indifference of a machine.

On the fourth pass, her hand struck something in the shallows near the bank. Plastic. A sample bag, torn, empty — one of theirs, torn free in the wreck, nothing more. She held it anyway, this scrap of nothing, this piece of the case that had cost more than it knew.

Downstream, far off through the rain, an engine started. Then another. Moving away — south, toward the junction, toward the gate.

Toward wherever they'd taken her.

Petra knelt on the broken hull in the middle of the flood, holding a torn plastic bag, nineteen days on the clock, half the evidence in her lap and the other half of herself somewhere behind her in the dark — and she understood, with terrible clarity, that the lowest point of her life was not behind her in the Andes after all.

It was here. It was now. And it was still going down.
