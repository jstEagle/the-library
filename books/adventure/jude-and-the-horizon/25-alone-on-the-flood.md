## 25. Alone on the Flood

The river spat her out face-down against something that groaned when she hit it.

For a long time Petra didn't know what she was lying on. She knew cold, and she knew noise — the roar of water going somewhere fast — and she knew that one of her arms worked and the other was a rumor. She got her good hand around whatever was under her and held on while the world tilted and righted itself, tilted and righted, like a drunk trying to walk a line.

A door. It was a door. Teak, swollen fat with water, torn off its hinges somewhere upstream, and she was draped across it like a drowned woman on a coffin lid.

She laughed. It came out as a cough that tasted of silt.

*Roll,* said the part of her brain that had kept clients alive in worse places than this. *Get your center on the wood before it rolls you off.*

She rolled. The door bucked, dipped, steadied. Her left arm answered when she asked it to — slowly, grudgingly, the shoulder screaming in a language she recognized from old injuries: not broken, but deeply offended. Something had opened a gash along her forearm at some point; she couldn't remember when, couldn't remember most of the last hour, which was its own kind of injury. The last thing she remembered clearly was the junction. Serey's men. Anong's face turning back toward her over the gunwale, mouth open, saying her name or maybe just screaming.

Then the river had reached up out of its own channel and taken her.

She checked herself the way she'd check a client. Head: intact, ears ringing but equal. Chest: bruised ribs, breathing painful but possible. Left arm: functional. Right hand: she could make a fist. Legs: she flexed them against the door and felt both respond, one ankle hot and wrong-feeling, probably sprained. Core temperature: the question that mattered. The rain was warm — monsoon-warm, bathwater that wanted to kill you — but the wind moving over the flood was not, and hypothermia didn't care what latitude you were at.

She was alive. That was the inventory. Everything else was pending.

* * *

The flood carried her through a country that no longer existed.

Villages became lakes with rooftops for islands. Banana groves stood to their shoulders in brown water, leaves thrashing. A dog swam past her with the flat determination of an animal that had decided drowning was for other creatures, and did not look at her, and she respected that completely. Once, a whole floating market went by in fragments — a boat awash and spinning, papayas bobbing in their hundreds like severed heads, a woman's sandal caught on the door's hinge pin and riding with her for a quarter mile until the current took it.

She didn't try to swim for any of it. Swimming was how people died in floods. The water was doing the work; her job was to stay on top of it and keep her head above the debris line, where the logs traveled — whole trees, stripped and traveling at the speed of the current, each one a battering ram with her name on it.

So she lay on her door and watched the drowned country go by and did the only useful thing left to her, which was think.

And thinking, in the absence of anything else, meant Anong.

* * *

Here was the arithmetic, laid out with the clean cruelty of all arithmetic:

Serey's men had come to the junction with two boats. Two boats, four men visible, probably more. Petra had been swept downstream by pure bad luck, and Anong had been taken by pure intent. There had been perhaps three seconds in which either of them could have done anything for the other, and the river had spent those seconds deciding for them.

So: not her fault. That was the first entry in the ledger.

But ledgers had more pages. Here was the second: if Petra hadn't refused Serey in the village — if she'd taken the money, the passport, the clean record, the exit — there would have been no reprisal, no burned village, no brother with his arm wrapped in boiled cloth, no flight downriver in a borrowed boat with half the province's security contractors looking for them. Anong would be in hiding somewhere, safe, hating herself but whole. Instead Anong was in the hands of men who had made an entire survey team disappear so cleanly that even the footprints were erased.

Third page: Petra had known, since the checkpoint, since the first body, exactly what these people were capable of. She had chosen to keep moving anyway, because the data mattered, because the dead mattered, because walking away from it would have been a second Andes — a smaller, quieter version of the same sin, committed with full knowledge this time.

And here was the question underneath all the pages, the one she'd been carrying since a rope went slack in the Cordillera six years ago:

Was duty to the dead worth anything at all?

Because that was what this was. Not duty to Anong — Anong was alive, and duty to the living had a shape, a direction, things you could actually do. But the survey team was dead. Tomás Reyes-Callahan and his five were dead or as good as, and every risk Petra had taken since the camp had been justified — by her, to herself, at three in the morning, in the rain — as something owed to them. Justice. Exposure. Truth. Words that weighed nothing and cost everything.

Her father had a saying about mountains: *the dead don't need you, they just give you an excuse.* He'd meant it about summit fever, about climbers who took stupid risks to plant flags for people who would never see them. She'd thought it was cynicism when she was nineteen. Lying on a door in the middle of a flooded province, she understood it was the most compassionate thing he'd ever taught her — and also that she had ignored it completely.

The dead don't need you. So who was all this for?

Not Anong, apparently. Not anymore. Anong was in a compound somewhere, and Petra was drifting away from her at the speed of a monsoon flood, and every hour of separation made rescue less likely and escape more tempting, and the payout — Vuthy's finder's fee, the debt, the family, home — sat at the end of the downstream route like a light in a window.

All she had to do was nothing. Keep holding the door. Let the river decide. Drift to the provincial capital, crawl to Vuthy, collect, go home, live.

It would even be defensible. Anyone she told the story to — anyone sane — would nod. *You were injured. You were separated. There was nothing you could do.*

That was the trap of it. The story was true, and it was also a door, and doors swing both ways.

* * *

Night came down on the flood like a lid.

She found the sandbar by feel before she saw it — the door dragging, grinding, the current suddenly arguing with itself around her — and she used the last of her working strength to roll off onto wet sand before the river changed its mind. She crawled above the waterline, dragged the door up after her as a windbreak, and lay there shaking while the rain came down with the patience of something that had all night and intended to use it.

Injury assessment, round two, by touch. The forearm cut was shallow, filthy, needed cleaning she couldn't give it. Ribs: two of them, at least, cracked or badly bruised; every breath was a negotiation. Ankle: swelling inside her boot, which was bad, because the boot was the only splint available and also the only boot. Shoulder: wrenched, would work tomorrow if tomorrow required it. Tomorrow would require it.

She should eat. She had nothing to eat. She should make fire. She had nothing dry enough to burn and no way to strike it with hands that wouldn't stop trembling. She should signal. She was lying on a piece of a house in the middle of a flood in a country where she was, as of this week, actively hunted, and signaling was the last thing a smart woman did.

So instead she lay on her back in the rain and let the despair arrive, because she'd learned a long time ago that it always arrives whether you invite it or not, and fighting it costs more calories than enduring it.

This was the lowest she had ever been, and she had a competitive history. Blacklisted twice. Bankrupt twice, once per blacklist. The Andes, which she still visited most nights behind her eyes: the whiteout, the client's voice going from annoyed to afraid to silent, the rope, the decision, the six years of interest compounding on it. She had built her whole adult life on the principle that competence was a moral position — that if you were good enough at the craft, you never had to make the kind of choice she'd made on that mountain. Get good enough, and nobody dies. Get good enough, and you're never the person lying on a door.

The flood had settled that argument. Competence hadn't saved the survey team. Competence hadn't saved Anong. Competence had gotten her to a junction at the exact moment two boats full of professionals arrived, and the difference between her and the corpses upstream was weather.

Maybe that was all any of it ever was. Weather. The Andes had been weather. The rescue that ruined her had been weather wearing the costume of a decision, and everyone — the family, the boards of inquiry, herself — had agreed to pretend otherwise because pretending otherwise kept the universe legible. Kept it fair. Kept it a place where guilt pointed at someone.

The rain drummed on the door beside her. Somewhere out in the dark, water moved tons of earth without effort or malice, and everything it destroyed it destroyed without meaning to, and no one called the river evil, and no one expected the river to testify.

*Wouldn't that be easier,* she thought. *To be the river.*

To do what the current does and call it nature. Take the fee, drift downstream, let the arithmetic carry her. *I was injured. I was separated. There was nothing I could do.* True words. River words.

But she wasn't the river. That was the whole miserable point of being a person. You got the current whether you wanted it or not, and then you got to choose what you did inside it, and the choosing was the only thing that was yours — the only thing Serey couldn't buy, the consortium couldn't erase, the blacklist couldn't revoke. Six years ago she'd made a choice inside a current and had spent every day since trying to convince herself it hadn't been a choice.

She wasn't going to spend six more building the same lie.

Anong had run downstream with the samples while her team was taken, and had hated herself for the arithmetic ever since — had confessed it on a sandbar with the honesty of someone handing over a knife handle-first. *I did the math and the math said the data was worth more than we were, and I have to live with being the kind of person who can do that math.* Petra had told her then: the math wasn't the sin. Abandoning the living would have been.

Well. Anong was living. The math was still open.

Petra turned her head and looked downstream, into the dark, as if the provincial capital were a glow on the horizon. It wasn't. There was nothing out there but rain and water and the occasional lightning-shot silhouette of a drowned tree. But the direction was real, and so was the other one — upstream, toward the junction, toward wherever Serey's boats had gone with a hydrologist who knew where every poisoned well in the province was.

Upstream was suicide. Upstream was a busted shoulder, a blown ankle, no food, no boat, no weapon, no ally, against a consortium with a private army and forty days of concession left to protect. Every professional instinct she owned voted downstream.

Every instinct she owned had also once told her, on a mountain, in a whiteout, that turning back was the wrong call.

"I'm coming," she said to the dark, to the north, to whoever was listening — Anong, the dead team, her father, no one. Her voice came out wrecked and small and it was the truest thing she'd said in six years. "I'm coming, and I'm sorry, and I'm coming."

* * *

Dawn came gray and merciless and showed her exactly how bad her situation was.

The sandbar had shrunk overnight to a strip maybe ten meters long, and the flood was still rising. The drowned village upstream had contributed a new fleet of debris: roof sections, a ladder, half a fence. And snagged against the bar's upstream tip, hull-down and rolling gently, was a longboat — one of the narrow river taxis, swamped but structurally sound, its stern line still knotted to a floating oil drum that had evidently kept it from sinking outright.

Petra looked at it for a long time.

Getting to it meant swimming twenty meters of floodwater with one good arm. Bailing it meant kneeling on a cracked rib cage for however long it took. Using it meant running a flooded river solo, injured, with no idea where the channel had moved to overnight — channels did move; she'd seen a whole bend reroute itself yesterday around a submerged temple — toward a junction crawling with the men who owned the province's water.

Downstream was at her back. The capital was down there. Vuthy was down there. Money, extraction, home, the end of six years of debt — all of it one lazy current away, requiring nothing from her but surrender.

She thought about her father's compass, which was gone now, lost with her pack somewhere between the junction and the door. She'd carried it through two blacklists and a bankruptcy on the theory that it was the last honest thing she owned. She didn't need it to know which way north was. She'd known since midnight.

Petra pulled off her boot, drained it, laced it back over the swelling ankle as tight as she could stand, and waded into the flood.

The water tried to kill her twice in twenty meters — once with a log she heard before she saw it and dove under, once with a whirl of current that pinned her against the longboat's hull until her fingers found the gunwale and her arm found a strength she didn't know she'd rented. Then she was over the side, in the bilge, among a stranger's abandoned life: a plastic bail bucket, a coil of rotten line, a child's rain jacket, half a bag of dried fish gone soft but edible.

She bailed until her vision went white at the edges. She rigged the drum as a drag anchor. She ate three pieces of fish and immediately regretted and appreciated it equally.

Then she put the nose upstream, into the current, into the teeth of it, and started to row.

Each stroke cost her something she'd want later. The river pushed back like it had a personal opinion. Progress was measured in single meters, then in tens, then — by some mercy of backwaters and eddies she read the way other people read street signs — in hundreds. By midday she'd learned the flood's grammar: where the current stacked up, where it rested, the seams a boat could ride. It was the best she'd felt in days, briefly, until she remembered why she was alone in the boat, and then it was the worst.

Near dusk, hauling through a cut between two flooded ridges, she saw smoke ahead. Thin, domestic, deliberate — not a burning village but a cooking fire, banked low, the kind of fire built by someone who didn't want to be seen and had accepted being smelled.

She shipped oars and drifted, silent, letting the current take her around the bend.

On a mudbank beneath a tarp shelter, a man was boiling something over a small flame, and beside him, weighted down with a stone, was a ledger — a fat, rubber-banded, water-stained ledger of the kind fixers keep, and the man was Vuthy's cousin, and he was alone, and he had not yet seen her.

Petra picked up the bail bucket, the heaviest thing in the boat, and stopped drifting.
