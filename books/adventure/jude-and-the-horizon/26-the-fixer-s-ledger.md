## 26. The Fixer's Ledger

Petra woke on the riverbank with mud in her teeth and a gash along her forearm that had stopped bleeding sometime in the night.

She lay still for a while and took inventory, the way she'd taught clients to do it — body first, gear second, plan third. Body: bruised ribs, left ankle swollen but bearing weight, forearm cut, everything else functional. Gear: knife, lighter, one waterproof pouch with the forged permits gone soft at the edges, half a chocolate bar fused into its wrapper. The compass was still around her neck. It always was.

Anong was gone. Serey's men had taken her at the junction, and Petra had spent four hours underwater and downstream learning exactly how little her opinion counted in that transaction.

Plan: find Vuthy.

It wasn't much of a plan. It was barely a sentence. But it was the only sentence she had, because every other road ran through him. He'd hired her. He'd known where the camp was before she did. And when she'd called from the sandbar, his voice had done something she hadn't caught until later, replaying it against the sound of bailing water — he'd asked about the case first and Anong second. Not by much. By a breath. But Petra Lockridge had spent fifteen years reading people in places where misreading them got you killed, and a breath was enough.

Vuthy had sold them out. Or Vuthy had been bought so long ago that selling anyone out required an act of will he no longer possessed. Either way, he was the door, and doors could be opened from either side.

She got up, wrung out her shirt, and started walking toward Kampong Thleak, the river town marked on her father's old route notes as a fuel stop and nothing more. Six kilometers of flooded track. She covered it in three hours, limping, and arrived looking exactly like what she was — a drowned woman with nothing left to lose — which turned out to be useful camouflage. Nobody stops a drowned woman. Nobody remembers her either.

* * *

Kampong Thleak sat on a bluff above the brown water like something the river kept meaning to swallow and never got around to. Stilt houses, a fuel depot, a market under corrugated roofing, three guesthouses of descending quality. In monsoon season the town doubled as a waiting room for everyone whose business on the river had been postponed by weather — boat crews, cargo runners, men who moved things without paperwork.

Men like Vuthy.

Petra didn't go to the guesthouses. She went to the fuel depot, bought diesel for a boat she didn't have, and paid too much for it, loudly, in the currency of complaint. Within twenty minutes she knew two things: the depot owner's brother ran cargo downriver twice a week, and a man matching Vuthy's description — small, neat, gold-rimmed glasses, the permanent expression of someone doing you a favor — had come through nine days ago heading upstream, then come back down four days ago, alone, and taken a room at the Champa Guesthouse, where he'd told the owner he was waiting for the water to drop.

Waiting. Nine days ago. Before the junction. Before Serey's boats ever came near them.

Which meant Vuthy hadn't been overtaken by events. He'd been positioned inside them.

Petra carried her diesel can back through the market, bought dried fish and betel nuts she didn't want, and let herself be seen as a trader's widow waiting out the rain. Then she found a spot across the lane from the Champa with a view of its single staircase, ordered tea she couldn't taste, and settled in to do the thing she was actually good at, the thing no blacklist had ever touched.

Watching.

* * *

Vuthy kept schedule the way small meticulous men do.

He came down at seven for noodles. Back up at seven-forty. Down again at eleven to buy cigarettes and argue cheerfully with the phone company about signal. Up. Down at four to sit under the eaves and watch the river, hands folded over his belly, a man at peace with his own patience.

He had a room at the back, second floor, window facing the latrine block. The window latch was broken — Petra watched him close it with a wedge of folded cardboard, watched the wedge sit on the sill all afternoon like a small confession.

She waited for full dark, then for the generator hour, when the town's lights stuttered and every dog within earshot lost its mind. She crossed the lane in the noise, went up the outside of the stairs on the latrine side, and slipped the cardboard wedge out of the frame with her knife.

The room smelled of mosquito coil and machine oil.

Vuthy wasn't there. That was the first surprise. The bed was made, the coil was burning, his spare glasses were folded on the nightstand — but the man himself was downstairs eating late noodles, visible through the floorboards as a shadow moving against lamplight. Petra stood in the dark of his room and understood, with a cold professional clarity, that she had perhaps ten minutes, and that ten minutes was not for searching.

It was for finding the one thing worth taking.

She'd spent years around guides and fixers and smugglers, and they all shared one religion: paper. Passports, permits, receipts, debts. Men like Vuthy trusted their memory for faces and their ledgers for everything else, because memory could be drunk away or beaten out of you, but a ledger in a false bottom was forever.

The room had a desk. Of course it had a desk. The desk had a drawer that stuck, and the sticking drawer had a bottom that sat two centimeters proud of where it should have, and beneath it, wrapped in oilcloth, was a ledger bound in grey plastic, the kind schoolchildren used, utterly anonymous.

She opened it by the light of her cupped phone screen.

The first pages were ordinary — ferry fees, bribes dressed as "port charges," a running account with a pharmacy in the capital. Vuthy's whole career in miniature, itemized in a cramped, patient hand. She flipped forward, past months, past a year, and the entries began to change.

Dates. Coordinates. Names reduced to initials.

And beside each set of initials, a figure. Not money. Days.

*Survey team, Khao Sen — 6 pax — delivered — 40 days concession buffer.*

Delivered.

She read it again. Then she read backward, because once you saw the architecture you had to see all of it. Three years of entries. A fisheries audit, upstream, five people, delivered. A land-titling team, delivered. A pair of journalists who'd come asking about sediment loads, delivered. Each one a group that had walked into the Khao Sen country or onto consortium water and simply ceased to exist — no bodies, no tracks, gear left neat as a showroom. Each one timed, she realized with a nausea that rose slow and total, to the renewal windows of mining concessions. Forty days before expiry, a survey appears. Forty days later, the permit renews unexamined, because the examiners are gone and the search finds nothing and eventually everyone agrees the mountains ate them.

It wasn't a crime Vuthy had committed. It was a system he maintained. He was the hinge between the consortium's money and the mountains' silence, and he had been turning on that hinge for years — long before Petra Lockridge ever pawned a watch in his office, long before he'd looked at her with those kindly gold-rimmed eyes and offered her a finder's fee.

Her own name was in there. Page after recent page, in the same patient hand. *P.L. — engaged — fee escrowed.* Then, dated the day after she'd called from the sandbar: *P.L. — location sold — S. informed.* And below that, a line that stopped her breathing for a moment:

*A. — held — vault site — pending exchange.*

Pending exchange. Anong was alive, held at what Vuthy's shorthand placed somewhere he called only the vault site, and she was being held as inventory — to be traded for the sample case, the same way the survey team had once been traded for a permit renewal. Everything in Vuthy's world was a delivery. Everything had a price and a buffer and a date.

Petra crouched in the dark with the ledger open on her knees and felt something she hadn't felt since the Andes — since the moment she'd understood that the disaster wasn't the storm, the storm was just weather, the disaster was the decision chain that had put six people on that ridge in that window. Betrayal, she thought now, was the wrong word for what she felt looking at Vuthy's handwriting. Betrayal implied a turn. A break in the pattern. There was no break here. Vuthy had never been outside the pattern. He'd built it, entry by entry, year by year, a quiet architecture of disappeared people holding up a tower of expiring paper, and when the tower needed one more stone he'd gone to a border-town hostel and found a blacklisted guide desperate enough to carry it.

Desperation dressed up as opportunity. His oldest tool. He'd used it on her the way he'd used it on everyone, and it had worked, because it always worked, because desperate people see doors where other people see traps.

The floorboards creaked.

Not the settling kind of creak. The weight-on-stairs kind. Petra closed the ledger, wrapped it in the oilcloth, and looked around the room with three seconds to make a decision that would shape everything after it.

She could put it back. Take nothing, learn nothing, slip out the window and spend the rest of this chase guessing.

Or she could take it — and take it in a way Vuthy would feel.

Downstairs, the shadow against the lamplight finished its noodles. Petra heard the small sounds of him counting coins, the scrape of the bench, footsteps crossing toward the stairs. She tucked the ledger inside her shirt against her skin, and on the desk, in the space where it had lived, she left the only thing she had that was worth anything as a message.

Her father's compass wouldn't fit the gesture — that stayed around her neck, always. Instead she took the folded cardboard wedge from the sill, smoothed it flat, and wrote on it in pencil from her pocket, in block letters legible by lantern light:

*I READ IT ALL. COME FIND ME BEFORE I FIND THE INQUIRY.*

It was a bluff. There was no inquiry yet, no journalist, no lab, nothing but a degraded sample case and a woman in a cage. But Vuthy's entire architecture rested on nobody ever saying certain sentences out loud, and she had just written one on his own windowsill.

She went out the window as his foot touched the third stair, dropped into the latrine shadows, and was gone into the generator noise and the barking dogs before the door upstairs finished opening.

* * *

She didn't run. Running was for people being chased, and she needed, for the next few hours, to be nobody. She walked the market lane at the pace of a tired widow, bought charcoal by torchlight, and took a room at the worst guesthouse in town — the one with no lock on the door, because no lock meant no landlord with keys and no landlord with questions. She wedged a chair under the handle anyway, out of habit, and sat on the bed with the ledger open across her knees and a candle stub burning down beside her.

Reading it cover to cover took until nearly dawn.

By the end she understood the shape of the thing entire. The consortium's lease didn't just expire in thirty-eight days — it had been dying and renewing on this same cycle for a decade, and every renewal had been purchased with a disappearance, and every disappearance had been brokered by the same neat hand. Vuthy hadn't sold them out last week. He'd recruited her into a machine whose purpose was to make sure no one ever came back from the Khao Sen with data, and the finder's fee, the extraction offer, even his warning that the price on her head had doubled — all of it was the machine doing what machines do, processing material toward its designed output. Her designed output had been the same as the survey team's: gone, unexplained, gear intact.

Except she'd read the ledger. And the ledger changed the arithmetic of everything.

Because here was the thing she turned over and over as the candle guttered: Vuthy's shorthand said *pending exchange*. He wanted the sample case. Serey wanted the sample case. Everyone in this whole rotten structure wanted a plastic box of water vials more than they wanted two women dead — dead was easy, dead was cheap, but the case was the one object in the world that could end the concessions for good, and they all knew it.

Which meant Anong was alive precisely because the case was still missing.

And it meant Petra finally had something to trade.

She closed the ledger and sat in the grey light coming through the slats, her cut arm throbbing, her ankle a hot wire, exhaustion sitting on her shoulders like wet rope. Somewhere across town, a small meticulous man was climbing his stairs to discover that his architecture had a reader. He would be afraid — she hoped he would be afraid, briefly, honestly — and then he would do what builders do when a wall cracks: he would calculate whether to repair it or demolish the room.

She had to reach him before he decided.

Petra tore a page from the back of the ledger, wrote a note in her blunt guide's hand — *Midnight. Fuel depot. I have your book and I know where the vault isn't. Bring me Anong or I bring the inquiry to you.* — and paid a market boy too much to leave it under Vuthy's door without being seen asking who sent it.

Then she packed the ledger deep in her pack, checked the knife, and went out into the last of the dark to choose her ground.

If Vuthy came to trade, she'd trade — Anong for the book, and the book was bait anyway, because she'd already memorized every page that mattered.

And if he came instead with Serey's men, well.

She'd read his ledger. She knew exactly what he was afraid of.

Now she just had to stay alive long enough to be it.
