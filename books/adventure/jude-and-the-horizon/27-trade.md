## 27. Trade

Vuthy's ledger sat in my lap like something that had died.

I'd read it twice by lantern light in the back room of a rice mill outside Kratie, where the owner took payment in cash and asked nothing, which in this country was its own kind of luxury. The ledger was a school exercise book, blue-ruled lines gone soft with damp, and Vuthy had kept it for eleven years because he was a man who believed every debt should be written down. Names on the left. Sums on the right. And down one margin, in his small patient hand, dates and figures that had nothing to do with money: *K.S. survey — 6 pax — moved — paid by C.C.M.* — dated four years before the team I'd been hired to find ever set foot in those mountains.

He'd done it before. That was the thing I couldn't stop turning over. The vanishing of a government survey team wasn't an improvisation for Vuthy. It was a service. A product line. He'd brokered disappearances the way other men brokered timber rights, and he'd written it all down because some part of him, somewhere under the charm and the gold tooth and the way he said *sister* when he wanted something, had always known that a ledger is the only friend that doesn't lie about you.

Which meant Vuthy was scared. Men who keep ledgers keep them for leverage, and men who keep them for leverage know exactly how much a page like that is worth to the consortium — and what the consortium would do to the man holding it if it surfaced. He hadn't sold me out yet. He'd gone silent instead, and silence from Vuthy wasn't absence. It was calculation.

So I stopped waiting for him to call me and made myself into the kind of problem he'd have to answer.

* * *

Finding Vuthy was easier than it should have been, because Vuthy was a creature of habit wearing the costume of a man without any. He drank at the same place every time he came upriver — a floating bar moored off the fish market in Stung Treng, a converted barge with plastic chairs and a sound system that only knew three songs. He sat with his back to the wall and his eyes on the water, because Vuthy trusted rivers more than people, and he always, always ordered the same brand of beer from the same woman whose cousin owed him money.

I didn't go to the bar. Going to the bar was what a desperate person did, and the whole architecture of what I was about to do depended on me not looking desperate.

Instead I spent two days being seen. I bought diesel at the fuel dock where the market gossips congregated. I ate breakfast on the terrace of the Sengtawan Hotel, alone, with the sample case — the real one, wrapped in a tarpaulin — sitting beside my chair like a sleeping dog. I let the woman who ran the guesthouse see me counting money I didn't have. By the second evening, I made sure the story had traveled: the farang guide was in town, she had the case, she was waiting for transport south, and she was running out of patience.

Then I sent Vuthy a message through the guesthouse girl, who I'd already established was one of his ears, because everyone in a river town is somebody's ear. The message was five words long:

*The ledger. Or the case.*

No signature. No threat spelled out. If he was half as smart as I needed him to be, he'd understand that I'd found the exercise book, that I knew what was in it, and that I was offering him the only trade he could survive: the case for Anong, clean, no names exchanged, no pages photocopied. And if he wasn't that smart —

Well. Then I'd learn that too, and quickly.

The reply came before dawn, folded inside a bag of dried fish delivered to the guesthouse with my name misspelled. One line, in pencil:

*Old ferry landing, 10 a.m. Come alone. Bring the case. Leave the attitude.*

I almost smiled. Almost. There was a version of me, two months ago, who would have smiled at that. She'd been a person who still thought Vuthy was a scoundrel with a code instead of a businessman with a body count.

* * *

The old ferry landing was a concrete ramp sliding into brown water, abandoned since the new bridge went in upstream. Rusting pilings. A shrine to a river spirit nobody maintained anymore but everybody still feared, its garlands bleached gray. Good sightlines for anyone watching from the tree line, which meant Vuthy would have watchers, which meant I planned for them.

I came alone, technically. Anong was three hundred meters downstream in the flooded treeline with our boat and a rifle we'd taken off a pirate who'd regretted his career choices, and she had instructions that if I raised my left arm above my head, she was to leave without me. We'd argued about that instruction for an hour the night before. She'd wanted it to be her arm, her signal, her leaving. In the end I'd won, not because I argued better but because I told her the truth: if they take me, they'll want to question me, and questioning takes time, and time is the only currency you can't counterfeit. If they take you, they don't need time. They need a riverbank and a shovel.

She hadn't liked hearing it. She'd agreed anyway. That was the shape trust had taken between us — not warmth, exactly. Arithmetic both of us hated but accepted.

At ten exactly, a longboat slid out from behind the barge wrecks midriver and came to the ramp. Two men I didn't know rode in it, young, wrong posture for fishermen. Vuthy stepped out onto the concrete in white sneakers that had never touched mud, a linen shirt, sunglasses pushed up into his hair. He looked like a man on holiday from a life that didn't exist.

"Sister," he said. "You look terrible."

"Monsoon," I said. "You look rich."

"I always look rich. It's cheaper than being rich." He glanced at the case at my feet. "That it?"

"That's it."

"And the ledger?"

"What ledger?" I said. "I've never seen a ledger. I've never seen anything. I'm a mountain guide, Vuthy. I find lost things. Somebody told me you might know where a person is, and I'm offering to trade a box for an address. That's the whole transaction."

He studied me for a long moment, and I watched him do the math behind the sunglasses — or rather, watch him fail to finish it, because the sunglasses were doing their job and so was mine. He couldn't know whether I'd copied the pages. He couldn't know whether copies were already on a bus to Phnom Penh, or in an email draft scheduled to send, or in the hands of a journalist whose name I'd make sure he heard eventually. Uncertainty was the entire product. I was selling him doubt, and doubt was the one thing a man with his history couldn't afford to buy cheaply.

"You went through my things," he said finally.

"You went through my life first. You sold me a missing-persons contract that was actually a cleanup job. You let me walk into those mountains thinking six people were lost." I crouched and rested my hand on the case, casual, proprietary. "We're even on trespassing."

"The team wasn't my idea."

"No," I agreed. "It was your product."

His jaw worked. Behind him, the two young men shifted, and I kept my voice level and bored, because the moment this became about force, everything I'd built the last three days collapsed into luck.

"Here's what happens now," I said. "You give me a location. Somewhere I can reach in twenty-four hours. I verify she's alive — I call her, I hear her voice, whatever verification you like, you pick the method. Then your men carry this case back to your boat, and I walk away, and you never see me again, and neither does anyone else. The ledger stays where it is. Which is nowhere. Because there is no ledger."

"And if I just take the case and the girl stays where she is?"

"Then the pages come out of wherever they live, and they go to the anti-corruption unit, the ministry, the Australian embassy, and a man named Daniel Okafor who writes for a wire service and has been very bored lately. And here's the part you should actually care about, Vuthy —" I stood up slowly. "They won't just get the survey pages. They'll get eleven years of them. Every name on the left side of every line. Including yours. Including the column where you wrote down what the consortium paid you per head. You didn't hide it well. Guilty men never do — they think writing it down makes it business instead of murder."

The river slapped against the ramp. Somewhere in the trees a bird made a sound like a rusty hinge.

"You're bluffing," he said, but his voice had changed key, and we both heard it.

"Probably," I said. "Want to bet your neck on it? Because I've got nothing left to lose, Vuthy. That's my whole position. I'm blacklisted in two countries, I owe a dead man's family more money than I'll earn in a decade, and the consortium put a price on me that I'm told went up last week. You cannot threaten a woman who's already spent. But you — you've got a bar, a reputation, a retirement plan. You've got things. Things are hostages."

He took off the sunglasses then, and for the first time since the hostel in the border town, I saw his actual eyes. Tired. Older than his face. The eyes of a bookkeeper who'd finally read his own entries all the way through.

"She's at the compound," he said quietly. "The C.C.M. river compound, past the confluence, the one with the dredge barges. They're holding her in the pump house because Serey thinks she's worth more breathing until the permit dies. Thirty-one days. After that—" He shrugged, a small ugly shrug. "After that the numbers change."

"Why tell me the truth? You could give me a false location and keep the case."

"Because you'd check, and if it was false, the pages move, and I can't risk maybe." He rubbed his face with both hands. "And because I liked you, sister. That was always my problem. I like people, and then I sell them, and then I feel bad about it, and the feeling bad never once stopped the selling."

"Verification," I said. "Phone call. Now."

He made one. Held out a cheap phone, speaker on, and a voice I'd know anywhere — flat, furious, alive — said, "Petra, if this is a trade, take the deal, and if it's not a trade, hang up and run, because—"

The line cut. Vuthy pocketed the phone. "Satisfied?"

"Almost." I nudged the case forward with my boot. "One more thing. This case is heavier than it looks, and the latches are wired to a flare compound I bought off a demolitions guy in Banlung. If anyone opens it with anything other than the combination — which is written on a piece of paper currently traveling toward Phnom Penh — it burns everything inside at four thousand degrees. Tell your men to carry it gently and open nothing. Tell Serey the same."

It was a lie. The case was full of river stones, a broken GPS, and Anong's spare shirt. The real samples were sealed in waterproof tubes inside the fuel tank of our boat, where heat and vibration couldn't touch them, and had been since the night on the sandbar when I'd finally understood that the case itself was never the evidence — the case was the story everyone wanted to believe, and a good guide knows the difference between the route and the map.

But Vuthy didn't know that. Nobody knew that. And by tomorrow, the whole river would know that Petra Lockridge traded away the evidence to save her partner, and the consortium would relax, and relaxed men guard pump houses badly.

Vuthy signaled his men. They picked up the case with the exaggerated tenderness of people handling a bomb, which, in fairness, was exactly what I'd told them it was. Vuthy lingered a moment at the top of the ramp.

"For what it's worth," he said, "the ledger entry about you — the finder's fee — I never collected it. I tore the page out."

"I know," I said. "I found the stub."

Something crossed his face that might have been respect, or might have been the beginning of a very long fear. "The compound has a generator that runs quiet hours after midnight. The fence on the north side is decorative — the real security watches the water. And Petra." He paused. "Serey isn't like me. I sold people and felt bad. He budgets for it. Don't let him talk. He's better at talking than either of us."

Then he was in the boat, and the boat was a wake, and I stood on the dead ferry ramp with empty hands and a heartbeat going like a bailing bucket.

* * *

Anong materialized out of the treeline twenty minutes later, moving low along the water, rifle slung, face unreadable. She stopped in front of me and looked at the space where the case had been.

"So it's done," she said. "You gave it up."

"I gave up forty pounds of rocks and your worst shirt."

She stared. Then understanding arrived, slow and total, the way weather arrives, and something in her shoulders came down half an inch — the first time since the checkpoint that she'd stood like anything other than a woman braced for impact.

"The tank," she said. "You split the samples."

"Half in the tank, half in the compass case lining, half in — okay, thirds. I panicked and used thirds."

"That's not how halves work."

"I'm a guide, not a mathematician." I turned us toward the boat, toward the confluence, toward a compound with a decorative fence and a generator that slept at midnight. "Thirty-one days on the permits. Twenty-nine on the degraded samples' chain of custody. And one night, starting tonight, to get you out of a pump house before Serey realizes the box he's guarding is full of gravel."

Anong swung the rifle off her shoulder and checked the action, and when she spoke again her voice had the same flat calm it had carried the day I caught her scavenging the camp at knifepoint — but aimed differently now. Outward.

"Petra," she said. "When we burn this place down, I want to be the one holding the match."

The current took our bow, and we turned upstream together, into the darkening water, toward the lights of the compound coming on one by one along the far bank like a row of small, patient fires.
