## 28. The Compound

The compound sat on a bend of the river like a fist closed around it.

Petra lay in the reeds on the far bank and studied it through the rain for the better part of an hour, because that was what her father had taught her to do before she could read: *watch until you're bored, then watch another hour, because bored is when you start seeing.* The flood had turned the compound into an island — a horseshoe of corrugated warehouses and stilt houses around a gravel yard now standing under thirty centimeters of brown water. A generator shed hummed somewhere behind the main building. Two boats were tied at the loading dock, both riding high and empty. Floodlights on poles lit the yard in overlapping circles, and the circles were wrong.

That was the first thing she'd noticed from two kilometers downstream, and it was still the thing that mattered. The lights overlapped too neatly. Nobody had adjusted them since the river rose; they'd been aimed for dry ground, which meant there were dead zones along the warehouse walls where the light fell short and the water swallowed the rest. Somebody had designed this place to be watched from inside, not approached from outside. Nobody had ever expected anyone to come by water, in monsoon season, alone.

*Alone* was doing a lot of work in that sentence. Petra flexed her left hand and felt the rope burn from the junction site pull tight across her palm. Four days since Anong went into one of Serey's boats and Petra went into the water. Four days of drifting, walking, stealing a canoe, and trading her last real shirt to a fisherman for the location of a place everyone on the river knew and nobody named out loud.

She wasn't alone. She just didn't have Anong yet.

* * *

Getting across took less courage than getting ready did.

She stripped to her underwear, wrapped the compass, the route notes, and Vuthy's ledger page in the dry bag she'd lashed to her back, and waded in upstream of the compound so the current would carry her down along the near bank instead of into the floodlit yard. The water was warm as bathwater and moving like a freight train. She let it take her, angling with small kicks, counting fence posts as they slid past — her father's trick again, counting things to keep the animal part of your brain quiet. At post eleven she caught the eddy behind a half-submerged clump of bamboo, hauled herself into it, and hung there breathing while the compound's generator hummed sixty meters away.

The wire fence started at the waterline and ran up the bank into elephant grass. It wasn't electrified — no insulators, no warning signs, and consortium money never spent where it didn't have to. It was, however, topped with three strands of barbed wire and backed by a cleared killing lane eight meters wide. Petra floated down its length once, memorizing it, then found what she was looking for: a drainage culvert where a creek used to meet the river before the flood erased the distinction. The culvert's mouth was submerged, rusted half open, and screened with mesh that someone had cut long ago and wired back badly.

She went in headfirst, bag held above her, and came up inside the killing lane behind a stack of empty fuel drums, close enough to the nearest floodlight pole to touch it. Her heart was going hard but her hands were steady. That was the old arithmetic, the one that had made her famous before it made her infamous: fear was fine, panic was fatal, and the line between them was procedure.

She dressed in the dark, wrung out what wouldn't wring, and crouched behind the drums to do what she'd crossed forty kilometers of flooded country to do: think.

Two buildings mattered. The big warehouse on the dock side — that was cargo, boats, probably men playing cards. The smaller block building at the center of the horseshoe had a steel door, a padlock hasp, a windowless face, and — this was the detail that had made her paddle all night — an air conditioner running in monsoon season, its condensate line dripping a bright thread of water into the yard. You air-conditioned paper. You air-conditioned things that rotted.

Serey hadn't burned the vault yet. Whatever he'd done with Anong, whatever he was waiting for, the records were still here, still cold, still real.

And if the records were here, there was a decent chance Anong was too. Men like Serey didn't kill assets they might still need to trade. He'd taken her alive at the junction for a reason, and the reason was sitting in a cold room twenty meters away, or standing in it.

Petra checked the ledger page one more time by feel — she couldn't risk a light — and traced the entry she'd underlined four days ago in Vuthy's floating office, right before she'd put a knife to his throat and asked him the question he'd spent twenty years not answering: *where does Serey keep what he can't afford to lose?*

Vuthy had laughed, actually laughed, blood in his teeth, and said: *the cold room. But you'll die in the yard, girl. Everyone dies in the yard.*

Everyone died in the yard because everyone came through the gate. Petra intended to come through the roof.

* * *

The block building's roof was corrugated iron on timber trusses, pitched low, and reachable from the generator shed — a jump of a meter and a half across a gap that dropped into standing water full of things she preferred not to think about. She waited for the patrol. There was one; she'd counted his circuit twice, eleven minutes, lazy, umbrella up, rifle slung, boots splashing without urgency. Men who'd been paid through a wet season stop looking at the water.

He passed. She counted to ninety, crossed the yard in the dead zone hugging the wall, climbed the generator shed's ladder rungs welded to its flank, and jumped.

The roof rang like a gong under her boots and she froze, flat against the iron, listening to the sound roll away into the rain. Nothing answered. The generator covered sins; that was why she'd chosen this approach. She crawled to the ridge, found the vent pipe she'd hoped for — a stovepipe of galvanized steel, wide enough for her shoulders if her shoulders were feeling optimistic — and worked the collar loose with her knife. Below it, darkness breathed out cool, machine-chilled air that smelled of paper and diesel and, faintly, of something else.

Something human. Sweat and river water and the particular sourness of someone who hasn't eaten properly in days.

Petra went down the pipe feet-first, elbows locked against the steel, letting herself slide slow, and dropped the last half meter onto a concrete floor in perfect blackness.

She stood still and listened. A compressor cycled somewhere. Rain drummed the roof. And ten meters away, in the direction the smell came from, someone was breathing — shallow, controlled, the breathing of a person pretending to sleep who has been pretending so long it's become a discipline.

"Anong," Petra breathed. "It's me."

The breathing stopped.

"If you throw something at me I'll deserve it," Petra whispered, "but please don't, because I'm holding a knife and I'm jumpy."

A long silence. Then, from the darkness, cracked and disbelieving and furious all at once: "You absolute idiot. They've been waiting for you. Serey *wants* you here. He told me — he told me you'd come, that you'd never leave the case, that you were the kind of person who—"

"I know what kind of person I am." Petra was already moving toward the voice, hands out. "Where are you?"

"Left wall. There's shelving. Don't trip over—" A metallic scrape, a curse. "Don't trip over me."

Her shin found a shelf bracket, her knee found Anong's shoulder, and then they were gripping each other's forearms in the dark, a clumsy fierce handshake that neither of them let go of longer than was strictly necessary. Anong's wrist was thin as a bird's bone. There was a raw ring around it where zip ties had been.

"They fed you?"

"Rice. Twice a day. They want me presentable." Anong's laugh was a dry rattle. "I'm inventory, Petra. Like the paper. He keeps me cold so I keep."

"Can you walk?"

"I can swim, climb, and testify. In that order."

"Then we're leaving through the roof, and on the way you're going to show me which of these shelves is worth dying for, because I'm not carrying out everything." Petra turned her head, reading the room by its sounds — the compressor, the drip, the vast soft mass of boxed paper. "How many boxes?"

"Hundreds. Most of it's garbage. Permits, payroll, shipping manifests, twenty years of ordinary dirty business." Anong's grip tightened. "But there's a steel cabinet against the north wall, second shelf, green binders. Water quality reports. Originals, signed, dated — mine, and Somchai's, and the lab chain-of-custody forms for the samples I carried out. The ones the consortium's own contracted lab certified before they realized what the numbers meant and tried to buy them back. Serey kept them because destroying evidence you've been offered money for is a confession with extra steps."

"He kept them as leverage."

"He kept them because powerful men are magpies." Anong was already pulling her upright, guiding her left along the shelving by touch. "North wall. Count the shelves."

They moved together through the black, Petra counting, Anong whispering corrections, and at the fourth count Petra's fingers found steel, then a handle, then the specific slick cardboard-and-vinyl texture of a binder she had last seen strapped to Anong's back at a river junction four days and one lifetime ago.

"How many?" she whispered.

"Three green. Take all three. Leave the rest." A pause, and then Anong's voice changed, dropped into something quieter and more dangerous. "Petra. There's something else. Something I only figured out after they brought me here, because I heard the guards talking."

"Tell me on the roof."

"No. Tell you now, because if we don't make the roof you need to know it anyway." Her breath was fast and shallow. "Somchai isn't in the river. They never put him in the river. He's alive. He's been alive the whole time — they've been keeping him at the upriver camp, signing documents, a signature a week, keeping the audit legally *open* so the permit clock doesn't trigger the government review. As long as the survey team leader is technically missing rather than confirmed dead, the concession dispute stays frozen, and the consortium keeps pumping until the lease expires and they walk away clean. He's their clock, Petra. They're winding him."

The cold of the vault got suddenly much colder. Petra stood in the dark holding three green binders and understood, with the flat clarity that always arrived too late to be useful, why Serey had taken Anong alive and let Petra go. Not mercy. Inventory management. Two witnesses, kept cold, kept signable, kept *available* — because a drowned hydrologist and a dead guide proved nothing, but two living women who could be traded, silenced, or produced at the right moment were worth more than any vault.

"He knows we're here," Anong whispered. "Doesn't he."

"The patrol's been lazy," Petra said. "Either that or it's theater."

Above them, faintly, through the iron roof, boots splashed — more than one pair, moving without urgency, taking up positions.

Petra looked up at the vent pipe she'd come down, a column of black against marginally-less-black, and did the math she'd been avoiding since the jump. One way in. Eleven-minute patrols that had stopped mattering. A yard full of floodwater and floodlights between her and the fence, and somewhere out there Serey, patient as silt, having arranged the whole evening the way you arrange furniture.

Anong's hand found hers in the dark and squeezed once.

"You have a plan," Anong said. It wasn't a question. It was a request, the way a drowning woman requests a rope.

"Yeah," Petra said, shouldering the binders and reaching for her knife. "Their boats are still tied at the dock, riding high and empty, and every man they've got is standing in my dead zones looking for me on foot." She grinned into the blackness, and it felt good, it felt like the old days, it felt like the last honest thing left in her body. "Nobody ever expects anyone to leave by water either."

She put her boot on the first shelf and started climbing toward the pipe, toward the rain, toward the dock, with the whole cold weight of the truth on her back and Serey's voice already, somewhere out in the wet dark, calling her name like an invitation.

"Petra Lockridge," it said, amplified, almost friendly. "Let's talk about your debt."
