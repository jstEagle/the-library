## 29. Vault Fire

The compound smelled of diesel and river rot, and under both, faintly, of paper.

Petra crouched in the shadow of a fuel drum behind the generator shed, rain running off her chin, and watched the vault. It wasn't much to look at — a concrete block the size of a shipping container, bolted to a slab at the compound's landward edge, one steel door facing the water. A single floodlight above the door turned the falling rain into static. Two men stood beneath it, smoking under an awning, rifles slung casual as umbrellas.

Inside that box, if Anong was right about Serey's habits, was everything: the survey team's original filings, the audit drafts, the correspondence between the consortium and the provincial office, years of it. Paper was how men like Serey worked. Not knives. Paper.

And somewhere inside the compound — the barracks block, maybe, or the long low building with barred windows near the dock — was Anong.

She'd been counting guards since midnight. Six she could account for. The two at the vault. One on the dock, sheltering from the rain with his back against a piling, more asleep than awake. Two who made a slow circuit of the perimeter every eleven minutes, their boots squelching audibly through the drumming water. And one who hadn't left the main house all night, a lamp burning behind its shutters — Serey, or whoever Serey answered to, awake and working the way powerful men work at 3 a.m., which is to say comfortably.

Eleven minutes. She'd timed three circuits. Consistent men were predictable men, and predictable men were dead men, eventually, if you were patient enough.

The plan, such as it was, had been simple: wait for the circuit pair to pass the generator shed, cross the open ground to the barracks, find Anong, get out the way they'd come in — over the collapsed section of fence where the river had undercut the bank. Petra had done harder things with less. She'd done this exact thing with less, once, in a country that no longer let her enter it.

But the rain was wrong tonight. It had been building since dusk, that heavy, breathless pressure she'd learned to read before she could read words, and now it broke all at once — not harder, exactly, but *lower*, the storm dropping out of the clouds like something landing. The river's voice changed pitch. On the dock, the sleeping guard woke, swore, and stumbled inside the boathouse.

Petra made her decision the way she always did: fast, then living with it.

She crossed the open ground flat-out, boots throwing mud, and reached the barracks wall while the thunder was still rolling. The barred windows were dark. She moved along them, fingers finding each bar, testing, until at the third window a hand shot through the gap and closed around her wrist hard enough to grind bone.

She didn't scream. Years ago she'd have screamed. Now she just went still and waited for the grip to resolve into something she recognized.

Anong's face came to the bars, pale, wet-haired, eyes enormous. "You're insane," she whispered.

"Probably." Petra got her knife under the hasp of the window shutter. Cheap padlock, older than either of them. "Can you fit through here?"

"I've been fitting through worse things for six days." But Anong hesitated, and in the lightning-flash Petra saw why: her wrists were raw, one eye swollen half-shut, and there was a stiffness in the way she held her left side that Petra's stomach understood before her head did. "They took my pack," Anong said. "First hour. They took everything."

"The samples?"

"Gone. Or—" Anong's jaw tightened. "Or where I put them is gone. I don't know yet. That's the problem with hiding things well."

"We'll deal with it. Come on."

The lock snapped on the second strike. Anong came through the window with a gracelessness that frightened Petra more than any wound — the hydrologist who'd scrambled down riverbanks like a gibbon was moving like a woman twice her age — and then they were pressed together in the mud behind the barracks, two heartbeats hammering against the same rain.

* * *

They almost made the fence.

That was the thing Petra would turn over later, in the long hours when memory became a kind of punishment: they *almost* made it. Forty meters of open ground, the broken fence line visible ahead, the black undercut bank beyond it. Anong was slowing her down and knew it and said nothing, just kept moving, one arm clamped to her ribs, and Petra had gotten a hand under her elbow and was half-carrying her, counting the seconds to the next patrol circuit—

The floodlight hit them like a hand.

Not the vault light. A second light, from the boathouse roof, that Petra had never clocked because it had never been switched on. It pinned them to the mud with no shadows anywhere to run into, and voices started up across the compound, unhurried voices, voices with no fear in them at all.

"Don't," Anong breathed, as Petra's hand went to her knife. "There are too many. Don't make it worse."

It was already worse. Men were converging through the rain, five of them, six, rifles loose in their hands, and at the center of them, when they opened a lane, walked Serey.

He was barefoot. That was the detail that lodged in Petra's mind and stayed there, absurd and horrible: the man had come out into a monsoon downpour at four in the morning in a linen shirt and bare feet, holding an umbrella one of his men carried for him, and he looked *unbothered*. He looked at Petra the way she imagined a man looks at weather he'd been told might come.

"Ms. Lockridge." His voice carried easily under the rain. "You cost me a great deal of sleep. I want you to know I resent that more than anything else you've done."

"Let her go," Petra said. "Your quarrel's with me."

"It really isn't." He glanced at Anong with something that wasn't quite cruelty — something more administrative than that. "Dr. Anong has been a guest. Guests who break into houses, though." He sighed, a small, genuine sound. "Bring them."

* * *

They put Anong in the boathouse and Petra in front of Serey, in the dry, in the main house, with tea.

That was the worst part, she thought. The tea. A white porcelain cup on a lacquer tray, steam rising, while outside the storm hammered the shutters and somewhere across the compound her only ally sat bleeding in a room full of rifles. Serey poured for her himself. His hands were steady. Everything about him was steady.

"You should understand your position," he began.

"I understand it fine."

"Do you? Because I don't think you do. I think you believe you're in a story, Ms. Lockridge — the brave guide, the corrupt company, the truth that will out. Stories are very seductive. But look at where you actually are." He gestured at the room, the lamplight, the rain. "You have no boat. No money. No ally in condition to walk. Your fixer sold you to me nine days ago — yes, Vuthy, don't look surprised, he's been mine longer than he's been yours. You have a case of degraded water samples and a story nobody will print, because everyone who could print it is paid or afraid. And in thirty-six days, my permits renew automatically, because renewals are procedural, and procedures require paperwork, and paperwork—" he smiled thinly— "is what I keep in that concrete building you were so interested in."

"You know what's in the water," Petra said. "You've seen the village."

"I've seen *costs*," Serey said. "Every project has them. The question is never whether there are costs. The question is who pays them, and whether they can afford the currency." He leaned forward. "Here is my last offer, and I'll only make it once. Walk away tonight. Both of you. There's a car at the gate and cash in the glovebox and no record of any of this — not the survey team, not the checkpoint, not the village. Your name stays exactly as dirty as it is today, which means it stays *survivable*. People survive dirty names, Ms. Lockridge. They do not survive graves."

"And if I say no?"

Serey looked at her for a long moment, and whatever he saw in her face seemed to settle something for him. He sat back. He finished his tea.

"Then tomorrow night," he said, "I burn the vault."

* * *

They locked her in a storeroom off the kitchen, which was either arrogance or contempt, and it didn't matter which, because both meant the same thing: he did not believe she could matter anymore.

The storeroom had a high window with a rotten frame. It took Petra forty minutes and most of the skin off her palms, and by the time she dropped into the mud behind the kitchen the sky had gone the color of wet ash and the rain had thinned to a steady, businesslike fall. Dawn was coming. She could feel it the way animals feel earthquakes.

She found the boathouse guard asleep again — men who work in monsoons learn to sleep through anything, and it would kill some of them someday — and she took him quietly, took his keys, and got Anong out into the grey light with her heart going like a trip-hammer.

"They're burning it tonight," Petra said. "Everything. He told me to my face. He wants me to watch, I think. He wants me to understand that knowing things counts for nothing if the paper's gone."

Anong leaned against the boathouse wall. In the pre-dawn grey her swollen eye looked terrible and her voice, when it came, was almost amused.

"He told you his schedule."

"Yeah. Arrogance or contempt."

"No." Anong pushed herself upright. "No, listen to me. Serey doesn't waste moves and doesn't boast. If he told you, it's because telling you costs nothing — because the paper was already dead the moment we were caught. Which means tonight isn't a threat." Her good eye fixed on Petra. "It's a *cleanup*. And cleanups happen on schedule, which means we know exactly when every man he has will be standing around a fire watching it."

Petra stared at her. Then, slowly, she started to smile, and it felt strange on her face, like a muscle she'd stopped using.

"You hid the samples before they took you."

"In the one place nobody in this country ever searches." Anong's mouth twitched. "The river. Sealed case, weighted, sunk off the drowned tree at the second bend downstream — the one with the fishing shrine. My brother's water. My markers. I can find it blind." She paused, and the amusement drained out of her. "The field logs, though. The logs were in my pack. They have them. Chain of custody needs my logs."

"Then we steal them back tonight," Petra said, "while the whole compound is busy watching Serey burn his own history. And then we go get your case, and we run this river ahead of every boat he owns."

* * *

Night came early under the storm, and with it, the fire.

They watched it from the undercut bank two hundred meters downstream, lying flat in the mud among the exposed roots, and Petra had to admit Serey understood theater. They brought the records out in armfuls under floodlights — boxes and boxes of them, she could see the paper edges even at that distance — and fed them into the vault doorway, and then Serey himself walked up with a jerrican and did it personally, barefoot again, unhurried, while his men formed a loose ring around the blaze with their rifles pointed outward at the darkness. At her. At the idea of her.

The fire went up orange and enormous, and the heat of it reached the riverbank, and the smoke rolled sideways along the water, and inside that fire was the survey team's last filing, the audit drafts, the letters, the signatures — every gram of proof that the consortium had known, had decided, had chosen the costs and assigned them to children.

Anong didn't cry. She lay beside Petra in the mud and watched eight years of her work become light and smoke, and her face never moved at all, which was somehow worse than crying.

"They'll say it was an accident," she said finally. "Electrical fault. Monsoon damage. There'll be an inspection, a report, a fine. Paper about the loss of paper."

"Let them say it."

"You don't understand. Without the filings, without the originals, my samples are just water in bottles. Anyone can claim I fabricated them. Anyone can claim I'm disgraced, unstable, vengeful — I *am* disgraced, Petra. That's the whole design of people like Serey. You discredit the witness first, so the evidence arrives pre-broken." Her voice stayed flat. "I watched that man burn my life's work and the worst part is he was right to be calm. He's won. He's actually won."

Petra reached into her jacket, into the inner pocket where she'd kept it since the storeroom window, wrapped in oilcloth, taken from the barracks office in the forty minutes before she'd gone for Anong — because she'd gone through the office first, on instinct, the way she always searched a camp before searching for people.

She set the bundle in the mud between them and unwrapped it.

Field logs. Three of them, water-swollen at the corners, Anong's cramped handwriting covering every page — sampling sites, dates, depths, readings, the chain of custody written out day by day in ink that wouldn't lie.

Anong looked at them for a long time. Then she looked at Petra, and something in her ruined face cracked open, and she laughed — one short, disbelieving sound — and pressed both hands over her mouth.

"You went in for the *logs*."

"I went in for you. The logs were on the way." Petra wrapped them back up and handed them over, and Anong took them the way people hold infants. "Serey burned the paper he knew about. He kept the paper he didn't. That's the difference between us and him — he thinks evidence lives in buildings."

Downstream, the fire was collapsing inward, the floodlights dying one by one, the ring of men breaking up into the ordinary business of the night. In thirty-six days the permits would renew, unless. Unless a sealed case came up off the bottom of the river at the second bend. Unless a disgraced hydrologist's handwriting reached a lab before the chain of custody expired. Unless two women with no boat, no money, and no country that wanted them could outrun everything Serey owned.

Thunder rolled upstream from the delta, where the real storm was still coming.

"Get some sleep," Petra said. "At first light, we dive."
