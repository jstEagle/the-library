## 30. Chase on the Rising River

The vault was still burning when they came out of the compound.

Petra could see it from the waterline — a column of black smoke leaning against the rain, feeding on thirty years of paper. Somewhere inside that fire were the leases, the audit reports, the signatures that had turned six surveyors into a rumor. Serey had lit it himself, she'd bet her father's compass on it, and he'd done it with the calm of a man closing out an account.

It didn't matter. Anong had the case strapped across her back like a child, and under the tarp in the bottom of the longboat were her field logs, wrapped in plastic, and the eleven samples that had survived the heat and the years. Eleven vials of river water, taken from places no official map admitted existed. That was the weapon now. Paper burned. Water kept its memory.

"Go," Anong said. "Now. Before they finish counting what's missing."

Petra already had the engine running. The compound's dock was a splintered tongue of concrete reaching into a river that no longer looked like a river. Three days of monsoon peak had turned the Khao Sen's main channel into something brown and muscular, carrying whole trees sideways, the current moving at a speed that made her stomach tighten in a way she hadn't felt since the Andes. This wasn't water anymore. It was freight.

She backed them off the dock hard, gunned it, and swung the bow downstream.

* * *

For the first ten minutes there was nothing behind them but rain.

Petra used the time the way she used all quiet: badly, then well. She read the river. That was the whole job now — reading. The flood had erased every feature she might have memorized on the way up. Sandbars gone. Gravel bars gone. The cutbank where she'd planned to make her first turn was now a standing wave four feet high with a whirlpool chewing at its tail. She steered by the old logic instead: the water told you where the land was. Fast water over deep channels ran smooth and green-brown. Slow water meant something underneath it, waiting.

"Stay off the smooth edges," she said. "Smooth means shallow or it means eddy, and both will kill us."

Anong crouched low behind the console, one arm locked through the sample case's strap. "How far to the falls?"

"Six hours if the river behaves." Petra didn't look at her. "It won't behave."

"Then say something useful."

"That was the useful part."

Rain hammered the tarp. The boat took the first set of rapids well — a quarter-mile of standing waves that Petra threaded left of center, riding the tongues of green water between the white, engine screaming whenever the prop came half out of a trough. Her hands knew this work. Her hands had always known this work; it was everything above the wrists that had ever failed her.

Then Anong said, quietly, "Boat."

Petra looked back.

Two of them, maybe eight hundred meters upstream of the compound dock and closing. Aluminum-hulled, big outboards, running the center of the channel with the arrogance of boats that didn't care what the river cost them. She counted heads. Four in the lead boat, three in the second. Rifles slung, not raised — not yet. Serey's men ran professional. They'd want to herd first, shoot second. Bullets punched holes in boats, and holes in boats sank evidence along with everyone in it.

"They'll try to pin us against the bank before the gorge," Petra said. "There's a straight stretch coming. Deep and fast. Perfect for them."

"So we don't be there."

"We can't not be there. It's the only channel." Petra's mind was already running the map — her father's map, the notes she'd found folded inside the compass liner, the route he'd run these rivers when she was learning to walk. *Left channel below the shrine rock. Right is a trap at any level.* He'd written it forty years ago for water a third of this height. But water logic didn't change. Only the margins did.

"Hold on to something that loves you," she said, and turned the boat hard right, into water no sane person would choose.

* * *

The right side of the channel at flood stage was a broken staircase.

Water came down it in steps, each drop followed by a boiling hole, each hole flanked by rocks that showed themselves only as swirls and seams. Petra ran it the way her father had taught her to run anything: commit early, correct small, never stop looking two moves ahead. The boat dropped, slammed, wallowed in a hole while the engine clawed for grip, then broke free and dropped again. Something cracked against the hull — a log, a rock, she'd never know — and the sound went through the frame and into her teeth.

Anong never made a sound. She just moved with the boat, braced low, arms around the case, eyes forward. Once, when a wave came over the bow and hit them like a thrown bucket, she spat out river and said, "If we lose these samples I'm walking back into that compound myself."

"Noted."

"I mean it, Petra."

"I know you do. Sit down."

Behind them, the first aluminum boat hesitated at the top of the staircase. She saw it in the mirror-flash of a wave — the bow swing wide, the driver reconsidering. Then it committed anyway, because Serey paid for commitment, and came down the steps twice as fast as her because it was twice as heavy and half as careful. It caught the third hole wrong. The stern kicked, the boat spun broadside, and the river rolled it like a hand flicking a coin. Men and gear scattered into the brown. Two of them surfaced immediately, swimming hard for the bank. The third came up twenty meters down, clinging to a fuel can, and the fourth she never saw at all.

Petra felt the old reflex rise — *stop, go back, you're the guide, going back is the whole job* — and she killed it with effort. Those men had helped burn a village. They had put Anong in a cage. The river was making its own argument, and she let it.

"One down," Anong said. No triumph in it. Just arithmetic.

"Four left," Petra said. "The second boat went around. They know this stretch better than their friend did."

* * *

The gorge narrowed and the river stopped being a river and became a chute.

Walls of limestone rose on both sides, black with rain, and the water between them ran fast and deep and strangely smooth — the kind of smooth that frightened Petra more than any rapid, because it meant speed without friction, speed that built and built with nothing to bleed it off. Their little longboat surfed it. The engine was almost decoration now; the current was doing the driving, and all she controlled was the angle.

That was when the shooting started.

The second aluminum boat had come down the left channel and now drew alongside, fifty meters back and outside, where the water was faster. A rifle cracked — once, twice — and rounds slapped the water near the stern. Not at the engine. At the boat's sides. Herding shots. They wanted her to turn toward the wall, toward the seam of slack water hugging the cliff, which at this level would be a recirculating nightmare that would hold a boat and roll it until the river got bored.

"He wants the case intact," Anong shouted over the engine. "He won't sink us."

"He doesn't have to sink us. He has to make me steer somewhere stupid." Petra's eyes flicked between the water ahead and the boat behind. Fifty meters. Forty. In the smooth water of the chute they were gaining, and once they had her pinned against the wall it was over — they'd come alongside, and men with rifles don't negotiate.

Ahead, the chute bent left around a buttress of rock, and on the outside of the bend — the fast side, the side she should take — her father's memory surfaced unbidden: *the shrine rock, left channel, stay left even when it looks wrong.* Below the bend, the left channel split around a midstream fin of stone. At normal water, the right side of the fin was the easy line. At this water, the right side was a compression — the whole river squeezed through a gap that today would be barely wider than the boat, a slot of standing water and undercut rock.

Everyone sane would go right of the fin. Everyone sane would die there today.

"Anong. When I say now, get flat."

"Why?"

"Because I'm about to do something my father would either admire or disown me for."

She waited until the bend. Waited until the buttress blocked the rifleman's line for exactly as long as it took to pass it — three seconds, maybe four. "Now."

She cut left, hard, across the current, into the slot.

The world became noise. The walls closed in and the water stood up around them, green and glassy and impossibly fast, and the boat went through the compression like a cork through a bottleneck — scraping rock on both sides, the hull shrieking, Anong flat in the bilge with the case under her body, Petra holding the tiller with both hands and steering by feel because sight was useless in the spray. For one endless second the boat stopped dead, pinned, the river pushing and the rock refusing—

—and then the pressure eased, the slot spat them out, and they were through, into the pool below, spinning slowly in the boil.

Behind them, the aluminum boat reached the fin and its driver did the sane thing. He went right. Petra watched him enter the compression with too much boat and too much speed, saw the bow rise, stall, and the river fold the aluminum around the undercut like paper. It didn't sink so much as stop existing as a boat. The men went into the water. One made the eddy behind the fin. One didn't surface where she could see.

Three left, then. Maybe.

Her hands were shaking now, the adrenaline bill coming due. She throttled up and ran the pool's exit current, putting distance on while distance was a thing you could put.

* * *

They ran for another hour in a silence broken only by the engine and the rain.

The gorge opened. The valley widened. The river spread out into flooded forest — acres of drowned trees standing hip-deep in moving water, the channel threading between trunks that at dry season lined a bank. It was slower here, more forgiving, and Petra hated it, because slow water meant they could be seen, and seen meant chased, and she had no illusions about how many boats Serey owned.

Anong crawled forward and sat beside her at the console, shoulder to shoulder, both of them soaked past the meaning of the word.

"You could have gone right," Anong said. "At the fin. Like a normal person."

"A normal person would be dead."

"My point."

Petra almost smiled. It hurt her face. "My father mapped this stretch. Both lines. He wrote *right looks easier* in the margin, underlined twice. He ran left his whole life."

"The margin man," Anong said. She'd heard enough about the compass and the notes to know the shape of the story. "You've been listening to him all day."

"Somebody has to. You listen to your water. I listen to mine."

Anong looked back the way they'd come, at the empty flooded forest, and her voice dropped. "They'll regroup. Whatever's left of them. Serey doesn't quit — quitting isn't in the contract structure." She patted the case between her knees. "And this is the last copy of everything. If the vault burned the way it looked like it burned—"

"It burned."

"—then we're not carrying evidence anymore. We're carrying the evidence."

Petra checked the fuel gauge and felt the day tilt. Half a tank. The falls were two hours ahead, maybe less at this water level, and after the falls the river braided out into the delta country where a boat could hide. But half a tank didn't leave room for hiding, or for error, or for the detour her gut was telling her the falls section would demand.

"How much farther can we get on what's in the tank?" Anong asked, reading her face.

"To the base of the falls. Nowhere past."

"And if they come again?"

Petra looked downstream, at the brown immensity of it, at the horizon line where the river dropped off the edge of the visible world in a smudge of spray. Even from here she could hear it — a low, continuous thunder under the rain, the sound of a hundred thousand tons of water deciding where the land ended.

"Then they'd better bring more than three boats," she said.

The thunder grew. The spray-smudge resolved into falling water, a white curtain across a third of the sky, and the river beneath them began, gently and then not gently, to pull.

Ahead, at the lip of the falls, something glinted. Metal. A boat, drawn up on the rocks at the take-out point, engine cover open, two figures standing beside it watching them come.

Not Serey's men. Wrong boats, wrong posture, wrong everything.

Fishermen? At the falls, in this?

Anong saw them too. "Friends of yours?"

"No," Petra said, and cut the throttle, and reached for the compass.
