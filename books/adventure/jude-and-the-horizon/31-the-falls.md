## 31. The Falls

The river told them before the sound did.

Petra felt it in the hull first — a long, smooth acceleration, the water tilting under them like a floor going wrong. Then the mist came off the banks in cold rags, and the roar arrived, not a noise so much as a pressure, a hand laid flat against her chest.

"That's it," she said. "That's the drop."

Anong looked over her shoulder at the two boats behind them — twin black hulls, outboard engines snarling through the rain, four men visible in the lead one and a fifth shape at the tiller that could only be Serey himself, standing upright like he was riding a bus. "How far behind?"

"Two minutes. Maybe less if they're stupid." Petra wiped the sheeting rain off her face with the back of her wrist. "They're stupid."

"Then we stop here and fight—"

"We stop here and die." She pointed downstream, to where the river bent left around a shoulder of black limestone and simply ended — a seam of white where green water folded over itself and vanished. "There's a beach on the right, maybe eighty meters above the lip. We could land there. They'd land next to us. Five men with guns against two women with a bail bucket and my good looks." She shook her head. "No. We go over."

Anong stared at her. Rain ran off her chin. "You've done this before."

"I've *read* about this before," Petra said, which was almost true, and both of them knew which half was the lie.

She opened the compass case across her knees. The brass lid was dented from thirty years of other people's rivers, and inside, beneath the needle that still swung faithfully toward a magnetic north nobody cared about anymore, lay the folded oilcloth packet her father had kept there since before she was born. She'd found it eleven days ago in a flooded cave with three days of storm water rising around her ankles, and she had read those route notes until she could recite them, because somewhere between the cave and this moment they had stopped being her father's handwriting and become the only map in the world that mattered.

*Falls section,* he'd written, in pencil gone soft as moth wings. *Khao Sen gorge, upper reach. Two channels. Left is the postcard — big water, big drop, everyone takes it, half of them don't finish. Right channel looks like nothing. Looks like a dead end. It isn't. There's a slot behind the midstream pillar, maybe six meters wide, and past it the river drops in three steps instead of one. You will be sure you are going to die twice. Both times you are wrong.*

He'd written it in 1987, running timber surveys for a company that had since been absorbed by the same consortium whose men were now forty seconds behind her. He had never known what he was mapping it for. She knew now. The irony was so large it had its own weather system.

"Right channel," she said. "Behind the pillar."

"There is no right channel," Anong said. "I've seen the survey charts. The gorge narrows to—"

"The survey charts were drawn by people who never went up there, because the consortium didn't want anyone going up there. My father went up there." She snapped the case shut. "Sit down. Hold the sample case against your chest with both arms. If we ship water, it floats better than you do, and I need you to let go of everything else and grab the gunwale. Everything else. Including me."

"That's a terrible instruction."

"It's the only one that keeps the data dry." Petra put the engine down hard and turned the bow toward the pillar.

* * *

The last kilometer happened in pieces.

First the bend, taken too fast, the hull skidding on the flood-current's shoulder so that the black limestone wall swung at them like a door. Petra corrected with throttle instead of rudder — rudder was a suggestion in water this heavy; throttle was an argument — and the boat came around with a foot to spare, close enough that she could see the individual crystals in the rock, wet and glittering like the inside of a geode.

Then the pillar. It rose out of the mist all at once, a cathedral column of stone with the main channel slamming against its left face in a brown explosion of spray. Behind it, exactly where a dead man's pencil had promised, a seam of darker water slid quietly into shadow.

It looked like nothing. It looked like a dead end. Her hands wanted to turn left with every instinct she owned, toward the big obvious water, the honest water, the water that at least showed you what it intended to do.

*Both times you are wrong.*

She went right.

The world changed key. The roar dropped away as though someone had shut a door on it, and suddenly they were in a slot no wider than the boat was long, walls of wet stone on either side, the water smooth and fast and green-black, moving with the eerie confidence of something that knows exactly where it's going. Mist drifted through the gap in slow ropes. It was, absurdly, almost peaceful.

"Oh," Anong said softly. "Oh, it's beautiful."

"Don't get attached."

"Why not?"

"Because according to this—" Petra tapped the compass case without looking down, eyes on the water ahead, reading the current the way her father had taught her to read it, not by looking at the surface but at what the surface was trying to hide — "in about twenty seconds it stops being beautiful."

The slot ended the way he said it would: all at once. The green water simply tipped over a lip and became white, and the first step dropped them maybe two meters into a churning trough that hit the hull like a truck. Petra had the engine trimmed up and the boat straightened into it, bow-first, the only way down, and for one full second they were airborne in the sense that mattered — weightless, committed, past the point where the river accepted arguments.

They landed. The hull cracked against the trough, took green water over both gunwales, and then the current had them again, hauling them toward step two.

"Second time!" she shouted over the noise. "This is the second time! We're supposed to be sure we're dying!"

"ARE WE?"

"NO!"

Step two was bigger. It was also, her father's notes insisted, slightly off-center — the safe line hugged the right wall where an eddy curled back on itself, a tongue of slack water wide enough to slide a boat through if you hit it within a boat-length of accuracy or drowned if you missed by more. Petra lined it up the way you line up anything that matters: pick your mark upstream, pick your mark downstream, keep them stacked, and trust the arithmetic of two fixed points more than you trust your own screaming nerves.

A dead tree stood on the right bank, leaning. That was upstream. A pale scar of rock below it. That was downstream. Stack them. Hold them.

She held them.

The eddy took the stern, kissed the bow sideways, and set them down the tongue of slack water like a mother setting down a sleeping child, and then step three took that tenderness back with interest — a five-meter drop into a pool the size of a stadium, ringed by walls, the whole gorge roaring around them on every side except the one they'd come down.

They hit the pool flat and hard. The engine coughed, caught, coughed again. Water sloshed shin-deep in the bilge. Anong was laughing — actually laughing, high and disbelieving, both arms locked around the sample case like it was a child.

"You're bleeding," Petra observed.

Anong touched her eyebrow, looked at the red on her fingers, and laughed harder. "You're *grinning.*"

"I am not." She was. She could feel it, enormous and involuntary, the grin of a woman who has just discovered that the best thing her father ever gave her wasn't the compass at all. "Bail. We're not done."

* * *

Because they weren't. Below the third step the pool drained out through a final chute into the lower gorge, and the lower gorge was fast, brown, and littered — the flood had spent three weeks tearing the jungle apart and feeding the results to the river. Whole trees spun in the eddies, root balls skyward like drowned giants reaching for a shore that kept moving. A corrugated roof sheet flashed past, spinning. Somewhere ahead a village's worth of debris was strung along the current lines.

And behind them, above, the left channel roared.

Petra pulled into the lee of the right wall and looked back up. Through the ragged mist she could see the top of the main drop, and she could see the black boat come into it — too fast, too far left, Serey's man at the tiller fighting the current that had already made his decisions for him. She watched the bow rise. She watched it hang there, suspended in the spray, and she thought, with a strange detached clarity: *he doesn't know it's one drop. He thinks it's rapids. Nobody runs blind water blind.*

The boat went vertical. For a heartbeat it stood on its tail like a rearing animal, the four men visible against the sky, one of them — she was almost sure — simply letting go.

Then the river closed over the whole argument.

The sound reached them a second later, a boom rolling down the gorge like a door slammed in a very large house. Debris geysered out of the foam. One man surfaced in the boil, thrashing, and the pool's circulation began the patient work of folding him under. Another clung to the wrecked hull as it cartwheeled. The rest did not surface at all.

Anong had gone quiet. "Petra."

"I see it."

"They didn't have to run it. They could have landed. They could have waited. They chose—"

"They chose to chase us into water they'd never seen, because a man who pays for obedience assumes the river obeys too." Petra's voice came out flatter than she felt. Inside she felt a great many things, and most of them were not grief, and some of them were, and none of them were simple. "Get the bail bucket."

Downstream, wedged against a rock shelf where the pool spilled into the chute, something orange snagged and held. A fuel can. And beside it, climbing out of the water onto the shelf with the unhurried movements of a man who had decided some time ago that drowning was beneath him, a figure in a soaked linen shirt.

Serey.

He stood on the rock, small with distance, and looked down the gorge directly at their boat — she could feel it, the specific weight of being looked at by a man doing arithmetic. Then he raised one hand. Not a fist. Not a weapon. An open palm, level, the universal gesture of *wait*.

"He wants us to come to him," Anong said.

"He wants us to think he wants us to come to him." Petra weighed the throttle. The chute ahead was runnable but ugly, and beyond it the lower gorge offered ten kilometers of floodwater, debris, and whatever remained of Serey's second boat, which had wisely hung back and was now, she noted, turning around. Running. Going to report. Going to bring more.

If she passed Serey's shelf, she might never see him again until the next ambush, with more boats, more guns, and no waterfall to do her work for her.

If she picked him up—

"We're not picking him up," Anong said, reading her face.

"I know we're not picking him up."

"But you're thinking about it."

"I'm thinking that a man who nearly killed us twice is standing on a rock with a broken empire, and every instinct I have is screaming that leaving him there is the smart move and the wrong move, and I'm tired of those being different things." Petra exhaled. The rain had thinned to a fine drifting haze; the light in the gorge had gone silver and soft, almost gentle, as if the day were embarrassed by what had just happened in it. "We pass the shelf. Slow. Close enough that he can talk. If he talks, we learn something. If he reaches for a weapon, the river gets him after all."

"And if he asks for mercy?"

Petra looked at the sample case between Anong's arms, at the degraded vials and the field logs and the chain of custody that thirty-eight days of other people's money had tried to break and couldn't. She looked at the water folding over the place where four men had been.

"Then he'd be the first man in this entire country to ask for something honestly," she said, and put the engine in gear.

* * *

The shelf came up on the right, slick and black, and Serey stood on it with his shirt plastered to him and his hair flattened, and up close he looked smaller than he ever had — not diminished physically but structurally, the way a building looks once you've seen the scaffolding. His pistol was in his waistband, visible, untouched. He'd made his choice about that before they arrived, and he let them see that he'd made it.

"Ms. Lockridge." His voice carried easily over the water; he'd pitched it for her, not shouted it. "You run a river beautifully. Your father would be proud."

Every muscle in her body went still. "You knew my father."

"I knew *of* him. Everyone in concessions knew of him. The farang who mapped the unmappable." Serey smiled, and it was the smile of a man laying down a card he'd been holding since chapter one. "Do you know why your father's notes were in that compass, Ms. Lockridge? Do you know who paid for that 1987 survey?" He spread his hands, dripping. "We did. The consortium. Your father worked for us. In a sense, you always have."

The lie was elegant. That was what frightened her — not the words but the architecture of them, the way truth and falsehood were mortared together so smoothly you'd have to take the whole wall down to find the crack. Her father had surveyed timber boundaries. Her father had never — she checked the memory, checked it hard — no. There was nothing. But doubt had been introduced, and doubt was the product Serey sold.

"Anong," Petra said quietly, "if he moves, run him down."

"With pleasure," said Anong, who hated him more cleanly than she did.

Petra brought the boat parallel to the shelf, ten meters out, engine idling against the current. Water dripped off Serey's fingertips into the pool. Up close, she could see the cut on his forehead, the tremor in his hands he was managing the way he managed everything — visibly, expensively, well.

"You have something I need," he said. "I have something you need. This is not a threat. The threats are finished — look around you. This is arithmetic." He glanced once, briefly, at the churn where his men had gone, and something crossed his face that was not performance. "Three of mine are in that pool. I am not asking you to mourn them. I am asking you to consider that I am the last man standing between you and the people who sent them, and those people do not stand on rocks. They sit in rooms. And when the permit clock hits zero in nine days, the rooms decide what happens to two women in a leaking boat, and I am offering to be in the room."

"You're offering to sell them out."

"I'm offering to survive them. There's a difference, and it's the only difference that has ever mattered." He took a slow breath. "Come ashore, Ms. Lockridge. Ten minutes. That's all I ask. Ten minutes, and then you can run your river and I'll run mine, and we'll see which of us the horizon favors."

Behind her, Anong said nothing, but Petra could feel her silence leaning forward like a hand on her shoulder.

Ten minutes. The last man standing. The room where the decisions got made.

Petra looked at the water between them, brown and patient, carrying the whole ruined jungle toward the sea.

"One condition," she said.

Serey tilted his head, waiting.

"She comes with me," Petra said, nodding at Anong. "Or nobody comes ashore at all."
