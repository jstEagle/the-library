## 32. Serey's Offer

The river spat them out into a bend of dead calm, and Petra steered for the gravel bar on the inside of it because her hands told her to before her head caught up. The longboat ground ashore with a sound like a rib cracking. Anong was already moving, gathering the sample case against her chest, and Petra let her — let her do anything, so long as she kept moving, kept breathing, kept being alive.

Behind them, maybe two kilometers back through the gorge, the sound of Serey's lead boat hitting the falls had carried all the way down. Not an explosion. Worse than that: a long tearing groan of aluminum and fiberglass surrendering to water that had been waiting years for something to break on it.

Petra killed the engine and listened. Cicadas. Water sliding over stone. Her own pulse, loud as a fist on a door.

"We can't stop here," Anong said. Her voice had gone thin. She'd been bailing since the second rapid, arms shaking, hair plastered flat, and still she'd held the case above the waterline through every one of them like it was a child.

"We're not stopping. We're breathing." Petra swung her legs over the gunwale and tested the gravel with one boot. Solid. Shallow gradient. Good sightlines up and down the bend. "Two minutes. Then we cut inland along the ridge line and pick up water again below the confluence."

"And if they have boats below the confluence?"

"Then we'll know very quickly."

Anong almost smiled at that. Almost. It died somewhere in the middle of forming, and what replaced it was the look Petra had seen on her face twice now — once in the flooded cave, once when they'd found Rattanak's body wedged in the roots upstream. The look of a woman doing arithmetic she hated.

Petra got out of the boat and her left leg announced its opinion of the last six hours. She'd taken a glancing hit off a rock shelf in the third rapid — nothing broken, she was fairly sure, but the thigh was swelling under her trousers and every step from here would be negotiated, not given. She stood anyway. You stood when the alternative was sitting down forever.

That was when she saw the man on the far end of the gravel bar.

He was just standing there, hands loose at his sides, dressed in a dry shirt that had no business being dry this deep into the monsoon. Behind him, half-hidden in the riverside scrub, sat a gray inflatable with an outboard and two men who made no move to reach for anything. They didn't need to. There were four of them and two of her, one of her limping, and the river behind her was a wall of white noise that would swallow any scream before it cleared the first bend.

Serey.

He'd come downriver ahead of his own men. Or he'd never been behind them at all. Either way, he was here, and he was smiling the way a banker smiles — politely, patiently, like time was a thing other people ran out of.

"Ms. Lockridge," he called across the bar. "You are a difficult woman to buy lunch for."

* * *

She made him talk from thirty meters. She walked the bar's length slowly, putting herself between him and Anong, keeping the swollen leg out of her gait as much as the gravel allowed. Anong drifted with her, case hugged tight, eyes moving between the two men in the inflatable and the treeline beyond. Petra noted the treeline too. Empty, or empty enough. If there were more men in it, they were disciplined about it, and discipline cost money, and everything she knew about Serey said he paid for exactly as much as he needed.

"You lost a boat," Petra said.

"I lost a boat." He spread his hands, rueful, a man discussing weather. "The pilot is alive. Swam out below the falls. Your father's route notes are better than my charts, I will concede that freely." His English was flawless, faintly formal, the accent of someone educated in three countries and loyal to none of them. "I have read your file, Ms. Lockridge. All of it. The Andes. The inquiry. The two blacklists. Do you know what struck me most?"

"The part where I'm dangerous?"

"The part where you were right." He took a step forward; she shook her head once, small, and he stopped, unbothered, folding the gesture into his rhythm like a dance partner. "The client who died — you told them the route was unsafe. You put it in writing. They overruled you because the permit window was closing and the mountain does not care about permits. And afterward, when the reports came out, everyone agreed you had been correct, and everyone agreed it changed nothing. Two countries decided the cheapest solution was that you not work there anymore." He tilted his head. "You must have noticed. Systems do not punish people for being wrong. They punish people for making the paperwork embarrassing."

"Is there a point coming?"

"There is an offer coming. I want you to hear the whole shape of it before you answer, because I suspect you have spent your entire adult life answering too quickly." He gestured at the river, the rain-soft hills, the whole drowned green country of it. "In thirty-one days, four mining concessions expire. Not just ours — three others. Ours is simply the largest, and the only one with a hydrologist running around with a plastic case full of evidence that the renewal committee has never seen. If that case reaches a lab, then a journalist, then a courtroom, the concession dies. Eleven hundred direct jobs. A provincial road contract. A hospital wing that is ninety percent funded by our community budget. All of it dies with it. That is what is inside your girlfriend's box, Ms. Lockridge. Not justice. Arithmetic."

"She's not my—" Petra stopped. Let it go. "The contamination is real. You've seen her data. Your own survey team collected half of it before you disappeared them."

"I arranged for their report to be filed incorrectly," Serey said, with the mild precision of a man correcting a menu order. "They were detained, compensated, and relocated. One of them — the leader — was foolish, and a man I no longer employ was cruel. I regret it. I regret it the way I regret the boat, which is sincerely and without it changing my plans." He clasped his hands behind his back. "Here is the offer. Listen to the whole shape of it."

The rain started again, fine and warm, beading on the gravel. Nobody moved.

"First: the record. I have retained counsel in La Paz, in Quito, and here. Within ninety days of your cooperation, both blacklists are vacated. Not appealed — vacated. The inquiries are reopened on procedural grounds, the findings set aside, and your certifications restored. You will guide again. Legally. Anywhere."

Petra felt that one land somewhere under her sternum, and hated that she felt it. Nine years. Nine years of border towns and cash jobs and pawning gear, of Vuthy's ledger and her father's compass as the only thing she owned outright. A door opening that she'd stopped believing was a door.

"Second: money. Two hundred thousand US, wired anywhere on earth, plus the finder's fee you were promised, paid in full by me rather than by Vuthy — who, incidentally, sold your location to me eleven days ago, so you may wish to recalibrate your remaining friendships."

Eleven days. Petra kept her face still. Somewhere behind her ribs, a door she'd left cracked open for Vuthy closed without ceremony.

"Third: the woman keeps her samples, her field logs, whatever survives of her degraded chain of custody. She publishes. I will not stop her. I cannot stop her — you've seen to that, and frankly, at this point, the story gets out with or without the physical samples, and a controlled scandal costs less than an uncontrolled one. The concession will take its lumps. We will remediate, publicly, expensively, on camera. The permits renew. Everyone eats."

"And what do I do?" Petra asked. "While all this magnanimity is happening."

"You go home," Serey said simply. "You call the family of the man who died in the Andes, and you tell them the truth you have never told them — that you warned them, that you begged them, that the system ate you for it. You tell them with a clean record behind you, so that this time it lands. You take the money and you pay your debt — the real debt, the one that isn't denominated in dollars. And you never see this river again." He paused, and for the first time his voice dropped some of its polish, went quieter, almost gentle. "You have spent nine years being punished for a decision that wasn't yours. I am offering to un-punish you. That is not corruption, Ms. Lockridge. Corruption is when I ask you to lie. I'm not asking you to lie. I'm asking you to walk away from a fight that was never yours, in a country that has already decided what you're worth to it, carrying the only things you ever actually wanted."

* * *

The rain thickened. On the far bank, thunder rolled down the valley like furniture being dragged across an upper floor.

Petra became aware that she could feel her own heartbeat in her injured thigh, a slow hot throb keeping time. She thought about the hostel in the border town, the pawn ticket for her climbing rack, the way Vuthy had slid the photograph of the missing survey team across the table like a dealer laying down a card he already knew the value of. Desperation dressed up as opportunity. She'd taken the job because she needed the money. Every step since had been her telling herself the money mattered less than the truth, and every step had also been a step further from any life she could recognize.

And here was a man offering her the old life back. Polished. Certified. Stamped by the same systems that had crushed her, suddenly willing to un-crush her, because un-crushing her was cheaper than the alternative.

"That's quite an offer," she said.

"It is a serious one," Serey corrected. "Offers are what people make when they want something cheap. This is expensive. For me." He glanced past her, at Anong, and his tone stayed courteous but something underneath it cooled by several degrees. "For her, I should say, the arithmetic is different. Her village drank that water for six years. Nothing I offer her will make that true retroactively. I understand why she fights. I even respect it." His eyes came back to Petra. "But you — you met her five weeks ago. Whatever this is, it is real, I don't doubt it, but it is five weeks old. Your grief is nine years old. I am merely asking which debt you intend to pay."

There it was. The knife inside the silk, and he'd known exactly where to put it, because he'd read the file, all of it, and files tell you where people bleed.

Petra looked at him for a long moment. Then she turned and looked at Anong.

Anong stood in the rain with the sample case clutched to her chest, soaked through, exhausted, a woman who had run from her own government and her own conscience and finally stopped running in the same week. She wasn't begging Petra with her eyes. That was the thing Petra noticed most, the thing that decided something in her before her mouth opened. Anong was watching her the way you watch a verdict — ready for either one, braced for acquittal, braced for conviction, unwilling to influence the court.

Five weeks old. Yes. And in those five weeks Petra had watched her bail a leaking boat until her arms gave out and then keep bailing. Had watched her hold a stranger's head above floodwater in a karst cave for three days. Had heard her say, on a sandbar at midnight, *I did the math and the math said the data lives and they die, and I have to live with being the kind of person who agrees with that math,* and meant it, and hated it, and kept walking anyway.

You didn't get nine years of that. You got five weeks of it, and it weighed more.

"Here's my problem with your offer," Petra said, turning back to Serey. "It's good. It's genuinely good. It's the best thing anyone has offered me since I was twenty-six years old, and I believe you can deliver most of it, which makes you more honest than half the fixers I've worked for." She took a breath. "But you said something a minute ago that gives you away. You said the system doesn't punish people for being wrong. It punishes people for making the paperwork embarrassing. And then you offered to clean my paperwork." She shook her head slowly. "You're not offering to un-punish me. You're offering to prove yourself right. To show me the whole machine runs on exactly what you say it runs on — price tags — and that I was a fool ever to think otherwise. If I take it, you win even when you lose. Every blacklist you get vacated becomes a receipt. Proof that everything's for sale, including me."

"Everything is for sale," Serey said. No anger. Almost kindness. "I have bought governments, Ms. Lockridge. I am offering you a discount."

"Yeah." Petra looked at the gray inflatable, at the two patient men, at the rain coming down harder now, veils of it marching up the valley. "See, that's the thing about discounts."

She shifted her weight off the bad leg, and her hand found the hatchet lashed inside the gunwale of her longboat — casually, resting, the way a tired woman leans.

"They only look like bargains to people who haven't read the full invoice."

Serey studied her for a moment, and then, incredibly, he sighed — a small, genuine sound, almost weary, the sigh of a man who had genuinely hoped to avoid expense.

"I was afraid of that," he said. "For what it's worth, I think you and I would have gotten along, in another life."

"In another life," Petra said, "you'd have been somebody worth knowing."

He clicked his tongue softly, once, and the two men in the inflatable began to rise.

* * *

Later — much later, hours later, when the gravel bar was behind them and the dark and the rain had swallowed everything — Petra would replay the next ninety seconds and count how many separate pieces of luck had to interlock for her and Anong to be standing in a drainage gully above the bar instead of lying on it. The lightning strike on the far ridge, close enough to turn the world white and freeze every man on that beach for one full heartbeat. The swollen tributary choosing that exact moment to dump a slurry of floodwater and debris into the main channel, shoving the inflatable sideways against its mooring. Anong, God bless her, hurling the empty fuel drum off the longboat's stern line into the current so that it came wallowing out of the dark toward Serey's position like something surfacing.

They didn't fight. Fighting was losing. They ran — up the gully, into the treeline, along the ridge Petra's father had noted in faded pencil forty years ago, *high path, west side, safe in flood* — while shouts bloomed behind them and flashlights stabbed the rain and the river rose beneath it all, indifferent, carrying everything downstream toward the capital: bodies, boats, bargains, and one plastic case full of water that remembered everything.

By full dark they had put six kilometers between themselves and the bar, and Petra's leg had stopped negotiating and started dictating terms, and they collapsed in the lee of a fallen karst slab to wait out the worst of the rain.

Anong passed her the last of the dried fish without a word. Petra ate it. After a while Anong said, "He offered to wipe your record."

"He offered."

"And?"

Petra leaned her head back against the wet stone and watched the lightning walk the ridgelines, counting seconds between flash and thunder, measuring how close the storm was, how much time the river would give them.

"He asked which debt I intended to pay," she said. "Nine-year-old or five-week-old."

Anong was quiet. Then: "What did you tell him?"

"Nothing yet." Petra pushed herself upright, leg screaming, and looked downriver, toward the capital, toward the lab, toward twenty-nine days on a clock that hadn't stopped for storms or offers or the wrecked boat of a polite man who bought governments at retail. "But I'm going to enjoy telling him."

Below them, the flood roared on, rising.
