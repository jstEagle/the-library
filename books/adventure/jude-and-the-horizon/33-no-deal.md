## 33. No Deal

The rain had stopped an hour before dawn, and in its absence the jungle made a different kind of noise — dripping, settling, the river chewing at the bank a few meters below. Petra stood with her back against a strangler fig the width of a bus, the compass in her left hand and Serey's pistol in her right, and watched the most reasonable man she'd ever met try to buy her soul for the third time.

He was sitting where she'd told him to sit, on a fallen log, hands visible, ankles crossed. His shirt was ruined — river water, mud, one long tear where a branch had caught him during the chase downstream — but he wore the damage like it was a tailored jacket. That was the thing about Serey. He could be drowning in a flooded karst cave and he'd still look like the only adult in the room.

"Your record," he said, "is not as unfixable as you think."

"You've said that."

"And you haven't asked me how."

Petra shifted her weight off her bad ankle. The fall from the rapids two days ago had left it swollen to twice its size inside her boot, and every hour of walking since had been a negotiation between pride and cartilage. "Because I already know how. A phone call to Lima. A phone call to La Paz. A quiet word with the right minister here. You people built my blacklist the same way you built everything else — out of paperwork and favors." She turned the compass over in her hand. Her father's compass, brass worn to shine at the hinge. "You don't erase records, Serey. You just change who wrote them."

Something flickered across his face — not anger. Respect, maybe, or the performance of it. With Serey it was impossible to tell, and that was the whole point of him.

"The family," he said. "The client who died in the Andes. The Reyes woman — no, Lockridge, forgive me, the *Lockridge* debt." He pronounced her name carefully, like a man handling evidence. "I've seen the file. It's substantial. Interest alone is eating you alive. Walk away from this river, hand me the case, and by Friday your ledger is zero. Not forgiven. Zeroed. There's a difference, and I suspect you're enough of an accountant to appreciate it."

Behind her, upstream along the bank, Petra heard the small sounds Anong was making — deliberately, so Petra would know where she was. A boot on wet stone. The click of a sample case latch being checked. Anong had spent thirty years making water tell the truth, and she moved through the world now like someone who'd learned that silence is what liars use.

"She's there," Serey said quietly.

"She's always there."

"Then let me say this part to both of you." He didn't raise his voice. He never raised his voice; that was how you knew when he meant something. "The consortium is finished. You know this. The permits expire in nine days, the journalist has the data, and no amount of money I spend tonight will stop what happens in the capital next week. I'm not asking you to save us. I'm asking you to save yourselves the trouble of what comes after."

"What comes after," Petra repeated.

"Inquiries have witnesses. Witnesses have names. And names—" he spread his hands, an elegant little shrug, "—names end up in rooms like this one, with people like me, being offered things. Or they end up in rivers. I'd very much prefer the first option, for everyone involved. Including me. I'm tired, Ms. Lockridge. I've been tired for eleven years. Take the money. Take the passport. Take Anong and go somewhere the word 'blacklist' doesn't follow you, and let the machine grind forward without either of you inside it."

The river said nothing. The jungle dripped.

And Petra found, to her surprise, that she was actually considering it.

Not the money. She'd stopped hearing the money somewhere around the burned village, around the sound of Anong's brother screaming under a beam, around the arithmetic of what her choices cost other people. What she was considering was the exit. The clean door. Nine days from now the concession would collapse and the story would belong to journalists and lawyers and men in air-conditioned rooms, and nobody would need a mountain guide with a ruined reputation anymore. She could take the zeroed ledger. She could call the Lockridges — the family of the man who died roped to her in the Cordillera Huayhuash — and tell them it was over, the debt was paid, please stop hating me. Eleven years of carrying that weight, and Serey was offering to lift it with a signature.

That was the dangerous thing about him. Not the gun she'd taken off his man at the junction. Not the boats behind them, wrecked now, scattered downriver. The dangerous thing was that he understood exactly which wound to press.

"You're doing it right now," Anong said.

Petra hadn't heard her come up. Anong stepped out of the green shadow with the sample case in one hand and her field logs wrapped in plastic sheeting under her arm, and she looked at Serey the way she looked at contaminated water — like something that needed testing before anyone drank it.

"Doing what?" Serey asked pleasantly.

"Giving her permission to want it." Anong came to stand beside Petra, close enough that their shoulders touched. "You did it at the village too. You offered her triple Vuthy's fee and then you offered her forgiveness, because you'd read her file and you knew the fee was for her head and the forgiveness was for her heart. You're very good." She set the case down in the mud between them. "But you made one mistake."

"Only one?"

"You assumed we're negotiating." Anong crouched, opened the case, and lifted out the first rack of vials — the surviving samples, chain-of-custody tags intact, the ones that hadn't cooked in the heat. Water from the tailings seep. Water from the village well. Water from the aquifer under the processing plant, dated three years running. "This isn't a transaction. This is testimony. The difference is that testimony doesn't care what you offer."

Serey looked at the vials for a long moment. Then he looked at Petra, and his expression changed — the pleasantness draining out of it, leaving something older and plainer underneath. Not fear. Recognition. The look of a man watching a door close that he'd genuinely believed was open.

"Ms. Lockridge," he said. "Last time. The ledger. The passport. Your record wiped in all three countries. All you have to do is nothing. Walk away. Let the case find its way to a lab that loses it, an inquiry that misplaces it, a journalist who sits on it. You don't even have to help us. Just stop helping her." His eyes flicked to Anong. "She's the one who ran. She left six people on that mountain road and took the data. Ask yourself why a woman like that deserves your loyalty more than your own name does."

The words landed exactly where he aimed them, because they were true, or true enough. Anong had run. She'd told Petra about it on the sandbar in the dark, bailing water, hating herself for the arithmetic — six lives weighed against a box of vials, and she'd chosen the vials, and she'd been choosing them again every day since. Petra had carried her own version of that arithmetic for eleven years. Two women, two ledgers, standing in the mud while a professional offered to balance the books.

Petra looked at the pistol in her hand.

It was a compact thing, black polymer, barely used. Serey's security chief had carried it at the river junction, and when the flood took the man sideways into the current Petra had caught the gun out of the air like it was a rope thrown to her, and she'd held it ever since without quite deciding what it was for. Through the compound. Through the vault fire. Through the falls, where Serey's lead boat folded itself against the rocks and spat him out alive into the shallows, coughing, alone, his escort dead or fled or drowned.

Now he sat on a log in the grey light with no boat, no men, no leverage except the truth about her, and Petra understood — with the cold clarity that sometimes arrived after enough fear, the way exhaustion arrives after enough climbing — that she could end it here.

One shot wouldn't even be murder, not really. It would be self-defense, defense of Anong, defense of the children in Anong's village whose blood was in those vials. Every inquiry in the world would nod and write *justified* and move on. The consortium's fixer, found dead by his own wrecked boat, armed, having pursued two women through forty days of monsoon. Nobody would ask hard questions. Hard questions cost money, and the consortium was about to be very poor.

Her father's compass sat heavy in her other hand. Under its liner, folded into quarters, were his route notes — the falls section, the karst channels, the river nobody had mapped because he'd mapped it alone in 1979 and never told a soul until she found the paper two weeks ago. He'd worked these rivers for a mining company too, once. She'd never learned whether he knew what they were doing to the water. She'd never get to ask. But she'd thought about it every day since, and she'd decided it didn't matter, because what mattered was what she did with what he'd left her: a way through the falls, and a tool that pointed north no matter how lost you were.

A tool. Not a weapon. He'd been a guide, not a soldier. The whole inheritance was the difference.

"Here's my counteroffer," Petra said.

Serey waited.

"I keep the case. I keep the logs. I testify — to everything, including the parts that make me look bad, including the fact that I took contract money from Vuthy to find a team I now know was murdered. I'll stand up in whatever room they build and say my name last, after yours, after the directors', after the ministers who signed the lease extensions. And you—" she raised the pistol, and saw him go very still, and lowered it again, and dropped it in the mud between them, "—you walk. Pick up your gun if you want it. Swim back downriver. Call whoever you have left. Do your worst, or do nothing. I'm not going to decide for you."

For a moment nobody moved. The river chewed the bank. Somewhere overhead a hornbill laughed its ugly laugh and flapped away through the canopy.

"You're letting me go," Serey said. It wasn't gratitude in his voice. It was confusion, genuine and almost childlike, a man encountering a rule he'd never seen enforced.

"I'm letting you go because if I pull that trigger, everything Anong bled for becomes a story about a killer guide instead of a story about poisoned water. They'd love that. You'd love that. The headline writes itself and the vials become footnotes." Petra crouched, picked up the sample case, and stood, her ankle screaming, her face giving away none of it. "I spent eleven years being the person in the wrong story. I'm done handing people the pen."

Anong was looking at her. Not at Serey — at her, with an expression Petra had seen maybe twice in forty days and never directed at her: open, unguarded, the face of a woman who had spent a lifetime measuring water and had just measured something else entirely.

Serey stood slowly. He bent, retrieved his pistol from the mud, examined it, and slid it into his waistband with the unhurried precision of a man reassembling himself. Then he straightened his ruined shirt, and for a moment the mask came back — the polite fixer, the reasonable man — and he almost smiled.

"There will be an inquiry," he said. "In nine days, perhaps sooner. They'll want the team leader's death explained. They'll want the fire at the vault explained. They'll want to know why a survey team vanished and who profited." He paused. "My name will come up. Names always come up. And when it does, I'll have lawyers, and ministers, and eleven years of paper saying I was never anywhere near any of it. You'll have a box of water and a hydrologist who abandoned her colleagues." He tilted his head. "Are you certain you want to bet on the box of water?"

"It's not a bet," Anong said. "It's a measurement. Measurements don't lose. They just wait."

Serey studied her, then Petra, then the grey river sliding past, and something in him seemed to finally accept the shape of the morning. He gave a small bow — ironic, formal, almost courteous — and turned upriver, away from the wreck of his convoy, toward whatever extraction he had arranged and whatever future he'd bought himself. Ten steps into the treeline he stopped, without turning around.

"For what it's worth," he said, "the offer was real. All three times. I don't make offers I can't honor. It's the only professional standard I've kept." A beat. "You should know that the team leader's widow lives in the capital. Fourth district. She's been writing letters for two years. No one answers them." He started walking again. "Someone should answer them."

Then the green swallowed him, and he was gone.

* * *

They stood in the dripping light for a long time after, neither of them moving, as if the jungle needed to confirm he was really gone before it resumed.

"He'll testify against us," Anong said finally. "Or he'll vanish. One of the two."

"He'll vanish," Petra said. "Men like him are never where the fire is. But he gave us the widow, and he didn't have to. That's the closest thing to a conscience I've seen from that side of the river." She looked down at the sample case, mud-flecked, dented, holding the entire reason any of them were still breathing. "Nine days."

"Eight and a half. The lab closes for the holiday."

"Of course it does." Petra laughed — a short, cracked sound, the first laugh in days, surprised out of her. "Everything in this country closes for the holiday. Including justice."

"Not including us." Anong picked up her field logs and slung them across her back, then did something she had never done: she reached out and took Petra's hand, the one holding the compass, and closed her fingers over it. Her palm was rough from bailing rope and river work, and warm, and steady. "Forty days ago I watched you pawn your gear in a border hostel and I thought, there's a woman selling pieces of herself to pay for a mistake. I wasn't wrong. But I was wrong about which pieces mattered." She squeezed. "You had a loaded gun and a perfect excuse and you put it in the mud. Do you understand what that was worth? In a country where everyone with power shoots? Everyone with power shoots, Petra. And you didn't."

"I wanted to."

"I know. That's what makes it worth something. Courage isn't not wanting. Courage is wanting and putting it down anyway." She let go, and nodded upriver, toward the capital, toward the lab, toward the room where eight and a half days from now a box of water would meet a machine that couldn't lie. "Come on. The river drops by noon. We can make the ferry crossing at Ban Sok if your ankle agrees to lie to you about how much it hurts."

"My ankle and I have an arrangement," Petra said. "It screams, I ignore it, and neither of us testifies against the other."

They started walking. Behind them the falls roared on, indifferent, the water going over the edge the way it had gone over for ten thousand years, carrying everything downstream eventually — silt, secrets, the wreckage of Serey's empire, the diluted ghosts of whatever the mine had poured into the watershed. Ahead the trail climbed away from the bank into bamboo and birdsong, and Petra limped up it with a box of poisoned water in one hand and her father's compass in the other, broke, blacklisted twice and soon to be thrice, and lighter than she had felt in eleven years.

At the top of the rise she stopped and looked back once. The river lay grey and enormous below, and far downstream, barely visible, a single boat was moving toward the capital — a fishing boat, local, slow, flying no flag.

"That's not ours," Anong said.

"No," Petra agreed. "But it's heading where we're heading, and it's got a radio." She turned north, checked the compass out of habit though the trail was obvious, and smiled despite everything. "Let's see if we can beat the news downstream."
