## 34. Thirty-Eight Days Later

The capital smelled like every capital Petra had ever washed up in: diesel, wet concrete, and someone frying something too good to trust. She'd been there nine days and had learned the rhythm of the guesthouse on Sothearos Boulevard — the generator that died at midnight, the monk who crossed the courtyard at dawn, the way the ceiling fan clicked on its third rotation like a metronome counting down something.

Thirty-eight days since she'd stood in a flooded karst cave with a sample case strapped to her chest and decided, for the second time in her life, not to sell.

Anong was across town at the Hotel Royal, in a room paid for by a woman named Marguerite Osei, who wrote for an outlet whose name made ministry spokesmen sweat. Anong had moved there four days ago because Marguerite needed her close — "for verification," Marguerite said, which everyone understood to mean *so they can't get to you before we publish*. Petra hadn't argued. Separation was tactics now. They were past the part of the story where the two of them huddled over one candle bailing a boat; this was the part where you became pieces on other people's boards and hoped the board held.

She still hated it.

The guesthouse phone rang at six in the evening, which meant nothing good. Phones in this country rang for bad news or money, and Petra had run out of the second.

"Petra." Anong's voice, low, fast. "It's moving. Marguerite's editor cleared it. Full package — the contamination series, the chain of custody, my field logs, the survey team's names. It goes out tonight, their time. Midnight here."

Petra sat down on the bed, which was the only furniture besides a chair missing a leg propped against the wall. "The permits expire when?"

"Day after tomorrow. The concession lapses at midnight on the thirty-eighth day." A breath. "Marguerite timed it deliberately. She wanted the story to land while the lease was still alive — so the government couldn't say it was moot."

"Will they renew anyway?"

"They'll try. That's why we're calling everyone tonight." Papers rustled on Anong's end. Petra could picture her exactly: sitting cross-legged on a hotel bed she thought was obscene, hair tied back, reading glasses she pretended she didn't need, surrounded by photocopies in neat stacks because Anong's chaos was always organized chaos. "I have a list. Ministries, the provincial governor, two opposition MPs, the water commission, the donor agencies. Everyone who ever took our samples seriously enough to file them somewhere."

"And Serey?"

A pause long enough to have a shape.

"Serey resigned Tuesday," Anong said. "'For personal reasons.' The consortium announced an internal review. Which means the review will find nothing, and Serey will surface somewhere else in six months doing the same work under a different flag. Men like him don't fall. They rotate."

"But he's gone from the board."

"He's gone from the board."

Petra looked at the window. Rain again — the monsoon's last stubborn weeks, the sky wrung out but not finished. Somewhere downriver, in the ranges, the survey team's camp was dissolving back into the jungle, tents rotting, the place where five men had been made to disappear quietly forgetting them. Except it wasn't quiet anymore. Tonight their names were going out on wires to forty countries.

"Anong," Petra said. "The team leader's name. Is it in the piece?"

"First line of the third section. With the others. All five." Another pause. "And yours is in it too. As the guide who found the camp and identified the staging. Marguerite wouldn't cut it. I didn't ask her to."

"I know." And she did know, and some traitorous part of her had even asked for it, back when asking felt like courage instead of self-destruction. "Fine. Fine. Call your list. I'll call mine."

"You have a list?"

"I have Vuthy's old contacts and a grudge." She almost smiled. "It's worked before."

* * *

It did not go smoothly, because nothing in this country went smoothly, and that was almost comforting.

Petra spent the evening in the guesthouse courtyard with a borrowed phone, working through numbers Anong had given her and a few she'd pried out of a fixer named Rath who owed Vuthy money and therefore owed Petra fear. Most calls went nowhere. A deputy minister's aide listened for ninety seconds and said, with genuine warmth, that this sounded like a matter for the proper authorities. A water commission official hung up on her twice and answered the third time out of curiosity, then stayed on the line for twenty minutes asking questions so precise that Petra understood he'd already suspected everything and had just been waiting for permission.

At 11:40, she called the number Anong had flagged as most important: a senior official in the Ministry of Mines, the man who signed the concession renewals. He picked up on the fourth ring.

"This is about the Khao Sen concession," Petra said. "In thirty-six hours your signature either renews a lease sitting on poisoned aquifers, or it doesn't. In about twenty minutes, an international wire service publishes hydrological data proving the poisoning, plus testimony from the surveyor your government reported missing. If you renew tonight, before the story breaks, you own it. Every sick kid in Anong's village becomes your signature. If you wait — if you let the permit lapse while the review happens — you're the minister who paused. That's survivable. Ministers survive pauses."

There was a silence on the line with weight in it, the silence of a man doing arithmetic.

"Who is this?" he said finally.

"The person who found your survey team," Petra said. "Or what was left of where they stopped being found."

He hung up. She counted that as progress. Men who hang up are men who heard you.

At 12:06 by the guesthouse clock — which ran four minutes fast, so really 12:02 — her borrowed phone buzzed with a message from Marguerite Osei, a woman she'd met exactly once, in a hotel lobby where Marguerite had shaken her hand like a man closing a deal:

*Filed. Embargo lifts 04:00 GMT. Hold tight.*

Petra put the phone face-down on the table and sat in the dark courtyard listening to the rain start up again, and realized her hands were shaking. Not from fear. From the specific tremor of a thing leaving your control forever. For thirty-eight days the truth had been a physical object — a case, a boat, a burden she could carry or trade. Now it was becoming weather. It would fall on everyone equally, including her, and no amount of competence on a river would steer it.

She thought about Vuthy's finder's fee. Thirty thousand dollars, wired on delivery of the survey team's location, enough to clear the debt to the Reyes family, enough to walk back into the world with her hands empty and clean. She'd lost it somewhere around day nineteen, when Vuthy went silent and she understood the ledger she'd later find in his office had her name in it from the first page. The money had never been real. But standing in the courtyard, watching the story go live without her able to touch it, she understood the debt wasn't going anywhere either. Truth didn't pay. Truth only cost, and you paid it forward, in installments, forever.

She went inside and slept three hours, which was more than she expected.

* * *

The story broke at eleven in the morning local time, and by noon the guesthouse owner was knocking on her door with his own phone held out like an offering.

"You are the guide," he said. Not a question. His screen showed a photograph of Petra taken outside the Ministry of Water Resources two days ago, grainy, looking over her shoulder like a woman who knew she was being photographed because she was. Below it, a headline in English and Khmer both:

*THE RIVER THAT WAS BOUGHT: INSIDE THE KHAO SEN CONCESSION AND THE SURVEY TEAM THAT VANISHED*

"It is everywhere," the owner said, and his face was hard to read — pride and alarm fighting for the same ground. "My cousin works at the port authority. Everyone is reading it. Everyone is angry." He hesitated. "Also, there is a car outside since one hour. Two men. They are not reading."

"Are they police?"

"No."

"Then let them read," Petra said, and took her pack down from the wardrobe, because a woman who'd survived thirty-eight days of being hunted didn't stop being hunted because the news got good. Good news was often the most dangerous hour. Desperate men do desperate things at the moment of losing, not the moment of defeat.

She went out the back, through the kitchen, past the cook who didn't look up, and into the alley, and walked eight blocks before letting herself check behind her. The car didn't follow. Maybe it wasn't hers. Maybe it was Anong's. Maybe it belonged to a government that had decided arrest looked worse than silence. You couldn't know, and knowing was a luxury the story had taken from her along with everything else.

She reached the Hotel Royal at one o'clock. Marguerite Osei met her in the lobby — a tall Ghanaian-British woman in her fifties with reading glasses pushed up into gray-threaded hair and the unhurried manner of someone who had been sued, detained, and deported often enough to find all three boring.

"Miss Lockridge." She shook hands. "You look terrible."

"Thank you. I've been working on it."

"Come upstairs. Your colleague won't sleep until she sees you, and I need thirty minutes of your time for the follow-up."

Upstairs, room 412 was a war office: laptops, cables, printouts fanned across both beds, a cold teapot, and Anong sitting in the middle of it looking like she hadn't slept in a week and had never been more awake. She stood when Petra came in, and for a second neither of them did anything, because there are things you can't do in front of journalists, and then Anong hugged her anyway, hard, brief, smelling of hotel soap and photocopier toner.

"It's real," Anong said into her shoulder. "People are reading it. My brother called from the village. He said the whole district is talking. He said—" Her voice caught and steadied. "He said the children's mothers are saying our names."

"That's the whole job," Petra said. "That's all of it."

They sat on the floor because the beds were full of paper, and Marguerite walked them through the fallout with the brisk pleasure of a general reviewing a battle going to plan. The wire story had been picked up by nineteen outlets in the first hour. Donor agencies had sent queries to the Ministry of Mines within two. The opposition had scheduled an emergency session. The provincial governor — the one who'd spent a year insisting the water tests were foreign fabrications — had issued a statement calling for "full transparency," a phrase that in this country meant *I am rearranging my furniture*.

"And the concession?" Petra asked.

"Lapses tomorrow at midnight. Renewal requires the minister's signature." Marguerite allowed herself the smallest smile. "This morning the minister requested a 'comprehensive independent review' before any renewal decision. Translation: the lease dies on schedule, and no one signs a new one while the bodies are still warm. The consortium's parent company has already issued a statement expressing 'disappointment' and announcing a strategic withdrawal from the region pending review. Their stock dropped four percent. For a company like that, four percent is a scream."

"So it's done."

"It's done," Marguerite said. "The concession collapses at midnight tomorrow. What isn't done is everything after. There will be an inquiry — the government can't avoid it now, too many international eyes. There will be subpoenas, or whatever passes for them here. And there will be witnesses." She looked at Petra over her glasses, and her voice didn't change at all, which was how Petra knew the sentence mattered. "Witnesses who testify. On the record. Under their own names. Including anything they did themselves along the way. The inquiry will smell a cover-up in the initial missing-persons report — the staged camp, the false timeline. Someone guided that fiction into existence, Miss Lockridge. When they ask who, what will you say?"

Petra looked at Anong. Anong looked back, steady, giving her nothing, taking nothing — the same look from the sandbar at night, from the cave, from every moment where the honest answer was the expensive one.

"I'll say what happened," Petra said.

"Good." Marguerite closed her notebook. "Because they'll offer you deals not to. Money, immunity, a passport with a new name. Power always offers, right up until the moment it punishes. The ones who take the offer become the next cover-up. The ones who refuse become the record." She stood, gathered her papers. "Rest tonight. Tomorrow the permit dies, and the day after, the inquiry announces its hearings. You'll want to be ready."

She left. The door clicked. The room settled into the particular silence of two people who have run out of running.

Anong slid down the wall until she was sitting on the floor beside Petra, shoulder to shoulder, both of them looking at the ceiling fan turning its slow, clicking turns.

"My brother says the village wants to hold a ceremony," Anong said. "For the river. For the five men. He asked if we'd come." A pause. "After. After the inquiry. After whatever they do to us."

"After," Petra agreed.

"Do you regret it?" Anong asked. "Any of it? The money, the countries, the—" She gestured vaguely at the whole ruined architecture of Petra's professional life, which was, admittedly, most of it. "You could have taken Serey's offer. Clean passport. Triple fee. Gone home to your mountains."

Petra thought about it honestly, because Anong deserved honesty and because lying to herself had gotten expensive lately. "I regret the debt," she said. "I regret that the family I owe gets nothing because I chose the dead over the living. Some nights that math eats me. But the offer itself?" She shook her head slowly. "Serey's offer was a door out of my own life. Taking it would've meant the Andes killed that client for nothing, and the survey team died for nothing, and the kids in your village drink that water for another twenty years. I'd rather be broke and barred from everywhere than be the reason it keeps happening." She glanced sideways. "You?"

"I abandoned my team to save data I half-destroyed in the heat," Anong said. "I will carry that arithmetic until I die. But no. I don't regret it either. Isn't that strange? We're the two worst people to ask about whether this was worth it, and we're the only ones who actually know."

Outside, the rain had stopped. Through the window, the city went on making its ordinary noise — traffic, vendors, a loudspeaker somewhere playing music for a festival Petra hadn't known was coming. Downriver, past the port and the bends and the falls only she and her father had ever mapped, the Khao Sen ranges stood in their clouds, holding the rotted tents and the buried truths and the beginning of everything.

Her phone buzzed. An unknown number. She almost didn't answer. Then she did.

"Miss Lockridge." A formal voice, ministry cadence. "This is the office of the Special Inquiry Commission on the Khao Sen Concession. The Commission convenes Thursday. You are requested to appear as a witness. The summons will be delivered formally tomorrow." A pause, professionally neutral. "The Commission also wishes you to know that your cooperation regarding the circumstances of the original disappearance report will be considered... relevant to these proceedings."

The line went dead.

Anong was watching her. "Well?"

Petra set the phone down on the floor between them, next to the scattered pages of the story that had ended a concession and started a reckoning.

"Thursday," she said. "They're going to put me under oath and ask me to name names." She picked up the teapot, found it cold, poured anyway. "Including mine."
