## 35. Testimony

The inquiry room in the provincial capital had been a customs hall once, back when the river trade was honest enough to need one. Now it held a long table of officials, a stenographer with tired eyes, a ceiling fan turning slow as a paddle wheel, and Petra Lockridge standing at a witness lectern with her hands cuffed to nothing at all.

That was the strange part. She'd expected cuffs. She'd rehearsed for cuffs. Instead they'd given her water in a plastic cup and a chair with a wobble in it, and the senior officer — a woman named Colonel Suriyawong with a face like weathered teak — had said only: *Begin where you think it began.*

Petra had thought about that all night in the government guesthouse, lying on a mattress that smelled of mildew and floor polish, listening to the monsoon's last tantrums rattle the shutters. Where did it begin? At Vuthy's table in the border town, with his soft voice and his finder's fee? At the staged camp with its tents pitched neat and its radios dead? At the Andes, six years ago, with Daniel Mercer's rope coming off the anchor she'd trusted?

No. It began earlier than any of that. It began the first time she'd signed something she hadn't read closely enough, or looked away from something she should have looked straight at. A career is built out of small lookings-away. You just don't see the architecture until it falls on you.

She stood now, and the fan turned, and forty faces waited.

"My name is Petra Lockridge," she said. "I'm a mountain guide by trade. I was hired on the twenty-ninth of March by a fixer named Vuthy Chamroeun to locate a missing government survey team in the Khao Sen ranges. I want to be clear from the start about what I am here." She let that sit. "I'm not here as a hero. I'm here as a witness who spent three weeks being paid by the wrong side without knowing it, and then two more weeks deciding whether to keep taking their money."

The stenographer's fingers paused. Somewhere behind Petra, a door opened and closed — journalists, she assumed. Anong had made good on her promise; the data had gone to an international correspondent named Calloway two days before the permits expired, and the concession had collapsed like a bad karst formation. The story was everywhere now. Which meant everyone wanted to know what the guide knew, and how much of what she knew could be prosecuted.

"Start with the camp," Colonel Suriyawong said. "The staging."

"The camp was theater," Petra said. "Six tents pitched in a hollow that floods every wet season — no experienced surveyor camps there. Food cached but cook fire cold, ash scattered to look older than it was. Radios dead, batteries pulled, which you don't do if you're waiting for extraction. And not one footprint leaving. Not one. Ground like that holds tracks for days. Someone carried the team out, or walked them out on a surface they laid down themselves, and then swept it."

"And your professional conclusion at the time?"

"That it was staged." She heard her own voice go flat, the way it went when she read terrain. "I concluded it within an hour of arriving. And then—" Here it was. The part she'd rehearsed at four in the morning, staring at the ceiling. "And then I buried that conclusion for eleven days."

The fan turned. Nobody moved.

"I found a permit document in the team leader's kit," Petra continued. "Concession audit, expiry date thirty-eight days out. It told me the survey wasn't lost — it was inconvenient. Anyone with half my experience would have understood immediately that this was a disappearance arranged by people with money, and that continuing to search meant becoming a problem for those people. That's exactly what I understood. And I kept searching anyway — but quietly, carefully, in ways that wouldn't force me to report what I suspected until I had proof. I told myself that was professionalism. Evidence first, accusations later." Her mouth tasted like the old copper of the Andes. "It wasn't professionalism. It was me protecting my fee. Vuthy was paying per day located, and there was a completion bonus that would clear a debt I owed a dead man's family. Every day I didn't call it murder was a day I got paid to not call it murder."

"Did anyone instruct you to conceal your findings?"

"No. That's the thing I want on the record." She gripped the lectern edge. "Nobody had to. Vuthy never asked me to lie. He asked me to *wait*, and he priced the waiting so I'd agree to it. That's how it works — that's how it worked on me, and I've been in mountains my whole life and I still walked into it. You don't bribe a person like me. You give her a debt and a deadline and let her corrupt herself at market rate."

Colonel Suriyawong made a small note. "Name the names you know."

"Vuthy Chamroeun, broker. He didn't arrange the abduction himself — he sold the location of the survey team to consortium security, and he'd been selling access to government surveys for years. His ledger proves it; I recovered it and turned it over with my statement. Serey — one name, I never got another — field operative for the mining consortium, now under arrest, I understand. And the consortium itself: Khemara Extraction Group, whose lease expired four days ago and whose directors approved both the burial of the contamination data and the removal of the survey team. I can't prove the order came from the top. But Anong Srisuk can prove the data existed, and the ledger proves the mechanism, and somewhere between those two things are men in offices who never touched a rope or a boat and never had to."

"Anong Srisuk," the colonel said. "She was attached to the survey unofficially."

"She was the hydrologist. She carried the samples out before the team was taken. She's the reason any of this is on a record instead of at the bottom of a flooded pit." Petra paused. "I also want the record to show what she paid. She left her team because the team leader ordered her to — Chai Sonklin sent her downstream with the evidence because someone had to survive carrying it. She has spent months believing she abandoned them. She didn't. Chai's body was upstream, staged as a drowning, and he died before flood season, and everything he did in his last week was to get her and that sample case out alive. If this inquiry remembers one name kindly, make it his."

* * *

They broke at midday. Petra sat on the guesthouse steps eating rice from a street cart, watching the river run high and brown with the season's last rain, and Anong came and sat beside her without asking.

"You named yourself," Anong said. "In there. Eleven days of silence. You put it in the transcript."

"It belongs in the transcript."

"You could have framed it as caution. Diligence. Half the room would have believed you."

"Half the room would have believed me because half the room has done the same arithmetic." Petra pushed rice around the tin plate. "I spent six years telling myself the Mercer family's anger was about a mistake on a mountain. It wasn't. It was about the week after, when I wrote the incident report and left out the part where I'd second-guessed the anchor and gone up anyway because the client wanted the summit photo. I did the same thing here. Waited. Softened. Priced my silence without ever calling it that." She looked at Anong. "You did your version too. We all do versions. The only difference between us and Serey is scale, and I'm done pretending scale changes the shape."

Anong was quiet a while. Down at the landing, boatmen argued over mooring lines; the river took no notice of any of them.

"When I testified," Anong said finally, "they asked why I didn't go to the police in the provinces I passed through. Three provinces, Petra. I had the samples. I had Chai's field notes. Any constable's office would have taken it." She laughed, short and joyless. "I told them the truth. I didn't trust the police because Vuthy's kind own half of them, and I was right not to — but I also didn't trust them because going official meant the story stopped being mine, and if it stopped being mine then Chai died for a file number. So I sat on the truth in a hiding place for months, telling myself I was keeping it safe. Same crime. Smaller cage."

"So we're both convicts."

"We're both witnesses who arrived late." Anong bumped her shoulder against Petra's. "Late is better than never. Barely. But barely counts."

* * *

Afternoon session. The room had filled — more officials, a legal attaché from an embassy, and in the back row, unmistakable even in a borrowed shirt, Vuthy's lawyer, a silver-haired man who watched Petra the way she'd once watched a cornice: calculating the load, waiting for the crack.

They walked her through the ledger. Through the checkpoint forgery — yes, she admitted, she'd forged transit permits from survey templates; yes, she understood that was a crime; yes, she'd do it again, and she'd like the sentence for it noted alongside the sentence for whatever the consortium's lawyers were preparing. They walked her through the compound infiltration, the vault fire, the falls. She gave them all of it, clean and chronological, holding nothing back except the moments that belonged to other people — Anong's confession on the sandbar, the brother's burns, the children at the village well. Those weren't hers to spend.

Then, near the end, the silver-haired lawyer rose. "Ms. Lockridge. You've described accepting payment from Mr. Chamroeun across five weeks while harboring suspicions of criminal homicide. Is that accurate?"

"Yes."

"You did not withdraw. You did not report. You continued to draw fees."

"Yes."

"Isn't it true that you only came forward when the payment structure collapsed — when Mr. Chamroeun went silent and your bonus became unreachable?"

Petra felt the room lean in. This was the crack he'd been waiting for, and the worst part was that it was real. There was a version of events where she was exactly what he described: a mercenary who turned righteous only when the money dried up.

She let the silence run three full seconds. Then: "No. And I'll tell you how you can check. On day nineteen I contacted Vuthy to arrange extraction. He offered to buy the sample case from me outright — double my total fee, cash, no questions. I refused. That refusal cost me the bonus and started the clock on every bad thing that happened after. You have his ledger. Check the entry. He recorded the offer and my refusal, because he recorded everything — it was the habit that hung him." She looked at the lawyer steadily. "I stayed bought right up until staying bought required selling a dead man's evidence. Then I stopped. That's not righteousness. That's a line I found out I had. Put it in the transcript however you like — but put the ledger entry next to it."

The lawyer sat down slowly. In the back, someone began writing very fast.

* * *

The third day, they called Anong, and Petra waited outside on the steps and listened to the fan-driven murmur through the tall windows, and thought about Daniel Mercer's mother, who would be reading about all of this soon if she wasn't already. There was a phone call Petra still had to make. She'd been carrying it since the village burned, since the falls, since the moment she understood that surviving a thing done right doesn't cancel the things done wrong — it just gives you the standing to face them.

Inside, the colonel's voice rose briefly above the murmur: "—and the samples' chain of custody?" And Anong's answer, steady as a level line: "Never left my hands. Not once. Ask me anything about the numbers. I'll know them until I die."

Petra smiled at the river. Until I die. That was the whole job, really. Everything else was gear.

At dusk an aide came out and told her the inquiry would resume Monday, that charges against her were "under assessment," and that she was advised not to leave the province — advice that landed, given her history, somewhere between irony and prophecy. Two countries already had her name on a list. A third was drafting the letter now; she could feel it being typed somewhere, bureaucratic and permanent.

She stood on the steps a long time, watching the lights come on along the waterfront, and then she took out the cheap phone she'd bought in the border town a lifetime ago, and dialed a number in Colorado she knew better than her own heartbeat.

It rang twice. A woman's voice answered — older now, harder, a voice Petra had heard only once before, six years ago, through a bad connection, saying words that had followed her across three continents.

"This is Petra Lockridge," she said. "I owe you a truth I've been paying interest on for six years. Do you have time to hear all of it?"

The line went quiet — the particular quiet of a person deciding whether to hang up — and then, instead of the dial tone, Petra heard a chair scrape, and a door close, and the small sound of someone sitting down to stay.
