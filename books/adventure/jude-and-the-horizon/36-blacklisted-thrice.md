## 36. Blacklisted Thrice

The letter came on a Tuesday, which felt like an insult. Catastrophes should arrive on Fridays, Petra thought, so you could at least drink through the weekend before the accounting began.

It was waiting for her at the hostel desk in the capital — a plain envelope, no return address, the government seal embossed in gold that had probably cost more to stamp than the paper was worth. The clerk behind the desk handed it over with the particular blankness of someone who'd seen a hundred guests receive a hundred envelopes and had learned never to guess at their contents.

Petra took it up to her room because she didn't want to open it in front of anyone.

The room was on the top floor, under a tin roof that turned noon into an oven and rain into applause. Anong was out — she'd been out every day that week, shuttling between the journalist's rented office and the ministry annex where the inquiry was grinding through its fourth week of testimony. The concession had collapsed eleven days ago. Forty days had become thirty-eight, then twenty-two, then nothing at all; the permits expired on schedule, the consortium's lawyers filed for extensions nobody would grant now, and the mining equipment sat rusting at the river compound while men in suits argued about who would pay to haul it out.

They had won. That was the word people kept using. *Won.*

Petra opened the envelope standing up, the way you'd open something you expected to bite you.

*The Ministry of Interior, pursuant to Section 14 of the Immigration Act, hereby declares Petra Anne Lockridge persona non grata...*

She read it twice. The language was careful, bureaucratic, almost gentle. No mention of the inquiry. No mention of what she'd done or why. Just the finding: presence in the Kingdom deemed contrary to national interest. Departure required within fourteen days. Re-entry prohibited indefinitely.

Third country. Third flag on the list. Peru first, after the Andes. Chile six months later, when the family's lawyers finished their work and the paperwork caught up with the grief. Now this one, the one where she'd actually done something right, and it didn't matter. The ink dried the same either way.

She sat down on the bed, which was the only furniture besides a chair, and looked at the letter for a long time.

The strange thing — the thing she kept turning over like a stone in her pocket — was that she'd expected it. Had known, somewhere below the level of decision, from the moment she stood up in the hearing room and said her own name into the record. *I arrived at the camp on the ninth of March. I observed evidence of staging and did not report it for six days, during which I pursued a private contract instead.* She'd watched the man from the Ministry write that down, watched his pen pause, and understood that whatever else happened, whatever the data proved, whatever the newspapers said about courage, there would be a file with her name on it by the end of the month.

You don't get to burn your career down and keep the warmth.

She heard Anong on the stairs — that quick, two-at-a-time climb she did when she had news — and folded the letter once and put it in her jacket pocket, which was becoming an archive. Vuthy's contract was in there too, soft at the creases now. The finder's fee. Ninety thousand US dollars, wired on confirmation and recovery, terms she'd memorized in a border-town hostel while pawning a climbing rack to cover a debt she still owed.

Anong burst in without knocking, holding her phone like a torch.

"The lab confirmed the second batch. All of it. Chain of custody intact — they're publishing Thursday." She stopped, reading Petra's face the way she read river gauges, fast and without sentiment. "What is it."

"Immigration."

"Ah." Anong sat down on the chair across from her. She didn't ask which country. There was only one it could be. "Fourteen days?"

"Fourteen days."

"And the money?"

Petra almost laughed. Anong always went straight to the ledger; it was one of the things Petra had come to love about her, the way a hydrologist looks at a flood and sees arithmetic. "Vuthy's contract paid on delivery of the team. Alive or dead, recovered and identified. I delivered them. I identified them. I testified about how they died." She shrugged. "But I also testified that I sat on the staging for six days, and that my original report to his office misrepresented what I found. Breach of good faith. His lawyers sent a notice yesterday. Contract voided. No fee."

"No fee," Anong repeated flatly.

"No fee."

"So you found the bodies, broke the conspiracy, ended the lease, put three men in custody pending trial—" Anong counted them off on her fingers, an inventory, "—and you will leave this country with less than you came with."

"I'll leave it with you," Petra said, and immediately wished she hadn't, because it sounded like a line, and because Anong's face did something complicated — a flicker of pleasure quickly buried under the practical frown she wore like armor.

"That's not money."

"No. It isn't."

"It doesn't pay the family in Colorado."

The name landed the way it always landed, square in the sternum. Daniel Marsh. Fifty-one years old, father of two, client on a Cordillera Blanca traverse who fell four hundred meters because his guide made a judgment call in whiteout conditions that every review board since had called defensible and that Petra, at three in the morning, in every hotel room since, still called fatal. The debt wasn't legal anymore — the settlement had been structured, insured, partially discharged. What remained was the part the courts couldn't touch. Two hundred and sixty thousand dollars, roughly, once you counted the interest Vuthy had been kind enough to calculate monthly. The number had been her north star for four years. Every contract, every borderline job, every season she took work she shouldn't have taken because the money was good and the clients were desperate and desperate clients didn't ask about blacklists — all of it pointed at that number.

And now she had testified herself out of the last chance to earn it honestly.

"They'll understand," Anong said, and then, because she was honest even when kindness would have been easier: "No. They won't. Why would they? Their father is dead and the woman who was roped to him is famous for the wrong reasons in three countries." She leaned forward, elbows on knees. "What will you tell them?"

"I don't know yet."

"You know exactly what you'll tell them. You've known since the vault burned. You just haven't picked up the phone."

Petra looked at her. Four months ago she'd held a knife on this woman in a looted survey camp, both of them soaked, both of them lying, and now Anong sat in a hot room above a tin roof telling her the truth about herself with the same precision she used on contamination data. It was unbearable. It was the best thing in her life.

"There's no version of that call where they're glad I called," Petra said.

"No. But there's a version where they believe you. Those aren't the same thing, and you keep pretending they are."

* * *

She spent the afternoon doing what she always did when the ground moved: inventory.

It was a guide's habit, drilled in from the first season — when everything goes wrong, count what you have. So she laid it out on the bed like gear before a push, item by item, because if she didn't make it physical she'd drown in it.

One passport, valid, but a document increasingly useful as a coaster. One father's compass, brass case scarred, the route notes folded inside the liner — notes that had matched the survey's secret sampling sites, notes that meant her father had run these rivers decades before she was born, a fact she still hadn't finished digesting. One duffel of clothes. One set of route notes in her own hand covering four hundred kilometers of river she would now never legally guide again. One bank balance that would cover a plane ticket and perhaps six weeks of living cheaply anywhere that wasn't Peru, Chile, or here.

Minus the debt. Always minus the debt. The debt sat underneath everything like bedrock.

Against that: the consortium in receivership. Three arrests, with Serey's name on the charging documents — though he'd been released on bail within seventy-two hours, because men like Serey were always released on bail, and Petra had stopped expecting otherwise. A collapsed concession worth, by the newspaper's estimate, nine hundred million dollars in future extraction that would not happen. A village upstream with clean water coming, filtration funded by an emergency grant Anong had shamed out of two NGOs in a single week. Children who would grow up drinking water that didn't poison them slowly, whose blood lead levels would fall year over year on some chart some researcher would publish, and none of whom would ever know her name.

That was the asset column. She stared at it a long time.

The trouble with an accounting like this — the trouble nobody warned you about — was that the columns didn't reconcile. Nine hundred million against two hundred sixty thousand. Clean water for a village against a debt to a widow. There was no exchange rate. You couldn't convert a child's unpoisoned bloodstream into dollars and wire it to Denver. The ledgers ran on different currencies and there was no bureau anywhere that traded between them, and every night for the rest of her life some part of her would stand at that counter trying to make the conversion anyway, and fail, and try again.

Vuthy had understood this about her, she realized. That was the real architecture of him. He'd built the whole machine — the finder's fee, the finder's desperation, the debt as bait — knowing that a woman like Petra Lockridge would take the contract precisely because she needed the money, and would break the contract precisely because she couldn't live with herself, and would end up holding neither the cash nor the absolution. He'd bet on her conscience both ways. It was the only wager he'd ever placed that paid out regardless of the result.

She wondered, idly, whether he was in custody. The papers said he'd left the country before the inquiry began. Of course he had. Men like Vuthy were never in the room when the room burned; they owned the matches and sold them wholesale.

* * *

Evening brought rain, the tail end of the monsoon running late and stubborn, hammering the tin roof like it had a grudge. Anong came back wet and triumphant — the journalist had secured the lab reports' publication, plus a commitment from a parliamentary committee to open a broader review of concession oversight — and found Petra sitting in the dark without the light on, watching the street below through the one window.

"You're doing the inventory," Anong said. Not a question. She'd learned Petra's silences the way she'd learned the river's, by immersion.

"Did the math."

"What's the total?"

"Depends which currency."

Anong toweled her hair and sat on the bed beside the neat rows of imagined gear, and for a while neither of them spoke, and the rain filled the silence the way water fills a channel — completely, without effort.

"My brother asked about you today," Anong said finally. "He wants to know if you'll come to the village before you fly out. The elders want to do something formal. I told them you'd hate it."

"I'd hate it."

"He also wants to know if you're coming back. After. When the blacklist is old news. These things soften. Ministers change. In ten years, maybe—"

"In ten years I'll be fifty-three."

"Fifty-one," Anong said, "and you'll have spent ten years being fifty-one somewhere else, which is the same everywhere." She turned to look at her. "Or you spend ten years being fifty-one somewhere specific. Somewhere with a river you know better than anyone alive. Somewhere with a woman who knows where the gauges are buried." A pause, and then, quieter, the armor off: "I'm asking, Petra. Badly. But I'm asking."

Petra looked at the window, at the rain coming down silver in the streetlight, at the town where she was now officially contrary to the national interest.

"I can't come back," she said. "Not for years, maybe ever. If I'm honest about the accounting — and I'm trying, God help me, to be honest about the accounting — I have to include that. This country is closed to me. The money's gone. The debt's still sitting there like a mountain I haven't climbed, and the only currency I have left that's worth anything is the truth, and I've been hoarding it." She pulled the envelope from her jacket and set it on the bed between them, the gold seal catching the streetlight. "There's one call I haven't made. Four years I haven't made it. And I keep thinking I'll make it when I have something to offer — when the debt's paid, when the record's clean, when I walk in with receipts. But the receipts are gone. There's never going to be a version of this where I arrive with receipts."

Anong said nothing. She'd learned that too — when to let the river run.

"So I'm going to make the call tomorrow," Petra said. "From here, before the fourteen days run out. And I'm going to tell them all of it. The rescue, the mistake, the debt, the contract, the six days I stayed silent, the bodies, the vault, all of it. Everything except the parts that belong to other people." Her voice held steady, mostly. "And then whatever they say — forgiveness, hatred, nothing at all — I'll hang up and I'll still owe them, and I'll owe them for the rest of my life, and that's just the truth of the ledger. Some debts don't clear. They just change what they're made of."

The rain hammered on. Down in the street, a motorbike hissed past through the flooded gutter, its taillight smearing red across the water.

Anong reached over and took the immigration letter, read it again even though she knew what it said, and set it down with the finality of a gauge reading.

"Thursday," she said. "The data publishes Thursday. Your flight's Friday. That gives you one day."

"One day," Petra agreed.

"And after the call?"

Petra looked at the compass on the sill, brass dull in the half-light, and at the folded route notes inside it — her father's handwriting, these rivers, this whole long inheritance she was only beginning to understand. Somewhere downstream, past the falls only two people had ever mapped, the horizon was doing what horizons do.

"After the call," she said, "I think I finally find out what I'm worth when I'm not buying anything."

Her phone sat on the table, dark and patient, holding a number she'd carried for four years without dialing. Tomorrow, she thought. Tomorrow she'd spend the last coin she had.

Outside, the rain began, very slowly, to stop.
