## 37. The Family

The hotel in the provincial capital had a business center on the second floor, a glass-walled room with three computers and a phone booth that smelled of other people's cigarettes. Petra stood outside it for twenty minutes, watching rain sheet down the windows, before she went in.

She had rehearsed this call for six years. In the Andes she'd rehearsed it on the walk down from base camp, in the police station in Huaraz, in every hostel and border queue and sleepless night since. The rehearsal always ended the same way: *I'm sorry. It wasn't your fault. The mountain took him.* A clean sentence, a closed door.

The problem was that it was a lie, and lies had a way of compounding interest. She'd learned that over forty days of monsoon, over a leaking boat and a burned village and a sample case worth more than her life. Lies were how men like Serey built their compounds. Lies were what Vuthy's ledger was made of, page after page of them, each one small enough to deny alone.

She had testified yesterday. Nine hours in a room with no windows, an interpreter, a stenographer, and a lawyer for the consortium who looked at her like she was something he'd scraped off his boot. She had named names — Serey, Vuthy, the two ministers whose signatures sat on permits that should never have been issued. And at the end, when the presiding officer asked if there was anything else, she had said yes, and told them about the staging at the last camp. How she'd known within an hour that the disappearance was manufactured, and how she'd said nothing for eleven days because eleven days was the difference between collecting Vuthy's fee and not.

Her own role. Her own silence, priced out in dollars.

It hadn't bought her anything except the truth of what she was. But sitting here now, with a phone card in her hand and the family's number written on the back of her father's route notes, she understood that the testimony had only been half the debt. There was another half, and it lived in Denver, Colorado, and its name was Margaret Hale.

* * *

Marcus Hale had been thirty-one years old. An actuary from Denver who'd trained for eighteen months to climb Illimani and hired the best guide his money could buy, which turned out to be Petra Lockridge, twenty-six years old and hungry for the kind of reputation that came with summit photos. The weather window had been closing. She'd pushed. He'd fallen at 5,700 meters on a slope she later admitted — to herself, in the dark, never out loud — she should have turned them around from two hours earlier.

The official finding was bad luck. Bad conditions, bad angle, bad rock. The Peruvian authorities cleared her. The Bolivian agency that licensed high-altitude guides did not, and neither did the American outfitting company that had brokered the trip. Two blacklists. The family didn't sue; they didn't need to. Marcus's mother simply wrote her one letter, and the letter contained a number: the cost of the funeral, the cost of the memorial bench in the park where he'd proposed to his fiancée, and a figure that represented, as near as grief could calculate, the value of a life cut short at thirty-one.

Petra had paid what she could, when she could, for six years. It was never enough and it was never on time, and the balance had followed her across two continents like a creditor with better legs than she had. Vuthy's finder's fee — the money she'd lost, all of it, when the case went sideways — had been meant to close the account forever.

Now she dialed the number.

Four rings. Five. She counted them the way she counted rope lengths, the way she counted seconds between lightning and thunder. On the sixth ring, a woman answered.

"Hello?"

"Mrs. Hale. My name is Petra Lockridge."

The silence on the line lasted long enough that Petra checked whether the call had dropped. Then: "I know who you are." Not hostile. Worse than hostile — tired. "Do you have any idea what time it is here?"

"I'm sorry. I don't— I'm calling from Thailand. I can call back whenever—"

"No." A breath. "No, you called. So say whatever you called to say."

Petra had crossed flooded rivers, bluffed checkpoints, run rapids that weren't on any map. Her hands had not shaken once through any of it. They shook now, holding a payphone receiver against her ear while rain hammered the glass wall behind her.

"Six years ago I told you Marcus died in an accident. That's what the investigation found, and I let you believe it because believing it was easier for both of us. It wasn't entirely true."

"Petra—" Mrs. Hale's voice changed, sharpened. "If you're calling to confess to something so a court can reduce your—"

"There's no court. There's no deal. Nobody's making me call." She pressed her forehead against the cool glass of the booth. "I'm calling because I spent the last month watching what happens when people bury the truth to protect themselves, and I watched it kill eight men and burn down a village, and I am done being one of those people. Even retroactively. Even uselessly."

Another silence. Somewhere behind Mrs. Hale's voice, a television was playing, and someone turned it off.

"What's not true?" the older woman asked finally. Flat. Braced.

"The weather window was closing. Every guide on that mountain knew it. I'd already turned back one client that week for less. Marcus wanted the summit badly enough that he'd paid a premium for a fast itinerary, and I let the money and my own wanting — I wanted the first woman-led commercial ascent of that face, I wanted the article, I wanted the name — I let all of it outweigh what the sky was telling me. We should have gone down two hours before he fell. Maybe four. If we'd gone down, he'd be alive, and the only thing I'd have lost is a photograph."

She heard Mrs. Hale breathing. Long, controlled breaths, the kind people learn in hospital waiting rooms.

"Why are you telling me this now?"

"Because I got offered a way out. Recently. More than once." Petra laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "Money, a clean passport, my record wiped. All I had to do was hand over some evidence and forget a woman I — forget someone who matters to me. And I said no. And then I sat in a hearing room yesterday and realized the reason I could say no is that I've been carrying your son's death around like ballast for six years, and ballast is heavy but it keeps the boat honest. I don't want to put it down by lying. I want to put it down by telling you the truth and letting you do whatever you need to do with it. Hate me. Sue me. Hang up. Anything except let me keep pretending the mountain did it."

The television clicked back on somewhere in Denver, then off again, decisively.

"Do you know," Mrs. Hale said, "that Daniel — Marcus's father — used to say you must have been the best thing that ever happened to him? Before. When Marcus would call home from training. He'd go on about this guide who could read weather like scripture, who made everyone else on the team look like tourists. Daniel died two years ago. He never stopped saying it."

"I know," Petra whispered. "That's part of why it—"

"I'm not finished." The voice was steel under the fatigue. "You just told me my son died because a young woman was ambitious and scared and human. Do you know what I've been doing for six years? Building you into a monster. It's easier to hate a monster than to think that some ordinary person's ordinary bad judgment took my boy. You've just made yourself ordinary. And God help me, Petra, that's worse, and it's also — it's the first time in six years anyone has respected me enough to tell me the truth."

Petra slid down until she was sitting on the floor of the phone booth, receiver jammed against her ear, rain running down the glass in front of her face.

"There's more," she said. "There's the money. I owe you—"

"I know exactly what you owe me. I have a ledger." A dry sound that might have been a laugh. "Your payments have been late for six years and they have never once been short. Did you know that? Never once short. I used to sit with the ledger and try to make myself stop depositing them, because taking money from the woman who killed my son felt like — I couldn't decide if it was justice or extortion or charity. I still can't."

"Then let me tell you the rest, and you can decide with everything on the table." Petra closed her eyes. "I took a job last month. Finding a missing survey team. I took it because the fee would have cleared the entire balance. Every cent. I'd have been free of you — of it — free of the whole weight. And then I found out the missing team had been murdered, and that the people who did it would pay me triple to look the other way, and I chose the truth instead. Which means the balance is still there. Which means I will keep paying, late, for as long as it takes, unless you tell me to stop."

Mrs. Hale was quiet for a long time. When she spoke again, her voice had gone soft in a way that frightened Petra more than shouting ever had.

"You said 'someone who matters to you.' Earlier. About a woman."

"Anong. Yes."

"And she knows? About Marcus?"

"She knows everything about me. That's what happens when you spend three days trapped in a cave with someone during the worst storm of the decade."

Another pause, and then Mrs. Hale said something Petra would carry for the rest of her life, folded flat against the ballast, keeping the boat honest:

"Bring her to Denver sometime. For the memorial bench. Marcus liked strong women; it drove his sister crazy." A breath. "And Petra — the ledger stays open. Not for the money. I'll tell you when it's closed, and it isn't yet, but it isn't a cage either. It's a rope. You hold one end. I hold the other. That's how it works now."

The line clicked, and the dial tone hummed, and Petra sat on the floor of the booth in a country that had just blacklisted her, holding a dead phone, crying for the first time since the Andes.

* * *

Anong found her there an hour later. She didn't ask questions — she'd learned that much about Petra in forty days of flood and fire. She sat down on the floor beside her, shoulder to shoulder against the glass, and watched the rain.

"Well?" she said finally.

"It's done. All of it. The whole ugly story."

"How does it feel?"

Petra considered the question honestly, the way she'd promised herself she'd consider everything from now on. Lighter and heavier at once, which shouldn't be possible, which was somehow exactly right.

"Like I set something down," she said, "and picked up something else. Something I can actually carry."

Anong nodded slowly. Then she reached into her bag and pulled out a battered envelope, softened by river water and re-dried, and held it out.

"This came to the hotel desk. Addressed to you." A pause. "It's from the inquiry. There's a seal on it."

Petra took the envelope and turned it over twice without opening it. Outside, the rain was easing for the first time in days, and somewhere below, in the street, a longboat engine coughed to life.

"They're summoning you back," Anong said quietly. "Aren't they."

Petra broke the seal.
