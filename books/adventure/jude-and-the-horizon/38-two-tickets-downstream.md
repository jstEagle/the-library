## 38. Two Tickets Downstream

The ticket office at the capital's river terminal opened at six, and Petra was there at five-forty, counting what was left of her money on the wet concrete steps. It wasn't much. After the inquiry lawyers took their cut of nothing — because there was nothing to take a cut of, that was the joke — after the hostel bill and the fine for entering the country on documents a magistrate politely described as "creative," she had enough for one long-haul deck passage downstream and half a plate of noodles.

Two tickets would cost everything, plus the compass if the clerk was in a bad mood.

She stood at the counter when it opened and asked for two.

The clerk didn't look up. "Destination?"

"End of the line."

"Everyone's destination is the end of the line." He stamped something. "Which end?"

Petra looked at the route board behind him, the painted rivers branching like veins off a lung, most of them unnamed in any language she read. Somewhere down in the delta country, past the last concession markers, past the last people who cared about her name or Anong's, there were rivers nobody had surveyed since her father ran them with a pencil and a tin boat. Rivers without permits, which meant rivers without concessions, which meant rivers where water was just water.

"That one," she said, and put down everything she had.

* * *

Anong found her an hour later at the noodle stall near the pier, carrying two canvas bags and wearing the expression of a woman who had slept four hours in three days and intended to keep it that way until further notice.

"You bought my ticket," Anong said. Not a question. She dropped onto the bench across from Petra and stared at her over the steam rising off the shared pot. "I have money."

"You have eleven dollars and a field logbook that three governments now want to subpoena. I've seen your wallet."

"I have a brother who owes me money."

"Your brother owes you a new roof." Petra pushed the better bowl across the table. "Eat. The boat leaves at nine."

Anong ate. For a while neither of them said anything, and the silence had the particular quality of silence between people who had spent three days trapped in a flooded cave together sharing a single headlamp and half a protein bar — silence that didn't need filling, that had already been through worse than words could do to it.

Around them the terminal woke up. Dockhands hauled crates of cooking oil and cheap electronics onto boats flying flags of convenience. A tour group in matching orange vests queued for a photo boat. Somewhere a radio played a song about heartbreak in a language Petra almost understood now, which was as close to fluency as she'd ever get.

"I got a call this morning," Anong said finally. She set her chopsticks down. "From the university. My old department."

"And?"

"They offered me a visiting lectureship. Contaminant hydrology. They used the word *rehabilitation* twice." Her mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "They want me to be respectable again. Publish the data properly, through channels, with co-authors who will make sure the conclusions are appropriately nuanced."

"What did you tell them?"

"I told them their nuance could swim." Anong picked the chopsticks back up. "Then I hung up and sat on the bed for about twenty minutes wondering why I felt like crying."

"Because it was the last door closing," Petra said. "Even the bad doors feel like loss when they shut."

Anong looked at her. "You got a call too."

It wasn't a question either. Anong had learned to read her somewhere between the sandbar and the falls, and Petra had stopped minding somewhere around the cave.

"The family," Petra said. "Marta called. From the Andes side." She turned her bowl slowly on the table, watching the broth swirl. "After the testimony went out on the wire, after the names. She said—" Petra stopped. Started again. "She said she'd watched the hearing footage. All of it. And she said that for eight years she'd needed someone to punish, and I was the easiest shape for it, and now that the punishment had actually happened — the third blacklist, the debt still standing, all of it public — she found she didn't need it anymore. She said the debt is forgiven."

The words came out flat, rehearsed, because she'd been rehearsing them since dawn, trying to find a way to say them that didn't sound like the miracle they were.

Anong set her chopsticks down again. "That's everything. That's the thing you've been carrying since before I met you. You're free."

"Yeah." Petra laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "Here's the funny part. I spent eight years thinking that if I could just clear the debt, pay it off, buy my way back into good standing somewhere — anywhere — then I'd be free. And now the family hands me forgiveness for nothing, and the countries hand me blacklisting for everything, and it turns out freedom was never a currency. It was just a decision I kept refusing to make."

"What decision?"

"To stop asking permission." She met Anong's eyes. "Every job I ever took, every fixer I ever paid, every border I ever sweet-talked — I was asking some authority somewhere to let me back in. To let me be a guide again, a professional, a person with a clean record. And this whole river, this whole forty days, I kept telling myself I was doing it for the payout. Then for the team. Then for you. But underneath all of it I was still auditioning. Still hoping somebody with a stamp would watch me do the right thing and say, *welcome back, Ms. Lockridge.*"

"And nobody did."

"Nobody did. Serey offered to wipe my record and I told him no, and the inquiry thanked me for my cooperation and banned me from the country, and Vuthy's ledger is sitting in an evidence locker while he negotiates from a beach I can't afford to visit." She spread her hands. "There is no version of this where they let me back in. There never was. The only question was whether I was going to keep living like there was."

* * *

They walked to the boat at eight-thirty, along the pier where the morning heat was already pressing down like a thumb. The vessel was a cargo decker converted for passengers, rust streaked down its flanks, hammocks strung three layers deep between the railings. No cabins. No schedule anyone would swear to. Just a heading and a river.

A young man was loading sacks of rice at the gangway, moving them like they weighed nothing, and Petra recognized the build before the face — broad shoulders, careful feet, the balance of someone raised on water. He saw her and straightened, suddenly formal, and she remembered: the boy from Anong's village, the cousin who had rowed them out under cover of dark after the fire, who had held the tiller steady through the checkpoint reach without being asked twice.

"Dara," Anong said, and hugged him, quick and fierce.

He pulled back and looked at both of them with an expression caught between admiration and grief. "Everyone in the village is talking about you. About the hearing. My teacher made us listen to the radio broadcast." He hesitated. "My mother says thank you. She says — she says now maybe the children born next year won't—"

He didn't finish it. He didn't have to. Petra had seen the village clinic, seen the little rows of photographs on the wall that the government said proved nothing and everyone's eyes said proved everything.

"Tell your mother the thanks goes the other direction," Petra said. "You kept us alive. Both of you."

Dara ducked his head, then rallied, because he was sixteen and pride was still winning most of his internal arguments. "Where does the boat go?"

"Downstream," Petra said. "Past the maps."

His eyes lit. "Nobody runs those reaches. My uncle says the channels move every flood season, that the charts from the French time are worth less than the paper." He glanced at the decker with open skepticism. "You'll want someone who can read water you haven't seen."

"We'll want someone who stays in school," Anong said, but gently, and put a hand on the back of his neck. "And who comes to visit us wherever we wash up, and tells us everything happening in the village, because we're going to be far away and badly informed."

Dara grinned. Then, as he turned back to the rice sacks, he said, casually, the way young people deploy important things: "My father has your father's book. The river notes. He bought it from a trader years ago, before we knew whose it was. He wants you to have it back."

Petra went still. "The route notes? The ones from the case liner?"

"There were more pages. Ones that were loose. My father kept them safe." Dara shrugged, suddenly shy. "He says a map should belong to someone who'll use it."

She thought about the compass sitting in her pocket, its case empty now except for the folded pages she'd recovered, the pencil lines of a man dead fifteen years tracing channels she was about to run blind. She thought about inheritance, and what you keep, and what you pass forward before it passes you by.

"Tell him yes," she said. "Tell him I'll come back for it. Or send it downstream — I'll leave an address. I'll figure out how to have an address."

* * *

The boat cast off at nine-forty, late and unapologetic about it. Petra and Anong claimed a stretch of railing on the shaded side, hung their bags, and watched the terminal shrink behind them — the cranes, the flagpoles, the ministry buildings where men in air conditioning were even now drafting the regulations that would govern whether anything they'd done mattered. Behind them, too, invisible but present, the third blacklist was being typed into a database. Somewhere a clerk was spelling her name wrong.

Neither of them waved goodbye to any of it.

"So," Anong said, settling into the shade with the ease of a woman who had decided something difficult and was enjoying the aftermath. "Neither of us is employable. You're banned from three countries and I'm banned from pretending to be neutral. We have no income, no plan, and a boat ticket to a place with no roads." She counted these off on her fingers like items of inventory. "This is the part where a sensible person asks what exactly we think we're doing."

"A sensible person would ask that."

"Good thing neither of us qualifies." Anong leaned her head back against a coil of rope and closed her eyes against the light. "I've been thinking about duty. What it actually is. I spent my whole career thinking duty was the institution — the ministry, the methodology, the peer review. Do the work correctly, submit it correctly, let the structure carry it. And when the structure turned out to be the thing eating the river, I thought duty meant protecting the data at any cost, including—" her voice caught, briefly, on the arithmetic she'd confessed once on a sandbar and would carry always, "—including the people. Including myself."

"And now?"

"Now I think duty is smaller and harder than that. Duty is showing up tomorrow with whatever you actually have — your hands, your training, your stupid ruined reputation — and putting it in service of the truest thing you know, without waiting for permission or protection." She opened one eye. "By that definition, you've been doing your duty for eight years. You just kept apologizing for it."

Petra looked out at the river. The brown water slid past, heavy with the monsoon's leavings, carrying silt from hills she'd bled on and villages she'd failed and saved in the same week. Somewhere upstream, the Khao Sen ranges stood in cloud, and the first surveyors of the next season were probably pitching tents in the same clearing where the last ones had vanished. The concession was dead. The permit clock had run out. The water, as Anong liked to say, remembered everything.

"I had it backwards too," Petra said. "I thought duty was the debt. Pay what you owe, serve your sentence, earn reentry. Transactional. Like the whole moral life was a ledger Vuthy would appreciate." She shook her head. "But Marta forgave the debt and it changed nothing about what I owe. Because what I owe was never money. It's just — the work. Finding people who are lost. Telling the truth about terrain, about water, about what happened on it. That doesn't get paid off. It just gets carried, and passed on."

"Like a compass."

"Like a compass."

Anong reached over and took her hand, lacing their fingers together with the unselfconscious directness she brought to everything — data, confessions, this. Her palm was warm and slightly rough from rope and bail-work.

"For the record," she said, "I'm not traveling with you out of duty."

"No?"

"I'm traveling with you because when the cave flooded and we had three hours of air and no exit, you spent two of them making me laugh. Because you run rapids like you're arguing with them and winning. Because you looked at Serey — a man offering you everything either of us will ever be offered again — and you said no like it was easy, and I know it wasn't, and I loved you for the cost of it before I ever admitted the rest." She said it plainly, facing forward, watching the banks slide by. "So. That's my disclosure. Full chain of custody."

Petra laughed — really laughed, the first time in forty days — and squeezed her hand and did not let go. "Noted for the record. Reciprocal disclosure: I bought your ticket with my last money and I'd do it again, and I'm not telling you which compass I'd pawn to manage it."

"You'd pawn mine."

"I'd pawn yours."

They stayed like that as the city fell away and the river widened into country — rice paddies drowning green in the flood, karst towers rising out of the haze like the backs of sleeping animals, kids waving from sandbars at a boat that had no business being this far down. In the afternoon a rain squall came through sideways and they stood in it deliberately, letting it wash off the last of the capital, and Anong named the fish they'd eat tonight and Petra argued about the best way to cook them, and the argument was pointless and perfect.

Near dusk, Petra took out the compass. She held it in her palm, the brass worn bright along the edges where her father's thumb had traveled, and beneath it the folded route notes with their confident pencil lines running off the edge of the known world.

"Tomorrow," she said, mostly to herself, "we hit the unmapped reach."

Anong looked at the compass, then at her. "Scared?"

"Terrified." Petra smiled and closed her fist around the brass. "That's how I'll know we're doing it right."

Ahead, where the charted river braided apart into a dozen uncertain channels, the sun was going down in a way that promised weather, and the boat's horn sounded once, low and unhurried, like something setting out rather than arriving.
