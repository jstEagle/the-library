## 39. The Compass Passed On

The boy's name was Rath, and he was sixteen the way river kids are sixteen — older in the hands, younger in the eyes, like the water had aged half of him and left the rest alone.

Petra had first seen him three days after the falls, poling a flat-bottomed skiff through the shallows below the wreck site, salvaging what the flood had given back. Serey's lead boat had come apart on the rocks like something unzipped, and for two days the river had been coughing up its pieces: fuel cans, a shredded life vest, a cooler lid spinning in an eddy. Rath collected it all with the methodical patience of someone whose family had lived off the river's leavings for generations. When he'd spotted Petra and Anong camped on the gravel bar, he hadn't run. He'd simply poled closer, looked at the two wrecked women sitting among their scattered gear, and said, "You need firewood. That wood is wet everywhere except the deadfall past the fig tree."

He'd been right. He'd been right about everything since — the safe channel through the braided section above Ban Sokha, which village headman could be trusted and which one had already sold his word to the consortium, how to get a damaged boat patched by a man in Chiang Saen who asked no questions because Rath was his sister's son. Somewhere in the last week, without anyone deciding it, the boy had become their pilot, their fixer, their translator, and the only reason they were sitting in this teakwood guesthouse in the provincial capital instead of drifting face-down somewhere below the third rapid.

Now it was the last morning, and Petra had the compass out on the table between them.

The room smelled of river and mosquito coil. Through the open shutters came the market noise — papaya being hacked, a radio playing something tinny and sweet, a woman arguing cheerfully about the price of fish. Below the window, the Mekong ran wide and brown and unhurried, carrying its usual freight of logs and longboats and things nobody wanted to look at too closely. Forty days ago Petra had come up that river hunting a vanished survey team. Now the permits were dead, the concession was collapsing in real time — she could see the consortium's compound from here, its gates shut, its flag gone, men loading trucks at dawn like thieves leaving a house before the owners came home — and the only thing left to do was leave.

Anong was downstairs settling the bill with the owner, arguing him down out of habit more than need. Petra was glad of the privacy. Some things you didn't do with an audience.

Rath sat across from her, cross-legged on the chair because chairs were still slightly foreign to him, eating sticky rice out of a plastic bag with two fingers. He watched the compass the way he watched water — openly, without pretending not to.

"You keep looking at it," Petra said.

"It is a good compass." He said it the way other people said *that is a beautiful woman* or *that is a fast boat*. "My uncle had one. Brass. Chinese. He lost it in the rapids above Khone in '09 and I think he still dreams about it."

"This one's not brass." She turned it over in her palm. The casing was scratched aluminum, dented at the crown where it had taken a rock strike in the Andes six years ago, the same strike that had put her on her knees beside a client she couldn't save. Her father had bought it in Fairbanks in 1978, secondhand, from a bush pilot who needed beer money. It had never once lied to her. Even in the karst caves, even in the whiteout rain, when every other instrument and instinct failed, the needle had swung and settled and pointed true north as if north were a promise someone had made to her personally.

She'd found the route notes under the liner eleven days ago, in the worst hour of the monsoon, and the notes had changed everything — her father had run these rivers in the seventies, sampling sites marked in his cramped handwriting, the same sites Anong's team had used, the same secret channels that had gotten them down the falls. She'd read them so many times since that she knew them by feel: the soft paper gone soft as cloth, the pencil fading where his thumb had rubbed it.

The compass she could give away. The notes she couldn't. Not yet. Maybe not ever.

"Rath." She set the compass down between them, needle trembling, then settling. "When we go tomorrow, we're going by road. Airport road, then the ferry, then whatever comes after. We won't need a guide." She paused. "But you're going to be guiding on this river for the next fifty years, if the river lets you. And there are things on it now that weren't on it last month."

He stopped eating. He understood immediately — she could see it land in his face, the way understanding always landed on kids from places where bad news arrived by boat. "The mining people," he said quietly. "What is under the water."

"What's under the water," she confirmed. "And who put it there. And who paid to make sure nobody measured it." She tapped the compass case. "There's going to be journalists coming upriver within the month. Scientists. Government people with clean shirts. They'll all want guides. They'll all pay. And most of them will be honest, but some of them will be the kind who ask you to take them somewhere and then ask you to forget you took them." She held his eyes. "I'm not telling you to be careful. You live on this river; you know how to be careful better than I ever will. I'm telling you that knowing exactly where you are is worth more than money. Money gets offered to you so you'll lose track of where you are. That's the whole trick. That's all it is."

Rath nodded slowly. Then, because he was sixteen and honest: "Your friend Anong. She is staying with you?"

"We're going together." Petra heard her own voice soften and didn't fight it. "For a while. For as long as the road goes the same direction."

He grinned then, quick and bright, and Petra thought: *there it is*, the thing the river hadn't managed to age out of him yet.

*

She made him work for it. Not out of cruelty — out of respect, because her father had made her work for it, thirty years ago, in a different mountain range, with a different debt hanging over the family, and she had never once resented the price.

They spent the afternoon on the water, just the two of them in Rath's skiff, running the local channels he'd grown up on. She taught him what she could compress into four hours: how to read a standing wave for what it said about the bottom; why the smooth tongue of current downstream of a confluence was a liar and the boil line told the truth; how to hold a bearing across featureless water by picking a far object and trusting the needle over your own drifting sense of straight. He absorbed it the way dry ground absorbs the first rain — instantly, greedily, everything running straight down into him.

At the confluence above town she made him stop the boat and drift, engine off, and take a bearing on the temple spire, then on the smoke from the cement works, and triangulate their position on the battered map she'd carried since chapter one of this whole disaster. He got it right on the second try. On the first try he'd been off by two hundred meters because he'd trusted his eyes over the needle, and she'd made him do it again, and when he got it right she saw him understand — really understand — that the instrument knew something he didn't, and that humility toward a small brass-hearted piece of metal was a survival trait.

"My father used to say," she told him, as they drifted, "that a compass doesn't tell you where to go. It tells you where you are. People get that backwards. They think direction is the hard part. Direction is easy. Knowing where you actually stand — that's the hard part. Most people spend their whole lives navigating from a position they've invented."

Rath considered this with the seriousness he gave everything. "Like the mining company," he said. "They knew where they wanted to go. They did not know — or they pretended not to know — where they were standing."

Petra laughed, a short surprised sound, and the boy grinned at having produced it. "Exactly like the mining company," she said. "You're going to be fine."

*

That evening Anong found her on the guesthouse balcony, cleaning the compass one last time with a rag and a drop of oil from the sewing kit she'd bought in Vientiane a lifetime ago. Below them the town was turning on its lights, and the river was turning silver-dark, and somewhere upstream the consortium's empty compound sat in the dark like a pulled tooth.

"He's taking it?" Anong asked.

"He's earning it." Petra screwed the cap back on the oil. "Tomorrow morning."

Anong leaned on the rail beside her. She'd been quiet all day — not the guilty quiet of the sandbar, months ago, when she'd confessed the arithmetic that haunted her, walking away from her team to save the data. This was a different quiet. Lighter. The quiet of someone setting down weight and not yet trusting their shoulders to believe it. Her samples were in a lab in Bangkok now, chain of custody intact, the surviving vials already producing numbers that would end careers and maybe indictments. Her field logs were copied in three places. Her village had buried two more children while they ran the river, and she carried those names the way Petra carried the client's name from the Andes — not as guilt anymore, exactly, but as cargo. Purpose with mass.

"I have been thinking," Anong said, "about what happens to people like us after a thing like this."

"People like us."

"The ones who testify. The ones who don't take the offer." She turned her head, and in the balcony light her face was tired and clear. "Serey offered you a clean passport. Vuthy offered you enough money to buy back your name. Your client's family offered you forgiveness. Every door was open, and you closed every one with your own hand. Do you regret it?"

Petra let the question sit. Down on the water, a night boat went by with its lamps lit, heading for the falls, running the dark the way her father had run it, the way she had run it, the way Rath would run it tomorrow with a dead man's compass in his pocket.

"No," she said. "And yes. Both, permanently. I think that's what it costs. I think anybody who tells you they walked away from that much money and that much safety and felt nothing but freedom is selling something." She looked at Anong. "But I'd close every door again. That's the part I know for certain. The rest is weather."

Anong reached over and put her hand briefly on Petra's forearm — the burned one, the scarred one, the one that had bailed water for forty days — and said nothing, because nothing needed saying. They had passed words weeks ago. What remained was the silence that two people can share only after they've been trapped in a flooded cave together starving, after they've each watched the other almost break and chosen, silently, to help hold them together anyway.

Below them, on the dark landing, a small figure was climbing the stairs with a wrapped bundle under one arm. Rath. Coming to say goodnight, probably, or to deliver the boat parts he'd promised for the ferry crossing.

Petra slid the compass into her jacket pocket and stood up. Tomorrow, she thought. Tomorrow morning, at the dock, before the ferry came.

Some inheritances you hand over in private.

*

The dock at dawn was all mist and rope creak and the smell of diesel and wet wood. The ferry wouldn't come for an hour. Rath was already there, of course — he'd been there since before light, Petra was sure of it — checking his skiff's mooring lines with the fussiness of a boy who owned almost nothing and maintained it perfectly.

She called his name and he came over, wiping his hands on his shorts, and she took the compass out and held it flat on her palm between them.

"For me?" he said. His voice cracked on the second word and he hated it, and she loved him for hating it.

"For you. But you have to hear the terms." She waited until he nodded. "One: you use it. Not as jewelry. You navigate with it until the needle wears loose, and then you get it fixed and keep navigating. Two: when some journalist or scientist or government man comes upriver asking about the contamination, you guide them true. You show them the real sites, the ones in my father's notes, the ones the company tried to erase. Three—" and here her voice caught, just slightly, and she pushed through it, "—when somebody offers you money to lose track of where you are, you take out this compass, and you take a bearing, and you remember exactly where you're standing. Can you do those three things?"

"Yes," Rath said. No hesitation. Sixteen years old, absolute as the needle.

She pressed it into his hand and folded his fingers closed over it, and felt the strange lightness rise up her arm where the weight had lived for thirty years. Her father's compass. Her father's rivers, in the end, though none of them had known it. It belonged here, on this water, in these hands, more than it had ever belonged in hers. Compasses weren't heirlooms. They were tools that chose their next keeper by proximity and need, and the need was here.

"There's something else," she said, and took out the route notes — the soft paper, the faded pencil, the map of her father's secret river — and showed them to him one last time. "These I'm keeping. For now. Because there's one more trip I have to make, and I need to know where I am when I make it." She smiled. "But I'm going to copy them for you before we leave. Every mark. Every sounding. And someday, when you've worn your own path along this water, you'll add yours, and pass the whole thing on to some kid who reminds you of yourself."

Rath looked down at the compass in his fist. Then he did something she didn't expect: he bowed, low and formal, the old way, the way you honor an elder or a teacher, and when he straightened his eyes were wet and furious about it.

"Khun Petra," he said. "When they write about this — the permits, the trial, all of it — they will say a foreigner found the truth." He shook his head. "But we on the river will say it differently. We will say the water gave it up. And that a woman listened."

Downstream, around the bend, the ferry horn sounded — one long note rolling up the brown water through the mist.

Petra shouldered her pack, lighter than it had been in years, and turned toward the sound. Anong was waiting at the top of the ramp, tickets in hand, watching her with that clear tired patience that had become, without either of them announcing it, the shape of home.

"One more thing," Rath called after her. "Where does the road go? After the ferry?"

Petra looked at Anong. Anong shrugged, and smiled, and it was the best answer either of them had.

"Nowhere particular," Petra said. "We haven't checked the compass yet."

She kept her father's route notes in her breast pocket, over her heart, and walked up the ramp into the mist, toward whatever river came next.
