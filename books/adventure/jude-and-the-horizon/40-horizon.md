## 40. Horizon

The ferry was a rust-bucket called the *Mekhala Star*, and it smelled of diesel, wet rope, and forty years of passengers who had all been going somewhere they couldn't quite name. Petra found the open deck at the stern, where the rail was low enough to lean on and the engine noise swallowed everything except what you chose to say out loud.

Anong was already there when she came up, sitting on an overturned crate with her knees drawn up, watching the brown water slide past below them like something patient and enormous that had decided, for once, not to be in a hurry.

"Good morning," Anong said, without looking at her.

"You're up early."

"I never went down." She turned her head then, and there were shadows under her eyes that hadn't been there three months ago, and Petra thought: those are mine too, now. We earned those together. "I wanted to see the sun come up over water I didn't have to test."

Petra laughed at that — a real laugh, the kind that surprised her own throat. It had been a long time since either of them had laughed about the water. For months the water had been the enemy: the thing that rose, the thing that hid bodies, the thing that carried poison into a village's children, the thing that had nearly drowned them both twice and once actually did drown Serey's lead boat while they watched from the high bank, silent, unwilling witnesses to a justice neither of them had asked for or could stop.

But this morning the water was just water. Moving. Going somewhere. Same as them.

She sat down next to Anong on the crate. It wasn't built for two people but they made it work, shoulder against shoulder, the way they'd learned to share everything else — bailing buckets, rations, silence, blame.

Below them, the river ran wide and slow through the morning haze. Fishing boats were already out, small dark shapes with lanterns still lit, their owners pulling nets with the unhurried rhythm of people who had done this ten thousand times and would do it ten thousand more. On the far bank, mist hung in the trees like breath held overnight, finally being released.

"Do you know where we are?" Anong asked.

"No."

"Do you know where we're going?"

"No." Petra paused. "Do you?"

"Not even slightly." Anong smiled without opening her eyes. "It's wonderful."

* * *

They had bought the tickets with the last of the money Rath had pressed into Petra's hand at the ferry ramp — not payment, he'd insisted, just fuel money, just enough, don't argue with me. Two third-class decks, no cabin, no meals included. The ticket agent had looked at their passports and frowned at both of them in turn, because word travels faster than boats in a country this size, and by now everyone from Vuthy's old clerks to the checkpoint police knew the names Lockridge and Suriyawong the way fishermen know a storm front.

Blacklisted three times. Unemployable on two continents. Broke down to the cost of passage and whatever food fit in a cloth bag.

And lighter than either of them had ever been.

It didn't make sense, and Petra had stopped pretending it needed to. Somewhere between the compound fire and the falls — somewhere between holding the compass steady over water no map had ever recorded and watching Serey walk away untouched because she would not become him — the arithmetic of her life had quietly inverted. Everything she'd spent fifteen years accumulating had turned out to be ballast. Everything she'd lost had turned out to be the boat itself.

She thought about that sometimes at night, lying awake in whatever room they could afford, doing the accounting she couldn't help doing. The debt to the Calder family, still unpaid. The career, gone. The countries, closed. The payout from Vuthy, forfeited the moment she'd used his own ledger against him instead of selling it. The client who died in the Andes because she'd read the weather wrong — that one she carried still, and would carry always; some weights weren't meant to be set down, only carried properly.

But underneath all of it, for the first time since she was nineteen years old and following her father up a river he wouldn't name, there was nothing she owed anyone that could be collected by force.

That was the thing nobody told you about freedom. It didn't feel like winning. It felt like the moment after a storm passes and you realize your hands have stopped shaking — not because the danger ended, but because you stopped bracing.

* * *

The sun came up around six, the way it does in the tropics — no gradual brightening, just a decision. Light poured across the water and turned the river to hammered bronze, and the mist on the far bank caught fire and dissolved, and suddenly they could see for kilometers: the bends ahead, the tributaries feeding in, the blue wall of hills to the east that no survey team had ever bothered mapping because there was nothing under them worth extracting.

Nothing worth extracting. Petra heard the phrase in Serey's voice, smooth and reasonable, and felt the old anger flicker and then, surprisingly, gutter out. Let them keep their empty hills. Empty was another word for unowned.

Anong had pulled out her field notebook — the surviving one, the one she'd kept dry inside her jacket through the cave, the flood, the compound, all of it. The pages were warped and soft as cloth now, the ink feathered where the humidity had gotten in, but legible. She was writing in it again. Not data this time. Not contamination readings, not chain-of-custody logs, not the careful forensic language of someone building a case.

Just notes. Observations. The way the current eddied below the confluence. A kingfisher on a snag. The depth of green in the flooded forest where the river spilled its banks.

"What are you going to do?" Petra asked. "Now that the inquiry's over. Now that it's public."

Anong considered the question honestly, which was one of the things Petra loved about her — she never gave an answer until she had one.

"I don't know," she said. "There's a university in Chiang Mai that wrote to me. They don't care about the blacklist; they care about the data. There might be work there. Teaching, maybe. Or I might go back to my village for a season — my brother's knee is healing wrong, and someone should be there who knows how to argue with doctors." She tapped the notebook against her knee. "And there's this." She nodded east, at the unmapped hills. "Nobody has sampled the headwaters up there. Nobody has tested anything past the second gorge. If there's mining moving into that valley in five years — and there will be, there always is — someone should have a baseline. Someone should know what clean looked like before they got there."

"So you'd go first. Before the companies."

"That's the job, isn't it?" Anong said. "Being there first. Knowing the water before anyone has a reason to lie about it." She looked at Petra. "What about you? What does a guide do when three countries won't let her guide?"

Petra watched the fishing boats for a while before answering.

"My father used to say there are two kinds of rivers," she said finally. "The ones on maps, and the ones that aren't yet. He spent his whole life on the second kind. Everyone thought it was because he was running from something." She touched her breast pocket, where the route notes sat. "He wasn't. He just liked being early."

* * *

They ate breakfast off the top of a supply crate: cold rice, dried fish, mangoes Anong had bargained for at the dock with a fluency in three dialects that still startled people who assumed the quiet woman with the notebook was a tourist. A deckhand's kid came over to gawk at them — two foreign-looking women eating on the floor like locals — and Anong taught him a card game in ten minutes and let him win twice, and his laughter carried across the deck, and other kids drifted over, and for an hour the stern of the *Mekhala Star* was the least lonely place in the world.

Petra sat back against the rail and let it happen around her. This, too, was new. There was a version of her — the version from a year ago, the version who took Vuthy's contract because desperation dressed up as opportunity was still opportunity — who would have been scanning the deck for threats, counting exits, tracking every stranger's hands. That woman had saved her life a dozen times on this river. But she was tired, and she was allowed to be tired now, and tiredness looked different when nothing was hunting you.

The kid with the cards asked her, in broken English, if she was famous. His cousin had seen the news, he said. About the testimony. About the permits collapsing. About the minister who resigned.

"No," Petra said. "Not famous."

"But you did the thing? In the news?"

"I did a thing," Petra agreed. "There were a lot of things. Most of them were done by other people. I just refused to stop walking."

The kid thought about this with the seriousness of the young, then went back to losing at cards.

Anong caught her eye across the little circle of children and didn't say anything, but her face said it: *refused to stop walking*. That was the epitaph for the whole year, if it needed one. Not brave, not clever, not righteous — just stubborn in the specific direction of the truth, long after stubbornness stopped paying.

* * *

Midmorning, the ferry slowed at a landing so small it wasn't a town, just a concrete apron and a stairway cut into the bank and a shrine with faded garlands. A handful of passengers got off. A handful got on, mostly traders with baskets, one monk, a man with a caged rooster who complained the entire time he boarded.

At the rail, Petra noticed a boy — sixteen, maybe seventeen, wiry, barefoot, with the rope-scarred palms she recognized from a hundred river towns — helping the traders haul their baskets up the stairs. He worked fast and watched everything, and when the ferry's wake rocked the landing he shifted his weight with the unconscious economy of someone who had grown up reading water the way other people read faces.

She'd seen that kid before. Not him specifically — she'd been him. Nineteen years old, strong and broke and watchful, knowing every eddy on his stretch of river and nothing about the world beyond it, waiting for something to happen to him because happening-to-you was the only future a place like that advertised.

Except it wasn't. She knew that now. Her father had known it before her.

She dug into her pack and took out the compass.

It was a stupid thing to be sentimental about, objectively — a brass-cased liquid-damped compass, baseplate cracked, needle sluggish in damp weather, worth maybe thirty dollars to a collector and priceless to exactly one person alive. Her father had carried it up rivers on two continents. She had carried it through a monsoon, a kidnapping, a flood, and a courtroom. The case liner, peeled back, still held the ghost of his handwriting: route notes, flow estimates, a sketch of a falls section that only two people had ever run and one of them was dead.

Anong saw it in her hands and understood immediately. She didn't say anything. She just moved closer, so her shoulder was warm against Petra's, and waited.

The boy came up the stairs with the last basket, sweating, grinning, alive. As he passed along the deck toward the cargo hold, Petra stepped into his path.

"You," she said, in the flat trade-English that works everywhere rivers meet roads. "You know this water?"

"Every sandbar from here to the gorge," he said, puffing up slightly. "And the gorge too. My uncle runs timber through it."

"And past the gorge? The eastern channels? The ones that aren't on any chart?"

His eyes went wide and honest. "Nobody goes there. Too shallow in dry season, too fast in rain. My grandfather said there's a falls section with no name."

"There is," Petra said. "I've seen it." She held out the compass. "Then this is yours."

He stared at it. At her. Back at it. "I can't—"

"You can. You will. Listen." She made him hold still with a look that fifteen years of clients had learned to obey. "The needle points north, but that's the least of what it does. It tells you when you're being lied to. When the current says one thing and the map says another, believe the current. When a man offers you money to carry something upstream and not ask questions, ask the questions. When someone tells you a river is empty, go look." She closed his fingers around the brass case. "A man named Lockridge worked these rivers twenty years ago. He left notes. Some of them are in that case. Read them. Then add your own, and someday give it away too. That's the whole inheritance. That's all it is."

The boy clutched the compass like it might bite him, or bless him, and didn't know which he wanted.

"What's your name?" he managed.

"Doesn't matter. In a month nobody will remember it, and that's the point." She almost left it there. Then, because some debts aren't paid in money: "Petra. My name is Petra. Remember the compass, not me."

He bowed the way river kids bow — quick, deep, embarrassed by his own sincerity — and ran for the cargo hold before she could change her mind.

Anong put her arm through hers as they walked back to the stern rail. "You gave away your father's compass."

"I kept the notes." She patted her breast pocket. "He'd have said I got the better half. The compass just tells you where north is. The notes tell you why it matters."

* * *

Afternoon came down golden and slow. The hills to the east grew nearer, or the ferry grew nearer to them, and the river began to narrow and quicken, the water going from bronze to green, riffles showing white teeth where the channel shallowed over gravel bars.

The pilot — a heavy, taciturn man who had ignored them for two days — appeared at the rail beside them around four o'clock, chewing betel, and studied the water ahead with professional disapproval.

"Past the next bend, the channel splits," he said, conversationally, in the way of a man who has decided you might be worth talking to. "Left side is the marked channel. Right side is nothing. No chart, no markers, no traffic. Shifting bars." He spat red into the wake. "Everyone goes left."

"Where does the right side come out?" Petra asked.

The pilot shrugged, a big rolling shrug that involved his whole spine. "Who knows? Nobody asks. Maybe it dies in the hills. Maybe it comes out at the sea. My grandfather said it joins the old river, the one that changed course a hundred years ago." He glanced at her sideways, betel-stained teeth grinning. "You want to see?"

"We want to see."

"Wrong boat for it. Wrong season, probably. Wrong everything." He pushed off the rail and headed back to his wheelhouse, calling over his shoulder: "But there's a landing past the split. Fisherman's staging, one hut, one leaky boat for hire, one old man who remembers more than he says. Tell him the pilot sent you. He'll charge you double and take you anyway."

When he was gone, Anong looked at Petra, and Petra looked at Anong, and neither of them said anything for a long moment, because there was nothing that needed saying. The right-hand channel. The unnamed falls. The old man with the leaky boat. Every element of it was a bad idea, professionally speaking — shallow, uncharted, monsoon-fed, illegal in spirit if not in law, the kind of run that had gotten her blacklisted twice before anyone conspired to make it three.

It was perfect.

"We have no money," Anong said, happily.

"We have a compass we gave away, so we'll navigate by the notes and by arguing."

"We have no permit for anything."

"We've never needed one less."

"We are the two most notorious women on this river."

"Which means nobody will believe it's us." Petra grinned, and felt the grin pull on muscles that had forgotten the shape. "We'll be anonymous. It'll be disgusting."

Anong laughed — head back, unguarded, the laugh of the woman in the cave who had held the sample case above the floodwater and sworn, and meant it — and the sound went out over the water and scattered the birds off a gravel bar, and Petra thought: *this*. Not the testimony, not the collapsed concession, not even the falls. This. The laugh. The argument about which channel. The morning neither of them checked the compass because they'd given it to a boy with rope-scarred hands, and discovered they didn't miss it.

Freedom earned at full price. Paid in full. Receipt kept in a breast pocket, over the heart, in dead ink in a dead man's hand.

* * *

The bend came at dusk.

The ferry slowed, and the channel split exactly as the pilot had promised — the marked channel swinging left, buoyed and dredged and busy, the right-hand channel sliding away east into the hills, narrow, green-shadowed, unmarked, its surface wrinkled with current that clearly knew things the charts didn't.

They stood at the stern rail and watched it fall behind them, and neither of them spoke, and the silence between them had long ago stopped needing translation.

Tomorrow they would get off at the fisherman's landing. Tomorrow they would bargain with an old man who remembered more than he said. Tomorrow they would load a leaky boat with a notebook full of baseline hopes and a breast pocket full of a dead man's route notes and absolutely nothing else, and they would point it up a river nobody had mapped, for reasons nobody would pay them for, and it would be the best work either of them had ever done.

Tonight there was just this: the deck, the dusk, the two channels diverging behind them, the one crowded and safe and marked all the way to the sea, and the other one dark and empty and theirs.

Petra leaned on the rail. Anong leaned into her. The river ran on ahead into the hills, into the dark, into the unmapped country where clean water was still just water, waiting for someone to come and know it first.

"North is that way," Anong said eventually, nodding vaguely east, at nothing, at everything.

"Says who?"

"Says me. I'm reliable. I have a notebook."

Petra laughed, and the last light went off the hills, and the stars came out over the horizon like a survey team taking positions — thousands of them, fixed and patient, marking a territory no consortium would ever lease.

Nobody had mapped any of it.

That was the best news she'd ever heard.
