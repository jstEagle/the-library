## 1. The Thief Who Walked Backward

There are two kinds of thieves in the Saltreach: the kind who run, and the kind who think.

The runners I catch by lunchtime. They leave a trail a blind mule could follow — dropped coins, snapped greasewood, the occasional boot abandoned mid-flight like a confession. Runners believe distance is the same thing as escape, which is charming, the way children are charming.

The thinkers are rarer. And Jude Vane was the rarest thinker I'd tracked in eleven years on the flats.

I picked up her trail at dawn, three days out of Corriander Wells, where the magistrate's ledger said she'd lifted something from the Hall of Records that was worth more than the building it sat in. The clerk wouldn't say what. The clerk had gone pale and started talking about weather, which in the Saltreach is what people do when they'd rather be struck by lightning than answer a question.

The trail itself told me more than the clerk ever would.

Here's what you need to understand about tracking on the salt flats: the ground remembers everything. Salt crust holds a print for days if the wind stays down, and the wind almost never stays down, so a tracker learns to read the edges of things — where crust has been crushed to powder, where the powder has drifted, which direction the drift leans. It's slow work. It's honest work. It's the only conversation the desert ever has with me, and it doesn't lie, because the desert has no reason to flatter anyone.

Jude Vane's trail did something I had never seen in eleven years.

She walked backward.

Not all the time. That's what unsettled me. A person walking backward the whole way is a curiosity, a madwoman, an easy story. But Vane walked forward when the ground didn't matter — across bare stone shelves, through old flood channels where the silt swallowed prints whole — and then, at the edges of every soft patch, she turned around and walked *backward* through it, so that her heel-marks faced the direction she'd come from. Anyone following her prints would swear she was traveling east when she was traveling west.

Except she wasn't erasing herself. She was forging herself. She wanted a false trail found. She wanted someone — me, specifically, though she couldn't have known my name yet — to spend a day and a half chasing her ghost east toward the Brackish Hills before figuring out the trick and turning around with a jaw full of salt and humiliation.

I figured it out by noon of the first day. It still cost me a day and a half, because knowing a trick and undoing a trick are different animals entirely. I had to read the ground against its own testimony, hunting for the faint second layer of disturbance under the confident first one — the ghost of a print inside a print, the way a liar's voice trembles just under the words.

By the second evening I stopped being angry about it.

That's the part I keep returning to, now, when I can't sleep. Somewhere between the Brackish Hills and the coast, the anger curdled into something else, something a ranger isn't supposed to carry. Curiosity. Admiration, if I'm honest, and I'm trying to be, lately, though it's a discipline I came to late.

Because here's the thing about walking backward: it hurts. Try it. Twenty minutes and your calves are screaming, your ankles hate you, your sense of direction starts whispering treason. Now try it across broken salt crust in the heat of the day, in boots, carrying whatever she was carrying, for three days. That's not cleverness. That's discipline of a kind most soldiers never achieve, and I've known soldiers who could march through a sandstorm singing.

So the question stopped being *where is she going* and became *what is she carrying that's worth this*.

You don't walk backward for coin. Coin spends the same whether your heels point wrong or right. You don't do it for spite, or pride, or the thrill — thieves who love the game take risks, they improvise, they get sloppy in ways that are almost endearing. This was none of that. This was a woman who had decided, somewhere back in Corriander Wells, that failure was not survivable, and had built her entire journey around that single certainty.

People only guard things like that when the thing is irreplaceable.

* * *

I nearly had her on the third night, and the way I nearly had her taught me the last thing I needed to know before everything went sideways.

The flats break apart near the coast into a maze of tidal channels — dry ones, mostly, these years, the sea having retreated a full league beyond the old markers sometime before my grandmother's grandmother. The channels cut the salt into plates and ridges, and in the dark they're treacherous as broken glass. I'd gained on her through the afternoon because the terrain slowed her backward-walking to a crawl, and by moonrise I could see her: a slim figure on the ridge of a channel bank, moving with that deliberate, reversed gait, silhouetted against the biggest moon I've seen in a decade.

I should have waited. Should have circled wide, come at her from ahead, given her nowhere to reverse into. Eleven years of training says exactly that. But three days of her tricks had gotten under my skin, and there's a particular itch a tracker gets when he can finally *see* the quarry — the itch to close the distance, to turn the long conversation with the ground into a conversation with a face.

I closed the distance. She heard me — of course she heard me; I was moving fast over crust that crackles like frying fat — and she did the last thing I expected.

She stopped. Turned. Looked straight at me across maybe forty yards of moonlit salt.

And she smiled.

Not a taunt. Not fear. Something worse than either — recognition, almost. Like she'd been expecting me longer than I'd been expecting her, and was relieved I'd finally arrived.

"Ranger," she called, and her voice carried flat and clear across the salt. "How far behind me is the rest?"

"There is no rest," I said. "It's just me."

Something moved across her face then — disappointment, I thought at first, which made no sense. Later I'd understand it was recalculation. A woman planning around one pursuer is a woman with room to maneuver. A woman planning around a company of rangers is a woman already dead.

"You should go home, ranger," she said. "Whatever they're paying you, it isn't enough for where I'm going."

"The Hall of Records pays me nothing," I said. "The Rangers pay me plenty. Hand over the satchel and we'll talk about it."

She looked down at the strap across her chest — not a satchel, I saw now, but a bandolier of some kind, holding a dozen objects the size of a man's fist, wrapped in oilcloth. She put her hand over them the way you'd shield a candle flame.

"No," she said simply. And then, before I could take a step: "Ask yourself one question before you chase me onto the flats' edge, ranger. Ask why the Commander signed the warrant herself. Personal seal. No intermediaries. For one thief and one theft." Her head tilted. "What do you suppose is in here that a woman like Halvard Vane wants destroyed so badly she won't trust it to her own people?"

I knew the name, of course. Everyone in the service knows Ranger-Commander Halvard Vane. What I knew — what I felt land in my chest like a dropped stone — was the surname.

"You're related to her."

"Was," said Jude Vane. "Past tense does a lot of work in my family."

Then she turned and ran — forward, finally, honestly, boots hammering the salt — down into the channel maze and toward the thin gray line on the horizon that even in the dark I recognized as the sea.

I chased her. Of course I chased her. Duty is duty, and besides, I wanted answers now worse than I wanted the arrest. That should have been my first warning, right there: a ranger who wants conversation more than capture is already halfway to being something else.

But I want to record, here, honestly, what I thought in those last minutes of the old life, pounding down a dry tidal channel under a fat white moon after a thief who shared blood with my own commanding officer:

I thought: *she never once lied to me.*

Three days of forged trails and reversed prints, and the moment she opened her mouth, every word had the ring of a struck bell. Thieves lie the way birds sing — constantly, beautifully, without thinking. Jude Vane had spent three days lying with her feet and then told me the truth with her mouth, and the truth was a grenade rolled gently into my hands with the pin already pulled: *the Commander signed the warrant herself.*

I'd carried sealed orders out of Corriander Wells. Standard practice, personal seal, unread until the objective was achieved. I'd carried them without a second thought, the way you carry a stone in your pocket.

Now the stone had weight. Now it had teeth.

* * *

I lost her at the waterline.

Not to skill — by then I was close enough to hear her breathing, ragged, human, a sound that did something unwelcome to my resolve. I lost her because the sea came back.

I want to be precise about this, because afterward everyone who heard the account assumed I was sunstruck or lying, and I have spent enough of my life reading the desert's honesty to know exactly how dishonest my own eyes are permitted to be. But this happened:

The tide came in.

Not the feeble lap of brine at the old tideline that the charts still grudgingly admit to. A real tide. A wall of dark water rolling up the flats under the moon, silent as spilled ink, swallowing the channel maze plate by plate, and moving inland with a purpose that tides don't have. Tides breathe. This thing *advanced*. The ground shuddered under my boots, a deep grinding groan, like some door older than the desert swinging open below the salt.

Ahead of me, Jude Vane stopped at the water's edge. Any sane person would have run along the tideline, away from it. Instead she stood there watching the sea reclaim a league of desert, and I watched her shoulders drop — watched terror turn into something that looked, impossibly, like relief. Like an answer arriving.

The water parted around a spur of black rock that no chart has ever marked, and where it parted, an opening showed in the cliff line behind it. A cave mouth, breathing mist. Old beyond old. There were carvings above it — I could see them even at that distance, spirals worn nearly smooth, the kind the legends attach to places rangers are ordered never to enter.

The Cave of the Landfall. My grandmother sang about it. The songs say it swallows the river that feeds the glacier, and that the river runs backward through it, upstream, toward a source no living map admits exists.

Jude Vane looked at the cave. Then she looked back at me — forty yards of flooding salt between us now, and closing — and she did not smile this time.

"They'll tell you I stole from the Hall of Records," she shouted over the groan of the water. "They won't tell you what I took. So I'm telling you: I took voices, ranger. I took the dead. And I am not giving them back."

Then she waded into the sea, strapped the bandolier tight across her chest, and dove into the black mouth of the Landfall.

I stood at the waterline with my sealed orders pressing their new teeth into my hip, and I made the choice that unmade my life: I followed her in.

Behind me, the sea kept coming. Ahead, the dark breathed.
