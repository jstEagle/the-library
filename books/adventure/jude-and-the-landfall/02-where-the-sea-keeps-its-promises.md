## 2. Where the Sea Keeps Its Promises

The desert ended the way bad marriages end — gradually, then all at once.

I'd been running for eleven days, and for eleven days the Saltreach flats had been my accomplice: white, blinding, featureless, the kind of country that forgives a thief because there's nothing in it worth stealing. I'd learned its moods. I knew where the crust would hold a horse and where it would swallow one to the girth. I knew the smell of rain three hours before it fell, and the smell of rangers two hours before that — leather and tallow and that particular scrubbed righteousness they all seemed to marinate in.

The ranger behind me smelled like all of that and something else. Something patient.

I hadn't seen him since the second day, which was worse than seeing him. A ranger you can see is a ranger you can fool. The invisible ones are the ones who've already decided where you're going and are simply waiting for you to arrive.

And I was arriving. There was nowhere else. The flats ran out at the coast the way a sentence runs out of words, and beyond it was the Sea of Vell, which the old charts marked with a single contemptuous word: *dry*. The sea had left the desert four hundred years ago, they said, walked out one tide and never walked back. The fishing towns along the old shore were monuments to the leaving — salt-cured hulls, anchor chains fused into stone, church bells green with a century of mineral weeping. Nobody lived there. Nobody but the legends, and legends don't charge rent.

I came over the last rise at dusk with my boots full of sand and my lungs full of blood, and there it was: the dead coastline, silver under a fat rising moon, stretching north and south as far as I could see. Behind me, miles back, I knew he was coming. I couldn't see him. I could feel him the way you feel a tooth you haven't decided to have pulled yet.

I stopped. There was no point pretending otherwise. The flats were behind me, the sea was in front of me, and the memory-stones were strapped against my chest in their oilcloth wrap, twelve of them, warm from my body, humming so faintly that sometimes I had to press my palm flat against them just to be sure I hadn't imagined it.

*Tell me about the sea, Judie.* Tam's voice, four years dead and still the only one I wanted to hear. *Tell me about the sea.*

He'd never seen it. Neither had I, really — not the living sea. I'd grown up a hundred miles inland in a town that smelled of tanneries, and Tam had been born with his legs wrong and his mind like a lantern in a high wind, always guttering toward the faraway. He'd wanted to be a sailor. Instead he'd been a stone-cutter's apprentice, and then a dead man, and then a set of memories pressed into riverstone by a recorder who'd charged my mother a season's wages for the privilege.

The Church called the stones abominations. The Rangers called them contraband. My mother called them *Tam*, and she was right.

I stood on the dead shore and listened to the wind come off the water, and I did the arithmetic I'd been refusing to do for eleven days. One ranger. Twelve stones. Nowhere left to run. The only question left was what I'd be holding when he caught me — the stones, or nothing.

That was when the sea began to come back.

* * *

I want to be honest about this, because I've lied to so many people for so long that honesty has become a kind of hobby: I thought I was dying, and my dying brain was decorating.

The tide came in from a sea that had no tide. It didn't roll — it *arrived*, a long low wall of black water maybe a mile out, moving up the dead flats with the unhurried confidence of something that had made an appointment and intended to keep it. No wind preceded it. No thunder. Just the water, walking inland over sand that hadn't been wet in four hundred years, and the moonlight lying on it like oil on a blade.

I should have run. I want that on the record. Any sensible person would have run inland and let the impossible thing be impossible somewhere else.

Instead I walked toward it.

Here's why, and it's a stupid reason, which is the only kind of reason that ever moves anybody: the water was coming up over the hulls of the dead ships, and as it touched them, they *sang*. Low, groaning notes, wood remembering buoyancy after four centuries of drought. And under that singing, faint as a pulse, I heard the stones answer. They'd never done that before. They hummed, yes, they murmured, but this was different — this was twelve small voices leaning toward something enormous, the way Tam used to lean toward the window when the storm fronts came through.

*The Cave of the Landfall,* my grandmother used to say, when she'd had enough cider to believe in things. *The sea keeps one promise. Once a generation, at the great tide, it comes back to the desert and opens the door it left behind. And what walks in walks out somewhere else — or doesn't walk out at all.* She'd say it like a warning. She'd also say it like an invitation, because my grandmother contained multitudes and most of them were thirsty.

I'd always filed the Cave of the Landfall with the rest of her inventory: the river under the glacier, the lights in the Halvard, the man in Corlow who was three brothers in a coat. Stories. But the sea was coming back, and the stones were singing, and I was out of sensible country.

The water reached me at the old harbor steps. It was warm — that was the detail that undid me. Not cold. *Warm*, like bathwater, like something alive, and it came around my ankles and then my knees with a gentleness that felt less like drowning and more like being collected. Ahead, where the charts said the cliffs had collapsed generations ago, the water was pouring into a gap in the rock — a black archway, half-drowned, and the sea was funneling into it with a sound like a held breath being slowly released.

The cave. The actual, impossible cave, opening its mouth exactly as advertised.

Behind me, faint but no longer invisible, a horse screamed. Not a ranger's disciplined whinny — a real scream, the sound of an animal that has decided its rider's plans are no longer its plans. I turned. Miles back, on the last of the flats, I could just make out a rider dismounting from a horse that wanted no part of the rising water, and even at that distance I could feel him doing the same arithmetic I'd done. Rider, water, cave, thief. One of these things was about to be lost.

I had maybe a minute. Maybe two.

I did the last of my arithmetic on the harbor steps with the sea at my waist. If I ran inland, he'd catch me, and the stones would go into a ranger's evidence bag, and a magistrate with clean hands would break them one by one on an anvil while my mother — no. I wasn't going to finish that sentence. Some sentences you leave unfinished the way you leave a room you can't bear.

If I swam into the cave, I would probably drown. But drowning is private. Confiscation is public. And Tam had spent his whole short life being looked at by people who saw a crippled boy instead of a blazing mind, and I had promised him — I had *promised* — that the world would hear what he actually sounded like. Not the sanitized hour the recorder sold my mother. All of it. The jokes. The swearing. The night he told me he wasn't afraid to die but he was afraid of being *forgotten wrong*.

I'd rather drown than surrender his voice. I want that understood. Whatever else I am — thief, liar, the woman who walked backward across eleven days of desert to erase her own prints — I am a person who keeps the promises that matter.

I cinched the oilcloth wrap tight, checked the straps one last time — twelve stones against my sternum, humming like a hive of very small bees — and dove.

* * *

The water took me the way sleep takes a child: completely, and without negotiation.

I'd like to tell you I swam like one of Tam's heroes, all clean strokes and held breath. The truth is I went under the arch and the current got me immediately, a warm muscular hand that flipped me end over end and shot me through blackness while I clawed at nothing and the stones pressed against my chest like twelve small hearts all beating at once. I don't know how long. Ten seconds. A year. There was a moment — I'll swear to this in any court, divine or otherwise — when I heard Tam laugh. Not the recorded laugh, the careful one the recorder had coaxed out of him. His *real* laugh, the ugly snorting one, the one he did when I fell off the roof that time, and it sounded like it was coming from everywhere, from the water itself, from the cave's throat.

Then the current spat me out.

I came up coughing in air that tasted of stone and cold and something older — mineral and deep, like the inside of a bell. I was on a shelf of wet rock in a darkness so total it had texture, and around me the water was settling into a long black pool that stretched back the way I'd come, glowing faintly, faintly, with a light that had no source I could find. The sea's voice was behind me, muffled now, still filling the entrance I'd come through. And ahead —

Ahead, the dark went *up*. I could feel it the way you feel a cathedral. Air moving in vast slow volumes. Water dripping somewhere with the patience of geology.

I got the lantern out of my pack — soaked, but the wick caught, because I'd waterproofed it in Corlow with tallow and paranoia, and paranoia, I'm pleased to report, pays its debts. The light went out across the rock and I saw what I'd swum into.

The cave was not a cave. Caves are holes. This was a *hall*, ribbed with stone like the inside of some enormous throat, and the walls were covered in carvings — thousands of them, spirals and rivers and little marching figures, worn smooth by hands that had touched them across centuries. And at the hall's far end, where the lantern light gave up, the black pool narrowed into a river, and the river ran *inward*. Away from the sea. Into the dark, under the mountains, in the direction — I felt this before I knew it, the way you feel a name on the tip of your tongue — of the Halvard Glacier. Of the source. Of the place every legend agrees you don't go.

The stones were humming. Not faintly anymore. Audibly. Twelve riverstones against my chest, singing in a chord I didn't know, and when I pulled one out — Tam's, the first one, the one I'd know blind — the hum climbed to something almost like speech.

Behind me, out in the drowned entrance, the sea's voice changed. Lower. Receding. The great tide, having kept its promise, was already thinking about leaving.

And with it — I understood this with a clarity that felt like falling — the entrance would go too. The collapse my grandmother's story always ended with. *The sea opens the door, the sea closes the door, and what's inside stays inside.*

I was still working out what that meant for me when I heard the water at the entrance churn — a body coming through, fighting the current, and a gasp of human breath where no human breath was supposed to be.

The ranger. Of course the ranger. Eleven days of patience, and he'd followed me into a drowning legend rather than lose me.

The lantern light reached the pool's edge just as a hand did — gloved, gripping the rock, hauling up a man in a soaked ranger's coat who came up coughing and furious and alive, and whose face, when he finally lifted it to the light, went through surprise, recognition, and something that looked almost like relief before it settled into duty.

He knew me. Which was bad.

But the sea was leaving, the entrance was groaning like a ship going down, and the stones were singing a chord that included, unmistakably, my brother's laugh.

Which was worse.
