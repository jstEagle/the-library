## 3. Admission Charged in Legends

I want it on the record — my record, the one I keep in my head because paper burns and rangers are supposed to be incorruptible — that I hesitated at the mouth of the Cave of the Landfall for a full four seconds. Four seconds is a long time when the sea is standing up on its hind legs and the desert behind you is becoming an archipelago. Four seconds is the difference between a ranger and a drowned man who used to be one.

Then I went in after her, because that's the trouble with me. I always go in after her.

The cave took me the way a throat takes a pill: reluctantly, with a great deal of wet noise, and with the clear implication that something worse was happening further down. I swam the first stretch — twenty yards of churning green-black water that tasted of salt and old stone — and then my boots found a shelf of rock rising out of the flood, and I hauled myself up onto it and lay there like a landed fish while the tide roared past me into the dark.

The lantern. I should say something about the lantern. Every ranger carries one — whale oil, brass-cased, rated for a hundred hours of honest dark — and mine was strapped under my coat, dry, thank whatever gods look after fools. I got it lit with hands that shook more than I'd have liked, and the flame stood up in the wind off the water and showed me the cave.

It was not a cave. Caves are holes. This was a hallway. A hallway built by something with ambitions — the ceiling arched forty feet overhead, ribbed with pale stone, and the river that had flooded in from the sea ran down its center like a carpet rolled out for a guest nobody wanted. The water was already dropping. I could see the line of it retreating along the walls, leaving a dark tide-mark like the ring a cup leaves on a table.

And there, thirty yards downstream, crouched against the wall like a thief caught mid-larceny — which, to be fair — was Jude Vane.

She had a knife out. Of course she had a knife out. I raised my hands, which was a lie of a gesture, since one of them held a lantern and the other could have had a pistol in it if I'd been the sort of ranger who shot first.

"Evening," I said.

"It's morning," she said.

"It's the end of the world out there. I don't think it has a time of day anymore."

She didn't lower the knife. I didn't lower my hands. We stood like that for a while, two people performing a play neither of us believed in, while the sea finished its impossible business and the water in the cave sank and sank and finally went quiet, pooling somewhere below us with a sound like a held breath.

Then the mountain behind us cleared its throat.

I've heard rockfalls. I've heard avalanches. I've heard the Saltreach flats split open in a drought year with a crack you could hear from the coast road. This was none of those. This was the sound of several hundred tons of the cliff face deciding, all at once, with the sea's help, that the entrance to the Landfall had been open long enough. The noise went through the floor and up my spine and out through the top of my skull, and dust came rolling down the hallway like a gray ghost looking for its body, and when it cleared and I got the lantern up and pointed, there was no door.

There was no door. There was a wall of tumbled stone where the sea had come in, packed tight, already weeping water through the gaps in thin silver threads.

Jude Vane said a word I won't repeat, though I'll admit I was thinking it in a more formal register.

"We're sealed in," she said.

"Observation noted," I said. "Award pending."

* * *

Here is what I knew about the Cave of the Landfall before tonight, which is to say here is what every ranger knows, because we are taught it in our first year along with how to read stars and how to tell which cactus is lying: the Landfall is a legend. It appears when the sea comes inland — once in a generation, once in a lifetime, the stories disagree because stories are lazy — and it runs under the mountains to the source of the river, the true source, the forbidden one beneath the Halvard Glacier. Rangers are forbidden from entering it. Not discouraged. Forbidden. The penalty is struck from the rolls, which in ranger law means they don't even write down what happens to you, which in ranger law means it's bad.

I had always assumed the legend was the desert's way of explaining the tide. Deserts do that. Give people a mystery and they'll build a cathedral on it inside a week.

Now I was standing in the cathedral, holding the candle, and the thief who'd led me here was looking at me like I was the mystery.

"You knew it was real," she said. "You followed me in anyway."

"You knew it was real," I said. "You dove in anyway."

"Touché," she said, in an accent that said she'd learned the word from a book and was proud of it.

We found a dry shelf of rock above the flood line and sat down on opposite ends of it, the lantern between us like a border checkpoint. She still had the knife. I still had my hands where she could see them. Somewhere below us the river ran on in the dark, patient as a debt.

I took stock. I'm good at taking stock; it's most of the job. She was soaked to the bone and shivering in the way that means the shivering is doing its work, which was good — shivering is the body keeping its own books. She had the knife, a coil of rope at her hip, a waterskin, and something strapped flat against her chest under her coat, wrapped in oilcloth, which she'd put her arm over the moment she sat down, the way a woman puts her arm over a sleeping child.

The stones. It had to be the stones. Three of them, the broker in Saltreach had said, memory-stones out of the old country, worth more than the town. I'd assumed greed. Greed I understood. Greed I could chase.

"Your brother," I said.

Her head came up fast. Too fast. "What about him."

"The stones. Everyone says you stole them for the money, but you swim like a woman carrying an infant, not a fortune. And you keep them against your chest, not in your pack. And when the tide came you looked at the water like it owed you something." I turned the lantern a little, so the light was softer. "So. What about him."

For a long moment I thought she'd give me the knife instead of an answer. Then she did something that frightened me more than the rockfall had.

She unwrapped the oilcloth.

There were three stones in her lap, each the size of a fist, gray-blue, veined with lines that caught the lantern light and moved in it — not reflected it, *moved* in it, the way water moves. And the cave, which had been quiet except for the river, went quiet in a different way, the way a room goes quiet when someone starts to sing.

They were humming. I could feel it in my back teeth.

"They record," she said. Her voice had changed — flattened out, careful, the voice you use walking on ice. "The old ones knew how. A person speaks into the stone, or lives near it, and it takes them down. Everything. Not just words. The way they laughed. The way they were afraid." She picked one up and held it in both hands, and I swear the humming got warmer. "My brother Tam died two winters ago. Fever. He was seventeen." Her thumb moved over the stone's veins. "This one is his last year. All of it. Every day I could get him to sit still."

I didn't say anything. There wasn't anything that would have improved the silence.

"The guild that owned them wanted them back after he died. Back to the vault, back to the catalog, back to being *inventory*." The word came out of her like a splinter. "They said the stones don't grieve, they just store. They said I could visit. By appointment." She laughed, one dry note with no floor under it. "So I stole my brother. All of him. And now every ranger and every guild-man in the territory wants to drag him back to the vault, and I'd drown the whole coast before I let them."

The lantern hissed. The river ran. I sat there in a drowned cathedral under a mountain with a thief and her dead brother, and I did the arithmetic every ranger dreads, which is the arithmetic where the criminal comes out ahead.

"Why tell me?" I said finally. "I'm the man chasing you. Telling me this is handing me the noose."

"Is it?" She looked at me across the lantern, and her eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely steady. "You followed me into a legend with no rope and no backup. Either you're the worst ranger in the territory, or you're not really chasing me at all. I've been watching you for two days, Brightwater. You had a hundred chances to shoot. You keep *not* shooting. So either you're a coward, or you're a liar, and cowards don't swim into collapsing caves."

"I could be both," I offered.

"Everybody's both." She rewrapped the stones with a gentleness that undid me a little, I'll confess it. "That's not the point. The point is the small lies. You've been lying to me about small things since Saltreach. You said you lost my trail at the ford — you didn't, you circled east because you didn't want me to see you. You said you were out of tobacco — you've got a pouch in your left coat pocket, I can smell it. And I've been lying to you too. I said I was alone. I said I knew nothing about the cave. I said I didn't know your name before you gave it."

I went very still. "You knew my name."

"Everyone out here knows Sebastian Brightwater." She almost smiled. It wasn't a kind smile, but it wasn't a cruel one either; it was the smile of a person laying cards on a table. "The ranger who walked out of the Halvard country alone. The one whose family the river took. I knew your name, your record, and your reputation for getting lost on purpose." She tucked the bundle back against her chest. "So here's my offer, ranger. A truce. Not friendship — I'm not stupid, and neither are you. A truce. We stop lying about the small things. Where we're going, what we know, what we're afraid of. The big things we can keep, everybody keeps the big things, but the small lies are what get you killed in the dark, and I think it's going to be dark for a while."

I should have said no. I want that understood too. I had sealed orders in my coat — sealed, unread, burning a hole through the oilskin and my conscience alike — and a ranger with sealed orders doesn't make pacts with thieves in forbidden caves. A ranger with sealed orders gets her out through whatever route the sea left open and delivers her and her brother to the people who catalog the dead.

"The river," I said instead. "It runs upstream. I could hear it under us when we came in — running *inward*, toward the mountain. Toward the glacier."

Her eyes narrowed. "You can tell that from a sound?"

"I grew up on that river." I heard myself say it and felt the small lie die in my throat, exactly as advertised. "The Halvard country. Before the river took my family, as you so kindly put it. I know its voice. This is it. Which means the legend's true, which means there's a way out of here, and it runs through country rangers are forbidden to enter." I held out my hand across the lantern, across the border. "No small lies. Starting now."

She looked at my hand for a long three seconds — she was good with three seconds, this woman — and then she took it. Her grip was river-cold and strong as a lock.

"One condition," she said. "When we get to wherever this goes — you tell me what's in those sealed orders you've been sitting on since Saltreach. I've seen the way you touch your coat. Like it's a wound."

The lantern flame leaned, as if the cave itself had inhaled.

"Deal," I said, and we shook on it, two liars in the dark with one truth between us and a river running toward the ice.

Above us, through a hundred fathoms of stone, the sea was withdrawing from a desert it had no business touching — and somewhere far upstream, in the blue dark under the glacier, something old heard us coming and began, very softly, to hum.
