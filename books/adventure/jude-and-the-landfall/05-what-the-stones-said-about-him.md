## 5. What the Stones Said About Him

The river in the dark made a sound like someone whispering your name just badly enough that you couldn't be sure.

I'd been listening to it for two days. Two days of walking the flooded shelf beside Sebastian Thornbury, ranger of the Saltreach, professional frown, owner of exactly one expression and a spare. He walked ahead with his lantern held low, the way rangers do — light for the ground, never for the sky, because out on the flats the sky was where the weather kept its knives. Down here there was no sky at all. Just the cave's black ribs going up forever, and the river running beside us like it had somewhere better to be.

"You're limping," he said, without turning around.

"I'm walking with character."

"Your left boot's soaked through. You've been favoring it since the second bend."

"I've been favoring it," I said, "because I'm saving my good limp for when it matters."

He didn't laugh. He never laughed, which I'd decided was either discipline or damage, and I hadn't yet worked out which was sadder. He did, however, stop, and hold out his hand for my pack without being asked about it, which was worse. Kindness from a man hunting you is a kind of arithmetic you can't check.

I gave him the pack. My feet were past pride.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about carrying the dead: they get heavy in shifts.

The memory-stones rode against my chest in their oilcloth wrap, six of them, each no bigger than a fist, each warm in a way stones have no business being warm. Tam's stones. My brother's whole recorded life, or the part of it the stone-cutters of Vane Hollow had managed to catch before the fever took him — his voice, his laugh, the day he taught me to swim in the irrigation channels and nearly drowned us both doing it. The guild wanted them melted down for the war effort, which is what the guild wants everything melted down for, eventually. The rangers wanted them destroyed because unlicensed recording is contraband, and contraband of grief is apparently the worst kind.

And me? I wanted them because they were all that was left of him, and I have never once pretended my motives were more complicated than that.

They hummed sometimes. Faintly, through the oilcloth, against my sternum. The stone-cutters say that's just resonance, the crystal remembering how to be touched. I say it's Tam, complaining about the accommodations.

That night we camped in a hollow where the river widened into a pool so still it reflected the lantern like a second lantern, one burning underwater. Sebastian rationed out dried fish and hardtack with the grim precision of a man who'd once had to feed a garrison on less. He ate facing the water. I ate facing him, because I liked watching him pretend not to notice.

"Tell me something true," I said.

"Why?"

"Because we're sealed in a hole in the world, following a river every law you enforce says is forbidden, and you haven't arrested me, and I haven't run. That's already a lie we're both telling. We might as well stop lying about the small things."

He chewed. Swallowed. Looked at me across the lantern with those flat grey eyes that gave away nothing except, occasionally, everything.

"My boots are two sizes too large," he said. "My father's. I've never told anyone that."

"That's it? That's your true thing?"

"You asked for small things." A pause. "It took me four years to stop walking like him."

And then he banked the lantern down to a coal-glow and closed his eyes, and I lay there in the dark with six dead voices humming against my heart, thinking: *there it is again.* The crack. The hairline fracture in the ranger. He kept showing them to me by accident, and I kept deciding not to use them, and I hadn't yet asked myself why.

* * *

It happened on the third morning, and it happened because of my own stupidity, which is the only honest way anything ever happens.

We'd come to a place where the shelf narrowed and the river ran fast beneath an overhang of wet stone, and Sebastian went first, testing holds, calling back the good ones in that clipped ranger cadence — *left foot high, trust the seam, don't look down at the water.* I followed with the stones strapped tight, both hands free, doing everything right until I did one thing wrong: I reached for a hold with my elbow instead of my hand, the pack swung, and the oilcloth knot — which I'd tied in a hurry three weeks ago in a barn outside Dunmarrow while the guild's finders burned lamps in the yard — came loose.

A stone slipped out. Bounced once off my knee. And dropped toward the black water.

I didn't think. Thinking is for people with time. I threw myself flat along the ledge and caught it against the rock with the heel of my hand, one inch from the current, my whole weight hanging off the overhang by the other arm — and the impact woke it.

Stones play on touch. Everyone knows that. What people forget is that a hard strike plays them *loud*, full voice, no wrapping of whisper-volume, and this one struck hard.

Tam's voice filled the cave.

Except it wasn't Tam.

It was a woman. Young, maybe twenty, with the particular bright authority of someone who has never once doubted she'll be obeyed — and underneath her voice, fainter, a second voice answering hers, and I knew that second voice. I'd been listening to it clip its consonants at me for three days.

*"—the water's higher than the charts,"* the woman said. *"Say it back to me, Brightwater."*

*"Higher than the charts. Maerin, if the survey's wrong—"*

*"The survey isn't wrong. I am. I mapped this reach myself when I was nineteen and I cut a corner at the third bend because it was raining. Everything downstream of my laziness is fiction. Isn't that wonderful?"*

She laughed. It was a real laugh, unguarded, the laugh of someone who has not yet learned what her signature will cost. And the younger Sebastian — fifteen, sixteen, his voice still cracking on its edges — laughed with her, and there was such easy love in it, the love of a boy for the older girl who took him along on surveys because everyone else said he was too young, that it went through me like a fishhook.

*"Play it back,"* the boy said. *"Play the source again. I want to hear it."*

*"You've heard it nine times."*

*"Play it anyway."*

A pause. Then another sound rose out of the stone — deeper, stranger, a vast slow chord like a cathedral clearing its throat, water being born somewhere enormous, and even filtered through crystal and years it raised the hair on my arms. The source. The actual forbidden source, recorded, sitting in a stone that had been riding against my chest for three weeks while I slept.

*"When I make Commander,"* the woman said, quieter now, *"they'll order this reach sealed. You know that. The source stays closed, the recordings stay buried, and everyone pretends the river starts nowhere."*

*"Will you do it?"*

*"I'll do worse. I'll believe it's right."* Another pause, longer. *"That's why I brought you here, Seb. Someone should remember it was beautiful. Someone who isn't me."*

The stone clicked off. Struck twice, always plays twice — old cutting flaw, Tam used to complain about it — and the second playing was only half a breath of that vast chord before my palm smothered it silent.

Sebastian stood above me on the ledge, motionless, lantern raised, and his face in that light was the most open I would ever see it: white, stricken, ten years younger and drowning in it.

I climbed up. Picked up the stone. Wrapped it, knotted the oilcloth properly this time, double knot, the way Tam taught me. Tucked it back against my chest.

And said nothing.

* * *

I want to be clear about what I understood in that moment, because I understood it completely, and understanding it changed nothing about what I chose to do with it.

The woman in the stone was Maerin Halvard. Ranger-Commander Maerin Halvard, who signed orders with a left-slanted hand and a wax seal pressed slightly off-center because she was always in a hurry. I knew her seal. I'd seen it on the guild's warrant for the stones — *destruction authorized, bearer to be taken alive,* signed above the name of the most powerful ranger in the Saltreach.

And the boy in the stone — the boy who begged her to *play it again*, who she loved enough to smuggle to a forbidden river, who she trusted with the one beautiful secret of her iron life — was Sebastian Brightwater, aged perhaps sixteen, standing on this exact shelf, beside this exact water, hearing the source sing.

His loyalty wasn't bought with gold. It was bought with *that*. With a childhood afternoon. With a woman who showed him the most beautiful forbidden thing in the world and then spent twenty years building the machine that buried it — and who had, somewhere in that machine, a sealed packet with his name on it, orders he carried unread because she asked him to carry them unread, because she knew — oh, she knew — that if he read them he might start remembering.

I could have used it. Right there. I could have said, *I know whose voice that was. I know what you're carrying in your breast pocket, ranger, and I know why you won't open it.* I could have turned his crack into a breach and walked him up the river like a prisoner of his own past.

Instead I sat down on the cold stone, retied a bootlace that didn't need tying, and said nothing.

Not mercy. Don't flatter me; I've never been merciful. It was this: a thief knows a hidden thing when she sees one, and the last thing you do with a hidden thing is announce it. You let it ripen. You wait for the moment it's worth more as truth than as leverage.

Also — and this is the part I'd deny under oath — watching his face when the stone played, I hadn't seen an enemy caught out. I'd seen a man hearing his own ghost, and it had looked exactly like me, every night, listening to Tam hum through the oilcloth.

We were carrying the same weight. His was just folded smaller.

* * *

We walked the rest of that day without speaking, which was normal, but the silence had changed key. He kept glancing at my chest where the stones rode. I kept glancing at his breast pocket, where the sealed orders made a rectangle in the cloth, patient as a knife in a drawer.

Toward dusk the cave began to change. The air came colder and moved — actually *moved*, a current, the first breath of wind we'd felt in three days, and it smelled of ice. Old ice. The smell of a winter that had been waiting underground since before either of our grandmothers was born.

Sebastian stopped. Held up a hand. Ahead, far down the passage, the darkness wasn't dark anymore — it was blue. A deep, living blue, the color of deep lake ice, glowing faintly off walls that were no longer stone.

"What is that?" I whispered.

He was quiet long enough that I thought he wouldn't answer. When he did, his voice had gone strange — the voice of the boy in the stone, almost, thirty years late arriving.

"The glacier," he said. "The river runs under the Halvard. And Jude—" he turned, and in the blue light his face was unreadable again, but only barely, only just, "—it's calving season. The mouth opens when the ice shears. Days, not weeks. If we're going to be at the source when it does—"

He didn't finish. Somewhere ahead of us, deep in the blue, something boomed. Once. Like a door being tested.

Like something enormous, deciding whether to let us in.
