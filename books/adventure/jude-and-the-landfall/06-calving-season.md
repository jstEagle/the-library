## 6. Calving Season

### Sebastian

The river had been carrying us toward the glacier for two days now, and I had stopped pretending I didn't know where it was going.

That was the trouble with childhood knowledge. It doesn't ask your permission. You can bury a thing under twenty years of ranger law and sealed orders and the careful blankness of a professional face, and then some woman walks backward across a salt flat and makes you follow her into a drowned cave, and suddenly you're navigating by the sound of water against stone that you last heard when you were nine years old and your sister was still alive.

"The current's picking up," Jude said behind me. She'd stopped asking where we were going somewhere around yesterday's noon meal. Not because she trusted me — I want to be clear about that. Because she'd done her own arithmetic and decided the river knew better than either of us.

"It always picks up here," I said. "We're close."

"Close to what?"

I didn't answer. Some answers you have to see to believe, and I wasn't sure I believed this one yet myself.

The cave walls had been narrowing for hours, then widening again in a way that felt less like geology and more like a held breath. The air changed too — colder, wetter, tasting of iron and old winters. And underneath everything, a sound I hadn't heard since I was a boy standing on my father's shoulders at the summer festival: a deep, rhythmic groaning, like the world clearing its throat before saying something terrible.

"What is that?" Jude whispered.

"That," I said, "is calving season."

* * *

### Jude

I want it noted, for whatever record survives us, that I did not scream when the glacier spoke.

Everyone screams the first time. Sebastian told me so later, with the smugness of a man who has catalogued other people's terror into a tidy field manual. But he wasn't there for my first time. He was ten feet ahead of me with the lantern, and when the ice ahead of us cracked open like the sky splitting, I grabbed the back of his coat and made a noise I will deny until my dying day.

The passage opened without warning into a cavern so large the lantern light simply gave up. The river spread out flat and black beneath us, and ahead — filling the entire far wall of the world — stood the mouth of the Halvard Glacier.

I have seen cathedrals. I have seen the Saltreach sunsets that make grown men weep into their beer. Neither prepared me for a wall of blue ice three hundred feet high, lit from within by some chemistry of pressure and age, glowing like a lampshade over the corpse of a god.

"It's beautiful," I said, because it was, and because I am constitutionally incapable of shutting up in moments of genuine awe.

"It's a deadline," Sebastian said.

He crouched at the water's edge, listening. That was another thing about him — he listened the way other men talked, constantly and with total commitment. "Calving's started early. We have days to get through the ice fields before the route closes. Maybe four. Five if we're lucky."

"And if we're not lucky?"

"Then the glacier seals itself and we spend the rest of our lives in a very well-lit tomb." He glanced back at me, and there was something almost gentle under the dryness. "You still could've taken the tide entrance."

"The tide entrance collapsed on top of us."

"Yes. Well." He turned back to the water. "Days, not weeks. Keep up."

* * *

### Sebastian

Here is what ranger law says about the Halvard approach, in the exact words of the statute I memorized at nineteen: *No civilian shall be guided past the Landfall line, by any ranger, under any circumstance, upon penalty of stripping of rank and remanding to civil authority.*

Here is what I was doing: guiding a civilian past the Landfall line.

I'd like to report that I agonized over this. The truth is more embarrassing. Somewhere between the second day of her walking backward and the third day of her asking questions I couldn't answer without lying, I had stopped thinking of Jude Vane as a thief and started thinking of her as the only person who'd ever followed me somewhere worth going. Ranger law didn't have a statute for that. I checked.

The ice shelf began a half mile from the glacier's face — a plain of ancient sea ice, hummocked and blue-veined, through which the river ran in channels that opened and closed with the tides. In my father's stories it was called the Walkway, and children were warned off it with tales of the Hungry Water. My sister Lark used to dare boys to walk out onto it. She never went herself. She said she liked watching people be brave more than being brave, which I understood much better after she drowned.

"We cross single file," I told Jude. "Thirty feet apart. If the ice starts talking, stop moving."

"Ice talks?"

"You'll know it when you hear it."

It talked within the hour. A low chattering, like teeth, running out ahead of us through the shelf — the sound of internal fractures propagating. I froze. Behind me, thirty feet back, Jude froze too, and I remember feeling something absurdly like pride. Two days ago she'd been a set of backward footprints. Now she read ice.

Then the shelf moved.

Not cracked — *moved*, a slow tilting, the whole plain shrugging as something vast shifted beneath it. The channel beside us yawned wider. And the ice under Jude's feet, the older, thinner floe she'd chosen because it looked smoother, tilted like a seesaw and slid her toward the open water.

She didn't scream. She did something worse — she went quiet, arms out, the memory-stones strapped against her chest, and I watched her calculate, in the space of a heartbeat, whether diving for the pack or staying on the slide gave her better odds.

Neither did. The water under the shelf is forty degrees and black and full of grinding brash ice, and a person goes under it in seconds and stays there.

I did the thing I was ordered never to do.

There's a technique — rangers call it anchor-and-line, though there's no line involved, which tells you how honest ranger naming conventions are. You throw yourself flat, distribute your weight, and let yourself slide faster than the person you're saving, angling to intercept. Then you take their wrist at the moment of transfer — not their hand, their *wrist*, the bones don't slip — and you roll, both of you, using their momentum against yours, onto the thicker ice beyond. It looks like madness. It works about six times in ten. The other four times you die together, which the manual describes, with typical ranger cheerfulness, as "acceptable mutual loss."

I slid. I caught her wrist. We rolled.

For a moment afterward we just lay there on the blue ice, breathing like bellows, her pulse hammering against my fingers where I still gripped her wrist because letting go seemed structurally unwise.

"That," she said finally, "was not in any legend I've heard."

"It's not in any legend. It's in a training manual I'm forbidden to share with outsiders."

"I'm an outsider?"

"You're a wanted criminal."

"Ah." A pause. "And you just broke federal technique law to save a wanted criminal."

"I'm aware of the irony. I've been aware of the irony since the salt flats."

She sat up, shook the frost out of her hair, and looked at me with an expression I couldn't file anywhere — not gratitude exactly, not suspicion, something newer than both. "Why?" she asked. Just that. No accusation in it. A real question.

Because you walk backward to protect something worth protecting. Because you dove into a flooded cave rather than give up your brother's voice. Because I have spent twenty years being the man who follows orders and I am so tired, Jude, I am tired down to the bedrock.

"Because the water's cold," I said.

She laughed — actually laughed, out here on the killing ice with the glacier groaning overhead — and I understood that she'd heard every word I didn't say. That's the trouble with thieves. They're trained to notice what people hide.

* * *

### Jude

He taught me anchor-and-line that night, or what passed for night in a cavern lit by a glowing wall of ice. Made me practice the roll on solid ground until I could do it half-asleep. "Wrist, not hand," he kept saying. "The bones don't lie."

"You're supposed to arrest me," I reminded him, somewhere around the tenth repetition.

"I'm aware."

"You're teaching a fugitive a classified rescue technique."

"I'm aware of that too." He watched me roll, corrected my elbow. "You're also the person most likely to need it, given your gift for choosing thin ice."

I should have been afraid of him by then. Everything I knew about rangers said fear was the correct response — they were the long arm of a government that had outlawed the stones, that had burned my brother's name off the registry, that called grief *contraband*. But here was the thing I kept circling and couldn't land: Sebastian Brightwater broke his own rules the way other men broke wind. Quietly, apologetically, and with visible relief.

And there was something else. When he thought I wasn't watching, he touched the ice. Just briefly, palm flat, the way you'd touch a grave marker. Like he'd been here before. Like the glacier owed him something, or he owed it.

I had a stone in my pack that might have answered that question. I didn't ask. Some debts you let people pay in their own time.

The calving came at what I decided to call morning, because a woman needs her rituals. The glacier's face — that impossible blue cathedral wall — shuddered, boomed like a fleet of cannons, and shed a chunk of itself the size of a village into the black water. The wave it threw across the shelf lifted us both off our feet, and Sebastian hauled me uphill by the collar while icebergs the size of houses tumbled and rolled around us like drunk giants.

When the water settled, he pointed through the smoke and spray to where the shelf lay broken open — a corridor of new ice leading straight toward the glacier's throat.

"There," he said. "Our window. Three days, maybe."

Beyond him, far down the corridor, the light shifted — and for one strange second I would have sworn I saw movement. Figures, small and dark, moving fast along the far ice.

Sebastian saw them too. I watched his whole body change, the way a door changes when the lock turns.

"No," he said, to himself, to them, to the glacier. "They can't have—"

"Who?" I asked. "Sebastian. Who is that?"

He didn't answer. But his hand had gone, without his noticing, to the sealed oilskin packet strapped inside his coat — the one he thought I didn't know about, the one he touched in his sleep — and I understood that whatever was coming through that corridor had come for both of us, and that he'd known it might, all along.

The glacier groaned overhead, patient as a judge.

Three days, he'd said. Suddenly it felt like three minutes.
