## 7. Orders, Sealed and Burning

The storm found us an hour after we'd stopped looking for shelter, which is how storms prefer to operate. One moment the river canyon was merely cold and hostile; the next it was a white throat swallowing itself, wind screaming down the ice like something with a grievance, and the temperature dropped so fast my teeth ached in sympathy.

"There," Jude shouted, pointing at a blue shadow in the canyon wall — a hollow where the glacier had melted itself a pocket and then thought better of it. We crawled in with our packs held over our heads like cowards, and I mean that as a compliment. Cowards live.

The hollow was maybe ten feet deep, floored with old meltwater gravel, its ceiling ribbed like the inside of a shell. When we'd got the little stove lit behind a windbreak of packs and Jude had wrung out her gloves with the grim efficiency of someone who has been wet before and did not care for it, we sat in the blue light and listened to the storm try to take the mountain apart.

It was, I reflected, as good a place as any to commit treason.

I'd known it was coming for two days. Maybe longer, if I'm honest — maybe since the cave, since the tide sealed us in together and she showed me the stones humming against her chest like a nest of sleeping birds. A ranger doesn't get to un-know things. That's the whole trouble with the profession. You're paid to notice, and then you're required to pretend you didn't.

The orders rode in the inner pocket of my coat, against my left ribs, where they'd ridden for six weeks. Sealed with grey wax, stamped with the Halvard antler-and-star, addressed to *Ranger S. Brightwater, by hand only.* I'd carried them through the Saltreach flats, through the flood, through the cave, up the river that remembers upstream. I'd slept with them. I'd eaten with them. They had become, through sheer proximity, a kind of organ — one I'd have missed if cut out, and one that had been quietly poisoning me the whole time.

"You keep touching your side," Jude said. She didn't look up from the stove. "Every time you think I'm not watching."

"I'm watching you not watch me. It's a demanding discipline."

"Mm." She fed a pinch of fuel to the flame. The light made her face look carved and younger than it was. "You've been carrying something in there since before the cave. Paper. Small. Flat. You check it the way other men check a wound."

I said nothing, which is a thing I do well.

"Ranger orders?" she asked, still not looking at me.

"Could be a love letter."

"From you? To whom?"

"That's the spirit of inquiry that made Saltreach what it is today."

She almost smiled. Almost. Then the storm hit a new register — a long descending howl, like the whole glacier clearing its throat — and the smile went wherever smiles go when the world reminds you it's bigger than you. In the silence after, she said quietly: "If it says what I think it says, you should read it. Out here. Where I can hear it too."

"Why would I do that?"

"Because you've already decided what it says. And because a man who won't read his own orders is a man who's already broken them in his heart, and hearts are worse liars than paper." She looked at me then, straight on, those flat grey eyes that had watched me across three hundred miles of salt. "Read it, Sebastian. Whatever it costs, it costs less than carrying it another day."

* * *

Here is what I will say about treason: it does not arrive like a thunderclap. It arrives like thaw. Slowly, invisibly, water finding the cracks in things you believed were solid, until one morning the whole face of the ice comes down and everyone acts surprised.

I took out the packet. My hands were steady, which annoyed me; I'd have preferred shaking, some theatrical evidence of struggle. But my hands were ranger's hands, trained to be steady while everything else goes wrong, and so they simply worked the wax loose the way they'd worked a hundred seals before, and the folded paper came free smelling faintly of lamp oil and the Commander's office — cedar smoke and old ink, the smell of authority, which I had loved once the way you love a house you grew up in without ever choosing it.

Jude moved closer. Not touching. Just close enough that the same lantern-light fell on both of us, which felt, absurdly, like a vow.

I unfolded the orders and read aloud, because she'd asked and because reading silently would have been a mercy to myself and I was done being merciful to myself.

*By order of Commander Elin Vane-Sorrel, Halvard Ranger Command:*

*One. The relics designated memory-stones, numbering eleven, are to be destroyed by fire or deep burial upon recovery. They are not to be played. They are not to be studied. Destruction is to be witnessed by no party outside the Command.*

*Two. The fugitive Jude Vane is to be taken alive and returned to Halvard for judgment.*

*Three. The source of the river beneath the Halvard Glacier is to be permanently sealed by whatever means necessary, including collapse of the calving route, prior to end of season. No record of the route is to survive. No ranger who has traveled it is to be reassigned within the territory.*

*Four. This order supersedes all others, including the Ranger's Oath, sections two through nine.*

*Signed under seal, E.V.-S.*

The storm filled the pause. I read it twice more, silently, because the fourth line kept sliding off my eye like water off wax. *Supersedes the Ranger's Oath.* There are nine sections to the Oath. Sections two through nine include *protect the innocent*, *speak truth to power*, and *let no command make you a weapon against the people you serve*. What remains when you strike those is section one: *obey.* Just obey, naked and alone, like a bone picked clean.

"Vane," Jude said. Her voice was very quiet. "Elin Vane-Sorrel. That's—"

"My commander. Yes."

"No." She was shaking her head slowly. "That's my aunt. My father's sister. The woman who raised me after—" She stopped. Started again, smaller. "She signed orders to destroy Tam's voice."

And there it was, the thing I had half-known and never let myself know whole. The stones weren't contraband in the abstract. They were her brother — the dead brother whose recorded memories she'd carried strapped to her chest through desert and flood and ice — and the woman who wanted them burned shared her blood. Family destroying family's dead. It was almost traditional. Half the wars in history were just that, with better uniforms.

"It gets worse," I said. "Section three. Seal the source. Collapse the route." I tapped the paper. "That's not about the stones. That's about making sure nobody can ever go back and ask why."

"Ask why what?"

I looked at the fourth line again. *No ranger who has traveled it is to be reassigned within the territory.* Reassigned. That was the polite word. I'd been a ranger twenty years; I knew what happened to rangers who became inconvenient, and it wasn't reassignment.

But it was the third item that finally turned my stomach, and not for the reason you'd expect. *Sealed by whatever means necessary.* I knew these canyons. I'd known them before I had words for knowing them — before the academy, before the oath, back when a boy named Sebastian Brightwater had a sister named Wren and a summer habit of following the meltwater up into the blue dark. The route we'd climbed was passable now only because it was calving season, because the glacier shrugs its shoulders open for a few weeks a year like a man loosening his collar. To seal it permanently meant charges, meant bringing down tons of ice into the river's birthplace, meant—

Meant Wren. Because whatever else the source was, it was the last place my sister had been alive, and the Commander knew it, and she'd handed the demolition orders to me specifically. Not despite that. *Because* of it. Who better to bury your ghosts than the man who shares them?

"She knows about Wren," I said. My voice came out flat, factual, a ranger's report delivered from the bottom of a well. "She was there. At the river. Before."

Jude went very still. "Before what?"

"Before Wren drowned." I heard myself say it the way you hear a stranger talk — clearly, at last, after years of mumbling. "Twenty-two years ago. I was fourteen. We came up the same route, her and me, in a summer when the calving ran late. And something happened at the source that I have spent my entire adult life not thinking about, and Elin Vane-Sorrel was there, and she signed these orders knowing that sealing it would mean sending me to stand in the exact spot where—" I stopped. Folded the paper along its old creases. Unfolded it again, as though the words might rearrange themselves out of mercy. They didn't.

"The stones," Jude said softly. "Eleven stones. Whose memories are on them, do you think? Why does a Commander want eleven voices burned?"

"I don't know."

"Yes, you do."

I did. Or I could guess, and the guess had been sitting in my chest since the cave like swallowed riverwater. Eleven stones. A woman who signed away her own niece's brother's voice. A woman who'd stood at the source twenty-two years ago and walked away and built a wall of law around the place. You don't seal a door that hard unless you're afraid of what knocks.

"Wren might be one of them," I said. "There might be a stone with my sister's voice on it. And Elin wants it buried before anyone — before *I* —"

I couldn't finish. The storm finished for me, a crack of shifting ice somewhere above that sent grit pattering down onto the stove's windbreak.

* * *

Jude didn't tell me what to do. That's the thing I'll remember longest about that night, maybe longer than the burning. She sat with her brother's stones against her chest and watched me hold my orders, and she offered nothing — no speech, no plea, no clever argument. She'd learned, somewhere in her flight across the Saltreach, that a cornered person makes their own choices or makes none at all, and she gave me the room to make mine.

The stove was small and iron-bellied and had been fed on resin-scrub all day, burning hot and clean. I suppose I could have used it. Instead I did it the ranger way, the field way: I struck my knife against the flint in my kit until I had a spark, touched it to the corner of the orders, and held the paper up in the blue hollow of the glacier and let it burn in the open air, where the light of it was visible and the smoke of it rose and where no one could later claim it had been an accident, a misfire, a regrettable loss to the elements.

Treason should be witnessed. That's what separates it from murder.

The wax seal went first, softening and dripping like a wound. Then the ink lifted off the page in curling ghosts — *destroy, capture, seal, supersede* — each word glowing briefly bright before collapsing into ash. I held it until the flame reached my fingers, and a little longer, because it seemed important to feel it.

When it was done, I let the ashes fall on the gravel between us, and the draft from the storm mouth stirred them into a small grey ghost that spun twice and died.

"Well," Jude said.

"Yes."

"You're a traitor now."

"I appear to be."

"To her. To the oath. To everything that made you." She said it without triumph, which I appreciated. Accusation I could have handled. Wonder was harder.

"To everything that made me," I agreed. And here was the strange part, the part I'd expected to feel as grief and instead felt as — space. Room. The terrible vertiginous openness of a man who has set down a weight he'd forgotten he was carrying and discovered his spine still works. I had been a ranger so long I'd mistaken the uniform for a skeleton. Take it off, and underneath: a man. Fourteen years old in one or two places, still standing at a blue river waiting for a sister to come back up from the water.

Terrifying. Genuinely terrifying. I recommend it to no one.

"They'll send someone," Jude said. "When you don't report. When the route isn't sealed. She'll know what you chose."

"She'll have known before I did. That's what makes her dangerous." I looked toward the storm-shrouded south, back the way we'd come, where the calving route lay open for days yet — days, not weeks — and beyond it, eventually, boots. "We need to reach the source before the weather breaks. And then we need to be gone before hers does."

Jude pulled her pack close and began checking straps, quick and businesslike, and for a moment neither of us said anything, and the storm howled its long opinion, and the little stove ticked as it cooled.

Then she paused, with her hand flat on the stones through the canvas, and looked at me with an expression I couldn't read — something between pity and recognition.

"Sebastian. If there's a stone with your sister on it — at the source, among the eleven — what happens when it plays?"

I fed the last of the fuel to the coals and watched the flames stand up straight, and I told her the truth, which was becoming, apparently, my new policy.

"I don't know," I said. "But I'm going to be listening."

Above us, the storm began, very slowly, to die.
