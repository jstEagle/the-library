## 8. The Forbidden Source

The river stopped arguing with us an hour before we found where it was born.

I'd gotten used to its voice by then — the mutter and grumble of water that had been traveling in the dark since before either of us was a rumor. For six days it had led us upstream through passages that narrowed and widened like a throat deciding whether to swallow, and I had learned to read its moods the way you learn a horse's. When it got loud, the passage ahead was open. When it went sullen, we climbed.

Now it went quiet altogether, and I felt the silence in my teeth.

"Sebastian," I said, and my breath came out white even though the air wasn't cold enough for it. "Do you hear that?"

He heard it. Or rather, he heard nothing, which is not the same thing, and his face did something complicated — the ranger mask trying to hold while everything underneath it leaned forward. He'd been doing that more lately. Letting the mask slip. I pretended not to notice, mostly because noticing meant admitting how much I liked watching it happen.

"The source," he said. Just those two words, but he said them the way other men say *forgive me* or *I'm sorry* or *run*.

We rounded one last shoulder of stone, and there it was.

I have tried to describe it since, to people who ask, and I always fail, so let me fail properly now. It was a cathedral. That's the only word, and it's a coward's word, because cathedrals were built by men trying to copy this and getting it wrong in every particular. A vault of blue ice, high as a cliff, glowing from within the way deep water glows — not lit, exactly, but *remembering* light, holding some ancient sun inside itself and letting it out slow. The river rose from a pool at the center of the floor, welling up without a sound, without a ripple, as if the ice above it were breathing it into existence. Pillars of frozen water stood around the pool like columns, and when I looked closely — when I made myself look — I could see things suspended in them. Leaves. A bird, wings spread, mid-flight forever. Small dark shapes I decided not to name.

"It's real," I said stupidly. "The legends are real."

"You say that like you expected otherwise." Sebastian's voice echoed strangely, doubled and tripled, as though the cathedral itself were repeating him back to himself, considering it. "You strapped your brother to your chest and swam into a drowning cave on the strength of a legend."

"I expected to die first," I said. "Dying simplifies things. You don't have to be right about anything."

That earned me half a smile. He'd been rationing those out lately, one per day, like a man hoarding coin in a collapsing currency. I'd started keeping count. I'm not proud of that. I'm not ashamed of it either.

I unslung my pack with hands that wouldn't quite behave. Six days of climbing and wading and trusting a thief's instincts and a ranger's impossible childhood knowledge of a river no map admitted existed, and here was the reason for all of it, humming at me through the leather.

Because they were humming. The memory-stones. All eleven of them, warm against my back, singing low and eager like dogs smelling home.

"They know where they are," I whispered.

Sebastian came closer. Close enough that I could see the frost in his eyebrows, close enough that the glow turned his eyes the same blue as the ice, which felt like cheating, somehow. "What happens now?"

"Now?" I laughed, and the laugh came out cracked. "Now I find out if I've been carrying my brother across a desert for two years, or just carrying stones."

* * *

You should understand what the stones are, since everyone else in this story apparently already does.

They're relics. Old ones — older than the Ranger Order, older than the Halvard line, older than any law anyone's ever used to forbid them. River-glass, the old folk called them, though they're not glass. They're what the river makes when someone dies in it, or near it, or thinking hard about it, depending on which grandmother you believe. Each one holds a memory. Not a recording, exactly — recordings are flat, they're dead things played back. A stone holds the memory the way amber holds an insect: whole, alive, still warm. You press it to your skin and you're *there*. You see what they saw. You feel what they felt.

My brother Tam had eleven of them. Our family had kept them for generations — voices of the dead Vanes, going back and back, a library of everyone we'd ever loved. And when Tam drowned in the Saltreach flood three years ago, the last thing he did was press a new stone into being. His own. His final memory, sealed, unlabeled.

I'd never played it. Two years, and I never played it.

"Do you want me to leave?" Sebastian asked, and I could hear what it cost him to offer. Rangers aren't built for waiting outside doors.

"No," I said. And then, because we'd agreed — upriver, after the ice shelf, after he pulled me out of the black water using a technique he claimed didn't exist and I promised to forget — "No lies about small things. Stay."

So I took out Tam's stone. It sat in my palm like an egg, gray-green, veined with silver, heavier than it had any right to be. The hum in the cathedral rose to meet it. Every pillar of ice seemed to lean in.

"He died in the flood," I said. My voice sounded very small under all that blue. "The same flood that took—" I stopped. "He was running messages along the coast road. He wasn't supposed to be anywhere near the water. But he told me once that the river calls some people. That it called our family especially. I thought he was being poetic. Tam was never poetic in his life."

Sebastian said nothing. He had a way of saying nothing that was louder than most men's shouting.

I pressed the stone to my sternum, over my heart, where I'd carried it every day for two years without ever once daring to listen.

The world folded.

* * *

Rain. That's the first thing. Rain coming down in ropes, the sky a bruise, and Tam — Tam young, Tam twenty and laughing, standing on the bank of a river I didn't recognize, a river running fast and brown with snowmelt—

No. Not brown. Blue. Blue as the walls of this place, and I understood with a lurch that I was looking at the source itself, seen from outside, centuries ago maybe, when the glacier's mouth stood open to the sky.

And Tam wasn't alone.

A girl stood beside him at the water's edge. Dark-haired, sleeves rolled, boots off, toes in the current like the water couldn't possibly kill her. She was laughing at something Tam had said, and the laugh went through me like a fishhook, because I knew that laugh. Everyone in the Saltreach settlements knew that laugh. It belonged to Wren Brightwater, the ranger's daughter, the girl who drowned in the flood year, the girl whose name was spoken in the lowlands the way you speak the names of the dead — carefully, and only when necessary.

She wasn't dead in this memory. She was gloriously, stubbornly alive, splashing Tam, calling him a liar, and Tam — my practical, unpoetic brother — was holding a memory-stone out to her. An old one. One of ours.

"You have to promise," Tam was saying, rain hammering both of them, both of them grinning like idiots. "When it's your turn, you make one. Your family doesn't keep them, so somebody has to. Swear it, Wren. Swear you'll come back here and sing yourself into the river."

"I'll come back," she said. "I swear it on the source."

And then the memory shuddered — Tam's fear arriving early, the way fear does, bleeding backward through time — and the sky went darker, and somewhere behind them the water began to rise, and the last thing the stone held was Tam turning back toward the river, toward her, shouting her name into the rain:

"Wren! *Wren!*"

Then nothing. Then me, on my knees on blue ice, with Sebastian Thornbury crouched in front of me wearing an expression like the end of the world.

* * *

"She was here," I said. My mouth was numb. Everything was numb except the part of me that was breaking. "Your sister. She stood right there. Right where that pool is. With Tam."

"I heard." His voice was very quiet. Very controlled. The control of a man holding a door shut against a flood. "I heard it too."

"You said she drowned in the flood year. You said—"

"I said what the records say." He looked at the pool. At the place where the river was born, silent and endless. "The records say she drowned. They don't say she was here first. They don't say she made a promise to a thief's brother to come back and give herself to the water." His jaw worked. "They don't say why."

"Why would she—" I stopped. Thought. Thought about a girl with her toes in the coldest water in the world, laughing. About a family that didn't keep stones. About a Commander who signed orders demanding that memory-stones be destroyed, every one, burned out of history — and whose own sister had sworn, on this exact spot, to become one.

"Oh," I said softly. "Oh, Sebastian. She wanted to be remembered. And her family forbids remembering."

The words landed on him like falling rock. I watched them land. I watched the ranger go down underneath them, and what was left sitting on the blue ice beside me wasn't a ranger at all — just a man, hollowed out, learning that his sister's last wish had been criminalized by the people who loved her most.

"She's in the water," he said finally. "Isn't she. Somewhere in this river. All this way down to the sea."

"Maybe." I took his hand. His fingers were freezing and he let me hold them anyway. "Or maybe she's in a stone. Maybe Tam helped her make one, after. Maybe it's still out there, waiting in the flood-mud for someone to find it."

He made a sound I will never be able to describe to anyone, and I held onto his hand harder, and the cathedral hummed around us, patient as geology, and I understood — with the terrible clarity that only arrives at the worst possible moment — that I loved him. That I probably had for days. That love had walked into my life disguised as shared mourning, wearing grief's coat, and I hadn't recognized it until now because nobody warns you it looks like this: two people kneeling on ancient ice, holding hands over a pool where a river is born, crying for the same dead.

"We should rest," I said, when I could speak. "Before whatever comes next."

"There's light moving in the passage behind us," Sebastian said.

I turned. Far back down the tunnel we'd come through — miles back, days back — a flicker. Lantern-light. More than one lantern.

"Pursuit," I breathed.

"Yes." He rose, pulling me up with him, and when he looked at me his eyes were dry and absolutely certain. "Jude. Whatever my orders say. Whatever she sent them to do. We finish this first. Whatever's in that water — we find out. Together."

Behind us, faint but growing, came the ring of boots on stone.
