## 10. Landfall

*Sebastian*

The glacier was closing like a door that had finally made up its mind.

We heard it before we saw it — a groan rolling through the ice from somewhere deep, the sound of a mountain clearing its throat, and then the first wall of the calving route began to lean. Not fall. Lean. The way a tired man leans against a doorframe, deciding whether to stay standing.

"Sebastian." Jude's voice came flat and clear across the ice, the way it did when she was frightened and refusing to spend anything on showing it. "How long?"

I did the arithmetic I'd been doing since dawn and didn't like any of the answers. Behind us, the source chamber still glowed faintly through the passage — blue light bleeding up the corridor like a memory of daylight. Ahead, maybe two miles of shelf, then the last opening where the glacier met the sea. Where we'd come in, if you counted a collapsed cave mouth as coming in.

"An hour," I said. "Maybe less if the ice decides to be dramatic."

"The ice has been dramatic this whole journey."

"Yes," I said. "It gets that from me."

She almost smiled. That was new. Three weeks ago she'd have looked at me like I'd suggested swimming back. Now she just adjusted the strap across her chest — the stones riding there like a heartbeat that wasn't hers — and started walking.

I walked beside her. Not behind, which is where a ranger puts a prisoner, and not ahead, which is where a guide puts himself. Beside. It had taken me most of the journey to learn that position existed.

* * *

*Jude*

Here is what nobody tells you about running: eventually your legs learn the route better than your fear does, and then you're not running anymore, you're just traveling, and traveling with someone is an entirely different crime.

Wren would have liked him. That thought arrived uninvited, somewhere past the second mile marker of groaning ice, and I let it stay because the alternative was thinking about the sound behind us — that slow, patient closing — and I'd spent enough of my life listening to doors shut.

My brother had been dead four years when I stole his voice back. Four years of the Commander's vaults keeping his memory-stones like evidence, like contraband, like something shameful. And now they rode against my ribs, warm from my body, humming so quietly only I could feel it. He'd recorded everything. Every stupid joke, every argument about whose turn it was to mend nets, every ordinary morning. The dead don't haunt you. They just wait for someone to press play.

"You're doing the thing," Sebastian said.

"What thing."

"The backward-walking thing. In your head. You're checking our trail even though the trail is being eaten by a glacier and no one alive could follow it."

"I am not."

"You're doing it right now. Your feet keep trying to turn around. It's very rude to walk forward while walking backward. Someone could get hurt."

I laughed. Out loud, at the edge of the world, with the ice coming down behind us. Wren would have called that progress. Sebastian called it nothing at all — he just kept walking, hands loose at his sides, watching the route ahead with those ranger's eyes that catalogued everything and confessed nothing.

Except now they confessed things. That was the strange inheritance of the last three weeks. I knew about his sister. I knew about the woman who'd signed his orders and signed away his childhood in the same ink. I knew he burned those orders in a storm shelter while I pretended to sleep, and that the firelight on his face had looked like a man setting down something heavy after carrying it so long he'd forgotten it wasn't part of his spine.

The opening appeared around the next bend — a slash of grey daylight, wind, the smell of salt. Real sky. I stopped.

"That's it," I said. "That's out."

"That's out," he agreed, and neither of us moved, because we both knew what was still behind us, and what was ahead, and how short the distance between treason and freedom really was. About forty feet, as it turned out. Give or take.

* * *

*Sebastian*

The mentor found us thirty feet from the door.

I should say that plainly, because everything else in this story has been dressed in weather and legend, and Halvard Voss deserved better than euphemism. He came through the calving route with six rangers and a lantern, and he looked older than the last time I'd seen him, which had been the day he taught me to read ice pressure by sound. He looked at me the way you look at a map that's been redrawn.

"Sebastian Brightwater," he said. "You're standing between me and a sealed source."

"I'm standing between you and a woman carrying her brother's voice," I said. "The source can look after itself."

Behind him, the rangers shifted. Behind me, Jude went very still — I felt it more than saw it, the particular stillness of prey deciding whether the predator has actually seen it. But Voss hadn't brought hunters. I knew his hunting posture. This was something else. His lantern hand was steady but his jaw wasn't.

"The Commander sent orders," he said. "Destroy the stones. Take her in. Seal the route."

"I know. I had copies."

"I know you had copies. I wrote them." He said it tiredly, the way you admit to an old scar. "I also taught you that ranger law serves the land, not the other way round. I've spent nine days walking through a calving field wondering which lesson took."

Above us, the glacier answered for everyone. A crack like the world splitting kindling, and a section of the ceiling the size of a chapel dropped fifty yards back down the route we'd come by. The floor jumped. Dust and ice-fragments hissed across the shelf. When the echo died, the way behind Voss was gone — not narrowing, gone, a wall of fresh fracture where the passage had been.

His rangers stared at each other. Voss didn't. He watched me instead, and I understood that he'd known this was possible, had perhaps chosen this route knowing it might close, and had come anyway. To ask, not to take.

"Your sister," he said quietly. "Maren. She used to sing at this river. Did you know the stones carry singing better than speaking? The resonance favors it."

The floor of my chest gave way, politely, without noise. "You've heard them."

"I signed the order to vault them. I never said I agreed with it." He looked past me at Jude, at the strap across her chest, at the whole improbable shape of what we'd done. Then he stepped aside. All the way aside, turning his body so his rangers had to read the gesture or disobey it. "The wilderness keeps its own records," he said. "Go home, Brightwater. Both of you. And whatever you do with that brother's voice—" he nodded at Jude, and something in his face was younger than his years, "—don't let anyone tell you grief is contraband."

The last opening stood forty feet away and the wind through it smelled like morning.

* * *

*Jude*

We ran the last forty feet because the ice behind us made the decision elegant.

The world outside was the ordinary world — grey water, pale sky, a shingle beach scattered with the bones of old icebergs — and it received us the way the sea receives everything, without comment. Behind us the Cave of the Landfall sealed itself with a sound like a held breath released, and the glacier settled into its own long silence, keeping everything: the source, the river, the singing, the confession, the mentor standing alone in the dark with his lantern and his choice.

Sebastian sat down on the shingle like his strings had been cut. I sat next to him. For a while neither of us said anything, because some silences are load-bearing.

Then, because I couldn't help it, I pressed one stone. Just lightly. Through my palm, Wren's voice came up small and warm and mid-sentence, the way it always was: *—and that's why you never trust a man who owns two coats—* and faded, the last echo of him going out over the water instead of into a vault.

Sebastian listened all the way to the end. He didn't ask who it was. He already knew.

"He sounds like you," he said.

"He'd be furious to hear that."

"He'd be right to be." He picked up a flat stone and turned it over in his fingers, not throwing it yet. "So. You're a thief with stolen property, and I'm a deserter with no orders left to follow. Neither of us is going home the same person who left."

"No," I agreed. "Home's going to be complicated."

"Home," he said, testing the word like river ice. "I haven't had one of those in twenty years. Any suggestions?"

I looked at the water, at the beach, at the man sitting beside me who had chosen treason in a storm shelter and never once made me thank him for it. Somewhere under the horizon, the river was being born again in blue dark, forgetting nothing, forgiving anyway.

"South," I said. "There's a town where nobody asks questions and the fish is terrible. We could start there."

He threw the stone. It skipped four times, which on that water, that morning, felt like a benediction.

"Terrible fish," he said. "Lead the way, Vane. And this time—" the ghost of a smile, "—walk forward."

So I did.
