## 1. The Last Spike Season

The frost came early that year, and Coral Galloway was glad of it. Frost meant the muskeg would firm up under his boots, that the creeks would run shallow and honest, that he could walk the old grades without sinking to his knees in ground that wanted to swallow everything men had ever built on it.

He came down out of the high country on a Tuesday, though the day of the week meant nothing out here, following the ghost of the Kettle River & Northern grade where it ran along the water like a scar that hadn't decided whether to heal. Ten years ago there had been ties under his feet, steel above his head, and forty men singing work songs in three languages to keep their minds off the cold. Now there was only the grade itself—a long green berm running through birch and alder, straight as a ruled line drawn by someone who had died before finishing the page.

Coral walked it with a surveyor's chain over one shoulder and a leather satchel riding his hip, and every few hundred yards he stopped to drive a stake or make a note in a book bound in oilcloth. The notes were precise. Elevations, bearings, culvert locations, the exact span of a trestle's rot. Precision was a habit he could not break, and had stopped wanting to. A man who measured things carefully did not have to think about other things—the things a man might be measuring instead, if he were a different sort of coward, or a braver one.

The map he was making would never hang in any office. It existed for himself alone: the whole skeleton of the northern lines, every abandoned spur and dead-end siding from the lake country to the divide, drawn in his own cramped hand. He knew them the way a priest knows his psalms. He had helped lay some of them, back when he'd been another man with another name, and he had watched the wilderness take the rest, mile by mile, tie by rotten tie, patient as rust.

By late afternoon he reached the place where the old line crossed the wagon road, and beyond it, tucked into a bend of the river like something trying not to be noticed, the town of Harrow's Landing.

* * *

Harrow's Landing had been a rail town once, which meant it had been a town at all only by accident, and now it was neither. Perhaps two hundred people remained, keeping a trading post, a church whose steeple leaned like a man listening hard, a hotel with eleven rooms and guests in perhaps four of them. The rails still came through—barely—one mixed train a week from the south, hauling freight nobody wanted and taking away lumber nobody had ordered, its crew too tired or too indifferent to wonder why a surveyor lived out past the old junction and came in only when his flour ran low.

That suited Coral fine. Harrow's Landing asked no questions because questions cost more than they paid. The town had learned during the boom years that curiosity about the railways was a luxury, like sugar, and like sugar it had been rationed away when times turned lean.

He took a room at the hotel because the alternative was sleeping in the ditch, and in the morning he went to see Marta Quist at the trading post.

Marta was seventy if she was a day, built like a root fence, and she had run the post through the boom, the bust, the fire, and the influenza, and she regarded all human activity since with the mild contempt of a survivor. She kept a ledger for the company goods and a second ledger, unspoken, for everything else. Coral's maps went into the second ledger.

"Back again," she said, when he laid the rolled sheets on her counter. "Thought you'd finished the north fork."

"Finished it. Started the west branches."

"Nobody lives on the west branches."

"That's why they need mapping."

She unrolled the top sheet with hands like knotted pine and studied it the way she studied everything—thoroughly, suspiciously, and without surprise. Somewhere in the south there were men who bought such maps. Railroad men, mostly, or lawyers sorting out who owned what along a dead right-of-way, or occasionally a fool with money who believed gold waited under some forgotten cut. They wrote to Marta; Marta wrote to Coral; the maps went south wrapped in oilcloth and the supplies came north the same way. Neither party ever learned the other's name. That was the arrangement, and in ten years it had never once been broken.

"Your flour's gone soft," she said. "I'll set aside hardtack instead. And you're low on lamp oil."

"I can render fat if I have to."

"You'll go blind squinting by tallow-light, and then who'll draw your pretty lines?" She was already pulling goods from the shelves, tallying in her head the way she did, lips moving slightly. "Chain's looking worn."

"It's holding."

"Everything's holding until it isn't." She glanced up at him then, quick and sharp, an old bird's look. "You look thinner."

"I always look thinner in October."

"Mm." She tied off the parcel but didn't push it across the counter. Her hands stayed flat on the paper, and Coral felt the small change in the room before she said anything—the way you feel a rail hum before any train appears. "There's a telegram for you."

The words landed strangely. In ten years, Coral Galloway had received exactly one telegram, and that had been a notice of a tax assessment so trivial it had been funny. Telegrams were for people who existed. "For me," he said.

"For the surveyor. That's how it's addressed. No name." Marta reached under the counter and produced the yellow flimsy, holding it a moment longer than necessary. "Operator at the junction asked me if I knew who it was for. I told him I didn't know any names out your way. Which is true enough—I don't ask, and I don't tell." Her eyes held his. "You want me to read it first? Some of those messages are bad news."

"They usually are."

He took it anyway. His fingers were steady—he had spent years training them to be steady, driving stakes in wind, pouring level in a rocking boat—but something behind his sternum had already begun to sink, slow and cold, like a stone dropped into deep water.

The message was brief. Telegraphese, clipped and expensive, every word paid for:

**GOVERNMENT EXPEDITION NORTHERN SURVEY OVERDUE STOP LAST REPORTED VICINITY OLD JUNCTION LINE STOP REQUEST YOUR SERVICES AS GUIDE AND TERRAIN EXPERT STOP REPLY THROUGH THIS OFFICE STOP — BRANDT**

He read it twice. Then a third time, standing in the smell of kerosene and cured hides while the stove ticked and somewhere outside a dog complained at the weather.

*Old Junction Line.* There was only one junction worth the name on the whole northern system, and Coral had been walking its outskirts for a decade the way a man circles a grave he cannot bring himself to visit. Wreck Junction. Everyone called it that now, though the timetables had never printed the word *wreck* in their lives. Eleven years ago it had been called Brandvold Siding, before the tunnel came down, before the inquiry, before the names in the newspapers and the funerals and the end of everything he had been.

And now a government expedition had gone missing near it, and someone signing herself *Brandt* wanted him to lead strangers into that country.

"They know your trade," Marta said. It wasn't a question. "Terrain expert. Somebody's been reading your maps."

"Maybe."

"Somebody doesn't buy maps of dead rail lines for the scenery." She pushed the parcel toward him at last, and her voice dropped into something almost gentle, which frightened him more than the telegram had. "You don't have to answer it."

"No," Coral agreed. "I don't."

But even as he said it, he understood that he would. Not because of duty—that coin had been debased for him years ago—and not for the money, though the money would be real. He would answer because the expedition had been *near the junction*, and because instruments and terrain experts and government interest were words that belonged together in a sentence he had spent ten years praying never to hear spoken aloud. Someone was looking for something out there. And whatever they were looking for, they had looked too close, and the country had closed over them the way the muskeg closed over a dropped tool.

He knew that country better than God did. If anyone could find those people, it was him. And if finding them meant walking back into the shadow of the tunnel—if it meant standing on the old grade with strangers asking questions about the disaster, about the crews, about the men who had been blamed—

Then the life he had built, this careful, narrow, anonymous life, stake by stake and map by map, was going to be tested down to the last fiber of it.

"Coral." Marta was watching him fold the telegram with hands that were, he noted distantly, perfectly steady. "Whatever's in that message—you've got the look my husband used to get. The one he wore the day he went back down the mine after the fire, because he figured somebody had to, and he figured it ought to be him." She shook her head slowly. "Don't wear that look. Nothing good ever came of it."

"Nothing good ever comes of anything out here," Coral said. "We just try to keep the bad from spreading."

He carried his supplies back to the hotel, sat a long time by the window watching the river carry the season south, and by lamplight wrote his reply, short as the original, costing him nearly as much thought per word:

**WILL GUIDE STOP REQUIRE TEN DAYS PROVISIONS AND FULL ACCOUNT OF EXPEDITION'S EQUIPMENT AND PURPOSE BEFORE DEPARTURE STOP GALLOWAY**

He signed the name he had chosen for himself, the name that fit him like a coat bought secondhand—close enough, warm enough, but never quite his. He gave it to the operator at dawn, and stood on the platform afterward in the iron-colored light, listening to the rails tick and settle as the frost let go of them, and wondered which of the two of them—the man named Galloway, or whoever had come before—would be walking north when the ten days were up.

The wire went south on the morning mixed train. Three days later, the answer came back, and it was waiting for him at Marta's counter when he came in for the last of his provisions:

**EQUIPMENT DETAILS FOLLOW BY COURIER STOP COME AT ONCE STOP PEOPLE ARE DYING OUT THERE STOP — BRANDT**

Below it, in different ink, in a hand that pressed hard enough to tear the flimsy, someone had added four words that the telegraph office had charged for and sent anyway:

**YOU KNOW THE COUNTRY.**

Coral stood holding the paper a long moment, hearing beneath the words all the ones that weren't written: *We know who you are. Or we will.*

Outside, the first snow of the season was starting to fall, fine and dry, erasing the grade south of town one flake at a time—as if the wilderness had looked ahead, seen what was coming, and begun quietly tidying up.
