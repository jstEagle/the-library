## 2. Gear Without Tracks

The river told him where to look before the government's coordinates did.

Coral had been reading water for twenty years—first as a surveyor's apprentice learning how a current bent around a bridge pier, later as a man who needed to know which channels would hold his weight and which would swallow it. The Kettle River ran low and glass-green through the gorge below Wreck Junction, and where the expedition had made its last camp, the water went quiet in a way that meant something upstream was holding it back. A slide, maybe. Or something else.

He left the canoe in the alder brush two miles short and walked in along the old tote road, the one he'd cut himself eleven years ago with a crew of forty men and a steam donkey that screamed like a living thing. The road had gone soft since then. Moss had taken the corduroy logs. Fireweed stood shoulder-high in the cuts, and the mountainsides above were already turning rust and gold at their edges, the first cold breath of autumn moving down from the icefields. He moved slowly, not because he was tired but because he was remembering, and remembering deserved a careful pace.

The camp sat on the gravel bench where he'd known it would be—the only flat ground within six miles that wasn't bog or scree. He saw the canvas first, pale against the dark timber, and stopped at the tree line with his hand on his rifle sling and let himself look for a long time.

Four tents. Pyramid style, military pattern, guy-lines taut and staked. A cook fire ring with a canvas fly stretched over it. Crates stacked in a neat row against a fallen spruce, lids nailed down. A theodolite on its tripod stood in the open maybe thirty yards from the tents, still set up over a station mark, still covered by its rain hood.

Nothing was torn. Nothing was scattered. Nothing was burned.

Coral had found lost parties before—not many, but enough. Lost parties left wreckage. They left gear dropped in the panic of flight or the slow arithmetic of starvation. They left signs of decision: a cache built, a note nailed to a tree, boots worn through and abandoned. Even the dead left something, if you knew how to read it. The wilderness was thorough but not tidy.

This camp was tidy.

He circled it once at a distance, downwind, watching for movement. Nothing. Then he came in from the river side, boots silent on the gravel, and stood at the edge of the cook area and looked at what a week of absence had left untouched.

The food was the thing that stopped him.

A crate stood open beside the fire ring—hardtack, tins of beans, a sack of flour rolled shut but not sealed. Beside it, a frying pan sat on a flat stone with grease in it that had gone white and rancid. Six tin plates were stacked by the fire ring, scraped more or less clean, and one of them still held a fork standing upright in a heel of bread gone hard as brick. Somebody had set that plate down expecting to come back after a few minutes. Maybe after washing up. Maybe after checking a line of sight through the theodolite.

Nobody came back.

Coral crouched and looked without touching. There was no mold on the bread worth mentioning—too dry for that—but the grease had turned, and a fine grey dust of woodsmoke ash lay evenly over everything, undisturbed. No squirrel tracks in the flour sack. No bear sign anywhere near the food, which was wrong all by itself; an unguarded camp on this river would have been opened up like a mail sack inside of two nights. Something about the place kept the animals off. He'd felt that before, in old workings, in places where men had died badly, and he didn't like feeling it now.

He walked to the theodolite.

It was a good instrument, better than anything the rail companies had issued in his day—a precision transit with a glass scale and a brass case worn bright at the hinges from handling. The rain hood was oiled canvas, tied down properly. He lifted it and looked through the eyepiece out of habit, and the telescope was focused not on the far hills but on the near slope across the river—at the dark mouth of country below the old Wreck Junction grade, where the mountain folded in on itself and nothing grew quite right.

They hadn't been surveying the river. He'd suspected as much from the telegram's careful vagueness—"hydrographic reconnaissance"—but suspicion was one thing and a focused instrument was another. You didn't aim a theodolite at a hillside to measure water. You aimed it there to watch something. To fix a position. To wait for a light.

Coral straightened up and made himself breathe.

* * *

He spent the rest of the daylight going through the camp the way he'd once taught green chainmen to go through a wreck site: systematically, gently, touching as little as possible.

The tents held four bedrolls, all made up or nearly so. Personal gear stowed. Boots—every man had left his boots. That detail sat in Coral's chest like a swallowed stone. Nobody crossed this country barefoot. Not in September, not with frost already forming in the shadows at night. Either they'd been taken without time to dress, or they'd walked out into rock and water and muskeg in sock feet, which amounted to the same thing.

There were papers in the lead tent, locked in an oilskin document case with a small brass padlock. He didn't force it. Instead he read what was loose: a page of figures in a careful engineer's hand, columns of numbers he recognized despite himself—declination readings, repeated obsessively, hour after hour, day after day. Magnetic variation, logged like a man logging a fever. And at the bottom of the last page, underlined twice:

*Anomaly persists. Bearing 214°. Depth estimate revised.*

Depth. You didn't log depth for a surface survey. Depth was for things underground.

Coral put the pages back exactly as he'd found them and stood in the tent doorway looking out at the fire ring, the stacked plates, the frying pan with its rancid grease, and felt the official story come apart in his hands like wet paper.

The telegram had said *strayed from camp during a storm; presumed lost in the river.* He'd read it a dozen times on the way north, tasting each word. Strayed. Presumed. The passive grammar of men who had already decided what they wanted found—or not found. But there had been no storm. He'd lived close enough to these mountains for ten years to know every blow that came through; the nearest rain in that window had fallen sixty miles south and barely wet the moss. And men did not stray from a dry camp in fair weather, leaving their boots, their rifles—he'd counted three rifle racks, all empty of rifles but full of dust—and their supper cooling on the plate.

And then there was the ground itself.

He went out in the last light and walked the perimeter slowly, head down, the way he'd once walked fresh ballast looking for what the rails could tell him. Inside the camp the gravel was churned and printed—boot heels, hobnails, the round scars of crate corners, a knee-print where someone had knelt at the fire. All of it weathered the same degree, softened at the edges, five days old or more. Fine.

Outside the camp, the story changed.

He worked outward in a spiral, into the grass and duff beyond the gravel bench, and found the trail where the party had come in from the north—a broad, beaten line through the fireweed, easy to follow even now. It ended at the tents. It did not leave them.

He widened the spiral. He checked the riverbank, walking it twice in both directions, looking for tracks in the mud at the waterline, for scuffed stones, for the pale underside of a turned cobble. Mud holds a print for days here; he'd tracked otter and moose on older sign than this. There was nothing. The bank was unbroken silt, smooth as poured cream, from the camp's edge to the bend half a mile downstream.

No footprints going out. None coming back. No tracks to the latrine trench—which existed, dug and used, and then abandoned mid-use, a spade still standing in the earth beside it.

Coral stood in the blue dusk with his hands hanging at his sides and admitted it to himself plainly, because plainness was the only discipline that had never failed him: these men had not wandered. These men had not drowned. These men had been *removed*—lifted out of their own lives so cleanly that even the ground had been made to forget them. And erasure took hands. Erasure took time and knowledge and a reason.

He thought of the telegram again, and of the name signed at the bottom—Ilsa Brandt, Deputy Commissioner—and of the way the message had specified *the junction* in its list of search sectors before he'd ever mentioned it. As if whoever wrote it already knew where the answer was buried.

The wind came down the gorge and moved the fireweed in long silver strokes, and somewhere above, in the black fold of the mountain across the river, the old tunnel mouth waited under its rock and its rubble and its eleven years of official silence. Bearing 214°. He didn't need a compass to know what lay on that bearing. He'd carried the number in his head for a decade, the way a man carries shrapnel.

Coral went back to the camp, boiled water for tea at the dead fire because his hands needed work, and ate hardtack from the expedition's own stores, which felt like a kind of trespass and a kind of inheritance at once. Full dark came early under the peaks. He spread his bedroll between the tents and the tree line, rifle across his knees, and watched the far slope where the telescope had been pointed, where the anomaly persisted, where depth had been revised.

Around midnight, he saw it.

A light—low on the mountainside, deep in the fold below the old grade. Not a star, not a reflection; it moved, slow and level, parallel to the contour, the way a lamp moves in a hand. It showed for perhaps ten seconds. Then the mountain closed over it, and the dark was just dark again.

Coral did not sleep after that. He sat with his back against a tent pole, listening to the river hold its breath, and understood that whatever had taken six men out of their boots had not gone far.

At first light, he broke camp, loaded the canoe, and began paddling upriver toward Wreck Junction—to the tunnel he had sworn never to enter again, where someone, alive or otherwise, was waiting to know his name.
