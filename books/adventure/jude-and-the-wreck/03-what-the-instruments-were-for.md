## 3. What the Instruments Were For

The camp sat in a bowl of dead grass where the river bent away from the old grade, and Coral circled it twice before he let himself go in.

Habit. Ten years of habit. You read a place the way you read a face—where it had been touched, where it hadn't, what it was trying not to tell you. The tents stood three of them, canvas taut, guy-lines still dressed with the little wooden tensioners that only government outfits bothered to carry because they'd never had to buy their own rope. A cook fire ringed with stones, ash inside it pale and dry, rained on at least once and then left. A crate of tinned beef with the lid pried halfway open and the contents untouched. A tin cup sitting on an upturned ammunition box, half full of coffee gone to a black skin, a spoon standing upright in it like a grave marker.

Nobody leaves a cup like that. Nobody walks away mid-sip unless they walk away all at once, together, without time to think—and if they'd been taken by force there would be something else. Spent brass. Blood in the grass. Boot scuffs going somewhere in a hurry. Coral walked the perimeter slow, hands in his coat pockets, and found nothing but his own prints filling in behind him.

He'd told himself on the walk up that he was here for gear inventory. That was the story he'd give Ilsa Brandt when she caught up with him, and she would catch up; the woman signed telegrams like she signed confessions. But the truth was simpler and worse: he'd known this ground before he ever saw it. He'd known it from the contour lines on the map he'd drawn himself, four years ago, for a man who paid cash and asked nothing—the same as everyone else who bought Coral Galloway's maps. He just hadn't known what lived at the center of those contours until now.

The instrument tent was the third one, set apart from the sleeping tents the way a church is set apart from a town. He unzipped the flap and stood in the doorway a long moment, letting the smell come out to meet him: oiled steel, dry cell batteries, the faint vinegar bite of old photographic plates.

* * *

Eleven years ago there had been six of them working the Wreck Junction cut, and the youngest, Ferro, used to say the tunnel breathed. They'd laugh at him, mostly, but not on the nights when the wind came down off the ridge and moved through that black mouth, and you could stand with your hand on the cold rail and feel it—something vast inhaling where no air should have had anywhere to go.

Coral had been the junior surveyor then. Not Coral Galloway—that name came later, purchased along with a new set of papers in a river town two hundred miles south—but a younger man with another name and a crew card and a foreman named Aldous Wren who chewed tobacco like it owed him money. Wren's orders came down from the company, always verbal, never written, and one of them had been simple: nobody surveys past the hundred-meter mark inside the north bore. Not because of rockfall. Rockfall they could map around. It was the kind of order a man doesn't ask about, because asking about it means writing your name next to the question.

They'd found things anyway. Ferro, running a line too far on a bet, came back white as paper meal talking about a wall—a dressed stone wall, mortared, way back where the mountain was supposed to be solid granite, older than any rail line, older than anything men were supposed to have built up there. Wren made him drink whiskey until he agreed he'd seen a natural formation. Two weeks later the mountain came down on the day shift, forty-one men, and the inquiry blamed the geology, and the geology couldn't argue. Wren testified with his hat in his hands. Coral's own field notes went into evidence and came back to him with certain pages missing, and he was young enough, and scared enough, and paid enough, to understand what the missing pages meant: that he was being given a choice between being a witness and being a scapegoat, and that witnesses to that particular disaster had a way of becoming casualties of the next one.

So he'd become nobody. He'd walked south with a bedroll and a drafting kit and let the wilderness swallow the name his mother gave him, and he'd spent ten years mapping closed lines for men who didn't want to be found, trading precision for silence, building a life the way you build a cairn—one anonymous stone at a time.

And now somebody had come back to the junction with instruments.

* * *

Coral knelt beside the transit first. Good brass, London-made, the kind of instrument that costs more than a section hand makes in a year, and it was set up wrong—or rather, set up right for a purpose it wasn't built for. A surveyor levels a transit to read the land. This one was leveled to read *through* the land: sightline dropped low, aimed not at the valley or the grade but at the hillside itself, at a point maybe two hundred meters inside the rock. Beside it, wired to a battery case with the patience of a man who expected to wait days, ran a coil of copper wound on a wooden frame—big as a wagon wheel, and Coral knew what that was because he'd seen its grandfather once, in a company office in the old days, under a tarp nobody was supposed to pull back. A induction rig. The kind of thing you used to find metal buried deep. Ore bodies, mostly.

Or sealed vaults. Or walls with doors in them, behind forty-one men's worth of collapsed rock.

His mouth had gone dry. He made himself keep moving, because stopping meant thinking and thinking meant remembering, and remembering was a door he'd nailed shut with ten good years.

There was a field ledger on the folding table, and it was locked—brass hasp, small padlock, the key gone with whoever carried it. But surveyors are creatures of habit even when they're spies, and this one had tucked a pencil stub into the ledger's spiral binding, and folded into the pencil's spiral was a scrap of graph paper with figures on it. Readings. Coral held it to the light through the tent canvas and felt the floor of his stomach tilt.

Depth readings. Repeated, refined, corrected over what looked like nine days of work. And every reading converged—not scattered across the terrain the way honest survey data scatters, but tightening, day over day, toward a single point. Two hundred and twelve meters into the north slope. Thirty meters below the old grade.

He didn't need the map in his satchel to know what lay at that point. He had drawn that ground so many times in his head that he could have carved it blind. Two hundred twelve meters in, thirty meters down, was the end of the north bore. The hundred-meter mark. The place Ferro had seen his wall. The place the company had sealed with more than rock.

They hadn't come to survey the junction. They'd come to *open* it.

Coral stood very still in the green-smelling dark of the tent and listened to the river saying nothing, and understood, finally, why the telegram had found him. Not because he knew the terrain—half a dozen men knew the terrain. Because he knew the *history*, though the sender couldn't have known how well. Someone had chosen him the way you choose a key for a lock you've only heard described. And whoever sent that telegram had also, somewhere in some ministry drawer, a file with the disaster in it, and the disaster had names attached, and one of those names belonged to a junior surveyor officially listed among the honored dead.

If he led this rescue, he would be walking back into the one place on earth where his old life was written into the ground itself. Every stake he drove, every line he shot, might turn up a record, a signature, a photograph of a crew of six with a young man in the back row whose face he still shaved every morning in the reflection of a tin plate.

And yet the alternative was to let them stay lost. Forty-one men had kept the mountain's secret with their lives. He would not add five more names to the ledger by walking away.

Outside, the light was going amber, and down the trail, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of a second set of boots on gravel—steady, unhurried, closing.

Ilsa Brandt had caught up with him after all.
