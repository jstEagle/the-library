## 4. The Woman Who Signed the Telegram

The telegram had been signed *I. Brandt, Deputy Commissioner, Northern Survey Directorate*, and Coral had spent three days telling himself that a signature was just ink on paper, that the woman behind it would be some desk-bound clerk with soft hands and a folder of instructions she didn't understand. He was still telling himself that when she stepped off the steamer at Grayling Landing, and the first thing he noticed was her boots.

They were trail boots. Broken in, resoled at least twice, the heels worn at the angle of a woman who had walked more miles than any deputy commissioner he'd ever imagined. Her coat was government wool but her pack was private purchase—good leather, cinched tight, riding high on her shoulders like it belonged there. She came down the gangplank without looking at the dock, without looking at the crowd, without looking anywhere except directly at him.

She'd known his face from a description. That was the first alarm. No photograph of Coral Galloway existed in any government file he knew of, because no such file existed, because the man who had signed the survey charts ten years ago under another name had made very sure of it. And yet here she came, threading through the fish buyers and freight handlers like a compass needle finding north, and stopped three feet away with her hand already extended.

"Mr. Galloway."

"That's me." He didn't take the hand immediately. When he did, her grip was dry and firm and brief—a handshake conducted for the record, by someone who understood records.

"Ilsa Brandt." She said it the way people say names they've worn a long time, without ceremony. "You got my wire."

"Got your wire." He picked up his own pack. "Thought the Directorate sent deputies to sit in offices."

"The Directorate sends whoever will get the job done." She fell into step beside him—not behind, not ahead, beside—and matched his pace along the plank road toward the outfitter's without being invited. "Six people went into that country. Four are unaccounted for. The man I answer to wants a report by freeze-up. I intend to give him one that isn't fiction."

"And you came all this way to make sure of it personally."

"I came all this way," she said, "because the last man the Directorate trusted to do this alone is the one who disappeared into it."

That stopped him for half a stride. He covered it by shifting his pack strap. "Hollis Vane was competent enough."

"Hollis Vane was following orders he didn't write." Her eyes came around to him then, gray as river ice, and held. "Which raises an interesting question, Mr. Galloway. Orders written by whom? For what? Because I've read the expedition manifest twice, and half the instruments listed in it don't belong on a terrain survey. Do you know what a gravimetric densitometer is?"

He did. He'd known since the second camp, since he'd knelt in the trampled grass and understood what the brass and glass had been built to find. But that knowledge sat in his chest like swallowed stone, and he wasn't ready to let her see how heavy it was.

"Sounds expensive," he said.

"It is. It's also classified." She let that hang between them for exactly as long as it needed to hang, then looked away toward the river, where the steamer was working its moorings and the water ran brown and fast with the season's first real drop. "I'm coming with you."

"No."

"Yes."

"Mrs. Brandt—"

"Miss." The correction came quick and flat, a door closing. "And it's not a request. I hold the commission. You hold the map. Neither of us goes up that river without the other, and we both know it."

He did know it. That was the worst part. He could refuse her passage, refuse her gear, refuse her everything short of the truth, and she would simply follow him anyway—hire her own boat, buy her own supplies, turn up at his shoulder somewhere above the falls with twice the questions and none of the leverage. Better to keep her close, where he could watch what she watched. Better to learn what she knew before she learned what he was hiding.

"One condition," he said.

"Name it."

"You follow my calls on the trail. No debates at altitude, no committee decisions at the rapids. Out there I'm the only authority, and if you can't live with that, stay at Grayling and file your reports about weather."

Something moved behind her eyes—amusement, maybe, or the memory of having given the same speech to someone else once. "Agreed," she said. "Provided you extend me the same courtesy off the trail. When I ask you a question in camp, Mr. Galloway, I expect an answer. Not silence. Not a change of subject. An answer."

"And if I don't have one?"

"Then say so." She shouldered her pack and started toward the outfitter's, and over her shoulder, almost lightly: "I'm very good at hearing what people don't say. It saves us both time if you skip to the part where you lie badly."

* * *

Ilsa Brandt had signed the telegram because no one else would.

That was the version she gave herself, anyway, standing at the rail of the steamer as Grayling Landing shrank behind them and the river widened into the country where six men had gone to ground. The truth was less noble and more complicated: she had signed it because she had read the expedition's true orders—the ones that lived in a separate envelope inside a locked drawer—and understood that whatever Hollis Vane had found out there, it was worth enough to someone in the capital to spend seven lives retrieving. And she had signed it because she did not trust the person who had sealed that envelope.

Deputy commissioners were supposed to administer. To process. To sit in rooms with radiators and sign things. Ilsa had spent eleven years being exactly that, and eleven years was long enough to learn that the Directorate's files told two stories at once: the story in the typed pages, and the story in what had been removed from them. She had gotten very good at reading the white space. Vane's expedition file had more white space than text. Pages referenced that weren't there. A prior survey, dated twelve years back, whose author's name had been struck through so hard the pen had torn the paper.

Someone had gone into that country before. Someone whose name the Directorate wanted forgotten. And when she'd traced the old supply ledgers north, through three towns that asked no questions, every road had ended at the same name—the name of the man who now stood at the bow of her steamer, watching the water like a man watching for something to surface.

Coral Galloway. Except she'd have bet her pension it wasn't his name, any more than *deputy commissioner* was really hers.

She joined him at the rail. He didn't look over. Most men would have; most men performed awareness of her, checked her reactions, managed her. Galloway simply stood there, hands loose on the rail, letting the wind come off the water and flatten his gray-streaked hair, and waited—as if conversation were a current he'd let her swim against if she chose.

"The instruments," she said. "You recognized them at the camp. I saw your face when you read the manifest."

"Did you."

"Don't do that." She kept her voice level. "Don't play the ignorant guide. You knew what a densitometer was for before I said the word. Which means either you've worked government surveys—which your file says you haven't—or you've spent a long time around people who did."

The river took a long time answering. Then: "Your file on me must be thin."

"My file on you is one page, and half of it is the paper it's printed on. That's not thin, Mr. Galloway. That's *scrubbed*. Somebody paid good money to make you boring." She turned to face him fully. "I'm not here to dig you up. Whatever you were before, it's yours. But four people are missing up that river, and the last expedition that went looking for them came back with instruments running and food untouched and not one footprint leaving camp. I need a partner who tells me the truth even when it costs him. Can you be that?"

He looked at her then, and she watched him weigh it—the same way she'd watched him weigh everything since the dock, careful and slow, a man spending coins he couldn't replace. The wind gusted. Somewhere below them the paddle wheel threw up spray, and for a moment the whole world was water sound.

"I'll tell you what you need to know," he said finally. "When you need to know it. Not before."

"That's not the same thing."

"No," he agreed. "It's better. The truth early is just weight. The truth on time is a tool." He nodded upstream, toward the gray wall of country waiting beyond the last bend. "There's a place called Wreck Junction. Your instruments were pointed at it. When we get there, you'll understand why, and you'll wish you didn't. Until then, Miss Brandt—let's both keep our files closed."

She should have pushed. Eleven years of administration said push, press, document, escalate. Instead she heard herself say, "Wreck Junction," tasting the words. "That's not on any chart I've seen."

"No," Coral said, turning back to the water. "It wouldn't be. I burned that chart myself."

* * *

They made camp that night on a gravel bar below the first canyon, and Ilsa lay awake in her tent listening to the river work the stones and thinking about struck-through names.

Galloway slept—or lay still—in his bedroll ten feet away, close enough to hear, far enough to reach his rifle. She noted that. She noted everything: the way he'd rigged the tarps against rain that hadn't been forecast, the way he'd counted the matches twice, the way he'd placed her tent uphill of the fire without explaining why and without making it look deliberate. This was a man who had survived something by noticing, and who now noticed the way other men breathed.

She thought about the torn page in Vane's file. About the envelope in the locked drawer, and the initials on the seal, and the instruction—*verify and contain*—that she had never been meant to read. Contain what? A cover-up? A discovery? Four living men?

The fire ticked down to coals. From the dark beyond it, Galloway's voice came low, not unfriendly:

"You're not sleeping."

"Neither are you."

"I sleep in shifts." A pause. "First watch is mine. Go to sleep, Miss Brandt. Tomorrow we climb."

She closed her eyes. Above the canyon rim the stars came out hard and cold, and the river kept saying the only thing it ever said, which was *downstream, downstream*, and Ilsa Brandt—deputy, investigator, keeper of one page and many secrets—slept at last, and dreamed of a tunnel mouth full of light.

Upstream, past the falls, past the dead camps, Wreck Junction waited with its mountain holding its breath—and tomorrow, she thought, sinking, tomorrow we find out what for.
