## 5. Dry Season Countdown

The river told the truth before anyone else did.

Coral had learned to read water the way other men read faces, and by the third morning out from the last camp, the Kettle River was confessing everything. It ran a hand's width lower than it had at dawn. The gravel bars that had been drowned in April now stood up pale and glistening like the backs of sleeping animals. Where the current had shouldered through the narrows with a sound like freight cars coupling, there was only a mutter now, a tired conversation between stones.

"Another week," he said, crouching at the edge with two fingers in the current, feeling for temperature and temper both. "Maybe less."

Ilsa Brandt stood behind him with her arms crossed, watching the water as if it might be made to answer questions directly. She had not slept well—neither of them had—but she wore her exhaustion differently than he wore his. He let his show in the slow way he moved, the extra minute he took over breakfast. She kept hers folded away behind those level gray eyes, surfacing only in small tells: the way she checked her watch too often, the way her thumb worried the stitching on her pack strap when she thought he wasn't looking.

"You're saying we can't come back this way." It wasn't quite a question.

"I'm saying the river decides when we come back, and it's deciding against us." He rose, wiped his fingers on his trousers. "Boats won't run these shallows in four days. On foot, the gorge is a month of bad climbing. There's one ford left above Wreck Junction, and after the dry season sets its teeth in, even that goes."

"So we're walking into a country that's closing behind us."

"That's the shape of it."

She absorbed this without complaint, which Coral found more unsettling than argument would have been. A woman who argued wanted something from you that you could see. A woman who simply recalculated was keeping her own ledger, and he had no way of knowing what entries she was making under his name.

They broke camp and went on.

* * *

The valley narrowed as they climbed, the walls drawing in like a throat. The old rail grade clung to the eastern slope, cut into the rock a generation ago by men with black powder and bad odds, and Coral walked it the way a man walks the floor of his own house in the dark—without looking, without thinking, his feet remembering what his mind preferred to forget.

But his mind would not forget today. Every mile brought back something: the chipped drill marks fanning across a cut face like the feathers of a shot bird; the rusted iron ring where they'd once anchored a derrick; the mile-post with its number worn to a ghost, 47 or 74, he'd never known which, because the day they set it he'd been arguing with Tomas Reyes about the proper way to brew coffee on a spirit stove, and the argument had mattered more than anything else in the world, because none of them knew yet how little time the world intended to give them.

"You knew this line," Ilsa said. Not accusing. Observing. She had a surveyor's habit of stating coordinates instead of asking directions.

"Everyone up here knew the lines."

"You walk it like you laid it out."

He didn't answer, and she didn't press, and the silence between them thickened into something almost companionable, which was its own kind of danger. Two people hiding things traveled lighter together than either would alone—that was the arithmetic of it—but the weight didn't vanish. It just got shared out, so neither carried all of it, so neither could put it down.

By midafternoon the heat came down the canyon like a lid. The river shrank as they watched it, pools going still and green between bars of exposed stone, and once they crossed a stretch where the water ran so shallow it barely covered the backs of the grayling holding in it, dozens of them, trapped and glittering, waiting for a rain that the sky had no intention of sending.

Ilsa stopped and looked at them a long moment. "That's us," she said quietly.

Coral said nothing, but he marked the pools' positions in his head, the way the river was dying from the top down, and adjusted his arithmetic. Three days. Maybe four. After that, the boats were firewood and the return route was a rumor.

* * *

They heard the man before they saw him.

It was near dusk, the light gone amber and long in the canyon, when the sound reached them—a voice, thin and cracked, carrying off the rock the way voices do in dry air. Singing, or trying to. Something formless and repetitive, the kind of noise a mind makes when it has been alone too long with itself.

Coral held up a hand and they went forward along the grade, quiet, until the trail bent around a shoulder of granite and showed them the flat below: an old loading platform, timber rotted to lace, and on it, a man.

He was sitting upright against a fallen beam with his legs straight out in front of him, boots split at the seams, and he was singing to the river. His lips were cracked and blackened, his cheeks hollowed down to architecture, and his eyes—when they found Coral and Ilsa standing on the grade above him—went wide with a terror so complete that for a moment Coral thought he might simply die of it.

"No," the man said. "No, no, you're not—you're not real. You're not real either."

"We're real." Coral came down slowly, hands visible, the way you approach a spooked animal or a man with a gun. "We're real, friend. We've come up from the lower camps. We're looking for the survey party."

"The survey—" The man laughed, and the laugh broke apart halfway through and became something else, something wetter. "You're looking for them. God help you. God help every one of you."

Ilsa was already moving, kneeling, opening her kit. "Name," she said, firm and level, a doctor's voice if not a doctor's hands. "Give me your name."

"Hollis. Peter Hollis." He said it like a password, like a thing he'd been repeating to himself for days to prove he still existed. "Instruments. I was instruments. Second assistant."

Coral knew the name from the expedition manifest. Hollis, P.—listed among the missing, listed among the dead-in-all-likelihood. And here he sat, alive by some margin the wilderness had never explained and never would.

Ilsa worked while she talked—water in measured sips, salt tab crushed into it, the blanket from her pack unrolled and wrapped around shoulders that shook though the evening was warm. Hollis drank like a man remembering how, then gripped her wrist with a strength that surprised them both.

"They're alive," he said. "The others. Some of them. They went into the tunnel."

Coral felt the words land in his chest like dropped stones. *The tunnel.* There was only one tunnel that mattered on this stretch of dead line, and it was sealed, and it had been sealed for eleven years, and he had helped pour the concrete himself.

"Wreck Junction," he said.

Hollis stared at him. "How do you know that? Nobody knows that. Nobody knows the name anymore. They burned it off the maps."

"What happened, Mr. Hollis? Start at the beginning. Slowly."

And Hollis began to talk, and the talking was worse than the silence had been.

* * *

He told it in pieces, the story coming out of him in the wrong order, circling back, snagging on details. The expedition had found readings at the junction—readings the instruments weren't supposed to be able to take, anomalies in the bedrock, a void behind the seal where the surveys said there was solid mountain. Brandt's superiors had ordered the seal opened. Not inspected. *Opened.*

"There are lights down there," Hollis said, and his voice dropped to the register men use for things they're ashamed to say aloud. "Not lamps. Not lanterns. Lights that don't flicker. The others went in on the fourth day and the lights were already burning. Eleven years, and nobody's paid the bill on them, and they're still burning."

"Phosphorescence," Ilsa said. "Mineral deposits. It's common in limestone."

Hollis turned to her with terrible patience. "Ma'am, I ran instruments for nine years. I know what mineral glow looks like. This isn't glow. Glow doesn't move when you move toward it. Glow doesn't go *out* when you ask it a question." His hand tightened on the blanket. "I asked it a question. I asked it what happened here in '09. And it answered me. It showed me. I saw the collapse. I saw who gave the order. That's why I ran. That's why I'm out here and they're in there—they stayed to guard it, ma'am. They stayed to guard what it *showed* them."

He was shaking now, full-body, and Ilsa held his shoulders and looked up at Coral over the man's head, and her face was doing something complicated—skepticism and hunger fighting for the same ground.

"He's delirious," she said. "Dehydration, exposure—"

"He's dehydrated," Coral agreed. "That doesn't make him a liar."

He looked down the darkening canyon, toward the junction he could reach in a day and a half if the weather held, toward the concrete mouth he had sealed with his own hands over the bones of his own history. Behind him, upstream, the river dropped another inch in the dark, closing another door.

Three days of water left on the return route. One tunnel ahead, sealed by him, opened by orders, lit by something that answered questions.

Hollis grabbed Coral's sleeve suddenly, pulled him close, and his breath was hot and foul and urgent against Coral's ear.

"You sealed it, didn't you?" he whispered. "I can see it in your face. You were there. You were there in '09—and they sent *you* to find us, because you're the only one who knows what's buried under that mountain."

Coral did not answer. But downstream, the last light failed on the shrinking river, and somewhere far off in the rock, faint as a heartbeat, he could almost believe he heard the water finding its way into places it had been forbidden to go.
