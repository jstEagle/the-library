## 6. Wreck Junction

The mountain announced itself long before they saw it—a smell of cold stone and old iron riding down the river valley like a passenger with a ticket for somewhere worse. Coral had been smelling it in his sleep for ten years. He'd hoped the memory was exaggeration. It wasn't.

They left the boats at dawn on the last gravel bar that would hold them, and climbed. Ilsa Brandt moved well for a woman who'd spent her life around drafting tables; she said nothing about the grade, which he appreciated, and she watched everything, which he didn't. The third survivor—Halloran, half-dead and raving—had come this same way eleven days ago, dragged along by two of his colleagues who had then turned back into the dark rather than keep running. Coral read the story of it in broken alder and a dropped canteen, tin dented where a boot had gone past it at speed.

"You've been here before," Ilsa said. Not a question. She'd been circling toward it for two days the way water circles a drain.

"I surveyed this line."

"For the company."

"For whoever paid." He stepped over a rusted fishplate half-swallowed by moss. "The company stopped paying before the end."

She let it lie. She was good at letting things lie, Ilsa Brandt, and bad at pretending she hadn't noticed them. He could feel her filing each of his answers away in whatever cabinet she kept behind those grey eyes, and he could feel, too, that the cabinet was nearly full.

The trail crested a shoulder of scree and there it was: Wreck Junction.

From the outside it looked almost gentle. A cut through the mountain's flank, timbered over, the portal framed in rotting cribwork furred with lichen. Ten years of weather had softened the scar of the collapse—the tons of rock that had come down in ninety seconds one October night and buried eleven men and the whole bright future of the northern extension. The company had sealed what remained, posted a notice, blamed the geology, and paid out settlements to widows who were told their husbands' names would appear in no report because there would be no report. Coral had signed the survey that declared the ground stable. His name was on the last document filed before the mountain disagreed.

He stood at the treeline and looked at it, and his hands went cold inside his gloves as if the decade between had been a door he could walk back through.

"Mr. Galloway?" One of the younger men behind him—Prewitt, the radio operator's assistant—shifted nervously under his pack. "Is that—"

"That's it." His voice came out flatter than he intended. "Camp here. Nobody approaches the portal until I say."

Ilsa's eyebrows went up. "You think they're dangerous?"

"I think they've been barricaded in a hole in a mountain for three weeks guarding something worth killing for, and I intend to be the first thing they see, so they don't shoot the second thing."

* * *

He went in alone, as he'd promised himself he would, though promise was too kind a word for it. Compelled. The way water is compelled downhill.

Inside the portal the air changed—colder, wetter, tasting of mineral and lamp oil and, faintly, of men. His carbide lamp threw a disc of light down the old right-of-way, and the tunnel swallowed it after thirty feet, but thirty feet was enough to show him the first barricade: cross-ties pried from the roadbed and driven upright into the ballast, wedged with rail stubs, built by someone who understood construction and expected assault. Behind it, darkness. And from the darkness, a voice.

"Stop there." Cracked, hoarse, but steady. A woman's voice. "Name yourself."

"Coral Galloway. I came upriver with Halloran. He's alive. He's fifty yards below your barricade eating soup out of a tin cup, if the others got him down the slope without dropping him."

A long silence. Then, low, to someone else: "It's him. The surveyor. Halloran said—" Another pause, and when the voice came back it had an edge of something that might have been relief or might have been its opposite. "Come forward slow. Hands visible. There are four rifles on you, Mr. Galloway, and we have had a very bad month."

He walked forward with his hands out and his lamp held high, and the light found them one by one as he came: a man crouched behind the ties with a service rifle braced; a second man standing, younger, bandaged at the temple; the woman—tall, sunburned, her hair tied back with what looked like survey flagging tape—and behind her, half in shadow, a fourth figure sitting against the tunnel wall with a canvas portfolio clutched in both arms the way a child holds a doll during a storm.

"Deputy Director Okafor," the woman said. "Northern Resources Survey. You'll forgive us if we don't shake hands." She looked him over with the flat professional attention he recognized from Ilsa, and something in his chest tightened. They were all the same, these people. Same training. Same eyes. "You're the mapmaker. Brandt said you'd come."

"Brandt sent word ahead?"

"Brandt sent word the day she left." Okafor's mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "She's thorough."

*That,* Coral thought, *is one word for it.*

"Why did you seal yourselves in?" he asked. "You had three weeks. You could have walked out."

"We tried walking out." The young one with the bandage spoke for the first time, bitter. "Something followed us down from the ridge. Two men, maybe three. Government issue boots, government issue rifles, and no interest in talking. Vasquez took a round in the shoulder getting back to the portal. We pulled the ties across and they settled in to wait us out." He jerked his chin upward. "There's a ventilation raise, forty feet up. They know about it. We hear them moving around up top most nights."

Coral turned that over. A pursuit party, armed, patient, officially nonexistent. The same kind of party, he'd have wagered anything he owned, that had erased the first camp so cleanly—not a footprint leaving it, tents intact, instruments still humming. Someone was managing this situation with great care, and the management included the possibility of bodies.

"And the thing you're guarding," he said. "The thing in the portfolio."

Okafor glanced at the seated figure—the archivist, he realized now, a small grey man named Lindqvist whose name had meant nothing to him on the manifest and meant everything now. "Show him," she said quietly.

Lindqvist opened the portfolio with fingers that shook, whether from weakness or reverence Coral couldn't tell. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth, were documents. Originals, by the look of the paper stock—the heavy rag stock the company had used for internal correspondence in the years before the disaster. Coral took the top sheet and read it, and the tunnel seemed to tilt around him.

It was a work order. Dated nineteen days before the collapse. Directive to continue blasting operations in the north heading despite water ingress readings exceeding safe limits—despite readings *he himself had taken*, readings he had wired south with his own hand, readings that had been acknowledged and then overridden. Signed by a regional director whose name Coral had spent ten years not saying aloud. Below it, a second memo, drafted after the collapse, marked confidential: recommended assignment of responsibility to field survey personnel, naming three men.

His own name was first.

The other two were Teodor Brandvold and Elias Marchetti. Teo had died in the collapse. Elias had lived another four years, drinking, and gone off the ice at Kettle Narrows one February with everyone agreeing it was an accident, because agreeing was easier than asking why a man who'd survived the mountain couldn't survive the peace afterward.

"They knew," Coral said. His voice sounded like someone else's. "They had my readings. They ordered the blast anyway, and when it came down they put it on us."

"The water table was misreported in the final inquiry," Okafor said. "Deliberately. These memos prove the misreporting originated above the field level. Mr. Galloway—your crew wasn't negligent. Your crew was *sacrificed*. That's why we came. That's why the ministry wanted this quiet, and why certain people want it quieter still." She held his gaze. "You understand now what you're carrying out of this mountain."

He did understand. He understood that the paper in his hands weighed more than all the rock above them, that it could crush or free a dozen families depending on which way it fell, and that every person who touched it became either a witness or a target. He thought of Teo's daughter, who would be twenty now, raised on the official version of her father's incompetence. He thought of Elias going off the ice. He thought of ten years of maps traded for supplies in towns that asked no questions, and how much of that silence had really been shame wearing anonymity's coat.

Behind him, boots on gravel. Ilsa had ignored his instruction completely and come in with Prewitt, and she stood now at the edge of the lamplight reading the memo upside down with an expression he couldn't parse—which frightened him more than the rifles had.

"You knew," he said to her. "When you signed the telegram. You knew what they'd find."

"I knew what they were looking for." She came closer, her voice low, meant only for him. "And I knew your name was on the scapegoat list, because I've read the file, Coral. All of it. Including the part where the surveyor who signed the stability certificate disappeared afterward and resurfaced mapping dead rail lines under a name nobody can trace." Her eyes didn't waver. "I'm not your enemy. But we need to talk about what happens when we get out—because there are people waiting for these papers, and not all of them want them published."

Above them, faint through forty feet of rock, came the sound of movement at the ventilation raise. Deliberate. Unhurried. Boots on stone, and then silence, which was worse.

Okafor's hand closed around her rifle sling. "Gentlemen," she said to the dark, "it seems our visitors heard the conversation too."

* * *

Coral folded the memos back into the oilcloth with hands that had finally stopped shaking, mostly. Whatever else happened in the next hour, the fault line under his life had finished opening. There was no sealing it again. There was only deciding which side of it to stand on when the shooting started—and from the weight of the footsteps overhead, it was going to start soon.
