## 7. Loyalty Is a Kind of Lie

Ilsa Brandt had learned long ago that the truth was a tool like any other—hammer, lever, blade—and that the first rule of tools was knowing when to set them down.

She sat across the fire from Coral Galloway and watched him feed the flames with the same economy he gave everything: one stick at a time, each placed as though the fire had asked for it specifically. Beyond him, the survivors slept in a huddle against the cold coming off the river, five shapes under canvas, breathing. The sixth had been buried that morning in a cairn of river stones, and the ground where they'd dug his grave still showed dark and raw, like a wound nobody had bothered to dress.

The records lay between them in a waterproof satchel. Coral had not touched it since they'd left Wreck Junction. He looked at it the way a man looks at a snake he knows is venomous but cannot bring himself to kill.

"You haven't opened it," she said.

"I know what's in it."

"You know what you think is in it."

His eyes came up. In the firelight they were the color of wet slate, and just as hard to read anything into. "You've had three days to tell me why you're really here, Miss Brandt. You've spent them counting my supplies and memorizing my routes. So either say it tonight or stop pretending you're going to."

There it was. She had rehearsed this conversation on the trail, in the boat, lying awake listening to the river talk to itself. Rehearsal, she knew from experience, was worth almost nothing. The real thing always went differently, because the real thing had Coral Galloway's face in it, and his face was doing something she hadn't planned for: it was tired. Not defeated—she doubted this man could be defeated by anything short of geology—but tired in the way stone is tired, worn down by water over years no one had counted.

"My name isn't Brandt," she said.

He didn't move. "I know."

That stopped her for half a breath. "You know."

"The telegram signature was wrong. A deputy who stays behind doesn't sign with her full name and title; she signs so the record shows she stayed behind. That's a witness signature, not an administrative one. And your boots were government issue, northern pattern, but resoled by a cobbler in the capital—the stitching gave it away. Civilians up here don't wear capital resoles." He turned the stick in his hands. "I didn't know what you were. Only that you weren't what your file said."

"And you let me come anyway."

"I needed someone who could keep pace and wouldn't panic at the ford." A pause. "And I wanted to see what you'd do when it got hard."

Ilsa laughed before she could stop herself—a short, dry sound with no humor in it. "And what did I do?"

"You carried a drowning man's pack after we lost the boats. You didn't complain about the rations. You sat with Ellery when he couldn't sleep and you didn't ask him questions while he shook." Coral fed the stick to the fire. "So whatever you are, you're not cruel. That narrowed the list considerably."

She should have felt exposed. Instead she felt something closer to relief, which frightened her more. Ten years of building a self out of paperwork and careful omissions, and this man had unwrapped it with a look at her boot heels.

"My name is Ilse Brandeis," she said. "I'm an investigator attached to the Ministry of Transport's internal review office. Three years ago I was assigned to audit the closure files of the northern lines. Most of them were ordinary—falling traffic, deferred maintenance, politics. Wreck Junction wasn't ordinary. The disaster file was thin. Too thin. Eleven dead, a line abandoned, and a final report of nine pages that read like it was written by someone in a hurry to be somewhere else." She held his gaze. "I requested the original survey records. They'd been destroyed. All of them. Every page, every map, every instrument log. Officially: water damage. Unofficially—" she gestured at the wilderness around them, the black trees, the indifferent stars, "—unofficially, water damage doesn't burn."

The fire popped. Downriver, something moved—a moose, probably, or the wind rearranging itself among the pines. Coral hadn't blinked.

"I came north with the expedition," she went on, "to verify what their instruments found. The Ministry suspected the old reports were falsified. They sent surveyors because surveyors produce numbers, and numbers can be entered into evidence. What they did not expect—" and here she made herself say it plainly, because he deserved plainness, "—was that the surveyors would find the men who built the line still alive inside it, sitting on proof that the collapse was ordered. That the drainage cut was signed off against every warning the crew filed. That when the mountain came down, the company needed someone to blame, and it chose eleven names, and yours was one of them."

"Coral Galloway died ten years ago," he said quietly. "Everyone knows that."

"Yes," she said. "I've read the death certificate. It's very convincing. Right down to the cause."

For a long moment there was only the fire and the river and the small sounds of sleeping men. Then Coral said, "Say the rest. Whatever you came here to say. Say all of it."

* * *

She laid it out the way she'd been trained to lay things out: facts first, then implications, then the offer. Her voice stayed level. Inside, she was aware of a strange sensation, like walking out onto ice and hearing it hold.

"Testify," she said. "Come back with us—not as Galloway, not yet, but as a witness. The Ministry will reopen the file. Your crew's names get cleared. Voss, Marchetti, the Okafor brothers—all of them. Their families get pensions, apologies, headstones that don't lie. You get your name back. Legally. Publicly. The man you were stops being a ghost." She let that sit. "In exchange, the surveyors' findings enter the record. The documents they guarded for weeks in the dark. The testimony of five exhausted men who nearly died bringing those papers out. Every family connected to the original disaster gets pulled back in—depositions, hearings, newspapermen. The widow in Fort Alder whose husband was the foreman. The daughter of the dispatcher who signed the order. All of them, subpoenaed, photographed, quoted. All of it public. Forever."

"That's not an exchange," Coral said. "That's a bill."

"It's how vindication works. It's never free. Someone always pays the difference." She leaned forward. "Coral. Listen to me. I have read every surviving scrap of that file. I know what was done to your crew. If you testify, I will personally guarantee—"

"You can't guarantee anything." His voice didn't rise. That was the terrible thing about it; it never rose. "You're an auditor. You don't control the newspapers or the committees or what happens to a woman in Fort Alder when a reporter puts her husband's name next to the word *murder*. You can promise me justice. You can't promise me mercy. And those people up there—" he nodded toward the sleeping forms, "—they didn't survive the river and the mountain so their discovery could become a headline."

"So the answer is no."

"The answer is that I'm thinking." He looked into the fire, and Ilsa saw something move behind his face that she recognized from her own mirror on bad nights: the shape of a temptation being taken seriously. "Ten years I've been dead. Do you know what that costs? Not the fear. The *smallness*. I sign maps with a false name. I buy flour from a man who thinks he knows me and I let him be wrong. My crew's families—I send money through two intermediaries so the postmark can't be traced, and every Christmas I write letters I burn instead of mail. You're offering me the chance to stop burning letters." His jaw tightened. "That's not nothing, Ilse Brandeis. Don't pretend it's nothing."

"I'm not."

"But you're also not telling me the whole price." He turned to face her fully, and now the slate had cracked and there was something underneath, hot and old. "Because if I testify, the inquiry needs the records authenticated, which means the surveyors testify, which means they're named, which means the people who buried this once—and they're not all dead, Ilse, companies like that grow new heads—know exactly who has the proof and exactly where to find them. You said it yourself. Water damage doesn't burn. Men do."

She had considered this. She had considered it on the train north, on the river, at the graveside this morning. She had no good answer, only an honest one.

"Yes," she said. "That's part of the cost. I won't insult you by pretending otherwise."

"Then why offer it at all?"

And there it was—the question she'd been circling for three days, the one she'd hoped he wouldn't ask, because the true answer would cost her more than the false one ever could.

"Because I've spent three years reading about eleven men who were erased so that a balance sheet could stay clean," she said. "And I told myself I was different. That I worked inside the system precisely so the system would have one honest person in it. But honest people inside systems mostly just write memos that get filed." She looked at her hands, at the rope burns and the blisters the river had given her. "I offered it because it's the best I can do. Because it might be enough. And because if I didn't offer it, I'd be just another person deciding what the truth weighs without asking the people it falls on."

Coral studied her for a long time. The fire burned low between them, and neither of them fed it.

"You're offering me my life back," he said finally, "and asking me to spend other people's peace to buy it."

"I'm asking you to decide. That's all anyone can ask."

He stood. For a moment she thought he would walk away, but he only crossed to the satchel and rested his hand on it—not opening it, just touching it, the way you touch a grave marker to confirm it's real.

"These men trusted me to bring them home," he said. "Not to decide what their suffering is worth. When we're across the last ford—if we cross it—you'll have your answer. Not before."

"That's fair."

"It's not fair," he said. "It's a delay. Fairness ran out on this line ten years ago." He paused at the edge of the firelight, and when he spoke again his voice had dropped, almost gentle. "Get some sleep, Investigator. Tomorrow we hit the badlands, and after tomorrow the river decides how much of this argument even matters."

She watched him settle against a log with his back to her, watchful even in rest, a dead man keeping vigil over living ones. Ilsa lay down and pulled her blanket to her chin and stared at the stars through the pine branches, and understood suddenly that whatever answer he gave at the ford, she had already lost something tonight: the comfortable belief that she was on the side of the light.

Downriver, faint but unmistakable, came a sound that did not belong to the wilderness—the far-off stutter of an engine, running rough, where no engine should be.

Coral's head came up in the dark. Their eyes met across the dead fire.

"They're early," he said.
