## 8. The River Takes Its Toll

The river had been a rumor for three days before it became a fact.

Coral heard it first as a change in the air—that flat, mineral smell of water moving over ground it hadn't touched in years, rising up through the spruce like a warning passed from tree to tree. By the time they reached the bluff above the lower ford, the Kettle was no longer the shallow, braided thing he'd charted a decade ago. It ran brown and muscular, carrying whole trees sideways, its voice a low continuous roar that swallowed conversation.

"Flash flood," he said. "Headwater storm. It'll drop in a day, maybe two."

"Maybe three," Ilsa said. She stood at his shoulder with her field glasses raised, and he could see her doing the arithmetic he was doing—the water against the calendar, the calendar against the dry season, the dry season against the distance still between them and the railhead at Copper Landing. Every day the river stayed high, the shallows downstream shrank another foot. In a week there would be no crossing at all, only forty miles of mud flats that would suck their boots off and leave them.

Behind them, the six survivors of Wreck Junction waited in the trees with what remained of their gear. They had come out of the mountain hollow-eyed and silent, blinking at daylight like men pulled from a cellar, and in four days of hard travel they had not once spoken of what they'd found inside the tunnel. They carried it instead—wrapped in oilcloth, strapped to their backs, guarded in shifts through the night. Whatever the records weighed on paper, they carried them like lead.

"We can't wait," said Halloran, the eldest of them, coming forward. His beard had gone gray in the dark of the tunnel, or so it seemed; his hands hadn't stopped trembling since Coral first saw him by lantern light behind the barricade. "If we lose the season, we lose everything. The river's crossable. Look—it's dropped since morning."

It had dropped, an inch maybe, the way a wolf drops its head before it lunges. Coral said nothing for a long moment. He looked at the water, at the drowned alders thrashing in the current, at the far bank where the trail climbed away toward safety, and he felt the old familiar pull of the man he used to be—the one who would have said *wait*, who would have made the cautious call and let someone else argue him out of it. That man had buried a crew once already. He knew exactly how it was done.

"We cross," he heard himself say. "Now, while it's falling. Light loads. Boats first, gear lashed down, swimmers on the lines. And nobody—" he turned and made himself look at each of them, one by one, "—nobody lets go of anybody. If you go in, you hold the rope or you drown holding it. Those are the terms."

Ilsa's eyes were on him. He didn't look back at her.

* * *

They built the crossing out of what the wilderness gave them: two canvas boats patched with pitch, a hundred feet of rope, and the last of their nerve. Coral went first, ferrying the line across in the smaller boat while the current shouldered it like a drunk shouldering a door. Halfway over, the river found a seam of deep water and the bow swung downstream, and for ten seconds that lasted an hour he understood, with perfect clarity, that this was how it ended—not with testimony, not with truth, but with brown water in his lungs and his name unwritten anywhere at all.

Then the keel bit gravel and he was across, hauling the line taut, anchoring it to a spruce thick as a church pillar.

The survivors came over in pairs after that, ferried and hauled, soaked to the chest, laughing the high thin laughter of people who have been frightened past the bottom of themselves. Ilsa crossed fourth, rowing with her jaw set, refusing the rope when she reached the far side, wringing out her sleeves with the brisk economy of a woman who considered being half-drowned a scheduling inconvenience.

Halloran went fifth with the records lashed inside his coat. Voss—the young one, the surveyor with the burned hand—went sixth, and the boy Aldous, barely twenty, went seventh, and somewhere in the middle of the channel the river decided it was done being merciful.

Coral saw it before anyone: a wall of debris rounding the upstream bend, cottonwood trunks riding the crest like battering rams. "Line!" he roared. "Haul! HAUL!"

Aldous was mid-channel when the wave took him. The rope snapped taut, jerked, and then the strain came back wrong—lighter—and Coral understood with a sick lurch that either the line had parted or the boy had let go. Then he saw Aldous surface, arms windmilling, going under again, and Voss did the thing that Coral would spend the rest of his life trying to describe and never manage: he simply let go of the bank. No hesitation, no shout, no calculation. He dove into floodwater with a burned hand and a surveyor's build and no chance at all, and he caught the boy by the collar, and the river took them both downstream like it was owed.

They got Aldous out. Coral himself went into the shallows below the bend and dragged the boy onto the gravel bar, coughing, alive, clutching nothing. Voss they found two hundred yards down, wedged under a sweeper with his face in the water and his burned hand still closed around a length of severed rope.

They worked on him for a long time. Nobody spoke during it. When Coral finally sat back on his heels, his knees in the wet gravel, the silence where Voss's breathing should have been was the loudest thing he had ever heard.

* * *

They buried him above the flood line because there was nothing else to do. Halloran read something from memory—a psalm, half-remembered, the words worn smooth like river stones. Ilsa stood apart with her hat in her hands, and Coral watched her and thought: *she'll write this down tonight. Every word. Name, date, cause of death.* It was her job. It was what she was for.

That night, in the firelight, the fracture came.

It started small, the way faults start. One of the survivors—Merrick, a geologist with a widow's stoop even standing upright—looked across the flames at Ilsa and said, quietly, "When we get out. What happens to what we know?"

"The inquiry takes the records," Ilsa said. "Testimony follows. That was always the arrangement."

"And the families?" Merrick's voice didn't rise. That was the terrible thing about it; it never rose. "Voss has a mother in Fort Selkirk. Halloran's brother drove the number two engine the year it collapsed. You put all that into a report, you don't vindicate them—you hang them out for every newspaper from here to the coast. *Scapegoats' kin demand apology.* That's what they'll print. That's all they'll print."

"I'm not the enemy here," Ilsa said, and her voice was level but Coral heard the hairline crack in it. "I was sent to find out if the cover-up happened. It happened. My report is how it stops happening."

"It's how it gets *used*," Halloran said. He hadn't spoken since the burial. Now he leaned forward into the firelight, and his gray-bearded face looked suddenly very old. "Miss Brandt. I spent eleven days behind a rock pile guarding those papers because I believed they'd set things right. But right isn't a document. Right is my brother dying with his name clean, and staying clean. Some truths are just wounds with dates attached."

"You'd bury it?" Ilsa turned to him. "After everything? After Voss?"

"After Voss," Halloran said, "most of all."

The argument went around the fire like weather. Merrick and the others lining up on one side, Ilsa alone on the other, and Coral sitting at the edge of the light saying nothing, feeling the moment arrive the way he'd felt the flood arrive—as a change in the air, a fact becoming unavoidable. They kept looking at him. All of them, eventually, looking at him, because he was the one who'd brought them out, and leadership is a thing that gets handed to you whether you reach for it or not.

He had spent ten years making himself unaccountable to anyone. Ten years trading maps for supplies in towns that asked no questions, keeping his name out of ledgers and his history out of conversations. And now seven exhausted people were waiting for him to decide what the truth was worth, and he understood that whatever he said next, he would be choosing it openly, in front of witnesses, as a man with a position—for the first time since the tunnel came down.

"Here's what I know," he said finally. The fire cracked. Everyone stilled. "I know that reports don't raise the dead. I know that governments don't keep promises, they keep *files*. And I know that the mountain doesn't care what any of us decide—it keeps what it keeps." He looked at Ilsa, and let her see everything in his face, because she had earned that much and he was tired of hiding things from people who bled beside him. "But I also know that if it stays buried, it happens again. Maybe not here. Maybe not to us. Somewhere, some crew gets ordered into bad ground because orders are cheaper than caution, and nobody writes it down because we taught them it stays buried."

"So you're with me," Ilsa said.

"I'm with the dead," Coral said. "And the living. In that order. We carry the records out—all of them. What happens after—" he held up a hand before the fire could erupt again, "—what happens after is a fight for another day, and I'll be in it. But nobody decides anything tonight except that we sleep, and tomorrow we move, because the river won't wait and neither will the dry season."

Silence. Then Halloran nodded, slow, like a gate swinging shut. Merrick lay back against his pack and turned his face away. And Ilsa looked at Coral across the fire for a long moment, and whatever she saw there, she didn't argue. She rolled into her blanket with her back to the flames.

Coral took the first watch. He sat with the river talking to itself in the dark, and he thought about Voss letting go of the bank without hesitating, and he understood that the man he had been—the invisible man, the man with no position—had drowned in that channel too, and something else had crawled out on the gravel bar in his place. Something responsible. Something that would have to live with whatever came next.

Downstream, faint on the night wind, he heard it: voices. More than one. Carrying along the water the way sound does in cold air, and beneath them, unmistakable, the clink of metal on stone.

Somebody was building something at the lower ford.
