## 9. Testimony at Gunpoint

The ford announced itself before they saw it: a widening of the river where the current spread thin and braided over pale gravel, the water running shin-deep and quick as spilled mercury. Coral had marked it on his burned-in memory years ago—the last place between here and the settlement where a man could cross without a boat, without a rope, without prayer. Beyond it the river dropped into a gorge no swimmer survived.

They came down to it in the grey of morning, eleven of them now, moving like survivors move—staggered, silent, watching each other's backs out of habit rather than trust. The surveyors carried their records wrapped in oilcloth against their chests. Ilsa walked beside Coral with her rifle slung and her eyes doing what they always did, cataloguing everything, trusting nothing.

She stopped first.

"Smoke," she said.

Coral smelled it a half-second later. Woodsmoke, faint, riding the cold air off the water. Not from their own fire, dead since midnight. Someone was already at the ford.

He raised his fist and the column froze among the jack pines. Through the trees he could see them: six men in government khaki, tents pitched neat as parade ground, a canvas screen around a latrine trench, a longboat tied at the near bank. And set up in the middle of the gravel bar, absurd and unmistakable, a folding table with a typewriter on it.

"They've been waiting," Ilsa said quietly. "That's not a patrol. That's an office."

"Then let's not keep them waiting." Coral handed her the records bundle without looking at it, the transfer smooth as passing salt. "You know the far bank?"

"I know it."

"There's a cut above the scree, two miles north. You can come back down to the trail after dark. I'll meet you at the burned spruce."

"And you?"

Coral stepped out of the treeline into the open, hands loose at his sides, walking toward the table and the men behind it with the unhurried gait of a man who had every right to be there. It was the oldest trick he knew, and the one he'd sworn never to use again: not hiding, but belonging. For ten years he had spent his invisibility like a miser. Now he meant to spend it all at once.

The nearest man reached for his rifle. A voice stopped him—a dry, unhurried voice from behind the table.

"Let him come."

The man at the table was fifty-odd, silver at the temples, wearing a civilian coat that fit like it had been measured for someone else. He had the look of a man who signed things for a living. His name, when he offered it, was Halloran, and he said it the way a man lays down a card he knows wins the trick.

"Coral Galloway," Halloran said. "Or should I say—" and he paused, delicately, "—the name you used before."

The word landed exactly where Halloran wanted it. Coral felt the old crew's names rise in his throat like floodwater and forced them down. *They're gone. They can't be touched by anything this man says.*

"You have my party's people," Coral said. "I want them across the river."

"Your party." Halloran smiled thinly. "Miss Brandt is not your party. She reports to me. As of yesterday, so do you—or so does whatever name we decide to write down when this is finished." He gestured at the empty chair across the table. "Sit. The records, if you please. Then everyone goes home, and nobody's history gets aired in front of a tribunal."

Behind him, two of the khaki men drifted casually toward the treeline where the survivors were hidden. Coral saw it and did not flinch, because flinching was spending money he might still need.

"The records are with your deputy," he said. "Across the river by now, probably."

Halloran's smile didn't move but something behind it did. "Ilsa Brandt," he said softly, "has been a problem since Ottawa. Sit down, Galloway. We'll wait together. The river isn't going anywhere."

*Neither am I,* Coral thought, and sat.

* * *

They talked for two hours while the sun climbed and the khaki men cast about the treeline finding nothing but old campsites and cold ash. Halloran talked the way water talks—patiently, ceaselessly, wearing grooves. He talked about tribunals and pensions, about how the dead could be honored properly only in daylight, about how a man with Coral's past owed the truth to the families of the scapegoated crew.

And that was the blade in it: everything Halloran said was almost true. Coral had wanted vindication once. Had dreamed of it, in the bad years, with a name like a stone in his boot.

"You believe I care about that," Coral said finally. "About being cleared."

"I believe every man wants his name back."

"No." Coral stood, slowly, and Halloran's hand moved toward the drawer of the folding table where Coral would have bet his life a pistol lay. "I spent ten years learning what a name costs. You want to put those families on a stand. You want the survivors testifying until the mountain's gutted and the junction's a tourist stop with a plaque. I've seen what daylight does up here. It doesn't heal anything. It just dries things out until they crack."

"So you'll lie for them."

"I'll stall for them," Coral said, "which is different," and he watched Halloran understand, one beat too late, that the conversation had never been a negotiation. It had been a clock.

* * *

He gave them the false route standing up, pointing north along the river with a surveyor's flat certainty he hadn't felt in his shoulders for a decade. There was a trail, he told them—high water route, good footing, would bring them around the gorge to intercept the fugitives by nightfall. Every word of it was true except the most important one: the trail ended at the badlands, where the shale terraces had been sliding into the river since before the railway died, where the ground shrugged off whole slopes in a wet season and this was the driest, most treacherous week of the year.

Halloran weighed it. Coral could see him weighing it—the alternative was admitting his office couldn't find four exhausted people in country he didn't know, and men like Halloran would rather gamble than admit that.

"Come with us, then," Halloran said. "Guide us."

"I'm done guiding," Coral said. "I'm crossing."

For a moment he thought Halloran would draw the pistol then, right there on the gravel bar with the typewriter glinting in the sun. Instead Halloran studied him for a long moment, and something almost like respect crossed his face, or its bureaucratic equivalent.

"You understand," Halloran said, "that when this is over, we will find you. There is no terrain for what you are."

"I know," Coral said. "I've been counting on it."

They took the longboat and went north, six rifles and one folded table, following a trail that would dissolve under them by mid-afternoon. Coral stood on the gravel bar and watched them go, and felt the strangest thing—not fear, not triumph, but lightness, as though he'd set down a pack he'd forgotten he was carrying. Ten years of silence, spent in a single morning. It bought four lives and a box of paper. Cheap, he thought. Cheap at twice the price.

* * *

He crossed the ford alone in the early afternoon, the cold water pulling at his knees, the gorge roaring somewhere below like a held breath. On the far bank, in the shadow of the burned spruce, Ilsa waited with the survivors, and her face when she saw him was the closest thing to open relief he had ever seen her wear.

"He took the bait," she said.

"He took the bait." Coral wrung out his trousers and looked north, where the badlands waited, patient as arithmetic. By now Halloran's men would be climbing the first terrace. By three o'clock the shale would start talking. By four—

A sound rolled down the valley, low and long, like the mountain clearing its throat. Dust bloomed white above the northern bluffs, a slow pale flower opening against the blue.

Ilsa stared at it. "That wasn't supposed to happen yet."

"It happens when it happens," Coral said. But his chest had gone tight, because the slide had come down closer to the river than he'd promised himself it would, and somewhere under that dust were six men who had followed his voice to get there.

He turned south, toward home, toward the inquiry waiting at the end of it—and found Ilsa watching him with an expression he couldn't read.

"How many routes do you know through these hills?" she asked.

"Enough," Coral said. "But there's one more thing between us and the settlement, and it isn't the government."

Below them, far downriver, the water level had dropped another foot overnight. The dry season was winning.
