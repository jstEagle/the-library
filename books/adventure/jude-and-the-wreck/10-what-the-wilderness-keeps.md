## 10. What the Wilderness Keeps

The inquiry room in Fort Alderman smelled of coal smoke and wet wool, and it had the particular silence of a place where men waited to be told what they were allowed to remember.

Coral Galloway sat at the long table for eleven days. Eleven days of questions asked in careful circles, of maps unrolled and weighted at the corners with brass, of voices that never rose because rising would suggest something had gone wrong that someone might have to answer for. The chairman was a thin man named Hobb whose cufflinks caught the lamplight every time he folded his hands, and who had a way of saying *the incident* as though the word itself were a lid.

Coral answered everything. He gave them the river gauges, the portage lines, the hour the water dropped below the ford at Kettle Narrows. He gave them the sequence of camps, the state of the provisions, the bearing he'd taken off the cairn above Wreck Junction on the morning they came down out of the trees, hollow-eyed and sunburned, seven survivors walking in single file behind him like men leaving their own funerals. He gave them the drowning—Halloran's name spoken plainly, once, so that it entered the record and could not be sanded out later. He gave them the flash flood, the lost boats, the badlands route he'd fed to the second government party at gunpoint, though he described that last part carefully, without flourish, letting the chairman believe it had been improvisation rather than arithmetic done in his head over three sleepless nights.

He gave them everything except the map.

The final map—the one he'd drawn himself, in pencil, on the last night before the ford, working by firelight while the survivors slept—showed the interior of the sealed tunnel at Wreck Junction. It showed where the old crew's records had been found, wedged in a rusted strongbox behind the third bulkhead: survey reports, telegrams, an order signed with a deputy commissioner's initials instructing that the east heading be worked past all safe measure through the winter of the disaster, and that if it failed, the failure would be attributed to "geological instability and crew negligence." It showed the names. His own name among them, spelled differently then, attached to a man who had died in the dark so that the paperwork could stay clean.

Coral had copied none of it. He had memorized what mattered and burned nothing yet, because burning too early is its own kind of confession. The map sat rolled inside his coat through eleven days of testimony, against his ribs, warm as a second heartbeat.

On the twelfth day, Hobb leaned back and said, "I think we have what we require, Mr. Galloway."

*Mr. Galloway.* Not the name from the old telegrams. Not the name from the crew manifest, which Coral knew sat in some archive three thousand miles south, misspelled and misfiled, waiting for a man curious enough to pull it. Hobb said *Galloway* the way you'd hand back a borrowed tool—politely, and with no intention of looking at it closely.

"Then I'll be going," Coral said.

"One matter." Hobb's cufflinks flashed. "The tunnel itself. Our engineers will want a full survey of the interior. You're the only man living who's been inside since the sealing. We'd expect your cooperation in the spring."

"The spring thaw floods the lower headings," Coral said. "You'd lose men."

Hobb smiled the smile of a man who had already decided the answer to that objection was more men. "We'd expect your cooperation regardless."

Coral looked at him for a long moment—at the cufflinks, at the lid he kept pressing down over the word *incident*—and understood that the inquiry had not come north for the truth at all. It had come north to establish how much truth could be safely buried a second time. The first cover-up had cost thirty-one lives. The second would cost only paper.

"I'll consider it," Coral said, which was the polite northern way of saying *no*, and left the room before his face could tell the whole story.

* * *

He burned the map that night, in the stove of the supply shack behind the trading post, where he'd lived for ten years under a name that fit him now better than the one he'd been born with.

He did it slowly. That surprised him. He had expected relief, or grief, or some clean single feeling, but what came instead was a kind of attention—the same attention he gave to reading water, to judging ice, to all the small acts of care that kept a man alive out there. He unrolled the map one last time on the plank table and let the lamplight fall across his own penciled lines: the bulkheads, the strongbox, the little cross he'd marked where Halloran's body had come to rest against the old rail bed when the flood finally gave him back.

Thirty-one names from the first disaster. One from the second. He read them all again, silently, the way you'd speak over graves.

Then he fed the map into the stove a corner at a time, watching the pencil lines lift and curl and go orange, then black, then nothing. The strongbox itself was still in the tunnel—he'd made sure of that, made sure the survivors' barricade held until they walked out and let the mountain close behind them—but a strongbox is only evidence, and evidence can be argued with. What he'd drawn was testimony, and testimony, once given, cannot be taken back. So he took it back himself, before anyone could ask for it.

The dead had signed their names to that mountain once already, and the government had spent those names like small coin. They would not spend them twice. Let the record say geological instability. Let the families keep the version they'd lived forty years with—the version where their fathers and brothers had been unlucky rather than expendable. Coral had stood in enough kitchens in enough company towns to know which lie a widow can carry and which one breaks her back. Mercy was not the same as truth. Sometimes it was heavier, and you carried it farther.

When the last corner went, he opened the stove door and stirred the ash with a poker until no fragment larger than a fingernail remained. Then he sat a while in the cooling shack, listening to the wind work at the eaves, and felt the strangest thing, which was lightness—not joy, nothing so simple, but the particular ease of a man who has set down something he was never meant to carry alone.

* * *

Ilsa Brandt came to the shack two mornings later, before the steamer south was due to take on freight.

She stood in the doorway without knocking, travel coat still buttoned, her breath smoking in the cold, and looked around at the interior of his life: the cot, the stove, the survey instruments racked with military neatness, the shelf of traded maps going soft at the edges from handling. She had spent nine days walking out beside him after the ford, and she had learned to read him the way he read terrain—which meant she saw everything the room told her and understood most of it.

"You didn't give them the interior," she said.

"No."

"They'll send engineers anyway. In the spring. Hobb told me himself."

"They'll find a flooded heading and a strongbox full of rust," Coral said. "If they find anything at all. Water does a thorough job over a winter." He did not tell her about the charge he'd set in the bulkhead on the way out, wired to the spring melt, timed to bring the third section down for good. Some facts were rationed even from allies.

Ilsa was quiet for a moment. Outside, a dog barked twice at the far end of the settlement and thought better of it.

"I was sent to verify the cover-up," she said, as if continuing a conversation they'd had in fragments across a hundred miles of river. "My instructions were to confirm the records existed and report their contents. Not to recover them. Not to protect them." She paused. "I've written my report. It says the expedition failed due to inadequate provisioning and hostile terrain. It says the survivors were delirious and their statements unreliable. It says Wreck Junction should be struck from any future survey consideration on grounds of safety."

Coral looked at her. "That's not what you were sent to do."

"No," she agreed. "It's what I'm choosing to have done." She took an envelope from inside her coat and set it on the table between them—a copy of her report, her handwriting small and level. "You'll want to know exactly what the record says. In case anyone comes asking what you know."

"Why?" Coral asked. It was the question he'd been carrying since the pass, since she'd stood in the shallows with the water tearing at her skirts and told him the truth instead of the order. "Vindication was yours to sell. Your career. Everything you crossed the country for."

Ilsa considered the stove, the ash still pale in its belly, and evidently understood without being told what had burned there.

"Because I watched you spend ten days keeping seven frightened people alive," she said, "and not one minute making them useful to you. Because Halloran drowned pulling a boy out of the current, and the only honest thing left to do with that is make sure it isn't turned into a footnote in somebody's file." She buttoned her coat higher against the draft. "And because someone once erased my father's name from a manifest too, Mr. Galloway. I recognized the handwriting."

She didn't explain further. She was not a woman who explained further. She touched the brim of her hat—half salute, half farewell—and went out into the white morning, and Coral heard her boots crunch away toward the landing until the wind took the sound.

He never learned whether she reported the burned map. He suspected she had guessed it within a day of the ford, when he'd given the inquiry his bearings a half-degree generous and his distances a mile short, and she had said nothing then either. Some silences are agreements. Hers was the largest he'd ever been offered, and he honored it the only way he could, by never speaking of her to anyone, ever, so that her choice stayed as private as his own.

* * *

Spring came late that year, the way it does after a hard winter, and when it came it came all at once, the river rising brown and muscular under the ice breakup, the willows flushing green along the cutbanks overnight.

In April, a government launch put in at the settlement with four engineers and a crate of instruments, bound upriver to inspect the junction. Coral sold them provisions, drew them a chart of the rapids—accurate, he was scrupulous about that; the river deserved honesty even if the government didn't—and watched them churn away into the narrows. Three weeks later they came back down, quiet, mud-streaked, having reported a total collapse of the eastern headings and no recoverable material. Whether the water had done it or his buried charge had, no one could say, and no one tried. The mountain had closed its account.

The inquiry filed its findings that summer: environmental hazard, equipment failure, regrettable loss of life. Hobb's lid, pressed down tight. Somewhere south, families received letters with phrases like *natural causes* in them, and wept, and buried the letters in drawers, and went on—which was, Coral told himself on the bad nights, the mercy he'd chosen, however much it tasted like ash.

He went back to work. There were always more closed lines, more dead spurs threading the wilderness like veins in a wrist, more country that needed mapping by a man who asked nothing of it and told it nothing in return. He traded his charts for flour and cartridges and lamp oil, and the town went on not asking questions, which was the finest luxury he had ever known.

But some evenings, when the light went long and gold across the water and the loons started up in the narrows, he would walk down to the bank and stand a while facing north, toward the junction, toward the mountain that kept what it kept. He thought about his old crew—about the names on the manifest, the men who'd gone into the east heading on orders and never come out, and the one who'd stayed behind in the dark so the rest of the world could be lied to comfortably. He thought about Halloran in the floodwater, arms locked around a stranger's boy. He thought about a woman on a steamer south, carrying a report that was a different kind of map—one drawn to show only what should survive.

Truth, he had learned, was like water in the dry season. You did not pour it out all at once. You measured it, you rationed it, you gave each man exactly as much as he could carry without drowning—and you let the river decide the rest.

One evening in late summer, a trapper came through with mail from the south, and among the bills of lading and the old newspapers Coral found a plain envelope with no return address. Inside was a single sheet, unsigned, with a line typed in the middle:

*The east heading is closed. So is the file. Some things are safer underground.*

Coral read it twice, then fed it to the stove, and stood watching it burn with something that was almost a smile.

Outside, the first stars were coming up over the ridge, and the wilderness lay quiet around the settlement, vast and patient, holding everything it had ever been given—and giving back, as always, exactly nothing.
