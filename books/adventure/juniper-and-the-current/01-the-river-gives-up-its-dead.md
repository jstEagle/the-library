## 1. The River Gives Up Its Dead

The Serrano River in late summer was a different animal than the one Rafael Thackeray had learned to fear in the mountains. Up in the high country, water was a knife—cold, quick, and entirely without opinion about whether you lived or died. Down here in the flats, two days' walk below the gorge mouth, the river slowed and spread and went shallow as a rumor, and a man could stand hip-deep in the middle of it with a drag-line and feel almost respectable.

Rafael was not respectable. Rafael was a salvager, which was a polite word the ferry folk used for people who pulled drowned things out of the river and sold them back to the living. Low-water season was salvaging season, and Rafael had worked the Serrano's flats for two years now, ever since coming down out of the mountains with nothing but a pack frame, a compass that didn't belong to them, and a name they'd learned to keep quiet.

The morning had started well, by Rafael's standards, which meant nothing had gone wrong before breakfast. The light came up gold over the eastern bluffs, the water ran low and glassy-green over the gravel bars, and the heron that Rafael had privately named the Colonel stood on his usual rock, judging everyone with the serene contempt of a creature who had never once had to fill out paperwork.

"Morning, Colonel," Rafael said, wading out with the drag-line coiled over one shoulder.

The Colonel blinked. Conversation, in Rafael's experience, was one of the few pleasures that cost nothing and embarrassed no one, provided the other party was a bird.

The technique was simple and undignified. You dragged a heavy three-pronged hook across the riverbed in long, slow pulls, and when it caught on something—a log, a sunken skiff, a drowned crate of somebody's failed ambitions—you hauled it up and found out whether you'd earned your supper. Most days it was junk. Rusty stove parts. A wagon axle. Once, memorably, a piano, which had produced a sound like a dying ox when the hook bit into it, and which Tam, the ferry's youngest hand, had sworn for a week afterward was haunted.

Rafael worked the channel below the old mill race, where the current slowed and dropped whatever it was tired of carrying. First pull: mud, and a boot, single, size large, no leg in it. Second pull: a tangle of fence wire that fought like a live thing and took ten minutes to subdue. Third pull caught on something that did not fight at all.

That was the first strange thing. Everything in a river fights. Waterlogged wood, dead livestock, sunk boats—all of it rolls and drags and resists, because the river never lets go of anything gracefully. But the hook bit down on this object and it simply *sat there*, heavy and square and immovable, like a fact someone had dropped on the riverbed and expected everyone else to deal with.

Rafael braced against a boulder and hauled. The line came tight, sang once, and held. Whatever it was, it weighed as much as a man—a big man, a man who'd eaten well his whole life—and it did not budge an inch.

"Well," Rafael said to the Colonel, who had wandered down the bank to watch with professional interest. "Either it's a safe full of somebody's sins, or I've finally hooked the bottom of the river itself."

It took the better part of an hour. Rafael worked the line, shifted angle, worked it again, waded closer, dove once into water so cold it rang like a bell against the teeth, and came up with fingers hooked into an iron handle. The thing was a chest—iron-banded, oak beneath the bands where the iron had rusted away, square as a coffin and about the same disposition. It took three more dives and a borrowed block-and-tackle from the ferry slip, and a great deal of language that would have made the Colonel blush had he been the blushing sort, before the chest came up the gravel bar with a sound like a door closing somewhere far away.

Rafael sat down next to it in the shallows, dripping, chest heaving, and looked at it the way you look at a stranger who's just called you by your childhood name.

River salvage came up stamped, usually. Shipping marks, ferry brands, the occasional customs stamp from a border office that no longer existed. You learned to read them the way other people read weather. And Rafael had learned, over two years of careful, deliberate, disciplined invisibility, to read one particular stamp faster than any other—and to feel it in the sternum before the eyes even confirmed it.

The chest was stamped with a crowned mountain over crossed keys.

The seal of Vessia.

Rafael did not move for a long moment. The river talked to itself over the gravel, the way it always did, saying nothing over and over. Somewhere upstream the ferry bell rang, thin and bright, calling the morning crossing. The Colonel stalked two steps closer and peered at the chest with the expression of a bird who has seen men make fools of themselves over boxes before and expects to see it again.

"Twenty years," Rafael said quietly. "Twenty years, and you're still turning up like a bad coin."

Vessia. The word itself had gone strange in the mouth, like a name you'd stopped saying and now couldn't say naturally. Twenty years ago there had been a country there—east over the range, down into the valleys where the terraced orchards climbed the hillsides and the mountain inns poured plum brandy for travelers and the survey offices kept the finest maps on the continent. Rafael had eaten breakfast in those inns. Rafael had drawn those maps, some of them, or walked the ground they were drawn from. And then the annexation had come—treaties, troops, a new flag over the customs houses, and within a season, new maps with the old country simply *gone*, the way a name is gone from a ledger when someone pays to have it struck.

The world had agreed, more or less, to pretend it had always been this way. That was the trick of it. Not conquest—*correction*. The new government hadn't erased Vessia; it had merely, with great bureaucratic tenderness, helped everyone remember that Vessia had never existed, and that the mines and passes and river rights had always, always belonged to the republic. There had been a word for the people who didn't go along with the correction. There were fewer of those people every year.

Rafael knew the seal the way you know a scar. Knew it from the survey office at Vess Pass, where it sat embossed in green wax on every grant and permit and boundary deed. Knew it from the pack of a dead man whose name Rafael still did not say out loud, not even to the river, not even on the bad nights, especially on the bad nights.

And knew it, most of all, from the last thing Rafael had seen before the world closed like a door.

Rafael got up, wrung out the hem of the coat, and did the sensible thing, which was to think about lunch. The sensible thing was a skill Rafael had practiced daily for two years, the way a musician practices scales. See a Vessian seal on a riverbed chest? Think about lunch. Notice that the chest's lock is a customs lock, government issue, the kind used for transporting sealed documents? Think very hard about lunch. Observe that the chest is not merely heavy but *heavy with paper*—that it sloshes with the dense, dry weight of reams and reams of it, packed against water damage with waxed canvas, packed by someone who very much wanted whatever was inside to survive a river crossing?

Think about lunch, and then think about dinner, and then go to sleep, and in the morning the chest will have been a dream.

Rafael got as far as standing up.

The problem was the packing. Rafael had hauled enough government freight over enough passes to know what careful packing meant, and this was careful. The waxed canvas wrapping was soldierly work, corners tucked, seams doubled. Someone had loved these papers, or feared them, or both. Someone had put this chest in a boat—or on a mule, or on a sledge—and someone had lost it, here, in the shallows below the old mill race, in the one stretch of the Serrano that went low enough, every few summers, for a man with a drag-line to reach the bottom.

And there was the thought Rafael had been walking around all morning the way you walk around a hole in the floor: *only a guide could have found this.* Not a fisherman, not a dredger, not a farmer watering his stock. The chest had settled into the channel below the mill race, in water two feet too deep for wading and too fast for diving, in a spot no one would drag except a salvager who knew the river's habits the way other people knew their own handwriting. Either that was luck—and Rafael had stopped believing in luck the day it stopped being on their side—or someone had counted on exactly this. Counted on the low-water season. Counted on a certain kind of person walking this stretch of river with a hook and a rope and a past.

The river said nothing, over and over. The Colonel said nothing, eloquently.

"Oh, stop it," Rafael told the bird. "I'm thinking about lunch."

But the hands were already moving, because hands are traitors and always have been. They turned the chest over, found the second stamp on the underside—smaller, a serial number, the prefix that Rafael knew meant *Survey and Boundary Office*—and found, wedged in the hasp, a scrap of waxed canvas with a strip of oilcloth stitched to it, and on the oilcloth, in ink that the river had been kind to, three words in the old Vessian administrative hand, the hand Rafael had once written in daily:

*Deliver to the mountains.*

Rafael sat back down in the shallows. This time the sitting was not about rest.

There was a whole country's worth of reasons to walk away. Rafael counted them off like beads, an old rosary. Reason one: a person could not be hanged for a chest they'd never opened. Reason two: the magistrate's men came through Juniper's Landing every few weeks, and Magistrate Corven's men had eyes like fish and memories like ledgers. Reason three—and this was the bead that always caught the thumb—*you buried that country once already, and look what it cost you. Look what it cost him.*

The name rose anyway. It always did, at the worst times. Rafael said the river's name instead, out loud, like a charm. "Serrano. Serrano." The river went on saying nothing.

Here was the trouble, and Rafael had known it since the first pull of the line: curiosity was not really what was happening. Curiosity was a light, pleasant thing, a market-day thing. What was happening had a different weight entirely. It was the weight of a door you'd nailed shut twenty years ago and now heard knocking, politely, patiently, from the inside. Dread, dressed up in curiosity's coat, hoping you wouldn't notice.

Rafael noticed. Rafael had spent two years learning to notice exactly this kind of thing, because noticing was what had kept a disgraced guide breathing in a border town where half the ferry folk would sell a secret for the price of a drink and the other half would sell it for less.

The sun climbed. The Colonel caught a fish and did not share it. And Rafael Thackeray, salvager, invisible man, keeper of nailed doors, did the thing that every instinct honed by two years of careful living screamed against, and which the hands had already begun anyway.

They dragged the chest up the gravel bar, above the high-water line, into the shade of the willows. They fetched the mule. They loaded the chest with a great show of boredom, whistling—badly, since whistling was another skill never quite mastered—and led the mule up the bank toward the lean-to on the edge of town where Rafael lived alone, paid rent in salvage shares, and was known to the neighbors as a quiet sort who kept to the river and was welcome to it.

Halfway up the path, Rafael stopped.

The trouble was the market. The path to the lean-to went along the top of the levee, and the levee path went past the morning market, and the morning market was full of people, and one of the people was going to see the chest. That was simply arithmetic. Rafael stood there with the mule and did the arithmetic and did not like the answer.

The chest could be covered. The chest could be tarped. The chest could be hauled the long way around, through the reeds, arriving at the lean-to looking like nothing more interesting than a salvager's ordinary morning. All of that was possible. All of that was sensible.

What was not possible, Rafael understood with a sinking, familiar feeling, was what came next. Because the chest had a lock, and locks opened, and Rafael had not slept a whole night through in two years, and there was going to be a night, very soon, when the lamp was trimmed low and the door was barred and the hands—those traitors—were going to find the crowbar.

"You could put it back," Rafael said to the mule, who flicked an ear. "Right now. Walk it back down to the mill race and drop it in and let the river keep it. The river kept it fine for twenty years."

The mule, who had opinions about everything and expressed none of them, stood patiently in the path. Down in the shallows, the Colonel speared his second fish of the morning and regarded Rafael with one cold yellow eye, and there was something in that look—Rafael would swear to it later, would swear the bird knew—that said, with the whole weight of heron wisdom: *You are going to open it. You know you are going to open it. I have watched men for thirty years, and you are going to open it.*

"Nobody asked you," Rafael said, and led the mule up the levee, the long way around, through the reeds, whistling badly, with the chest swaying behind like a passenger who knew exactly where it was going.

* * *

The lean-to was one room and a loft, a stove, a table with a wobble Rafael had never bothered to fix because the wobble kept honest company—it reminded you the world was imperfect and so were you. Rafael unloaded the chest, watered the mule, ate bread and cheese standing up, and then spent four hours doing absolutely nothing with tremendous discipline. Mended the drag-line. Split kindling. Repointed the loose stones at the doorway. Watched the light move across the floor like a slow animal.

By dark, the discipline was gone.

The lamp was trimmed low. The door was barred. The chest sat in the middle of the floor where the light fell, dripping the last of the river out of its seams, and Rafael sat across from it with the crowbar across their knees, and for a long time neither of them moved, man and chest, in a standoff as old as curiosity itself.

"Twenty years," Rafael said to it. "Whatever you are, whatever you were carrying—you've been down there longer than I've been a ghost. Whatever trouble you're in, it's had twenty years to go stale. I could leave you be. That's the smart play. The smart play is I put you under the floorboards and in the spring I take you out past the border stones at night and sink you in the deep channel and nobody, ever, has to know the river gave you up."

The chest said nothing. It was very good at saying nothing. It had, after all, been practicing.

Rafael thought about a green wax seal on a survey grant, and a man's laugh on a high trail, and a compass in a coat pocket that did not belong to them, and a door closing, and a name struck from a register. Thought about how the smart play had kept them alive for two years, and how alive was not the same thing as living, and how the difference between those two words was about the width of this chest.

"Ah, hell," Rafael said, to the chest, to the Colonel, to the river two miles off saying nothing in the dark. "You knew I would. Everybody knew I would."

The crowbar went in under the hasp. The old iron groaned, held, groaned again, and gave with a crack that sounded, in the small room, like ice going out in spring—like something long frozen finally, finally letting go.

The lid came up. The lamp flame leaned toward it as if to look.

And Rafael Thackeray, who had spent two years being no one, leaned over the open chest and saw, in the first row of oilcloth-wrapped bundles, a green wax seal, intact, unbroken, stamped with a crowned mountain over crossed keys—and beneath the seal, in the old administrative hand, a name.

A name Rafael knew.

A name that had no business being in this chest, in this river, in this world, twenty years after Rafael had last said it over a cairn of stones on the Vessian side of the range, in the wind, to no one.

Rafael did not touch the document. Rafael did not breathe. Outside, far down the dark levee, a dog began to bark, and would not stop, and somewhere beyond it, faint as a fish rising, a bell rang once at the ferry slip—though no crossing ran at this hour—and the night, which had been merely dark, became attentive.
