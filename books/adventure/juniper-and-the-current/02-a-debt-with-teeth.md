## 2. A Debt with Teeth

The chest sat under Rafael's bed like a sleeping dog that might bite.

Rafael had told it so, out loud, on the first night. "Stay there," they'd said, kneeling on the floorboards of the rented room above the cooper's shop, staring into the dark beneath the frame where the iron box glowed faintly with river-cold. "You stay there and we'll both pretend this never happened."

The chest had not answered, which Rafael chose to take as agreement.

It was a lie between them anyway. Rafael hadn't slept more than two hours since hauling it out of the Serrano, and every hour of those two had been spent listening to the river through the wall, the way you listen to a witness who hasn't decided yet whether to talk.

* * *

Two years ago, almost to the week, Rafael Thackeray had come down out of the mountains with nothing but a rucksack, a compass, and a name that was worth less than the compass. The name had been blacklisted in three valley towns and one republic; the compass had been a gift from a dead man, which made it either priceless or worthless depending on how you felt about ghosts.

Rafael felt plenty about ghosts.

Juniper's Landing had been the last town before the border marshes, and the last ferry across the Serrano belonged to Osric Bellweather, a man built like a rain barrel and about as forthcoming. He'd looked Rafael up and down on the landing — mud to the knees, eyes doing the thing tired animals' eyes do — and said, "Crossing's four coppers."

"I have two."

"Then you can cross halfway."

Rafael had laughed. It had been the first laugh in eleven days, and it came out cracked, like a door in a house nobody lived in. Osric had studied the sound for a moment the way he might study a rope he didn't trust, then jerked his thumb at the boat.

"Get in. You'll owe me."

"I'll owe you," Rafael had agreed, and that was the whole contract. No paper. No witnesses. Just a fat man poling a flat-bottomed ferry through brown water and a disgraced guide sitting in the bow like cargo that had opinions.

The debt had teeth from the start, because Osric never named a figure. He let it grow. He fed it little kindnesses over the years — a dry corner of his boathouse during the flood spring, a sack of barley when Rafael's guiding work dried up along with everything else, an introduction to the cooper upstairs whose roof leaked but whose rent was honest. Each kindness went on the account. Rafael could feel the number swelling somewhere behind Osric's cheerful eyes, the way water swells behind a beaver dam, patient and structural.

"You're good for it," Osric would say whenever Rafael tried to settle up. He'd wave a hand thick as a ham at whatever coins were offered. "Not yet. When you're good for it, you'll know."

"How will I know?"

Osric would smile, and there would be something in the smile that wasn't entirely friendly, some small hook of it. "Because I'll say so."

* * *

Now Rafael lay awake above the cooper's shop while the chest breathed cold under the bed, and did arithmetic that wouldn't close.

Four coppers had become a debt without a number. A debt without a number is the worst kind, because your imagination does the accounting, and imagination is a greedy clerk. Some nights the debt was thirty crowns. Some nights it was Rafael's other hand. Most nights it was simply *the day Osric decides*, and that day had always been theoretical, comfortably far off, a mountain on the horizon that never got closer no matter how many seasons you walked.

Until now. Now there was a chest stamped with a seal that got men hanged, sitting six feet from a ferryman who read faces the way scholars read scripture, and Rafael had been unable to keep the dread off their own face for three days running. Tam had noticed. Tam noticed everything; that was the trouble with young people, they watched you like you were weather. And if Tam noticed, Osric would notice by Thursday.

Rafael turned over. The floorboard above the chest creaked, a small reproachful sound.

"Oh, hush," Rafael told it.

* * *

The rule Rafael had set was simple: open it once, catalogue what was inside, and never again. A sensible rule. Rafael had broken it every night since.

There was a method to the madness, or at least a ritual. Wait until the cooper's household went quiet — old Marta snored like a sawmill with grievances, so this took a while. Then light no lamp. Then drag the chest out by its ring-bolts, wincing at each groan of the boards, and sit with it in the dark like a sinner at a shrine, hands resting on the lid, heart going like a bird in a sack.

The first night, Rafael had opened it just to confirm the seal. Vessia: the double-headed heron, wings spread over crossed keys. Twenty years gone from every map, and still the stamp cut clean and deep, because whoever had made this chest had expected it to last longer than its country. That thought had done something to Rafael's chest that Rafael declined to examine, and had shut the lid, and had put the chest back, and had lain awake until dawn listening to the river.

The second night, Rafael told themself they were only checking that the contents hadn't been damaged by the water. This was even true, in the way that most reasons are true — technically, incidentally, beside the point. The documents were wrapped in oilcloth, three layers of it, tied with waxed cord, and dry as a sermon. Someone had packed this chest to survive exactly what it had survived. Which meant someone had thrown it in the river expecting it to be found. By whom? A river keeps few secrets and gives them up selectively. It had given this one to Rafael, of all people — to a guide who knew every ford, every snag, every mood of the Serrano from glacier mouth to marsh. That was not luck. Rivers don't deal luck. Rivers deal currents, and currents are chosen.

Rafael had shut the lid again. Hadn't slept.

The third night — tonight — Rafael didn't even bother with a reason. They simply got up, because the alternative was lying there being interrogated by the ceiling, and dragged the chest into the middle of the floor where the moonlight came through the salt-glazed window in a pale stripe.

In the moonlight, the heron seal looked almost alive.

"You're a long way from home," Rafael murmured to it. "So am I. We should get along."

The lock was a simple tumbler, older than Rafael, and Rafael had picked it the first night out of pure professional habit and felt vaguely ashamed ever after, though of what, exactly — the picking, or the shame — remained unclear.

Inside: the oilcloth bundle. Beneath it, a false bottom, and beneath that, the thing Rafael kept coming back to. A single document, separate from the rest, folded around a strip of lead seal. Not a grant, not a permit, none of the dry machinery of land and money. A letter, short, in a careful hand:

*To whoever the river chooses.*

Rafael had read that line perhaps forty times now. It had the specific horror of a message in a bottle — the certainty that someone, somewhere, had stood on a bank much like this one, in a country that no longer existed, and committed their whole hope to moving water. *To whoever the river chooses.* Well. The river had chosen. The river had chosen a person with no country, no license, no name worth speaking, and a debt with teeth. Whoever had written that letter would have wept at the selection process.

And yet. Rafael's fingers hovered over the fold. There was a name in the letter. Rafael had seen it the first night, had read it once, and had not read it again since, because reading it again would make it real, and real things required decisions, and decisions were the one commodity Rafael Thackeray had sworn off two years ago along with everything else.

The client's expedition had taught Rafael that lesson thoroughly. Decide the route, and someone dies. Decide the pace, and someone dies. Don't decide, and someone dies anyway, but at least it isn't your signature on the decision. Two years of invisibility, of taking only low-water work and day-hire porters, of being *the guide who used to* — it had all been one long refusal to decide anything larger than dinner.

The letter sat in the moonlight with its name folded inside like a bone in a napkin.

"Not tonight," Rafael told it, and meant it, and reached for it anyway.

* * *

What stopped Rafael's hand was not conscience. It was the stairs.

Boards on the stair to the street, third from the bottom, sang a particular note when stepped on — Rafael knew every voice in this building the way they knew every voice in a rockfall. These steps were singing the wrong song: slow, deliberate, one at a time, past midnight. Not Marta, who descended like an avalanche. Not the cooper, who slept like the dead and rose like a later dead.

Rafael closed the chest, slid it under the bed in four economical movements, and was lying down with breathing arranged into convincing sleep-shape before the knock came.

Three knocks. Pause. Three knocks.

Rafael considered the window. Second floor, drop onto the woodpile, doable in the dark for someone with their particular history, but the chest weighed as much as a well-fed child and made noise like a church bell with a grudge. Leave the chest, lose the chest. Take the chest, break an ankle, explain the ankle and the chest to whoever was knocking.

Second round of knocks, slightly harder.

Rafael opened the door on the chain first, an old habit from a life that had taught old habits. Lamplight from the landing spilled in and lit half a familiar face.

Osric.

He stood there in his oilskins despite the clear night, holding a lantern up as though serving papers, and his small bright eyes went past Rafael's shoulder into the room with the unhurried thoroughness of a man counting fish in a neighbor's barrel.

"You're awake," he said.

"I was asleep."

"You were asleep standing behind your own door with a chain on it." Osric smiled his hooked smile. "May I come in, or shall we conduct our business through the gap like smugglers? Though I suppose one of us knows how that's done."

Rafael unchained the door and stood back. Osric filled the room the way weather fills a valley. His gaze touched the bed — lingered on the bed, on the shadows beneath it, for exactly one heartbeat too long — and moved on to the window, the cold hearth, the single cup on the table.

"No drink offered. No light offered. Rude, for a guest."

"It's past midnight."

"Best time for business." He set the lantern on the table and lowered himself onto Marta's good chair, which complained. "I've been thinking about accounts."

Here it was, then. Rafael leaned against the doorframe and kept their voice bored, which took work. "It's the middle of the season. Work's thin. You know that better than anyone."

"I do." Osric laced his fingers over his stomach, a gesture like a man settling in front of a fire he intended to enjoy for years. "Thin season. Low water. Funny thing about low water — it shows you things. Snags. Old wrecks. All manner of items the river's been keeping polite about." His eyes came up, mild as milk. "A man could pull most anything out of the Serrano this time of year, if he knew where to look. And you'd know where to look better than any soul in this valley, Rafael Thackeray. Better than any soul, I sometimes think, in any valley."

The room got very quiet. Somewhere below, Marta rolled over in her sleep and the whole building shifted like a ship.

"I pulled a stove out Tuesday," Rafael said. "Cast iron. You want it?"

"I don't want a stove." Osric rose, unhurried, and collected his lantern. At the door he paused, and for a moment the cheerfulness dropped away entirely, and what was underneath was not unkind, exactly, but it was vast and patient and had teeth of its own. "Here's my thinking. Debts are like rivers. You can leave them alone a long while, and they seem fine, and then one day the water drops and everything's standing in plain sight. I'll call mine due soon. Not today. Soon. When I do, I'll want it settled proper — coin, or kind, or something else of value." He tapped the side of his nose with one thick finger. "Whatever a man happens to have of value. Even things he doesn't know he has."

He let himself out. His boots sang the stairs down, each step a note, and Rafael listened to the whole descending tune until the street door thumped and the night swallowed him.

Then Rafael sat on the edge of the bed for a long time, hands hanging between their knees, looking at the floorboards.

*Soon.* Osric didn't know. Not yet — Rafael was nearly sure of the *not yet*. But he suspected the shape of it, the way a ferryman suspects weather, and Osric had never once in two years named a date, and now he had. Something had moved him. Something upstream.

Rafael knelt and drew the chest out into the moonlight one more time.

"All right," they said quietly. "You win."

They unfolded the letter. The waxed paper crackled like frost underfoot. In the careful hand, beneath *To whoever the river chooses*, the name waited where it had waited every night, patient as the seal, patient as the river, patient as the debt:

*Deliver this to Mirela Vance, bookbinder, Juniper's Landing. She will know what it is. Trust no officer of the republic. Trust the river, and trust her.*

Rafael read it twice. Then a third time, because the world had just become a different shape and the mind needed the measurements taken again.

Mirela Vance. The bookbinder. The quiet woman with ink-stained fingers who mended the ferry ledgers every quarter and never once asked Rafael a question about the past, which in Juniper's Landing was so rare it was practically a confession of something.

Trust no officer. Trust her.

Rafael sat back on their heels and laughed once, softly, without much humor in it. Of course. Of course the river hadn't chosen at random. The river had chosen the one person in town too broke to run, too watched to hide, and too deep in debt to a man who was already sniffing at the waterline.

Outside, far off toward the bridge, a lantern moved along the landing where no lantern had business moving at this hour. Rafael watched it through the salt-glazed glass until it stopped — directly across the street, beneath the sign of the bookbinder's shop — and stood there, still, for a long, long moment before it went out.

"Well," Rafael said to the chest, to the river, to the whole conspiring world. "That's torn it."

Tomorrow, apparently, Rafael was going to buy a book.
