## 3. The Woman Who Reads Ciphers

Mirela Vance could tell a man's whole history from his hands.

The ferryman's were cracked and river-browned, nails cut short for rope work. The butcher's carried a permanent ghost of tallow in every crease. The magistrate's clerks had ink stains at the second knuckle of the right hand only—lazy writers who dragged their fingers across wet lines and didn't care whose record they smudged.

And the disgraced mountain guide who came to her stall every Thursday to buy paste and linen thread? His hands told a story Mirela read slowly, over three weeks, the way she might work through a difficult binding: broad palms scarred along the heel where rope had bitten, a crooked index finger badly set years ago, and calluses that belonged to ice axes, not oars or knives. Hands that had held on to things above the snowline. Hands that had perhaps, once, let go of something they shouldn't have.

She knew who he was, of course. Everyone in Juniper's Landing knew Rafael Thackeray by reputation before they ever learned his face—the guide who'd walked a client off the Vessian side of the range and come down alone. The town said it in the particular hushed way people say such things when they enjoy saying them. *The one who lost the Vessian lordling. The one who can't go back.*

Mirela had opinions about people who couldn't go back. She was one herself.

So she sold him paste and thread and said nothing, and he bought them and said nothing, and this arrangement suited them both fine until the morning the river gave up its dead.

* * *

Market day in Juniper's Landing began with gulls and ended with mud, and in between there was the long business of pretending the town was more than a hinge between two countries that had both agreed to forget it. Mirela's stall sat between the fishmonger and a man who sold what he insisted were apples but which everyone understood to be a form of punishment. Her awning displayed her wares: rebacked hymnals, mended ledgers, a shelf of novels she bound herself because the printers' paper fell apart in river damp.

She was trimming the headcap of a prayer book when Thackeray passed her stall, and she saw it.

Not on his hands this time. On his sleeve.

A stain—rust-colored, dried, worked deep into the wool as though he'd scrubbed at it and failed. And within the stain, faint as a watermark, the ghost of an impression. A circle broken by a vertical stroke, crossed at its heart by a single horizontal line.

Mirela's trim knife stopped moving.

She knew that mark. Every Vessian knew that mark. It had been on their coins, their church doors, the flyleaves of every book her father had ever owned. The seal of Vessia—the double-crossed circle, the sign of the old land registries, stamped into deeds and grants and birth records for four hundred years before the annexation erased all of it in a season. They'd burned the registries first. Her mother used to say you could measure a country's death by how thoroughly they destroyed its paperwork.

And here it was, printed backward onto a mountain guide's sleeve, the way a seal prints onto whatever presses against it. Which meant Thackeray had been pressing against something sealed. Recently. Something heavy enough to leave its shape in wax or lead.

He bought his paste. He paid in small coin counted out without looking at her. He did not see her staring at his sleeve, because he was very good at not seeing things—a skill, Mirela suspected, that had kept him alive.

"Good thread this week," he said, which was the longest sentence he'd ever offered her.

"Keep it dry," she said, "and it'll hold a boat together."

Something flickered across his face—not quite a smile, more like the memory of one. He nodded and moved off into the market crowd, shoulders hunched, a big man trying to take up less space than he occupied.

Mirela looked down at the prayer book. Looked up. Looked at the space where he'd been.

Then she closed her stall early for the first time in two years, told the fishmonger her grandmother had died—her grandmother had died eleven years ago and would not mind—and followed him.

* * *

Following a mountain guide through a town built on a riverbank turned out to be humiliatingly easy, because mountain guides, Mirela discovered, moved like men still walking terrain. Thackeray took the high lane behind the chandlery, avoided the bridge entirely—crossing at the ford instead, where sightlines were long and no one could block either end—and paused twice in doorways without appearing to pause at all.

He was checking whether he was followed.

Which meant he knew he had something worth being followed for.

Mirela adjusted her strategy accordingly. She stopped following him and started shopping. She examined apples of punitive quality. She argued cheerfully with the fishmonger about the freshness of a trout that had clearly died of natural causes sometime last week. She let Thackeray get well ahead, then drifted after him the way water drifts downhill—unhurried, inevitable, finding every gap.

He lived, she learned, in a room above the cooperage on Rope Lane, rented from a widow named Petch who charged too much and gossiped enough to make it fair value. He carried water up himself. He ate bread and hard cheese and, once, standing at his window with the light behind him, he unwrapped a parcel and looked at something inside it for a long time before wrapping it again.

Mirela stood in the shadow of the cooper's woodpile across the lane, feeling the rain start, and asked herself a question she hadn't permitted herself in years:

What if it's real?

The thought arrived like a splinter working under skin. Because if that impression on his sleeve was the Vessian registry seal—if the river had given up some piece of the drowned country, some actual document—then everything changed. Not the world, maybe. Not yet. But *hers*. There were names in the old grants. Names of families. Names of what had been taken and from whom. She had spent six years in Juniper's Landing binding other people's books and telling herself that memory was a private grief, that survival meant silence, that a woman who talked about Vessia in a border town was a woman asking to be reported to Magistrate Corven's office.

But her mother had also taught her cipher-work at a kitchen table by lamplight, back when learning it was illegal and therefore necessary. *Paper is how they steal,* her mother had said. *Paper is how we prove it.* Her mother had died believing someone, someday, would find the proof.

Mirela looked up at the lit window of the cooperage, where a man who had lost everything except his guilt was wrapping a parcel like a man burying something alive.

"Come on," she murmured to him, to the rain, to the dead. "Don't be a coward. I've been one for six years; it's terrible."

* * *

The problem, of course, was approach. You could not simply knock on the door of a hunted man and say *I saw a seal on your sleeve and I read ciphers and my family died for the same stamp.* Well—you could. But the outcomes ranged from slammed door to worse, and Mirela had learned to calculate odds the way gamblers did, except her stakes were always her neck.

She spent two days thinking about it, which nearly undid her. On the third day, Thursday, Thackeray came to her stall again for paste, and Mirela made her move with all the subtlety of a dropped anvil.

"You've got iron rust on your sleeve," she said.

Thackeray went very still. It was remarkable, the stillness—a whole large man becoming furniture. "River water," he said. "It does that."

"To your sleeve. In the shape of a seal."

The market noise went on around them—gulls, haggling, the fishmonger lying about his trout—but between the two of them there opened a silence so complete Mirela could hear her own pulse.

"I don't know what you mean," Thackeray said.

"Mm." Mirela picked up her trim knife and began, with great calm, to shave the edge of a hymnal. "My father was a registrar in Velika Morava. Do you know it? No, of course not—it's called something else now, some governor's name. He kept the land grants. When the annexation came, men came to burn the registry office, and my father stood in the doorway and told them the fire would have to go through him." She blew a shaving of leather off the blade. "They were reasonable men. They went through him."

Thackeray's eyes had come up. For the first time in three weeks of Thursdays, he was actually looking at her—not past her, not through her, but at her, the way you look at terrain you realize is not what the map promised.

"That's a sad story," he said carefully.

"It is. Here's another. There's a woman in this town who reads ciphers. Old Vessian court hand, registry shorthand, the lot. My mother taught me at a kitchen table while the government pretended our language was a dialect and our country was a mood." Mirela set down the knife. "I'm not asking what you pulled out of the Serrano, Mr. Thackeray. I'm telling you that if it's what I think it is, and you sit on it alone, you will either drown in it or die of it, and either way it will have been for nothing. Whereas if you bring it to me—"

"Why?" The word came out rough. "Why would I do that?"

"Because your hands are shaking," Mirela said gently, "and mine aren't. Yet."

He looked down at his own hands as if betrayed by them. Then he laughed—one short, startled breath of a laugh, gone as fast as it came, but real. "You're not afraid of me at all, are you?"

"Should I be?"

"Everyone else is."

"Well." Mirela resumed her trimming. "Everyone else hasn't watched their father be removed from a doorway. Fear and I are old acquaintances; we simply don't share a household anymore." She glanced up. "Thursday next. Bring the paste money and nothing else. Or don't. But if you don't, I'll follow you home again, and I'll be less polite about the woodpile."

For a moment she thought he'd refuse. She watched the calculation move through him—the same calculation she'd watched in exiles a hundred times, the arithmetic of *how much can I trust, how much has trusting ever cost me.* She watched him arrive at the sum.

"The widow Petch talks too much," he said finally, which was not yes, and was not no, and was somehow both.

"They all do. That's why I never tell them anything true." Mirela wrapped his paste in brown paper and held it out. "Dry thread, Mr. Thackeray. It holds boats together."

This time the smile almost survived the crossing of his face. Almost. He took the parcel, nodded once, and walked into the crowd—and Mirela noticed, with a warmth she found deeply inconvenient, that he forgot to hunch his shoulders until he was nearly out of sight.

* * *

That night she couldn't sleep. She lay in her rooms above the shop listening to the river, doing what her mother had called *the honest accounting*: listing everything she wanted against everything it would cost.

She wanted the documents to be real. She wanted the names in them—her family's name, maybe, among the thousands—to be written somewhere the burning hadn't reached. She wanted, with an ache that surprised her after six careful years, to be useful to the dead instead of merely faithful to them.

And she wanted—this was the part she kept trying to strike from the ledger and it kept writing itself back in—to know more about the man with the shaking hands. There was damage in him; she'd recognized it the way you recognize weather coming. But there was something else underneath, something the town's gossip had never managed to stain. He bought thread and thanked her for it. He checked his corners like a man who'd learned the hard way. And when she'd mentioned her father, he hadn't performed sympathy—he'd just *listened*, the whole of him, like a man taking bearings.

Two cautious people, she thought. Two people who had each decided the safest thing was to need no one. And now the river had gone and complicated everything, the way rivers do.

Across the lane and down Rope Lane and up the cooperage stairs, she'd have wagered anything, Rafael Thackeray was sitting awake too, guarding a parcel, arguing with himself.

She was right. Though she couldn't know that the argument was going badly—that he had already opened the chest twice that night, and that the third time, he would not close it again. That inside, beneath the seals and the forged transfers and the name that made his blood run cold, there were documents in old Vessian registry hand. Dense, coded, official.

Documents that needed a reader.

And that by morning, word had already reached the magistrate's office that a chest had surfaced in the Serrano, and that Magistrate Corven—who did his own honest accounting, and always balanced it—had begun, quietly, to post watchers in Juniper's Landing.
