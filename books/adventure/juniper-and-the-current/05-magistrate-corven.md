## 5. Magistrate Corven

The report arrived on a Tuesday, which Corven considered an insult. Tuesdays were his day for the drainage assessments—forty-one pages of culvert complaints from the lower valley, each one more tedious than the last—and he had been looking forward to them the way other men looked forward to a good meal. Instead, Sergeant Brandt stood in his doorway holding a single sheet of paper with rain-spotted edges, and Corven knew before she spoke that his pleasant morning was dead.

"Say it," he said.

"A chest came up out of the Serrano, sir. Low water pulled it loose from wherever it's been sitting. A fisherman saw a man hauling it off the gravel bar below the old mill race."

Corven set down his pen with great care, as though it were a thing that might go off. "A chest."

"Iron. Sealed." Brandt hesitated in a way she almost never did. "The fisherman says the man who took it didn't look surprised. He looked like he recognized it."

Ah, thought Corven. There it is.

He thanked her, dismissed her, and sat for a long moment listening to the rain work at the shutters of the magistrate's residence—a building that had once been the Vessian customs house, back when Vessia was a country instead of a memory with excellent masonry. The irony was not lost on him. It was never lost on him. Corven had built twenty years of careful, patient work on top of that particular irony, and he knew its weight to the ounce.

Then he opened the drawer where he kept the things that did not officially exist, and began to think.

* * *

People imagined that men like Corven spent their days scheming, hunched over candle-lit maps, twirling mustaches. In truth, most of his work was arithmetic. He thought of himself less as a magistrate than as an accountant of consequences, and his ledgers balanced beautifully.

Here was the sum that mattered:

Twenty years ago, Vessia had been dissolved—absorbed, annexed, erased, depending on which historian one paid. Its land grants had been declared void. Then, eleven years ago, the republic had issued mining concessions across the high country, and those concessions had required provenance: clean chains of title showing the land had passed legitimately out of Vessian hands before dissolution. Corven had arranged for such chains to exist. He had commissioned them personally, in a rented room in a valley town whose name he made a point of never saying aloud, working by lamplight with three forgers and a bottle of brandy, producing documents so good that even he sometimes forgot they were lies.

Those concessions were worth—conservatively, and Corven was nothing if not conservative—more than the entire annual revenue of the province. They employed four hundred men. They anchored two banks, one railway spur, and the political careers of six people who owed Corven favors they would rather die than acknowledge.

And here was the beautiful part, the part Corven admired even now, the way a man might admire his own reflection in a well-polished boot: the concessions expired at season's end. On paper, all grants came up for renewal every twenty years. If no challenge was lodged before expiry—if no one produced a rival claim, a protest, a scrap of contrary paper—then the renewals would issue automatically, uncontested, and the forged titles would be laundered into permanence. Not forgiven. *Forgotten.* There was a difference, and the difference was everything. Forgiveness implied wrongdoing. Forgetting was simply history doing its housekeeping.

Sixty-three days remained until expiry. He had counted that morning, over breakfast, the way other men read the weather.

Which meant that a sealed iron chest surfacing out of the Serrano River was not a curiosity. It was a threat with a deadline attached.

Corven poured himself tea—he had given up spirits before noon years ago, on the principle that a man should be sharp when he lied—and considered what the chest might contain. The original Vessian land grants, most likely. The true ones. Someone, sometime in the chaos of annexation, had gathered documents into an iron box and hidden them along the river, presumably intending to come back for them, and had then died or fled or simply lost their nerve, because that was what happened to idealists. They mistook hiding a thing for saving it.

But why now? Why had it surfaced now, sixty-three days from expiry?

Coincidence, said one voice in his head—the reasonable voice, the administrative voice. Rivers moved things. Low water exposed things. Chests rusted, banks slumped, luck happened.

Nonsense, said the other voice, the one he trusted. Nothing about this was luck. Someone had placed that chest where only a certain kind of person could find it. Not a farmer—farmers didn't dredge the mill race. Not a soldier—soldiers reported finds. A guide. A ferryman. Someone who knew the river's moods and the mountain's secrets, someone who could pull a box from the water and understand exactly what they held.

Someone like Rafael Thackeray.

Corven wrote the name on a slip of paper, looked at it for a while, and then burned it in the teacup, because he was a careful man and because there was a certain satisfaction in watching even a name submit to flame.

Thackeray. The guide who had carried Corven's orders up a mountain five years ago without knowing what they weighed, and who had carried the blame back down without ever learning why. Corven had felt no particular malice toward Thackeray at the time. That was the part outsiders never understood. Malice was inefficient; it clouded the arithmetic. Thackeray had simply been the load-bearing element available, and when the expedition failed—as it had been quietly arranged to fail—one either found a scapegoat or became one. Corven had chosen, and he slept well. He slept well most nights. It was one of his finest qualities, and he wished people would mention it more often.

If Thackeray had the chest, then Thackeray had a problem: he was a blacklisted man in a border town, a ghost with debts, and ghosts could be managed. But if Thackeray had passed the chest to someone else—to the exile network, to the bookbinder woman, to any of the quiet Vessian hands that still reached out of Juniper's Landing like roots under pavement—then the problem was larger, and larger problems required earlier solutions.

There were sixty-three days. Corven intended to need none of them. Deadlines were for men without initiative.

* * *

By Thursday, Juniper's Landing had acquired three new residents.

The first was a wool merchant named Doss, who took a room above the tavern and displayed a genius for conversation so relentless that within two days half the town avoided him and the other half told him everything. Doss was Corven's best listener precisely because nobody believed anyone could be that boring on purpose.

The second was a surveyor's assistant, ostensibly measuring flood lines for the spring assessment. He walked the riverbank daily with a tripod and a notebook, and his notebook contained very little about floods and a great deal about who visited the ferry landing, at what hours, carrying what.

The third was Sergeant Ilsa Brandt herself—not in uniform, but in a plain oilskin coat, posing as a fisherwoman of modest talent and enormous patience. Corven had chosen her deliberately. She was the only officer he trusted to observe without acting, and acting prematurely was the entire danger. A chest could be stolen back, a witness frightened into silence, a rumor smothered—all recoverable. But a public scene, an arrest, a body in the river: those were permanent, and permanence was precisely what Corven was trying to prevent. You did not put out a fire with a bonfire.

"Rules," he had told her on the Wednesday, in the back room of the customs house, while rain ticked against the glass like a clerk counting coins. "You watch. You do not approach. You do not search. You do not speak to the subject except as a fisherwoman speaks to another citizen, which is to say about weather and the poor state of the trout. If the chest moves, you follow it. If it leaves town, you send word ahead, and you do *not* try to stop it yourself. Am I understood?"

"And if it looks like they're going to destroy it?" Brandt asked. She had a flat way of asking questions that Corven privately enjoyed; it was the verbal equivalent of setting a stone on a table.

"Then you let them," Corven said. "Destruction is a gift. Dead documents threaten no one. What I cannot permit—" and here he leaned forward slightly, and watched her lean back, and noted it, "—is publication. Copies. Witnesses. A chest in a river is evidence. Evidence in forty hands is a movement. Do you see the difference?"

"Yes, sir."

"Good. And Brandt—" he had almost let her go, then added it lightly, the way one adds salt, "—the guide. Thackeray. If he surfaces near this business, I want to know his habits, his debts, and his friends. Especially his friends. Ghosts are harmless until they find company."

She had nodded and gone, and Corven had returned to his drainage assessments with the peculiar contentment of a man who has set several small clocks ticking and knows they will all chime on schedule.

* * *

Friday evening brought the first reports, and they were better than he'd hoped, which immediately made him suspicious.

Doss reported that a bookbinder named Vance—a Vessian, of course, they always were—had been seen speaking to a riverman at the market, and that the riverman's sleeve had borne a rust-colored stain that Doss, in his fumbling way, had gotten close enough to examine. Iron oxide, most likely. Or the residue of a seal pressed long against wet metal.

Brandt reported that the same riverman had pawned nothing, sold nothing, told no one anything—which was itself telling. An innocent man who pulled treasure from a river crowed about it within the hour. Guilt this quiet meant the man knew what he'd found.

And the boy, Tam—the ferry-hand, all elbows and hero worship—had been seen running errands between the ferry landing and the bookbinder's shop with the furtive energy of a boy who believed he was in a story.

Corven sat with these facts after supper, feet toward the fire, and performed his favorite exercise: imagining himself his opponent.

If I am Thackeray, he thought, I have a box of dynamite and no matches. I cannot sell it—the buyers are watched. I cannot publish it—I have no standing, no name, no court that would hear a blacklisted guide. I cannot destroy it—it is my only leverage against the debt I owe and the blame I carry. So I wait. I decode. I look for a partner. And when I have one, I run for the pass, because the only audience left to me is over the mountains, and the season is closing.

Which gave Corven his own deadline, and he resented it. He had planned to let the permits expire in peace, unremarked, a victory so bloodless it wouldn't even qualify as a victory—just a Tuesday, one Tuesday among many. Now he would need to act, and action, however carefully staged, left marks.

Very well. Marks could be sanded.

He reviewed his assets the way a miser reviews coin. Twelve riders he could raise from the garrison on a word, though riders on the southern approach were conspicuous and conspicuousness cost him. He had the debt Thackeray owed the ferryman Osric—a small, grubby lever, but levers were levers. He had the guild, which owed its charter to his signature and could be reminded of the fact. He had, above all, time measured against weather: the passes would close within weeks regardless, and a man racing snowfall made mistakes, and mistakes made by desperate men were the cheapest kind.

And he had one asset he touched only rarely, kept at the bottom of the drawer like a coin too valuable to spend: the truth of what had happened on the Vessian side, five years ago. The order he had signed. The route he had changed. The client whose death had been, in the strictest accounting, an expenditure. If it ever came to it—if Thackeray ever stood in front of him with the chest open and witnesses gathering—Corven could still bury him under that old mountain. Dead men testified poorly, and disgraced ones worse.

He hoped it wouldn't come to that. Genuinely. Violence was untidy, and untidiness offended him the way a misaligned shelf offended other men. His preference, always, was for events to resolve themselves quietly: for fear to do the work of force, for a man to hand over a box because he had calculated, correctly, that keeping it would kill him. No blood, no inquiry, no marks. Just a river giving something back and a river taking it away again, and the world none the wiser.

Corven finished his tea, which had gone cold, and drank it anyway on principle.

On the desk before him lay the renewal schedule for the mining concessions, sixty-two days out now, each entry neat as a hymn. He took up his pen and, in the margin beside the earliest date, wrote a single small notation in his private shorthand—a mark meaning, roughly, *watch closely; intervene only if provoked.*

Then he paused, considered the chest sitting somewhere in Juniper's Landing—under a floorboard, behind a bookbinder's press, in the hull of some ferryman's skiff—and added a second mark beneath the first.

It meant: *and if provoked, finish it.*

Outside, the rain kept on, patient as arithmetic, wearing the mountains down one grain at a time. Corven listened to it with something like affection. He had always admired water. It never hurried, never announced itself, and in the end it moved everything.

In the morning, he would write to the garrison. Tonight, he allowed himself the small luxury of believing he would not need to.

Somewhere across town, he had no doubt, a man who pulled drowned boxes from rivers was lying awake doing sums of his own.

Let him, Corven thought, snuffing the lamp. His sums will come out wrong. Mine always do.

They always had. That was the trouble with the other side of an equation: it never got to see the whole ledger.
