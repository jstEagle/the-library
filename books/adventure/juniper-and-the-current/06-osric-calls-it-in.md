## 6. Osric Calls It In

The knock came at an hour when honest people were asleep and Rafael was doing neither.

Rafael had been sitting at the table with the chest open again, which had become a habit the way a wound becomes a habit—touching it to check if it still hurt. It did. The documents lay in their oilcloth sleeves like sleeping snakes, and every night Rafael told himself *I will not look at them tonight*, and every night looked anyway. There was a particular page—a land grant bearing the seal of Vessia, countersigned by a surveyor whose name Rafael knew from maps that no longer existed—that seemed to have been written specifically to keep Rafael awake. It was dated three years after Vessia was dissolved. Which meant someone had been signing away a dead country's mountains while the ink on its grave was still wet.

The knock came again. Three raps, unhurried, spaced like a man who knew the door would be opened eventually because he owned, in some spiritual sense, the door and the house behind it and possibly the person inside it.

Rafael closed the chest. Put the blanket over it—the same blanket that covered it every night, as though wool could make iron forgettable—and went to the door.

Osric Fen stood on the step with a lantern in one hand and a paper-wrapped parcel under his arm, smelling of river water and fried onions, which was how Osric smelled at all hours; the man had made his peace with onions decades ago and saw no reason to hide it.

"You're up," Osric said.

"I'm up."

"Good. I brought eels." He held up the parcel as if this explained everything, which, in fairness, it half did. "Caught them myself this morning. Well. Pulled them out of nets I set yesterday. The point is they're fresh and you look like a man who hasn't eaten anything but worry since the low water started."

It was true, and Rafael resented being legible. "Come in, then."

* * *

They ate the eels at the table where the chest sat under its blanket, and Rafael spent the entire meal aware of the chest the way one is aware of a bruise—constantly, uselessly, with a small flare of attention every time anything brushed near it. Osric ate with his elbows wide and his eyes narrow, which was his way of thinking. He'd been a ferryman on the Serrano for thirty years, a broad weathered slab of a man with hands like knotted rope and a laugh that could be heard across the river, though he wasn't laughing now.

"Low water's been good to you," Osric said at last, in the tone of a man opening negotiations he'd already concluded in his head.

"It's been good to my backache."

"Found anything interesting down there? In the mud?"

Rafael kept chewing. This required effort. "River gives up what it gives up. Old boots. A church bell two years ago, remember that? Half of Juniper came to look at it."

"A bell." Osric nodded slowly. "A bell has no value to anyone. Nobody pays for bells." He set down his fork with great care, aligning it parallel to the edge of the plate like a man squaring accounts. "But a chest, now. An iron chest, hauled up out of the shallows by a guide who's supposed to be keeping his head below the waterline. That's worth something. To certain parties."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, a dog was making its opinions about the moon known.

"Who told you?" Rafael asked.

"Nobody told me. I've got eyes. You've been walking around town for two weeks with a stain on your sleeve shaped like a seal, scrubbing at it like it'll come off if you hate it enough. And you bought lamp oil twice in one week from Petra at the chandlery, and Petra tells me everything, because Petra tells everyone everything, which is why I buy my oil there instead of from her sister, who charges less but keeps her mouth shut, which is no use to me at all." He leaned back, chair creaking under him. "Also, Tam saw you dragging something heavy up from the bank and Tam can't keep a secret any better than a net can hold soup. So. Who told me? Everyone, more or less. Quietly. The way news travels in a town this size: not loudly, but thoroughly."

Rafael put down the fork. The eel sat in Rafael's stomach like a stone with ambitions.

"How much do you want, Osric?"

"Now that," said Osric, "is the right question, and I'm glad we've arrived at it, because I rehearsed several worse ways to get here." He pulled a folded paper from his coat—not a fine paper, just a scrap of ferry ledger, but the figures on it were written in the careful hand of a man who had counted this debt many times, probably aloud, probably while drunk. "Two years ago, I rowed a disgraced guide across the Serrano at night, past a checkpoint, with soldiers on the far bank close enough to hear them clearing their throats. Two years of room and board I never charged for, because I'm sentimental. Interest, because I'm not stupid. Repayment schedule, which you have honored exactly once, in fish."

"The fish were good fish."

"The fish were adequate fish. The total stands at forty-one crowns, twelve shillings." He turned the paper around so Rafael could read it, which was unnecessary cruelty, since Rafael had known the number would be bad and it was. "And I'm calling it in. Now. In full."

"Eleven days," Rafael said. "That's what I have. Eleven days before—" Rafael stopped, because finishing that sentence meant explaining what happened in eleven days, and explaining that meant showing Osric the chest, and showing Osric the chest meant—

Meant what? That was the question that had been circling all evening like a gull over a fish market. Osric already knew about the chest. Osric had just said so, in front of the eels, in front of God and the fire. The only secret left in this house was what was *inside* the chest, and Rafael was beginning to suspect that the inside was the part that got people killed.

"Before what?" Osric asked, mild as milk.

"Before winter. Before the passes close. Before everything gets expensive."

"Everything's already expensive. That's my point." Osric rubbed his jaw, and for a moment the ferryman's mask slipped and Rafael saw underneath it something tired and genuinely sorry. "Look, Rafe. I don't enjoy this. You crossed my water and I carried you, and there's a rule older than either of us that what crosses a ferryman's water belongs to the ferryman until paid for. I've bent that rule for two years because I liked having you around. You fix things. You fixed my winch. You taught Tam knots without me asking, and you never once asked me who I used to be before Juniper's Landing, which in this town is practically a virtue." He tapped the ledger scrap. "But somebody else has been asking questions too. Not the friendly kind. A clerk, week before last. Neat little man, gloves in summer, asking after 'recent recoveries from the riverbed.' Paid for his tea and left exact change, which in my experience is the mark of either a saint or a magistrate's man, and this fellow didn't have the face for sainthood."

Corven. The name arrived in Rafael's mind without being invited, cold as river water in March.

"So here's how it stands," Osric went on. "You pay me forty-one crowns, twelve, within eleven days, or I sell what I know to the neat little man's employer. Not because I want to. Because if I don't, and they find out I knew and stayed quiet, it's my neck next to yours, and I've only got the one neck and I'm using it."

"And if I tell you what's in the chest," Rafael said slowly, "you might not need selling to anyone. You might find you're already sold—on the other side of it."

Osric was quiet for a long moment. Outside, the dog gave up on the moon and went to bark at something more reasonable.

"That," he said finally, "is exactly the kind of sentence that makes me nervous. Because it means whatever's in that chest isn't treasure. Treasure, you'd have opened it in daylight and thrown a party. Whatever's in there is trouble wearing a seal." He stood, gathered his lantern, and paused at the door with his hand on the frame. "Eleven days, Rafe. Forty-one crowns, twelve. Or I go to the neat little man, and I'll hate myself while I do it, but I'll sleep after, which is more than I can say for you—you've got the look of a man who sleeps sitting up facing the door."

He let himself out. The lantern light swayed away down the path toward the river, and Rafael sat alone with the bones of the eels and the weight of the arithmetic.

Forty-one crowns, twelve. Rafael had, in the tin on the mantel, four crowns and some shillings that had been saved against a pair of boots. Eleven days. No clients—no licensed guide may take clients, that was rather the point of being blacklisted—and no honest work that paid in crowns instead of vegetables.

Rafael looked at the blanket-covered chest for a long time.

Then, because the night demanded it, lifted the blanket and opened it again, and read the same page again, the one signed by a dead country's surveyor, and thought: *someone hid this where only a guide could find it.* Someone had weighted that chest into the shallows of the Serrano knowing—knowing—that the low-water season would expose it, and knowing who worked those waters. That hadn't been luck. That had been a message, addressed to whoever was fool enough to pick it up.

Fool enough. Picked up. Delivered.

There was a knock at the door again, softer this time, and Rafael's heart performed a maneuver it had no business performing, because Rafael knew that knock too—two quick, one slow, apologetic even in rhythm.

Mirela Vance stood on the step with her shawl drawn tight and a bookbinder's smudges still on her fingers despite the hour. She glanced past Rafael into the room, at the open chest, and did not pretend she hadn't seen it.

"We need to talk," she said. "About the dates on those grants. I've been reading them all day, and there's something you need to see before the season turns."

Rafael stepped aside to let her in, and thought: eleven days, forty-one crowns, twelve shillings, one magistrate's clerk with immaculate change, and now a bookbinder who decoded ciphers standing in the doorway with the look of a woman carrying news that had a deadline attached.

The walls were closing in. But for the first time in two years, they seemed to be closing in around something worth keeping.
