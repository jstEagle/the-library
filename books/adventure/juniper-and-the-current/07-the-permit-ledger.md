## 7. The Permit Ledger

Mirela Vance had bound four hundred books in her life, and she could tell you the temper of every glue she'd ever mixed. Fish glue was patient. Hide glue was vain and set fast. Wheat paste forgave everything. But the glue that held a lie together—she'd learned this in the archive rooms of Vessia, before the maps were redrawn—was the strongest adhesive known to humankind, and it only needed one thing to fail: someone willing to read closely enough.

She had been reading closely since dawn.

The chest sat open on the floor of her workroom above the bindery, its iron lid propped against the wall like a door someone had kicked in. Rafael Thackeray had gone out at first light—she'd heard the boards creak, the careful tread of a person trying not to be heard, which is itself a kind of confession—and left her alone with the documents, the good lamp, and a pot of tea going cold in increments she refused to acknowledge.

She worked the way she'd been taught in the Vessian state archive, which was to say she worked like a suspicious person. First the paper. She held each sheet to the window light, tilting it, hunting for the watermark. There. In the lower corner of the third land grant, faint as a thought: the interlocked mountain and star of the Vessian Crown Survey. Nobody forged watermarks twenty years ago. Counterfeiting paper was expensive, and the republic that had swallowed Vessia had never imagined anyone would care enough to check.

Then the ink. Iron gall, browned at the edges the way iron gall does when it has sat for years, not months. The handwriting on the grants was a surveyor's hand—cramped, certain, numbers written like they were carved in stone. The handwriting on the transfer endorsements, the ones that moved Vessian land into the hands of the concession companies, was a different animal entirely. Loopy. Confident. The hand of someone who had never once worried about being audited.

And the dates. Mirela laid three documents side by side on her workbench, weighting the corners with her bone folder, her pouncing pad, and a half-eaten roll she'd been meaning to eat since yesterday.

The Vessian land grant for the Aldermine valley: dated the fourteenth of Harvestmonth, in the last year of Vessia.

The republic's annexation decree dissolving Vessia: dated the second of Frostmonth, the same year.

The transfer of the Aldermine valley to the Corven Consolidated Mining Company: dated the ninth of *Harvestmonth*—eleven days *after* the grant, and nearly a month *before* the land had legally ceased to be Vessian.

Mirela sat back on her stool and laughed. It came out of her sideways, startled, the laugh of a person who has found a body in a library and doesn't know what else to do with her face.

"They didn't even try," she said to the empty room. "They couldn't even be bothered to forge the calendar."

* * *

By midday she had the whole architecture of it spread across her workbench like a dissected animal, pinned open with bone folders.

The republic's mining concessions—the great southern works that had made three ministers rich and paid for two new government palaces—were all built on the same trick, repeated eleven times with the diligence of a man copying lines at school. Each concession required a clean chain of title. Each chain of title began with a "transfer from dissolved Vessian holdings." And each transfer was dated in the impossible window: after the Vessian grant, before the annexation, when the land in question belonged, legally and impossibly, to a nation that still existed and an authority that had already been told to stop existing.

The signatures were worse. Mirela knew the hand of Chancellor Davor Ilic—every Vessian schoolchild had copied his surrender address in penmanship class, a fact she now found darkly funny. The "Vessian signatory" on six of the eleven transfers was a competent imitation of Ilic's signature, except that Ilic had been under house arrest in the capital for the entire month in question, a matter of public record, or it had been, back when there was a public and a record.

The seventh transfer was signed by someone named "M. Vance, Registrar of Vessian Lands."

Mirela looked at that one for a long time.

She had never been a registrar. She had been nineteen when the annexation came, an apprentice binder with ink permanently under her fingernails and a father who worked in the Crown Survey office. Her father had *died* in the Crown Survey office, in the fire that had consumed eleven years of grant records three weeks after the annexation—the fire that had made all of this forgery possible, because who could dispute a title when the originals were ash?

She had never known, until this morning, why the fire had mattered. She had thought it was chaos. Chaos doesn't date its documents eleven days early. Chaos doesn't need a registrar who never existed.

Her hands were steady. That surprised her. She had expected them to shake, and instead they were steady the way water is steady in a deep well—still on the surface, and very far down.

* * *

The ledger was the thing that made it real.

It was bound in the chest beneath the loose documents—a thick, ugly, official thing, quarter-bound in green goat over boards, its spine stamped in the republic's bureaucratic shorthand: *CONCESSION REGISTRY, SOUTHERN DISTRICT, VOL. IX.* Someone had stolen it out of a government office, and Mirela blessed them for it, because loose papers could be called forgeries, but a registry ledger was a registry ledger. It had marginalia in three different clerks' hands. It had tea rings. It had the beautiful, boring authenticity of an object nobody had ever wanted to fake.

She turned to the Aldermine entry, and there it was, in the column every binder and archivist knows to look for, the column that is the whole reason ledgers have margins:

*Term of concession: twenty years from date of registration. Renewal contingent upon unchallenged title at expiration.*

*Expiration: last day of the grazing season, this year.*

She did the arithmetic without wanting to. The grazing season ended when the passes closed. The passes closed with the first real snow. And the first real snow in the Serrano range came, in a good year, in five weeks. In a bad year, in three.

After that date, the concessions would roll over. Unchallenged title. The forgeries would have stood unchallenged for twenty years, and twenty years, in the eyes of every court the republic recognized, was not a fraud anymore. It was history. It was *the way things had always been.* The Aldermine valley, the Kestrel lodes, the river-bottoms where Vessian farming families had been moved off at gunpoint—all of it would pass from "contested" into "settled," and settled things, Mirela knew, were the hardest things in the world to unmake. You could unmake a war. You could hardly unmake a property line that had grown grass.

She looked out the window at Juniper's Landing, at the smoke rising off the ferry landing, at Rafael Thackeray's silhouette on the bridge—she'd learned to pick him out at a distance now, by the way he walked, like a man perpetually listening for rockfall—and she felt the hope arrive, whole and warm and immediately saddled with a deadline, like a gift horse with a countdown carved on its teeth.

The documents were real. The fraud was provable. The proof was in her workroom.

And it was worth exactly nothing in thirty-five days.

* * *

The exile council was the other half of the equation, and the other half was worse.

There were perhaps four thousand Vessian exiles scattered across the borderlands, and most of them, Mirela thought not unkindly, were tired. They had spent twenty years being from nowhere, working other people's trades, keeping their language alive in kitchens and at funerals. The exile council—the loose, quarrelsome, magnificent body that met twice a year in a rented hall over a tannery in the valley town—had exactly two functioning committees: one that organized relief for widows, and one that argued about whether to petition the republic for recognition or to pretend the republic didn't exist. Neither committee had ever accomplished anything that couldn't be measured in soup.

But they had one thing. They had standing. Under the old border treaty—the one the republic still honored, because it bound *them* to nothing but looked very noble—a claim against transferred Vessian lands had to be lodged by "the lawful representatives of the Vessian people," which meant the council, which meant the documents had to be *in their hands*, authenticated and witnessed, before the expiration date. Not sent. Not promised. In their hands.

Over the range. Before snowfall.

Mirela stood at her workbench and looked at the map of the Serrano range that hung above her press—she'd drawn it herself, from memory, over three winters, because the new maps left Vessia off entirely and she had decided, in a small and stubborn way, that this was not acceptable. The range ran like a spine of broken glass. There were four crossings. Two were closed by rockfall years ago. One belonged to the mining company and had gates and guards. And the last one—

The last one was marked on her map in her own handwriting, in ink she'd mixed the winter after the fire, when she was young and furious: *Thackeray Pass.* She had never been over it. Nobody local went over it. It was a guide's route, a hard route, a route with a reputation, and the reputation had a name attached to it, and the name had spent two years in Juniper's Landing trying to be furniture.

She heard the workroom door, and didn't turn. She'd have known that particular attempt at quiet anywhere.

"You walk like a man trying not to wake a sleeping bear," she said. "In a house with one bear. Who is already awake."

"I'll bear it in mind," Rafael said. There was a paper bag in their hand and the particular set of the shoulders that meant they'd spent the morning being careful in public. "I brought rolls. The good ones, from the widow Anka. She asked after you."

"Anka asks after everyone. It's how she finds out things." Mirela took the bag anyway, because the rolls were genuinely good and because she was learning that Rafael showed care the way other people showed receipts—practically, and slightly embarrassed about it. "Sit down. I need to show you something, and then I need to tell you something, and the second thing is going to ruin your afternoon."

Rafael sat on the good stool, the one by the window, without being offered it. That was new, too. Two weeks ago they'd have stood in the doorway like a man waiting for a verdict.

Mirela laid the three documents out first—the grant, the decree, the transfer—and walked Rafael through the dates the way she'd walked herself through them, slowly, letting the impossible window open up in the air between them. She watched Rafael's face do what faces did: the frown, the recount, the moment the arithmetic lands in the stomach.

"They dated it wrong," Rafael said. "Eleven days. They stole a country and they couldn't get the calendar right."

"They never thought anyone would look. The originals burned. Who checks a title against ashes?" Mirela touched the transfer with its forged *M. Vance* and decided, not for the first time, that she would tell Rafael about her father some other evening, an evening with wine in it. "But here's the ledger. Look at the margin."

Rafael read. Mirela watched them read, and watched the understanding arrive, and watched—this was the part she'd been waiting for, the part she'd turned over all morning—watched the understanding curdle into something heavier. Because Rafael Thackeray was a guide before they were anything else, and a guide reads a date like that and does not think *fraud.* A guide thinks *weather.*

"First snow," Rafael said quietly. "On the high passes. Thirty days, if we're lucky. Less if we're not."

"Thirty-five days until expiration. The documents have to be in the council's hands before the season ends. Over the range." Mirela folded her arms. "I've been trying to find another way all morning. The southern road adds eleven days. The river is low but it bends east, away from the tannery hall. There is no way. There's the pass, or there's nothing."

"And the pass is mine." Rafael said it flatly, not a boast. A fact being handed over, like a rope being handed over.

"The pass is yours," Mirela agreed. "Which brings me to the thing that ruins your afternoon."

"I'd wondered when that was coming."

She took a breath. She had rehearsed this on the walk back from the well, and the rehearsal had been confident, and now that the moment was here the confidence had gone off to look for work elsewhere, and what remained was a woman standing in a room full of a dead nation's paperwork asking a disgraced stranger to walk back into the place that had destroyed them.

"I need you to guide us over Thackeray Pass," she said. "Me, the documents, whoever we can trust. Before the snow. And I know—" she held up a hand, because Rafael had gone very still, the stillness of a person holding a door shut against weather, "—I know what that mountain took from you. I know the name you carry. I'm not asking you to stop being careful. I'm telling you that for the first time in twenty years, being careful is the *expensive* option. If those permits roll over, every Vessian family on this side of the border loses their claim forever, quietly, on a date nobody will even notice. My father's office burned so that this ledger could exist. I am not letting it expire in a margin."

The room was quiet. Down in the street, the widow Anka was arguing cheerfully with someone about the price of onions, and the argument rose up through the floorboards like proof that the ordinary world was still running, indifferent, on schedule.

Rafael looked at the ledger for a long time. Then at the map on the wall, at the pass marked in a furious young woman's ink. Then, last, at Mirela, and there was something in their face she couldn't file yet—not gratitude, not fear, something that had the shape of both and the weight of neither.

"You said *us,*" Rafael said. "And *whoever we can trust.* Mirela. In this town, I can count the people who trust me on one hand, and two of them are you and a ferryman who hates me. Corven has watchers on the bridge—I've seen them, have you? The moment we start buying rope and dried meat, we're announcing ourselves. And even if we get up the mountain—" Rafael's voice did something complicated, "—even if the mountain lets us, the council is a room full of tired people over a tannery. What if they won't act? What if we carry this over the range and they file it under *argue about it in spring?*"

"Then we carry it back down and put copies in every kitchen in the exile quarter," Mirela said. "But Rafael. Look at the dates again. The fraud has a deadline. That means the fraud is *afraid.* Things that are safe don't have expiration dates." She tapped the ledger, gently, the way she'd tap a book she was proud of having bound. "Somebody hid these documents in that river knowing that only a guide would ever find them. Somebody has been waiting twenty years for exactly the person standing on my good stool. I don't believe in much anymore, but I believe the mountain has been holding this door open for you, and I believe it is going to close."

Rafael was quiet long enough that the tea, which had been merely cold, achieved a kind of geological state.

"There's a debt," Rafael said at last, and the words came out like something being pried up. "To Osric. For the crossing, two years ago. It's called in, or it's about to be. And Corven—" A muscle moved in their jaw. "Corven knows I'm here. He's always known. If I go up that mountain, I'm not hiding anymore. Whatever I am when I come down, I'll have to be it in the open."

"Good," Mirela said.

Rafael looked at her. "*Good?*"

"You've been furniture for two years. I've watched it. You apologize to doorframes." She picked up the forged transfer with her own impossible name on it and held it up between them, like a small, ugly flag. "Somebody signed my name to a theft before I was old enough to sign anything at all. Somebody made me a registrar of a country that stopped existing. So no, I don't have any patience left for hiding. The fraud expires in thirty-five days. So does my willingness to be careful."

Outside, the light had begun to go the color of weak tea, and off toward the range the peaks had that clean, hard-edged look they got when the air was thinking about winter. Mirela saw Rafael notice it too—saw the guide's eye go to the high country the way a sailor's eye goes to the horizon, helplessly, professionally, the way you look at a thing that owns you.

"Thirty-five days," Rafael said. "To raise a party, outfox a magistrate, cross a pass that killed the last person I took over it, and hand a dead country's receipts to a committee that argues about soup." They rubbed their face with both hands. When they lowered it, something had settled in their expression—still afraid, but the fear was standing up straight now, at attention, given orders. "The first thing we need is money. The second thing is rope. The third thing—"

The knock came at the workroom door. Three slow raps, evenly spaced, the knock of a person who has never in their life had to wait for anything.

Mirela and Rafael looked at each other.

Through the frosted glass of the door, distorted but unmistakable, fell the long shadow of a man wearing a magistrate's hat.
