## 8. Two Countries That Want Me Gone

Rafael had a theory that every town kept one bench nobody sat on, and in Juniper's Landing it was the one behind the salt shed, facing the river, in the shade of a willow that had given up on being beautiful sometime before the war. Rafael had claimed it the first week and had defended it since by the simple method of looking like someone you wouldn't want to share a bench with. It worked. Two years of practice had made the look effortless, which was the saddest kind of effort there was.

It was here, on the unsittable bench, that Rafael did the thing that had become a nightly ritual and a daily shame: thinking about the mountain.

Not the whole mountain. Just one afternoon of it. Just one rope length of it, actually, if you wanted to be precise, and Rafael was always precise about that particular rope length, the way a man who has swallowed a fishhook is precise about his throat.

The Serrano went by below, low and brown in the dry season, carrying its usual cargo of silt and secrets. Somewhere upstream, in a locked room above the bookbinder's shop, an iron chest sat full of paper that could burn down a government. Rafael had been trying not to think about it all day, which was like trying not to think about the chest, which was like trying not to think about the mountain. Rafael's mind had exactly one trick, and the trick was going back.

"You're brooding," said a voice, and Tam dropped onto the bench's far end, uninvited, radiating the cheerful immunity of the young. He had a fish on a stringer and river mud to his knees and the total self-satisfaction of a boy who had out-fished his elders.

"Go away."

"You're brooding *professionally*," Tam amended. "Like it's your trade. Like you get paid for it."

"I don't get paid for anything. That's the problem."

"Osric says you used to be famous."

"Osric says a lot of things. Osric once told me the Serrano was safe to swim in September, and I believed him, and I have never fully forgiven myself."

Tam laughed, then didn't. He was learning, in his slow, dogged way, when Rafael's jokes had a floor under them and when they didn't. He squinted out at the water. "What happened to you? Really. Nobody'll say. They just do this—" he pulled a face, a kind of respectful wince, the face of a town that had decided some questions were rude, "—whenever your name comes up. Which is never. Which is its own kind of answer."

Rafael looked at the boy. Seventeen, maybe. Hands like oars. The kind of earnest that made older people either protective or cruel, depending on their reserves.

"Ask Osric," Rafael said.

"I did. He said, and I'm quoting, 'Ask him yourself, you great gosling, it's not my scar.'"

Rafael almost smiled. Almost. "He's right. It isn't."

"So?"

The willow dropped a leaf into the current, and the current took it, and Rafael watched it go with the particular envy of someone who had once tried to be a leaf and failed.

"Sit still," Rafael said finally. "This isn't a story. Stories have shapes. This is just a hole with edges."

* * *

The client's name was Aldric Vey. Rafael said the name to himself the way you touch a bruise—briefly, to check it still hurts, and hating that part of you hopes it won't.

Aldric had been a surveyor out of Vessia, back when Vessia still appeared on maps, back when the border ran along the eastern shoulder of the range instead of through the middle of everyone's lives. He was fifty-one years old, precise as a compass needle, and he had hired Rafael Thackeray—the Rafael Thackeray, guild license number 114, three first ascents, a waiting list a season long—to guide a survey party up the western approaches and across to the Vessian side, where he was mapping old grazing boundaries for a land commission.

Rafael remembered everything about the morning they set out. Aldric had brought too much paper and not enough rope, and had argued cheerfully about both. He had a habit of naming things. Not officially—Aldric had no power to name anything—but privately, in his notebook, in his dry, patient handwriting: *this col shall be called Windrow, because the wind rows the snow off it like a farmer raking.* He'd named a glacier after his late wife. He'd named a particularly stubborn boulder field after his mother, and had laughed harder at his own joke than anyone else did, and Rafael had found, to their private astonishment, that they liked him.

That was the part Rafael could never get past, in the long ledger of that trip. Not the mountain. The liking. You can lose a stranger and grieve and go on. Rafael had lost a friend and had been handed the bill for it.

The third day on the Vessian side, the weather turned. Nothing dramatic—no storm with a name, no thunder. Just the particular grey that Rafael had been taught, by a father who died on a mountain himself, to treat as a door closing. Rafael called the camp. Said they'd hold at the shelf below the col and cross in the morning if the sky cleared.

Aldric agreed. Aldric always agreed; he was the most agreeable man Rafael ever guided, which made what came later worse, because agreeable men don't wander off.

But in the morning Aldric was gone.

Not gone-gone. His boots were gone. His notebook was gone. His ice axe was gone, and a note in his tidy hand said, in Vessian, *gone ahead to check the line, back by noon, do not follow, the slope is yours to judge.* Which was Aldric all over: considerate to the point of folly. He had left the note weighted under a stone so it wouldn't blow away, because even walking into the white he was thinking of other people's convenience.

Rafael waited two hours. Rafael was later told—by the inquiry, by the guild, by every mouth in two countries—that two hours was cowardice dressed as caution. Rafael had judged the slope unstable. Fresh loading above the col, wind slabs forming, the whole face holding its breath. Every instinct said *stay*. Every instinct also said *he's out there*, and the two instincts fought like dogs while Rafael stood at the edge of camp with a rope coiled and uncoiled in their hands.

Rafael went. Of course Rafael went. That was never the question. The question, the only question the inquiry cared about, was *when*, and whether the *when* had killed him.

They found him at noon, or rather the mountain produced him at noon, at the toe of a slide that had run while Rafael was still descending toward camp from the search line above. The slide had come off a slope Rafael had looked at, that morning, and judged stable. Rafael had looked at it and been wrong, or had looked at it and been right and the mountain had changed its mind, and no one would ever be able to tell the difference, and that was the sentence. Not the death. The *unknowing*.

The inquiry sat in a valley town hall that smelled of lamp oil and boiled mutton, and it asked Rafael the same question eleven ways: *Guide Thackeray, did you or did you not assess the eastern face as safe for travel.* And eleven times Rafael said what was true: *I assessed it as safe at dawn. I cannot assess what it did at nine.* And the inquiry wrote down, in the language of institutions, that the guide had proceeded onto avalanche terrain with a client unroped and unsupervised, and that the guide's judgment was found wanting, and license number 114 was suspended, pending, and then not pending.

There was more. There was always more, with institutions. A witness—a porter, a nervous young man from a village that had recently become the wrong side of a new border—testified that he'd seen Rafael and Aldric arguing the evening before, and that the argument had been about the route, and that Rafael had wanted to push on and Aldric had wanted to hold. The porter's testimony was shaky, secondhand, translated twice. It was also, Rafael understood much later, the only testimony the inquiry wanted, because the inquiry was not really asking what happened on the mountain. The inquiry was asking what could be said about the mountain that would be useful.

Vessia was dissolving that year. Everyone could feel it coming, the way you feel a slope loading. The annexation was being negotiated in rooms Rafael would never see, and a dead surveyor mapping *old grazing boundaries*—old Vessian claims, old Vessian lines—was an inconvenience in those rooms. A dead surveyor killed by a *negligent foreign guide* was not an inconvenience. He was a gift. A tragedy with a tidy moral attached, useful in a dozen ways Rafael was never shown and could only guess at.

Rafael didn't know any of that at the time. At the time, Rafael knew only that Aldric Vey was dead, and that Rafael had looked at a slope and been wrong, or been right, and that the difference no longer mattered to anyone who mattered, and that the guild had taken the license, and that the name Rafael Thackeray had become a thing said in a certain tone of voice, and that two countries—the one that had employed Rafael and the one that had erased Aldric's—had each found a use for the story, and in neither version was Rafael a person.

So Rafael did what ghosts do. Went where names don't follow. Crossed the border with Osric's help and Osric's price, and landed in Juniper's Landing, and learned to be nobody, and got very good at it, and told no one, and carried the slope of that eastern face around like a stone in the chest, and checked it nightly, the way you check a bruise, and it always, always still hurt.

* * *

"—and you're doing it again," Tam said. "The brooding. You went somewhere and left your body here on the bench. Your eyes went all river-y."

Rafael surfaced. The light had gone long and gold; the fish on Tam's stringer had stopped looking smug and started looking like dinner. "How long was I gone?"

"Dunno. Long enough I finished a whole thought, and I'm slow." Tam was quiet a moment, picking at the bench's splintered arm. Then, with the bluntness of the young, which is either cruelty or grace depending on the day: "Was it your fault? What happened up there?"

Rafael considered the question honestly, which is more than the inquiry ever did. Considered the slope, the sky, the note under the stone, the two hours, the rope coiled and uncoiled. Considered the porter's testimony, and the inquiry's appetite for it, and the way the story had hardened into something with a use.

"I don't know," Rafael said. "That's the answer. That's the whole answer, and it's the one nobody would take. I don't know. And I've decided that not knowing is worse than guilt, because at least guilt you can carry. Not knowing you just have to *hold*."

Tam frowned at the river, working through it. "So you didn't kill him."

"I don't know."

"But you didn't *not* kill him either."

"I don't *know*, Tam."

"Yeah, but—" the boy turned, and there was something in his face that Rafael hadn't seen before, some stubborn new thing taking root, "—everybody in this town acts like not knowing and guilty are the same thing. My da does it. Osric does it, sort of, in his way. But they weren't there. You were the only one who was there, and you're the only one who says you don't know. Everybody else knows *too much*, if you ask me."

Rafael looked at him for a long moment. "You're smarter than your fishing suggests."

"That's a low bar."

"It's a riverbed bar."

Tam grinned, shouldered his stringer, and stood. Then paused. "The chest," he said, carefully, the way you step onto ice. "The one from the river. Osric says it's got Vessian writing in it. Mirela Vance has been seen buying lamp oil like she's lighting a cathedral. And you've been walking around for two days looking like a man deciding something." He shrugged, all innocence, all ambush. "I'm just saying. If you're deciding something, I know how to row."

He went, before Rafael could answer, which was probably the wisest thing anyone had done all week.

Rafael sat on the unsittable bench as the light died and the first lamp in the ferry house came up across the water. Two countries wanted Rafael gone. One had taken the license, one had taken the blame, and between them they had manufactured a ghost, and the ghost had gotten comfortable, and the ghost had almost believed its own namelessness.

But the mountain didn't forget, and neither, it turned out, did rivers. The Serrano had given up an iron chest stamped with a dead nation's seal, and inside it were papers about land and lines and who had moved which boundary, and somewhere in that paper, Rafael was almost certain, was the same machinery that had chewed up Aldric Vey and spat out Rafael Thackeray. The same rooms. The same appetite. The same tidy stories.

Rafael had spent two years not knowing. Here, suddenly, was a chance to know—to open the whole wound, not to heal it, but to finally see its shape. And the terrifying thing, sitting there in the dark with the river talking its low talk, was that Rafael wanted it. Wanted it the way the drowning want air.

Tomorrow, Rafael would go to the bookbinder's shop. Tomorrow, the chest would be opened properly, and the papers would be read, and whatever was in them would put an end to two years of not knowing—one way or the other.

Rafael stood, and the bench was finally, truly empty, and somewhere upstream a light was burning over a locked door, and the current went on carrying everything—silt, secrets, leaves, the dead—toward a sea that kept no register at all.
