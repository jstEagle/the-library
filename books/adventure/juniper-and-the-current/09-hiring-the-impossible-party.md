## 9. Hiring the Impossible Party

Rafael had recruited expedition parties for nineteen years, and in all that time had never once had to beg.

There was a protocol to it. You named the mountain, named the pay, named the danger in honest terms, and let the mountain do the persuading. Men who needed to be talked into the high country were men who froze at Windrow Col. But this morning, standing on Osric's ferry dock with river fog beading in his beard, Rafael was composing a speech that was mostly begging, and he hated every word of it.

Osric was mending a net, which he did most mornings whether the net needed mending or not, because it gave him an excuse to sit down and an excuse to look busy when his wife called from the house. He was a broad man gone soft at the edges, with a face like a river stone—worn smooth, hard to read, and liable to crack your boot if you kicked it.

"Osric," Rafael said.

"Ferry's closed."

"The ferry's never closed. You'd row a corpse across for a copper."

"The corpse would have to pay in advance." Osric didn't look up. "What do you want, Thackeray?"

"I want to hire you."

That got the net down. Osric squinted at him across the fog. "Hire me. To do what, exactly? Because if you're asking me to row, I'll remind you I own the boat, and owners don't row."

"A crossing. Over the range. Before the snows."

Osric set down the net with great care, the way a man sets down a thing he might later throw. "You want to take a ferryman up a mountain."

"I want to take a ferryman up a mountain."

"In winter."

"In late autumn, with luck."

"Thackeray." Osric stood, joints cracking like kindling. "I've known you two years. You smuggled me nothing, you stole nothing, you paid your debt slow and honest like a man paying off a mule. You are the single most boring criminal I have ever done business with. So when you come to my dock at dawn and ask me to climb a mountain, I know one of two things is true. Either you've gone mad, or you've found something in that river."

The fog hung between them. Downstream, a heron stood in the shallows with the patience of a creditor.

"Both," Rafael said.

Osric nodded slowly, as if this were the most reasonable answer in the world. "And the pay?"

"That's the difficult part."

"Ah." Osric sat back down and picked up the net. "There it is."

"Your debt. Eleven days of it. I'm asking you to consider it paid in kind—one crossing, your legs, your boat knowledge for the river fords, and your silence."

"My silence about what?"

"About what's in the chest."

Osric's hands went still on the netting. "So there *is* a chest."

Rafael closed his eyes. Two years of careful, boring, mule-like honesty, and he'd undone it in four sentences. "There is a chest."

"And it's worth a mountain crossing."

"It's worth more than that. It's worth—" Rafael stopped. He'd rehearsed this. He had a whole speech about justice and history and the dead of Vessia, and standing in the fog with a ferryman's eyes on him, every word of it sounded like a sermon delivered to a man who'd heard better ones over cheaper wine.

"Sit down, Thackeray," Osric said, "before you fall down. You're the worst liar on this river. It's why I like you." He jerked his chin at the dock boards. "Tell me the truth, all of it, and then I'll tell you no, and we'll both feel better."

Rafael sat. He told him. Not the whole truth—Mirela would have his hide if he handed the contents to a man with a debt and a temper—but the shape of it. Documents. Old ones. The kind that could unmake a magistrate's whole ledger of permits. The kind that had to cross the range before the first snow made the argument permanent.

When he finished, Osric was quiet for a long moment, working a knot in the net that didn't need working.

"You know what my wife will say," he said finally.

"She'll say no."

"She'll say *yes*, and then she'll say *take the boy*." Osric's jaw tightened. "Tam's been asking after you all season. Follows you around like a duckling that's decided you're the pond. If there's danger in this, and there is, because you've got the face of a man describing danger, then I'd rather he were tied to my rope than loose on this river with his imagination."

"Osric—"

"Don't argue. It's not a favor to you. It's a favor to me. A boy who idolizes a disgraced guide is a boy who'll try the pass alone someday if nobody shows him how it's done properly." He stood, net forgotten, and stuck out a hand like a boat cleat. "One crossing. Debt paid. And Thackeray—if I die up there, you tell my wife it was quick and I was brave, and you tell her I said the debt was forgiven, because she'll want it in writing and I'll be in no position."

Rafael took the hand. "You're not going to die."

"Everyone says that." Osric grinned, and it was the grin of a man who had ferried corpses and knew exactly how they'd paid in advance. "Nobody's ever been right."

* * *

Mirela was harder.

Rafael found her in her shop behind the chandler's, and she heard him out standing at her workbench with a bone folder in her hand, tapping it against her palm in a rhythm that meant *I am letting you finish for my own reasons*.

"You need me on the crossing," she said when he was done.

"I need someone who can read the documents if the council asks questions. Someone who can prove they're genuine. That's you."

"And you need someone to carry them if you fall in a crevasse."

"That's also you."

"Because I'm the only one who knows what they say."

"That's also you."

She set the bone folder down. "Rafael. I have spent six years in this town being nobody. Nobody is *safe*. Nobody gets watched. Nobody gets a magistrate's men asking questions at the market. And in one week you have made me into somebody with a name and a mission and a mountain to climb." She wasn't angry. That was the strange part. Her voice had gone quiet and bright, like ice in sun. "Do you know how long I've waited to be somebody again?"

"Then you'll come."

"I'll come." She was already pulling a satchel from under the bench, already counting wax and paper and the little brass decoder she wore on a cord like a saint's medal. "But I'm not coming as your passenger, Thackeray. I'm coming as the reason. If you get tired on the ice, if you think about turning back, if you get some noble idea about saving your own skin—you'll look at me and remember that the documents are the *point*, and you are only the legs." She cinched the satchel. "Can you live with that arrangement?"

"I was hoping for it."

Something flickered across her face—surprise, quickly filed away. "You were?"

"Mirela, I've been carrying that chest's weight since I pulled it out of the river. I'd hand it to the first honest person who'd take it. It just happens the only honest person in Juniper's Landing is you."

"That's demonstrably false," she said. "Tam exists."

* * *

Tam was the easiest and, in Rafael's private accounting, the most troubling.

The boy was sixteen, all elbows and enthusiasm, with a ferryman's shoulders coming in early and a guide's eyes—those quick, cataloguing eyes that read water and weather without being taught. He was waiting on the dock that evening, which meant Osric had told him, which meant the boy had run the whole way from the house and then composed himself into an attitude of casual availability, leaning against a post with a coiled rope he had no reason to hold.

"Evening, Mr. Thackeray."

"Tam."

"Heard you might be needing hands."

"Heard that, did you."

"River talk." Tam shrugged with the elaborate indifference of a boy who had rehearsed the shrug. "I can row, I can haul, I can cook—well, I can boil things, Osric says that counts—and I know the low fords better than anyone my age. Better than some people twice my age." A pause. "Better than Osric, honestly, his knees being what they are, though don't tell him I said—"

"Tam."

The boy stopped. Waiting. That was the trouble with Tam: he wanted it too much, and want like that gets people killed on ice, because a boy who wants the mountain will lie to himself about the weather.

Rafael had seen that want before. He'd seen it in a mirror, at sixteen, on the docks of another river town, begging a guide to take him up. The guide had said yes. Rafael had never decided, in twenty years, whether that had been kindness or the first link in the chain that ended on the Vessian side of the range with a man dead and his own name in the mud.

"I'll be straight with you," Rafael said. "This crossing has teeth. There's a magistrate who wants what we're carrying, and he has riders, and we'll be moving through country where a broken ankle is a death sentence and a storm is a verdict. I'm not hiring you because I need a rower. I'm hiring you because Osric won't come without you, and I need Osric. You'd be along because of arithmetic, not because of anything you've earned."

Tam took this the way he took everything—straight on, chin up. "That's fair."

"It's not an insult."

"I know. It's fair." The boy looked out at the darkening river, and when he spoke again his voice had lost its rehearsed edge. "My da went up the range with a survey crew when I was nine. Never came back. Nobody would ever tell me what the high country's actually like—every grown man in this town either won't talk about it or lies about it. You're the only person I've ever met who tells the truth about mountains, Mr. Thackeray. Even when the truth is ugly. *Especially* then." He turned back. "So yes. Arithmetic. I'll take arithmetic. It's more than anybody else ever offered me."

Rafael looked at him a long moment. The river talked against the pilings. Somewhere upstream, the first real cold of the season was already walking down out of the passes, patient as a magistrate.

"Dawn," Rafael said. "Boots oiled, rope inspected, and if you bring that coat your mother gave you, you'll leave it, because it's cotton and cotton kills. Wool or nothing."

Tam's whole face lit like a lantern. "Wool. Yes. I have wool."

"Tam." Rafael held his eyes until the boy's grin settled into something steadier. "On the ice, when I give an order, you don't think, you don't improve it, you don't volunteer. You do it, and you ask why at camp. Can you do that?"

"Yes, sir."

"Don't call me sir."

"Yes—Thackeray."

"Better."

* * *

They gathered at Osric's table that night, the four of them, and it was, Rafael thought, watching the candle gutter in the draft from the ill-fitting door, the strangest expedition muster of his career. A ferryman arguing with his wife in the next room about a mule. An exile bookbinder drawing route lines on the back of a flour sack with a pencil she'd sharpened with a knife. A boy trying so hard to sit still that he vibrated.

"Three days to the gorge," Mirela said, pencil scratching. "Two through the gorge if the fords are low. Four to Windrow Col. Two more to the council's valley. Twelve days, and the permits expire in eleven."

"Then we don't camp on the twelfth day," Osric called through the wall, and his wife's voice came back through the wall with something that made Tam choke on his tea.

"We move fast and we move light," Rafael said. "One rope of four. If anyone can't keep the pace, we don't split—we turn around. Everyone agrees to that now, at this table, warm, with food in them. Not later. Later is too late for honest answers."

"I agree," Mirela said, not looking up.

"I agree," Tam said, too fast.

Osric came back in rubbing his hands. "I agree, and Marta says if we die she's keeping the boat, and she says it like she means it, so let's not die."

Mirela finally looked up from her flour-sack map, and her eyes went around the table—the ferryman, the boy, the guide—and settled on Rafael with an expression he couldn't quite read. "You understand what we are, don't you?" she said. "A man with no name, a woman with a false one, a debtor, and a child. No guild. No charter. No witnesses." She tapped the map. "If Corven's riders find us on the ice, there is no one in the world who has to care what happened to us."

"Then we'd better be the ones who care," Rafael said.

The candle flame leaned in the draft. Tam, unexpectedly, raised his tin cup. "To arithmetic," he said solemnly.

Osric laughed, a great river-stone crack of a laugh, and clinked his cup against the boy's, and then Mirela's, and then, after a moment's resistance that fooled nobody, Rafael's own.

It was the closest thing to a blessing they were going to get.

They were still laughing—Tam was doing an impression of Osric's knees, which was unkind and accurate—when Rafael saw the shadow cross the window. A man standing in the lane outside, hat down, not moving, not knocking. Just standing, the way a man stands when he's been paid to stand.

The laughter died in the room by degrees, the way warmth leaves a tent.

"That'll be one of Corven's," Osric said quietly. "Been on the dock since Tuesday. Thought you knew."

"I knew," Rafael said. He looked at the flour-sack map, at the thin pencil line climbing toward Windrow Col, and felt the old mountain weight settle onto his shoulders—familiar now, almost comfortable. "I just didn't know he'd found us this fast."

Mirela began rolling up the map. "Then we leave before dawn," she said, "and we leave him standing in the cold."
