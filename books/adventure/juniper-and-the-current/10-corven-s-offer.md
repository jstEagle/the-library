## 10. Corven's Offer

The knock came at dusk, and it was the wrong kind of knock.

Rafael had lived above the cooper's shed on Ferry Lane long enough to know the taxonomy of knocking. Osric knocked like a man demanding entry he already considered his — three heavy blows and a shove of the latch before anyone answered. Tam knocked in quick apologetic bursts, like the door might be embarrassed by the attention. Mirela, when she came at all, knocked once and then waited with a patience that was somehow louder than any drumming.

This knock was measured. Two raps, evenly spaced, and then nothing. A knock that expected the world to arrange itself around it.

Rafael set down the whetstone they'd been pretending to use for an hour and crossed the small room. Through the shutter slats they saw him: Magistrate Corven, alone, no escort, standing in the lane with his hands folded behind his back as though he were inspecting a garden that disappointed him.

For a long moment Rafael considered not answering at all. But a magistrate who knocks twice will knock a third time, and the third time usually comes with witnesses.

They opened the door.

"Thackeray." Corven's voice was mild, almost pleasant, the voice of a man discussing drainage. "You look well. The river air agrees with you."

"Magistrate." Rafael stepped back — not an invitation, exactly, but the only alternative was a conversation conducted through a doorway, and something told them Corven would win either version. "I don't recall inviting the law up my stairs."

"No need. I'm not here as the law." Corven entered, removed his hat, and looked around the room with the unhurried attention of a man cataloguing it. The cot. The stove. The chest, currently hidden under a tarp and a pile of netting that would fool nobody who'd come looking for a chest. He did not look at the chest. That was the most frightening thing about him. "I'm here as a neighbor. As one person who understands the weight of the past, calling on another."

"You don't carry any weight," Rafael said. "That's your defining feature."

Something flickered behind Corven's eyes — not anger. Interest. He hung his hat on the peg by the door as if he owned the peg, and sat down on the single stool without being asked, leaving Rafael standing, which was surely the point.

"Two years ago," Corven said, "a ferryman named Osric Brann paid off a gambling debt he didn't have the coin for. Curious thing, debts. They get paid one way or another. Coin, or goods, or information." He laced his fingers over his knee. "Osric has been very forthcoming lately. A chest came out of the Serrano during low water. Iron. Old. Stamped with a seal that hasn't been legal currency in this valley for twenty years."

The stove ticked. Somewhere below, the cooper's dog barked twice at nothing and gave up.

"I fish what the river gives me," Rafael said. "Mostly it gives me boots and drowned cats."

"And yet you pawned nothing, sold nothing, told no one. A man who pulls a chest of old iron from a river and tells no one is a man who knows what's inside it." Corven smiled thinly. "Or knows what it will cost him. Both, I suspect. You always were thorough, Thackeray. It's why I hired you, once."

*Once.* The word landed where he'd aimed it, right in the soft place between Rafael's shoulder blades where the past lived.

"What do you want?"

"Ah. Good. Directness saves us both an evening." Corven leaned forward, elbows on knees, and for a moment the mildness dropped away and Rafael saw the machinery underneath — patient, oiled, enormous. "Here is what I want. You surrender the chest and its contents to me, tonight, quietly. In exchange: a clean passport under whatever name you prefer. Your debt to Brann, settled — I'll see to it personally; consider it a public service. And passage out of Juniper's Landing before the season turns, with letters of introduction good anywhere south of the range. You could be guiding pilgrims on the southern shrines by midwinter. Warm work. Honest work. Work where nobody asks about Vessia."

He said the name lightly, the way a man says *the pox* or *the war* — a thing finished, a thing best not dwelt upon.

"And if I decline?"

Corven sighed, genuinely regretful, or performing genuine regret so well that the difference stopped mattering. "Then I'm afraid the arithmetic changes. A disgraced guide, hiding under a false name in a border town, found in possession of stolen state documents — because they are stolen, Thackeray, whatever sentimental nonsense the exiles tell themselves. Vessia was dissolved by treaty. Its papers are ash. Possession of them is a crime, and obstruction of a magistrate is a worse one. You'd hang, or you'd rot in the quarry at Kessel, and honestly I'm not sure which is crueler." He rose, retrieved his hat, and turned it slowly in his hands. "I've watched you for two years, you know. Since you surfaced here. Do you know why I never moved against you? Because you were harmless. A ghost keeping to itself. Ghosts are cheap. It's when ghosts start carrying documents that they become expensive."

"The river gave me iron and rot," Rafael said. "If there were papers, they'd have been pulp twenty years ago."

"Probably," Corven agreed pleasantly. "Almost certainly. And if that's true, you lose nothing by handing the box over. Think of it as clearing silt from your conscience." He set the hat on his head and paused at the door, and now he did glance — briefly, precisely — at the tarp and the netting, the way a chess player taps a piece he intends to take later. "You have until the market bell tomorrow evening. After that, my men stop being neighbors too."

The door closed behind him with the softness of a lid.

* * *

Rafael did not sleep.

They sat by the cold stove through the small hours doing the arithmetic Corven had left them, and the arithmetic was brutal in its simplicity. A passport meant a life. Not survival — survival was already managed, mean and narrow, but managed. A *life*. Papers under a new name, roads going south, work that asked nothing of them except competence, which was the one thing Rafael had never lost even when everything else went into the river.

And all it cost was silence. One more silence. They'd carried silences before; they had a whole pack-frame of them, worn smooth from years of hauling. What was one more?

Except.

Except that Mirela Vance had spent three nights bent over those documents by candlelight, her fingers moving across the old land grants like a woman reading braille, reading names — and not just any names. Her mother's name was on the fourth page. Rafael had watched her find it. Had watched her go absolutely still, the way people go still when they step on ice and hear it hold instead of crack. She hadn't cried. She'd said, "They kept the orchard rows in the survey. Whoever forged the transfer couldn't be bothered to change the survey notes. My mother's apple trees are still listed there, Thackeray. On paper, somewhere, her trees still stand." And then she had laughed, one dry note with no humor in it at all, and gone back to work.

A passport could buy Rafael a life. It could not buy back an orchard. It could not un-forge a grant. All it could do was make Rafael comfortable inside a lie that Corven had spent twenty years upholstering.

And underneath that — deeper, in the place Rafael rarely let daylight reach — was the other thing. The mountain. The client whose name Rafael still couldn't say aloud. The route that had been changed by someone above Rafael's pay grade, the storm that had come early, the report that had been written before Rafael ever descended to give testimony. If Corven had done that once — and Rafael had never been able to prove it, only feel it, the way you feel weather coming in a healed fracture — then Corven had built his career on Rafael's ruin. Every year of exile, every false name, every night above the cooper's shed listening to the river: all of it, interest on a debt Corven owed and would never acknowledge unless someone made him.

The chest wasn't just paper. It was the first honest scale anyone had ever put on that particular ledger.

Near dawn, Rafael pulled the tarp back and looked at the seal. The double-headed eagle of Vessia, stamped deep, stubborn, refusing to fade. *Erased from maps,* everyone said. But maps were just paper too, and paper could be reprinted. That was the whole point of the chest. That was the whole point of everything.

"Damn you," Rafael said quietly — to Corven, to the river, to the dead, to no one in particular — and began, methodically, to move the chest.

* * *

Corven walked back along the river road with his collar up against the pre-dawn damp, and permitted himself the small pleasure of assuming the matter was closed.

It was nearly always closed, at this stage. People imagined that corruption required violence, that men like him operated by threat and knife. Amateurs. Violence was a confession of failure. No — you offered a frightened man a door out of a burning building, and he took it, and he thanked you, and the fire was never spoken of again. Thackeray was frightened; Thackeray had been frightened for two years, wore fear like a coat grown to the skin. By tomorrow evening the chest would sit in Corven's cellar, and by spring the permits would expire unchallenged, and the fraud would harden into law the way footprints harden into stone, and no one would ever be able to say it hadn't always been so.

There was one variable. He turned it over as he walked, the way he turned over all variables, patiently, looking for the flaw.

The bookbinder. Vance. She'd come to town four years ago with a forged journeyman's card and excellent references, both of which he'd allowed, because a known exile was a useful exile — a hook on which other fish might eventually impale themselves. But she'd been seen near Thackeray's lane three times this week, and Corven's watchers were not decorative. If the two of them had joined forces — the ghost and the archivist — then the chest was no longer a dead man's luggage. It was a project.

Projects could still be ended. But they required different tools, and Corven disliked changing tools mid-task. It created noise.

He paused at the bridge where the millrace thundered beneath, dark and certain, and allowed himself one moment of something that was not quite doubt. Twenty years ago he had ordered a route changed on a mountain, and a man had died, and a guide had been broken and scattered to the winds, and the concession maps had been signed by autumn. It had been clean. It had been *necessary* — the republic needed those mines the way a body needs blood, and sentiment was a luxury nations could not afford. He had repeated this to himself so many times over so many years that it had worn smooth as a river stone, and he no longer noticed that it was a stone and not a thought.

Tomorrow evening, then. If Thackeray was wise, a passport. If Thackeray was what Corven suspected he might be — what the file suggested, what the old reports hinted at between their careful lines — then something else entirely.

Corven found, to his mild surprise, that he was hoping for wisdom. He had invested two years in Thackeray's harmlessness. It would be irritating to be wrong.

He was wrong.

* * *

The market bell rang at six, and at half past, Rafael climbed the outside stair to the bookbinder's shop on Tanner's Row, chest strapped to their back under a fish-crate lid, heart going like a ferry engine.

Mirela opened the door before the second knock. She looked at Rafael, at the crate, at Rafael's face, and stepped aside without a word.

"He came last night," Rafael said, setting the crate down among the presses and the smell of glue and leather. "Passport, debt forgiven, a new life south. All I have to do is hand him the past and promise to forget it exists."

Mirela untied the crate lid with steady fingers. "And?"

"And I spent all night trying to talk myself into it, which is how I knew I wouldn't." Rafael laughed, short and unsteady. "Do you know what the worst part is? He was reasonable. He was kind, almost. That's how he beat me the first time, too. Nobody ever beats you with cruelty, Mirela. They beat you with paperwork."

She looked up at him then, and something passed between them that neither of them would have been able to name under oath — recognition, maybe, or alliance, or the beginning of a thing that would take a mountain and a snow cave and several hundred miles of rope to finish becoming what it was going to become.

"So we don't give it to him," she said. "Which means we run. Which means the passes."

"Which means the passes," Rafael agreed grimly. "Before the snows. With a party I haven't hired, money I don't have, and a ferryman holding my debt like a knife at my throat."

"Then you'd better start recruiting," Mirela said, and reached for her coat.

Below in the lane, unnoticed by either of them, one of Corven's watchers peeled away from the shadow of the tannery wall and walked, at an unhurried pace, toward the magistrate's house — carrying word that the ghost had chosen to become expensive after all.
