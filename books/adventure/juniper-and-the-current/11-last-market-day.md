## 11. Last Market Day

The last market day of the season smelled of woodsmoke and bruised apples, and Mirela Vance had promised herself she would not spend it thinking about Rafael Thackeray.

She lasted until mid-morning.

Her stall at the far end of the square was the smallest one—the bookbinder's corner, wedged between a woman who sold pickled everything and a man whose entire inventory appeared to be rope. Business was slow in the way autumn business always was: a farmer wanting a ledger rebound, a child pressing her nose against a display of marbled paper until her mother dragged her away. Mirela worked the glue brush in slow strokes along a spine and let the town wash over her. Juniper's Landing on market day was a river of its own—bargaining upstream, gossip downstream, everyone eddying around the magistrate's notice board at the center of the square without appearing to look at it.

She had looked at it. Of course she had. The new notices were printed in that particular bureaucratic gray ink that meant *we are watching*, and there were more of them this week than last. She'd counted four. Then five.

"You're going to ruin that spine," said the pickle woman, nodding at Mirela's hands. "You've brushed that same seam six times."

"It's a difficult seam."

"It's a straight line, girl."

Mirela looked down. The seam was, in fact, a straight line, already glued to perfection, and her brush was simply moving back and forth across it like a small anxious animal pacing a cage. She set the brush down.

"I'm closing early," she announced, to no one in particular.

"Because of the wine," the pickle woman said knowingly.

"There's no wine."

"There's always wine on last market day. Everyone buys the cheap stuff because the good stuff's gone by noon, and then everyone pretends they meant to buy the cheap stuff." The woman handed her a jar of pickled quinces as if levying a tax. "For your walk. You look like you need salt."

Mirela took the jar because refusing Olena's gifts was a two-hour negotiation, and folded her awning.

* * *

She found him at the ferry landing, which surprised her not at all. Rafael Thackeray had a way of positioning himself near water the way other people positioned themselves near exits. He was crouched at the edge of the boards, coiling rope with the absent precision of a man whose hands needed work the way lungs needed air, and Tam was beside him chattering about something—a knot, from the gestures, some knot of great historical importance—and Rafael was giving the boy exactly one nod per minute, which Tam seemed to experience as rapt attention.

Mirela stood at the top of the ramp for a moment, quince jar under her arm, and watched them. There was something in the picture that hurt gently, like pressing a bruise on purpose. The disgraced guide and the boy who worshipped him, the river running brown and low beneath the pilings, the mountains behind it all wearing their first serious expression of the season. Snowline had come down overnight. She could see it from here, a white hem drawn tight around the peaks, higher than it would be next week.

Rafael looked up before she made a sound. They always did that to each other now—that uncanny awareness, two animals who had learned the same forest. Something crossed his face that he didn't quite manage to file away in time. It wasn't surprise. It was closer to relief, and that frightened her more than any of the notices on the board.

"Mistress Vance," he said, formal as ever, as though they hadn't spent three evenings bent over a decoded conspiracy together.

"Guide Thackeray." She came down the ramp. "Tam."

"Miss Mirela," Tam said, glowing. He glowed at everyone; it was his gift. "Did you know there are eleven ways to tie a bowline but only one that holds when it's iced?"

"I did not know that."

"Neither did I until Tuesday." He beamed, gathered an armful of rope, and then performed the world's least subtle exit, announcing that Osric wanted the skiff bailed, immediately, urgently, right away, and vanishing up the landing before either of them could respond.

"He's getting better at that," Rafael said.

"At what?"

"Leaving." A pause. "He used to just stand there."

Mirela sat down on the edge of the dock, boots hanging over the water, and patted the boards beside her. Rafael hesitated the way he always hesitated—as if sitting near another person were a route with questionable anchors—and then sat, leaving a careful handspan of space between them that she noticed and he pretended didn't exist.

She produced the quince jar. "Taxation," she explained. "From Olena. She believes I need salt."

"She's probably right. You eat like a scholar."

"I *am* a scholar."

"You eat like a scholar who's being pursued." He took the jar anyway, worked the lid off with his thumb, and held it out. Their fingers touched over the brine, briefly, and both of them became extremely interested in the quinces.

Below them the Serrano moved slow and shallow, the bones of the summer showing—sandbars that hadn't been visible since spring, a drowned cart wheel, the ribs of some old boat nobody claimed. Low water season. The season that gave things up. She thought of the chest, iron-heavy and seal-stamped, sitting wrapped in oilcloth under her floorboards like a sleeping animal, and made herself think instead about the wine.

"There's a stall," she said, "by the tannery. The man waters his wine and lies about the vintage and charges nothing, because he likes company more than coin. On last market day he sets out benches."

"That sounds terrible."

"It's the best thing in Juniper's Landing."

Rafael looked at her sideways. "You realize we shouldn't be seen together. Corven has watchers—I counted two on the bridge this morning alone, and one of them has been following me since Tuesday and still hasn't learned to vary his route."

"Then we'll sit where the light is bad and talk about rope." She stood, brushing off her skirt. "Come on, guide. The passes close soon. The permits expire. The whole world is on a deadline." She offered her hand, half-joking, entirely not. "We can afford one afternoon of pretending otherwise."

He looked at her hand for a long moment. Then he took it—briefly, firmly, like a man crossing a log bridge—and let go too fast, and they walked up into the market noise side by side, not touching, closer than touching.

* * *

The wine was, as promised, terrible.

It was also warm, in the way of things left in the sun, and it came in clay cups that tasted faintly of the previous decade, and the tanner's bench was squeezed into a wedge of late light between buildings where the wind couldn't find them. The vintner—fat, cheerful, deaf in one ear—greeted Mirela by name and Rafael by a long appraising stare followed by the announcement that guides drank free if they told stories, and Rafael said he didn't tell stories anymore, and the vintner said perfect, those were the best kind, and poured him a cup anyway.

They talked about nothing for a while. Rope, as promised. The price of lamp oil. Whether Olena pickled things that had no business being pickled (they established that she did; the quinces were actually one of her better efforts). Tam's eleven bowlines. The particular comedy of Juniper's Landing weather, which couldn't decide between autumn and winter and so delivered both simultaneously, sun on one shoulder and sleet on the other.

Then the bench emptied around them—the vintner called away by a customer, the other market idlers drifting home—and the wedge of light went gold and long, and the conversation went quiet in the specific way that meant something was coming.

"You never told me," Mirela said, turning her cup slowly, "how you learned Vessian."

Rafael's shoulders did the small tightening they did whenever Vessia entered a room. "I told you. Worked the eastern routes."

"The eastern routes are in Vessia, yes. But you speak it like someone who loved there, not worked there. There's a difference. The vowels soften." She glanced at him. "You say *good evening* in Vessian like an apology."

That startled a laugh out of him—short, rusty, real. "Does it?"

"Every time."

He was quiet for a while. A cart rattled past the mouth of the alley; somewhere a dog was losing an argument with a goose. When he spoke again, his voice had dropped into the register he used for true things.

"Her name was Anka. She ran a waystation on the high trail, third switchback above the tree line. Widow of a shepherd. I guided through her valley maybe forty times over six years, and every time I swore I'd stop staying the night, and every time I stayed the night." He turned the cup. "She taught me the language badly and the mountains well. She used to say I had a compass for a heart—always pointing somewhere else. North, or money, or the next contract. Never at whatever was in front of me."

"And was she right?"

"Yes." No flinch, no deflection. That was the thing about Rafael that kept undoing her: he met his own failures head-on, like inspecting a wound. "She was right for years. And then—" His jaw worked. "Then the expedition. And after, when my name was mud in three valleys, I understood that the compass had finally pointed somewhere and I'd been looking the wrong direction the whole time."

Mirela felt the old cold move through her chest, the annexation cold, the cold of names read off lists. "I had a husband," she said, because he had given her a true thing and the ledger demanded balance. "Tomas. A printer. In Vessa City, before." She smiled at the middle distance. "He inked everything. His hands were permanently black. He'd touch my face and leave prints, and I'd wear them all day like jewelry."

"What happened to him?"

"The same thing that happened to the country. Slowly, and then all at once." She drank. The wine had gone bitter and she barely noticed. "When the annexation decrees came, printers were the first rounded up—you can forge a map, but you can't forge a printing press out of existence, so they burn the presses and take the men. He got a letter out through a shepherd. One sentence. *Bind the truth well.*" Her thumb traced the rim of the cup. "So I became a bookbinder. Everyone thinks it's a trade. It's a vow."

Rafael was looking at her with an expression she couldn't name and didn't want to interrupt. Not pity—he'd had enough pity aimed at himself to know its uselessness. Something more like recognition. Two people standing at the same grave from different sides.

"Bind the truth well," he repeated softly.

"That's why the chest matters," she said. "Not the politics—or yes, the politics, the permits, the theft, all of it. But underneath. Someone hid those documents where only a guide would find them. Someone bound the truth and dropped it in the current and trusted the river." She looked up at him. "And the river sent it to you. Of all the people on this stretch of water. I don't believe in much anymore, Rafael, but I believe the river knew what it was doing."

"Don't," he said quietly. "Don't make it mean something. Meaning gets people killed on my routes."

"Everything means something. That's what gets people killed. The ones who pretend it doesn't die just as dead, only confused."

He huffed—not quite a laugh, not quite surrender—and refilled both cups from the vintner's abandoned jug. The light had gone amber to copper. Above the rooftops, the mountains had put on their evening alpenglow, pink and merciless, and Mirela watched Rafael watch them with the look of a man reading a letter he both longs for and dreads.

"Eleven days," he said. "Osric's deadline. Nine, now. And the snow's coming down the col faster than the almanac said it would. Everything's converging." He shook his head. "I spent two years being no one. It was restful. I recommend it."

"You were never no one. You were someone hiding very well. There's a difference, and the difference is exhausting."

"Is it exhausting for you? Hiding?"

She considered. "Less, lately." Which was as close as either of them had come to saying it, and they both heard how close it was, and both looked away with the synchronized guilt of teenagers.

A silence settled—but a different quality of silence than before. Warmer. Banked, like a fire tended down for the night, holding its heat in the coals. Mirela found herself cataloguing small things with an urgency that alarmed her: the scar splitting his left eyebrow; the way he held his cup by the rim, delicately, big hands making allowances; the fact that he laughed twice tonight, which was, by her count, a seasonal record.

"Ask me," she said suddenly.

"Ask you what?"

"Whatever you keep not asking. You have a question. You've had it since the night I decoded the second grant. It sits behind your eyes like a stone in a boot."

Rafael's ears went faintly red, which she noted with enormous satisfaction. He stared into his cup as if the vintage might advise him.

"Why me?" he said finally. "The chest surfaced, and you could have taken it to the exile council yourself. You know the network. You know the ciphers better than I do. Instead you tied your fate to a blacklisted guide with debts and a magistrate's mark on him. Why?"

It was a fair question and she made herself answer it honestly, which cost more than she expected.

"Because when you opened that chest and saw the seal, you didn't look excited. You looked like a man at a funeral." She set her cup down. "Everyone else in this town who wants those documents wants them as a weapon. The council wants leverage. Corven wants ash. But you—" She searched for it. "You looked like someone who'd been waiting twenty years for permission to grieve properly. I trusted that. Grief that honest doesn't lie."

He said nothing for a long time. The goose outside won, decisively.

"My mother used to say," he managed at last, voice rough, "that the mountain gives back what you carry up it. Weighted. I'm starting to think rivers do the same."

"There you go. Meaning something."

"Don't push it."

But he was almost smiling, and she was almost smiling, and the space between them on the bench had shrunk by a handspan neither of them would admit to closing. The copper light lay across the boards like spilled wine. Somewhere beyond the rooftops the first stars were pricking through, and the snowline on the peaks had turned silver, and Mirela thought: *remember this. Bank this warmth. There is cold coming that will need it.*

She reached over and, very deliberately, took a sip from his cup instead of her own.

Rafael went absolutely still, staring at her.

"What?" she said, the picture of innocence. "Yours tastes better."

"It's the same jug."

"Is it?"

"It is literally the same jug, Mirela."

"Hm," she said, and did it again, and Rafael Thackeray—disgraced guide, keeper of graves, man with a compass for a heart—made a sound suspiciously close to a laugh and covered it with a cough, and neither of them named what was taking root between them, because naming things makes them real, and real things can be lost, and they had each lost enough for one lifetime.

The light failed. The vintner returned, saw them, grinned enormously, and brought a second jug unasked, saying the first was clearly insufficient for such serious rope discussion.

* * *

They walked back through the emptying square as the lamps were lit, one by one, little islands of flame in the blue dusk. At the corner where their routes divided—her street toward the bindery, his toward the landing—Rafael stopped.

"Nine days," he said.

"Nine days."

"If we cross, it'll be ugly. Weather, terrain, Corven. Possibly worse than ugly." He was looking at the mountains, not at her, which was how he said the hardest things. "I want you to know I've done the arithmetic. Every version of it. And I keep arriving at the same sum, which is that some things are worth carrying even if the carrying breaks you."

"I know," she said. "I've done the same arithmetic."

"We should compare figures sometime."

"Over better wine."

"Over any wine."

He nodded once, awkward, and turned toward the water—and then stopped, and turned back, and for one suspended moment Mirela was certain he was going to close the distance, and found she was not afraid of it, which frightened her more than anything Corven could arrange.

Instead he said, "Your face," and touched his own cheek. "You've got—"

"Ink," she said. "Always ink. Tomas used to say—"

She stopped. Rafael waited, patient as bedrock.

"Tomas used to say it meant I'd been working at something true," she finished, quieter.

Rafael looked at her the way he looked at a route he'd decided to climb despite every warning. "Then you've been working at something true a long time," he said, and walked down toward the river, and she stood in the lamplight watching him go, hand pressed to her cheek, wearing the mark like jewelry.

Behind her, at the edge of the square, a figure detached itself from the shadow of the notice board and started after him—unhurried, keeping to the dark side of the street, varying his route for the first time all week.

Mirela watched him go, and the banked warmth in her chest cooled by one degree, and she began, quietly, to count the watchers between here and the water.
