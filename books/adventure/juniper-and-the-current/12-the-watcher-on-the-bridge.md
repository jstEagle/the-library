## 12. The Watcher on the Bridge

Tam Voss had three talents in this world, and he'd catalogued them honestly by the age of nineteen. He could read water better than any ferry-hand on the Serrano—could tell you where the current ran deep and where it lied, just by the way the light broke on the surface. He could tie eleven kinds of knots blindfolded, a skill he'd learned from Osric and practiced on everything from mooring lines to the drawstrings of his own trousers, which had once resulted in an afternoon of considerable embarrassment. And he could idolize Rafael Thackeray with a completeness that his grandmother said would ruin him.

"You put that man on too high a shelf," she'd told him a dozen times, "and all you'll ever see is his boots."

But Tam had seen Rafael pull a drowning boy out of the spring flood two years back, had seen him wade into water that was eating the pilings whole, and had decided that some shelves were earned. So when Rafael Thackeray himself came to the ferry landing and asked—quietly, in that voice like gravel poured slow—if Tam wanted work on a crossing party, Tam said yes before Rafael finished the sentence. Said it so fast he nearly bit his tongue.

He hadn't asked what the work was. He still didn't fully know. There was a chest involved; there was Mirela Vance, the bookbinder with the careful hands and the eyes that measured you like you were a page she might have to mend; and there was Osric, who was coming along to settle a debt and complained about it the way other men complained about weather, which is to say constantly and without any real conviction.

They were leaving on the morning tide, such as it was—the Serrano's tide being more of a suggestion—and Tam spent the last evening checking gear with the seriousness of a priest preparing an altar. Ropes coiled. Provisions wrapped in oilcloth. The chest itself, which he was not allowed to touch but was allowed to look at, which felt like being invited to stare at a fire without warming your hands.

It was while he was carrying the last of the rope down to the landing at dusk that he saw the man on the bridge.

* * *

The bridge over the Serrano at Juniper's Landing was old enough that everyone had stopped noticing it, the way you stop noticing a scar. It had a humped back and a railing of iron gone soft with rust, and in the evenings the lamplighter's boy lit the lamp at its center, which threw a circle of orange light about as reliable as a promise from a magistrate.

The man stood just outside that circle.

Tam noticed him the way he noticed things on the river—not with his eyes first, but with something under his ribs. The man wasn't doing anything. That was the thing. Nobody stood on the bridge at dusk doing nothing. Fishermen fidgeted. Lovers leaned. Old men spat and commented on the weather to anyone within shouting distance. This man simply stood, hands folded behind his back, facing the landing, and his stillness had a quality Tam recognized from watching herons: the absolute patience of something waiting for fish.

Tam set down the rope coil slowly, as if his arms had suddenly remembered they were tired, and took up a position by the mooring post where he could watch without appearing to watch. He was not good at appearing not to watch. His grandmother said his face was a public notice board. But the lamplight was against the stranger, and dusk was thickening, and Tam did his best.

The man wore a gray coat, good wool, cut plain. Plain was the tell. Everything about him was arranged to be unremarkable, and unremarkable, done deliberately, takes effort. His boots were city boots. His hat sat at the angle of a man who'd been told how to look casual by someone else.

A watcher. The word arrived in Tam's head fully formed, and with it a coldness that had nothing to do with the evening air.

He thought: *Corven.* He didn't know why he thought it—he knew of Magistrate Corven the way everyone in Juniper's Landing knew of him, the way you know of a hole in the road you've learned to step around—but the thought came anyway, complete and certain, and settled in his stomach like a swallowed stone.

He should tell Rafael. Of course he should tell Rafael. Rafael would know what to do; Rafael always knew what to do; that was the entire architecture of Tam's admiration.

But then a second thought arrived, and this one was worse: *What if I'm wrong? What if I go running to Rafael Thackeray, hero of the spring flood, and say 'there's a man standing on a bridge,' and Rafael looks, and the man is just a man, and I've made myself into the fool of the expedition before we've even left?*

Tam Voss had been the fool before. He knew the shape of it.

So he did what seemed, in the moment, like cleverness. He decided to handle it himself.

* * *

The plan, such as it was, formed over the course of an hour of intense internal debate conducted mostly through pacing. The problem: the watcher was positioned between the landing and the bridge, which was the only road out of town heading north, and the party meant to leave at dawn. If the watcher reported their departure—and watchers report departures, that was surely the entire point of watching—then whoever sent him would know, and whatever they were carrying across the range would be known too, and Tam understood without being told that the chest was a thing that must not be known.

The solution, as Tam conceived it around midnight, lying in his bunk staring at the rafters: leave early. Leave now. Leave in the dark, before the watcher's shift ended or his supper arrived or whatever watchers did when watchers were relieved, and be gone upriver before the fish-heron had anything to report.

It was, in retrospect, a plan with the structural integrity of a knot tied by someone showing off.

He woke Rafael an hour before dawn, and Rafael came awake the way Tam imagined bears came awake—all at once, no grogginess, eyes open and level in the dark.

"Bridge," Tam whispered. "There's a man. He's been watching the landing since dusk. Gray coat. City boots."

Rafael was silent for a long moment. Then: "You watched him?"

"From the mooring post. Three hours." Tam hesitated. "Mostly."

"Mostly."

"I got cold. I went in for tea once. Twice."

Something that might have been a smile moved in the dark. "Three hours, minus tea. Go wake the others. Quietly. We cross the footbridge downstream and pick up the drover's path on the far side. No one uses the main bridge."

Which was exactly right, exactly sensible, and would have worked perfectly if Tam had simply done as he was told.

* * *

Here is what happened, and Tam would replay it many times in the weeks that followed, usually at night, usually wincing.

They moved out in the blue-black hour before dawn, single file, quiet as a debt. Osric grumbled—Osric considered pre-dawn a personal insult—but even he kept his voice down. They crossed the footbridge downstream, planks wet and singing softly under their boots, and came up onto the drover's path on the north bank, which wound away from the river through alder scrub toward the high country.

And it was there, on the drover's path, that Tam looked back—one glance, just to confirm they'd slipped the net—and saw the gray coat moving.

Not on the bridge. On the path. Behind them. Half a mile back, maybe less, walking with that same patient heron-stillness translated into motion, and beside him, resolving out of the pre-dawn murk, a second figure.

Two of them. There had been two of them, or the one had called to another, and either way the arithmetic was suddenly terrible: the watcher hadn't been watching the landing. He'd been watching the *footbridge*. Or both. Or he was simply better at this than Tam, which Tam was beginning to suspect was true of most of the world.

Panic is a strange animal. It doesn't roar; it whispers, and what it whispered to Tam was: *Fix it. You found them. Fix it yourself. Don't make Rafael look at you with disappointment in front of everyone.*

So Tam did the worst possible thing, which was the thing that felt most like help. Without a word to anyone, he took the lead.

"Drover's path forks ahead," he said, loud enough for all of them, casual as Sunday. "Left fork's flooded this time of year—goes down to the millrace. We want the right."

The left fork was not flooded. The left fork was, in fact, the correct fork, the one that climbed steadily along the river toward the gorge mouth. The right fork—the one Tam steered them onto with the confidence of a born liar, which perhaps he was—was the old shepherd's track, a wandering, switchbacking goat-path that meandered westward into the hills, lost elevation twice, crossed the same stream three times out of what appeared to be sheer indecision, and eventually rejoined the drover's path several hours later, having accomplished nothing except distance from where anyone wanted to be.

Tam knew this because every ferry-hand knew the hill tracks; you learned them the way you learned the river's moods, in idle seasons, out of boredom. He also knew—or told himself he knew—that the shepherd's track was rough going, that no rider would bother following it, that it would shake off the gray coats like a dog shaking off rain.

For two hours it worked. For two glorious hours, Tam listened to Osric complain about the grade ("Who built this path, a drunk goat?"), watched Mirela navigate the stream crossings with her skirts knotted up and her face set in an expression of profound editorial disapproval, and felt the warm glow of a secret well-kept. Behind them, presumably, the watchers hit the fork and took the sensible left, and were now miles away and falling further.

Then Rafael stopped.

Rafael had been quiet the whole climb, which wasn't unusual, but the quality of the quiet had changed. Tam had learned to read Rafael's silences the way he read water, and this silence had an undertow.

"This track," Rafael said mildly, "doesn't go to the gorge."

"It—it comes around," Tam said. "Rejoins the drover's path past the second ridge. It's just—rougher. Safer. No riders."

"No riders," Rafael agreed. He turned, and his eyes were not disappointed, which was somehow infinitely worse; they were *interested*, the way a guide is interested in a client who has just lied about their climbing experience. "Tam. Why are we on the shepherd's track?"

The question landed in the middle of the group like a dropped stone. Osric stopped complaining mid-syllable. Mirela looked from Tam to Rafael with the quick attention of someone reading a page where the margins had suddenly filled with writing.

"I told you," Tam said. "The left fork—"

"The left fork is dry as a sermon in August. I walked it Tuesday." Rafael crouched, touched the ground, read it like print. "And here—" he pointed back down the track, at a patch of soft earth where the path narrowed between stones, "—here's a boot print. City boot. Fresh. Someone's been on this track ahead of us since before dawn, and there's only one reason a man in city boots walks a shepherd's track at four in the morning."

The cold came back into Tam's stomach, colder than before, because he understood it all at once, whole: the watcher hadn't followed them. The watcher had *anticipated* them. Had known the footbridge, known the paths, known that anyone slipping out of Juniper's Landing quietly would take the hills—and had posted himself accordingly, or had a partner posted accordingly, and Tam's clever detour had delivered them directly onto ground the enemy already held.

"They're ahead of us?" Mirela said quietly.

"Were. Could be. Could be doubling back." Rafael straightened. He didn't raise his voice; he never raised his voice; but the whole hillside seemed to lean in. "We get off this track. Now. Down through the alder, cross the stream low, and we're in dead ground until the gorge mouth. Move."

They moved. It was not dignified. Osric swore with the fluency of a lifelong practitioner; Mirela clutched the satchel with the documents the way a mother clutches a child in a crowd; Tam brought up the rear, burning with a shame so hot it felt like sunburn, crashing through alder brush and hating himself with a thoroughness that at least had the virtue of being familiar.

They crossed the stream three more times—the stream again, the indecisive stream, mocking him—and pushed through a stand of birch, and came out at last on a shelf of rock above the river where Rafael finally called a halt.

No one spoke for a moment. Below, the Serrano ran silver and indifferent. Above, the shepherd's track was empty in the morning light, and no gray coat appeared on it, and no hoofbeat sounded, and gradually the party's breathing slowed.

"We lost them," Mirela said at last. "Or they let us think we did."

"Both, probably," Rafael said. He looked at Tam then, fully, and Tam braced for it—for the dismissal, the anger, the boots-on-the-shelf moment when the idol looked down and saw the smallness underneath.

Instead Rafael said: "You saw the watcher on the bridge. Before anyone else. Before me."

Tam blinked. "I—yes. But then I—"

"You bought us a night's start and cost us half a morning, and you did both alone, without telling anyone, because you thought it was your job to carry it." Rafael's voice was gravel poured slow. "That's the same mistake that gets clients killed, Tam. Not stupidity. Carrying alone what should be shared." A pause. "Next time, you wake me. Even if you think you're wrong. *Especially* if you think you're wrong."

"Yes," Tam said, and his voice cracked on it, and he didn't care.

"And for what it's worth," Osric offered, settling onto a rock with the sigh of a man taking his rightful throne, "that was the worst lie I've heard since my nephew swore the ferry sank itself. 'Flooded,' he says. In August. Boy, if you're going to deceive a mountain guide, at least invent a flood with some ambition."

"Noted," Tam said miserably.

Mirela sat down near him, close enough that he caught the smell of glue and paper that always hung about her, and said, not unkindly: "You were trying to protect us."

"I was trying not to look like a fool."

"Mm." She looked out at the river. "Those are often the same attempt. Only one of them works."

* * *

They made the gorge mouth by late afternoon, hours behind schedule, and camped in the lee of the first great walls where the river went narrow and loud. Tam took first watch, partly because it was his turn and mostly because he needed to be alone with his humiliation somewhere with a view.

The consequence came at dusk, and it came quietly, the way consequences do.

Rafael sat down beside him on the watch stone, handed him half of a flatbread, and said nothing for a long time. The gorge darkened around them. Somewhere below, the water talked to itself.

"There'll be more of them," Rafael said finally. "Watchers. From here on, we assume we're seen. Every choice we make, we make knowing someone may be reading it." He tore his own bread. "That's what your detour cost. Not the hours. The hours I can walk off. But they know our direction now, roughly, and they know we spooked, and a spooked party makes mistakes."

"I know."

"Do you?" Rafael glanced at him, and there was no cruelty in it, only the flat honesty of the mountains. "You will. Out here, mistakes teach faster than guides do." He chewed, swallowed. "But here's the other thing, and mark it, because it matters more. You spotted that man on the bridge when nobody else did. Your eyes are good, Tam. Better than mine were at your age. The trick—the whole trick—is learning to trust your eyes and distrust your conclusions. Both at once. It's uncomfortable. Like rowing against your own current."

Tam turned that over. "Trust the eyes. Distrust the conclusions."

"And tell somebody," Rafael said. "Always tell somebody. The mountain doesn't forgive secrets, and neither does Corven."

The name landed between them, plain and unhidden, and Tam felt a strange relief at hearing it spoken aloud—as if a splinter long buried had finally been acknowledged.

Below them, far back down the valley, where the river bent toward Juniper's Landing, a light flickered in the gathering dark. Small. Orange. Steady. Too far to be a farmhouse; wrong place for a shepherd; exactly where a man in a city coat would build a fire if he wanted to see who moved on the gorge road by night.

Tam watched it, and trusted his eyes, and this time he opened his mouth.

"Rafael," he said. "Fire. Down-valley. On the bend."

Rafael rose without hurry and looked, and was silent a moment, and then said, in a voice that had gone very quiet indeed:

"Well. They're not chasing us." He turned back toward the camp, where Mirela was already standing, the satchel clutched to her chest, her face pale in the firelight. "They're herding us. Up the gorge. Exactly where they want us to go."

Behind them, high above the trailhead, a loose stone clicked in the dark—and did not fall.
