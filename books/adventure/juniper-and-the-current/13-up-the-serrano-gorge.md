## 13. Up the Serrano Gorge

The gorge did not care that they were late.

Rafael had told them this at the ferry landing, and again at the treeline, and once more at the mouth of the Serrano Gorge itself, where the river they'd followed for two days vanished into a slot of granite so narrow the sky became a rumor. The gorge did not care about permits, or magistrates, or the four days of margin Mirela kept recalculating on her fingers like a woman counting coins she didn't have.

"The gorge," Tam had said, "cares about nothing?"

"The gorge cares about gravity," Rafael said. "That's the whole of its personality."

Now, three hours in, Rafael felt something uncoil in their chest that they had not felt in two years and had privately assumed was gone forever. It was not happiness, exactly. It was more precise than happiness. It was the feeling of a problem presenting itself in clean language—*loose flake left of the waterline, step right, test before weight*—and knowing, without doubt or theater, what to do about it.

Their hands remembered. That was the miracle of it. Two years of hauling ferry rope and mending nets, and their hands still read stone like a first language.

"Left side's rotten," Rafael called back. "Stay on the dry line. If it looks wet, it isn't your route."

"How can you tell from here?" Osric asked. He was bringing up the rear with the chest strapped to his pack frame, and he was doing it without complaint, which Rafael had learned over thirty years of mountains was the truest measure of a man—not how he climbed, but how he carried.

"Color. Sound. Smell, if you're desperate." Rafael tapped a boulder with one knuckle. It rang dull, like a struck pot with a crack in it. "This one's singing flat. Nobody touches it."

Tam touched it.

Not out of defiance—the boy simply hadn't been listening, because he'd been watching Rafael with the open, unguarded admiration of a dog watching someone throw sticks, and dogs do not take notes. His boot scuffed the flat-singing boulder, and the boulder answered by shedding a piece of itself the size of a loaf of bread down into the water below.

"Sorry," Tam said. "Sorry. Sorry."

"Don't be sorry," Rafael said. "Be *here*. Eyes on the rock, not on me. I'm not going anywhere interesting."

"That's debatable," Mirela said quietly, behind him, and Rafael chose—with real discipline—to let it pass unanswered, mostly because answering would have required a face, and their face was currently busy.

* * *

They camped that first night on a gravel shelf above the roar, in the lee of a fallen slab that made a windbreak shaped like a cupped hand. Rafael got the fire small and hot, the way you do when wood is scarce and smoke is visible, and cooked the dried fish Osric had salted himself, which was, Osric announced, "the finest catch the Serrano has produced this year," a claim he made every year regardless of the truth.

"It's brine with ambitions," Mirela said, chewing anyway.

"It's *tradition*," Osric said.

Rafael watched the party settle around the fire and noticed the thing they had been afraid to hope for: the edges were softening. Tam sat closer than he needed to. Osric had stopped checking the sky every time a bird crossed it. Even Mirela, who trusted people the way cats trust furniture—useful, but never entirely—had arranged her bedroll within arm's reach of the group instead of apart from it.

Small distances. But in Rafael's experience, trust never arrived all at once. It came the way dawn came: imperceptibly, then suddenly, then all at once obvious.

"You've done this crossing before," Mirela said. Not a question.

"Twice. Once as guide, once as—" Rafael turned the fish over. "Once running."

"And the gorge?"

"The gorge is honest. It tells you everything up front. It's the easy ground above it that lies."

Osric snorted. "You always talked like a funeral pamphlet. Even young. Remember, Tam? No, of course you don't, you weren't born." He pointed his fish at Rafael. "This one gave safety lectures to paying clients like a priest giving last rites. 'The mountain forgives nothing.' 'Respect the stone.' Half of them wanted their money back."

"And yet here you are," Rafael said, "paying with labor instead."

"Here I am," Osric said, and there was something under the joke when he said it, some sediment of feeling that settled too fast for Rafael to read. Rafael marked it and let it go. Debt made men strange; Rafael knew that better than most.

Tam, emboldened by the firelight, asked the question Rafael had been waiting two days for him to ask.

"What happened? Before. When they took your license."

The fire popped. Somewhere above them, water dripped off stone with the patience of a clock.

"A client died," Rafael said. "On the Vessian side of the range. On my route. They said I took a line I shouldn't have taken." They fed a stick to the flames. "I don't remember taking it. That's either the truth or the excuse, depending on who's telling it."

"Do *you* think you did?" Mirela asked. She asked it plainly, without cruelty, the way she seemed to do everything—as if gentleness were simply the efficient form of speech.

"I think I'll know when I remember why," Rafael said. "And until then, I'm going to get us over this range, because the documents matter more than my name does."

It was more honesty than Rafael usually spent in a month. Tam looked stricken, as though he'd broken something. Osric looked at his boots. And Mirela looked at Rafael for a long moment with an expression Rafael couldn't file anywhere, and then she said:

"Then we should sleep. Tomorrow's the narrows."

* * *

The narrows earned the name.

By midmorning of the second day the walls closed to less than an arm's span in places, and the party moved single-file along ledges the width of a coffin lid, the river thundering forty feet below with enough force to make conversation impossible and falling fatal. Rafael went first, calling holds back over the noise in a shorthand they'd drilled at breakfast: *high, low, reach, trust, rest.*

Trust meant exactly what it sounded like—a hold that looked wrong but held, that you simply had to commit to. Tam hated trust-holds. Rafael could hear him breathing through each one, quick and shallow, talking himself onto the rock.

"Good," Rafael called back each time. "Again."

Praise, Rafael had learned long ago, worked on frightened climbers better than instruction. Instruction gave the fear something to argue with. Praise starved it.

By noon Rafael had stopped thinking of them as four people and started thinking of them as a rope team, which was the highest compliment Rafael's mind paid to anyone. Osric moved like a barge—slow, unstoppable, mildly offended by the terrain but never stopped by it. Mirela climbed the way she did everything else, with a bookbinder's economy, no wasted motion, her eyes always finding the next hold before her hands needed it. And Tam—

Tam was going to be good. Not today, maybe not this season, but the boy had balance in his ankles and courage in his gut and only his head left to train.

Which is why, when Rafael heard the sound, they knew before they turned around whose luck had run out.

It began as a click. A small, domestic sound, like a knuckle cracking, somewhere high on the south wall. Then another click. Then a sound like the mountain clearing its throat.

"*Rockfall!*" Rafael shouted, and the word tore up the gorge ahead of them. "*Under the overhang—MOVE!*"

They scattered. There was no art to it. Rafael pressed into a shallow alcove and pulled Mirela in after them by the strap of her pack, and the world became noise—a long, rolling avalanche of smaller sounds, stones chattering down the walls, bouncing, gathering authority, the deep bass note of the big ones arriving underneath like drums in another room.

Dust swallowed everything. The river's roar came back slowly, as though the gorge had to remember how to make sound.

"Mirela?" Rafael said into the grey.

"Here." Coughing. "Here."

"Osric!"

"Barging!" A wheeze. "Still barging!"

"Tam?"

Silence.

Rafael's heart performed a maneuver that no anatomy text would endorse. They shoved out of the alcove into the settling dust, blinking, hands out, scanning the ledge—and found him ten feet on, wedged into a crease in the wall where he'd thrown himself flat, dust-white from crown to boot, both arms wrapped around his head.

He was shaking. But he was whole.

"Tam." Rafael crouched, put two fingers to the boy's wrist without asking, found a pulse hammering like a trapped bird. "Look at me. Are you hurt?"

"I—" Tam's voice came out an octave high. He tried again. "I moved. You said move and I moved."

"You moved." Rafael checked him anyway, hands quick and impersonal—wrists, collarbone, ribs, the sweep down the shins where boots hide damage. Nothing. Nothing but dust and terror. "You moved *fast*. That was the difference between a story and a funeral, and you chose the story. Do you understand me? That was exactly right."

"I thought—" Tam's breath hitched. "When it started, I thought about what you'd say. So I just—did it."

Something moved through Rafael's chest then, unwelcome and enormous, something that felt disgracefully like pride. They stood up quickly, before their face could betray them, and clapped dust off their palms with unnecessary violence.

"Well," they said gruffly. "Don't make a habit of nearly dying. It's bad for the schedule."

But Mirela had seen Rafael's face anyway. She saw everything. She was looking at Rafael over Tam's white-capped head with the ghost of a smile, and Rafael turned away and busied themselves with the rope coil as if it had personally insulted them.

* * *

They cleared the narrows an hour before dark and came out, abruptly, into space.

The gorge opened like a hand unclenching—a wide bowl of grey stone rising on three sides, the river spreading thin and bright across the floor, and above the western rim the first peaks of the high range standing against the evening in colors that had no business existing: rose, then copper, then a blue so deep it looked solid. Snowfields glowed up there, catching the last light and holding it like coals.

Nobody spoke for a full minute. Even Osric.

"There it is," Rafael said finally, and their own voice came out rougher than intended. "Thackeray Pass. Three days' hard walking. Four, if the weather argues."

"It's beautiful," Tam said.

"It's trying to kill you," Rafael agreed. "Both things are true. That's mountains for you."

They made camp on the gravel bar, and Rafael did the rounds the way they always had—checking knots they'd tied themselves, checking boots, checking the chest straps on Osric's frame, checking Mirela's ankle wrap because she'd rolled it slightly on a trust-hold and claimed, with a straight face, that it was "fine," a word Rafael had learned to translate as *not fine.*

At Tam's bedroll, the boy caught Rafael's sleeve.

"I keep thinking about the rockfall," he said. "How fast it happened. How there wasn't time to be scared until after." He looked up, and in the firelight his face was very young and very serious. "Is that what it was like? For you. The thing that happened before."

Rafael sat down on the gravel beside him. Above them the stars were coming out in the cold, thick as spilled salt.

"Worse," Rafael said. "Because afterward, there was plenty of time. Years of it. Fear's quick—it's the remembering that's slow." They squeezed the boy's shoulder once, briefly, because affection rationed properly lasts longer. "Sleep, Tam. You climbed well today. Better than well."

From the other side of the fire, Mirela's voice drifted over, soft and dry: "He's going to be insufferable now."

"He already is," Osric said. "It's tradition."

Tam grinned into his blanket, and Rafael banked the fire down to embers and lay back on the cold ground and listened to the river working the stones in the dark, patient as anything, moving everything it touched toward somewhere else.

Three days. The permits expired in nine. Corven's watchers were somewhere behind them, and Corven himself was somewhere beyond guessing, and the weather over Thackeray Pass had begun, that evening, to build a different kind of cloud—one with weight in it, one that sat on the peaks like a man sitting down to stay.

Rafael watched those clouds a long time before sleeping, and did not like the arithmetic they suggested.

Because in the morning, an hour's climb above camp, they would find the ruins—and Mirela would stop walking mid-stride, as though the ground itself had spoken her name.
