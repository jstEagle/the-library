## 14. The Burned Village

Mirela knew the village before she saw it.

She knew it the way you know a word in a language you were forbidden to speak—by shape, by weight, by the way it sat on the tongue. *Vesna Dol.* Spring Meadow, though there had never been much of a meadow and the spring flooded every year until the Vessian engineers built the stone culvert that still channeled water somewhere beneath the ruins, singing to itself in the dark like a fool who hadn't heard the news.

She stopped at the edge of the terraces and let the others catch up.

"What is it?" Tam asked, because Tam asked everything, bless him, the way a puppy asks everything, with his whole body.

"A village," Rafael said quietly, coming up beside her. He didn't say *a dead village* or *a ruin*. He said *a village*, present tense, and Mirela could have kissed him for the grammar alone.

"Vesna Dol," she said. "It was a weaving town. Wool from the high pastures came down these paths—" she pointed at the faint green seams where sheep tracks had once been roads "—and cloth went out. My grandmother bought Vesian Dol linen for her shop in Krajova. She said you could tell real Vesna Dol weave by holding it up to the light. There was a pattern in it that only showed when the sun was behind it."

"What pattern?" Tam asked.

"Water," Mirela said. "Running water. The weavers here put the river into everything they made."

The river was still running. That was the obscene part, she thought, as they picked their way down through the terraces. Twenty years since the annexation, since the soldiers came through in the autumn of the Occupation with their orders about *resettlement* and *security*, and the river under the village went on singing its idiot song as if nothing had happened. Water didn't hold grudges. Water didn't remember. That was its whole nature, and Mirela had spent twenty years envying it.

The houses were roofless but standing, which surprised her. She had expected rubble. Instead there were walls—good stone walls, mortared by hands that had known what they were doing—and doorways without doors, and window frames filled not with glass but with twenty years of weather. Fireweed grew out of a hearth. A rosebush, gone feral and enormous, had swallowed an entire corner of what must have been the weaver's hall, and it was blooming, absurdly, defiantly pink against all that gray.

"Somebody should tell the roses," Osric said behind her. His voice was carefully light. "They've clearly missed the memo about grief."

"Osric." Mirela didn't turn around. "Shut up."

A pause. "Fair," he said, and to his credit, he did.

* * *

Rafael chose the camp with the efficiency Mirela was learning to read like handwriting: the long low building at the village center, walls still shoulder-high on three sides, floor swept clean by wind, a corner deep enough to block the evening wind coming down off the glacier. A guide's eye. She watched him walk the perimeter, testing stones with his boot, checking sightlines, and thought: *this is what he was, before.* Not the disgraced ghost who pulled chests from rivers and flinched at his own name. This. Someone whose competence was a kind of care.

"Fire in the far corner," he said. "Smoke will draft up through the broken roof. Tam, wood—but only deadfall, nothing from the structures."

"Even the burned parts?" Tam asked.

"*Especially* the burned parts," Rafael said. "We're guests."

Mirela looked at him. "You know what this place is."

"I know what this place is," Rafael said. "I crossed through it once, years ago, before—" He stopped. Recalibrated. She was learning his recalibrations too, the small pauses where a whole history got folded up and put away. "Before. The guild route used to pass through here. Weavers sold tea to passing parties. There was a woman who kept bees."

"Mother Anka," Mirela said.

Rafael blinked. "You know her?"

"Everyone knew Mother Anka's honey. It was famous in three valleys." Mirela sat down slowly on a fallen lintel, the stone cold even through her coat. "My mother traded with her twice a year. I came with her when I was nine. Anka gave me a piece of comb straight from the hive and told me if I ate it facing east I'd never be homesick." Her mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "I ate it facing west by accident. I've been homesick ever since. Clearly my own fault."

Tam laughed before he caught himself, then looked stricken, then looked at the ground. Mirela reached over and tugged his sleeve.

"No," she said. "Laugh. Please. If this place can't bear laughter, then it's been killed twice, and I refuse to help with the second one."

* * *

They made camp while the light went long and gold across the terraces. Osric cooked—the man could make trail food taste almost like intention—and Tam came back with an armload of deadfall and a report.

"There's a well," he said. "Still good water, cold as anything. And there's—" he hesitated "—there's a building with writing on it. Over the door. I couldn't read it."

"Show me," Mirela said, though she already knew.

It was the schoolhouse. Of course it was the schoolhouse. Small, one room, built the way Vessians built schools in those years—solidly, stubbornly, with the literacy fund's motto carved above the lintel in letters deep enough to survive fire and frost and the specific malice of men with orders:

**A NATION IS ITS READERS.**

The paint was gone. The carving remained. Mirela stood in front of it for a long time, her hand flat against the stone, feeling the letters under her palm like braille, like a pulse.

"Mirela?" Tam said softly.

"It says a nation is its readers," she told him. "The republic that annexed us burned our books first. Did you know that? Before the soldiers, before the land grants, before any of it—there were clerks who went town to town with lists of titles. They called it *harmonization*. My father was a printer." She heard her own voice go flat and distant and made herself come back. "He printed the books they burned. He used to say the joke was on them—he'd printed four copies of everything important, and he'd buried three."

"Where?" Tam breathed.

"He never told me. He said a secret shared by two people is a secret; shared by three, it's a plan; and plans get people shot." She turned away from the lintel. "He was right. They shot him anyway. But not for the books."

* * *

Night came down blue and cold, and the fire drew them in. Rafael sat across from her with his forearms on his knees, and Tam curled nearby like a comma, and Osric smoked his pipe with the bowl cupped so the glow wouldn't show beyond the walls—an old smuggler's habit, Mirela guessed, one of the thousand small skills of men who lived in other people's margins.

"You knew people here," Rafael said. Not a question. He'd been watching her all evening the way he watched weather.

"I knew *of* them. Vesna Dol was my mother's trading circuit. But—" Mirela wrapped her arms around her knees. "In Krajova, after, there was a group of us. Children of the annexed towns. We met in a cellar and we did a thing that probably looked strange from outside."

"What thing?"

"We memorized the dead."

The fire popped. Somewhere under the village, the culvert sang.

"There were lists," she went on. "Incomplete, always incomplete—the republic destroyed the parish records, the census rolls, everything. But survivors wrote down what they remembered, on scraps, in margins of other books, and the scraps found their way to people like my mother, and my mother brought them home, and we children learned them. Names. Ages. Trades. It was a game, at first, for the little ones—a recitation game, points for getting the trade right. Then it stopped being a game and became something else, and nobody decided when. It just became the truest thing we owned."

She looked into the fire.

"You asked me once why I bind books," she said to Rafael. "Why a woman trained in cipher work spends her days gluing spines in a border town. This is why. Every book I repair is a book that didn't burn. I can't raise the dead. I can keep the reading alive. A nation is its readers—we carved it over the schoolhouse doors because we meant it, and they burned the schools because *they* meant it too."

Nobody spoke. Even Osric had taken the pipe out of his mouth.

Then Mirela did the thing she had not done in twenty years outside that cellar in Krajova. She began to name them.

"Dorotea Malenko," she said. "Loom-master. Sixty-one. She taught half the valley to weave and hid the pattern-books in her flour bin, which everyone knew about and no one reported, because Dorotea also made the best plum brandy in Vessia and loyalty has always followed the brandy."

A sound escaped Tam—half laugh, half sob—and Mirela nodded at him, permission granted, and went on.

"Stefan and Ilka Brac, brothers, teamsters. Stefan was thirty-four, Ilka twenty-nine. They drove the wool wagons. When the order came for resettlement, they loaded their wagon with other families' children instead of goods and drove south through the pass in November. Eleven children alive today because of two brothers who froze on the Kraj side of the mountains." She paused. "I have met three of those eleven. One of them is a doctor now. In Maribor. He doesn't know their names—I gave them to him myself, five years ago, and he cried into his soup in a public house, and I pretended not to notice, because mercy includes pretending not to notice."

"Mother Anka," Rafael said quietly.

"Anka Vesely. Fifty-three. Beekeeper." Mirela's voice caught and steadied. "Her hives were counted in the harmonization inventory—did you know that? The clerks counted her bees. Forty-one hives, entered in a ledger, assessed for value, and then the ledger closed and the soldiers came and the bees—" She stopped. Started again. "Wild swarms from her stock still live in these hills. The shepherds call them the free bees. Nobody owns them. They came back on their own."

"The roses too," Tam said suddenly. Everyone looked at him. He flushed but held on. "That big rosebush on the weaver's hall. Roses don't grow wild like that. Somebody planted it. And it just—it kept going."

"Yes," Mirela said. "Exactly that. You understand exactly that, Tam." And the boy sat up straighter than she'd seen him sit all journey, named and seen, and she thought: *this is what memory does when you do it together. It doesn't just honor the dead. It recruits the living.*

She went on. She named the miller, Havel Ostrik, forty-seven, who had drowned himself in his own millrace rather than sign the transfer papers—no, she corrected herself aloud, *rather than be made to sign them, the papers were signed anyway, they always were, that was the whole machinery of it.* She named the schoolteacher, Petra Lindu, thirty-eight, who had refused to teach the occupation curriculum and walked the children over the pass instead, eleven miles, in October rain, delivering them to Vessian households in Krajova one load at a time until the eleventh trip, when she didn't come back. She named the priest, or rather she named the absence of the priest, because Father Bojan had died the winter before the annexation and the village had buried him under the apple tree behind the chapel, and the chapel was burned but the apple tree—

"The apple tree might still be there," she said, standing up abruptly. "Behind the chapel. Is there a chapel?"

"North end," Rafael said, rising with her. "I'll bring a lamp."

* * *

They found it by lamplight: a blackened shell of a building, and beside it, half-swallowed in brush, an apple tree, ancient and crooked and—because the world insists on its jokes—loaded with fruit. Small, hard, late-season apples, more acid than sweet, but apples.

Mirela stood under it and laughed until the laughing turned into the other thing, and Rafael stood beside her and did not touch her and did not leave, which was its own kind of touching, and when it passed she picked six apples, one for each of them, and they ate them sitting on the chapel steps like children stealing fruit, juice running down their wrists, and it was the most sacramental meal Mirela had eaten in twenty years.

"Father Bojan is under this tree," she said, "and he would be furious that we're eating his apples without blessing, and delighted that anyone is eating his apples at all, and I choose to believe the delight wins, because he was that kind of priest."

"Was he?" Rafael asked. "That kind?"

"My mother said he once told the bishop, to his face, that God spoke Vessian too and the bishop's Latin was showing." She wiped her eyes. "Yes. That kind."

Back at the fire, Osric had refilled everyone's cups with something better than trail tea—he'd been carrying a flask, the scoundrel—and when Mirela sat down he handed hers over with an odd formality, almost a bow.

"For the record," he said gruffly, "my grandmother was from the Kraj side, married a ferryman, took a lot of grief for the accent. I used to pretend I didn't have family stories." He knocked out his pipe. "Tonight I'm done pretending. Ask me sometime about the wedding shawl. It's a good story. It ends with a constable falling in a canal."

"That's not the ending," Tam protested. "That's the middle. The ending is the shawl."

"The ending is the shawl," Osric agreed solemnly, and Mirela laughed, wet-eyed, and thought: *look at us. Four strangers and a chest full of dangerous paper, sitting in a murdered village, telling stories.* Grief hoarded is a poison. Grief shared is a fire. You don't extinguish it—you gather around it, and you pass things across it, bread and cups and names, and you get warm enough to walk on.

Later, when Tam slept and Osric took first watch and the fire burned down to a red philosophy, Rafael moved around it to sit beside her, shoulder just touching hers.

"The compass," he said, after a while. "The one I carry. It belonged to my client—the one who died on the Vessian side. I've never told anyone the whole of it." He turned the instrument over in his hands, brass worn bright at the hinges. "Tomorrow we climb into that country. I think—tomorrow I'll tell you."

"You don't owe me your wounds, Rafael."

"I know." He was quiet a moment. "But you gave the village its names tonight. Seems fair I give you mine."

Above them the stars wheeled, indifferent and beautiful. Below them, under the singing culvert, the dead of Vesna Dol lay in ground that had never been legally transferred, because the living had refused to sign, and the grass had grown over the refusal, and the roses had climbed the refusal, and the bees—somewhere in the dark hills—the free bees kept their own unregistered census.

Mirela leaned her head briefly against Rafael's shoulder, then went to her bedroll.

She was nearly asleep when she heard it: Osric, on watch, at the edge of the firelight, speaking low and fast—not to any of them. The words carried only in fragments on the wind. *...the shelf route... yes, before the col... how many riders...*

Then silence, and the culvert singing on, faithful and oblivious, under the graves of a nation that was its readers.

Mirela lay very still, eyes open in the dark, and did not yet decide what she had heard.
