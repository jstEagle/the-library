## 15. Osric's Bargain Shifts

The runner came at dusk, which was the first thing wrong with him. Nobody honest traveled the last mile of the Serrano road after the light went soft, because the low-water shoals would tear the bottom out of any boat fool enough to try them in the dark, and because honest people, by and large, preferred their suppers to their errands.

Osric Vane was mending a net on his porch when the man came up the path — a lean fellow in a gray coat with river mud to the knee and the particular walk of somebody who had been told to look unremarkable and had practiced it badly. He carried no pack. A runner with no pack was a runner carrying only words, and words were heavier than any cargo Osric had ever ferried.

"You're Vane," the man said. Not a question. That was the second thing wrong.

"Depends who's asking," said Osric, and kept his hands moving on the net. Mending was good for conversations you didn't want to be having. It gave your eyes somewhere respectable to go.

"Magistrate Corven sends his regards."

"Never heard of him." This was a lie so comfortable it practically poured itself a drink.

"He thought you'd say that." The runner sat down on the porch step without being invited, which took a certain kind of nerve or a certain kind of stupidity, and looked out at the river going copper and then iron as the sun went down behind the ridge. "He says you're the best-informed man on this stretch of water. He says you know where every chest that comes down the Serrano ends up."

Osric's hands did not stop moving. He was proud of that. Inside, something cold turned over, the way a boat turns when the current takes the stern.

"I know where driftwood ends up," he said. "That's my trade. Wood, drowned sheep, the occasional coffin. Chests are above my station."

"Mm." The runner smiled like a man being patient with an old dog. "He also says you're owed money. Quite a lot of it. By quite a specific person."

* * *

Here is what you need to understand about Osric Vane, and he would have told you himself if you'd bought him two drinks: he was not a bad man. He wanted that on the record. He had smuggled eleven families across the border during the annexation years and charged six of them nothing, and he still knew all their names, and he still worried about the youngest of them, who was now a bookbinder in town with ink-stained fingers and a way of looking at you as though she were reading a difficult page.

But Osric was also a man with debts, and debts, in his long experience, were the one cargo that gained weight in transit.

Two years ago, Rafael Thackeray had come to him at night, soaked, half-frozen, with no papers and no name worth having, and Osric had rowed them across the Serrano under the noses of a border patrol because Rafael had once — years before, in another life — pulled Osric's brother's body off a glacier when nobody else would climb for it. That was the kind of currency Osric traded in. Favors. Debts of honor. The trouble with debts of honor was that they didn't keep books, and Osric's actual, physical, numbered debts did.

There was the loan from Hesk the tanner, which had grown teeth. There was the mortgage on the ferry rights, held by a man in the valley town whose smile Osric trusted about as far as he could throw the man's ledger. And there was the small daily arithmetic of a business that had gone soft since the patrols doubled: fewer crossings, fewer coins, more winter coming.

So when Thackeray had come back into his life hauling an iron chest out of the river — *the* chest, the one stamped with a seal that made even Osric's blood go quiet — Osric had done the arithmetic every man does. He had named a price for silence. Eleven days, he'd said. Pay me or I talk. And he'd hated himself a little for saying it, but only a little, because a man has to eat, and honor doesn't float a ferry.

Now here sat Corven's runner on his porch step, offering to make the whole ledger vanish.

"Twice over," the runner said, when Osric asked — carefully, casually, the way a man asks about weather — what exactly was on offer. "Your debts to Hesk and to the valley. Gone. Both. And a stipend besides, if the information proves useful past the first telling."

"And what information would that be?"

"The party going up the gorge. Where they'll make high camp. When." The runner examined his fingernails. "The magistrate is a reasonable man. He doesn't want blood. He wants paper. But paper travels with people, and people need to sleep somewhere, and a man who knows where people sleep is worth more than a regiment."

Osric mended his net. The knot under his fingers had gone wrong three times now, and he pretended it hadn't.

"And if I say no?"

The runner stood, brushed off his coat, and became very slightly less unremarkable. "Then the magistrate will assume you've chosen a side, and he'll treat you accordingly. He asked me to remind you that ferry licenses are granted by his office. Also that Hesk's patience is shorter than yours." He put a folded paper on the porch boards between them. "No signature needed. Just think about it. You have until the high camp. After that, the offer walks back down the mountain without you."

He went down the path and along the darkening road, walking like a man who had never once been afraid of shoals, and Osric sat with the net in his lap and the paper on the boards and the river making its old sounds, and he felt — there was no other word for it — *recruited*, the way a plank feels recruited when the shipwright picks up the nails.

* * *

He didn't open the paper that night. That mattered to him, somehow. As long as it stayed folded, he was still a man who hadn't read a bargain; he was merely a man in possession of suspicious stationery, which was nearly innocent.

Instead he drank one glass of plum brandy — medicinal — and thought about Rafael Thackeray.

He thought about the night two years ago, the wet ghost at his landing stage saying *I'll owe you, Osric. I'll owe you anything.* And he thought about how, in two years, Rafael had never once tried to weasel. Had come by every month with whatever coin they had, sometimes a few coppers, once a whole silver piece that Osric suspected had been meant for boots. Had never lied about the size of the debt, never dressed it up, never pretended it wasn't there. In Osric's experience, that was rarer than honesty itself. Plenty of men were honest. Very few were honest *about owing.*

And he thought about Tam.

Tam Weller, sixteen, all elbows and enthusiasm, who had been ferrying with Osric since he was twelve and who had recently transferred his entire heart, without consultation, to Rafael Thackeray. The boy followed Rafael around like a duckling that had imprinted on a wolf and saw nothing odd about the arrangement. Last week Tam had spent an entire evening explaining rope coils to Osric — *to Osric* — because Rafael had shown him a new one, and the boy's face had been lit up like a lantern festival.

If Osric sold the high camp, and things went badly at the high camp — and things, with magistrates involved, had a way of going badly in directions nobody ordered — then sooner or later Tam would find out who had drawn the map. Boys always found out. That was practically their trade.

Osric finished the brandy and looked at the folded paper sitting on the table like a spider that had learned to hold still.

"Damn it," he told it conversationally.

* * *

Three days later they gathered at the landing to load for the crossing, and Osric watched the party assemble and felt the bargain shift its weight inside him like ballast.

Rafael arrived first, of course, before dawn, checking the boats with that old professional hunger — touching gunwales, testing lashings, reading the water the way Mirela read her ciphers. Two years of hiding had bent them, but the moment their hands closed on a rope, some original version of Rafael Thackeray stood up inside the ruined one, and Osric had to admit it was good to see. Like watching a house remember it was a house.

Mirela came next with a pack too big for her and refused help with it, which Osric respected. She'd bound the documents into false book covers — he'd seen her do it, a week of nights in her shop, glue and linen and a bone folder clicking like a clock — and she carried them now the way a mother carries a sleeping child through a crowd: casually, and with total attention.

And Tam came running down the path late, hair everywhere, with a coil of spare line over one shoulder and a grin you could have moored a barge to, and Osric felt something in his chest do a slow, painful turn.

Because here was the thing. Here was the whole rotten, beautiful thing of it. These three — the disgraced guide, the exile, the boy — were walking into the mountains carrying the only honest paper in twenty years of a dishonest country, and they had asked *him*, Osric Vane, debt-ridden ferryman, to carry them. Not because he was noble. Because he knew the water and the approaches and the shepherd trails, and because Rafael was paying down a debt the honorable way, coin by coin, without ever once asking for a discount.

And Corven's man was offering to wipe Osric clean for selling them out at altitude, where the selling would be easiest and the consequences most permanent.

"Osric." Rafael stood at the bow, hand out. "You ready?"

"Born ready," said Osric, which was a lie, and took the hand, which was not.

They loaded the boats. The current took them. The gorge opened ahead like a sentence nobody had finished yet, and Osric sat at the oars with the folded paper still in his breast pocket — he hadn't burned it, he noticed, hadn't even gotten it wet, and he filed that fact away about himself with a certain grim curiosity — and he said nothing.

He said nothing at the first camp, where Tam burned the porridge and Rafael ate it anyway and pronounced it *rustic*. He said nothing at the second camp, where Mirela taught Tam cipher-letters scratched in dirt until the boy fell asleep mid-alphabet, mouth open, utterly trusting. He said nothing when Rafael laid out the route by firelight — Windrow Col, then the shelf camps, then the high camp below the glacier — and Osric sat there knowing that every word was worth money, real money, freedom-from-Hesk money, and feeling the knowledge sit in him like a swallowed stone.

On the fourth night, high up where the pines gave out and the wind started doing the talking, Rafael caught him alone at the woodpile.

"You've been quiet," Rafael said. "Quieter than usual, I mean. For you that's practically a eulogy."

"I'm conserving breath. Altitude."

"We're at eight thousand feet, Osric. You live at six."

"Conserving anyway." Osric split a log with more force than the log deserved. Then, because the stone in his stomach had grown teeth of its own, he heard himself say: "Thackeray. If it came to it — if a man had a chance to get clear of everything he owed, all of it, at once — but the price was steep, and the price landed on other people. What would you tell him?"

Rafael considered this seriously, the way they considered everything, as though the question were cargo that had to be stowed properly before the boat moved on.

"I'd tell him," Rafael said slowly, "that I spent two years trying to pay for my life with my name, and it doesn't work. You can't buy yourself clear. You can only decide, each day, what you're carrying and who for." A pause. The firelight moved on their faces. "Why? Is someone in that position?"

"Nobody," said Osric. "Hypothetical fella. Bad with money."

"Sounds like half my clients." Rafael clapped him on the shoulder and went back toward the fire, and Osric stood alone in the cold with the paper against his ribs and the stone in his gut, and understood, finally, that the bargain had shifted — not because Corven's offer had changed, but because he had.

He still hadn't burned the paper. He told himself that was caution. A wise man keeps evidence. A wise man might need to prove what was asked of him someday.

But that night, lying in his bedroll under a sky crowded hard with stars, Osric Vane took out the folded page and held it over the coals of the dead fire, and hesitated — one breath, two — before letting it drop.

It caught, flared, and was gone, and the ash blew up the mountain toward Windrow Col, and Osric lay back down feeling lighter and more frightened than he had in years, because he knew now what Corven's runners did to men who declined politely, and he knew the riders were already on the southern approach, and he knew — the way ferrymen know a flood before the rain stops — that the storm coming over the col was going to arrive early, and angry, and that everything he'd just decided would be tested at the high camp before the week was out.

Above him, the first clouds were already sliding over the pass like a hand closing.

"Damn it," Osric said again, to no one, and slept badly, and dreamed of ledgers that weighed as much as iron chests.
