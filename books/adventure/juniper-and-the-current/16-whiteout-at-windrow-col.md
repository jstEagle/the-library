## 16. Whiteout at Windrow Col

The storm did not announce itself. That was the first lie of it.

Rafael had spent twenty years learning the mountain's manners—the way it cleared its throat with a wind shift, the way it coughed lenticular clouds over the western peaks a full day before it meant business. But this one came up from the south like a debt collector, no warning, no courtesy, just a wall of gray that ate the sky above Windrow Col while they were still two hours below it.

"Is that supposed to be there?" Tam asked, pointing his chin at the horizon, which had ceased to exist.

"No," Rafael said.

"Because I feel like the horizon is usually—"

"Tam."

"Right. Shutting up."

Mirela had gone quiet in the way she went quiet when she was doing arithmetic she didn't like. She stood with her arms crossed against the cold, watching the wall of weather come on, and said only: "How long?"

"If we're lucky, it passes in a day." Rafael squinted into the rising wind. "If we're not lucky, three."

"And if we're unlucky?"

"We don't use that word on a mountain."

Osric, bringing up the rear with the mule and the chest strapped under oilcloth, called out over the wind: "I've got opinions about luck! Most of them involve being somewhere else!"

"Then walk faster," Rafael called back, "old man," and Osric made a noise that in any other context would have been laughter.

They climbed. There was nothing else to do. The col was the way through, and below them was four days of exposed valley they'd already crossed, and behind them—Rafael didn't look, but he felt it anyway, the way you feel a hand between your shoulder blades—was whatever Corven had sent up the southern approach.

The snow hit them at the treeline. Not falling so much as arriving sideways, horizontal, with intent. Within twenty minutes Rafael's eyelashes were crusted and Tam looked like something a baker had left out overnight. The world shrank to a circle ten paces across, then five, then to nothing but the sound of wind trying to peel the mountain down to its bones.

And that was when Rafael stopped being able to see the route.

* * *

Here was the problem, and Rafael had known it since Juniper's Landing and had told no one, because telling no one was a specialty of theirs: the route through Windrow Col did not exist.

Not officially. Not on any map printed in the last twenty years. There was a mapped route—a good one, a sensible one, switchbacking up the eastern shoulder—but it had been closed by decree after the annexation, closed the way Vessian things were closed: erased, fenced with paper, patrolled by men who asked questions with their fists. The old route, the shepherd's route, threaded through the western face through country no surveyor had bothered to record because the people who lived there hadn't counted as people worth surveying.

Rafael knew it because a dead man had walked them through it once, laughing, saying *the maps are wrong on purpose, Thackeray, that's how you know which ones matter.*

Elias Wren. The name arrived uninvited, as it always did, wearing its usual expression of patient disappointment.

"You've stopped," Mirela said beside him. She'd tied herself to him with ten feet of rope an hour ago without asking permission, which was either trust or a very polite form of arrest. "Your feet stopped. I noticed because mine kept going and then the rope objected."

"I'm thinking."

"That's what worries me. You have a thinking face. It looks exactly like your face has died and someone else is wearing it."

"Genuine concern. Thank you."

"You're welcome." She leaned closer, snow streaming off her hood. "Rafael. Can you find the way or not?"

The honest answer was *not in this*, because navigation in whiteout wasn't seeing—it was counting. Steps and slope and the pull of the wind on your left cheek, the memory of where the rock bands ran beneath the snow like ribs under skin. It was the kind of thing you could do drunk on exhaustion if you trusted yourself completely, and Rafael's trust in themselves had been in receivership for two years.

But there was the compass.

It sat in the inner pocket, against Rafael's chest, warm as a second heartbeat. Brass-cased, dented on one side where it had taken a fall it hadn't deserved. Elias Wren had pressed it into their hands the night before the expedition went bad, grinning like a man sharing a joke with himself. *"Guild issue, but don't tell the guild. North is north, Thackeray. Everything else is negotiable."*

North was north. Everything else was negotiable. Rafael took it out now, cupped it against the wind, and watched the needle tremble and settle, tremble and settle, faithful as a dog that didn't know its master was dead.

"All right," Rafael said. "Everyone rope up tight. Ten feet. No one wanders, no one sneezes in a direction, no one does anything interesting. Boring is survival today."

"Boring," Tam said solemnly, "is my entire personality."

"For once, that's useful."

* * *

They climbed into the teeth of it for two hours by Rafael's count, though counting had begun to feel theoretical. Step, step, check the needle, feel for the slope's lean. The western face here should fall away gently to the left; instead the ground rose, which meant either they'd drifted right or the mountain had been rearranged overnight out of spite.

"Rafael." Osric's voice, close, stripped of humor. "The mule won't move."

"The mule is smarter than all of us, then."

"She's planted. She does this when—" A pause, wind screaming through it. "When there's a drop she doesn't like."

Rafael's stomach performed a slow, professional assessment. They should not be near a drop. The route they should be nowhere near had no drops until the headwall. Which meant they were on the other route, the dead route, the one that existed only in Rafael's memory and in the muscle of a man six years buried—and that route had a corniced shelf along its northern lip that would drop you four hundred feet onto rocks that would apologize for nothing.

"Nobody moves," Rafael said. "Tam, hold the rope. Mirela, hold Tam. Osric, hold everything else."

Rafael went forward alone, on hands and knees, probing with an ice axe, and found the edge within six feet. Found it with the axe, thank every indifferent god, and not with a foot. Below the lip the snow curved away smooth and blue-white and utterly silent, a cornice hanging over emptiness like a held breath.

For a long moment Rafael knelt there with the wind tearing at their back and understood, with terrible clarity, that the mountain was not trying to kill them. The mountain didn't care enough. This was just geometry, and geometry killed the careless and the mapless alike, and Rafael was currently both.

The compass. Rafael pulled it out again, sheltered it, and thought—not for the first time—that it was less an instrument than a question. *Where are you? Where were you? Where did he die?* Because the needle pointed north, and north was where the expedition had turned wrong, and north was where Elias Wren had walked out ahead of the party one morning in weather like this and never walked back, and Rafael had spent two years unable to say whether the compass had failed or whether Rafael had failed to read it.

Both answers were unbearable. So Rafael carried the thing against their heart like a stone they'd chosen.

"Thackeray!" Osric bellowed. "Whatever you're doing, do it faster! The mule has opinions!"

"THE MULE CAN WRITE A LETTER!"

Back. Left. They traversed south, away from the lip, counting steps aloud so Tam could hear them through the wind—forty steps, sixty, ninety—and at a hundred and forty the ground changed its mind and began to climb in earnest, steep and solid, bedrock under the snow, and Rafael nearly wept with relief at the feel of it. The chimney. The western chimney, hidden in the fold of the col, the one no map showed because no mapmaker had ever thought these people worth the ink.

"There's a gully ahead," Rafael shouted back. "It looks like the mountain is swallowing itself. It is. We're going to let it."

"WONDERFUL!" Osric shouted. "I LOVE BEING SWALLOWED!"

They went up the chimney in the roaring dark, and it was awful, and it worked.

* * *

The cave—if you were generous—was a hollow beneath an overhanging slab, big enough for four people, a mule, and a great deal of mutual resentment. If you were not generous, it was a wound in the mountainside. Rafael got a stove lit in the lee of the entrance, and the small blue flame became the center of the universe, and everyone arranged themselves around it like planets around a very cold sun.

Tam's lips were blue. Rafael fed him sugar tea and made him drink it twice, and the boy complained about the taste with such sincerity that Rafael knew he'd live.

"How far did we come?" Mirela asked. She'd wedged herself against Rafael's side without appearing to decide to, the way snow accumulates.

"Through the col. Past it, maybe. Hard to say in that."

"But we're through?"

"We're through the hard part. Whether we're through the *hard parts*—" Rafael shrugged. "Ask me tomorrow."

Osric saw to the mule, muttering endearments to her that were frankly more tender than anything he'd ever said to a human being. Then he settled against the wall, pulled his hat over his eyes, and said, conversationally, into the dark:

"You know, in thirty years of moving things across borders, I have never once been swallowed by a mountain. Ferries, yes. Customs men, twice. But never geology."

"Congratulations," Rafael said.

"It occurs to me," Osric went on, hat still over his eyes, voice mild as milk, "that you know things about this range that aren't written anywhere. Things the guild doesn't teach. Things the *government* doesn't know." A pause, delicately weighted. "Almost like someone taught you. Someone who isn't here anymore."

The cave went very quiet except for the wind and the stove.

"Osric," Mirela said carefully.

"I'm making conversation."

"You're fishing."

"I'm an old man making conversation on a cold mountain," Osric said, and rolled over, and in a few minutes his breathing slowed into the performance of sleep that Rafael recognized, having performed it many times himself.

Mirela waited until she was sure, then leaned her head against Rafael's shoulder. Her hair smelled of woodsmoke and wet wool. "He's right, though. Isn't he. Someone taught you this route."

"Yes."

"A Vessian someone."

Rafael looked at the compass, lying in their open palm, the needle swinging slightly with the tremor of their hand before settling. North. Always north. Faithful to a fault, faithful past the point of usefulness, pointing at a place on the mountain where the truth was buried under six years of snow and one enormous lie.

"Elias Wren," Rafael said. The name came out rough, like a stone turning over. "My client. The one who died. The one they say I killed."

Mirela didn't flinch, didn't pull away. She reached out and closed Rafael's fingers over the compass, one by one, gentle as binding a book.

"Tomorrow," she said quietly. "Tell me tomorrow. Tonight you should sleep."

But sleep was a country Rafael's passport had been revoked from, years ago. They sat awake while the storm hammered the slab overhead, holding a dead man's compass, listening to the boy breathe and the old man pretend and the woman beside them dream, and thought: *the needle points north, and north is where I'll finally have to say it out loud.*

Because tomorrow, weather willing, they'd make the high camp below the pass. And beyond the high camp lay Thackeray Pass itself—the pass that bore Rafael's own name now, a bitter joke of the guild's, named for the guide who lost a client on it. And Mirela would want to know why. And Rafael was beginning to understand, sitting in the dark with the storm screaming Elias Wren's name against the stone, that they could not carry the chest across that pass without also carrying the truth of what happened there.

The compass trembled. Settled. Pointed home.

Outside, the wind shifted—southwest now, backing, which meant the storm was turning, which meant morning might come clear and merciless and early. Rafael banked the stove, checked the ropes one last time, and woke Tam for his watch.

"Anything I should listen for?" the boy whispered.

"Yeah," Rafael said, looking north into the black. "Silence. If the mountain goes quiet, wake me fast. Quiet is worse than noise. Quiet means something's decided."

Tam nodded gravely and took the watch, and Rafael lay back against the stone with the compass against their chest, and did not sleep, and listened to the silence gathering like a held breath over the pass.
