## 17. What Happened on the Vessian Side

The storm had pinned them at Windrow Col for two days, and by the second night the snow cave had developed that particular intimacy of shared misery—four people, one stove, and a smell of wet wool that no amount of pine boughs could disguise.

Tam had fallen asleep mid-sentence, which was his gift. Osric sat against the wall of ice with his hat over his eyes, ostensibly sleeping, and doing such a poor job of it that Rafael could see the man's mouth working through whatever argument he was having with himself. Mirela sat closest to the stove, a book she'd brought from her satchel open on her knees but unread for an hour, her eyes on the flame.

The compass lay in Rafael's palm. It always ended up there, in the quiet hours. Aldous Wren's compass, brass-cased, the needle trembling toward north as if it were nervous about something.

"You keep holding it like it might apologize," Mirela said softly.

Rafael turned it over. The case was worn smooth where a thumb had rubbed it for years—Aldous's thumb first, then Rafael's own, inheriting the habit along with everything else.

"It's not the compass I owe an apology to."

Osric's hat didn't move, but his breathing changed—the careful stillness of a man pretending very hard to be furniture.

Mirela closed her book. "You've never told me what happened up here. Not really. You've told me *that* something happened, the way you'd tell me the weather happened. Distantly. As if you weren't standing in it."

"The guild report is public record," Rafael said. "You could read it."

"I've read it. It reads like a man describing a funeral from across a river." She shifted closer, tucking her blanket around them both without asking, which was becoming her way. "I want to hear it from this side of the river."

The stove ticked. Outside, the wind moved through the col with its long, patient voice, the sound that had been the last thing Rafael heard clearly on the worst day of their life.

"His name was Aldous Wren," Rafael said. "He was sixty-one years old. A surveyor from the valley towns, self-taught, the kind of man who corrected other people's maps in pencil and mailed them back with polite notes. He hired me to guide him across Thackeray Pass into Vessia—not the country, then; the territory, they called it by then—to survey the old boundary markers before the annexation paperwork buried them entirely."

"He wanted proof," Mirela said quietly. "Of where Vessia actually ended."

"He wanted the truth written down somewhere it couldn't be erased. Yes." Rafael's thumb found the worn place on the compass case. "He was the best client I ever had. He carried his own pack, he never complained about the cold, and every night in camp he taught me something—a star, a bird, a bit of Vessian grammar he'd learned from his grandmother. He used to say the mountains don't lie, they just wait for you to stop lying to yourself."

* * *

"We went up in late autumn. Same season as now. The forecast window was good—ten days of clear high pressure, the kind you dream about. And we made the pass on schedule. We were coming down the Vessian side when the message reached us."

"A message? Up there?"

"A runner. Which should have been my first warning. Nobody sends runners into the high country in late autumn unless the news won't keep." Rafael heard their own voice go flat, the way it did when they handled this memory—the way you handle a blade you've cut yourself on many times. "It came from the magistrate's office in Juniper's Landing. Corven wasn't magistrate yet—he was the survey commissioner, the man responsible for ratifying the new boundary. The message said the route was changed. Instead of descending by the eastern shoulder, we were to take the northern couloir and survey three additional marker sites along it. Signed orders. Official seal. Aldous read it twice and said, 'Well, that's odd, the northern route is a death trap in November,' and then he said, 'But orders are orders,' because he was that kind of man. Honest men always assume the paper is honest."

Mirela had gone very still. "And you?"

"I argued. I'll say that much for myself—I argued until my voice gave out. The couloir holds wind-loaded snow above a rock band. In November. I told him it was suicide. And Aldous put his hand on my shoulder and said, 'Rafael, if we refuse, they'll take your license, and then who will carry the truth out of these mountains?' Because he understood immediately what I took another day to understand—that the order wasn't about surveying. It was about him. About making sure his boundary work died in the mountains with him."

The stove hissed. Somewhere in the walls of the cave, the snow settled with a sound like a long exhale.

"So you went."

"So we went." Rafael's jaw tightened. "For two days we climbed that miserable gash in the mountain, and Aldous surveyed every marker like it was a cathedral, and I hated every honest minute of it because I kept waiting for the mountain to do what mountains do. On the third day, above the rock band, the slope released."

* * *

Rafael stopped. This was the part that had never been said aloud, not to the tribunal, not to the guild, not to anyone, and saying it now felt like pulling a splinter that had grown into the bone.

Mirela waited. She was good at waiting—that bookbinder's patience, mending things by letting them hold still.

"There was a rope between us," Rafael said finally. "There was always a rope. When it went, I felt it—one hard jerk, and then slack. And I did the arithmetic every guide does in that half-second: anchor held, rope cut on rock edge, he's gone. Two hundred meters down the couloir. I climbed down to him anyway. It took me four hours. He was—" Rafael's breath caught, and Mirela's hand found theirs in the dark, small and steady. "He was still alive when I reached him. Broken past any hope, but alive. Long enough to talk."

"What did he say?"

"He pressed the compass into my hand. His hands couldn't close anymore, so he pressed it and held on until I promised to take it. And he said, 'They'll blame you. Don't let them make you believe it.' Those were nearly his last words. Not goodbye. Not forgive me. *Don't let them make you believe it.* He knew, even bleeding out in a couloir, exactly what the machinery would do next." Rafael laughed, and there was no humor in it, only the ragged edge of an old wound finally opened to air. "And I let them. That's the shame I've carried, Mirela. Not the climb, not the order—even the fall. The shame is that I believed it. Eight years of believing it. Because the alternative was believing that a whole system had decided to kill a sweet old man with a pencil behind his ear, and that I was just the instrument they'd chosen, and instruments don't get to matter."

"And the inquiry?"

"Was theater. Corven presided—of course he did, he'd written the order. The finding was negligence: the guide chose a dangerous route in unsafe conditions. My license revoked, my name struck from the register. Aldous's family got a letter expressing regret and a bill for recovery costs." Rafael's voice cracked on the word *bill*, and rage came up through the crack like water through stone—not the hot useless rage of the young, but something older and colder and far more useful. "Eight years I thought: *if only I'd refused harder, if only I'd found another way.* And the truth is there was no other way. There was no version of that week where Aldous Wren walked home. The order was the murder weapon. The couloir was just where they aimed it."

* * *

Silence filled the cave, the good kind, the kind that has room in it.

Osric's hat had come off at some point. He was looking at the stove with wet eyes he pretended were smoke-irritated, and when he caught Rafael watching him he cleared his throat and said, gruffly, "Never liked that Corven. Man shakes your hand like he's counting your fingers."

"Osric," Rafael said gently, "you've been awake this whole time."

"I'm a light sleeper. It's a professional hazard of owing money." But he didn't put the hat back on, and he didn't leave, and after a moment he added, quieter, "My brother ferried a surveyor once. Years back. Talked about stars the whole crossing, apparently. Wouldn't shut up about them." He pulled the hat down firmly. "Anyway. Go back to sleep, all of you."

Tam slept on, blessedly, through all of it.

Mirela hadn't moved her hand from Rafael's. Now she turned toward them fully, firelight moving on her face, and Rafael braced for pity—and found none. What they found instead was fury, banked and precise, burning in her eyes like coal.

"They didn't just kill him," she said. "They built the blacklist out of him. Your disgrace isn't an accident, Rafael—it's load-bearing. Every year the permits stand unchallenged, your name is the keystone that keeps the story true. *An unreliable guide led a good man astray.* That's why Corven wants the chest destroyed so badly. It's not just the documents. It's that the documents prove the same hand forged the land grants and broke you."

"Yes," Rafael said. And then, feeling the strange vertigo of it: "Yes. That's exactly it. I've spent eight years apologizing to a ghost for a crime someone else committed, and the whole time the man who committed it has been climbing the ranks off the theft." They looked down at the compass, and the needle trembled in its housing, faithful as ever, pointing north, pointing home, pointing at everything Aldous had loved. "He told me not to let them make me believe it. I let them for eight years. But I'm done."

"Done how?" Mirela asked. Not challenging—measuring. She always wanted to know the load before she trusted the bridge.

"Done carrying shame that belongs to Corven. Done being a ghost. Whatever happens at that pass—whether the documents reach the council or the snow takes them or Corven's riders take us—we come down off this mountain as witnesses. Both of us. Loud ones."

Mirela smiled then, slow and fierce, and squeezed their hand once, hard, the way you'd test a knot you intended to trust your life to.

"Good," she said. "Because I've been composing speeches since Juniper's Landing, and I'd hate to waste them."

Rafael laughed—a real laugh, startled out of them, echoing faintly off the ice—and for a moment the cave was warm in a way the stove had nothing to do with.

Then Osric stirred against the wall, and when he spoke his voice had lost its gruffness entirely, gone flat and careful, the voice of a man reading a note he wished he hadn't received.

"Rafael. There's something you should know before you decide what kind of witness you're going to be." He turned the hat in his hands, once, twice. "Corven's not following us anymore. He's ahead of us. My cousin runs stock on the southern approach, and word came up yesterday with the last supply runner before the storm. Twelve riders. Corven himself. And they're not taking the long way around."

The wind rose outside, pressing against the cave mouth like a hand testing a door.

"They'll reach the pass two days behind us," Osric said. "Maybe less, if the weather breaks for them. And Rafael—they're not riding up there to negotiate."
