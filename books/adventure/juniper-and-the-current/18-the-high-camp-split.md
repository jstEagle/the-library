## 18. The High Camp Split

The storm had spent itself sometime before dawn, and what it left behind looked like a war the mountain had won.

Rafael stood at the mouth of the tent and took inventory the way they used to take inventory after bad nights on the Vessian side: what's broken, what's gone, who's bleeding, who's lying about it. The cook tarp was in three pieces and one of those pieces was in a tree forty feet upslope, flapping like a flag of surrender. The supply sled had shed half its load into a crevasse that hadn't been there yesterday—or rather, that had been there yesterday under six feet of snow bridge, and had eaten quietly through the night while everyone slept.

"Who packed the flour on the outside?" Osric called from the wreckage, holding up a canvas sack that had frozen into something resembling a paving stone.

"The same man who tied it down," Tam said, "which was you."

"I tie beautiful knots. The mountain unties them. There's a difference between us and I want it noted."

Mirela said nothing. She was standing at the edge of camp with the chest strapped to her back, looking up at Windrow Col, where the wind was still combing spindrift off the ridge in long white banners. Rafael watched her do the arithmetic she'd been doing since Juniper's Landing—the days against the miles against the weather—and he knew, before she turned around, what the sum was going to be.

* * *

They gathered around the stove in the largest surviving tent, five people and a pot of tea thin enough to read through. Rafael laid out the situation the way he'd learned to lay out route problems: plainly, without decoration, because decoration was how people died.

"The col is passable today. Tomorrow, maybe. After that—" He nodded at the ridge, where the banners were thickening. "We lost two days to the whiteout and most of our food margin. We're four days from Thackeray Pass at this pace. Five if the glacier crossing goes slow, which it will, because half the snow bridges on it are now rumors."

"And the permits expire when?" Tam asked.

"Season's end. Which is not a date on a calendar out here—it's the first real snowfall that closes the southern passes for the winter." Rafael let that sit. "Corven doesn't need to catch us. He just needs the weather to do his work."

Osric blew on his tea. "So we go faster."

"We can't all go faster. Not with the sled, not with the injured rope team, not at the pace the weakest of us can hold." Rafael looked at Mirela, because there was no honest way to say what came next without looking at her. "Which means we split. One group goes light and fast with the documents—two days to the pass, maybe less. The other shelters here, recovers, comes down when the weather allows."

"No," Mirela said.

It was not a loud *no*. It was the quiet kind, the kind that has already been through every argument with itself in the dark and come out the other side with its boots on.

"Mirela—"

"No." She set her cup down with the deliberate care of someone who did not trust her own hands. "You are not doing this. You are not standing there being reasonable at me."

"I'm being a guide. It's the only thing I know how to be."

"You're being afraid," she said, "and dressing it in rope work."

Tam made a small sound, like a man trying to become furniture. Osric drank his tea with the studied attention of a ferryman watching a flood from high ground.

* * *

The argument moved outside, because some arguments need cold air the way wounds need cleaning. Rafael followed her to the edge of camp, where the drop-off fell away toward the glacier and the wind carried the smell of snow that hadn't fallen yet but was thinking about it.

"You'd take Tam," she said. "Fast pair, you and Tam, chest between you. Osric and I shelter here and come down in three days."

"That's the sensible split."

"It's the coward's split and you know it. If you take the documents over that pass yourself, you get to be the hero again. You get to hand them to the courier with your own hands and be *Rafael Thackeray, restored*, and nobody has to trust anybody else with the thing that matters." Her breath came out white and fast. "And I stay behind in a tent eating frozen flour, waiting to find out if the world got saved without me."

"That's not—"

"It is exactly what it is." She pulled the chest strap higher on her shoulder, an unconscious gesture, the way a mother adjusts a child. "I have carried this country's dead weight for twenty years, Rafael. My family is in the ground under a village you haven't seen yet. Every book I've bound since Juniper's Landing, I've bound with these hands"—she held them up, cracked and ink-stained—"and every one of them was practice for carrying *this*." She touched the chest. "You don't get to decide I'm too fragile for the last mile."

"I don't think you're fragile." His voice came out lower than he intended. "I think you're the strongest person on this mountain, and that's precisely why I can't—"

"Can't what?"

He didn't answer, and the silence did the answering for him, and they both heard it.

* * *

Here is the thing Rafael had never said aloud, not in the snow cave, not by any fire, not even to himself in the honest hours before dawn: somewhere between the burned village and the glacier crossing, between her hand on the rope and her voice naming the dead, the mission had stopped being the most important thing he was carrying.

And a guide knows what happens when the most important thing isn't the plan. A guide knows that the moment you start making decisions to protect a person instead of to complete an objective, you've already made the mistake that gets written up in incident reports. He had watched it happen. He had, once, been the reason it happened—or believed he had, which amounted to the same thing for twenty years.

"You want to send me back because you love me," Mirela said. It wasn't a question, and she didn't soften it, and Rafael felt the word land in his chest like a piton driven home. "And you think love means putting me where nothing can reach me. But I have been where nothing can reach me, Rafael. It's called exile, and it's warm and safe and it lasts forever, and I would trade every safe year of it for one dangerous day of mattering."

The wind filled the pause. Below them, the glacier creaked—a sound like a door opening very slowly in an empty house.

"So we don't split," Rafael said finally.

"Now you're being stubborn."

"I'm being a guide. The party stays together or nobody crosses. That's not sentiment, it's arithmetic—weaken either group and both groups die. A fast pair caught in weather without shelter dies faster than a full party, because a full party can dig in, rotate watches, haul each other out of a crevasse. Two people can't. You want to carry the documents? Fine. We all carry them. We all move together, we all make the pass together, or we all turn around together."

She studied him, and slowly the anger went out of her face, replaced by something more dangerous: understanding. "You're not refusing to split because it's tactically wrong."

"I'm refusing to split because I spent two years learning what losing people costs, and I decline to rehearse it."

"There it is," she said softly.

"Don't look pleased."

"I'm not pleased. I'm terrified. Both things can be true; you taught me that on the glacier." She reached out and straightened his collar, an absurd domestic gesture at eleven thousand feet, and it undid him more thoroughly than the shouting had. "Then we go together. All of us, one rope, one pace. And if the weather turns—"

"If the weather turns, we dig in and wait it out, and Corven's permits expire while we're drinking melted snow and complaining about Osric's cooking."

"Heard my name," Osric announced from the tent flap, having eavesdropped with the shamelessness of a man who considered privacy a rumor. "And for the record, my cooking has kept worse parties than this alive. Barely. But alive."

* * *

The council of war reconvened, minus the shouting. Rafael laid out the new plan: everything abandonable would be abandoned. The sled, the spare rope, the tent poles they could replace with ice axes and hope. Each person carried food for five days and no pride whatsoever. They would move at the pace of the slowest member—which meant Tam—and Tam, hearing this, stood up straighter than Rafael had ever seen him stand.

"I can be fast," Tam said.

"You can be steady," Rafael corrected. "Fast is easy. Steady is what gets people over passes. Walk like the ground owes you nothing and you'll do fine."

Osric voted with a shrug that managed to convey both reluctance and total commitment, which was very Osric. Mirela kept the chest. Tam took the medical kit. Rafael took the compass, because he always took the compass, because the compass was the closest thing he had to a conscience that fit in a pocket.

They broke camp at midmorning, working in the rhythm that had grown among them without anyone announcing it—Osric striking tents, Tam coiling rope badly and then, lately, less badly, Mirela checking straps twice because the chest was the whole reason any of them were freezing on a mountainside. Rafael watched them and felt something in his chest that had no business being there on a day like this: something almost like happiness, or at least its rough draft.

They made good distance until midafternoon. Then Tam, ranging ahead as point, came back down the slope at a jog that Rafael's spine recognized before his brain did.

"Smoke," Tam said. "Down-valley, south approach. A lot of it. Cooking fires—dozens."

Rafael climbed the rise and looked. Far below, threading up the southern approach like ants following a dropped jam jar, was a column. Riders. Twelve, maybe more, moving with the unhurried confidence of men who had supplies, orders, and time.

Corven wasn't waiting for the weather anymore. Corven was climbing.

Rafael turned back to his impossible party—his debt-ridden ferryman, his exiled bookbinder, his boy who idolized him—and found them all looking at him, waiting, the way people always waited, for the guide to say what was true.

"They've got four days of climbing ahead of them," Rafael said. "So do we. Let's make sure ours are better spent."

Behind them, far to the north, the first flakes of the next storm were already falling on the high peaks—quiet, patient, indifferent to whose documents reached the pass first.
