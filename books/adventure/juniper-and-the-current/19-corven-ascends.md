## 19. Corven Ascends

Magistrate Corven had climbed mountains before, but never willingly. Mountains, in his experience, were where sensible arrangements went to die. They were where documents blew out of men's hands, where witnesses froze before they could testify, where the whole tidy machinery of jurisdiction simply stopped working because the air got thin and the law had no grip on ice.

He climbed this one anyway, because the alternative was worse.

"Four days," he said to Sergeant Brandt, who rode half a length behind him on the switchbacks above the Serrano headwaters. "The concessions expire at season's end. Four days, and every forged transfer in that chest becomes a footnote instead of a catastrophe."

"Yes, Magistrate." Brandt had a voice like gravel poured slowly. She had been saying *yes, Magistrate* for eleven years, and in all that time Corven had never once caught her saying it with an opinion attached. He valued that the way other men valued gold.

The southern approach was a bad road pretending to be a trail. Twelve riders, pack mules complaining in the universal language of pack mules, and the pines closing overhead like witnesses turning their backs. Corven rode at the front because he had learned long ago that a magistrate who rode at the back was a magistrate whose orders arrived pre-diluted.

He did not look like a hunter. He knew that. Hunters had a look—lean, hungry, coiled. Corven had the look of a man who did his accounts twice and found them balanced both times. Round-faced, silvering at the temples, hands soft from decades of signing things. People underestimated him for exactly as long as it took to be ruined by him, and then they understood: the weather doesn't look like anything either, until you're standing in it.

That was the comparison he'd settled on, somewhere around the third switchback, when his knees began their annual complaint. He was not pursuing Thackeray. He was not hunting the Vessian woman or the ferryman or the boy. He was simply the season they had failed to prepare for. You don't negotiate with weather. You don't appeal to its better nature. You get inside, or you get buried.

* * *

He had made one mistake in this affair, and he had spent two sleepless nights locating it, the way a man runs his tongue over a chipped tooth. The mistake was the river.

The chest should have gone to the bottom of a lake. Deep water, no current, no dredging rights for fifty miles. But the man he'd trusted with the disposal—a survey clerk named Pell, competent in every measurable way—had chosen the Serrano at flood stage, and the Serrano at flood stage was a liar. It took things and it kept them, and then, twenty years or two years or two months later, in a low-water season, it gave them back with the cheerful indifference of a dog returning a stolen slipper.

Low water. Corven had reviewed the gauges afterward. Of course it was a low-water season. The universe, in his experience, was not cruel, but it was punctilious—it kept perfect books, and it always collected.

So now: twelve riders, four days, and a disgraced guide somewhere above him on a route Corven knew better than he wanted to. He knew it because he had drawn it. Seven years ago, in a different life of the same one, he had redrawn a survey line on the Vessian side of the range, and a client named Aldous Wren had walked into a gully that the original map said wasn't there. Corven had signed the redrawn map himself. He remembered the weight of the pen. He remembered thinking, *this is a small thing, a line on paper,* and he had been right—it was a small thing, the way a keystone is a small thing. The arch came down on Rafael Thackeray instead of on him, which had been the entire design, and for seven years the arch had held.

Now a chest had floated out of a river like a subpoena.

"Magistrate." Brandt drew level, lowering her voice for the mules' benefit. "The runner from the shelf camp. You'll want to read this before we make the high pasture."

Corven took the folded paper without breaking stride. Osric's hand—no, not Osric's. The advance rider's, reporting what Osric had told him. *Party of four. Taking the old survey route through Windrow Col. Chest carried in a waxed satchel, center of the column. They move slow. The woman is not a climber.*

Corven read it twice. Something in it itched. *The old survey route.* Thackeray was many things—drunk for a while, ruined, careless with other people's secrets—but he had never once in his career taken a route because it was on a map. Thackeray took routes because he could see them, the way other men could see a sentence before they wrote it. If he was on the survey route, it was either because the survey route was genuinely the fastest line—

—or because someone had told him it was, and he'd believed the teller, which Thackeray did not do.

—or because someone had told Osric it was, and Osric had believed *him*, and Osric was a ferryman with debts like barnacles, and barnacles could be scraped.

Corven folded the paper away. He did not share the itch with Brandt. An itch was not evidence, and Corven had built a career on the difference.

"Note it," he said. "If the party is not where this report says they'll be, I want to know who was paid to say so."

Brandt wrote it down. She always wrote everything down. Sometimes Corven wondered what she did with the notebooks. He had decided years ago not to wonder too loudly.

* * *

They made the high pasture by late afternoon, and Corven dismounted the way he did everything—deliberately, with a small private negotiation involving his knees. The camp went up around him with the efficiency he paid for: picket lines, cook fire shielded in a cut bank, riders rotating on watch. Twelve men. He had asked for twelve specifically. Not twenty—twenty was an expedition, and expeditions generated reports, and reports generated questions in the valley. Twelve was a *patrol*, and patrols were weather.

The men were good. That was the trouble with the arrangement, if he was honest, and Corven was honest with himself at least once per expedition, usually at altitude where the lying got expensive. They were not thugs. Half of them were survey men and garrison regulars who believed, because Corven had arranged for them to believe it, that they were escorting a magistrate to recover stolen state property from smugglers. Which was even true, if you held the words up to the light at the correct angle. Corven had never much cared for lying. Lies required maintenance. He preferred to arrange the truth in rows, like orchard trees, so that anyone walking through it arrived naturally at the fruit he wanted them to find.

"Magistrate." The advance rider, a narrow young man called Doss, stood at attention by the fire. "You've read the report. The shelf camp—"

"Tell me about Osric."

Doss blinked. "The ferryman? Big fellow. Talked a great deal about fish."

"Did he take the money?"

"Yes, Magistrate."

"Did he look like a man who'd taken the money?"

Doss considered this with the seriousness the question deserved, which was more than he knew. "He looked like a man who'd taken the money and wished someone else had asked him to."

Corven nodded. That was the honest portrait of nearly every man he had ever bought. They didn't want to be traitors. They wanted to be *relieved*—of debt, of fear, of the grinding arithmetic of their lives. He offered relief. What they did with their own consciences afterward was, he felt, their own department. He had never once forced a man to betray anything. He had only made the alternatives expensive. The rest was the man's own weather.

"Four days," he said to the fire, or to Doss, or to the mountain generally. "The permits lapse at first snow. After that, the grants are uncontestable—twenty years of possession, surveyed, taxed, sworn. The chest becomes paper. Old paper. The kind bookbinders cut up for endpapers." He almost smiled at that. He had read Mirela Vance's file. Bookbinder. There was a certain poetry in the opposition, and Corven appreciated poetry he could use.

"And if they've gone to ground?" Doss asked. "The passes close. They'd be trapped up there all winter."

"Yes," Corven said. "And in the spring, they'd come down with the documents intact and thirty witnesses' worth of story. No. They're not going to ground. Thackeray is going over." He looked up at the col, a gray notch in the darkening ridge, already wearing its first rime like a man trying on a coat that would eventually eat him. "He has to be. Everything he is—every debt, every ghost, that woman with her ciphers—everything is on the far side of that pass before the snow. He's not running from us, Doss. He's running toward a deadline. That makes him predictable. Deadlines are the only honest thing left in this business."

* * *

He wrote his evening report by lantern, as he did every evening, in the small neat hand that had signed away a man's career seven years ago. *Patrol proceeding. Weather holding. No irregularities.* Three sentences. He had learned that the most dangerous documents were the short ones; they gave nothing back when interrogated.

He sat with the lantern a while after the ink dried, and permitted himself—this was the once-per-expedition honesty, arriving punctually at altitude—to review the shape of the thing he was doing.

A republic's mining concessions, built on forged transfers from a nation that no longer existed on paper and therefore could not complain. Twenty years of revenue. Fortunes. The garrison itself was paid, in part, out of those concessions. If the chest reached the exile council and the council made it sing, the arch came down—not on Corven alone, though he would be under it, but on the whole tidy structure: the survey office, the land registry, three ministers, two judges, and a widow in the capital whose late husband's fortune was, at its root, a line Corven had redrawn with a pen that had since been lost, which he sometimes thought of as the single merciful fact of the entire affair.

Against that: four people on a mountain. A ruined guide, an exile with ink-stained fingers, a ferryman with barnacled debts, and a boy.

It did not balance. He knew it did not balance. Four people against a republic's foundations was no contest at all, which was precisely why he could not let it proceed. It was not the four he feared. It was the arithmetic of *witnesses*—the way a single authenticated document, read aloud in the right room, could turn four people into four hundred, then four thousand, then a tribunal. Corven had spent his life managing numbers. He knew better than anyone that the smallest numbers were the ones that multiplied when you weren't watching.

He was not evil. He had met evil, in a customs office in Vessia, in the last weeks before the annexation—a man who enjoyed it, who hummed while he worked—and Corven had understood even then that he himself was a different animal entirely. He simply believed that some things, once built, should not be questioned, because the questioning would cost more than the crime. That the arch should stand because people lived under it. That a line on a map, once signed, was more real than the ground it misrepresented.

It was, he supposed, a philosophy. It had the advantage of never keeping him awake, except on mountains.

The lantern guttered. Outside, Brandt was walking the picket line, and he heard her low voice steadying a young rider's horse, and he thought, not for the first time, that she was the best officer he had ever had and the one he understood least. Eleven years of *yes, Magistrate*, and notebooks. He had a vague, unexamined plan to deal with her someday, the way one deals with a load-bearing wall: carefully, and never.

* * *

Dawn came up gray and hard, the temperature dropped like a verdict, and the mules knew before the men did. Corven stood at the edge of the high pasture with his hands behind his back, watching the col.

Snow in the high country, four days early. Not the true winter yet—the passes would hold—but a warning shot. The mountain, too, kept books, and was calling in its own debts.

"Magistrate." Brandt beside him, breath steaming. "Riders report movement on the survey route. Distant. A column of four, maybe five, moving well. Faster than the report suggested."

"Faster." Corven turned the word over. "The report said the woman was not a climber."

"She isn't, by file. But someone's carrying her, or teaching her, or—" Brandt paused, and for the first time in eleven years Corven heard something almost like an opinion enter her voice, disguised as a fact. "Or the report was wrong."

"Or," Corven said pleasantly, "the report was *arranged*."

He watched the notch of the col, empty at this distance, full of four people he could not see. Somewhere up there, Rafael Thackeray was doing what Thackeray did—reading the mountain the way Corven read a ledger—and somewhere behind Thackeray, a ferryman was carrying a secret the size of a debt.

"Break camp," Corven said. "We ride for the shelf camp by noon. And Brandt—send a runner ahead to the narrows below the pass. Not to engage. To watch. I want to know every soul who passes through that canyon, and I want to know it before they know we know."

"Yes, Magistrate."

She turned to go, and he added, because it was true and because true things, arranged in rows, were his whole religion:

"Four days. The weather's coming, the permits are dying, and everything that man has spent his life hiding is about to be worth nothing or everything. There's no third column in that ledger." He pulled his gloves on, finger by finger. "We're not chasing them anymore, Sergeant. We're the front. And fronts don't hurry. They just arrive."

Brandt said, "Yes, Magistrate," and went to give the order, and if her voice had a fraction less gravel in it than usual, Corven—busy being the weather—did not notice at all.

What he also did not notice, because no report had reached him yet, was that young Tam had already found the ferryman's messages.
