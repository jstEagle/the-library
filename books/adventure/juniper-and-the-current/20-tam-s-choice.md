## 20. Tam's Choice

Tam had learned three things about secrets in his seventeen years, and all three he'd learned from the ferry.

First: secrets were heavy. Not heavy like a crate of nails, but heavy like wet rope—you didn't notice the weight until you'd been carrying it all day, and by then your shoulders had memorized the shape of it.

Second: secrets leaked. Always. Slowly, usually, through the seams, the way the Serrano leaked through the ferry's oldest planks no matter how much pitch Osric slapped on them every autumn.

Third—and this was the one that mattered tonight—secrets had a smell. Or maybe it wasn't a smell exactly. Maybe it was a change in the air, the way you could stand on the landing and know a storm was coming down the gorge before you saw a single cloud, just from the way the water went flat and green and quiet, like it was holding its breath.

Osric had been holding his breath for two days.

* * *

Tam noticed it first at dinner. The party had made camp in the lee of a granite shoulder above the tree line's last stubborn pines, and Mirela had cooked the barley porridge that was becoming, by universal and unspoken agreement, the worst meal any of them had ever eaten eleven nights in a row. Rafael had eaten theirs without complaint, the way Rafael did everything—like a man performing an act of quiet penance for the crime of existing. Mirela had eaten hers while reading a document by firelight, frowning at it as though the porridge and the document were both personally insulting her. And Osric—

Osric had barely touched his.

That was the first wrong thing. Osric Thane, who had once eaten an entire boiled marmot on a dare and then eaten a second one on a principle, who described food the way other men described women ("she was gamey, but she had character"), pushing his bowl away half-finished, was like the river running backward. Tam had stared at him across the fire until Osric caught him looking, and then Osric had smiled—big, easy, the smile of a man with nothing on his mind but the next meal—and Tam had smiled back, because that's what you did with Osric, and had thought: *that's the second wrong thing. He smiled too fast.*

The third wrong thing came an hour later, when Tam got up in the dark to see to his water skin and found Osric's bedroll empty.

Now, in fairness, a man of Osric's age was entitled to get up in the night. Tam himself got up in the night, usually twice, a fact he'd been careful never to mention in front of Mirela because she would have found a way to make it educational. But when Tam came back from the rocks, he paused at the edge of the firelight—and there was Osric, twenty paces off, standing very still at the edge of the dark, holding something small in his cupped hands. And the way he was holding it—not reading it, not looking at it, just *holding* it, the way a man holds a coal he's decided not to throw and not to keep—

Tam knew that posture. He'd seen it on his father, the year the ferry license came up for renewal and the guild clerk wanted a little something extra under the table. His father had stood exactly that way over a purse of coin for three days before paying the clerk and never telling a soul, and he'd carried that coal in his hands, in his shoulders, in the flat green quiet of his voice, until the day he died.

Tam went back to his bedroll and lay staring up at the stars with his heart going like a ferry wheel in flood.

* * *

He didn't confront Osric the next morning. He wanted to—his whole body wanted to, the way a dog wants to chase the thing that startled it—but Rafael had said a thing on the second day of the climb that Tam had turned over in his mind maybe four hundred times since. They'd been roping up above the scree, and Tam had asked how you knew when a rock was going to come loose, and Rafael had said, "You don't. You test everything like it's already falling. And you don't yank on it. You lean your weight on it slow, and you feel what it does."

So the next morning Tam leaned.

He leaned by volunteering to walk with Osric at the rear of the line, which was unusual, because the rear was where the loose rock was and Tam had a well-documented policy of being nowhere near it. He leaned by asking Osric questions about the ferry—about the new winch, about whether old Marta's boy could be trusted with the toll box, about the winter moorings—and watching Osric answer. And Osric answered well. Too well. Every answer smooth and quick and generous, the way a plank is smooth when someone's been sanding it all night.

And then, at the midday halt, Tam leaned harder.

"Osric," he said, casual as a dropped mitten, "when we get back, you'll be square with Rafael, yeah? The debt?"

Something crossed Osric's face. It was quick—quicker than the flick of a trout's tail—but Tam had grown up reading that river, and he read it.

"Square as a coffin lid," Osric said, and laughed, and the laugh was the flattest, greenest, quietest sound Tam had ever heard him make.

* * *

He found the proof that evening, and he found it by accident, which was somehow worse, because Tam had been telling himself all day that he was wrong, that he was being a suspicious little ferry-rat, that Osric Thane had pulled Rafael Thackeray out of a frozen river two years ago and fed him and hid him and never once asked for anything but what was owed—

Tam had drawn the short straw for firewood. He'd gone down into the pines with the axe, and on the way back up he'd cut the switchback, the way Rafael had told them all not to, and he'd come up behind the big boulder at the bend where the party had stopped for lunch, and Osric was there, and Osric was not gathering wood.

Osric was standing with his back to the trail, and in his hands was a strip of paper, and he was feeding it to the fire he'd built in the hollow between the stones—a small, careful fire, the kind of fire a man builds when he's been building careful fires for days.

Tam stood absolutely still behind the boulder, which was a stupid place to stand, because if Osric turned around there was nowhere to go, and Tam's policy on loose rock applied double to being caught eavesdropping on a man holding an axe's worth of firewood and a burning secret.

But Osric didn't turn. He was watching the paper burn, and his face—Tam had never seen Osric's face do that. Osric's face was a public building: big, warm, open to all comers, slightly worse for wear. This face was a locked room. The firelight moved on it and the face didn't move back.

Before the paper curled all the way to ash, Tam read what he could of it, upside down, at twenty feet, in flickering light. He was a ferryman's boy; he could read a manifest at a gallop in the dark.

*...route confirmed... shelf camp... the tall one will vouch for... payment on delivery... C.*

He couldn't have said, afterward, how long he stood there. Long enough for the ash to go gray. Long enough for Osric to scatter the fire with his boot and stand there a moment longer with his head bowed, like a man at a graveside, and Tam thought—absurdly, furiously—*he's grieving it already. Whatever he's sold, he's standing at its grave before it's even dead.*

Then Tam did the thing he would think about for the rest of his life, the way you think about a rock you leaned on and felt move: he put down the firewood—deliberately, loudly, where Osric would see it from the trail—and he walked away.

* * *

He caught Osric alone an hour after full dark, at the horse lines, which weren't horses, because they couldn't afford horses, but which Tam privately called the horse lines anyway because "the mule lines" sounded like an insult and "the place where Buttercup stood" took too long.

"Buttercup" was the mule. She was gray, and she had opinions.

Tam had brought two cups of Mirela's tea, which was less tea than hot water that had been threatened with barley, and he handed one to Osric and said, "You look like a man who needs to talk to somebody too stupid to understand him."

Osric took the cup. "That's the nicest thing anyone's said to me in a week."

"I found your fire," Tam said.

And there it was. The flat green water. Osric didn't move, didn't spill the tea, didn't even blink—Tam had to give him that, the old man's hands were steady as a mooring post—but the air between them changed, went flat and quiet, storm-quiet, and Tam stood in it and made himself not look away.

"Did you, now," Osric said.

"The boulder on the switchback. The paper with the C at the bottom." Tam made himself say it plainly, because he'd learned from Rafael that you tested things by leaning your whole weight on them, and half-measures were how men fell. "Shelf camp. The tall one will vouch. Payment on delivery."

The silence went on long enough that Buttercup, sensing drama, leaned her head over the picket line and breathed fish-and-hay into Tam's ear, which was either solidarity or an attempt to eat his hair.

"Say it plain, boy," Osric said at last. His voice wasn't angry. That was the terrible thing. It was tired. "Say what you think you know."

"I think Corven's men reached you. I think they offered to wipe the debt—Rafael's debt, your debt, the whole ugly ledger—for the route to the high camp." Tam's throat was tight and his eyes were hot and he made himself keep going. "I think you've been writing to them since the gorge."

Osric looked at the tea. He looked at the fire across the camp, where Mirela was bent over her documents and Rafael sat watch with the compass in his hand, turning it over and over like a man worrying a tooth.

"Your father," Osric said, "died owing me eleven crowns. You know that?"

"I know it."

"Never asked you for it. Never will." Osric turned the cup in his big hands. "You know why I pulled Thackeray out of the river? Everyone thinks it was for the debt. That I ferried him across and the crossing put him in my book and that's the whole story." He shook his head. "I pulled him out because twenty years ago, before you were born, I ran cargo on the Vessian side of the range. Before the annexation. And there was a man—a customs officer, Vessian, honest as a plumb line, the only one on the river—who caught me twice and fined me twice and let me go twice, because he said a ferryman with a family shouldn't hang for feeding one." Osric's voice did something rough. "Rafael Thackeray has that man's eyes. Or I decided he does. At my age, boy, the difference between seeing a thing and deciding a thing is mostly weather."

"Osric—"

"I took Corven's coin," Osric said, flat and green and quiet. "First payment came by runner at the gorge. There's more coming at the shelf camp. And when it's done, the book closes, and I'm a free man, and Rafael Thackeray goes up a dead-end shelf in the snow and Corven's riders come down the real route, and nobody hangs, and everybody's fed, and it's the best bargain I ever made and I hate it more than I have ever hated anything in my life." He looked up, and his eyes were wet and furious. "There. Plain. Are you happy?"

Tam discovered that he was shaking. Not from cold.

"Why are you telling me this?" he whispered. "You could've lied. You lie beautiful, Osric, everybody says so."

"Because you put down the firewood where I'd see it," Osric said, "instead of running to Thackeray with it. Because you came at me with tea instead of a rope." He laughed, and it cracked in the middle. "Because you're seventeen and you already know something it took me fifty years to learn, which is that you catch more flies with a cup of terrible barley tea than with an accusation."

"What are you going to do?"

And here was the thing Tam would carry, the coal in his own hands now, the weight in his shoulders: Osric looked at him for a long time, and the old public-building face came back, and behind it Tam could see the locked room, and the man in the locked room was pacing, and couldn't stop pacing, and knew it.

"I'm going to sleep on it," Osric said. "That's what I keep telling myself. One more night, and I'll sleep on it, and in the morning I'll be the man I decided I was." He drained the tea and grimaced at it with genuine feeling. "Your Mirela makes tea like a grudge."

"She's not my Mirela."

"Boy, I've run a ferry for thirty years. I know cargo when I see it being loaded." He clapped Tam on the shoulder—his hand was warm, and heavy, and it trembled, just slightly, the way wet rope trembles—and walked off toward his bedroll.

Tam stood with the mule in the dark.

He should wake Rafael. That was the plain arithmetic of it. A man had confessed treachery; you woke the guide; the guide decided. Tam was seventeen and a ferry-hand and nobody, and nobody's job was to carry this.

But Osric had pulled Rafael out of a river. Osric had fed a stranger for a winter on credit and hope. Osric had stood at a graveside tonight for a thing that wasn't dead yet—and Tam, leaning his weight slow against the rock of it, had felt it move.

*He hasn't thrown the coal. He's holding it.*

Tam lay down and did not sleep, and somewhere past the middle of the night he heard, very softly, footsteps stop beside his bedroll, and Osric's voice, low, not meant to wake him—which was a lie, because Osric knew exactly how lightly Tam slept, had known since Tam was six years old and fell out of the ferry at every wave.

"Boy," the voice said, "if it comes to it—don't let me be the reason the tall one doesn't get home."

Then the footsteps went away, and Tam lay in the dark with his heart going like a wheel in flood, and above the camp the stars were going out one by one behind clouds coming down the gorge, and far to the south, where the shelf camp lay under its first thin snow, he could almost hear it: hooves. Many hooves. Climbing.
