## 21. The Glacier Crossing

The glacier woke before we did.

I heard it from the tent—a long groan under the ice, like an old man turning over in bed and complaining about it—and lay still for a moment, listening the way you listen to a horse in the night, deciding whether the sound is nothing or everything. The ice settled. Somewhere deep below us, a meltwater channel swallowed something whole and went on about its business.

"Is that normal?" Tam asked from his corner of the tent, in the voice of a boy trying very hard not to sound afraid.

"That's the glacier saying good morning," I said. "It's rude not to answer."

"What do I say?"

"Nothing. It's a glacier. It's heard better jokes than yours."

Mirela was already up outside, her breath standing in the air like a small ghost that had followed her out of her dreams. She had the stove going and water heating for the oats, and she'd laid out the rope in three neat coils, flaked so it wouldn't kink—she'd watched me do it two mornings running and now she did it better than me, which I chose to find charming rather than humiliating.

Osric sat on his pack eating hard bread with the expression of a man attending his own funeral and finding the eulogy generous. He'd been quiet since Windrow Col. Quiet for Osric was a bad sign, the way a quiet river is a bad sign—it meant depth where you'd expected shallows.

"Eat," I told them all, because that was the morning's first job. "Today is long, and the glacier doesn't care how strong your opinions are if your legs give out."

"And what does the glacier care about?" Tam asked, mouth full.

"Rope work," I said. "And nothing else."

* * *

I'll say this plainly, because Rafael Thackeray has lied enough in one life: glaciers are the most honest country a guide will ever cross, and the least forgiving. A mountain warns you. A mountain shows you its loose rock, its rotten snow bridges, its moods, days in advance, if you know how to read it. A glacier hides everything. Its dangers live underneath, wearing a pretty white lid, and the only defense is procedure—distance between travelers, rope taut but not tight, axes ready, and every step tested before trusted.

We roped up at first light: me on point, then Mirela, then Tam, then Osric at the rear with the second rope coiled over his shoulder for rescue work. The chest rode in a waterproof wrap lashed high on my pack, because I wanted it where I could feel it against my shoulders, the way you carry a sleeping child or a grudge.

Mirela checked my knots twice without asking permission. The second time I let her finish before I said anything.

"They're fine."

"I know," she said. "I'm checking anyway."

"That's not how trust works."

"That's exactly how trust works," she said, tugging the knot snug with two fingers. "Trust is checking anyway and being glad when it holds." She looked up at me then, and there was frost in her lashes and something warmer behind her eyes, and I understood that we were no longer talking about knots, and possibly never had been.

"Move out," I said, gruffly, like a man who hadn't just been seen straight through.

The glacier took us gently for the first hour. It was a river of ice poured down between two shoulders of black rock, its surface wind-scoured into ridges and hollows, blue in the shadows and blinding white everywhere else. We moved in the rhythm I'd taught them on the gorge—step, probe, weight, step—and the rope between us paid out and gathered like something breathing. Tam sang under his breath, some ferry-song about a girl with a crooked smile, and Osric told him to shut up, and Tam sang quieter instead of stopping, which was the correct negotiation, and even Osric's mouth twitched.

Then the surface changed.

It's hard to explain to people who haven't done it—the way a glacier goes from sounding solid to sounding hollow under your boots. A drum becomes a door. I stopped, probed ahead with my axe, and felt the shaft sink through six inches of crust into nothing.

"Snow bridge," I said. "Roped movement from here. Nobody steps where I don't step."

"How do you know where's safe?" Tam whispered, as though the ice might overhear and take offense.

"You don't know. You find out. That's why we're tied together." I turned and made sure each of them heard the next part. "If someone goes in, everyone drops flat, away from the hole, and braces. The rope holds or nobody holds. There's no third option, so don't go looking for one."

"Cheerful," said Osric.

"I'm a cheerful man."

"You're a funeral with legs," Osric said, "but you're our funeral," and he spat ceremonially to seal it, which on a glacier means your spit freezes before it lands and you have to pretend that's what you intended.

* * *

Midmorning, the glacier showed us its teeth.

It began as a change in the light—the white ahead of us dimmed, went gray-blue, and I realized we were walking into a field of crevasses wide enough to swallow the whole party and polite enough to paper themselves over with last winter's snow. I could read them by the sag in the surface, the faint dish where the snow was thinning, the way the wind had sculpted little lips around the edges. But reading them and avoiding them were different trades. The safe line threaded between blue mouths wide as barn doors, and twice I backtracked us fifty yards to find a crossing that didn't make my stomach climb into my throat.

At the worst of it, we crossed a bridge no wider than a coffin. I went first, probing every footfall, and called Mirela across. She came lightly, precisely, eyes on her feet, and stood breathing hard on the far lip.

Tam froze halfway.

He didn't panic—not exactly. He just stopped, one boot on the bridge, and I saw the moment the arithmetic finished in his head: all that blue underneath, hundreds of feet of it, and him balanced on winter's thinnest promise.

"Tam." I kept my voice level, the voice I used on frightened clients, the voice I'd once used on myself. "Look at me. Not down. Me."

His eyes came up. They were enormous.

"The bridge held me. It held Mirela. Ice doesn't care who's heavier—it cares where you step. Step where I stepped. Left foot on the gray patch. Now."

He stepped. Then again. And again, and then he was across and sitting down hard on the safe side, laughing in that shaky way people laugh when their body needs to spend fear somewhere and laughter is cheapest.

"You walked on a coffin," Osric told him, passing over without ceremony. "Most people wait till they're dead."

* * *

It happened an hour past noon, and it happened fast, the way everything important does.

We'd come off the worst of the crevasse field onto a long gentle slope, and I confess—I relaxed. Half a degree of relaxation. The kind a guide isn't allowed. The snow here looked thick, wind-packed, honest, and I gave it my full weight without probing, because we were making time and time was the one thing Corven couldn't sell us more of.

The world opened beneath me.

There was no dramatic crack, no movie-moment warning. One instant I was walking; the next, the snow was gone and I was falling through cold blue silence, the rope snapping taut above me, my axe handle cracking against the ice wall as I spun. I got my arms jammed across the shaft of the axe, wedged myself against the far wall of the crevasse, and stopped—swinging gently, forty feet down a throat of ancient ice, breath booming in my own ears.

Above me, muffled through the lid I'd fallen through, shouting. Good shouting—commanded, organized shouting, which meant somebody up there was doing their job.

"Rafael!" Mirela's voice, stripped of everything soft.

"Alive!" I called up. "Wedge is good! Don't come to the edge—all of you, back from the lip!"

Because here was the ugly truth of crevasse rescue: the rescuer at the edge is the next casualty. Every instinct screams run to the hole, look down, help—and every year the mountains collect the people who listen to that instinct.

"Osric!" I shouted. "You know the work?"

A pause. Then his voice, dry as chalk: "Boy, I've hauled drunk ferrymen out of the Serrano in flood season. Talk to me."

"Anchor behind you—axe buried flat, deadman style. Tam, flatten yourself and brace on the rope, feet against anything solid. Mirela—" I hesitated, because the arithmetic was brutal. "Mirela, you're lightest and steadiest. You'll need to approach the edge on your belly, keep the rope running smooth over the lip. Everyone else holds."

Silence above. Then her voice, closer, calm in a way I knew cost her something: "Tell me what to do."

I talked her through it while I kicked steps into the near wall and climbed, hand over hand, the rope burning through my gloves. It's strange, hanging in a crevasse. The light comes down in shafts, blue and holy, and the ice around you is older than every nation that ever drew a border on it—older than Vessia, older than the republic that ate it, older than any permit Corven would kill to protect. Hanging there, I thought: this ice doesn't care whose documents these are. It would swallow the chest and all of us and keep our secret forever, and be neither merciful nor cruel about it.

That thought should have comforted me. Mostly it made me angry. I climbed harder.

My head broke through the collapsed edge of the hole just as Mirela reached it—flat on her belly, rope running over her shoulder, her face inches from mine, framed against the sky.

"Hello," I said, because apparently my mouth handles terror with courtesy.

"Hello," she said. Her hands found my wrists and gripped. "You're heavy."

"You're supposed to say I'm worth it."

"You're worth it," she said, without a flicker of hesitation, and hauled.

Between her pull, Osric's anchor, Tam braced like a fence post, and my own climbing, I came up over the lip in a graceless avalanche of arms and elbows and lay gasping on the snow with four faces staring down at me.

Nobody spoke. Then Tam said, weakly, "You dropped the chest."

I went cold all over. My hands flew to my shoulders—

Empty. The lashings had torn loose in the fall. I turned, lunged toward the broken lip, and there it was: the chest, snagged ten feet down on a shelf of old ice, half over the edge of the shelf, swinging by its strap against a spur of rock. One swing further left and it would have gone into the blue dark, down whatever throat led to the bottom of the glacier, and twenty years of theft would have been sealed under a hundred feet of moving ice until the snout coughed it out in fifty years' time, too late for anyone.

"It's caught," Mirela said. She was already re-tying the rope end around her waist. "Lower me."

"Mirela—"

"You're heavier than me and you've just spent your luck. Lower me." She met my eyes, and hers were absolutely steady. "You taught us the rope work. Trust is checking anyway. Trust me."

So I anchored, and Osric and Tam paid her out, and she went down into the blue light of the crevasse like something returning to a country she'd been exiled from—smooth, controlled, unafraid in a way that broke my heart a little, because I knew where she'd learned to be unafraid, and it wasn't anywhere gentle.

She hooked the strap, freed it from the spur, wrapped the rope twice through the handle, and called up, "Haul!"

We brought her up with the chest strapped to her back, and when she cleared the lip, Tam grabbed her arm and Osric gripped her shoulder, and I—well. I took her face in both my mittened hands, which was as close as a man like me gets to kneeling, and I said, "Thank you," and the words came out rougher than I meant them, carrying more than they were built to carry.

She smiled, cheeks scarlet with cold and effort. "The documents survive. You survive. Good day's work." Then, briskly, before either of us could drown in the moment: "Re-tie the chest. Double lashings. And Rafael—" she tapped my chest, right over the heart, "—probe *every* step. Even the honest-looking ones."

"Yes, ma'am."

"*Especially* the honest-looking ones."

"Yes, ma'am."

Osric cleared his throat with the delicacy of a landslide. "If the two of you are finished, some of us are holding a rope in a wind."

* * *

We crossed the rest of the glacier in the gold light of late afternoon, and something had changed in the rope between us. I can't describe it better than that. Rope is an honest metaphor because rope is an honest object: it connects, and it holds, and after it has held once—at the worst moment, with everything depending on it—you stop thinking of the other people on it as passengers. They become, in the oldest sense of the word, company. The people you share your bread with. The people whose weight you carry and who carry yours.

Tam walked taller. He'd held. He knew he'd held, and it sat on him like a new coat, slightly too big, that he'd grow into.

Osric hummed. Actually hummed—an old Serrano working song, tuneless and contented—and when Tam looked at him in astonishment, Osric said, "Shut up," without heat, and kept humming.

And Mirela walked beside me whenever the rope's spacing allowed, and once, crossing a broad safe stretch where procedure loosened, her glove found mine and stayed there for perhaps thirty seconds, which on a glacier, roped, in wind, with witnesses, is roughly equivalent to a sonnet.

Neither of us said anything. There was a whole language we weren't speaking yet, and both of us knew it, and both of us were letting it gather like snow on a ledge—patiently, beautifully, with the full knowledge that ledges are temporary things.

"We should talk," she said at last, quietly, eyes on the ice ahead. "Really talk. When we stop."

"I know."

"There are things I've never told anyone. About that night. About why I was the one who left Vessia alive."

"I know," I said again, uselessly, and then, because she deserved better than useless: "I have things too. Things I've carried since the pass. Since Thackeray."

Her hand tightened around mine, once, and let go as the rope drew taut and the spacing closed. "Tonight, then. Whatever shelter the mountain gives us."

"Tonight," I agreed.

Behind us, the sun dropped behind the western shoulder and set the whole glacier burning rose-gold, every crevasse suddenly visible, every hidden danger lit up honest for one brief minute—as if the ice wanted us to see, before dark, exactly what we'd survived and exactly how much of it remained.

Ahead, the clouds along the northern horizon had begun to build—high, hard, wind-torn clouds with the particular bruised color I'd spent twenty years learning to dread.

Osric saw them too. He stopped humming.

"That's weather," he said.

"That's weather," I agreed, and put my head down, and lengthened our stride toward the shadowed flank of the mountain, where—if the mountain was kind, and the mountain owed me nothing—we might find snow enough and stone enough to dig ourselves a roof before the sky came down.
