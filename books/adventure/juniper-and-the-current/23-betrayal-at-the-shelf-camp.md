## 23. Betrayal at the Shelf Camp

Osric Ferrow had spent fifty-two years learning exactly how much trouble a man could carry and still walk upright. It was a ferryman's education. You learned it on the water: how many crates, how many passengers, how much guilt stacked on the stern before the gunwales kissed the current and you started taking on green water.

By his own reckoning, he was now three days past capsizing.

He sat at the edge of the shelf camp with a tin cup of snowmelt tea going cold in his hands, watching Rafael Thackeray check rope coils by firelight like a man who hadn't been dead to the world for two years. Tam was asleep with his mouth open, dreaming whatever nineteen-year-olds dreamed—probably glory, probably breakfast. The woman Vance was awake, of course. She was always awake. She sat against her pack turning pages of that cipher-book by firelight as if the mountain were a library and she had all the time in the world.

And Osric sat there with Corven's message folded into his boot, pressed against his ankle bone like a stone he'd swallowed whole.

*Betray them at the high camp. Your debt vanishes twice over.*

Twice over. He'd turned that phrase around in his head for two days now, looking for a crack in it. Twice over meant the eleven crowns Rafael owed him, plus whatever Corven imagined a man's soul went for these days. Osric had done the arithmetic the way he did everything—slowly, honestly, and with a flask involved—and the answer kept coming out wrong no matter which way he divided it.

The trouble was, he liked them.

That was the whole disaster of it. If he'd been guiding strangers, men whose names he'd forget by spring, it would have been simple business. But Thackeray had carried him half a mile on his back when his knee went out in the gorge, grumbling the entire time about Osric's weight being "mostly opinion." And the girl—Vance—had mended his coat sleeve without being asked, using thread she'd clearly been saving for something important, and when he'd thanked her she'd said, "It's not charity, ferryman. It's maintenance. You're equipment."

He'd laughed so hard he'd scared a ptarmigan off the scree.

You couldn't sell people who called you equipment. There were rules. Unwritten ones, admittedly, but Osric had built his whole life on unwritten rules, because written ones could be confiscated.

* * *

The rider came at dusk.

Osric heard him first—the particular wrongness of one hoofbeat where there should be none—and was on his feet before his mind caught up with his knees. He moved down the slope away from camp, casual as a man answering a call of nature, which was the oldest and most reliable lie in the mountains.

The rider waited in the shadow of a rock fin: a lean man in Corven's grey, frost in his mustache, a face like a ledger with nothing interesting entered in it. Sergeant's pins. He didn't draw. He didn't need to. The magistrate's men never needed to; they just stood there radiating paperwork until you felt arrested.

"Ferrow," the rider said.

"That's me. Though I answer to 'hey, ferryman' and occasionally to 'you old reprobate,' depending on the company."

"Magistrate Corven extends his regards."

"He can keep them. I've got nowhere to put them." Osric held out his hand anyway, palm up, because a man might as well see what was being offered before he refused it. "Let's look at this regard, then."

The sergeant placed a folded paper in it. Then a small purse that clinked with the specific, unmistakable music of silver—not promise-money but payment-money, the kind you got after, except here it was, before, which told Osric exactly how little Corven trusted him and exactly how much Corven wanted this done.

"The route," the sergeant said. "Where do they cross above the icefall? Where does Thackeray mean to come down?"

"And if I tell you?"

"You're a free man. Debt forgiven. Passage home paid. And the magistrate remembers his friends."

"The magistrate remembers his friends," Osric repeated. "Aye, I've heard what happens to the magistrate's friends. They get remembered right into shallow graves along the Serrano. He's got a whole churchyard of friends nobody visits."

The sergeant's expression did not change, which was itself an answer. "Ferrow. You're a smuggler and a debtor. Nobody in that camp is your friend. The woman is Vessian. The boy is a ferry-hand with more loyalty than sense. And Thackeray—" he paused, delicately, the way a butcher pauses over the good cut, "—Thackeray is a dead man walking who doesn't know it yet. You can drown with him or stand on the bank. You've always struck me as a man who likes dry boots."

Osric looked down at his boots. They were, in fact, soaked through. Had been for three days.

"There's a trick to it," Osric said slowly, and heard himself stepping off the bank even though every sensible part of him screamed. "Thackeray won't take the obvious line down from the col. Too exposed. He'll swing east along the shelf—you see it there, the broad one below the seracs—and come down through the notch where the rock's red. Red rock, you can't miss it, looks like the mountain's blushing."

The sergeant produced a pencil and a scrap of paper. Wrote. *East shelf. Red notch.*

"Aye," Osric said. His mouth tasted like coins. "That's the way of it."

"You understand what happens if this is false?"

"I understand what happens either way, son. That's the joke of my life." He pocketed the purse anyway—he wasn't a saint, and silver spends in any weather—and watched the rider pick his way back down into the dark, careful and competent, a good horseman carrying bad news.

Then Osric stood alone on the shelf in the wind and discovered what he'd done.

* * *

Here is the thing about Osric Ferrow that nobody, including Osric, had ever fully admitted: he was a coward with an excellent memory for other people's kindnesses. He would lie, cheat, smuggle, haggle, and run—but he kept accounts. Not of money. Of debts that couldn't be counted in coin. The widow Brandt he'd ferried free for six years after her husband drowned. The time he'd pulled a rival ferryman's son out of the spring flood and never once mentioned it, not even drunk, which for Osric was heroic restraint.

Rafael Thackeray owed him eleven crowns. That was the account in silver.

But Rafael Thackeray had also, two years ago, stood on Osric's deck at midnight with a forged pass and shaking hands, and instead of taking the boat across alone—as any sensible fugitive would—had gone back up the bank to help a stranger's cart out of the mud, costing them both the tide window, and had said afterward only, "Sorry, Osric. Couldn't leave them stuck."

Eleven crowns against that. Against a coat-sleeve mended. Against a boy like Tam looking at Thackeray the way Osric had once looked at his own father, back when his father had still been worth looking at.

The purse in his pocket weighed maybe forty crowns. It weighed nothing at all.

* * *

He came back into camp whistling, which was his tell, and he knew it was his tell, and he whistled anyway because the alternative was coming back silent, which was a worse tell.

"Trouble?" Mirela Vance asked, not looking up from her book.

"Watered the horses. Talked to the stars. Stars are poor conversationalists." He sat heavily by the fire. "Thackeray. A question. Hypothetical-like."

Rafael glanced up. In the firelight his face had some of its old mountain-animal alertness, and Osric felt the familiar twist of it—that this was the guide everyone had sworn about, the one whose name had been dragged through every guild hall from here to the coast, and he was sitting here checking knots like the disgrace had never happened.

"Hypotheticals are free," Rafael said. "Ask."

"If a man were coming up behind us. Say, with horses and poor intentions. What route would you want him to think we'd taken?"

The fire popped. Tam stirred in his sleep. Mirela's page-turning stopped.

Rafael studied Osric for a long moment—not angry, not yet, just reading him the way he read weather, and Osric had never in his life felt so thoroughly forecast.

"Why do you ask, Osric?"

"Because a grey rider found me on the ridge an hour ago, and I told him we'd go east along the shelf and down through the red notch." He said it flat, all of it, because a confession delivered in installments was worse than useless. "And because I took his purse, and I'm giving it to the party fund, and before you decide what to do with me I want you to know the rest: the route I gave him is true as far as the shelf goes, and a lie the rest of the way. The red notch is a dead end. Sheer drop, rotten rock, no descent. I fished that stretch of country twenty years. There's no way down. Any riders who follow that line will spend tomorrow climbing into a box canyon and finding out what the mountain thinks of magistrates."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight and temperature.

Tam had woken somewhere in the middle of it and was sitting bolt upright, staring at Osric with an expression Osric recognized from mirrors: the look of a man watching his own opinion of someone collapse and rebuild simultaneously.

"You sold us," Tam said hoarsely.

"I took money to sell us," Osric corrected. "Then I declined to complete the transaction. It's a distinction, lad. Not a large one. But it's mine and I'm keeping it."

"Why?" That was Mirela, quiet, precise. "Why give the truth at all? Why not send them chasing shadows from the start?"

Osric turned his cup in his hands. "Because a lie with no truth in it gets found out fast, and then they come back angry and thorough. A lie wearing truth's coat walks right past them." He looked up. "And because if I lied clean, I'd be the man who betrayed you. This way I'm only the man who almost did. I've been both kinds of man in my life. The second kind can still look at himself in the morning, provided the light's bad."

Rafael hadn't spoken. He fed another stick to the fire, considered the flames, and finally said, "The purse."

Osric dug it out and set it on the pack between them. It landed with a satisfying clink.

"Forty-odd crowns," Osric said. "Blood money, pre-paid. Spend it on rope and food and consider it Corven's contribution to his own embarrassment. That's the best use of a magistrate's silver I know."

Mirela laughed—a short, surprised sound, quickly strangled, but real. Even Rafael's mouth twitched at one corner, which Osric had learned to count as a festival.

"You understand," Rafael said quietly, "that when they find the dead end, they'll know it was you. Corven doesn't forgive. He itemizes."

"Aye." Osric nodded slowly. "I've thought on that. Here's my thinking: a man my age has two choices about how he's remembered. He can die owing money, or he can die owing an apology. I've spent my whole life doing the first. Thought I'd try the second while I've still got the legs for it." He tapped his knee, the bad one, the one Thackeray had hauled him uphill on. "Besides. You carried me up that gorge, you great stubborn ghost. Nobody carries Osric Ferrow uphill and walks away clean. That's not debt. That's theology."

* * *

They moved camp within the hour, cold and dark, Rafael running the evacuation with the crisp economy of a man finally doing the thing his bones remembered. Osric packed in silence, and it was Tam who ended up beside him, cinching straps with furious competence.

"You should've told us sooner," Tam muttered.

"Aye."

"You should've told Corven no."

"Aye, that too." Osric sighed and slung his pack. "Lad, let me learn you something about men my age. We don't get to choose whether we're cowards. We chose that decades ago, and the choice set like concrete. All we get to choose, late in the day, is which direction we point the cowardice." He patted the boy's shoulder. "Tonight I pointed mine downhill, at Corven's riders, and it's carrying a map to a cliff. First useful thing it's ever done."

Tam chewed on that. "Does it count? The betrayal, I mean. If you unsold us?"

Osric looked back at the shelf camp, already vanishing into the dark, at the place where he'd traded his name for silver and then quietly stolen the name back.

"Not to the mountain," he said. "The mountain saw the whole transaction. But ask me again when the riders reach the red notch and find four hundred feet of nothing." He grinned, and it felt strange on his face, like a boot broken in on the wrong foot. "Then ask me again when Corven comes asking after me personally. Because he will, lad. And when he does—"

Below them, far down the valley, a lantern swung in the dark. One lantern, then two. Riders, moving toward the shelf, moving toward the route Osric had sold them, trusting a smuggler's word the way Corven trusted everyone: completely, and as a tool.

"—when he does," Osric finished softly, "I intend to be standing next to Rafael Thackeray at the top of the world, holding the evidence that ends him. And I want a front-row view."

They climbed. Behind them, faint on the night air, came the sound of hooves finding the shelf trail—going east, going easy, going exactly where a guilty man had sent them.

Going, Osric thought, toward the prettiest dead end in the whole cursed range.

He only hoped the mountain would hold its blush long enough to finish the job.
