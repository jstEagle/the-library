## 24. The Pass Ahead of the Snows

The wind changed in the night.

Rafael woke in the grey hour before dawn with their face turned toward it the way a plant turns toward light, and knew before they opened their eyes what the change meant. The south wind that had pushed them up the gorge for six days had died, and in its place came something thin and clean from the northwest—the smell of distance, of ice fields, of weather still deciding whether to be kind.

They lay in the shelter of a granite overhang with Mirela's shoulder against theirs and listened to the mountain breathe. Somewhere below, Osric was snoring with the confidence of a man who had made his peace with whatever came next. Tam was on watch, or was supposed to be; Rafael could hear him talking softly to the mule, which was not watch behavior, but the mule seemed to appreciate it, and Rafael had learned long ago that morale sometimes mattered more than protocol.

*One more day,* Rafael thought. *Maybe less.*

Thackeray Pass sat at the head of the valley like a door left ajar—eighteen hundred feet of switchbacks above them, then the col itself, a saddle of shattered stone between two peaks that had been named for other people's ambitions. Rafael had crossed it eleven times in the years when crossing mountains was a profession instead of a penance. They had crossed it once badly, in the year everything ended, and had spent every day since walking away from it in one direction or another.

Now the whole road home led through it.

Rafael slid out from under the shared blanket without waking Mirela—a small mercy, since she slept like a scholar, deeply and with occasional muttering about margins—and went to stand at the mouth of the overhang where the world showed itself.

The sky was doing something complicated. Clouds stacked along the northern horizon in long gray shelves, orderly as books on a shelf, and beneath them the high country lay bare and glittering, scoured by last week's winds. It was beautiful the way a contract is beautiful: precise, binding, and full of clauses you'd better read twice.

"Snow's coming," said Tam, materializing at their elbow with the mule's lead rope in one hand and an expression of tremendous self-satisfaction in the other. "I felt it too. In my knee."

"Your knee is nineteen years old."

"My knee is *wise,*" Tam said. "Osric says ferry-men's knees know weather before ferrymen do. He says it's from standing in cold water."

"Osric also says he once out-drank a customs officer, a bear, and a bishop, in that order."

"He swears the bishop started it."

Rafael smiled despite themselves, which was becoming a habit, and habits were dangerous at altitude. They looked back down the valley—the way they'd come, the long white throat of it winding down toward the tree line, toward Juniper's Landing, toward Corven.

Because that was the arithmetic that wouldn't leave them alone. They had outrun the riders so far, but outrunning was not the same as escaping. Corven had twelve men and the southern approach, which was gentler but longer. Rafael had three companions, a mule, and the direct route, which was steeper but shorter. The mathematics favored them by perhaps a day—but only if the pass stayed open, and only if Corven hadn't found some third path that Rafael didn't know about, and only if the chest on the mule's back stayed intact, and only if the courier they were racing to meet was even there to be met.

A great many onlys. Mountains were made of them.

Mirela emerged from the overhang pulling her coat on, her hair flattened on one side in a way she would have corrected instantly if she'd known, and Rafael decided this information was hers alone to keep.

"You're staring at the north," she said.

"I'm negotiating with it."

"How's that going?"

"It's being very reasonable about the price and very vague about the terms."

She came to stand beside them, and together they watched the light come up the valley floor, gold climbing the walls of stone like water rising in a well. Below them, Osric emerged from his bedroll looking like a man who had been folded and stored overnight and was now being unfolded with complaints.

"Tea," he announced, in the tone of a man announcing a legal right. "There will be tea, and then there will be walking, and then, if the mountain has any decency, there will be a pass, and I will stand on it briefly, appreciate the view, and go back down to sea level where God intended people to live."

"The pass is at nine thousand feet," Rafael said.

"Then God and I have words to exchange."

* * *

They climbed.

It went the way good climbing goes, which is to say it barely felt like anything at all while it was happening—just the rhythm of boots and breath, the switchbacks unspooling beneath them, the party finding its pace the way a crew finds its stroke. Rafael led. Behind them came Tam with the mule, who had been named Bureaucracy by Osric on the grounds that she was slow, stubborn, and ultimately carried everything worth carrying. Behind Tam came Mirela, who climbed the way she did everything else—carefully, precisely, without a single wasted motion, as though the mountain were a difficult text she intended to read correctly rather than quickly. And behind Mirela came Osric, complaining in a steady undertone that Rafael had come to understand was actually a form of prayer.

By midmorning they were above the trees entirely, in the country of stone and lichen where the wind spoke first and everyone else asked permission. Rafael called the rest stops by the clock in their head, the old guild intervals, and noticed without saying so that nobody needed telling anymore. Tam checked the mule's feet without being asked. Mirela watched the sky the way Rafael did, reading the cloud shelves building in the north. Even Osric had stopped asking how much farther, which from Osric was roughly equivalent to a knighthood.

It was on the seventh switchback—the one that traversed beneath the leaning tower of rock the old guides had called the Deacon, because it disapproved of everyone—that the valley opened below them and Rafael made themselves look back.

The southern approach was visible from here: a pale thread of trail stitched along the far wall of the range, miles distant. And on it, small as punctuation marks, movement.

Rafael counted twice. Twelve riders, strung out in column, moving with the unhurried discipline of men who believed time belonged to them.

"There," they said quietly, and pointed, and watched the party gather at the rail of the switchback and look.

"They're two days behind us," Tam said. "Aren't they? You said two days."

"I said two days yesterday." Rafael studied the column. The riders had reached the point Rafael's route had covered a day and a half ago. "They've moved faster than I gave them credit for. Corven's pushing them hard."

"Can riders even cross the pass?" Mirela asked. "In snow?"

"Some of it. The upper narrows would take horses, barely, if the snow holds off. If it doesn't—" Rafael shrugged. "Corven didn't bring twelve men because he expected an easy ride. He brought twelve men because he intends to arrive regardless of what the mountain thinks about it."

Osric squinted down the valley for a long moment, and Rafael saw something move across his face—not fear exactly, and not guilt either, though both lived somewhere in the neighborhood. Calculation. Osric was always calculating; it was the ferryman's native tongue.

"We could push harder," Osric said. "Skip the long lunch. Walk into the dark."

"And break an ankle in the dark on scree," Rafael said. "No. We keep our pace. Tired climbers make mistakes, and mistakes up here are permanent." They turned back to the trail. "We have the day. We use the day."

But all through the next hours, part of Rafael's mind stayed down in that valley, watching the column crawl, doing the arithmetic again and again as though repetition might change the sum. A day ahead. Perhaps less now. The storm building in the north would close the pass behind them—which cut off Corven, yes, but also cut off any retreat, any rescue, any second chances. Once they crossed, they would be committed, sealed on the far side of the range with whatever waited there.

And what waited there was the other half of the problem, the half Rafael kept trying not to look at directly, the way you don't look straight at a sore tooth.

The exile council's courier was supposed to be waiting beyond the pass. That had been the plan Mirela had built, patient and clever, back in Juniper's Landing: carry the documents over the range, meet the courier at the way-station in the high valley, hand the truth across, done. But the plan depended on a man Rafael had never seen, keeping an appointment none of them could confirm, at a station that might be abandoned, might be watched, might have burned down in a bad winter for all anyone knew.

"What if he's not there?" Rafael had asked Mirela on the fourth night, and she had answered with terrible calm: "Then we keep going until we find someone who knows someone who knows him. The network exists, Rafael. I've trusted it with my life for twenty years. I'm trusting it with yours now too."

That was not an answer. That was faith wearing an answer's clothes. But Rafael had said nothing, because they had no better offer, and because watching Mirela speak about the network—with that fierce, guarded tenderness, the way other people spoke of family—made arguing feel like kicking a dog.

Now, climbing through the thin bright afternoon, Rafael let themselves think it plainly for the first time: *the courier might not come.* The storm might have held him on the far side. Corven's men might have caught him weeks ago. Or the message might simply have gone astray, the way messages do, and the council might be sitting in some valley town waiting for news that was currently strapped to a mule named Bureaucracy, ascending a switchback at four thousand paces an hour.

And behind them, patient as winter, the column climbed.

* * *

They came over the last shoulder of the climb as the sun dropped toward the western peaks, and there it was.

Thackeray Pass.

Rafael had prepared themselves for it—or believed they had—for six days, through the gorge and the burned village and the glacier and the long white silence of Windrow Col. They had told themselves it was just a saddle, just geography, just the lowest point between two high points, no different from a hundred others. Names were nothing. Names were ink.

It didn't work.

The pass opened before them like a held breath finally released: a broad saddle of gray stone maybe two hundred yards across, littered with the small debris of twenty years of travelers—cairns and cairn-ruins, a rusted iron pole that had once held a guide rope, scraps of color frozen into the rocks where prayer flags or pack straps or hope had come apart in the wind. Beyond it the land fell away northward into the high Vessian valleys, blue-shadowed and vast, and the evening light lay across it all like a benediction nobody had thought to cancel.

Rafael stood at the top of the final pitch and did not move.

They knew this stone. Not the way you know a place you've visited—the way you know a place where something happened to you. There, thirty yards out, was the shallow depression where the tents of the fatal expedition had stood, where Rafael had spent the worst night of their life listening to the wind and doing sums that refused to balance. There was the cairn—larger than most, carefully built, the one the guild had raised afterward, the one Rafael had never once visited in twenty years because visiting it would have required admitting they remembered exactly where it was.

Which, of course, they did. To the foot.

"Rafael?" Mirela's voice, gentle. Her hand, light between their shoulders.

"I'm fine," Rafael said, which was true in the way that a broken bone set crooked is fine—it functions, it carries weight, it simply never stops reminding you.

Tam came up beside them, flushed and wide-eyed, and for once said nothing at all, which from Tam was a speech. Osric arrived last, breathing like a bellows, planted his fists on his hips, and surveyed the pass with the air of a landlord inspecting a disputed property.

"Well," he said. "It's smaller than I imagined."

"Everything's smaller than you imagine," Tam said. "You imagined the bear was bigger, though. You tell that story different every time."

"The bear grew in the retelling. Bears do that. It's known."

Rafael laughed—actually laughed, the sound surprising them, cracking something loose in their chest—and the laugh carried across the saddle and came back off the far wall thinner, like an echo from another decade. Then they walked out onto the pass.

Their boots found the old trail without guidance. Twenty years, and the feet remembered. Rafael stopped at the tent depression, looked down at the stones, and let the memory come the way you let cold water come—fully, once, rather than by inches forever.

The expedition. Eleven clients, two assistant guides, and a magistrate's surveyor who had insisted, with documents and authority and that soft reasonable voice Rafael could still hear in nightmares, that the route be changed. *The eastern line. Better going. Trust me, Thackeray.* And Rafael—licensed, experienced, and fatally obedient to the idea that paper outranked judgment—had said yes.

Three days later, in the country beyond this pass, the ice bridge had failed under the clients' second rope team. Two dead. One of them Aldous Wren, the young surveyor's clerk who had argued hardest against the route change and been overruled by his own superior. And Rafael had carried the blame across the border and down into Juniper's Landing and through every year since: *the guide who took the bad line.* Blacklisted. Erased. A ghost with a compass.

They had believed it themselves, that was the thing. For twenty years they had believed the mountain's verdict, because the mountain doesn't lie, does it? The mountain only weighs.

"Whatever you're seeing," Mirela said quietly, beside them now, her eyes on the cairn, "you don't have to say it here."

"No," Rafael agreed. "But I might have to say it soon anyway. There are things you should hear before—" They stopped. Started again. "Before we're on the other side of this. Whatever this turns out to be."

Mirela looked at them then, fully, with those reader's eyes that missed nothing, and Rafael saw that she already understood there was a confession being scheduled, and had the grace not to open the book early.

"After the pass," she said. "When we're down. When there's wine."

"When there's wine," Rafael agreed, and discovered that having a deadline for your shame made it slightly easier to carry.

* * *

They made camp in the lee of the western wall, below a shelf of stone that broke the wind, and Rafael set the watches with more care than they'd used at any point on the journey. Because here was the truth of the pass, the truth Rafael had been carrying all day alongside the triumph: they had won the race, and winning it had delivered them into a box.

Behind them: Corven's column, a day back, maybe less, closing with every hour. Ahead: the descent into Vessian country, where the courier might or might not be waiting, and where—if he wasn't—they would be strangers in a lawless high valley with a blizzard coming and a magistrate's warrant effectively nailed to their backs. The storm would seal the pass behind them within a day of crossing, probably less. Whatever happened on the far side, there would be no going back for help, no retreat, no second attempt.

Everything they had—the documents, the proof, twenty years of stolen history, and four lives—was about to be committed to the far side of a closed door.

Rafael said as much around the fire, plainly, because these people had earned plainness. Tam's face fell in a way that made Rafael want to apologize to him personally for the entire concept of consequences. Osric chewed on it like gristle and finally said, "So we make sure the courier's there. Simple."

"It is not simple."

"It's simple in the way most things are simple," Osric said comfortably. "Difficult, but simple. Like rowing upstream. You just don't stop."

Mirela said nothing for a long moment, firelight moving on her face. Then: "There's a signal." She said it slowly, as if retrieving it from deep storage. "At the way-station. If the courier has come and gone safely onward to the council, there's a lantern hung in the north window. If he's there and waiting, two lanterns. If the station's dark—" She paused. "Dark means we decide for ourselves."

"Dark means we walk into the biggest decision of our lives with no information whatsoever," Rafael said.

"Yes," Mirela said. "That's what dark means."

"Wonderful," said Osric. "I've missed ambiguity. It reminds me of my marriage."

Nobody asked, and Osric looked pleased about it.

They turned in early. Rafael took first watch, wrapped in every layer they owned, sitting with their back against the stone while the stars wheeled overhead in the brutal clarity of altitude. To the north, the Vessian valleys lay invisible beyond the pass's edge, holding whatever they held. To the south, far below, Rafael fancied—though it was surely imagination, surely too far to see—that they could see fires. Small ones. Orderly. A column making camp and banking its cooking fires like a man banking a stove before bed.

Corven wasn't hurrying anymore. That was the detail that sat in Rafael's stomach like a swallowed stone. All day the column had moved fast; now it had stopped for the night at a sensible hour, at a sensible camp, with the unhurried patience of a hunter who has stopped worrying about the trail.

He knows where we're going, Rafael thought. He's always known. The pass is a funnel, and funnels are where hunters stop running and start waiting.

The wind shifted again in the small hours, and Rafael tasted it: moisture now, and iron. The storm had finished deciding.

Below the pass, far down the southern slopes, the first flakes came sifting through the starlight, dry and silent, beginning the long work of closing the door.

Rafael sat with the compass in their palm—the dead client's compass, the gift that had become a burden, the needle steady and indifferent—and watched the snow come, and thought: *One day. We have one day, and then the mountain takes the scales out of everyone's hands.*

From the darkness behind them, Osric's voice, quiet and unusually unguarded:

"Can't sleep either?"

"No."

Osric settled onto the stone nearby, a shape against the stars. For a while neither of them said anything, which was its own kind of company. Then Osric said, "You know what I keep thinking? Down there, when I looked at those riders." He paused. "I keep thinking I know how this ends. And I keep hoping I'm wrong."

Rafael turned to ask him what he meant—

—and froze, because down in the southern dark, where the column's fires had been, one fire had moved. Detached itself. Begun, small and steady, to climb.
