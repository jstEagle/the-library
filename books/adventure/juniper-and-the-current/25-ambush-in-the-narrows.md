## 25. Ambush in the Narrows

### Rafael

The Narrows announced itself the way bad news always did—quietly first.

Rafael heard it before they saw it: the river, which had been a companionable murmur all morning, suddenly dropped its voice. Water that had chattered over stones went silent and deep, sliding between canyon walls so close together that Mirela could have touched both sides with her arms outstretched. The trail narrowed to a shelf of gravel barely wide enough for two abreast. Above them, the rock rose four hundred feet in layered slabs, red and grey, like the pages of some enormous abandoned ledger.

"Thackeray," Osric said behind him, and there was something in the old ferryman's voice Rafael didn't like. Something too careful. "You've been quiet since the col."

"I'm saving my words for warmer weather."

"Mm." Osric shifted the rope coil on his shoulder. "Save one for me, then. In case I need it."

Rafael glanced back. Osric's face was doing what it always did—nothing much, a weathered mask of mild complaint—but his eyes were fixed on the canyon walls instead of the trail, and he was sweating despite the cold. Rafael filed it away the way he filed away loose scree and darkening clouds: a thing to watch.

They were eleven hours out of Thackeray Pass with a day to spare, and Rafael had let himself feel it—that dangerous, warm feeling of almost-made-it. The chest rode on Mirela's back now, wrapped in oilcloth, strapped tight. She'd insisted. *I carried nothing across the border when I was nine years old,* she'd said. *I'll carry this.* Tam walked ahead of Rafael, young legs eating the miles, occasionally pointing out things he thought Rafael might miss—a cairn, an eagle, a patch of old snow holding on in the shadow of a ledge—and every time he did it, Rafael felt a pang of something between pride and grief. The boy guided like it was a gift he was offering, not a job.

The Narrows ran half a mile, maybe less. Rafael had crossed it a dozen times. He knew there was no way around it, no high route, no alternative—the canyon simply swallowed the trail and gave it back on the far side. He knew, too, that a dozen riders could seal both ends of it in twenty minutes.

He knew all of this. And still, when the first stone came down, it took him by surprise.

It wasn't large—a chunk of sandstone the size of a loaf of bread—but it struck the trail ten feet ahead of Tam and burst apart, and the sound of it went through the canyon like a rifle shot, multiplying off the walls until it seemed the whole cliff face was speaking.

"Halt!" Rafael shouted. "Off the trail! Against the wall!"

Mirela was already moving, pressing herself flat to the rock, the chest between her body and the open air. Tam froze in the middle of the trail like a startled deer—which was exactly wrong—and Rafael grabbed his collar and hauled him back as a second stone came down where he'd stood, then a third, then a rain of smaller ones, pebbles hissing down like angry bees.

And out of the echo and the dust, from the mouth of the canyon behind them, came hoofbeats.

### Tam

Tam had imagined this moment, in the way boys imagine things—himself standing brave at some crossroads, saying the right words, doing the right thing while everyone watched. What he had not imagined was how fast it would happen. How little time there would be to be anyone at all.

They came out of the southern mouth of the Narrows at a canter: six riders, then four more behind them, spreading across the width of the canyon floor with the ease of men who had done this before, who had perhaps done nothing else for weeks. Their horses' breath steamed. Their faces under the hooded hats were ordinary faces, which somehow made it worse. These weren't monsters. They were employees.

"Down!" Rafael's voice again, cracked and fierce. "Weapons down, hands visible!"

Nobody in their party had weapons worth the name. Tam had a skinning knife. Osric had a boat hook he'd carried three hundred miles out of pure stubbornness. Rafael had a hatchet and forty years of mountain sense, none of which stopped bullets.

The lead rider reined in twenty feet away. He was lean and grey-stubbled, with the patient look of a man who had waited a long time in cold places, and Tam recognized him from Juniper's Landing—one of Corven's own garrison troopers, though he wore no colors now.

"Master Thackeray." The man's voice was almost courteous. "Magistrate Corven sends his regards."

"He should have sent them by letter," Rafael said. "I'd have burned it."

A few of the riders laughed. The lead man didn't. "The documents. Hand them over, and we ride on past you. Nobody needs to be late for winter."

"And if we don't?"

"Then somebody does need to be late for winter." The rider's eyes moved—not to Rafael, but along the line of them, counting, weighing—and stopped on Tam.

Tam felt it like a physical touch. He was standing closest to the open ground, he realized. He had frozen in the middle of the trail and never made it fully to the wall, and now he stood in plain view with his knife looking very small in his hand, and the rider was looking at him the way a man looks at a tool he hadn't expected to find useful.

"No," Mirela said. It came out low and absolute, and she stepped forward with the chest still strapped to her back, putting herself between Tam and the riders. "You want the papers. Take the papers. Leave the boy."

"The boy isn't the point," the rider said mildly.

"The boy is the point," said another voice, and Tam's stomach turned over, because he knew it.

Osric stepped out from against the wall. His hands were up, palms out, in the universal gesture of a man surrendering, but his eyes found Tam's and held them, and what was in them wasn't triumph or even guilt exactly—it was something more terrible than either. It was apology arriving too late to matter.

"They were going to take everything anyway," Osric said, to no one, to everyone. "This way nobody bleeds."

"You're the one who led them here." Mirela's voice shook—not with fear, Tam realized, but with fury. "The messages. The runner. It was you."

Osric flinched as if she'd struck him. "I owed money I couldn't pay to men who don't forgive. I'm sorry, girl. I'm sorry, boy. Sorry doesn't unring bells."

The lead rider swung down from his horse. "Enough. The chest."

For one moment—one long, ringing moment—nobody moved. The river slid dark and silent below the trail. Dust still drifted down the canyon walls. Tam could hear his own heart, absurdly loud, and beneath it the small sound of Mirela's fingers working at the straps of the chest, because Rafael had put one hand on her shoulder and given the smallest shake of his head, and she understood what he understood: six of them, ten riders, a canyon with no exit, and a boy standing in the open.

She unbuckled the straps. She set the chest on the gravel between them like a woman laying down a verdict rather than a burden.

The rider kicked it open with his boot. Papers. Oilcloth. The Vessian seal winking up at the grey sky. He crouched, rifled through it with quick professional hands, nodded once.

"That's all of it? No copies?"

"Why would there be copies?" Osric asked, too quickly.

The rider looked at him for a long moment. Then he straightened, and Tam saw the order arrive in the man's face before he spoke it—the small settling of the jaw, the glance at his sergeant's nod.

"Take the boy," the rider said.

* * *

### Mirela

Later—hours later, days later, she would think about it for the rest of her life—Mirela would understand that the whole ambush had been built around that sentence. Not the chest. The chest was paper; paper could be copied, hidden, remembered. But a hostage was leverage that couldn't be forged, and Corven, whatever else he was, understood leverage the way she understood bindings: thoroughly, and without sentiment.

What she felt in the moment was simpler and hotter. It was the feeling of being nine years old again, watching soldiers roll up the border post's map of Vessia while her mother gripped her hand too hard, and knowing that the world was about to become larger and crueler and that there was nothing—*nothing*—her small hands could do about it.

She had spent twenty years making sure her hands were never small again.

She moved before she decided to move. Later she couldn't even reconstruct the decision. One moment she was watching the two nearest riders walk their horses toward Tam, and the next she was in front of him, arms spread, the same stance she'd taken over the chest, and someone was going to have to go through her to get to the boy.

"Mirela." Rafael's voice, low, urgent. "Don't."

"They're children, Rafael. He's a *child*—"

"He's also alive," Rafael said, "and will stay that way if you let them take him without a fight. That's the arithmetic. I hate it. Do you hear me? I hate it more than you do. But it's the arithmetic."

She wanted to scream at him. She wanted to tell him that arithmetic was exactly what Corven used, that the whole theft of a nation had been conducted as arithmetic, permits and expirations and losses written down in neat columns, and that somewhere someone had to refuse to do the sums. But the riders were closing, and Tam's face had gone white as the snowfields above them, and the truth—the ugly, grinding truth—was that Rafael was right.

One of the riders dismounted. He was younger than the rest, nervous, and he took Tam's arm not roughly but not gently either, the way you handle something you've been told matters.

"It's all right," Tam said. And the astonishing thing, the thing that broke Mirela's heart clean through, was that he said it to *them*. To her and Rafael and even Osric, standing there with his confession still hanging in the cold air. "It's all right. Get the papers through. That's the mission, right? That's what you said a mission is—what you carry, not what you keep."

"Tam—" Rafael started forward and the lead rider's hand came up, and two carbines came off slings with a sound like knives being drawn, and everyone in the canyon understood the new rules at once.

"He rides with us to the valley," the lead rider said. "He eats, he stays warm, he's returned when the magistrate confirms the documents are the only ones in existence. You have my word."

"Your word," Mirela said. "From Corven's garrison."

"My word is better than his," the rider said, and to his credit—or something like it—he seemed to mean it.

They put Tam on a horse. He didn't struggle. He looked back once, only once, and found Rafael's eyes, and lifted his chin in a small, deliberate nod—the nod of a ferry-hand who has watched a hundred crossings and knows that sometimes the current takes something, and the crossing goes on anyway.

Then the riders wheeled, and the hoofbeats receded down the canyon, taking the light with them, and the Narrows was silent except for the deep dumb voice of the river.

* * *

### Osric

Osric sat down on the gravel. Not gracefully. His knees simply ended the argument, and he sat, and he looked at the empty place on the trail where the boy had been.

He had told himself a story, these last three days. It had been a good story, well-made, the kind a man can live inside: *Corven gets his papers, the debt dies, nobody bleeds, the boy comes home in a week fat and sassy and full of complaints.* He had believed it the way you believe a fire will warm you without burning the house down. He had even, in the black honest hour before dawn, known it was a lie, and carried it anyway, because a man carrying a debt will carry anything.

Now the boy was gone, and the chest was gone, and the story lay around him in pieces on the canyon floor.

Rafael stood over him. Mirela stood beside Rafael, and her face was the face of a woman deciding something, and Osric found he couldn't look at either of them.

"Say it," Osric said. "Whatever it is. Say it and be done."

Rafael was quiet a long time. When he spoke, his voice was strange—flat, tired, older than the mountains around them.

"I'm not going to hit you, Osric. I want you to know that I thought about it, and I'm not going to. Because the man who sold us just handed Corven the one thing Corven needed to believe—that was the only set of papers in the world." Rafael crouched down, eye level, and his eyes were bright and hard and alive in a way Osric hadn't seen since Juniper's Landing. "But it wasn't. Was it?"

Osric's head came up.

"There's a second set," Rafael said softly. "There always was. The client cached originals on the Vessian side twenty years ago, and I've spent two decades trying not to remember where. And Corven's riders are riding south with a hostage and a bag of paper, and they think they've won." He stood, and offered Osric his hand—an offer so unexpected that Osric stared at it like a snake. "Get up, old man. You're going to spend every favor you have left, and then you're going to spend some you don't. Because the boy comes home, and the truth crosses that pass, and you're going to help me do both or die wondering why I bothered to pull you out of your own river all those years ago."

Mirela had already unshouldered her pack. Her hands were steady. Her voice, when it came, was quiet as snowfall and twice as heavy.

"The compass," she said. "Rafael. The needle. It's been pulling all morning—I thought it was the ore in the rock."

Rafael went still. Then he reached to his belt, and drew out the old brass compass—the dead client's compass, the gift and the burden—and held it flat in his palm, and they all three watched the needle swing, trembling, and settle.

Not north.

South. Back the way they'd come. Toward the cairn. Toward the cache. Toward everything they had buried and everything they had lost.

"Well," Osric said, and his voice cracked, and for the first time in forty years on the water, the old ferryman laughed—wet, broken, relieved. "Well. Point the way, Captain. Point the damn way."
