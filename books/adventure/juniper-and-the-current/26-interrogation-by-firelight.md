## 26. Interrogation by Firelight

They tied her wrists in front of her, which Mirela decided was either courtesy or arrogance, and sat her on a stone that some ancient Vessian shepherd had probably dragged up this canyon for the exact same purpose—waiting out someone else's bad decisions.

The fire was a good one. That was the first thing she noticed, and she hated herself a little for noticing. Corven's men had built it properly: windbreak of stacked rock, dry juniper, flames that threw heat all the way to her face without spitting sparks at her eyes. Whoever ran this man's field camps ran them well. Evil, Mirela thought, is rarely sloppy about comfort.

Her hands hurt. The rope was tight enough to remind her it existed but loose enough not to numb her fingers, and she flexed them slowly, one at a time, the way she did when a spine needed re-sewing and she couldn't afford cramps. Bookbinder's hands. Cipher-reader's hands. Prisoner's hands, tonight, but they were still hers, and as long as they were hers they could refuse things.

Across the fire, two of Corven's riders sat on their heels eating stew and pretending very hard not to look at her. They were young. One of them had a scar through his eyebrow that he kept touching when he thought no one saw. The other had finished his bowl and then, after a visible internal struggle, ladled a second portion and set it near her without meeting her eyes.

"Thank you," Mirela said.

The young rider flinched like she'd thrown something.

"Don't talk to the prisoner," said the scarred one, without conviction.

"She didn't say anything to me," the boy muttered, staring into the fire. "She said thank you."

Mirela almost smiled. Almost. Somewhere behind her, deeper in the narrows where the canyon walls pinched together, Tam was being held under separate guard, and every few minutes she heard his voice—muffled, indignant, going on about something. He'd been talking for an hour. She caught fragments. *My uncle ferried for the guild for eleven years, you know. Eleven years. Do you know what kind of trust that takes?* He was, she realized with a swell of affection that nearly undid her, boring his captors to death on purpose. Buying attention. Making himself memorable, so they wouldn't forget a hostage existed, so they wouldn't decide hostages were inconvenient.

Clever boy. Rafael would be proud, wherever Rafael was—if Rafael was anywhere at all. She shut that door gently before grief could lean on it.

* * *

Magistrate Corven came to the fire an hour past full dark.

He arrived without ceremony, which was itself a kind of ceremony; she understood that much about him by now. No lantern, no escort announced. Just a man stepping into the light as though he'd always been part of the camp, folding himself onto a flat rock opposite her with the ease of someone who had sat across from frightened people many times and found the practice agreeable.

Up close, Corven was disappointingly ordinary. Grey at the temples, a face built for ledgers rather than violence, ink stains on his right cuff that no amount of riding had managed to fade. He looked less like a magistrate than like a clerk who had wandered too far from his desk and refused to admit it.

"Mistress Vance." He pronounced it correctly. Vessian-style, the *V* soft, the name given its full weight. "Or should I say Vance-Vessin? Your family kept the old form longer than most."

"You've done your reading."

"I do my reading." He produced a tin cup from somewhere, poured tea from a pot one of the riders brought forward, and held it out to her.

She looked at it. He looked at her looking at it.

"It's chamomile," he said. "I'm told your mother grew it."

The world went very quiet and very sharp. "You never met my mother."

"No," Corven agreed pleasantly. "But I've read her petition. Filed 4 March, the year of annexation. Requesting exemption from land transfer on the grounds of continuous cultivation. Denied." He set the cup on a stone between them, within her reach if she wanted it, and folded his hands. "I read everything, Mistress Vance. It's how I know what people will do."

"And what will I do?"

"That's why I'm here."

* * *

He began, as she'd expected he would, with kindness—or its costume.

"The others are fine," he said. "The boy is eating. The ferryman is sleeping off a bruise he earned falling down, nothing more. Thackeray—" a fractional pause, the first honest thing he'd shown her, "—Thackeray got away. Downriver, we believe. My men lost him in the dark, which reflects poorly on my men and worse on the terrain." He sipped his own tea. "So. You're alone, your documents are in my saddlebag, and your guide is a rumor. Let's talk about how everyone lives through tomorrow."

"You have a strange way of asking for help."

"I don't need help. I need a signature." He reached into his coat—not toward a weapon, he made sure she saw that—and drew out a leather folio, setting it open on his knee. Inside, she glimpsed paper. Her paper. Her careful transcriptions, the decoded grants, the chain of transfer she and Rafael had spent nights bent over by lamplight while Osric grumbled about candle costs. "Here is what happens next, if you do nothing. Tomorrow I ride these papers to the valley garrison. A tribunal convenes—it will convene quickly, because I will make it convene quickly—and the tribunal finds that the documents recovered from the fugitives Rafael Thackeray and Mirela Vance are forgeries. Fabricated by exiles seeking to destabilize legitimate concessions. The originals, conveniently, will never be located. The permits stand. The season closes. And twenty years of theft becomes, permanently, law."

"And if I sign?"

"If you authenticate them as forgeries—yes, I want your hand on it, because your hand is known to the exile council, because you were trained in Vessian archival script and any expert they produce will recognize your work—then the story holds. Completely. The forger confesses. The conspiracy collapses. And the three people who followed you up this mountain go home." He turned a page with one finger. "The boy especially. Tam, isn't it? Sixteen. His record with the ferry guild is clean. It would stay clean. He has a long life ahead of him that does not include a charge of aiding sedition."

Mirela looked at the fire for a while.

It was a good offer. That was the obscenity of it. Not cruelty—cruelty she knew how to face; her whole childhood had been a seminar in facing it. But this was reasonable. This was *temperate*. This was a man offering arithmetic instead of a knife, and expecting her to do the sum and arrive, sensibly, at surrender.

"You've thought about my mother's chamomile," she said. "You've read her petition. You know her handwriting, probably. You know exactly what Vessia was and what was done to it."

"I know history is expensive," Corven said. "I know who pays."

"Who pays?"

"Everyone, eventually. My job is deciding who pays *first*." He leaned forward slightly, and for a moment the clerk fell away and she saw the thing underneath, patient and cold as groundwater. "Mistress Vance. I am not a monster. I want you to understand that, because monsters can be dismissed and I cannot. I am the reason the valley has roads and courts and no war. If those permits fall, the concession charters collapse, the mining companies pull out, four thousand laborers lose wages, and the borderlands burn again inside five years. You've read the same archives I have. You know what the last burning looked like." He gestured, mildly, at the dark canyon around them—at the whole country, perhaps. "I am preventing a fire. You are carrying kindling and calling it justice."

"My family's farm is under forty meters of tailings," Mirela said quietly. "Your roads run over our kitchen garden. You prevented a fire by burying the house that would have reported it."

Something moved behind his eyes—there and gone, a fish under ice. "Yes," he said. "That happened. I won't insult you by denying it. But denial wasn't on the table tonight. A signature is." He turned the folio around and slid it across the stones, along with a pen, uncapped, and the tin of ink she recognized as the cheap iron-gall sold in Juniper's Landing market—the same tin she'd bought her own supply from, weeks ago, from a woman who'd smiled at her. "Authenticate the documents as fabrications. In your own hand, in Vessian archival script, so there's no ambiguity. Do that, and in the morning the boy walks free, the ferryman walks free, and a warrant goes out for Thackeray alone—a warrant, Mistress Vance, not a kill order. I keep my word. Ask anyone whose word-keeping mattered to them."

"And if I refuse?"

Corven sighed, genuinely, the sigh of a man burdened by other people's choices. "Then tomorrow I present forged documents as evidence of forgery, the tribunal confirms whatever I tell it to confirm, and the three of you become footnotes. The boy's freedom stops being a bargaining chip and starts being a legal question. Legal questions," he added, "are slower and less kind than bargains."

* * *

The riders had withdrawn to the far end of the firelight, pretending to check tack. The boy with the second bowl of stew had not withdrawn quite far enough. Mirela noted his position the way she noted everything now: exits, shadows, the slope of scree above the camp, the direction the wind carried sound. Not because she had a plan. Because her father used to say a mind that keeps measuring keeps living.

She picked up the pen.

Corven watched her with the composure of a man who had already read the last page. And why shouldn't he? Every frightened person he'd ever sat across from had done the sums and signed. Courage, she thought, is easy in stories, where it gets an audience. Here there was only a fire, a clerk, and a boy pretending not to listen, and no one would ever write down what she chose in the next minute unless she chose wrong.

She dipped the pen. Drew it across the top sheet in a long, deliberate stroke—in Vessian archival script, the hand her mother taught her, the hand of petitions and land grants and everything the republic had tried to erase.

She wrote: *I, Mirela Vance-Vessin, having examined these documents—*

Corven's shoulders eased by a degree.

*—find them genuine.*

The pen scratched on. She wrote it out fully, formally, the way a certification was written, citing the watermarks, the seal impressions, the grant numbering sequences, the specific irregularities that proved the *republic's* transfers false and the Vessian originals true. She dated it. She signed it. Then she turned the folio back around and pushed it across the stones to him.

Corven read it. She watched him read it, watched the composure hold and hold and then, at the signature, flicker—because he understood, a half-second too late, what she'd done. He had asked for authentication. He had received it. In her hand, in Vessian archival script, dated and witnessed by the firelight and by two of his own riders who had been close enough to hear every word of his offer—including the part where the Magistrate of the Borderlands had proposed manufacturing evidence and buying a confession.

"You—" he began.

"You wanted my hand on the documents," Mirela said. "You have it. Read it aloud if you like. Or don't, and explain to your tribunal why the prisoner's certification contradicts your finding. Either way, Magistrate, the truth now has a witness with a signature, and it isn't yours." She set down the pen with the small precise click of a bookbinder closing a cover. "My mother taught me that script so I could certify true things. You may burn this page. But two of your riders heard you offer to trade a boy's freedom for a lie, and boys remember. That's the trouble with witnesses, Magistrate. You can't unmake them. Believe me. My whole country tried."

For a long moment Corven said nothing. The fire popped. Somewhere up-canyon, wind moved through the pines with a sound like a crowd drawing breath.

"You'll freeze tonight," he said finally, standing, gathering the folio. His voice had gone back to ledger-flat, but the ink stain on his cuff trembled slightly as he closed the leather. "I'll have a blanket sent."

"How generous."

"It isn't generosity. Dead prisoners make poor witnesses, and you've just made yourself the most important witness in these mountains." He paused at the edge of the firelight, and looked back at her, and for one unguarded instant he looked tired—tired the way she imagined a dam must feel, holding back a river forever. "Thackeray is dead in a ravine by now, or he'll wish he were. Whatever you think you've won tonight, Mistress Vance, the permits expire in three days. Time doesn't sign certifications."

"Time," Mirela said, "reads everything too."

He left. The scarred rider brought her a blanket, dropped it beside her without a word, and—she was almost certain—loosened the rope at her wrists by two fingers' width before walking away.

* * *

She slept in pieces, the way she'd learned to during the annexation years: lightly, in installments, one ear always on the wind. Once she woke to voices at the far end of the camp—Corven's, low and clipped, giving orders she couldn't parse—and once to the boy guard humming, badly, a song she knew. A Vessian lullaby. He probably didn't even know where he'd learned it. She lay still and let the tune wash over her and thought: *this is how we survive. We get into their songs without permission.*

Toward the grey hour before dawn, she woke for real, because the camp had changed.

She couldn't have said how at first. Then she counted. The picket line—four horses at dusk. Three now. And the scarred rider's bedroll stood empty, and Corven's tent flap was tied back, and two men were saddling in the dark with the hurried silence of men following orders they disliked.

Riders leaving. At night. Downstream.

Which meant either Corven had found Rafael's trail—

—or Rafael had found a way to make Corven believe he had.

Mirela pressed her bound hands against her mouth and laughed, silently, until her eyes stung. Somewhere out in that black canyon, a disgraced guide with a stolen compass and a talent for vanishing was doing whatever impossible thing he did best, and every rider Corven sent chasing him was a rider not watching her, not watching Tam, not watching the chest.

She settled back against the cold stone, pulled the blanket to her chin, and began, methodically, to memorize the camp: the guard rotations, the boy who hummed Vessian songs, the slack in her rope, the scree slope no one was guarding because no one believed a bound woman could climb it.

Three days, Corven had said. Time doesn't sign certifications.

No, Mirela thought, closing her eyes. But time keeps books. And she had spent her whole life learning how to bind one.
