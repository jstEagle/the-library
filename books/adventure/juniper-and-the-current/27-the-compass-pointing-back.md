## 27. The Compass Pointing Back

The river took them the way it took everything in Juniper's Landing—without ceremony and without questions.

Osric poled the flat-bottomed skiff through the dark with the ease of a man who had been arguing with this water for forty years and had learned to lose gracefully. Rafael lay low against the gunwale, ribs aching where the rope had dragged them over rocks, and watched the banks slide past: black pines, black stone, the occasional orange eye of a farmhouse lamp that made them both duck like guilty teenagers.

"You're bleeding on my boat," Osric said, not unkindly.

"I'm bleeding on your *debt*," Rafael said. "Consider us even."

"Ha." The pole dipped, bit, lifted. "Even would've been you dying quiet. This is you owing me *more*. I keep accounts, Thackeray. It's my one virtue."

"It's your only virtue."

"And it's a *magnificent* one. Now shut up and hold that tarp down before someone on the bank sees white canvas moving at midnight and starts asking ferryman questions."

Rafael held the tarp. The river did its work.

They had come out of the Narrows in the way of all disasters—suddenly, loudly, and then with an awful silence. Tam taken. The chest seized. Mirela's voice cut off mid-word when they put the hood over her head, and Rafael still heard the exact pitch of that cut-off word every time he closed his eyes. Corven's riders had been efficient. Twelve years in the mountains had taught Rafael what efficiency looked like from the wrong end of it.

They'd escaped because Osric, who was supposed to be somewhere else entirely, had appeared out of the willows with a skiff and a face like a man arriving late to his own funeral.

"Don't thank me," Osric had said, hauling Rafael over the gunwale by the collar. "I haven't decided whose side I'm on yet. I just know I'm not on *his*."

Now, miles downstream, the current carried them past the last of the farms and into the wilder stretch below Juniper's Landing, where the Serrano narrowed and quickened between walls of granite. Osric shipped the pole and let the river do the steering, which it did better than either of them.

"There's a landing," Osric said quietly. "Old shepherd track, two bends up. You can walk out from there toward the high country without crossing a single road Corven watches."

"The high country," Rafael repeated. "And then what? I have no documents. No party. Half a rope and a compass. I'm going to walk into the mountains and *feel bad* at them?"

"You always were a dramatic crier."

"I don't cry."

"You cried at my daughter's wedding."

"That was *onions*."

"In the *dessert tent*, Thackeray. There were no onions within a mile of that tent."

Rafael didn't answer, because the compass had shifted against his chest.

He'd carried it in the inside pocket since the Narrows—the one thing the riders hadn't taken, because it looked like nothing. Brass case, dented lid, a hairline crack across the crystal that he'd stopped seeing years ago. Halvard's compass. The dead client's compass. The gift Rafael had never wanted and never managed to throw away.

He'd checked it out of habit a hundred times on the climb, the way a man touches a coin in his pocket. North, more or less. North with the small westward lie it had always told, three degrees of drift that Rafael had memorized and corrected for so long it felt less like an error than a personality.

Except now the needle wasn't lying three degrees west.

It was pulling hard west. Straining, almost, the way a dog strains at a lead—trembling against the card, swinging a few degrees past true north and holding there, insistent, as if the little brass heart of the thing had remembered something urgent.

"Osric," Rafael said slowly. "How far are we from the old survey line?"

"Which one?"

"Halvard's. The '61 traverse. The one we never finished."

Osric was quiet for a moment, reading water. "Two bends up, then west into the granite. A day's walk if the weather holds. Why?"

Rafael turned the compass in his fingers. The needle swung with the boat, steadied, and pulled west again. Same bearing. Same stubborn insistence.

"Because this thing is pointing at it."

"It's a compass. It points north. That's the entire job description."

"It's pointing *west of north*. Three degrees past where it should be. Every compass drifts, but they drift the same way every day. This one—" He stopped. Started again. "Halvard recalibrated this compass himself. He was fussy about instruments. He'd never carry one that lied to him."

"So it's broken now."

"Or it's *magnetized*." Rafael said the word and felt it land somewhere deep, in the place where two years of unanswerable questions lived. "A strong magnet near the housing pulls the needle off true. Permanently, if it's close enough long enough. Iron. A lot of iron, in one fixed spot, and a compass carried near it often enough—"

He stopped again, because his mind had arrived somewhere and his mouth hadn't caught up.

The cache. Halvard's cache. On the '61 traverse, the third night, Halvard had gone off alone for half a day with a shovel and come back lighter of spirit and evasive of tongue, and when Rafael had teased him about it he'd said only, *"A guide keeps a spare of everything, Thackeray. Rope, rations, opinions."*

And then Halvard had died on the Vessian side, and the expedition had died with him, and Rafael had spent two years carrying the blame like a stone sewn into his coat—and never once thought to ask what a careful man keeps spares *of*.

Documents. A man who suspected his own government might forge its way across a mountain range—a man who had seen the original grants with his own eyes—would keep spares of exactly that.

"He hid them under the cairn," Rafael whispered. "He hid them at the cache site, and he kept the compass near it long enough to—no. No, that's backwards. He *set* the compass. He magnetized it deliberately, or he stored it beside the iron box until the needle learned the way, and then he gave it to me." His hands were shaking. "He gave it to me at the trailhead, Osric. He said, 'In case you lose the map.' I thought it was sentiment. I thought it was an old man being kind."

Osric had stopped poling. The skiff drifted in the current, turning slowly, and neither of them corrected it.

"You're telling me," Osric said, "that a dead man has been navigating you for two years."

"I'm telling me that a dead man has been *waiting* for me. And I've been too busy hating myself to read the instrument."

The river said nothing. Rivers never did; that was their whole philosophy.

"Two bends up," Rafael said. "Put me ashore."

* * *

They grounded the skiff on the old shepherd landing as the sky went the color of wet slate. Osric tied off, then stood in the shallows wringing his hat in his hands like a man rehearsing a speech.

"Say it," Rafael said.

"I'm not saying anything."

"You've been composing it since the Narrows. Your face does this thing when it's drafting."

"My face does *nothing*. My face is a locked ledger." But he sat down heavily on a boulder anyway, hat in his lap, and looked older than Rafael had ever seen him. "Corven's man came to me. Before the climb. Runner, sealed letter, very polite. Betray the party at the high camp and the debt vanishes twice over. Once for the money, once for whatever else I might want someday."

Rafael went very still. "You took the letter."

"I took the letter. I answered it too—I gave up the route through the gorge shelf. Only I gave them the *dead-end* shelf, because apparently there's a limit to what even a magnificent accountant will sell." He rubbed his face. "Tam found the messages. Confronted me on the glacier like a little judge in a borrowed coat. And I couldn't recant, because recanting meant admitting, and admitting meant—" He gestured vaguely at the entire situation. "*This*. Sitting on a rock confessing to a man whose friends I sold."

"You sent them to the dead-end shelf," Rafael said. "That bought us hours at the Narrows. Hours we used to get Mirela and the documents as far as we did."

"I got Tam *taken*."

"No. Corven got Tam taken. You got us hours, and then you got me out of the river tonight, which by my arithmetic makes you a man who owes nobody." Rafael held out his hand. "The debt's done, Osric. All of it. Forgiven. Not paid—*forgiven*. There's a difference, and it matters."

Osric stared at the hand. Then he gripped it, hard, and his eyes went bright and furious and he said, thickly, "This is why nobody likes you, Thackeray. You make a man feel things in *public*."

"Noted. Now show me the shepherd track before I start crying in the dessert tent."

* * *

The track climbed away from the river through pine shadow, and Rafael walked it as the light failed, and the compass walked with him.

West of north. Always west of north. When the track bent east around a spur, the needle strained after its hidden bearing like a hand reaching back for something dropped. Rafael began, absurdly, to talk to it.

"All right," he muttered, hopping a stream in the dark. "But if you're wrong about this, Halvard, I want it noted that I forgave Osric tonight and I'll need something to feel superior about tomorrow."

The needle trembled on, indifferent, certain.

It occurred to him, somewhere in the second hour, that this was what memory actually was—not a picture but a *bearing*. Not the scene itself, which had blurred at the edges these two years, but the pull of it. The way his mind kept straining west of north, toward the Vessian side, toward the question with no answer. He had thought the pull was guilt. Maybe guilt was just a compass reading he'd misinterpreted. Maybe the needle had been trying to tell him the truth the whole time, and he'd been correcting for it like an error.

*"In case you lose the map."*

He hadn't lost the map. He'd lost himself, and the map had been waiting.

Near midnight the clouds tore open and the moon came through, and Rafael stopped on a granite shelf and took his bearings properly for the first time. Below, the Serrano ran silver. Ahead, the range stood up against the stars, black on blacker—and there, in the notch he knew better than his own heartbeat, Thackeray Pass, wearing its first thin crown of snow.

Somewhere beyond it, in a camp he couldn't see, Corven was holding Tam and Mirela and a chest full of proof, and the permits were ticking down toward permanence, and Rafael had nothing but a rope's worth of gear and a dead man's compass.

Except the compass was pulling west of north. Toward the '61 traverse. Toward the cairn.

Toward the spare.

"If there's a second set of originals under that cairn," Rafael said aloud to the mountain, to the moon, to the stubborn brass heart in his palm, "then Corven doesn't hold the only truth in the world. He holds a *copy*."

And copies could be burned. Originals, witnessed, carried down by thirty pairs of hands—originals were harder.

He checked the bearing one last time, set his boots along it, and started climbing.

Behind him, far downstream, Juniper's Landing slept. Ahead, the needle pulled steady and sure, west of north, home—the way a dead man's memory goes on guiding the living, if only they finally stop correcting for it.
