## 28. The Cache Under the Cairn

The compass needle did not waver.

Rafael had checked it eleven times since the river fork, and eleven times it had pointed the same stubborn way: not north, not toward the pass, not toward any direction a sane guide would call *forward*. It pointed up the old shepherd's track, past the burned village they'd wept through two nights ago, to a saddle of grey stone where a cairn stood against the sky like a man who had been waiting a long time and had learned not to mind.

*The cache under the cairn.* The thought had come to Rafael somewhere on the descent from Windrow Col, in the white hours of the storm, when the mind stops being polite and starts telling the truth. The compass had been Edric Vale's. Edric had been fastidious about his instruments the way other men were fastidious about their children. And Edric had died on the Vessian side of the range with his papers scattered and his reputation—unlike Rafael's—still intact, because the dead are easy to leave alone.

*He wouldn't have carried originals into a survey season,* Rafael thought, climbing. *He'd have hidden them. He'd have marked them. He'd have—*

The needle tugged. Rafael stopped, heart hammering, and looked at the cairn.

*He'd have used the only landmark on this whole ridge that nobody would ever disturb.*

Because who touches a memorial? Who digs beneath a pile of stones raised for a dead man? Not shepherds, who are a superstitious lot. Not Corven's surveyors, who had been ordered to falsify their records and had no reason to linger. Not even Rafael, who had come here once, two years ago, in the grey aftermath of disgrace, and stood before the cairn and could not make himself kneel, because kneeling would have meant accepting that Edric was dead and that Rafael Thackeray had killed him.

Rafael knelt now.

The stones were cold and lichened, some of them furred with the pale moss that grows only where nothing has disturbed it for years. Rafael lifted the top stone first—the capstone, flat as a table, heavier than it looked—and set it aside with the care owed to something sacred. Then the next. Then the next. The cairn came apart in layers, like a sentence being unpicked, and Rafael's hands shook so badly on the fourth stone that they had to stop and breathe, and breathe again, and press both palms flat against the frozen ground as if the mountain might lend some steadiness through the rock.

Behind them, Osric sat down on a boulder with a grunt and made a show of examining his boot.

"I'm not watching," Osric announced. "In case you were wondering. A man deserves privacy for this sort of thing."

"You're sitting six feet away."

"Six feet is privacy. On a mountain it's practically a separate room." He fished a heel of bread from his coat and tore it in half, and offered the larger half up the slope without looking. Rafael took it. It was stale and slightly frozen and it was the kindest thing anyone had handed them in weeks.

The cairn's heart was a hollow lined with flat stones, and in the hollow, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, was a box.

Rafael lifted it out and did not open it. Not yet. Instead they sat back on their heels in the ruin of the memorial they had built—no, that Corven's men had built, officially, with a plaque that named no cause of death and assigned no blame, the most dishonest sentence in stone Rafael had ever read—and looked at the box the way you look at a letter you've been afraid to open for two years.

"Edric," Rafael said aloud. The name sounded strange up here, where it had only ever been wind. "You absolute, meticulous, insufferable man."

Osric chewed. "Was he?"

"The most careful person I ever guided. He'd re-tie knots that were already sound. He'd check a bearing three times and then ask me to check it a fourth, and when I did, he'd nod like I'd confirmed something he'd suspected all along." Rafael's throat tightened. "I used to find it funny. Then I used to find it exhausting. Now I'd give—" They stopped. Started again. "Now I understand it was love. That's what care is, when you do it that thoroughly. It's love with a ruler."

Rafael worked the oilcloth loose. Underneath was a tin document case, the kind surveyors used, dented at one corner. The latch was stiff with cold. Rafael's fingers were stiff with cold. Between the two of them it took a small eternity, and then the case opened with a click that sounded, in the enormous silence of the saddle, like a door unlocking in an empty house.

Inside: papers. Dozens of them, wrapped in waxed linen, dry as the day they'd been hidden. Rafael lifted the top sheaf and the breath went out of them all at once.

Land grants. Original Vessian land grants, stamped with the seal of a nation that no longer existed, in the same hand, with the same registry marks, as the documents in the iron chest pulled from the Serrano River. Edric Vale had found them. Edric Vale had *always* known. He had been surveying the concessions, and he had traced the permits back through the forged transfers to their stolen roots, and he had made copies—of course he had made copies, he was Edric—and he had hidden the originals here, under his own memorial, where only a guide who knew the compass was his would ever think to look.

And beneath the grants, folded once, sealed with a smear of wax that had cracked but held, a letter.

Rafael's name was on the outside of it.

Not *R. Thackeray*. Not the cold, clipped *Thackeray* that Corven's tribunal had used, that the guild's blacklisting order had used, that Rafael had heard in their own head every night for two years. It said *Rafael*, in Edric's careful, upright hand, the letters standing at attention, and under it: *to be opened by the person who finds this, who I hope to God is Rafael.*

The shaking came back. Rafael held the letter and could not open it and Osric, six feet away in his separate room, said quietly, without any of his usual theater:

"Take your time. The snow's a day behind us, not an hour. Take your time."

Rafael broke the wax.

* * *

*Rafael—*

*If you're reading this, then either you found my compass or you're the finest gravedigger in the range, and I know which I'd bet on.*

*I'm writing this on the fourth of Harvestmonth, the night before we go up the Vessian side. I want it down on paper while my hand is steady, because by the time you read it my hand won't be doing anything at all, and there are things that shouldn't depend on a dead man's memory.*

*I know about the route change. I signed the amended survey order because Corven's clerk told me it was routine, and then I read it properly, which is more than the clerk did, and I understood what it was: a route pushed onto the unstable face, in late season, with a guide who would carry the blame. Corven wants the concession surveys clean and the Vessian grants buried, and I have spent three months quietly proving the grants were never buried at all—they were stolen, and the theft was papered over with forgeries, and I have the originals. I have them because I am careful, and I am careful because I was raised by a mother who believed that the world is run by people who count on everyone else being careless.*

*I don't think I'm coming down. A man who reads his own death warrant as a survey amendment knows how these things go. So I'm leaving this where only you will find it, because you're the only one I trust with it, and because I owe you an apology I won't live to deliver.*

*I let them change the route. I could have refused. I could have pulled the whole expedition and blown Corven's operation wide two years early. But I wanted my survey finished, Rafael, I wanted the grants documented in my own hand, and I told myself the face would hold, and I let you walk us onto it because it suited me. Whatever they say happened up there—whatever they make you carry—it was mine to carry too. More than mine. I was the one with the evidence. I was the one who knew.*

*The originals are under my cairn, because I am either very clever or very morbid, possibly both. Take them. Take them and the letter and go to the exiles, or the tribunal, or the river if you have to, and when they ask you what happened on the Vessian side, tell them the truth: that Edric Vale made a coward's bargain with a careful man's conscience, and it cost him his life and nearly cost you yours.*

*You didn't kill me, Rafael. Corven did, with my signature as the knife. Forgive yourself before you forgive me, because I think you'll find the first harder.*

*The compass was my father's. It pulls a half-point east of true—always has, some quirk in the needle, he never fixed it and neither will I. I'm leaving it to you. Not because you need it to find north. Because I want you to have something of mine that points somewhere, even if it points crooked.*

*Yours in debt and friendship,*
*Edric Vale*

*P.S. — The grants are wrapped in the blue linen. The linen is from my mother's curtains. She'd have wanted them used for something that mattered.*

Rafael read it twice. Then a third time, lips moving, though the wind took the words as fast as they came. The letter did not blur, exactly—Rafael's eyes were too dry for that, had been too dry for two years, dry the way a riverbed is dry, all the water gone somewhere it couldn't be reached—but something in the chest cracked along an old seam, and what came through the crack wasn't weeping so much as weather. A change in pressure. The sense, at last, of a storm that had been parked overhead finally moving on.

Two years. Two years of the guild's cold letter, of Corven's polite, poisonous sympathy, of ferry landings where conversation stopped when Rafael walked up, of the question that had no answer—*what happened up there?*—because Rafael had never known. Had gone over it ten thousand times and never known. Had blamed the mountain, then the weather, then the route, then God, then, always, in the end, in the dark, themselves.

And the answer had been sitting in Edric's careful hand all along, under a pile of stones, half a point east of true.

"I let them change the route," Rafael said. The words came out cracked, half voice and half wind. "I *argued* against it. I told him the face was rotten. And he—" A breath. "He signed it anyway, and let me think it was my fault. He knew, and he let me carry it, because he wanted his survey. Because he was—" The anger arrived then, late and enormous, two years overdue, and Rafael welcomed it like an old friend at a door they'd given up knocking on. "He was a *coward*, and he knew it, and he was sorry, and I don't—" Rafael stopped. Pressed the letter flat against their knee. "I don't know what I feel. All of it. I feel all of it at once."

Osric came up the slope then, separate room be damned, and sat down in the wreckage of the cairn without asking, and put one heavy hand on Rafael's shoulder the way you'd steady a rope under load.

"Good," Osric said. "That's what it's supposed to feel like. All of it at once. Anyone tells you grief comes in tidy stages, they've never buried anything that mattered." He looked at the papers, at the seal, at the letter. "He wrote you clean. That's rarer than gold, that is. Most men die owing words. He paid up."

"He let me hang for it."

"Aye. And then he spent his last night making sure you'd get untied." Osric shrugged, a big slow shrug that moved his whole coat. "Men are both things, Rafa. Always both things. The trick is deciding which one you'll be today." He nodded at the tin case. "Which one are you today?"

Rafael looked at the grants in their curtain-linen wrapping. At the compass lying open on the stone, needle steady, pointing its faithful crooked east. At the saddle below, where the trail dropped toward the valley, toward Juniper's Landing, toward Corven's garrison and Corven's archive and Corven's four days of expiring patience—and beyond that, up the pass, toward the courier they could not reach and the council that did not yet know the truth was coming.

The past had just handed them the future. It was wrapped in blue linen and it weighed almost nothing.

"Today," Rafael said, and heard their own voice come back level, the first level voice they'd had in two years, "I'm the one who digs the cairn back up properly. He deserves that. And then we go down the mountain, and we raise the valley."

* * *

Rebuilding the cairn took an hour, because Rafael would not do it quickly. Every stone went back in its place, fitted and settled, the capstone last, and then Rafael stood a long moment with one palm flat on the cold rock.

"Rest now," they said. "You're paid in full."

Then they took out the iron chest's key—the chest itself waited with Tam and Mirela at the shelter below, guarded like a sleeping bear—and wrapped Edric's tin case in spare rope and slung it inside their coat, against the back, where it rode warm as a second heartbeat.

They were halfway down the saddle when Osric, who had been uncharacteristically quiet, said: "The letter. The part about Corven. The clerk, the amended order. That's testimony, that is. Written testimony. Dated."

Rafael stopped.

They had been thinking of the letter as a wound and a bandage both, as something private, something that belonged to the two of them—Edric and Rafael and the mountain between. It had not yet occurred to them, in the rawness of the reading, that it was also a *weapon*. That a dead man's hand could do what no living witness had been able to: put Corven's order in writing, in Corven's own chain of command, dated two years to the day before the tribunal that had blacklisted Rafael Thackeray.

"It's testimony," Rafael said slowly. "And Sergeant Brandt's word, and the originals, and the chest. Corven built his whole tower on the story that there were no originals. That the grants were ash and the guide was guilty and the permits were law." They turned and looked back up at the cairn, small now against the sky, and felt something that was not quite laughter and not quite tears move through them—something lighter than both. "He built it on a grave. And the grave just testified."

Osric grinned, and it transformed his whole weathered face into something boyish. "My mother always said: never trust a careful man, and never, ever bury him carelessly."

They came down to the shelter as the light went long and copper. Mirela was outside before Rafael reached the door—she had been watching the saddle, she admitted later, since noon—and she read Rafael's face the way she read ciphers, quickly and completely, and she did not ask *what did you find*. She asked, "Are you all right?" and when Rafael said, "No. Yes. Both things," she nodded as if that were the only honest answer in the world, and took their hand, and did not let go.

Inside, by the stove, Rafael unwrapped the blue linen and laid Edric's grants beside the chest's documents, and the registry marks matched line for line, seal for seal, like two halves of a sentence finally joined. Mirela bent over them and her breath caught, and she said, very quietly, in Vessian, a word Rafael didn't know and didn't need to.

Then she looked up. "There's more. Tell me there's more."

Rafael set the letter on the table between them, Edric's name facing up.

"There's more," Rafael said. "And tomorrow we take all of it down to the valley. Every ferryman, every shepherd, every soul Osric can raise. Because Corven's coming up this mountain thinking he's hunting a thief and a chest." Rafael touched the letter, the compass, the linen. "He doesn't know yet that he's riding into a courtroom."
