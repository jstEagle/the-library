## 29. Raising the Valley

Osric Ferro had spent forty years on the Serrano River learning one lesson above all others: never wake a sleeping ferryman before dawn unless the water itself is on fire.

He broke that lesson eleven times before sunrise.

The first was his own fault, in a way. He'd sat up most of the night with a cup of cold tea going colder, turning over what he'd done at the shelf camp—what he'd given Corven's rider, and what he'd made sure the man would never find—and somewhere around the third hour of staring at the ceiling beams, Osric had arrived at the uncomfortable conclusion that a debt you can't pay off by lying down is a debt you have to stand up for.

So he put on his boots, and he went to raise the valley.

* * *

The first knock was at Denna Kroll's door, upriver past the willow stands. Denna ran the second ferry crossing, or third, depending on how you counted the one that flooded out every spring and nobody bothered rebuilding. She opened the door with her hair in a rag and her father's old boathook in her hand, which told Osric she'd been expecting trouble of some kind for years and had merely been waiting to learn its shape.

"Osric." She looked him up and down. "You're either here about money, or you're here about something worse."

"Something worse."

"Thank the saints. I'm sick of money." She let him in.

He laid it out plain, because Denna had no patience for dressing things up: Rafael Thackeray was on the mountain with documents that could unmake twenty years of theft, Magistrate Corven had twelve riders on the southern approach, and by morning there might be nothing standing between those riders and the pass except weather.

"Thackeray," Denna said slowly. "The guide they blacklisted."

"The same."

"My cousin crossed with him once. Said he counted heads twice at every river bend like a woman counting children at market." She was already pulling her coat off the peg. "What do you need?"

"Boats. Men who can row in the dark. And word carried up the valley faster than a horse can carry it, which means feet and mouths and people who owe me favors."

Denna snorted. "People who owe you favors. That's a short list, Osric Ferro. You've spent your whole life making sure nobody owed you anything."

"That's why the ones who do are worth so much," Osric said, and had the grace to look slightly ashamed when she laughed at him.

* * *

The ferrymen came easiest. Ferrymen understand debts; it's half their trade, carrying people across water they can't cross themselves, keeping accounts of who went where and who paid and who didn't. When Osric stood in the doorway of the Boatmen's Hall at four in the morning, dripping river fog, and said, "I'm calling in every marker anyone holds on me, all at once, tonight," there was a long silence, and then old Piet Voss said, "Saints preserve us, what did you *do*?"

"Nothing yet. That's rather the point."

It took an hour to explain, and another hour of argument—Piet wanted to know why they should stick their necks out for a disgraced foreigner, and Osric found himself saying things he hadn't planned to say, words coming up from somewhere below his ribs where he kept the parts of himself he didn't inspect often.

"Because I know what it is to be bought," Osric said. "I've been bought twice this month, if you want the truth of it. Once by a magistrate with a debt to forgive, and once—" he paused, and the hall was quiet enough to hear the river through the walls, "—once by my own conscience, which paid better. Thackeray is carrying something true up that mountain. I don't fully understand it. But I understand being given a choice between what's safe and what's right, and I understand choosing wrong, and I am asking you—begging you, Piet, and I'll not beg again in this life—to help me choose right while the choosing's still open."

Old Piet looked at him for a long moment. Then he reached for his oiled coat.

"Well," he said. "Forty years I've wondered what it would take to shut you up. Turns out it's honesty. Who knew."

* * *

The shepherds were harder, because the shepherds were already asleep in high huts above the tree line, and waking them meant climbing, and Osric's climbing days were thirty years behind him and had ended badly, with a broken ankle and a vow he'd kept faithfully ever since.

But there was Tam's aunt, Wren, who kept sheep on the lower slopes and had raised the boy after his mother died, and Wren didn't sleep much anyway in lambing season. She listened to Osric's whole account without interrupting once, which frightened him more than shouting would have.

"You're telling me," she said at last, "that my Tam is up on that mountain. With Thackeray. With soldiers chasing them."

"With one soldier column chasing them, yes."

"And you waited until now to tell me?"

"I only decided to tell anyone an hour ago."

Wren put down the lamb she was bottle-feeding, wiped her hands on her apron with great deliberation, and took down her husband's old hunting rifle from above the lintel.

"You're not bringing that," Osric said.

"I'm bringing it. Whether it comes off my shoulder is another matter entirely." She tied her scarf. "Tam thinks I don't know he practices standing guard at the ferry landing when he thinks everyone's gone to bed. Stands there for hours, straight as a fence post, guarding absolutely nothing. Well. Now he has something to guard, and I'll be damned if he guards it alone."

* * *

The exile network was the strangest part, because Osric had only ever brushed against it the way a man brushes against a current—he'd ferried quiet people across at odd hours for fair prices and asked no questions, and the quiet people had always tipped well and never spoken of it, and that was the whole of the arrangement as far as he'd understood it.

Now he knocked on the back door of Mirela Vance's bookbinding shop, where a lamp still burned, and found three people inside whom he'd ferried across the Serrano at various times over six years, all looking at maps of the range as though the maps owed them money.

"We heard," said the one called Anselm, the schoolteacher with the burned hands. "Word travels. The valley knows something's happening."

"The valley knows *nothing*," Osric said. "That's the problem. By dawn it needs to know everything, or Corven's riders walk up to that pass and take whatever they like from whoever's standing there."

Anselm exchanged a look with the woman beside him—a shepherd's widow named Katrin whose face Osric knew from the market but had never heard speak above a murmur.

"There are more of us than Corven thinks," Katrin said, and her voice wasn't a murmur at all. "There always were. We learned to be quiet, not few. Give us until dawn."

"What do you need?" Anselm asked, already pulling paper toward himself.

"Lanterns. Boots. People who can stand in the cold and be seen. That's all I'm asking for—not fighters, not a battle. Just witnesses. Thirty of them, fifty if we're lucky, standing on the pass road where the riders can't pretend they aren't there."

Katrin almost smiled. It transformed her face entirely, and Osric thought, with a small internal jolt, that this was probably what Vessia had looked like before: ordinary faces, unafraid.

"Witnesses," she repeated. "Yes. That we can do. We've been practicing being seen our whole lives without meaning to."

* * *

By midnight the thing had a momentum of its own, which was either wonderful or terrifying depending on the minute. Osric stood at the ferry landing watching lanterns move along the valley paths like embers rolling uphill, and Piet Voss appeared at his elbow with two cups of something hot and alcoholic and handed him one without asking.

"You look like a man doing arithmetic he doesn't like," Piet said.

"I'm counting favors. I've spent every one I own tonight. Twenty-two years of hauling people's furniture across a river, of lending boats and never pressing for return dates, of remembering birthdays and funerals and which widow likes her wood split before frost—" Osric drank. "All of it, gone, in one night."

"And was it worth it?"

Osric considered the question honestly, because Piet deserved honesty and because Osric had recently made a private pact with honesty that he intended to keep even when it stung.

"When Corven's rider found me at the shelf camp," he said, "he offered to wipe my debt clean. Twice over. And do you know what I felt? Relief. Real relief. And underneath the relief, something rotten, like meat gone off under a clean cloth. I've been carrying that smell for three days." He turned the cup in his hands. "Tonight the smell's going away. So yes. Worth it. Ask me again when the soldiers come and I may give you a different answer."

"Soldiers don't frighten ferrymen," Piet said comfortably. "We've been running from customs men since before Corven could walk. There's a technique to it."

"Oh?"

"Stand still," Piet said, "in very large numbers, and look disappointed."

* * *

The hours blurred. Osric sent runners to the mill town, to the tannery cottages, to the fishing families who lived so far downriver they were practically rumors. He argued with the cooper, who wanted to bring his apprentices; he talked the blacksmith's daughter out of bringing the forge hammer ("it's a symbol, Osric," she said, "symbols matter," and he said, "so does the skull of whoever you hit with it"); he wrote, in his terrible hand, a note that got copied and recopied and passed hand to hand up the whole valley:

*They are carrying proof of what was taken from us. The magistrate means to stop it reaching the light. Come to the pass road at first light. Bring nothing but yourself and your eyes. What is witnessed cannot be unwitnessed.*

He hadn't written anything that good in his life. He suspected Mirela had drafted it weeks ago and left it in a drawer for exactly this purpose, which struck him as either very wise or slightly terrifying, and he'd decided it was both.

Around three in the morning, Wren caught up with him on the switchback trail, breathing hard, rifle slung, three shepherds behind her and a boy of maybe fourteen leading a mule loaded with bread and cheese.

"Fifty-one," she reported. "That's the count from the lower valley. More coming from the mill town."

"Fifty-one," Osric repeated. He'd hoped for thirty. He'd feared for ten.

"And Osric." Wren's voice dropped. "Half of them aren't Vessian. Half of them are just... valley folk. Farmers. Your ferrymen. People Thackeray never met and owes nothing to."

Osric stopped walking. For a moment the night seemed very large and very cold and full of lanterns.

"They don't come for Thackeray," he said finally. "Or for Vessia, or for justice with a capital J. They come because a neighbor knocked on their door and told them the truth and asked. That's all it ever takes, Wren. That's all it's ever taken. Somebody has to knock."

"You knocked late," Wren said, not unkindly.

"I knocked," Osric said. "At my age, you take credit for the knocking."

* * *

They came up through the last dark in a loose, straggling line that no drill sergeant would have tolerated and no army could have produced: ferrymen with their boat cloaks stiff with frost, shepherds with crooks, the cooper's apprentices, the blacksmith's daughter empty-handed and stubborn about it, Anselm the schoolteacher walking beside Katrin the widow, old Piet Voss wheezing but game, Denna Kroll with her father's boathook over her shoulder like a halberd, and behind them, strung up the pass road as far as the lantern-light reached, the ordinary population of a valley that had decided, sometime between midnight and dawn, that obedience was no longer the default.

Thirty of them at the narrow place below Thackeray Pass where the road pinched between rock and drop. Then more arriving. Forty. Osric lost count past fifty and stopped trying, because counting was arithmetic and arithmetic was Corven's language, and tonight he refused to speak it.

The sky went grey, then pearl, then the pale gold of a mountain morning that promised weather by afternoon. Somewhere above, hidden in the folds of the pass, Rafael Thackeray was either alive or not, and the chest was either close or lost, and none of that was in Osric's hands anymore. All that was in his hands was this: a road, a crowd, and the refusal to move.

Denna appeared beside him, stamping her feet against the cold.

"Now what?" she asked.

"Now," Osric said, "we stand here and look disappointed."

And then, faintly, from up the southern approach—the crunch of hooves on frozen scree, many hooves, riding hard—the sound of twelve riders coming up the valley toward fifty people who had nowhere else to be.

Osric squared his shoulders, arranged his face into an expression of profound civic disappointment, and whispered to Denna out of the corner of his mouth:

"If I fall, tell Piet he still owes me for the salmon."

The first rider crested the rise.
