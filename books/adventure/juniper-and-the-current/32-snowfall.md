## 32. Snowfall

The first flake landed on the barrel of Sergeant Ilsa Brandt's carbine, and she had the distinct impression it was mocking her.

It was a small thing, that flake—fat and unhurried, spinning down through the cold air above Thackeray Pass as though it had all the time in the world. Which, Ilsa thought, was more than any of them could say. She watched it settle on the gunmetal, hold for a heartbeat, and vanish.

Then another came. And another.

Around her, twelve riders sat their horses in a ragged line across the narrow throat of the pass, breath steaming, and every one of them had gone quiet in the way soldiers go quiet when they realize the weather has stopped being scenery and started being an opponent. Corven sat at the front on his grey gelding, spine straight as a surveyor's plumb line, staring down Rafael Thackeray and that improbable crowd of ferrymen and shepherds as if he could simply out-stubborn them into dispersing.

Ilsa had served under Magistrate Corven for six years. She knew his silences the way sailors know clouds. This one had weather in it.

"Magistrate," she said quietly, reining closer. "Snow's coming in earnest. Look at the light on the eastern ridge."

"I see it."

"The horses won't hold a charge uphill in this. And if it settles—" She didn't finish. She didn't have to. Every rider on that pass knew what early snow did to the high routes: closed them like a door, and kept them closed until spring, and buried whatever fool remained on the wrong side of that door.

Corven's jaw worked. Below him, Thackeray stood with the documents held against his chest—not brandished, not waved about, just *held*, the way a man holds something he has already decided to die for, which is somehow more frightening than any weapon. Behind Thackeray, that absurd civilian wall of thirty-odd souls stood shoulder to shoulder in the wind. Osric the ferryman. A bookbinder woman with frost in her dark hair. The boy Tam, who couldn't have been more than seventeen and was standing like a fence post anyway.

And scattered among them, Ilsa noted with a coldness that had nothing to do with the temperature, were faces she recognized from her own garrison's payroll. Ferrymen who carried Corven's letters. Shepherds who reported to his runners. They were standing *with* Thackeray.

That was the thing Ilsa could not stop looking at. Not the documents. The people.

* * *

Rafael felt the snow arrive the way they felt everything on a mountain: first in the teeth, then in the bones, then everywhere at once. The air changed texture. The light went flat and pearly. Somewhere above them, the wind found its voice.

*First snows,* they'd told Mirela weeks ago in a bookbinder's shop that smelled of glue and secrets. *After that, the passes are shut.* They had beaten the calendar by hours. Perhaps by minutes. Rafael looked up at the sky and thought, with a strange detachment, *we are all of us standing inside the last hour of autumn.*

Corven's voice cut across the pass, pitched to carry. "Thackeray. You are harboring stolen state property and inciting unlawful assembly. Disperse these people and surrender the chest, and I will consider leniency."

"You'll consider it," Rafael called back. Their voice was rough from the climb but steady. "That's generous of you, Magistrate. Will you consider it the way you considered Halvard Bren's route change? In private, after the fact, with no witnesses?"

A ripple went through the riders. Small, but Rafael saw it—and saw Corven see it too. That was the poison of witnesses, Rafael thought. You couldn't un-ring them.

Behind Rafael, Osric shifted his considerable weight and muttered, not entirely under his breath, "If he charges uphill at us, I want it noted I never agreed to be shot. I agreed to stand here. Standing is the extent of my contract."

"You're doing beautifully," Mirela murmured beside him.

"I'm freezing beautifully. There's a difference."

"Shh," said Tam, who had appointed himself the rear guard of a crowd that contained no enemy behind it, and was taking the role very seriously indeed.

Rafael raised the letter—the one from beneath the cairn, Halvard Bren's own hand absolving them by name—and held it up where the front rank of riders could see it. "Sergeant Brandt. You were given orders once to falsify survey records. Some of your colleagues already know it. Ask yourself what happens when this letter reaches the tribunal in the valley town, and then ask yourself whose name appears in it besides mine."

Brandt said nothing. But her horse took a half-step sideways, away from Corven's flank, and Rafael had spent enough years reading mountains and men to know that half-steps were how avalanches started.

* * *

Corven did his arithmetic, and for the first time in twenty years, the numbers refused to behave.

He had come up the southern approach with twelve riders and absolute certainty. Certainty had gotten him this far—a forged concession here, a silenced witness there, a guide's career burned like a field ahead of a fire. Certainty had been his entire method. And now the snow was falling faster, thickening from suggestion to statement, and his permits had expired at dawn, and there were thirty civilians below him who had no legal obligation to obey him at all, and half his riders were looking at Sergeant Brandt instead of at him.

He ran it again. Charge the crowd: impossible, the slope was ice-glazed and the horses would founder before they closed half the distance, and he'd have dead shepherds on the pass and a massacre in every account that survived the winter. Withdraw: the documents lived, the tribunal convened, and his name went into the record beside the words *falsified* and *forged*. Hold position: the snow decided for him, and Corven did not lose to snow. He lost to nothing. He had arranged his whole life so that he would only ever lose to nothing.

But the snow, he was beginning to understand, did not care how he had arranged his life.

"Magistrate," Brandt said again, and this time her voice carried deliberately, so the other riders heard. "The passes close tonight. If we're not down to the shelter rocks by dark, we winter here. All of us."

One of the younger riders—Voss, a boy who still polished his buttons—said, very quietly, "Sir, my mother's in the valley town," which was not a military observation at all, but it landed harder than one.

Corven looked at the crowd below. Thackeray's face was tipped up toward the snow, and there was something unbearable in it—not triumph, nothing so usable as triumph. Relief. The look of a person watching the sky take a side.

"You think this is over," Corven called down, and his voice cracked on the last word, just slightly, a flaw in the plumb line. "You think a letter and a mob make law. I will bury you, Thackeray. I will bury the ferryman, the girl, the boy. I have buried better people than you for less."

"I know," Rafael called back, and the gentleness in it was worse than any insult. "That's rather the whole point of the letter, Magistrate."

For a long moment nobody moved, and the snow came down between them like a curtain being drawn on a play nobody had wanted to end this way.

Then Corven turned his grey gelding.

"Column, about," he said. "We ride for the valley town."

* * *

They went down the southern approach in single file, twelve riders shrinking into the white, and Ilsa Brandt rode at the rear because someone competent had to, and because she did not trust herself to ride near Corven just yet.

Halfway to the shelter rocks, Voss drew up beside her. "Sergeant. The survey records. Is it true? What Thackeray said?"

Ilsa watched the snow fill the hoofprints ahead of them, erasing the column's passage as neatly as if they had never been. "Ask me again at the tribunal," she said.

"And will you answer?"

She was quiet for three switchbacks. "I expect," she said finally, "that answering is going to be the easiest thing I've done all year."

* * *

On the pass, nobody cheered.

Rafael had expected—had braced for—cheering, and its absence confused them until Mirela explained it, or tried to, her gloved hand finding theirs in the deepening white.

"They haven't won anything yet," she said. "Neither have we. Everyone up here understands that. He promised ruin, and Corven keeps his promises; it's the honest part of him." She shivered, and Rafael pulled her hand against their coat without thinking about it. "The tribunal hasn't met. The permits aren't voided. We've only bought the chance to argue."

"Bought," Osric said, stamping his feet, "at the price of standing on a mountain in a blizzard. Someone remind me why I didn't take the debt forgiveness."

"Because you'd have had to live with yourself," Tam said, with the devastating simplicity of the young.

Osric stared at him. "That's exactly it," he said, wounded. "And you said it out loud, which is uncalled for."

But even Osric's grumbling had gone soft at the edges, and when Rafael looked around at the thirty—counting again, because shepherds kept arriving, drawn up off the lower slopes by some shepherd telegraph no one had ever successfully mapped—they were already turning downhill, helping each other, roping the oldest ones together for the descent, moving with the unhurried competence of people who had lived with mountains all their lives and respected the argument the sky was making.

"We need to be off this pass within the hour," Rafael said, and the guide's voice came out of them clean and certain, the old voice, the voice that had been in exile longer than they had. "Single file. Tam on point—he knows the lower traverse. Osric, you take the rear and count heads. Nobody counts themselves; you'll lie."

"I would never," said Osric, who absolutely would.

Mirela lingered a moment, face lifted to the snow. Flakes caught in her lashes and did not melt. "My mother used to say the first snow was Vessia counting her children," she said quietly. "Making sure everyone was still there." Her breath fogged. "Look at us, Mama. Still here. All of us. Even the ones who shouldn't be."

Rafael squeezed her hand. There wasn't anything useful to say, so they said nothing, which between the two of them had lately become its own fluent language.

* * *

The descent was slow, careful, almost tender. The mountain had stopped trying to kill anyone—for now—and seemed content merely to make its opinion known, which it did by dropping a fine dry powder that squeaked underfoot and erased the world beyond ten yards. Rafael navigated by compass and memory, calling turns before they appeared, and the party moved through the whiteout like a thread pulled through cloth.

Twice, Tam stopped the column at places where the cornice above had grown fat and dangerous, and both times he was right, and both times Rafael felt a pride so fierce it was nearly embarrassing. *His first licensed crossing,* Rafael thought, and then corrected it: *if we get him through the winter.*

At the tree line, the wind dropped, and the forest received them with the hushed, forgiving air of a church after a storm. Someone—Osric, inevitably—produced a flask. It went down the line and came back empty, and no one asked what had been in it.

They made camp in the lee of a granite shelf, and the work of survival organized itself the way it does among people who have recently faced death together: silently, cooperatively, with a great deal of gentle teasing. Tam built a fire that smoked only moderately. Mirela unpacked the documents, checked them for damp with the vigilance of a mother checking a sleeping child, and rewrapped them in oilcloth. Osric counted heads, lied about the total by one, and was caught immediately.

"That's thirty-one," Mirela said.

"It's thirty," Osric insisted. "I count myself twice. It's a self-esteem practice."

Rafael sat apart for a moment, at the edge of the firelight, and let the day catch up with them. They had won nothing. Corven's promise hung over the camp like the snow over the pass—patient, inevitable, waiting for spring. The tribunal might be bought. The riders might recant their recanting. The republic's courts had swallowed worse truths than this one whole.

And yet.

Rafael looked at the fire, at the circle of faces around it—shepherds and ferrymen, a bookbinder, a boy, a ferryman lying about arithmetic—and thought about what Ilsa Brandt's half-step sideways meant, and what Voss's question meant, and what thirty civilians standing on a frozen pass meant. Corven had spent twenty years building a machine out of silence. Today, in the space of an afternoon, a dozen gears had started asking questions.

You couldn't un-ring people either.

"You're smiling," Mirela said, settling beside them. "I want it noted that you're smiling at a blizzard."

"I'm smiling at the bookkeeping," Rafael said. "Corven thinks debts only run one direction. He's about to learn what the mountain thinks of his ledgers."

Mirela leaned her head against their shoulder. Above the trees, through a tear in the clouds, stars were coming out—cold, indifferent, magnificent. The snow went on falling, covering the pass, covering the cairn, covering the burned village and the glacier and every road back to Juniper's Landing, sealing the high country like a letter pressed shut.

Which was when Osric, returning from a circuit of the picket lines, stopped short at the edge of the firelight and said, in a voice stripped of all his usual theater:

"There's someone coming up the trail. One man, on foot, no horse. He's flying a white rag on a stick and shouting that he's got dispatches for the exile council—and Rafael, he's half-frozen, and he keeps saying—" Osric paused, frowning, as if he couldn't quite believe the words himself. "He keeps saying he was supposed to reach the pass two days ago, and that the council needs to know the copies arrived safe. Copies. Plural. Says somebody mailed them from Juniper's Landing weeks ago."

Mirela sat bolt upright, and the look that crossed her face was the look of a woman remembering, with enormous satisfaction, exactly what she had done with her Sundays in September.

"Well," she said. "Bring him to the fire."
