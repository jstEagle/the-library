## 35. Down the Mountain

The snow came with them, patient as a debt.

Rafael had guided descents in every season the range allowed, and they knew the truth that no map printed: going down was its own mountain. The legs that had carried them up were spent. The mind that had been sharp with fear was now soft with relief, and soft minds slipped on ice. So Rafael did what they always did on the way down—walked last, counted heads at every turn, and let the mountain believe it was still in charge.

They left Thackeray Pass two days after the standoff, after the tribunal's runners had come up through the southern col with hot food and colder news: Corven's riders had withdrawn to the valley garrison; the permits had expired unchallenged; the documents were safe in three places now, which Mirela said was the minimum number for anything true.

"Four," she'd corrected, tapping her own coat pocket, where she kept the single page she refused to surrender—the original grant bearing her grandfather's name. "Three copies and one heart."

Rafael had not argued. They were learning not to argue with Mirela Vance about what belonged where.

* * *

The first day down, Tam walked point.

It should have been Osric's place, or Rafael's, but Tam had planted himself at the front of the line at dawn without asking, and nobody had stopped him. He carried his pack correctly now—hip belt cinched, straps trimmed, nothing swinging—and he called out the footing before anyone needed telling. *Ice under the drift, left side. Rock bridge, one at a time.* His voice cracked only twice, and both times he covered it by coughing like an old man, which made Mirela smile into her scarf.

"You've grown," she told him at the midday halt, in the tone of someone reporting a scientific finding.

"I have not," Tam said, scandalized. "I'm the same height as at the pass."

"Not the height." She reached up and knocked a knuckle gently against his sternum. "The ballast."

Tam went red to his ears and spent the next hour being aggressively competent about the rope coils, which was its own kind of confession.

Rafael watched him from behind and felt something loosen in their chest that had been tight for twenty years. It wasn't pride exactly—or it was, but pride with a floor under it, the kind that didn't tip over into fear. On the ascent, Rafael had caught themselves teaching Tam the way Corven had once taught them: by trusting him with real weight. The difference was that Rafael remembered what it cost to be trusted by the wrong person, and so they never looked away while Tam carried anything that mattered.

At the second river crossing, the water running gray-green with glacier milk, Tam anchored the ferry line himself and talked Osric through the knots rather than tying them. Osric grumbled the whole time—"I've run this crossing since before your father could whistle"—but his hands did what the boy's voice said, and when the last pack came across dry, Osric clapped Tam on the shoulder hard enough to stagger him.

"Steady hands," Osric announced, to the river as much as to anyone. "Wasted on a ferry, maybe. We'll see."

Tam glowed like a banked fire all afternoon.

* * *

They camped that night in the lee of the burned village—not the Vessian one, higher up, where Mirela had named the dead, but a shepherd's settlement abandoned longer ago, its stone walls shoulder-high and softened with moss. Rafael got the fire going while Mirela walked the perimeter of the ruins, touching the doorposts the way other people touched gravestones.

When she came back, she sat close enough that their shoulders touched, and neither of them moved away.

"Do you know what I keep thinking?" she said. The firelight did something kind to the sharp lines of her face. "I spent six years learning to bind books so I could repair things. Spines, mostly. Broken spines." She huffed a small laugh. "I thought that was the work. Fixing broken things quietly, in a back room, where nobody sees."

"And now?"

"Now I think the work was always going to be loud." She turned her hands toward the flames. Her fingers were cracked from cold and rope-burn, and Rafael wanted to hold them and did not yet. "You can't restore a country in a back room, Rafael. You can't even restore a man that way. Believe me. I watched you try."

Rafael looked at the fire. "I wasn't restoring myself. I was hiding."

"Were those different things, where you stood?"

The question sat between them, warm and uncomfortable as a stone from the coals. Rafael turned it over honestly, because Mirela had earned honesty the way climbers earn a summit—one hard step at a time.

"For a long time, no," they said finally. "Hiding felt like safety. Then hiding felt like punishment. Somewhere on the glacier—I don't know which day—it stopped feeling like either. It started feeling like waiting."

"For what?"

"For someone to ask me the right question, I suppose." Rafael glanced sideways at her. "You're insufferably good at that."

"I'm a bookbinder. We open things carefully or we ruin them." She smiled, and Rafael felt the smile land somewhere below the ribs, in a place they'd boarded up years ago. "You opened."

Above them, the wind moved through the pass they'd crossed, and Rafael listened to it the way you listen to a door closing on a room you'll never need again.

* * *

Osric sang on the third day.

This was how Rafael knew the old man had made his peace—or at least signed a truce with it. Osric only sang when the guilt got too heavy to carry silently, river songs mostly, bawdy and off-key, the kind of music that dared you to be solemn near it. He sang about a ferryman's daughter with improbable talents, and Tam begged him to stop, and Mirela translated fragments into Vessian for her own amusement, making everything worse.

But between verses, on the long traverse above the gorge, Osric dropped back beside Rafael and was quiet for half a mile, which for Osric was a geological era.

"The debt," he said at last, not looking at them. "You forgave it. In front of everyone. And I want to say—" He stopped. Started again. "I sold you once. Nearly twice. A man my age ought to know better than to put a price on people, and I did it anyway, and you just—" He made a gesture that encompassed forgiveness, fury, and indigestion all at once.

"Osric."

"Don't 'Osric' me, I'm confessing."

"You're singing about a ferryman's daughter."

"That's part of the confession. She was real." He scowled at the trail. "What I mean is, I don't understand it. Mercy. I've lived sixty-one years on this river and I've never once seen it given free. There's always a rate."

Rafael considered the question seriously, because Osric deserved that much. "I spent two years thinking I owed everyone. The dead client. The guild. The mountain. Every debt compounded until I couldn't move under the weight." They stepped over a frozen stream, offered Osric a hand across the slick stones, which he took. "Then I forgave yours, and I felt lighter. Not because you deserved it—you didn't, particularly—but because I finally understood the debts I couldn't pay weren't mine anymore. Some of them never were. Corven built most of them. I was just carrying his freight."

Osric was silent another quarter mile. Then: "So mercy's selfish."

"Completely."

"Huh." He nodded slowly, satisfied, and moved forward to sing about the ferryman's daughter again, louder than before, which Rafael understood to mean *thank you* in the only dialect Osric spoke fluently.

* * *

On the fourth day the valley opened below them like a held breath released—the Serrano running wide and slow through its winter brown fields, Juniper's Landing a scatter of smoke among the poplars, and beyond it, faint on the horizon, the flat gray line of the lowlands where the tribunals sat and the world did its paperwork.

Mirela stood beside Rafael at the overlook, and after a moment, without ceremony, she took their hand.

Not dramatically. Not the way it happened in the ballads Tam secretly read. She simply laced her cold fingers through theirs, adjusted the fit once, and kept looking at the valley, as though her hand had always lived there and had merely been away on a long expedition.

Rafael's heart performed several maneuvers they would have found undignified in a client.

"Well," Mirela said.

"Well," Rafael agreed.

"That's settled, then."

"It appears to be."

She squeezed their hand once, precisely, like a signature, and they began the descent into the trees.

That night, camped within sight of the ferry landing's lanterns, Rafael lay awake listening to the party breathe—Tam's steady rhythm, Osric's heroic snoring, Mirela's quiet—and understood that the fear was gone. Not the memory of it; that would stay, the way scars stay, useful in cold weather. But the living weight of it, the thing that had sat on their chest every morning for two years, checking the roads, counting the exits—that had stayed up on the mountain somewhere, probably at the cairn, probably keeping the dead client company.

Their name was going to be spoken in the valley tomorrow. In the taverns, at the guild hall, in the tribunal record. *Thackeray.* For two years the name had been a wound; now it was going to be a word again, ordinary, spoken without flinching, including by Rafael themselves.

They fell asleep planning nothing, which was the strangest luxury of all.

* * *

Juniper's Landing met them at the landing itself, because of course it did—Osric had sent word ahead by the last ferry pigeon, and word in a border town travels faster than any bird. Half the town stood on the dock in the falling snow: ferrymen with their caps off, shepherds who'd stood at the pass, the innkeeper with a barrel already broached, and old Marta from the bakery, who took one look at Rafael and said, loudly enough for the whole dock, "There's our guide, then."

Our guide.

Rafael had braced for suspicion, for sidelong looks, for the careful distance towns keep around a story they're not sure is finished. Instead Marta pressed a loaf still warm through its cloth into their hands, and a fisherman's wife shook their hand with both of hers, and a boy of perhaps eight asked, with devastating directness, if it was true Rafael had outrun twelve riders on a glacier.

"One rider," Rafael said. "And I didn't outrun him. My friends handled that."

"Friends," the boy repeated, tasting the word, looking at Tam with naked envy.

Tam, standing straighter than Rafael had ever seen him, said, "I'm going to be licensed in spring," in the tone of a man announcing a coronation.

"He is," Rafael said. "I've seen his rope-work."

The cheer that went up embarrassed everyone equally, which Rafael supposed was the point of cheers.

Later, in the tavern, warm and loud and smelling of bread and spilled ale, Rafael sat wedged between Mirela and the window, watching snow come down on the harbor lights, and let the evening happen to them. Osric held court by the fire, re-telling the glacier crossing with himself as hero and the crevasse "wide as a church." Tam corrected the width twice, gave up, and glowed. Mirela's shoulder rested against Rafael's arm, and her hand found theirs under the table without either of them looking, the way you find a familiar tool in the dark.

Halfway through the evening, the tavern door opened and let in a swirl of snow and a rider in tribunal gray, travel-worn, stamping ice from her boots. The room quieted in the way rooms do when authority walks in.

She scanned the room, found Rafael, and crossed to them directly.

"Rafael Thackeray?" At Rafael's nod, she produced a sealed letter, wax stamped with the tribunal's scales. "Summons. You're requested to testify—the hearings begin in four days. The concessions, the permits, the matter of Magistrate Corven." Her eyes flicked, briefly, to the room full of witnesses, and something almost like sympathy crossed her face. "You'll want to read the enclosure."

Rafael broke the seal. Inside, beneath the formal summons, a second sheet in a clerk's careful hand:

*Magistrate Edran Corven has been taken into custody by the garrison at Juniper's Landing, on charges including falsification of survey records, conspiracy, and attempted destruction of public archives. He has named no accomplices. He has asked, repeatedly, to speak with you.*

The tavern noise came back slowly, like tide returning. Mirela read the letter over Rafael's shoulder, and Rafael felt her go very still.

"He wants to talk to you," she said quietly.

"I know."

"After everything." Her hand tightened on theirs. "What are you going to do?"

Outside, the snow thickened against the windows, and Rafael looked at the fire for a long moment before answering.

"I'm going to sleep on it," they said. "For the first time in two years, I can afford to."

But even as they said it, Rafael knew—knew the way they knew weather coming, the way they knew load-bearing ice—that sooner or later, they would walk into whatever cell held Corven, and sit across from the man who had built their ruin, and hear what he thought he had to say.

The question was whether they would go as the ghost he'd made, or as the witness they'd become.

Rafael ordered another round for the table, and decided to let the mountain decide. It had better judgment than they did, and a longer memory.
