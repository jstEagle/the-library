## 36. Corven's Last Card

Magistrate Aldous Corven had always believed that a man's ruin could be read in his ledgers, the way a physician read fever in the skin. For thirty years he had kept his own books immaculate—every favor entered, every debt discharged, every compromise recorded in a cipher of his own devising that no clerk could crack and no confessor could absolve. He had built a career the way other men built bridges: deliberately, load by load, with no ornament that did not also bear weight.

Now, on a gray morning in late autumn, he sat in his office above the Juniper's Landing garrison house and read a single sheet of paper that had arrived by courier two days past, and he understood that the bridge was singing.

*By order of the Chancellor's Office, an inquiry is hereby convened into the administration of survey concessions, border registrations, and associated instruments executed under your authority during the years following annexation. You are requested—not yet summoned—to remain at your post and render full cooperation.*

*Requested.* The word was a courtesy, and courtesies from chancellors were like calm water over a weir: they told you nothing except how fast you were about to go over.

Corven set the paper down, aligned its edges with the blotter, and allowed himself the small luxury of honesty. He had known this day would come. He had known it since the chest came up out of the Serrano River, since Thackeray—the disgraced guide, the convenient scapegoat, the man Corven had spent two decades keeping small—had walked back down off the mountain with witnesses behind him and a letter in his hand that named Corven's own orders in Corven's own words.

He had lost the pass. He had lost the standoff. He had lost Sergeant Brandt, who had stood in front of thirty civilians and twelve of his own riders and testified in a clear voice about falsified survey records, and who had then requested transfer to a posting so remote it was rumored to consist of one goat and a signal flag. He had even, in a sense, lost the snow, which had fallen that afternoon with the impartial malice of nature settling a bet.

But Corven was not a man who confused losing a battle with losing the war. There remained, in Juniper's Landing, a room behind the records office where the original concession instruments were stored pending the tribunal. Paper, he reminded himself, burned beautifully.

* * *

He went down to the garrison yard that evening to take the air, as was his habit, and found the mood of his men changed in ways he could not immediately name. They still saluted. They still stepped aside. But the salutes had a new texture—slower, more deliberate, the salute of men making sure you saw them doing it.

Sergeant Voss, his adjutant for eleven years, met him by the stable with a lantern held slightly too high, so that Corven had to squint.

"Word from the valley town," Voss said. "The tribunal's sitting next week. They've subpoenaed the archive."

"I'm aware."

"They've also subpoenaed—" Voss paused, and Corven watched him choose between loyalty and accuracy, and watched accuracy win, because it always did eventually, that was the whole trouble with it. "They've subpoenaed your private correspondence, sir. The cipher letters."

"Then the tribunal will have the pleasure of learning to read," Corven said dryly, "which will be good for them."

Voss didn't smile. That was the second thing Corven noticed. Eleven years, and Voss had smiled at every joke, including the bad ones, *especially* the bad ones, out of what Corven had always assumed was professional discipline. Tonight there was nothing.

"The men are asking questions, sir," Voss said quietly. "About Brandt. About what she said at the pass. About whether the survey records were—" He stopped himself, which was more than most men would have managed. "The men are asking questions."

"Questions are free," Corven said. "Answers cost." He turned back toward the office. "Have the night watch doubled at the records office. I want no loiterers."

"Yes, sir." A pause. "Sir—is there something we should be watching *for*?"

Corven looked at him for a long moment. "Fire," he said. "Towns like this burn. It's practically a tradition."

Voss wrote it down. And Corven, walking away, understood that he had just made an error he would once have caught: he had given a careful man a reason to be careful *about him.*

* * *

The plan, when he finally committed it to paper—in cipher, naturally, though he was beginning to suspect ciphers were only honest men's secrets waiting to be dishonest—was elegant in the old way. Not arson, which was vulgar and traceable. A chimney fire. The records office shared a flue with the ferry-master's counting house next door, and flues were notorious, and towns were sympathetic about flues. By morning there would be ash where the grants had been, and without the originals, the tribunal would be trying a ghost. Testimony could be dismissed. Copies could be challenged. But a thing that no longer existed could not be proven anything at all, and Corven had built twenty years on exactly that principle.

What he failed to account for—and this, later, in the cell, in the long gray afternoons, was the part that genuinely astonished him—was Tam.

* * *

Tam Weller was seventeen, employed by the ferry, devoted to Rafael Thackeray with the uncomplicated ferocity of a young man who has decided what goodness looks like and intends to guard it personally. Corven knew him only as a name attached to reports: *the Weller boy was seen carrying documents; the Weller boy was asking after riders on the southern road.* Small nuisance. Background weather.

What Corven did not know was that Tam had appointed himself the archive's guardian with the same total seriousness, and that Tam, in the manner of the young, had told absolutely everyone.

"He talks," Osric Ferrar explained later, in testimony that Corven would hear through a wall and find, against his will, funny. "That's the whole of it. Boy can't keep a secret the way other folk can't swim. He tries, mind. You can see him trying. Face goes all purple with the effort. But sooner or later it comes out of him like water finding a low place, and the low place was me, and I'll tell you true—I didn't much fancy letting a magistrate burn the only honest paper this valley's ever produced."

So when Corven's man—a hired chimney-sweep named Dullard, whose name Corven had taken as an omen and ignored—came sniffing around the flue on the third evening, word reached the ferry landing before the sweep had finished his supper.

* * *

What followed was, by any military standard, a shambles, and by the standards of Juniper's Landing, a triumph.

There was no plan. There was Osric, who owned the counting house and therefore the flue, declaring loudly at the Ferryman's Rest that he'd been meaning to have his chimneys swept for years and would thank nobody to do it now, he liked his chimneys filthy, they kept the draft honest. There were six ferrymen who suddenly developed a passionate interest in card games conducted directly beneath the records office window, in all weathers, at all hours. There was Mirela Vance, who did something in the records office itself that Corven never fully reconstructed but which involved, according to the clerk, "a great many labels being moved," so that within a day the concession originals were filed under headings like *GARDEN SEEDS, PERSONAL* and *HYMNS, ASSORTED,* while the drawer marked *CONCESSION INSTRUMENTS* contained a decade of laundry lists and one very old sandwich.

And there was Tam, who took it upon himself to stand watch, and who—this was the detail that would circulate through the valley for years, growing grander with each telling—fell asleep.

He fell asleep in the records office doorway on the fourth night, wrapped in Osric's spare coat, and woke at dawn to find Dullard the chimney-sweep standing over him with a brush and a look of profound professional embarrassment, because Dullard, whatever his faults, had not expected his crime to come with a witness, and certainly not one who greeted him with a cheerful "Morning! Are you the sweep? Because if you're the sweep, you're three days early, and if you're not the sweep, you're under arrest, and either way my boots are wet."

Dullard ran. Tam, being seventeen and having just woken up, did not chase him so much as lurch after him shouting, which was sufficient. Word went to the garrison. And here Corven's blueprint, examined closely, showed its flaw: he had planned for fire, for theft, for silence. He had not planned for a boy announcing the whole scheme to a streetful of ferrymen before breakfast.

* * *

Corven heard the news within the hour, and did the arithmetic he had done all his life, and for the first time the columns refused to balance.

If Dullard talked—and sweeps talked, it was practically the trade—the inquiry became an indictment. If Dullard stayed silent, Corven could plausibly disavow him, but the disavowal required the garrison to believe it, and the garrison had stopped believing things on a mountainside two weeks ago, in the snow, in front of a shepherd named Brakka and a woman named Vance and a guide who used to be a ghost.

He considered flight. He was honest enough, alone in his office, to note the consideration and set it aside: flight was for men with somewhere to be, and every road out of Juniper's Landing now ended at a tribunal.

So he did the last thing his blueprints allowed. He went down to the records office himself, in person, at dusk, with a lantern and a key, intending—at least this is what he told the tribunal, and what he half-believed—to inspect the security arrangements personally.

What actually happened, according to the six card-playing ferrymen, was this:

Magistrate Corven unlocked the records office, stood in the doorway for the better part of a minute, and then was joined by Sergeant Voss, who had followed him from the garrison at a distance that fooled no one, and by Tam, who materialized out of a woodpile with the timing of a born dramatist, and by Osric, who arrived last and breathing hardest and stood in the street with his arms folded like a man attending a hanging he'd paid for.

"You'll want the GARDEN SEEDS drawer," Tam offered helpfully, into the silence. "The real ones. We moved everything. It took ages. The sandwich was already there, though. Nobody claims the sandwich."

Corven looked at the boy. Then at Voss. Then at the lantern in his own hand, which had been meant, everyone understood, for the flue.

"Sergeant," Corven said at last, and his voice was level, and he would be credited later—even by people who hated him—for that. "Am I under arrest?"

Voss shifted his weight. "Inquiry's asked for your cooperation, sir. Full cooperation. And the men—" he glanced at the ferrymen, at the boy, at the darkening street where more shapes were gathering, shepherds, boatmen, a woman with a bookbinder's ink-stained fingers— "the men think cooperation starts tonight."

"It does," said a voice from the edge of the group, and Rafael Thackeray stepped forward, hatless, unhurried, wearing the expression of a man who had carried a great weight a long way and finally set it down. "It started on the mountain, Magistrate. You're just catching up."

Corven set the lantern down on the step. Very carefully. Aligned it with the doorframe, because a lifetime of straight edges does not release a man easily.

"In my defense," he said, to no one and everyone, "I built the roads you're all standing on."

"Aye," said Osric comfortably. "And we're tolling 'em."

* * *

They took him back to the garrison, which was now, by unanimous and unspoken consent of the garrison, no longer his. Voss drew up the arrest papers himself, in plain language, no cipher, and read them aloud, and Corven signed with the neat, pressureless hand that had signed twenty years of quiet thefts.

They gave him a cell with a window. He noted this too, catalogued it, entered it in the ledger of his mind under *small mercies, unearned.*

On the first night, unable to sleep, he listened to the river. It was louder than he remembered—or perhaps he had simply never been on the listening side of it before. All those years he had stood above the current, directing it, damming it, forging its permits, and now it ran past his window indifferent as weather, carrying ice, carrying light, carrying toward the valley town a tribunal and a chest of papers and a boy who couldn't keep a secret and a guide who wouldn't stay drowned.

He thought about the compass Thackeray had carried. He'd heard the story by now, the way stories travel even into cells—how it had belonged to a dead client, how its needle had pulled toward a cache under a cairn, how memory itself had turned out to be the surest instrument on the mountain. Corven had spent his life believing that the past was a country best paved over. He had built his roads on it. And the frost had come up through the paving, year by year, heave by heave, until the whole surface broke.

*Frost heaves,* he thought, absurdly, staring at the ceiling. *Should have accounted for frost heaves.*

Near dawn, footsteps stopped outside his door. Through the barred window slit he saw Tam, on some errand or other, pause, look in at him with frank seventeen-year-old curiosity, and say—not cruelly, which was somehow worse—

"You know what I don't understand, Magistrate? You were clever enough to forge everything. Why weren't you clever enough to just *not*?"

Corven opened his mouth to explain—about systems, about necessity, about the difference between a man and the machine a man serves—and found, to his genuine surprise, that he had no answer that would fit through the bars.

The boy shrugged and went on about his errand. Down the corridor, a guard laughed at something. Somewhere beyond the walls the ferrymen were loading the archive onto a barge for the trip downriver to the tribunal, and the river took it, and the current, as it had been trying to tell him for twenty years, went on.

Three days later, the summons came at last—no longer *requested*, no longer courteous—and Corven was put on a barge himself, bound for the valley town, for the witness box, for the reading aloud of everything.

He spent the journey rehearsing. And it was not until the tribunal hall doors opened, and he saw at the witness table a bookbinder arranging her papers with a curator's precision, that he understood the inquiry was not the end of it.

She looked up at him, and there was something in her face he recognized with a cold, professional admiration: the look of a person holding a document they have been saving.

"Magistrate Corven," Mirela Vance said pleasantly, "we have so much to go through."
