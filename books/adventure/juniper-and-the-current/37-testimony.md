## 37. Testimony

The valley town of Corren's Ford had one courthouse, two churches, and a bell that rang for fires, funerals, and—on this particular morning—for the reading of a nation back into existence.

Mirela Vance stood at the window of the room she'd been given above the inn and watched people come up the street in ones and twos: ferrymen with their collars scrubbed stiff, shepherd women in their good shawls, old men who'd walked three days from the high valleys because they'd heard that Vessia was going to be spoken aloud in a courtroom and they intended to be present when it happened. Some of them carried flowers. Not bouquets, exactly—more like armfuls of juniper cut from the hillsides, the branches still silvered with frost, because someone had decided that juniper was the right thing to bring and the decision had spread through the crowd like warmth through bread.

"They'll need to open the windows," Tam said behind her, already dressed, already nervous, his hair attempting several directions at once. "There's going to be four hundred people in a room built for sixty."

"Then we'll all get very well acquainted," Mirela said.

She was not nervous. That was the strange part. She had spent twenty years being nervous—nervous in border towns, nervous around magistrates' clerks, nervous whenever anyone asked where her accent was from—and now, on the morning when everything she had carried since she was nine years old would finally be weighed by strangers, she felt only a deep and level calm, like water that has stopped arguing with its banks.

Perhaps it was because the documents were no longer hers alone. For twenty years they had been a secret, and secrets are heavy in a way that has nothing to do with weight. Now they were everyone's.

* * *

The courthouse had been built by optimists who never imagined a full gallery, so the tribunal sat in the magistrate's raised box while the overflow stood along the walls, filled the aisles, and spilled out onto the steps, where a ferryman named Doss read each page aloud through the open doors to those who couldn't fit inside. By mid-morning his voice was hoarse and someone had brought him honeyed tea, and by late morning there were three relays of readers working in shifts, which meant the testimony was now traveling through the town faster than the tribunal could hear it.

Mirela sat at the petitioners' table between Rafael and Osric, with Tam pressed against her other side like a barnacle with excellent posture. Across the aisle, under guard, sat Magistrate Corven.

He looked smaller than he had on the mountain. That was the first thing she noticed, and she was ashamed of how much satisfaction she took in it. He wore civilian grey instead of his magistrate's sash—the sash had been taken from him the day his own garrison arrested him—and without it he seemed less like an institution and more like what he was: a thin man of fifty-odd years with careful hands and eyes that moved constantly, doing arithmetic even now. She caught him counting heads in the gallery, calculating odds, and understood that some men could not stop being what they were any more than a river could stop running downhill.

The presiding judge was a woman named Halvard, broad-faced and unhurried, with the kind of patience that made liars exhausted just looking at her. She had been a land arbitrator before the annexation courts swallowed her career, and everyone said she had waited twenty years for a case worth her attention.

"Read the grants again," Judge Halvard said, for the third time that morning. "Slowly."

So they read them again. The original Vessian land grants—signed, sealed, witnessed—granting the eastern slopes and the Serrano headwaters to families whose names Mirela knew the way other people knew prayers: Aldervane. Kostic. The Marchetti line of the upper gorge. Her own grandmother's name was in the third document, in a clerk's careful hand, transferring grazing rights to a woman named Yelena Vance, and when the reader reached it Mirela did not look down at the table or squeeze Rafael's hand, though both were available to her. She sat still and let her grandmother's name be spoken in a courtroom, publicly, as a true thing. It had been so long since anything about Vessia had been permitted to be a true thing in public that the moment felt almost physical, like a bone setting.

Then came the forged transfers—the "concession permits" under which the republic's mining consortium had worked those same slopes for two decades, each one purporting to derive from a sale that had never occurred, each stamped with a seal whose authority had legally died with the annexation. Then the survey records, falsified on Corven's direct order, as Sergeant Ilsa Brandt had testified the previous afternoon in a voice that never wavered and never rose, which somehow made it worse for the defense than shouting would have been.

Corven's advocate—a pale, competent man who had clearly drawn the short straw among his colleagues—rose once more near noon.

"The tribunal will note," he said, "that my client's objection is procedural. Vessia, as a legal entity, no longer exists. Grants issued by a dissolved state cannot—"

Judge Halvard looked at him over her spectacles with the expression of a woman watching a child insist the stove isn't hot.

"Counselor," she said, "the land exists. The families exist. I am looking at forty of them right now, and several more are standing on my steps. Whatever else may be argued about states, I have not yet been introduced to a lawyer who can argue a family out of existing. Sit down."

He sat down. In the gallery, somebody laughed—not cruelly, exactly, but with the relief of people who had braced for a long fight and were watching it end early.

* * *

They broke for the noon meal, and the town fed everyone. This was Corren's Ford's answer to a crisis: soup. Great cauldrons of it appeared in the square as if summoned, and Mirela found herself seated on a bench between an eighty-year-old shepherd named Batta Kostic—who claimed, with mounting enthusiasm, to be a distant cousin twice removed, a claim Mirela neither confirmed nor denied because the soup was excellent and the company better—and Osric, who was eating enough for three men and radiating the specific contentment of a man whose debts had recently been forgiven in front of witnesses.

"You know what I keep thinking," Osric said, gesturing with his spoon. "Twenty years. Twenty years those papers sat in the dark, and then the river just—" he mimed a lazy current with his hand, "—hands them up. Like it was holding them for us."

"Rivers don't hold things for people," Mirela said.

"This one does. I've worked it thirty years. It's got opinions." He leaned toward her, lowering his voice to a stage whisper audible to the entire bench. "It also gave up Rafael Thackeray, if you think about it. Washed him across my border two years back looking half-drowned and twice-ashamed, and look at him now. Sitting up straight. Eating like a person."

Mirela glanced down the bench. Rafael was talking quietly with Sergeant Brandt, of all people—the two of them bent together over a shared map, pointing at places on the southern approach, Brandt nodding, Rafael's hands steady. There had been a time, Mirela knew, when Rafael could not sit with a uniformed officer without his whole body preparing to run. She watched him now, laughing briefly at something Brandt said, and felt the tenderness she'd stopped trying to name months ago settle over her again like a coat that fit.

"He eats like a person," she agreed, "because someone finally taught him the soup here is compulsory."

Batta Kostic tugged her sleeve. "The Vance name," the old woman said. "Upper gorge?"

"My grandmother. Yelena."

Batta closed her eyes. "I drank plum brandy with a Yelena Vance in the summer of the last fair, before it all went dark. She beat me at cards and refused to gloat, which was the most infuriating thing about her." She opened her eyes and gripped Mirela's wrist with surprising strength. "Tomorrow, when they read the restoration order, you stand where I can see you. I want to watch a Vance hear it."

Mirela's throat did something complicated. "I will," she managed.

* * *

The afternoon belonged to the families.

One by one they came forward—not to testify, exactly; the legal questions had been settled by paper—but to be heard, which Judge Halvard ruled was different and more important. An old man described the night soldiers burned his village and how his mother had buried the family's grant deed in a tin box beneath a pear tree, and how he had gone back for it every year for ten years until the tree itself was cut down, and how he had assumed the paper was lost forever until this morning, when a bookbinder from Juniper's Landing had put a copy in his hands.

"That copy," the old man said, holding it up with both hands, trembling slightly, "is the first piece of paper in twenty years that says my mother told the truth."

A woman spoke about her father who had died waiting for restitution that never came, and asked only that his name be entered into the record. Judge Halvard entered it herself, writing slowly, saying the name aloud as she wrote so it was spoken into the court's memory twice.

Tam, sitting beside Mirela, had gone very quiet during these testimonies, and stayed quiet, which for Tam was its own form of eloquence. When the session ended for the day he said, mostly to his boots, "I used to think history was something in books. Big dead stuff." He shrugged. "Turns out it's just people waiting a long time for somebody to write their names down properly."

"Out of the mouths of ferry-hands," Osric said solemnly, and cuffed him gently on the ear.

* * *

On the third day, Rafael testified last.

Mirela had known he would be called last—Judge Halvard arranged her courtroom the way a good guide arranges a climb, hardest pitch saved for when the party is warmed and trusting—and she watched him rise from the petitioners' table and walk to the witness stand with the unhurried gait of a man crossing terrain he has chosen himself.

He was sworn in. He gave his name plainly: Rafael Thackeray. Just hearing it said aloud in that room, without flinching attached, did something to the air.

He told it in order, and simply. The chest in the river. The seal. The documents. The debt to Osric, which he recounted with such dry precision that Osric's ears went red and the gallery chuckled. Corven's offer—a clean passport, debt forgiveness, silence—which Rafael declined, and he described the declining without heroics, as though it had been less a decision than a fact of his nature, the way stone declines to float. The expedition. The storm at Windrow Col. The ambush in the Narrows, where Tam was taken and the chest seized. His voice stayed level throughout, and Mirela realized she was the only one in the building who could see, in the set of his shoulders, what each sentence cost.

Then Judge Halvard asked the question the whole valley had been waiting for.

"Mr. Thackeray. Eight years ago you were blacklisted by the mountain guild following the death of a client on the Vessian side of the range. The record attributes that death to your judgment." She folded her hands. "Tell the tribunal what actually happened."

The room went still. Even Corven's arithmetic eyes stopped moving.

Rafael looked at the judge, and then—he must have known exactly where she was, because he always knew—at Mirela. And then he told it. How the route had been changed by order, not by weather. How the survey records had been altered afterward to place the fault on the guide. How he had carried the blame for eight years because the alternative was a word against a magistrate's, and everyone knew which word the world believed.

"I didn't lose my license because I failed," he said, and his voice did not shake, and Mirela fell a little further in love listening to it not shake. "I lost it because I was convenient. But I'll say this for the record, because it matters more than my name does: Elena Marchetti died because a man wanted her family's land and needed her family's claim discredited. Everything else—the blame, the blacklist, the eight years—was bookkeeping."

He produced the letter then—the one from the cache beneath the cairn, Elena's own hand, absolving him by name—and the clerk read it aloud, and in the gallery Batta Kostic wept openly and without embarrassment, and Mirela did not weep, but only because she was holding very still, the way you hold still when something broken inside you is being set.

Judge Halvard let the silence stand for a long moment after the letter was read. Then she said, quietly, "The guild will be notified. Formally." And turned the page.

* * *

The verdict, when it came on the fourth day, took eleven minutes to read and twenty years to earn.

The forged transfers were void. The concessions were dissolved. The original grants were recognized, restored, and ordered registered. Restitution proceedings would follow under Judge Halvard's supervision, and she announced this with the grim cheer of a woman who intended to spend her remaining career making mining consortiums miserable. Magistrate Corven was remanded for trial on charges that the clerk read for a full quarter hour—falsification, conspiracy, extortion, and the deaths, named individually, that had been purchased to protect the scheme.

As guards moved to take him, Corven paused at the bar of the court. He did not look at Rafael, or at Mirela, or at the packed gallery of people whose lives he had spent two decades rearranging for profit. He looked at Judge Halvard.

"You understand what you've done," he said. "Every claim, every boundary in three provinces. You've opened twenty years of litigation. There will be chaos."

"There will be paperwork," Judge Halvard agreed placidly. "Chaos is what you made. Paperwork is what we're cleaning up. Take him down."

And that was all. No thunderclap. No vengeance. A tired, honest process, grinding forward at the speed of justice, which is slow precisely so that it cannot be rushed into cruelty. Mirela walked out of the courthouse into cold bright sunlight and found the steps carpeted with juniper branches, and the readers on the steps were already reading the verdict aloud to the crowd beyond, and somewhere a fiddle had started up, badly, with great conviction.

Tam appeared at her elbow, breathless. "Osric's buying drinks at the ferry landing. All of them. He says it's mathematically impossible for him to spend too much tonight given what he owes the universe."

"Go on ahead," Mirela said. "We'll follow."

She waited on the courthouse steps until Rafael came out, blinking in the light like a man surfacing. They stood together a moment, shoulder to shoulder, watching the crowd, the juniper, the terrible fiddle.

"Well," she said. "You're cleared. Officially. There's a document and everything."

"Mm." He was quiet a moment. "Strange. I thought it would feel bigger."

"It doesn't feel big," Mirela said. "It feels like a Tuesday. That's how you know it's real. The false things are always dramatic. The true things just... become the weather."

He smiled—that rare, unguarded one—and put his arm around her, and they started down the steps into the crowd, where Batta Kostic was already waving them toward the ferry landing and Osric's ruinous generosity.

But halfway down, a guild runner pushed through the throng, breathless, travel-stained, a leather dispatch case strapped across his chest. He scanned the steps, found Rafael, and held out a sealed letter bearing the mountain guild's crest.

"Thackeray? R.?" the runner said. "This came up from the valley office this morning. They said you'd want it today."

Rafael took it, turned it over once, and broke the seal—and Mirela watched his face change as he read, watched something move through it that was not grief and not quite joy, and heard him say, half to her and half to the whole waiting valley:

"They've convened the licensing board. My hearing's in Juniper's Landing. Next week."
