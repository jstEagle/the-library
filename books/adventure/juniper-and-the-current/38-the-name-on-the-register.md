## 38. The Name on the Register

The guildhall in Juniper's Landing smelled the same as it had eleven years ago: beeswax, cold stone, and the particular staleness of paper that had been argued over too long. Rafael stood in the doorway a full minute before going in, because some doors you had to let yourself want to walk through first.

Inside, everything was smaller than memory insisted. The great ledger still sat open on its iron stand by the window, its pages thick as slate, its corners worn round by forty years of thumbs. The hearing table had been pushed against the wall to make room for chairs—more chairs than Rafael had ever seen in this room, and nearly all of them full. Ferrymen in their good coats. Shepherds who still smelled of the high pasture despite the season. Tam's mother, who had brought a basket of seed rolls and was distributing them with the stern generosity of a woman who believed no one should testify hungry. Sergeant Ilsa Brandt, out of uniform, sitting very straight near the front as though she were still awaiting orders and had decided, permanently, not to follow them.

And Mirela, third row back, hands folded, watching Rafael with an expression that managed to be both *I told you this would go fine* and *I will fight anyone in this room who says otherwise.*

"You're blocking the light," said Master Ellery Voss, the guild registrar, without looking up from his papers. He was seventy if he was a day, half-blind in one eye, and had signed Rafael's original apprenticeship papers back when Rafael Thackeray had been nobody at all—just a river kid with good balance and a head for weather.

"Sorry, Master Voss."

"Don't apologize. Observe." Voss finally raised his head. His good eye was sharp as a chisel. "Eleven years ago you stood in this hall and heard your license struck from the register. Do you remember what I said?"

Rafael did. Every word. *"The guild does not judge the mountain. The mountain has judged. We merely record."* It had felt like being buried under an avalanche of paperwork.

"I remember," Rafael said.

"Good. Then you'll appreciate that I intend to say something different today, and I've been practicing." Voss cleared his throat, consulted a card he clearly did not need, and put it away. "The guild does not judge the mountain. The mountain has already rendered its verdict—in snow, in ice, in thirty civilians standing on a pass in the first storm of winter, refusing to move for twelve armed riders. We are merely recording it. And the record reads: *cleared.*"

A sound went through the room—not quite applause, not yet. Something held in reserve, like a rope under tension.

Voss turned the great ledger toward himself and licked his thumb. "Thackeray, R. Originally registered spring, year of the annexation surveys. Struck—" here his pen hovered, and Rafael watched the old man's jaw tighten, "—struck under administrative order, which is a polite word for what Magistrate Corven made us do. Today I am entering the restoration. Read it back to me, someone with younger eyes."

Tam shot up from his seat like a cork from water, leaned over the stand, and read aloud, slow and careful, savoring every word:

"*Thackeray, R.—guide, cleared.*"

"That's it," said Voss. "That's the whole entry. Eleven years of ruin, undone by four words and a date." He set down the pen. "Some of you expected speeches. There will be none. The guild apologizes for nothing today except how long it took."

That broke the tension, and the room came alive—handshakes, back-slaps, Osric bellowing something about how he'd always known Rafael was innocent, which drew a snort from Mirela, since Osric had spent two years threatening to sell Rafael to Corven and only changed sides under conditions Rafael had generously agreed never to specify in public.

* * *

The formalities took until midday. There was testimony to enter into the record—Ilsa Brandt gave hers again, flat and unflinching, and Rafael noticed several guild members could not look at her while she did it, and noticed also that she made them, walking her gaze down each row until they did. There was the reading of the letter found beneath the cairn—the dead client's own hand, absolving Rafael by name—and Rafael had braced for it to hurt the way it had hurt by firelight on the mountain, but instead it landed strangely light, almost gentle, like a hand on the shoulder rather than a weight. *Whoever reads this: the route change was not Thackeray's doing. I knew it before I died. Tell them.* Someone had finally told them.

Afterward there was wine in the guildhall yard, cheap and warm and perfect, and Tam got progressively more heroic about his own role in events until Mirela said, "You led Corven's agent in a circle around the tannery for two hours," and Tam said, "Yes, and?" and she said, "Nothing. Carry on," and he did, magnificently.

It was late afternoon when Voss appeared at Rafael's elbow with a second book under his arm—a slimmer one, newer, its cover still stiff.

"The permit ledger," Voss said. "Next season's crossings. First signature goes to whoever the guild designates lead guide for the Thackeray Pass route." A pause, dry as gravel. "There was some debate about the name of the route. A motion was made to rename it. It failed unanimously."

Rafael looked at him. "Whose motion?"

"A young man named Tam. He thought 'Thackeray Pass' honored a man who'd been disgraced. This was three weeks ago, before the tribunal." Voss's mouth twitched. "He withdrew it himself after the testimony. Came to my office, very red, and asked if motions could be un-made. I told him anything can be un-made; that's rather the theme of the year."

"He's a good boy."

"He's a terror, but he's ours." Voss held out the ledger and the pen. "Well? The guild doesn't wait forever. Actually, the guild waits constantly, it's mostly waiting—but symbolically, we don't."

Rafael took the pen. And found, to genuine surprise, that their hand was not steady at all—it was steady as a compass needle, steady as rope-work on glacier ice, because it turned out that signing your own name was easiest when you finally knew what the name meant.

*Rafael Thackeray. Guide.*

Below it, in the column for route designation, Voss had already written: *Thackeray Pass — full season.*

"First crossing of the year," Voss said. "Traditionally the hardest. Storms come early up there now. You'll want a strong second."

"I have one in mind," Rafael said. "She decodes documents, binds books, saves chests from crevasses, and refuses to authenticate forgeries even under threat. I'm told she's available."

"She's standing right behind me," said Mirela, "and she accepts, provided the lead guide stops describing her résumé to strangers."

Osric drifted past with a cup in each hand and pressed one into Rafael's free hand. For a moment the old ferryman just stood there, looking at the ledger, at the fresh ink drying on a name everyone in this valley had spent eleven years either pitying or avoiding.

"My debt's paid," Osric said quietly. "Yours too, looks like."

"Not paid," Rafael said. "Carried. There's a difference. Paid things you put down."

Osric considered that, then nodded once, the way he nodded at a river when it behaved. "Carried, then." He wandered off toward where Tam was re-enacting the tannery chase with increasingly liberal embellishments.

Mirela slipped her arm through Rafael's and looked at the open ledger together with them, the way you'd look at a horizon.

"Spring," she said. "Full season. Thackeray Pass."

"Mm."

"You're going to make me carry the heavy rope, aren't you."

"Absolutely. You're terrifyingly competent. It's one of the many problems with you."

She laughed, and above the yard the evening came down soft off the mountains, and the pass stood white and patient in the last light, waiting the way good routes wait—for the people strong enough and stubborn enough to walk them again.

Rafael closed the ledger gently, as one closes a door on a long illness, and turned toward home.

Behind them, in the guildhall window, Master Voss was already blowing out the lamps—one by one, unhurried, a man putting the past properly to bed.
