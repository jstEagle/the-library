## 39. Juniper in Spring

The chest had been iron once. Now it was a book.

Not literally—though Mirela had considered it, in the first feverish weeks after the tribunal, when she'd dreamed of melting the whole thing down and casting its hinges into a single volume with clasps like manacles. But the tribunal's clerks had wanted the documents preserved exactly as found: creased, water-stained, smelling faintly of river and twenty years of someone else's secrets. So the chest itself sat on a plinth in the reading room of the new archive, lid propped open at forty-five degrees, and inside it lay a bound copy of everything it had once held—the grants, the forged transfers, Corven's survey falsifications, Ilsa Brandt's sworn testimony, even Rafael's letter of absolution, copied out in a clerk's careful hand because the original was too fragile to be handled by strangers.

Mirela had done the binding herself. Of course she had. She'd argued with three clerks about thread weight and won, which was the kind of victory that sustained her for days.

"Touch it," she said now, to the girl standing in front of the plinth with her hands clasped behind her back like a soldier at inspection. "That's what it's for. It isn't a relic. It's a record."

The girl—maybe fourteen, ferryman's daughter, one of Osric's endless parade of nieces and nephews and neighbor-children who drifted through the archive on slow afternoons—looked up at Mirela with the particular suspicion of youth confronted by an adult being reasonable. "You're allowed to just... touch history?"

"You're *required* to," Mirela said. "A document nobody touches is a document nobody reads. And a document nobody reads might as well still be at the bottom of the Serrano." She lifted the bound copy from the chest—it came away easily; she'd designed the cradle herself—and set it in the girl's hands. "Carefully. But touch it."

The girl turned pages. Her expression did something complicated. Mirela watched it happen and thought, not for the first time, that this was the whole job. Not the binding, not the cataloguing, not the endless polite warfare with the valley town's records committee over shelving budgets. This. A child holding proof that a country could be erased from maps but not from paper, if somebody bothered to keep the paper.

"My gran says her family had land near Vessia," the girl said. "Before. She says they got a letter last month saying—" The girl frowned, working at it. "Saying the land was theirs again? Or that they could claim it? Gran keeps reading it out loud and crying, so I don't fully understand."

"She can claim it," Mirela said. "There's a process now. There's an office in the valley town. Tell your gran the office has copies of everything, and tell her—" Mirela hesitated, then said it anyway, because it was true and because the girl would carry it home like a stone dropped in a pond, "—tell her the woman who made this archive is Vessian, and that we waited twenty years for this, and that crying while reading the letter is completely correct procedure."

The girl laughed, sudden and bright, and Mirela felt the laugh land somewhere under her ribs and stay there.

* * *

The house above the ferry landing had been Osric's idea, which surprised no one who knew him, since Osric considered himself the architect of every happy outcome within thirty miles whether or not he'd had anything to do with it.

"It's got a leak," he'd said, standing in the dooryard with his arms crossed, selling it hard. "Roof leaks over the east room. Chimney draws backward when the wind's northerly. Floorboards in the kitchen talk to you at night."

"That's a terrible advertisement," Rafael had said.

"It's honest advertising. You two are honest people now. Famous for it." He'd spat thoughtfully into the river. "Besides, you want to be above the landing. You want to hear the water. Trust me. People who've been downriver always want to be where they can hear the water."

He'd been right about the water, as he was insufferably right about most things riverine. On spring evenings, with the snowmelt running high and loud, the whole house hummed with it—a low constant voice beneath the floorboards, under conversation, under sleep. Mirela had feared she'd find it maddening. Instead she'd found it was the sound of the year turning, the sound of things moving on, and she slept better above the Serrano than she had anywhere since Vessia fell.

The roof leaked over the east room, which had become her workroom, which meant there was a copper pot under the drip that sang a small bright note whenever it rained, and Rafael had taken to calling it "the second clock," and she had not yet told him that she'd stopped noticing it months ago and simply liked knowing it was there.

The chimney drew backward on northerly winds, so on those nights they cooked on the little iron stove instead and ate early, and Rafael—guide, witness, restored man—had revealed himself to be, of all things, a superstitious and devoted bread baker. He kneaded like he climbed: methodically, silently, with total attention, and he checked the oven heat by feel with the same hand that had once read rockfall tremors through boot leather. His loaves came out lopsided and magnificent. He refused all instruction. When Mirela suggested, gently, that scoring the top in a straight line rather than a spiral might improve the crumb, he looked at her with the wounded dignity of a mountain being told it was the wrong shape.

"The spiral is structural," he said.

"It's decorative."

"It's *structural*," Rafael said, and cut her the end slice anyway, which was how their arguments ended now: in slices, in shared plates, in the small treaty of daily life.

* * *

On Thursdays the archive closed early, and on Thursdays Mirela walked down to the landing to meet the supply boat, partly because someone had to sign for the paper shipments and mostly because Rafael met the supply boat too, ostensibly to check the ferry cables before the weekend traffic, actually because Thursday was Thursday.

They had never named it. That was the joke between them now, or one of them—that two people who had survived ambushes and avalanches and a magistrate's interrogation by firelight could not manage four words in a warm kitchen. They had said other things. In the snow cave, shaking, breath fogging, she had said *I'm falling in love with you* and he had said *I know, I have been for months, I was waiting until we weren't about to die,* and that had been plain enough. But the smaller vocabulary—the words for ordinary mornings, for a life shaped around another person—still came hard, and so they'd built rituals instead, and the rituals said it for them.

Thursday was one. He stood at the cable post pretending to inspect the turnbuckle. She came down the path with her shawl over her arm. Neither hurried. The boat took its time docking, because boats always did, and Osric's deckhand took his time unloading, because deckhands always did, and so they got perhaps six minutes of standing side by side watching the river, shoulders touching, saying nothing that needed saying.

Today, though, Rafael broke protocol.

"I signed something today," he said.

Mirela turned. Rafael Thackeray was not a man who volunteered information; information had to be pried out of him like pitons from frozen rock. "Signed what?"

"The guild register. New season's permits." He was looking at the river, ears slightly red, and she realized with a jolt of tenderness that he was nervous. "They asked me to take apprentices. Two of them. Proper ones, through the guild, with my name on their training records." A pause. "One of them is Tam."

"Tam," Mirela said. "Who already follows you around like a duckling."

"He doesn't follow me around."

"Rafael. He re-lashed your pack straps the way you lash them. He stands the way you stand. Last week I saw him refuse to walk on the left side of a bridge because you walk on the right side of bridges."

"That's—" Rafael stopped. Considered. "Huh," he said, in the tone of a man discovering a fact about his own life that everyone else had known for a year.

"You'll be good at it," Mirela said. "Teaching. Better than you think."

"I nearly killed him twice on the glacier."

"You *saved* him twice on the glacier, and he knows the difference, and so do you." She bumped her shoulder against his. "What's the second signature?"

His ears went redder. He reached into his coat and produced a folded paper, and handed it over with the stiff formality of a man surrendering contraband.

It was a deed application. For the house above the ferry landing. Both their names on it—hers first, she noticed, hers first—and below the names, in the space marked RELATIONSHIP OF APPLICANTS TO ONE ANOTHER, Rafael had written, in his blunt guide's handwriting:

*Partners.*

Just that. No elaboration. No legal category. As though the word were self-evident, which, Mirela supposed, after everything, it was.

"The clerk said I needed to specify," Rafael said, to the river. "Household partners or business partners or—"

"Rafael."

"—and I said partners, and she looked at me over her spectacles for a long time, and then she stamped it." He finally looked at her. Under the weathering and the gray at his temples and the new steadiness in him, his eyes were the same eyes she'd first seen across a market stall, wary and watchful, two cautious people recognizing each other's damage. They weren't wary anymore. That was the miracle she still didn't quite trust, some mornings: that she could look at this man and find nothing guarded left. "Is that all right? I should have asked you first. I'm told that's how it's done. Asking first."

Mirela folded the paper along its existing crease, very precisely, and put it inside her coat, against her heart, because she was a bookbinder and believed in proper storage of important documents.

"It's all right," she said. "It's more than all right. But you're cooking tonight. The spiral loaf. Structural."

* * *

The archive's grand opening—or as grand as Juniper's Landing got, which meant the ferrymen closed the crossing for an afternoon and half the town squeezed into the reading room and the other half listened from the street—was set for midsummer, but word traveled faster than schedules. Exiles came first: quiet men and women off the valley coaches, Vessian-born, who stood in front of the open chest for long minutes without speaking, then touched the bound copy the way you'd touch a sleeping child. Then came others. A surveyor who wanted to check old boundary lines. A schoolteacher with a class of ferociously well-behaved children who filed past the plinth in pairs. An old shepherd who'd fought at the annexation and who read Brandt's testimony twice, nodded once, and bought nothing from anyone ever again who'd voted to fund Corven's garrison, which Mirela learned secondhand and counted among the archive's measurable effects.

And one morning in late spring, a coach from the capital, and out of it a thin man in a good coat with a government seal on his satchel, who introduced himself as an undersecretary of lands and asked, very politely, whether the archive might consider lending the original grants to the capital for "verification purposes."

Mirela served him tea in the reading room and let him wait.

"No," she said, when the tea was gone.

The undersecretary blinked. "I beg your pardon?"

"They stay here. Copies go wherever they're needed—I'll make them myself, and they'll be exact, I bind state archives, sir, I've forgotten more about document preservation than your verification office knows. But the originals stay in this room, in this town, where the people they belong to can put their hands on them." She smiled, and it was the smile Rafael called her *market smile*, the one that preceded either a sale or a rout. "You may quote me to your minister. The theft happened here, on this river, in these mountains. The accounting happens here too."

The undersecretary, to his credit, wrote it down.

* * *

Evenings, when the light went long and gold across the water, they sat on the porch above the landing—two chairs, angled toward each other, a habit rather than a plan—and Mirela worked on bindings while Rafael read. He'd become a reader since winter, voraciously, apologetically, as though catching up on decades lost to survival. Currently it was a history of the range, borrowed from the archive, and he kept making disapproving noises at it.

"This man claims Windrow Col runs northeast-southwest."

"Mm."

"It doesn't. It runs north-northeast. Anyone who's crossed it in whiteout knows that. The compass lies at Windrow; you steer by the wind-scour on the stones." He turned a page with the air of a judge postponing a sentence. "I should write to him."

"You should write to him," Mirela agreed, not looking up from her work. Below them, the evening ferry crossed with its last load—a flock of sheep, two arguing merchants, Tam waving up at the house with both arms until Rafael, mortified, raised one finger in acknowledgment, which sent Tam into visible raptures. "Or you could write your own. A guide's account. Routes no map shows. The compass that points at cairns."

Rafael was quiet a moment. Down at the landing, the sheep complained their way ashore.

"Maybe," he said. Which, from Rafael, was a shout of enthusiasm.

Mirela smiled at her stitching. She was rebinding, that month, a set of Vessian primers—children's readers, rescued from the burned village, salvaged from a collapsed schoolhouse shelf, water-warped and fire-kissed at the corners. Someone had asked her once why bother restoring damaged books when clean copies existed. She'd answered that the damage was part of the record too. That a book that had survived a burning carried its survival in its body, and that throwing that away to make it pretty was its own small erasure.

She felt much the same about herself, lately. About Rafael. About the two of them sitting above a river that had given up a dead country's secrets and then, having done so, simply gone on carrying boats and meltwater and the seasons, indifferent and generous, the current going on.

The primer in her lap opened to a page she knew well by now: a lesson-poem, Vessian, the kind every child in the old country had memorized. *The river does not remember, the river only runs—but we who stand on banks remember for it, and our remembering is the bank.* She'd bound eleven copies of this primer. There would be more. There would be as many as it took.

"Tam wants to know," Rafael said, "if the archive takes donations. He found something in the ferry shed. Old manifest books from before the annexation—Vessian shipping records, cargo that came down from the high valleys. He thinks they matter."

Mirela looked up. "Do they?"

"He thinks so. He says—" Rafael paused, and something moved through his face, the particular softness he still hadn't learned to hide when talking about the boy. "He says everything that was almost lost should get to count."

Below them the ferry lanterns came on, one by one, small flames against the blue dusk. The river ran on underneath, dark and certain, toward whatever sea receives everything eventually.

"Then tell Tam," Mirela said, "the archive absolutely takes donations. And tell him to bring them Thursday."

She bent back to her stitching, and beside her Rafael turned another disapproving page, and the copper pot in the east room waited for rain, and the house above the landing held its two names like a spine holds a book—together, creased, surviving, open.
