## 40. The Current Goes On

The morning of Tam's first licensed crossing, Rafael woke before the alarm out of habit rather than need, lay in the dark listening to the river, and decided that some habits deserved to be kept.

The Serrano ran high and bright below the house, the way it always did in late June, carrying snowmelt down from the range with a sound like continuous applause. Seven years now Rafael had lived above the ferry landing, and the sound had never once gotten old. It was the sound of water that knew where it was going.

Beside them, Mirela slept on, one arm flung across the empty half of the bed where the guide's pack usually sat. She'd been up late at the archive again—there was a traveling exhibit from Vessia coming through in the autumn, and she'd been corresponding with curators in three countries, arguing about display cases with the particular ferocity she reserved for things she loved. Rafael dressed quietly, made coffee strong enough to stand a spoon in, and left a note on the table in their careful, unlovely hand:

*Gone to make history. Back by dark. There's pie if Osric doesn't find it first.*

* * *

Tam was already at the trailhead when Rafael arrived, and had been, judging by the state of him, for some time. He stood at the register post with his brand-new license held in both hands like a bird he was afraid might fly off, wearing boots so new they still had the creases from the box, and a grin that could have been seen from the valley floor.

"You're early," Rafael said.

"You're *late*."

"I'm exactly on time. You're just earlier than time." Rafael took the license from him, examined it with theatrical gravity, and read aloud: "'Tham Aldous Ferrow, licensed guide, grade two, authorized to lead parties across Thackeray Pass in fair summer conditions.'" They handed it back. "Fair summer conditions. Which means if it snows, you turn around, no matter how close you are. Say it back to me."

"If it snows I turn around," Tam recited, "no matter how close I am." He'd said it every day for a week. He'd say it wrong eventually, everyone did, but not today. Today his party was waiting by the marker stone: four pilgrims from the valley towns who wanted to see the pass where Vessia had been given back its name, plus one elderly woman who claimed she'd been born on the far side and hadn't been back since the annexation, and who had informed Tam at the outset that she would be setting her own pace and everyone else was welcome to keep up.

"Your client list is a challenge," Rafael observed.

"My client list is *excellent*," Tam said. "Mrs. Havel brought sausage."

Rafael looked at the small procession—the pilgrims adjusting their packs, Mrs. Havel standing with her chin up like a woman boarding a ship—and felt the old familiar tightening behind the ribs, the one that used to mean dread and now meant only care. "First crossing rule," they said. "You count heads more often than you think you need to."

"I know."

"Second rule. The mountain doesn't care that today matters to you."

"I know."

"Third rule." Rafael paused. "It's allowed to matter anyway. Just don't tell the mountain."

Tam laughed, shouldered his pack, and turned to his clients, and Rafael watched him begin—watched him check straps, assign walking order, listen patiently while Mrs. Havel explained that she would be setting her own pace—and thought: *there it is.* The thing Corven had tried to bury under a falsified survey report and a dead man's name. Not a reputation. A person doing the work, doing it well, handing it forward.

They fell in at the rear, as agreed. Guide of record, supervising. Officially Rafael was there as guild observer, a formality required for a first licensed crossing. Unofficially, everyone in Juniper's Landing knew what it actually was: Rafael Thackeray walking Tam Ferrow up the mountain the way you walk someone to the edge of a lake and let go of the boat.

* * *

The lower trail was kind that morning, dry underfoot, larches throwing long stripes of shade across the switchbacks. The pilgrims asked questions, and Tam answered most of them and deferred the rest—"Guide Thackeray can tell you about the gorge section"—and Rafael gave the short versions, the ones without blood in them, or gave the true versions when asked directly, because that was the whole point of everything now. The story of what had happened on this mountain belonged to anyone who wanted to carry it.

"The pass is named for you," one of the pilgrims said, somewhere past the second bridge. "Thackeray Pass. Was it always?"

"No," Rafael said. "For twenty years it didn't have a name anyone would print. Before that it was called the Vessian Gate." They let a few steps go by. "A magistrate named Corven renamed it after me, during the inquiry. I've never been sure whether it was an apology or an alibi. Possibly both."

"And which do you take it as?"

Rafael considered. Below them, faintly, the Serrano chattered over its stones. "I take it as a reminder," they said, "that names are given by other people, but what you do is given by you. He put my name on the map. What the name means up there—that part was mine to earn back."

Mrs. Havel, tramping steadily ahead, snorted. "In my day we called it the Gate and we called the magistrate worse," she said, and the pilgrims laughed, and Rafael laughed too, and the laugh went up the mountain with them like something light being carried to altitude.

* * *

They took lunch at Windrow Col, in the lee of the old shelter wall, and Tam ran the stop with a competence that made Rafael's chest ache in a way they had no intention of examining in front of anyone. He counted heads. He checked feet. He made Mrs. Havel sit down and eat her sausage instead of standing sentinel at the col's edge looking north, though he did it gently enough that she pretended not to notice being managed, which is the highest art of guiding.

From the Col, on a clear day, you could see all the way into what had been Vessia—the folded green valleys, the thread of the high road, the smoke of towns that appeared on maps again now, spelled properly. One of the pilgrims had binoculars and passed them around. When they reached Rafael, Rafael looked for a long moment, longer than the view required.

"You were there," Mrs. Havel said. It wasn't a question. She'd settled beside them on the wall, uninvited, in the manner of women who have outlived the need for invitations. "During the bad years."

"I was," Rafael said.

"My brother stayed when the annexation came. Kept the orchard. Wrote letters for six years and then didn't." She looked north. "I'm not going up there to see his grave. I know which tree it's under; my niece wrote me. I'm going up there to stand in the orchard. That's different."

"It is," Rafael said. "That's exactly different."

She nodded once, satisfied, as if Rafael had passed some examination of their own, and went to bother Tam about the afternoon's pace.

* * *

The cairn sat a half-hour below the pass, on the shoulder where the trail bent around a granite spur, and Rafael had known since the trailhead that this was the true destination of the day—not the pass itself, not the register box, but this pile of stones with the weathered plaque and the small brass compass rose set into its face.

The party reached it mid-afternoon. Tam, who had walked this route twice before as an assistant and once, memorably, as a terrified ferry-hand fleeing Corven's agent, slowed as they approached, and Rafael saw him glance back—checking, the way you check with someone who has a history with a place.

"Take ten," Tam told the clients. "There's water at the spring behind the spur. We'll move on together."

The pilgrims drifted toward the spring. Mrs. Havel did not. She stood at the cairn, reading the plaque with her lips moving slightly, and then she took something from her coat pocket—a small strip of cloth, orchard-green, faded nearly white—and tucked it into a crevice between stones, the way people had been tucking things into this cairn for seven years. Ribbons, notes, a child's carved horse once, buttons, a wedding ring that had caused a brief diplomatic flurry until the widow in question wrote to explain that her husband had proposed here in another century and she wanted the ring to finish the journey he never got to make.

"He was your client," Mrs. Havel said, without turning. "The one the cairn is for."

"He was," Rafael said.

"You don't talk about him much. People notice."

"People notice everything. That's the tax on having a name on a map." Rafael came and stood beside her. The stone was warm from the sun. "His name was Edvard Lune. He hired me to guide a survey party over this pass, seven years ago next month. He carried a compass his father gave him, and he was better company than any client I ever had, and he died on the far side of this spur because a magistrate changed our route and arranged for me to carry the blame." Rafael paused. "He also hid a second set of documents under this cairn, because he trusted the mountain more than he trusted the government, and he was right to. So this isn't only a grave. It's the place where the truth was keeping itself safe until somebody dug."

Mrs. Havel looked at the stones a while. "Then it's a good cairn," she said finally. "Most cairns only hold grief. This one held evidence." She patted Rafael's arm once, briskly, the way you pat a horse you approve of, and went off to find her sausage.

Rafael stayed.

The brass compass rose gleamed in the plaque's corner, added by the guild the year the pass was formally renamed—though *renamed* wasn't right either, since the name had come home rather than arrived. Rafael touched it, as they always did. North. The needle's ghost, fixed in metal. Edvard's actual compass hung at Rafael's belt now, as it had for seven years, its glass scratched, its needle still true. Some mornings it pointed at nothing in particular and meant everything.

"I brought him up here," Rafael said quietly, to the cairn, to Edvard, to whatever portion of a person a mountain agrees to hold onto. "You'd have liked today. The boy's good. Better than I was at his age—he counts heads without being reminded, and he hasn't yet learned to bluff about the weather, which means he'll live longer than I would have." A marmot whistled somewhere down the slope, insolent. "Mirela says hello. She's curating you, by the way. Your letters are going in the autumn exhibit. She fought the curators over the display case for three weeks and won. You'd have found that funny. I find it funny and I lived through it."

The wind moved through the larches. Somewhere below, patient as arithmetic, the river went on.

Rafael took the last weight from their pack—the last of it, the piece they'd carried up this mountain every year and carried back down every year, unable to leave it, unable to look at it too long. It was a page. Edvard's handwriting, from the letter they'd found under the cairn seven years ago, the line Rafael had cut out with shaking hands and kept apart from the archive copies because it seemed too large to file:

*I absolve R. Thackeray entirely. Whatever happens to me, the route was changed over my objection, and my guide's name will not be the price of a magistrate's convenience.*

Seven years of carrying it. Seven years of knowing it, and believing it, and being unable quite to feel it all the way down, the way you know a rope holds without ever quite testing it with your whole weight.

Rafael read it once more. Then they folded it small, and slid it into the crevice beside Mrs. Havel's ribbon, deep enough to stay, shallow enough to be found.

"There," they said. Their voice came out rougher than intended. "Filed."

The wind picked up, moved down the slope, and went on toward the pass, and Rafael stood in it and felt—finally, completely, the whole weight of themselves resting on the rope—the rope hold.

* * *

At the pass, Tam did everything right.

He signed the register—*Ferrow, T., guide, first licensed crossing, party of six, conditions fair*—and had each client sign beneath, and showed Mrs. Havel how to work the stamp, and she stamped it with such force that the ink bled through two pages, which would annoy the guild clerk and delight everyone else. He gathered the party for the photograph that the guild now required, everyone squinting into the sun with the northern valleys spread out behind them, green and named and open. And then, when it was done, when the clients had turned back down the trail chattering about supper, Tam stood a moment longer at the top of the pass, looking north, and Rafael watched him take off his hat and hold it against his chest for a breath or two—not for show, not for the register, just privately, between himself and the country his mother had been born in.

Then he put the hat back on and came over, grinning, seventeen kinds of triumph fighting for space on his face.

"Well?" he said.

"Well *what*? You want me to say it? Fine." Rafael put out their hand. "Congratulations, Guide Ferrow."

Tam shook it, then abandoned dignity entirely and hugged them, quick and hard, the way young men hug when they've decided not to be embarrassed about it. "Seven years ago I was ferrying you across a river at night and lying to a magistrate's men about it," he said into Rafael's shoulder, "and now look."

"Now look," Rafael agreed. "Though for the record, you were terrible at lying to them. You blushed like a sunset. It's a wonder we weren't all arrested."

"I was *sixteen*."

"You were a security risk is what you were." Rafael stepped back, thumped him once on the shoulder. "Go bring your party home. I'll catch you up—I want a minute."

Tam went, calling his clients to order, counting heads without being reminded, and Rafael stood alone at the top of Thackeray Pass with the whole world laid out on both sides of the line.

South: the Serrano valley, tiny and glittering, the ferry landing a pale scratch beside the river, the house above it with its blue door Mirela had painted without asking and Rafael had defended to the neighbors without hesitation. Smoke from the bakery. The archive roof, new slate, where the chest's contents lived now behind glass, read daily by schoolchildren who found the whole affair thrilling and the handwriting appalling.

North: Vessia. Orchards. Roads. Towns with their old names on the signposts, and one particular orchard, invisible at this distance but exactly located in Rafael's memory, where an old woman was at this moment probably bossing her niece's family around and standing in the grass her brother had planted.

Between: the pass, and the wind, and the long drop of the trail, and the river running through all of it, south to north of every border anyone had ever drawn, asking permission of no one.

Rafael stood there until the cold came up, and then shouldered the pack—lighter now, noticeably, though the scale would have shown no difference—and started down.

* * *

They caught the party at the second bridge, where Tam had stopped to let Mrs. Havel rest and the pilgrims were eating the emergency rations they'd been explicitly told to save, and by the time the trailhead came in sight the light had gone long and gold and the river below the landing was running amber.

Osric was waiting at the ferry dock, ostensibly checking his moorings, actually waiting, a pie box under one arm with a suspicious absence of pie inside it. "Well?" he said.

"He passed," Rafael said.

"'Course he passed. I taught him knots." Osric handed over the pie box, empty, with ceremony. "Your wife ate the evidence hours ago. Said to tell you she's proud of you both and also that you're cooking tonight."

"She *what*—"

"Her words. Verbatim. I'd write them down but she made me swear." He squinted up the trail at the descending party, at Tam in his new boots walking tall, and something crossed the old ferryman's face that he would have denied under oath. "Seven years," he said. "Sometimes I still expect Corven's riders to come over that ridge. Then I remember they're mostly giving depositions." He spat companionably over the rail. "Best trade I ever made, that debt of mine. Cost me everything and paid double."

"That's not how debts work, Osric."

"That's how *this* one worked. Don't argue with your elders." He jerked his chin at the trail. "Go on. She's on the porch. Been watching the bend in the path for an hour and pretending she's reading."

Rafael climbed the path from the landing as the sun went down behind the western peaks, and Mirela was indeed on the porch, indeed holding a book upside down, and she lowered it when she saw them and looked at Rafael's face for a long moment, reading it the way she read everything—thoroughly, and without mercy, and with love underneath.

"Well?" she said.

"He did it. He was good. He counted heads." Rafael sat down beside her on the step. "I left the page at the cairn."

Mirela went very still. Seven years she had known about the folded paper in the bottom of the pack, the way you know about a splinter someone carries. She didn't ask *are you sure*. She didn't ask *why now*. She took Rafael's hand, turned it over, and pressed her thumb into the palm, once, firmly, the way you test a knot.

"Good," she said simply. "Now it's filed."

Below them the river ran on, high and bright with snowmelt, carrying the mountain down to the sea one grain at a time, unhurried, unstoppable, asking nothing. Rafael listened to it a long while, the applause of it, and found that for the first time in longer than they could measure, there was nothing in them that needed carrying anywhere.

Tomorrow there would be permits to sign, and a pie to bake in retaliation, and Tam would come round wanting to compare route notes, and in the autumn the exhibit would arrive with its crates and its curators and Mirela would fight them all and win. The current went on. So did they.

Rafael leaned back against the porch post, and watched the water, and let it run.
