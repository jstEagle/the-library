## 1. Weather Coming Down

The fog came down the valley like something with intent.

Sebastian Oyelaran saw it first as a smudge on the northern horizon, a gray thumbprint where there should have been blue. By the time he'd crossed the Kettle River and turned up the Moosehorn drainage, it had become a wall, soft-edged but absolute, eating the ridgelines one by one. He keyed the radio out of habit more than hope—there was nobody to talk to this far in, not since the relay tower on Caribou Point went dark two winters back—and got the static he expected.

"Medicine run," he said anyway, to the plane if not to anyone else. "Routine. In and out before lunch."

The Cessna 180 answered with the particular shudder of an engine that had heard that promise before. Fifteen years of flying this country had taught Sebastian that the interior kept its own calendar, and it did not consult his.

He checked the manifest taped to the yoke: insulin for Marta Okonkwo at the Last Chance homestead, antibiotics for her husband's chest, a crate of batteries, a bolt of canvas. Ordinary cargo. Ordinary flight. That was the whole point of ordinary—it left no wake. He'd built his life out of it, these small unremarkable runs, the kind of flying that never made the registry logs worth reading. A man could disappear into routine the way a stone disappears into a river, if he was patient enough and the current was steady enough.

Fifteen years of patience. He'd gotten good at it.

The wall of fog reached him at the mouth of the Moosehorn. One moment the valley lay open ahead, the creek a bright stitch through muskeg; the next, the world went the color of wet ash. He pulled up, banked south, felt for the sun through the murk. Nothing. The ceiling dropped as he watched it, or seemed to—the way fog does when you're alone with it, closing like a hand around whatever light remains.

"Okay," he said quietly. "Okay."

There were three options. Backtrack sixty miles to Fort Meridian and land blind on instruments he half-trusted, in crosswinds the forecast hadn't mentioned. Or push through, which in country like this was how bush pilots became statistics. Or—

Or the old prospecting camp on Loon Lake. Derelict now, mostly. He'd overflown it a hundred times without landing. Long dock, decent length, oriented north-south. A gravel strip behind it that some optimist had cleared twenty years ago and the alders had been reclaiming ever since. If the dock was still sound, if the water was calm enough to read, he could put down, wait out the fog, and be gone before anyone knew he'd stopped.

That was the thing about invisibility. It wasn't just avoiding people. It was avoiding *stops*, the unplanned ones, the places where a man's trail might puddle and leave a print.

But the fuel gauge said what it said, and the fog said what it said, and the insulin in the crate behind him had a temperature window narrower than either of their opinions.

Sebastian turned north.

* * *

Loon Lake appeared beneath him all at once, the way lakes do in fog—a sudden silver opening in the white, like tearing paper. The water was glass. Good. Glass water let you read your own altitude off the surface, let you feel the flare instead of guessing it.

The dock was still there. Grayed to bone, one section sagging near the shore end, but the outer thirty feet stood on pilings that looked honest. He brought the 180 in low, slow, floats kissing the surface with barely a whisper, and taxied to the dock with the engine idling like a dog told to stay.

Silence, when he shut it down. The heavy kind. Fog silence, which isn't empty at all—it's full of small drippings and settling wood and the lake breathing against the hull. Somewhere across the water a loon called once, that rising, lunatic note, and didn't call again.

Sebastian stepped onto the dock and immediately knew something was wrong.

It wasn't anything dramatic. It was the details. A camp that's been abandoned for years has a texture—moss on the rooflines, windows gone gray with grime, everything softened, rounded, reclaimed. This camp had that, mostly. But the door of the main cabin hung open, and the hinges had no rust bloom on them. Someone had opened it recently. And there, at the far end of the dock, half-hidden under a tarp that had blown loose, sat a freight canoe with fresh paint on its gunwales. Not fresh-fresh. Months old, maybe. But newer than the camp deserved.

And there was blood on the dock boards.

Old blood, brown-black, weathered by rain—but not *years* of rain. Weeks. Maybe less. A smear of it, dragged, as though someone had crawled.

Sebastian stood very still and listened to the part of himself that had kept him alive for fifteen years, the part that said *get back in the plane, taxi out, take off into nothing, log this as a weather stop, tell no one.* That voice had saved him more times than he could count. It had a specific weight, that voice, a cold gravity he trusted the way other men trusted God.

Then he heard the coughing.

It came from inside the main cabin—wet, ragged, the coughing of a man whose lungs were filling with something they shouldn't hold. It carried through the fog with terrible clarity, and it was followed by a voice, thin and cracked but unmistakably alive:

"Hello the dock! Whoever's out there—I can see your plane. I can see it plain." A pause, another wet cough. "I'd take it kindly if you'd come see me before you go."

Sebastian closed his eyes for one second. Just one. Then he walked toward the cabin.

* * *

The man on the bunk was dying, and he knew it, and he had arranged himself accordingly—which was somehow worse than if he'd been thrashing. He'd propped himself against a rolled sleeping bag, pulled a wool blanket up to his waist, set a canteen and a tin cup on the crate beside him within easy reach. A rifle leaned against the wall by the door, close enough to grab, too far away to matter anymore. His hands, resting on the blanket, were steady. His eyes were not. They had the fixed brightness of a man running a fever and a ledger at the same time, tallying what he had left to spend.

He was sixty-something, maybe older—the interior ages people unevenly—and built like the kind of driftwood that survives winters: all knots and grain, nothing soft. His shirt was open over a bandage wrapped tight around his belly, and the bandage had soaked through and dried and soaked through again, layers of rust-brown like tree rings.

"You're not the supply plane," the man said. His voice was gravel dragged over gravel. "Supply run comes Thursday. You're not Thursday."

"No," Sebastian said. "I'm weather."

The man almost smiled. "Weather. That's a good name for it." He gestured at the window, at the fog pressing against the glass like something curious. "Been three days of you. Figured I'd die before you lifted." Another cough, longer this time, and when it passed he lay back and breathed in shallow sips. "Name's Vann. Elias Vann."

Something turned over in Sebastian's chest, slow and cold. He knew the name. Not from any wanted poster, not from anything official—he'd spent fifteen years making sure nothing official touched him. From the country itself, from the gossip that moved along the rivers faster than boats did. Elias Vann, prospector, the man who'd worked the headwaters of the Sustut for twenty years and never registered a claim anyone could find. The man who'd had a daughter once, people said, and then didn't anymore. The man who asked no questions and answered none.

A man, in other words, very much like Sebastian himself. Which meant the blood on the dock had a story attached, and stories were exactly what Sebastian had crossed half a continent to stop collecting.

"Oyelaran," he said. "Sebastian Oyelaran. I fly medicine runs out of Fort Meridian."

"Medicine." Vann's eyes flicked toward the Cessna visible through the window, its floats pale against the dark water. "You carrying anything for a gut wound?"

"I'm carrying insulin and antibiotics. Not a surgeon. Not a hospital." Sebastian crouched by the bunk, keeping his hands visible, an old habit from a different life. "Let me look at it."

Vann hesitated—Sebastian watched him weigh it, watch the pride and the arithmetic fight—and then nodded once and pulled the bandage aside.

It was bad. Sebastian had seen gut wounds before, in a life he didn't talk about, and this one had the look of a wound that had been dressed competently by someone with no supplies: cleaned, packed, bound, and then left to do what gut wounds do, which is fester. The skin around it was hot and tight. The smell was wrong. Three days of fever, judging by the man's eyes, maybe four.

"This needs a hospital," Sebastian said. "Real one. Fort Meridian has a clinic with a doctor who knows what she's doing. It's—" he did the math against the fog and the fuel and hated both numbers, "—two hours' flight, if the weather breaks. Longer if it doesn't."

"It won't break today." Vann said it flatly, a man reporting conditions, not complaining about them. "Maybe tomorrow night. Maybe the day after." He looked at Sebastian, and there was something in the look that Sebastian recognized and dreaded: the look of a man about to ask for something large. "I've got maybe two days in me. Maybe three if I'm stubborn, and I am famously stubborn. So we've got a scheduling problem, you and me."

"The antibiotics will help. They won't fix it."

"No. They won't fix it." Vann reached for the canteen, drank, spilled a little down his chin, didn't seem to notice. "You want to know who did this."

"I didn't ask."

"You're asking with your face. Everybody asks with their face." The old man's laugh turned into a cough, and he waved off Sebastian's hand. "Salvage men. Corvane contract crew, working the coast concession. Came up the river looking for something I've got. I told them I didn't have it. They expressed skepticism." He tapped the bandage with one knuckle. "This is their counterargument."

Corvane. The name landed in Sebastian's stomach like a stone dropped down a well. He kept his face still, kept his breathing even, and was grateful for the fog outside because it gave him somewhere to look. Corvane Salvage held concessions along half the coast now, big money, bigger lawyers, a fleet of tenders and dive barges and spotter planes. And fifteen years ago, before it was Corvane, when it was still the company it had grown out of, Sebastian Oyelaran had known it intimately. Had testified about it. Had buried a name because of it.

"You should report that," he said, and heard how hollow it sounded even as he said it.

"To whom?" Vann smiled, and it was a real smile, tired and crooked. "The concession inspector? The Mounties who come through twice a year? Son, I've lived out here long enough to know the difference between law and weather. Law is what happens to other people."

Sebastian stood and went to the window. The fog hadn't moved. The loon called again, farther off. Behind him, Vann breathed in those shallow sips, and the cabin smelled of woodsmoke and blood and the sweetish wrongness of infection, and Sebastian thought: *walk out. Taxi out. Take off into nothing.* The insulin was still cold in its crate. Marta Okonkwo was waiting. There was a whole architecture of reasons to leave, load-bearing reasons, and every one of them was true.

"Here's my offer," Vann said.

"Don't."

"You don't even know what it is yet."

"I know what offers are." Sebastian turned around. "I've taken them before. They cost more than they pay."

The old man studied him for a long moment, and something shifted in his face—not suspicion, exactly. Recognition. The look of a man finding a familiar scar on a stranger.

"Fifteen years ago," Vann said slowly, "there was a pilot who flew the Sustut country. Young fellow. Good stick. Flew supply for the survey crews before the survey crews went bad." He paused. "Callsign was Kestrel."

The silence in the cabin got very large. Outside, the fog pressed closer, and the lake made its small patient sounds against the hull, and Sebastian Oyelaran—who had spent fifteen years becoming no one, who had practiced stillness until it was indistinguishable from emptiness—felt the floor of that stillness crack straight down the middle.

"That pilot's dead," Sebastian said.

"Sure he is." Vann's voice was gentle, which was worse than if it had been sharp. "Dead men don't fly medicine to Marta Okonkwo every second Tuesday. Dead men don't tip their hats to the ranger at Caribou Point. But dead men also don't walk away from a stranger bleeding out on a dock, so I'll take my chances with the ghost." He reached under the blanket, slow, showing his hand the whole way, and drew out a folded square of oilcloth, cracked and soft with age. He laid it on the blanket between them. "Get me out of here alive, and this is yours. And if I don't make it—if I die in that seat of yours—then you finish what I started. That's the offer. Both halves."

Sebastian looked at the oilcloth and did not touch it. Through the window, the Cessna sat on the black water, patient, ready, a machine built for leaving. Everything in him that had survived fifteen years was screaming the same word.

"What is it?" he heard himself say.

"A map," Elias Vann said, and smiled through the fever like a man watching something arrive from a long way off. "Half of one, anyway. To the Meridian Star."

The name went through Sebastian like cold water. Everyone on the coast knew the story of the Meridian Star—the survey vessel that went down in the channels eleven years back with her whole season's work aboard, unregistered claims, sample cores, rumors of stones worth more than the towns they'd have built. Wreck hunters had been picking the channels apart for a decade. Corvane's fleet had been picking them apart hardest of all.

And here was a dying man in a derelict cabin, holding half the answer under a wool blanket, offering it to a ghost.

"I can't promise you'll live," Sebastian said. His voice sounded strange to him, distant, like radio traffic from another aircraft. "Nobody can promise that. Not with that wound, not with this weather."

"I know."

"And I'm not promising the rest. Whatever you started, whatever you want finished—that's yours. I fly people. I don't carry cargo like that."

Vann nodded, unsurprised, unsurprising. "Take the map anyway. Consider it payment for the ride. Fair market rate." He coughed, long and terrible, and when it was done he lay back and closed his eyes, and for a moment Sebastian thought he'd lost him right there. Then the old man spoke again, barely above the fog-silence.

"She's coming, you know. My girl. Juniper. She'll have tracked my last call, or she'll find your trail, or she'll simply show up the way she does, angry as a struck match. When she comes—" his eyes opened, fixed on Sebastian with that fever-bright certainty, "—you give her my half. You hear me? Whatever you decide about me, about the wreck, about all of it—you give her my half."

Outside, the fog began, very slightly, to lift. Light came through the window in a pale wash, touching the oilcloth, touching the blood-ringed bandages, touching the old man's hands lying flat and steady on the blanket like a man who had finally set something down.

Sebastian picked up the map. It weighed nothing. It weighed everything.

"I need to check my fuel," he said, which was true, and walked out onto the dock, and stood in the lifting fog beside his airplane, and understood that the routine flight, the invisible life, the fifteen careful years—they had ended somewhere between the coughing and the name *Kestrel*, and he had not yet found out what came after.
