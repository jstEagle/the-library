## 2. The Price of a Ride

The fog didn't lift. It thinned, the way a lie thins when you press on it—enough to see through, not enough to trust. Sebastian stood on the dock with his hands jammed in his jacket pockets and watched the water for movement that wasn't there, and behind him, in the cabin that smelled of kerosene and old blood, Elias Vann was dying by inches and pretending otherwise.

"You should get the stove going," Elias called through the door. "Nights come down fast this time of year."

"I know what nights do."

"Course you do." A wet laugh, quickly strangled. "Bush rat. You people all know the same things and none of the important ones."

Sebastian let that sit. He'd spent fifteen years learning which conversations to walk away from, and this one had *walk away* written on it in letters a mile high. A dying man in a derelict camp, a gut wound nobody in the interior could treat, and a plane on the dock with just enough fuel to make the coast if the weather held and just enough not to if it didn't. Any one of those facts was a reason to leave. Together they were a verdict.

He went inside anyway.

Elias had propped himself against the wall beneath a window fogged with condensation, one arm wrapped around his middle like he was holding a door shut against something trying to get out. His beard was grey going white, his hands were a prospector's hands—knuckles like river stone, nails cracked to the quick—and his eyes were the worst part. Not feverish. Not afraid. *Calculating.* Sebastian had seen men in pain before, plenty of them, and pain made most people stupid. It was making Elias Vann precise.

"You've been standing on my dock for an hour," Elias said. "Thinking."

"I've been checking my plane."

"You've been thinking about whether I'm worth the fuel." He said it without accusation, which somehow made it worse. "Smart. That's the right question. Most men would ask it and then lie to themselves about the answer."

Sebastian crouched by the stove and fed it a split of spruce. The fire caught with a soft rush, and the cabin's shadows swung and resettled. "I'm not a rescue service. I fly medicine and mail. What happened to you?"

"Rifle. Two days ago. Up the channel." Elias shifted, and the sound he made was one Sebastian had heard from animals and tried never to hear from men. "Before you ask—who and why is a longer story than I've got hours left for. Ask me the short version."

"Which is?"

"I wouldn't sell, and they don't take that for an answer anymore. Not out here. Not since the permits started meaning something." He looked at Sebastian across the firelight, and there was something in his face that wasn't about the wound at all—something older and more tired. "You know how it is when a company decides a piece of country belongs to it. Everything that lives there becomes either an asset or an obstacle."

Sebastian did know. He knew it the way a man knows a scar he doesn't look at.

"I can get you to the coast," he said. "Coastal hospital at Meridian Point, if the fog holds off six hours. Maybe seven. After that I can't promise anything, and I won't pretend to."

"I'm not asking you to pretend." Elias fumbled at his coat, which was hanging off a nail by the door, and Sebastian watched him do it and understood, a half-second too late, that the whole conversation had been a runway and this was the takeoff. "I'm asking you to carry something."

The coat came down. From inside the lining Elias drew a roll of oilcloth, tied with copper wire, and held it on his knees like it weighed more than it did. Which, Sebastian thought, it probably did.

"I know what you're going to say," Elias went on. "You're going to say you don't want trouble. Good. Sensible. Trouble is heavy and it doesn't pack down. But hear the terms before you refuse them, because I've been watching you stand on that dock for an hour, Sebastian, and a man who stands that long is a man looking for a reason to stay."

The use of his name went through him like cold water. He'd given it at the dock, sure—first name only, the way he always did, the way that had worked for fifteen years across four hundred strips and two names' worth of distance. But the way Elias said it, flat and certain, made it sound like a word in a file somewhere.

"Elias—"

"Meridian Star." The old man laid the oilcloth roll across his knees and began, with maddening slowness, to work the copper wire. "Survey vessel. Went down in the channels north of here, nineteen years back. Official record says grounding, weather, bad luck. Official records are written by the people who need them written." The wire gave. He unrolled the cloth with both hands, and Sebastian made himself not lean forward and failed.

A map. Hand-drawn on the back of a fuel requisition sheet, in ink gone brown with age and water-stained at one corner. Coastline rendered in a sure, economical hand—channels, shoals, a bay with depth soundings penciled in like a confession. And across the middle, in letters that had been traced twice for emphasis, a set of coordinates and a single word: *HOLD.*

"Half a map," Elias said. "That's what you're looking at. The other half I gave to someone a long time ago, and that's not your problem tonight, so don't ask." He tapped the bay with one cracked fingernail. "Wreck sits under a shoal that only shows at low water. Nineteen years I've known where it is and nineteen years I've told no one, because what's in that hold is worth more than gold and costs more too. I was going to go back. Get it out myself, on my own terms, while I still had the hands for it." He laughed, short and terrible. "Then somebody put a rifle ball through me on my own dock, so my terms have changed."

"Who shot you?"

"Short version, I told you." His eyes came up, and for a moment the calculating look burned away and Sebastian saw what was underneath: a man alone in a country that had eaten everyone he'd ever stood next to. "Long version involves a name you'll wish you'd never heard, and I'm saving it, because names are the only currency I've got left and I'm not spending them on a man who's about to walk out that door."

Sebastian stood. He walked to the window. Outside, the fog was doing its slow arithmetic, thinning here, thickening there, the water invisible and then suddenly, treacherously, visible. Somewhere out there was a strip he knew, a coast he knew, a life built out of the deliberate practice of not knowing things.

"I'll fly you out," he said. "That part's settled. I don't need a map to do it."

"I know you don't." Elias's voice had gone quiet, and quiet was worse than the calculating. "That part was never the price. The price is the *or if not.*"

Sebastian turned around.

Elias had the map held out across the space between them, arm trembling with the effort, face grey as ash, and his eyes were steady as a surveyor's. "Get me out alive, and you owe me nothing. The map's a gift, a thank-you, whatever you want to call it. But if I die in that plane—if the fog takes us or the wound takes me or the coast is too far—then you finish what I started. You take my half of the map, you find the other half, and you do what I couldn't." He swallowed, and Sebastian watched the effort of it move down his throat like something being forced. "You go to the wreck. You see what's in it. And then you decide—with your own eyes and your own conscience—whether it comes out of that water or goes down forever."

"That's not a price," Sebastian said. "That's a debt with no amount written on it."

"It's the only kind worth signing."

"No." The word came out of him before he'd decided anything, the way true words do. "I'll fly you. I'll do everything a pilot does. But I don't take open-ended promises off dying men. I've seen what happens to people who do."

"Have you." Elias didn't lower the map. "Tell me about it."

And there it was—the thing he never talked about, sitting in the middle of a stranger's cabin like a passenger who'd forced the door. Sebastian looked at the stove instead of the old man. The fire had burned down to a red heart, and in its light the cabin's clutter resolved into a life: tin plates, a cracked rifle case, a shelf of journals with spines soft from handling. A man who'd lived alone out here a long time and kept accounts of it.

"There was a company," Sebastian said, and then stopped, because the next sentence was a cliff edge and he'd spent fifteen years not walking off it. "There was a time I promised something like that. Open-ended. And I kept it, and it cost—" He shut his mouth. Opened it. "It cost more than the thing was worth. That's all you get."

Elias studied him for a long moment. The fire cracked. Somewhere out in the fog, water lapped against the dock pilings with the patience of something that had been counting longer than either of them had been alive.

"You testified once," Elias said softly. "Didn't you. That's the shape of it. I've met your kind before—men who came north carrying a sentence they already served in some other court." He lowered the map at last, laid it on the blanket over his legs, and smoothed it flat with both hands, an oddly tender gesture, like settling a child. "I'm not going to ask your name. Whatever it is, it's yours, and out here a man's name should be his own business. But I'll tell you something for free, pilot, since you gave me a piece of the truth and that's more than most do."

Sebastian waited.

"The wilderness forgets nothing." Elias said it the way other men said scripture, or weather. "Every promise, every body, every name you bury—it all stays in the ground up here, and the ground doesn't care whether you meant it. You can run from people your whole life. You can't run from the country. Sooner or later it calls in what you owe, and it doesn't take payment in new names." He tapped the map once, twice. "This is the country calling. That's all this is. I'm just the hand it's using."

"You're a dying man trying to make your last problem mine."

"Yes," Elias agreed, without a flicker of shame. "Both things are true. That's how the wilderness works. It never gives you a clean choice, only a real one." He pushed the oilcloth roll across the blanket, toward the edge of the bunk, toward Sebastian. "Take it. Not as payment. As *weight.* You don't have to promise me a damn thing. Just carry it, and let it be heavy, and see how long you can stand on a dock pretending you're the kind of man who leaves things behind."

Sebastian looked at the map. Brown ink, water stain, the word *HOLD* traced twice. Nineteen years of a man's life folded into a rectangle of requisition paper, offered by someone with nothing left to lose and everything left to give away.

He thought about the dock. He thought about how long he'd stood on it, and about the fact that Elias had been watching him the whole time, and about what it meant that he hadn't been able to make himself walk to the plane. He thought about a courtroom in the south, fifteen years and a lifetime ago, and a promise he'd made with his hand on a Bible, and the two funerals that had followed it, and the third name on the list that had never gotten a funeral because the man had gotten on a floatplane instead and pointed it north.

The wilderness forgets nothing. Fine. Then let it remember this: he took the map, and he made no promise, and he told himself—firmly, the way you tell yourself things at altitude, where the air is too thin to argue—that those two facts were separate.

He picked up the oilcloth roll. It was lighter than it looked. That was the trap of it; he understood that already.

"I fly you out at first light," Sebastian said. "If the fog gives us a window, we're at Meridian Point by noon. You get a doctor, you get a chance, and when you can hold a pen again, you tell me who shot you and we go from there. That's the whole deal. No *or if not.* No finishing anything. If you die, I land the plane, I file a report, and this—" he held up the map "—goes in a drawer until you're well enough to take it back. That's my price. Take it or wait for the next plane, which is never."

Elias looked at him for a long time. Outside, the fog shifted, and through the window Sebastian saw the water appear for one clear second—black, flat, holding the sky's grey reflection like a mirror that remembered everything it had ever shown—and then the grey came down over it again and the water was gone.

"You'll change your mind," Elias said at last. There was no triumph in it. Just the flat certainty of a man reading a depth sounding. "Not tonight. Maybe not this week. But that map's going to feel heavier every day you carry it, and one morning you'll wake up and it'll be the heaviest thing in the plane, and you'll wonder how that happened, because you never promised anybody anything." He settled back against the wall, wincing, and closed his eyes. "The country doesn't need your signature, pilot. It just needs you to keep flying."

Sebastian put the map in his jacket, inside the pocket where he kept his license—the real one, the one with a name on it that matched the face, which was more than some of his documents could say. It sat there against his ribs, light as paper, and already he could feel it starting to weigh.

He banked the stove, checked Elias's pulse because it seemed like the kind of thing a man who hadn't promised anything would do, and went out onto the dock to sleep in the plane's cargo bay, where the fog couldn't reach him and the past, for a few more hours, couldn't either.

He woke once in the black before dawn, certain he'd heard an engine.

Not his. Not a floatplane's lazy thrum—a drone, high and thin, circling somewhere south of the bay. A spotter, maybe. A survey flight. Nothing. He lay in the dark with the map against his ribs and told himself it was nothing, and the sound faded, and the fog held, and he almost believed it.

Then, an hour later, as he was pulling the covers off the propeller in the first grey light, he heard it again—closer now, and lower—and out of the south, skimming the fog tops like something hunting, came a plane he didn't recognize, painted a white that no bush pilot in this country would choose.

It passed over the bay once. Twice. And on the third pass, it tipped a wing toward the dock, and held there, looking down at him.
