## 3. Juniper

The floatplane came in low over the treeline an hour before dawn, and Juniper Vann watched it from the gravel bar with her father's binoculars and no intention of being seen.

She'd been on the ground since midnight, having walked the last six kilometers from where she'd paid a fisherman to drop her—cash, no name, no questions. The airstrip belonged to someone called Oyelaran, according to the registry, but registries lied for a living, and she'd learned long ago that the truth about a man lived in what he'd built rather than what he'd signed. What he'd built was this: one hangar with a sagging door, one fuel cache under a tarp that hadn't been retied since spring, one windsock faded to the color of old bone. A man who lived alone and wanted it that way. She knew the type. She was the type.

The plane came out of the east with its running lights off, which told her something right there. Honest pilots flew with their lights on. It flared over the water, touched down smooth as a knife through skin, and taxied to the dock below her position without a single wasted motion. The pilot stepped out onto the floats, tied off, and stood for a moment in the gray light looking north like a man checking whether the weather had followed him.

He was tall, Black, somewhere past forty, moving with the economy of someone who'd stopped spending energy on anything unnecessary years ago. He carried a canvas mail sack up to the hangar and came back down twice more, hauling crates, and Juniper tracked every trip through the glass, cataloguing: strong, careful, alone, armed—she caught the outline of a holstered sidearm when his jacket swung. Not a medicine runner's habit. Or maybe exactly a medicine runner's habit, up here, where the medicine sometimes wasn't.

Her father's last radio call had come from a coordinates grid forty miles northeast of this strip, eleven days ago, on a frequency Elias Vann had sworn he'd never use again because it belonged to a life he'd buried. That was the part that kept her awake at night. Not the call itself—her father calling for help was rare enough to be remarkable, but people changed. No. What kept her awake was that he'd used *that* frequency. The one from before. Which meant whatever had opened his gut out there in the interior had also reached back fifteen years into his past and dragged it into the open.

She'd spent those eleven days doing what she did best, which was finding people who didn't want to be found. It paid poorly and it paid in cash, and it had taught her that everyone left a wake: a fuel receipt, a rumor in a river town bar, a charter pilot who remembered a face. Her father's wake led here—to a bush pilot named Sebastian Oyelaran who appeared in the coastal registry eight years ago with no history before it, flying routes nobody else wanted, asking no questions and answering none.

People with no history were either boring or hiding. Boring men didn't turn their running lights off.

* * *

She waited until he went inside the hangar, then moved.

Down off the gravel bar, along the treeline, boots quiet on wet moss. She crossed the open ground in the last of the dark without hurrying—hurrying was how you made noise—and let herself into the lee of the hangar wall where the woodpile stood. Then she sat down on the split cedar rounds with her rifle across her knees and waited for the door to open.

It opened at first light. The pilot came out with a tin mug in one hand, took two steps toward the dock, and stopped.

Juniper watched him take inventory of her the way she'd taken inventory of him: hands, waistline, eyes, exits. His eyes found hers and stayed there, unbothered, which she noted. Most men looked away first.

"You're parked on my woodpile," he said.

"I'm sitting on your woodpile. There's a difference." She didn't move. "Sebastian Oyelaran?"

"That's the sign on the hangar."

"And underneath the paint?"

Something flickered behind his face—not fear, exactly. Recognition of the question's shape. "Who's asking?"

"Juniper Vann. Elias Vann is my father."

The mug came up and he drank, slow, buying time or just deciding how much of it to spend on her. "Never heard of him."

"Eleven days ago he made a distress call on 121.5 from a grid northeast of here. Forty miles, maybe less. Somebody answered it. Somebody flew in." She let that sit. "There are maybe four pilots who'd fly into that country in November, and three of them I've already talked to. They all pointed here."

"Maybe nobody answered."

"Then why do you have fresh mud on your floats from a lake system that only connects to the coast through the Vann River delta?" She nodded at the plane. "I've fished that delta my whole life. That mud's not local."

He looked at the floats. Looked back at her. There was a long moment where she felt him weighing something, some private arithmetic, and she recognized the expression because she wore it herself most days—the calculation of how much truth a stranger could be trusted to carry.

"Your father's alive," he said finally.

The words hit her harder than she expected. She'd prepared for dead. Dead was clean; dead meant grief and a grave and an end to hoping. Alive meant a man bleeding somewhere, or worse, a man who'd sent for a stranger instead of his daughter.

"Where."

"A prospecting camp off the Kettle chain. He's hurt. Bad. Gut wound, going septic by the look of it. I got him stabilized enough to argue, and then he argued me into leaving." The pilot set the mug down on the woodpile beside her, deliberate, within her reach—a small gesture she didn't miss. "He wouldn't come out. Said he'd die before he'd fly out, and I believed him, so I flew back for supplies and a plan. That was four days ago. Weather's had the whole coast socked in since."

"He sent you instead of me."

"He doesn't know you exist," Oyelaran said, and then seemed to hear how it sounded, and didn't soften it. "That's not an insult to you. He didn't mention anyone. He mentioned a map, a wreck, and a debt. In that order."

Juniper stood up slowly, rifle still in her hands but barrel down, and the movement made him shift his weight—just slightly, just enough that she saw he'd marked where her weapon was and where his own was and the distance between both. Two animals measuring the same clearing.

"What wreck?"

"He didn't say. He drew half a map and gave it to me as payment for the flight out. Half." He almost smiled, and it didn't reach anywhere near his eyes. "Left half. Coastline, bearings, a shoal mark. No destination."

The cold went through her then, sudden and total, colder than the morning. Because she had the other half. Had carried it folded against her ribs for three years, since the day her father handed it to her at a ferry terminal without explanation and said, *If I ever go missing, find the man this leads to, and don't trust him further than the job.* She'd thought it was paranoia. With Elias Vann it was always hard to tell where paranoia ended and prudence began; the line ran through the middle of everything he did.

She reached inside her jacket, slow enough that he could watch every inch of the motion, and drew out the oilcloth packet. Unwrapped it. Held up the right half of a hand-drawn chart, water-stained, drawn in her father's cramped surveyor's hand.

Oyelaran went very still.

"The Kettle chain camp," she said. "Is he guarded? Is someone else after him?"

"Not when I left. But I wasn't followed, if that's what you're asking, and I can't promise it stays true." He was looking at her half of the map now the way a starving man looks through a restaurant window. "You've been carrying that for how long?"

"Three years. He gave it to me and told me to find whoever held the other piece, and to trust them exactly as far as the work required." She wrapped it back up. "So. Here we are. How far does the work require?"

* * *

They negotiated on the dock, standing a careful six feet apart, while the tide slid out and exposed the barnacled pilings below them.

His terms: they flew together, they split whatever the wreck turned out to hold fifty-fifty, and she followed his calls in the air without argument, because it was his plane and his funeral if she didn't. Her terms: they went back for her father first, before anything else, wounded or not, willing or not; she kept physical custody of her half of the map; and he answered one question honestly, whenever she chose to ask it, about who he'd been before he was Oyelaran.

"That last one's expensive," he said.

"So's lying to me at altitude."

A muscle worked in his jaw. "Fair."

They shook on it, and his grip was dry and hard and brief, and she registered that he shook like a man who'd learned handshakes in rooms where they meant more than greetings.

Then, while he preflighted the plane, she asked the question early—couldn't help it, the way you scratch a bite. "Why'd you answer his call? You didn't know him. Nobody flies into that country in November for a stranger."

He crouched by the strut, checking the rigging, and didn't look up. "Because the voice on the radio was scared," he said. "And I know what it costs to be scared and alone and have nobody answer."

It was the most honest sentence he'd offered, and she understood immediately that it had cost him, and filed it away with the rest of what she was collecting: the lights-off landings, the mud on the floats, the handshake, the scar tissue showing through the seams of a name that fit like a borrowed coat.

They loaded the plane in silence—her duffel, her rifle case, his supply crates, fuel drums lashed to the floats until the aircraft sat heavy in the water. As he ran up the engine she climbed into the right-hand seat, uninvited, and buckled in, and he glanced over at her with something that might have been the ghost of approval.

"Rules," he said over the noise. "You see anything in the air we don't like, you say so. You touch nothing on the panel. And if I put her down somewhere that isn't where we planned, you get out fast and you don't ask why until we're clear."

"Same rules I'd give you," she said.

He laughed—one short breath of it, surprised out of him—and pushed the throttle forward, and the floats came off the black water into a sky the color of hammered tin.

Forty miles northeast, weather was building over the Kettle chain, great gray towers of it, and Juniper watched them grow in the windshield and thought about her father lying in a cold cabin with a hole in him, drawing maps for strangers because his daughter was three years and one silence away. She'd have her reckoning with him about that silence, if he lived long enough. If he didn't, she'd have it with whoever put the wound in him.

Below, the coastline unspooled, and somewhere ahead of them, high and thin and closing fast, a spotter plane turned lazily against the clouds—white fuselage, no markings she could read at this distance, flying a search pattern too systematic to be fishing.

Oyelaran saw it at the same moment she did. His jaw tightened.

"Hold on," he said, and banked toward the storm.
