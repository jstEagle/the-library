## 4. Half a Map

**Sebastian**

The spotter plane came out of the northeast at nine-forty in the morning, high and patient as a gull over a fish school, and Sebastian knew what it was before he could read the tail number. Corvane didn't fly for pleasure. Everything that company put in the air had a purpose, and the purpose was always the same: find something, and take it.

He was halfway through preflighting the Otter when the engine drone crossed the bay. He froze with his hand on the strut, oil-smudged to the elbow, and watched it come. Juniper Vann stood by the fuel drums with her arms crossed, and he saw her track it too—saw her head tilt the way a hunter's does when something moves at the edge of the trees.

"That's not a survey plane," she said.

"No."

"It's flying a grid." She said it flatly, but there was something underneath, something tight. "Quarter-mile legs. That's search pattern."

Sebastian wiped his hands on a rag that had been gray since before either of them owned anything. The plane passed overhead, close enough now that he could see the camera blister under its belly, and turned wide and unhurried, beginning its next leg two miles east.

"They're looking for something," Juniper said.

"Or someone." He hadn't meant to say it out loud. It slipped out the way truth does when you're tired—sideways, unguarded. He covered it fast. "Or nothing. Companies fly grids because grids look like diligence on an invoice."

She looked at him then, really looked, the way she'd been doing since yesterday when she climbed out of her father's old Cessna 180 onto his strip without so much as a how-do-you-do. Her eyes were the color of shallow water over slate, and they didn't blink enough. "You're lying," she said. "But you're lying carefully, which means you know something. So let's stop pretending we're strangers, Oyelaran. My father gave you half a map. I have the other half. And there's a Corvane spotter plane drawing boxes over our heads. I'd say pretending has a short shelf life this morning."

The drone of the engine filled the silence between them, swelling as the plane swung back around on its leg.

Sebastian looked at the sky, then at the woman, then at the hangar where Elias's map fragment lay folded inside a chart case behind his spare propeller. Fifteen years of instinct told him to say nothing, to wait, to let weather or distance or time do what it always did—separate him from trouble before trouble learned his face. But the spotter plane had changed the arithmetic. A man alone can hide. Two people standing on the same airstrip with the same secret are a target whether they trust each other or not.

"Inside," he said. "Now. Before it makes another pass."

* * *

**Juniper**

The hangar smelled of dope and fabric, hydraulic fluid and old coffee, and Juniper stood in the middle of it with her arms still crossed while Sebastian rolled the door shut against the daylight. Through the gap before it closed she caught one last glimpse of the spotter plane, a silver sliver against cloud, methodical as a loom.

Her father's half of the map rode against her ribs in a waxed paper envelope, where it had ridden for six days, from the coastal registry office to the float pond at Teller's Landing to here. She'd slept with it. She wasn't ashamed of that. When your father is dying in some interior camp with a hole in him and his last radio call is a string of coordinates and your name, you hold on to what he left you.

"Show me yours first," she said.

Sebastian leaned against his workbench. Up close, in the flat light through the hangar's small windows, he looked older than he had on the dock—fifty maybe, or fifty-five, gray coming into his beard, a scar through one eyebrow she hadn't noticed before. His hands were pilot's hands, knuckles scarred, nails cut short. Hands that had done more than fly.

"Why?"

"Because mine proves I'm not bluffing. Yours proves you're not either. That's how trades work."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I walk out of here, flag down that spotter plane with a signal mirror, and tell them a bush pilot named Sebastian Oyelaran is holding half a map to the Meridian Star." She kept her voice level. It cost something. "I don't want to do that. But my father is bleeding out somewhere north of here, and I am out of patience and nearly out of time, and you—" she pointed at the ceiling, toward the grid pattern being drawn above them, "—you're scared of that airplane. Which tells me you have things to lose too. So we both have reasons to deal honestly. That's the only kind of deal worth making."

Something moved behind his eyes—not anger. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man meeting his own reflection in unfavorable lighting.

He pulled the chart case from behind the spare propeller, worked the zipper, and unfolded the paper on the workbench with the care of a man handling a live shell.

Juniper took out hers and laid it beside it.

For a long moment neither of them spoke. The two fragments were the same paper—old hydrographic chart stock, salt-stained, torn along a single ragged line, as if someone had ripped one sheet in half in a hurry or in fury. Elias's half showed the northern channels, a coastline rendered in faded ink, depth soundings marching offshore in their neat italic columns. Sebastian's half showed the south: a river delta drawn freehand over the printed geography, cairns marked with tiny x's, tide notes in Elias's cramped engineer's hand.

And across the tear itself, matching edge to ragged edge, ran a single continuous feature—a shoal symbol, the coiled hazard marking, broken cleanly in two.

"Tide-dependent," Juniper said quietly. Her finger traced the soundings on her father's half. "Six fathoms here, on the printed chart. But Dad wrote over it." She tilted the paper to the light. "Two point four at low water. He corrected it. Somebody else's numbers were wrong and he knew it."

"He would." Sebastian said it before he could stop himself, and she saw him hear himself say it—the familiarity in it—and press his lips together.

"You knew my father."

"I flew him twice. Years ago." He said it smoothly, but smoothness was its own confession; nobody remembered cargo runs from fifteen years ago in that much detail unless they'd mattered. "Medicine, mail, parts. He was quiet. Kept his own counsel. Paid cash."

"That's it? That's all you've got?"

"That's all that matters right now." He tapped the joined halves of the map, careful not to touch her fingers. "Look at what we actually have. Your half gives us the coast and the approach channel. Mine gives the river system and the markers inland. Neither one of us can find the wreck alone—you'd be searching forty miles of shoreline for a shoal that only exists at low tide, and I'd be poking up dead-end sloughs until winter. Together—" he drew a line with one blunt finger, coast to river, tear line to tear line, "—together it's a route. Three days' flying if the weather holds. Four if it doesn't."

Juniper stared at the map instead of at him, because looking at the map was easier than admitting he was right. Her father's handwriting swam in front of her. *Slack water +40 min. Do not trust the eastern channel after blowdown.* Little instructions, little warnings, written for someone. Written for whoever carried this half. Had he written them for her? For the stranger on the workbench? For himself?

"The spotter plane," she said. "You think they know about the wreck."

"I think Corvane filed salvage permits on this coast three months ago, which is public record, which means they're not searching—they're sweeping. They want to find it before anyone else does, or make sure nobody else can." He straightened. "Either way, that plane up there means our timeline just got shorter than yours."

"My timeline is my father dying."

"Your father," Sebastian said, and his voice went quiet in a way that made her look up, "gave me that map in exchange for a promise I haven't decided whether to keep. So believe me when I tell you I understand timelines."

* * *

**Sebastian**

She didn't ask what promise. He watched her decide not to ask—watched her swallow the question whole, file it away, add it to whatever ledger she was keeping on him. She was Elias's daughter down to the bone. The old man did the same thing. Asked nothing, noticed everything, paid cash.

Outside, the spotter plane's drone rose and fell, rose and fell, working its grid. Through the hangar's dusty window Sebastian tracked it by sound alone, the way you learn to track weather, the way you learn to track trouble. It was moving north now. Working away from them. For the moment.

"Alliance," Juniper said. "Terms."

He raised an eyebrow.

"You heard me. If we're doing this, we do it properly. I've watched deals rot in this country because nobody wrote down who owed what." She pulled a stub of pencil from her jacket and flipped his chart case lid over to use as a backing board. "One. We split everything the wreck holds. Whatever it is—stones, records, salvage rights—half each, decided together, no unilateral moves. Two. You fly me to my father's camp first chance the weather gives us, wreck or no wreck. He comes before the treasure. Three." She paused, and the pencil hovered. "Three. Nobody lies about the past anymore. Not the big things. Small lies I can live with—I'll even supply some myself—but if it bears on why Corvane is sweeping this coast, or why my father tore a map in half, I get the truth. Same goes for you. Ask me anything."

It was more than he'd offered anyone in fifteen years. It was more than anyone had asked of him in fifteen years, which was precisely the point, and precisely the danger.

"Counter-term," Sebastian said. "On your number three. Truth when it bears on the matter at hand. Not before, not all at once. Some of what you'd want to know isn't mine alone to give—it involves other people, some of them dead, some of them maybe not. When it becomes relevant, you get it straight. That's the best I can do today."

She studied him for a long moment. Outside, the plane droned, distant now, working its way up the far shore.

"Relevant," she repeated. "Defined by whom?"

"By events. Events are honest. They'll tell us when."

To his surprise, she almost smiled. It was a small thing, quickly gone, but it rearranged her whole face for half a second—made her look young, made her look like photographs he'd never seen of a girl growing up in prospecting camps with grease under her fingernails.

"Events," she agreed. She wrote the terms on the back of a fuel slip in a hand as cramped and certain as her father's, then signed it. *Juniper M. Vann.* Then she held out the pencil.

Sebastian looked at it. A signature was a small thing. A signature was an enormous thing. He thought of other documents, other names, other lifetimes, and his hand hesitated over the paper for one breath too long, and he saw her see it, and catalog it, and say nothing—which was, in its way, the first loyalty between them. She could have asked. She didn't.

He signed. *S. Oyelaran.* The name felt like a coat that fit well enough, most days.

"Temporary alliance," she said, folding the fuel slip and handing him his copy. "Dissolves when the wreck is found and divided, or when one of us breaks terms. Whichever comes first."

"Fair."

"And Oyelaran—" she shrugged her rifle sling higher on her shoulder and nodded at the window, at the pale northern sky where the clouds were organizing themselves into something with intent, "—that plane will be back. And when it's back, it won't be alone. Spotters find things. Fleets take them. So we leave today, while it's working the far shore, and we stay ahead of it. Agreed?"

He looked at the map spread on the bench, the two halves joined at the torn seam, the shoal symbol whole again for the first time in however many years. Somewhere north of here, Elias Vann was dying or dead, and somewhere south of here, a company with a fleet and a permit clock was hunting the same water they intended to fly into.

"Agreed," Sebastian said. "Fuel's already in. Weather window closes by evening—we ride the front in behind it. Corvane's pilots won't fly that cell."

"You will?"

"I grew up in weather like that." He began rolling the map, and stopped, and looked at her. "There's one more term. Mine."

She waited.

"When we find it—whatever it is—if there's a moment where it has to be sunk or saved, we decide that together too. No exceptions. Because I've seen what happens when one person decides for everyone." He tied the roll with a length of safety wire, quick and practiced. "And I'm not going to watch it happen twice."

Juniper held his gaze for a long moment, and whatever she read there, she nodded once, slow, like a door closing on a room she intended to come back to.

Outside, far to the north, thunder muttered along the front like something clearing its throat. They had perhaps five hours of light, one shared map, and a storm to outrun—and two miles east, unseen, the spotter plane turned onto a new heading that would, within the day, put a name to the little airstrip on the bay, and start a clock none of them could see yet.
