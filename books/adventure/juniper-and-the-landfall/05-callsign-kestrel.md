## 5. Callsign Kestrel

The Cessna came in low over the treeline at 0740, before the fog had finished burning off, and Sebastian knew it was trouble before he saw the markings. Nobody flew into Meridian Strip at that hour without a reason. The morning traffic here was floatplanes hauling fish, and they came at noon when the water warmed. A wheeled aircraft on a gravel strip at dawn meant government, or money, or both.

He was elbow-deep in the Cowhawk's cowling, chasing a fuel line that had developed opinions overnight, and he didn't straighten up right away. He gave himself ten seconds with his hands on warm metal, listening to the engine tick and the gulls argue over the tide flats, and he let himself pretend—for ten seconds—that the plane was just a plane, and the morning was just a morning.

Then he wiped his hands on a rag that had been black longer than he'd owned it, and walked out to meet whatever was landing.

The Cessna 206 touched down smooth as a signature, rolled to the end of the strip, and turned. Government white, with the concession authority's crest—a stylized current line over crossed survey bars—stenciled on the tail. Sebastian had seen that crest maybe four times in fifteen years, always from a distance, always with his stomach dropping through the floorboards of whatever he happened to be standing on.

The prop spun down. The door opened.

The woman who climbed out was fifty-something, silver at the temples, wearing field khakis that fit like she'd stopped caring about fashion somewhere around her second decade in the bush. She carried a leather document case under one arm and nothing else—no rifle, no bear spray, no overnight bag. That told him things too. She wasn't planning to stay long, and she wasn't afraid of what she'd find.

She looked across the strip at him, and the wind coming off the water moved her hair, and for one long moment neither of them moved.

Then she smiled, and it was the worst thing he'd seen all year.

"Hello, Kestrel," Marguerite Iles said.

* * *

Fifteen years is a long time. It's long enough for a river to change course, for a burn scar to green over, for a man's face to go from young to middle-aged in a way that fools everyone except the people who knew him when his voice hadn't finished settling. Sebastian had counted on that. He'd built a life on that arithmetic—the assumption that time was a kind of camouflage, that if you stayed still enough and long enough, the country itself would grow over you like moss on a wreck.

Marguerite Iles walked toward him across the gravel, and every hour of that arithmetic came due at once.

"Iles," he said. His voice came out level. He'd have paid money for that; he'd have paid more not to notice how hard he'd worked for it. "Concession Authority. You're a long way from your office."

"My office has wheels now." She stopped six feet away, close enough to talk, far enough to be polite about it. Her eyes went past him to the Cowhawk, to the open cowl, to the fuel line hanging loose. "You still work on your own engines?"

"Cheaper."

"Mm." She let the syllable sit there like a stone dropped in still water. "You were always better with machines than paperwork. That hasn't changed, I take it."

"Depends who's asking."

"The Concession Authority is asking." She opened the document case—not dramatically, not as a threat, just the way a person opens a folder when business is business—and drew out a single sheet. "Sebastian Oyelaran. Licensed commercial pilot, bush endorsement, based out of this strip since—" she glanced at the page, though he suspected she knew the date cold, "—eleven years. Clean record. No violations. No complaints. Not so much as a parking ticket at the coastal registry." She folded the sheet along its crease. "It's a very quiet file, Mr. Oyelaran. Almost suspiciously quiet."

"Fifteen years of flying medicine runs and freight doesn't make headlines."

"No," she agreed. "It doesn't. But fifteen years ago a different pilot made plenty of them, and then vanished between one filing season and the next. Testified before the land claims tribunal in the south, gave evidence against Halvern Consolidated Mining—Corvane's predecessor, back when Corvane was still eating smaller companies to get its teeth—and then, mid-hearing, while witnesses were still being called, that pilot simply ceased to exist. New name. New region. No forwarding address." She tucked the sheet away. "The tribunal never closed the file. Neither did I."

The wind came down the strip and pushed against them both. Somewhere behind the hangar, the generator coughed and caught. Sebastian became aware of his own heartbeat the way you become aware of a sound only when it changes.

"That pilot," he said, "is dead."

"Yes," Iles said. "I imagine he had to be. Dead men don't get shot at." She said it without cruelty, which somehow made it worse. "I've spent fifteen years knowing that callsign existed and not knowing where it landed. Do you know how many Kestrels there are in the aviation registries? Eleven. I checked every one. Cross-checked flight hours, home fields, physical descriptions. Eleven dead ends." She looked at him, and her eyes were tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep. "And then three days ago, a dying prospector makes an emergency call from a derelict camp two hundred miles into the interior, and the responding pilot logs a flight plan under the name Oyelaran—but the tower tape catches his radio callsign, and the callsign is Kestrel, and I sat in my office listening to that tape eleven times because I did not want to believe it."

* * *

He made coffee because his hands needed something to do that wasn't shaking, and because refusing her hospitality would have been its own confession. They stood in the open doorway of the shack—he wouldn't ask her to sit, and she didn't try—and drank bad coffee while the fog finished lifting off the water and the day arrived, indifferent, around them.

"Elias Vann," Iles said. "How is he?"

"Alive when I left him. Barely."

"You flew him out?"

"He refused. Said he'd rather die in his own camp than in a hospital corridor." Which was true, and also not the whole truth, and Iles watched him say it with the particular patience of someone who had spent a career letting people lie to her until they ran out of breath.

"And instead you took something else out of that camp. Something he gave you."

The coffee cup was warm in his hand. He kept his eyes on the water. "You'll have to ask him."

"I intend to, if he lives." She sipped. Grimaced, honestly, like the coffee offended her personally. "This is worse than the tribunal cafeteria. That's saying something."

"Why are you here, Iles? If you've known for three days, why wait?"

"Because I wanted to see your face when I said the name." She set the cup on the windowsill. "Because I wanted to know whether you'd run. Pilots run. It's the first thing we learn—when the weather turns, when the engine quits, when the ground comes up wrong—you point the nose somewhere else and you go. Fifteen years ago you pointed the nose somewhere else and you never landed." She turned to look at him fully. "You're still standing here. That matters to me."

"It shouldn't matter. I'm nobody. I fly fish and insulin."

"You're a witness," Iles said. "The most important one the tribunal ever had, and the only one still breathing. Do you remember the others? Of course you do. Okonkwo, who drowned in eight inches of water. Delacroix, whose stove exploded. The surveyor whose name I'm not permitted to say because his death was ruled accidental by a coroner who later took a consulting job with Halvern." She recited them flatly, without performance, and each name landed like a nail driven into wood. "I was junior counsel then. I watched you walk into that hearing room looking like a man carrying something heavy, and I watched you carry it for two days, and I watched the third day never come. And I have wondered, for fifteen years, what broke. What they threatened, or who they killed, or what you saw that made silence seem survivable and speech seem fatal."

"You don't want to know."

"I've spent my whole career wanting to know things I'd be happier without." She picked up her document case. "So here is what happens now, and I'll say it once, plainly, because you were always a plain-dealing man under everything else. I am required to file a report confirming the identity of the witness known as Kestrel. That report goes to the tribunal's successor body, which will reopen the file, which will put your name—your real name—into systems that Corvane reads the way sharks read blood. Once I file, you are visible again. Permanently. There is no second disappearance. Companies like Corvane keep better records than governments do."

"How long do I have?"

"Three days. I can justify the delay—weather, scheduling, the state of my aircraft's alternator, which is genuinely failing and will take me a day to nurse home. Three days, and then I file, because it's my job, and because I have spent fifteen years watching good people die badly while the paper trail stayed clean, and I am done sitting on files." She paused at the top of the steps. "But I'll tell you something else, Kestrel, man to man, off the record. Whatever Elias Vann gave you—whatever he's mixed up in—it's connected to the same company that hunted your witnesses. And if you're going to be visible again anyway, you might think about what you want to be visible *for*."

She walked down the steps and across the gravel, unhurried, and Sebastian stood in the doorway holding a cup of terrible coffee and feeling the ground shift beneath a life he'd built on the certainty that it never would.

At the Cessna she stopped and half-turned. "The girl," she said. "Vann's daughter. She filed a charter request out of the coastal registry yesterday. Heading north." A beat. "I mention it because I liked you, fifteen years ago, and liking someone is a professional liability I've mostly cured. But not entirely. Three days, Kestrel."

The engine caught. The 206 rolled, lifted, and shrank into the bright morning until it was a sound, and then a memory of a sound.

* * *

Sebastian stood on the empty strip for a long time after the drone faded. The gulls came back. The tide kept doing what tides do. The world resumed its ordinary business, and none of it helped.

Three days.

He thought about the map folded inside his jacket, the half of it that was his, drawn in a dying man's careful hand. He thought about Juniper Vann, who had landed on his strip uninvited with a rifle and the other half, and who was probably awake by now in the bunkhouse, cleaning the weapon or reading her father's journal or simply watching him through the window with those flat, measuring eyes that trusted nothing—including, especially, him.

He thought about Okonkwo drowning in eight inches of water, and about the sound his own feet had made running.

Invisibility, he understood now, had never been a strategy. It was scar tissue. You don't choose a scar; you just live inside it afterward and call the shape of it a life. And scars don't protect you. They only mark the place where you were cut, and tell anyone who knows how to read them exactly where the blade went in.

Marguerite Iles had read him in eleven passes of a tower tape.

He walked back to the hangar, pulled the fuel line free, and began again, because the plane had to fly tomorrow no matter what, and because hands that are working cannot shake. But halfway through the job he stopped, rag in hand, staring at nothing.

Three days. And a girl heading north with half a map, into country where Corvane's fleet already had a permit and a head start—and now, thanks to a tower tape and a tired woman's conscience, they had something else.

A name.

His name.

From the bunkhouse, faintly, came the sound of a rifle bolt closing—once, deliberate, unmistakable. Juniper Vann had heard the Cessna land too.
