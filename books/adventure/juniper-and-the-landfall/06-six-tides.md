## 6. Six Tides

The registry office at Port Calloway smelled of diesel and old paper, and the woman behind the counter looked like she'd been poured into her uniform sometime during the last administration and never quite set.

Juniper had been in a hundred offices like it—mining claims in the Yukon, fisheries permits up the Skeena, the kind of government outposts that existed mostly to prove the government existed. She knew the rhythm: the forms, the ledgers, the clerk who could tell you everything and nothing depending on how you asked. So she let Sebastian do the talking, because he wore the kind of face that made clerks want to be helpful, and she stood by the window with her hands in her jacket pockets and watched the harbor through salt-hazed glass.

The *Meridian Star* was a ghost. That was the whole problem with ghosts—they left paperwork.

"Salvage concession," Sebastian said, easy as asking about the weather. "Corvane Marine Services. I'm doing a survey contract and I need to know where their permit boundaries sit so we don't cross them."

The clerk—her name tag said ODELL, and her eyes said she'd heard every lie this coast had ever told—pulled a ledger without argument. Concession maps weren't secret. That was the joke of it. All the money on this coast moved through rooms like this one, stamped and filed and available to anyone who asked politely.

"Corvane," Odell repeated, running a finger down the column. "Northern Shelf survey block. Filed out of their Kestrel Point office." Her finger stopped. "Huh."

Juniper turned from the window. "What?"

Odell tapped the page. "Concession expires." She checked the wall clock, then a tide table pinned crookedly beside it, its corners soft with damp. "Six tides. They filed for a ninety-day extension in the spring and it was denied—insufficient progress toward recovery, per the assessor. So as of the sixth tide from now, that whole block reverts to open registry, and if they haven't pulled whatever they're after, they lose standing. Anyone can file on it after that. Including—" she glanced up, and there was something almost human in her flatness, "—whoever's asking me about their boundaries while not looking at me."

Sebastian smiled his pilot smile, the one Juniper had already learned meant nothing at all. "Just being careful."

"Mm." Odell turned the ledger around so they could see it. The concession block was drawn in red ink over the chart of the northern channels—a big lazy polygon that swallowed half the coastline. "They've got tender traffic in there daily. Dive barge anchored off Serrated Point most of the season. And here's the funny part."

"I love funny parts," Juniper said.

Odell's mouth twitched, which on her face probably counted as laughter. "A salvage captain is required to file working coordinates when he locates a target. Corvane's lead captain—name of Brandt—filed these three weeks ago." She slid a carbon copy across the counter. Coordinates, handwritten, official. "I grew up on that water. Those numbers put his target in the middle of the Maren Channel, which is forty fathoms of silt and nobody's grandmother. There's no wreck in the Maren Channel. There's no *bottom* worth mentioning in the Maren Channel."

Juniper picked up the slip and read the figures twice. She'd spent enough years around charts to feel the wrongness of them before she could name it—the way you feel a stair that isn't there.

"He's lying," she said. "He found something and he doesn't want anyone else looking."

"Or he hasn't found anything," Odell said, "and he's stalling. Either way, he's got six tides to make it true or lose the block." She took the slip back, squared it, and looked at them both with the weary clarity of someone who processed other people's secrets for a living. "Anything else?"

"The Meridian Star," Juniper said, before Sebastian's elbow could find her ribs. "Survey vessel. Lost up north, what—twelve, fifteen years ago? Is there a loss report?"

Something changed in Odell's face. Not much. A shutter moving a quarter inch. "Vessels are reported lost to Transport, not to us."

"And yet."

"And yet," Odell agreed, and for a long moment the only sound was the tick of the wall clock and the gulls screaming over the harbor. Then she opened a drawer beneath the counter—not the ledger drawer, a different one, deeper—and pulled out a file so thin it was nearly a rumor. She didn't hand it over. She just laid it flat and turned it so they could read.

*MERIDIAN STAR — SURVEY VESSEL — REPORTED LOST WITH ALL HANDS, [DATE]. CAUSE: FOUNDERING, WEATHER. NO WRECK LOCATED. FILE CLOSED.*

No coordinates. No survivor statements. No mention of cargo. The report was four pages long and three of them were boilerplate.

"Foundered in weather," Sebastian read aloud, softly. "In a season when the weather bureau logged calm for eleven straight days."

"That's what it says," Odell agreed.

"Who filed it?"

"A company that doesn't exist anymore." She closed the file. "Which is also what it says about half the companies on this coast. Gentlemen. Lady. Tide table's free, coffee's fifty cents, and I was never unfriendly to either of you." She turned back to her ledger, and the conversation was over.

* * *

Outside, the wind had come around from the northeast, carrying the smell of open water and something colder under it. Juniper stood on the office steps and watched the fishing boats knock against the pilings and tried to think past the noise in her chest.

Six tides.

She'd grown up on tide tables. Her father had taught her to read them before she could read words—taught her that the sea kept two clocks, the one everyone saw and the one underneath, and that you ignored the second one at your peril. Six tides was three days. Three days until Corvane's claim on the northern shelf evaporated, and every dollar they'd sunk into that dive barge and those tenders went with it. Which meant that for three days, they would be desperate. And desperate men with a fleet at their backs did not file polite complaints.

"They know where it isn't," she said.

Sebastian leaned on the rail beside her, arms folded, watching the water the way he always watched it—like it owed him an answer. "What?"

"Brandt's coordinates. Forty miles wrong, minimum. You don't miss by forty miles unless you're missing on purpose." She turned to look at him. "So either he's bluffing to hold the concession while he searches—and he's running out of time—or he knows exactly where the wreck is and he's hiding it from his own company."

"Third option."

"Name it."

"He knows where it is," Sebastian said slowly, "because someone told him. Someone who knew the real location well enough to give him numbers that were wrong in a convincing way." He was quiet a moment. "Numbers don't come from nowhere, Juniper. Somebody drew that map. Half of it's sitting in my flight bag right now."

The wind pushed at them. Down the dock, a man in coveralls was coiling rope, and Juniper made herself stop watching him because she'd been watching everyone since the airstrip and it was wearing grooves in her.

"Elias," she said. The name came out rougher than she intended. It still did that—caught on something on the way out.

"It fits."

"It doesn't fit, that's the problem." She pushed off the rail and started walking, because she thought better in motion and because Sebastian's calm made her itch. He fell into step beside her, unhurried, infuriating. "My father hated Corvane. Hated them the way you hate a thing that's hurt you personally. He wouldn't sell them directions to a used canoe. So either he sold them something false—which means he was playing a game deep enough to get himself gutted over—or—"

"Or the wound didn't come from Corvane at all."

She stopped walking.

That was the thing she'd been circling since the derelict camp, since the smell of blood and woodsmoke and her father gray-faced on a cot, pressing a torn half-map into a stranger's hands. Everyone bled for a reason on this coast. And Elias Vann had been alone up there for months, in a camp with no other tracks, and he'd taken a wound low in the belly that no bear makes and no fall explains.

"You saw him," she said. "You saw the camp. Was there a weapon? Signs of a fight? Anything?"

Sebastian was silent long enough that she turned fully around to face him. His expression hadn't changed—he had a face built for not changing—but something behind it had gone careful.

"There was a rifle rack," he said. "Empty. One cot slept in. Food stores gone through, recent. And blood on the dock boards that had been there more than a day before I landed." He met her eyes. "Your father wasn't attacked, Juniper. Not by anyone who came from outside. Whatever happened, he was there for all of it."

"Self-inflicted?"

"No." He said it fast, and she caught it—the speed, the certainty. "No. It wasn't that."

"How do you know?"

"Because I've seen men do that to themselves." Flat. Final. A door closing. "This wasn't that."

She wanted to push. Everything in her wanted to grab that sentence and shake it until it gave up the rest—the *seen men*, the *this wasn't that*, the whole buried country behind his eyes that surfaced for a second and went under again. But they were standing in the open on a public street in a port town where Corvane ran tenders out of Kestrel Point, and she'd learned discipline from the same man who'd raised her badly, so she banked it. Put it with the other questions. The pile was getting tall.

"So he hurt himself some other way," she said instead. "Or somebody was already inside. Or—" and here was the thought she'd been refusing to say aloud, the one with teeth, "—or he gave Corvane those false coordinates, and they figured it out, and they came to ask him why."

Sebastian looked at her for a long moment. The gulls wheeled. Somewhere down the waterfront a winch started up, groaning.

"You think he traded with them," he said.

"I think everybody trades with somebody." She held his gaze and watched him take that one on the chin, because she'd meant him to. "You're flying me north for a map. I'm flying with you because you're the only plane on this coast that'll go. Halloran's people will trade silence for silence. My father traded something to somebody, and now he's got a hole in him and I've got half a chart. Nobody out here does anything for free. Including you."

"Including me," he agreed, without flinching, and she hated how much she respected that.

* * *

They bought fuel, or rather Sebastian bought fuel with money he counted out twice, and Juniper bought the tide table and a paper chart of the northern channels from the chandlery next door, and a bag of apples, and a box of shells that fit the rifle she'd carried onto his plane without asking permission. The chandler was missing two fingers and talkative about everything except the northern shelf, which he declined to discuss with a shrug that said *ask the fish, they're the only ones who go there.*

By late afternoon they were back at the floatplane, tied at the public dock, and the light had gone long and gold and lying. Port Calloway looked gentle in that light. Juniper knew better. Every place on this coast looked gentle right before it showed you what it was.

She sat on the dock with her boots over the black water and spread the two halves of the map beside her, weighted at the corners with stones and an apple. Elias's half was older—the paper soft as cloth, drawn in pencil gone silver with handling, annotated in his cramped hand. Her half was newer. She'd found it taped inside the lining of her mother's old seabag, which her father had shipped to her eight months ago with no note, which she had not opened until the radio call came through and she understood it was a message, not a gift.

Her half covered the outer approaches—a stretch of coast she'd sailed with him at fourteen, though he'd called it by names that matched no chart. His half covered the interior channels, the maze. Where the halves overlapped, the lines met almost perfectly. Almost. In one place, near the center, his line bent where hers stayed straight, a discrepancy the width of a channel, and she'd stared at it for an hour on the flight down without deciding whether it was age, error, or a correction.

Sebastian crouched beside her, studying it upside down. He'd been quiet since the registry office. Quiet in a way that had weight.

"Six tides," he said finally.

"Three days."

"Corvane's going to spend all three of them tearing that shelf apart, because Brandt's numbers are garbage and he knows it, and the moment the permit lapses with nothing salvaged, heads roll in Kestrel Point." He traced the air above the map without touching it. "Desperate companies get creative. Creative means spotter planes, paid informants, and men walking docks asking questions about bush pilots flying north with a passenger."

"So we don't fly north."

"We fly north."

"At night?" She looked up at him. "Through weather, with a dead radio, on half a map?"

"On half a map," he said, "with the other half." And then, quieter, almost to himself: "Elias drew this to be flown. Look at the annotations. Headings, drift allowances, landmarks you can't see from the water. This isn't a sailor's map, Juniper. Your father expected someone to come in by air."

She looked down at the penciled notes—at *hold 200' below ridge line until the notch bears 040*, at the little symbols she'd taken for rocks and now recognized as clearings—and felt the ground shift under an assumption she hadn't known she was standing on. Elias had planned this. Not the wound, maybe, not the dying. But the route. The escape. He'd drawn a road out of the wilderness for someone with wings, months ago, and then waited alone in a camp with an empty rifle rack to see who'd come.

"Who was he expecting?" she whispered. "Not me. I didn't even know he was alive."

Sebastian didn't answer. He was staring at the map, but she could tell he wasn't seeing it anymore. He was seeing something else—something with the same look he'd worn in the registry office when he'd said *I've seen men do that to themselves* too fast, too sure.

"Sebastian."

"Hm."

"What aren't you telling me?"

He gathered the map halves together with deliberate care, folded them along their old creases, and handed them to her handle-first, like a man returning something he'd borrowed.

"When we're north of the range," he said, "with water under us and nobody on the frequency, I'll tell you everything I know about your father." He stood, and offered her his hand, and his eyes when she took it were the first honest thing she'd seen in Port Calloway. "But right now there's a man down that dock buying fuel for a Corvane tender, and he's been looking at this airplane for ten minutes, and I'd very much like to be airborne before he finishes his walk over here."

She didn't turn to look. Discipline. She just slung her rifle, stepped up onto the float, and got the door while Sebastian cast off the lines with hands that were suddenly very quick.

The engine caught on the third try, coughed, caught. As the floats came clean off the water and Port Calloway tilted away beneath them—rooftops, masts, the red-roofed registry office small as a stamp—Juniper looked back once and saw the man in coveralls standing at the end of the dock, phone raised, not waving.

Northeast, the sky over the range had gone the color of a bruise coming up.

Six tides, she thought, as the first rain hit the windshield. And somewhere behind them, the clock was already running faster than the sea.
