## 7. The Long Water

The weather came down off the divide like something with intent.

I'd been watching it build since dawn—a bruised anvil of cloud stacked over the northern ranges, the kind of formation that looks decorative from a distance and murderous up close. By the time we finished the preflight at the coastal strip, the barometer had fallen hard enough to make my ears pop on the ground, and the wind had swung around two points in under an hour. Old-timers called that a weather change. I called it a deadline.

"Six tides," Juniper said, buckling into the right-hand seat like she'd been born there. She wasn't smiling. Neither was I.

"Five and a half by now."

"Then stop talking and start the engine."

The Cessna 180 wasn't pretty—fifteen years of bush work had given her a patchwork cowling and a paint job the sun had chewed to bone—but she was honest. Every dent had a story, every rivet held because I'd checked it myself more times than I could count. I ran the mag check twice anyway. When the sky ahead of you looks like that, you want your airplane telling you the truth.

Juniper climbed in with her rifle slung across her knees and her half of the map folded inside her jacket, against her chest, the way people carry things they've decided matter more than their own comfort. She hadn't slept much. Neither had I. The spotter plane yesterday had seen to that—Corvane's eyes in the sky, lazy circles over the strip, close enough that I could read the registration if I'd cared to. They knew we were moving. The only question was whether they knew where.

We lifted off into air that felt like wet wool, heavy and unstable, the horizon smearing into cloud before we were five hundred feet up. I turned north, put the nose into the long corridor between the coast range and the plateau, and settled in for what bush pilots call the long water—three hundred miles of nothing but lakes, muskeg, and weather, with maybe four strips between here and the shoal country where Elias's map fragments pointed.

For the first hour, neither of us talked much. That's not unusual. Cockpits are quiet places; there's a discipline to them. You speak when the machine needs you to or when the silence gets heavier than the words would be. Juniper sat with her eyes moving constantly—horizon, instruments, my hands, the sky behind us—and I recognized the habit because it was mine. Two animals in a small cage, each counting the other's breaths.

It was she who broke first.

"How far to the first cache?"

"Fuel cache? Ninety minutes, if the weather holds." I nodded at the murk building off our left wing. "If it doesn't hold, longer."

"And if it really doesn't hold?"

I glanced at her. Her face was calm in a way that cost her something—I could see the effort of it around her eyes. She was twenty-six, maybe twenty-seven, and she had her father's jaw and none of his softness. Elias Vann, even dying, had been a man who apologized to the world. Juniper looked like a woman who expected the world to apologize to her, and who intended to collect.

"Then we land wherever the ground is flat enough and wait," I said. "That's the whole secret of flying up here. The sky always wins eventually. You just try to be on the ground when it does."

"You sound like you've lost that argument before."

"Once or twice."

She almost smiled. Almost. "Tell me about the plane instead. How old is she?"

"Nineteen sixty-eight. Rebuilt engine at eleven hundred hours, new fabric on the tail three years ago. She's got maybe two thousand hours left in her if nobody abuses her."

"And how many hours do you have?"

"Fifteen thousand, give or take."

Her eyebrows went up despite herself. "That's a lot of invisible years."

The word landed harder than she probably meant it to. *Invisible.* I kept my eyes on the instruments and let the moment pass, but she saw me flinch—I could feel her see it—and after that the cockpit got quieter again, and stayed that way through the next forty miles.

* * *

The weather caught us at the mouth of the Kestrel River valley—which is a coincidence of naming that I did not appreciate, given everything else attached to that word.

It came out of the west as a wall, no leading edge of scattered cloud, no courtesy warning. One minute we were skimming above broken stratus with blue holes showing the country below, and the next the world simply ended: a gray curtain from fifteen thousand feet down to the treetops, boiling slowly eastward like smoke filling a room. Lightning flickered inside it, silent at this distance, the way lightning is when it's still deciding whether you're its problem.

"I see it," Juniper said, before I could say anything. She'd been watching it too.

"There's a pass through the range about thirty miles north. If we can beat the cell to it, we get through clean. If we can't—" I did the arithmetic out loud, because saying numbers makes them real and real numbers can be argued with. "Ground speed's a hundred and ten knots. Cell's moving maybe twenty-five. We make the pass with twenty minutes to spare, if the headwind doesn't build."

"And if it builds?"

"Then we turn south and land at Taku Lake and lose six hours."

"We don't have six hours."

"We don't have anything if we're inside that." I banked slightly north, tightening the line to the pass. "Elias waited fifteen years to sink whatever's in that wreck. It'll keep another day."

"That's not what he told you."

No. It wasn't. Elias had made me promise—or tried to—with blood coming through his fingers, and I'd refused him, and then taken the map anyway, which is the kind of thing a man does when he wants to feel like he hasn't promised without actually promising. I pushed the thought down with the mixture control.

The headwind built.

Of course it did. Twenty-five knots became thirty, then forty, the airspeed indicator sagging while the groundspeed bled away beneath us, and the wall of weather stopped being something we were racing and became something we were losing to. I watched the numbers and hated every one of them.

"Sebastian." Juniper's voice was level. "Talk to me."

"Forty-knot headwind. Pass is twenty-two miles. We'll hit the edge of the cell eight miles short." I scanned the terrain below—black spruce to the horizon, muskeg brown and drowned, one thin ribbon of lake catching gray light. "Options are getting narrow."

"What's that?" She pointed down and left. A river, braided and shallow, glinting through a gap in the trees.

"Kestrel River. Gravel bars every few miles. Landable, technically, in an emergency."

"Then we have an emergency option. Good." She pulled the map fragment from her jacket and spread it on her knee, weighting the corners with her thumb and forefinger. "How far to the pass along the river?"

"Twenty-two miles. Why?"

"Because I'm going to navigate us there, and I need to know what I'm aiming at."

I looked at her. She didn't look back. She was already working—eyes on the map, on the river, on the clock on the panel—her lips moving faintly as she counted.

"Dead reckoning," she said. "Dad taught me. Before he taught me anything else. He said GPS is a rumor and radios are opinions, but time, speed, and heading don't lie." She glanced up. "So give me true values. What's our heading?"

"One-nine-seven magnetic. Call it two-oh-one true, this far west."

"Groundspeed?"

I fed her the numbers as the GPS computed them, and watched her work: pencil stub appearing from somewhere inside her sleeve, marks on the map margin, the clock, the river sliding beneath us like a dark vein. She tracked our position by the bends in the waterway, calling corrections—five degrees right, hold, now seven left where the river doglegged—and I flew exactly what she told me, and the strange thing, the thing I'd think about later, was how quickly I stopped checking her math. Somewhere in the second mile of doing it, I simply trusted her. Trust, in my experience, was a muscle I'd atrophied. Apparently it remembered.

The radio died at minute nine.

Not dramatically—it gave a crackle, a whisper of static, and then the com panel went dark except for a single amber fault light. I cycled the breaker. Nothing. Tried the standby set. Static, then nothing. In the cell's electrical shadow, even the navigation receiver was hissing like a kettle.

"No contact with flight service," I said. "No weather updates. Nobody knows where we are."

"Nobody ever knew where we are," Juniper said. "That's the point of this country. Fly the airplane. I'll find the pass."

And she did. With the radio dead and the wall of storm chewing up the last of the daylight ahead of us, she navigated us up the throat of the Kestrel valley by pencil marks and clock ticks, correcting for the wind as it gusted and backed, calling headings in a voice that never rose, never shook, while rain began to stipple the windshield and the turbulence came down the valley at us in rolling waves that bounced the yoke in my hands like a live thing.

Eight miles short of the pass, the cell reached it first.

I saw it happen—the gray wall sliding across the notch in the range like a door closing—and for one cold second I did the math on turning back, on Taku Lake, on six hours lost, on Corvane's fleet working unopposed while we sat on a beach drinking melted snow. Then I looked at Juniper, and she was looking at the same door closing, and her expression wasn't fear. It was calculation.

"There's a saddle," she said. "South of the pass. Look—the map shows a fire road, survey cut, nineteen fifty-four. Survey cuts run straight over saddles, not passes. It'll be lower."

"It'll also be unmaintained for seventy years."

"So will the pass, in this weather. Which one's wider?"

I studied the terrain ahead, the rain-hazed ridgelines, the memory of this country from a dozen flights in better conditions. She was right. There was a saddle—broad, rounded, maybe four hundred feet lower than the pass, hidden behind a spur from most approach angles. I'd never used it. I'd never needed to.

"Two miles south of the pass," I said. "We can slip down the valley and cross at the saddle if the ceiling holds above the trees."

"The ceiling will hold," she said, "because it has to."

I laughed—actually laughed, once, sharp, surprised out of me—and hauled the plane into a slipping turn as rain hammered the windshield and the world outside shrank to a gray tunnel with a green floor. We crossed the saddle at six hundred feet above the trees, in rain so heavy the wipers were useless, Juniper calling the drift corrections and me flying by the seat of my pants and the ghost of the ridge off the left wingtip, and then we were through—through—into the cold clear air on the far side, the storm slamming shut behind us like a verdict.

Neither of us spoke for a full minute. The Cessna droned north over open plateau, wings steady, sunlight breaking through torn cloud far ahead and laying gold stripes across the tundra.

"Well," Juniper said finally. Her hands, I noticed, were shaking now that it was over. She tucked them under her arms, out of sight, and pretended they weren't. "Your radio's dead and your navigator isn't."

"No," I said. "She certainly isn't."

* * *

We put down at the fuel cache on a lake called, on the charts, Unnamed 447—an honesty in cartography I respected—and while the drums drained into the tanks I stood on the gravel beach and let the silence of the north press in around me. Juniper wandered the tree line with the rifle slung, not hunting, just walking the perimeter the way she'd walked it at every stop since the coast. Checking. Always checking.

She came back as I buttoned the cowl and sat down on the float strut—or the wheel pant; the cache strip was gravel—and looked at me for a long moment with an expression I couldn't file anywhere.

"You flew that saddle like you'd done it before."

"I hadn't."

"You flew it like you'd dreamed it, then." She sat down on the drum beside me, close enough that our shoulders nearly touched. "There's something I've been turning over since the airstrip. You fly like a man hiding. Not from weather—from *people*. You check the mirror every ninety seconds. You landed yesterday facing the treeline with the engine warm. And yesterday, when that spotter plane came over, you went white as the gravel." She paused. "Fifteen thousand hours. You're too good to be a nobody. So who are you, Sebastian Oyelaran?"

The question hung in the cold air. Behind us the lake lay flat and pewter-colored, holding the sky upside down, and somewhere in the spruce a loon made its sound, that rising cry that has always seemed less like birdsong than like grief remembering itself.

I could have lied. I'd lied well for fifteen years; it was practically a rating on my license. But she had just navigated us through a storm cell on faith and arithmetic, and there is a debt that forms between people who come through weather together, and lies don't survive it.

"You know the name Corvane," I said.

"Everyone in the concession country knows Corvane."

"Corvane had a predecessor. Meridian Consolidated. Same money, same families, different letterhead. Fifteen years ago I was a pilot for a survey outfit that contracted to them, and I found things I shouldn't have found—shipping manifests, sample logs, things that didn't match what Meridian told the regulators about what they were pulling out of the ground and what they were leaving in the rivers." I watched the loon cross the lake, low and fast. "There was an inquiry. I testified. Under my real name, which was a mistake, though I didn't know it yet."

Juniper had gone very still.

"Three witnesses died inside a year. Not murdered—not provably. One drowned, one drove off a logging road, one burned in a cabin fire that the inspector ruled accidental. All three had testified. All three had names on paper." I heard my own voice go flat, the way it does when I'm reciting something I've rehearsed too many times in the dark. "I understood the lesson. Names on paper are targets. So I buried mine. New territory, new license under the new name, and I flew medicine runs and freight and never touched a claim, never filed a report, never signed anything that mattered. Fifteen years of being no one. It worked."

"But the inspector," Juniper said quietly. "Iles. She knew your callsign."

"Kestrel." The word tasted like rust. "My old callsign. She flew search-and-rescue out of the same base, back when. She's the only person in this country who could look at my face and see through fifteen years of nobody." I turned and met her eyes. "You asked why I hide. That's why. Not because I'm ashamed of what I did. Because the last time I signed my name, people died for reading it."

Juniper was quiet for a long time. The loon cried again, farther off now, heading up the lake into the evening.

"My father used to say the wilderness forgets nothing," she said at last. "Every cut, every campfire ring, every grave. He said that's why he loved it and why he couldn't live near people—people forget too easy. They forget what they did to each other, and then they do it again." She looked out at the water. "You didn't forget. You just moved somewhere the forgetting wouldn't follow you. And it followed you anyway."

"Yes."

"Then running was never going to work."

"No," I said. "It was never going to work."

She nodded slowly, absorbing that, filing it somewhere behind those watchful eyes. Then she stood, brushed gravel off her jeans, and pulled the journal from her pack—Elias's journal, the one she'd brought from the coast, oil-stained and swollen with damp.

"Then we should probably know what else he buried," she said. "There's a fuel cache marked on his half of the map, forty miles up the next leg. He wrote coordinates in the margins here that don't match any cache I know about." She tapped the page. "And there are pages missing. Torn out. Recently, by the edges."

I looked at the journal, at the ragged stubs of paper where something had been removed, and felt the old unease stir in my chest—the instinct that had kept me alive for fifteen years, whispering that maps and journals and dying men all leave things out, and the things they leave out are always the reason for everything else.

"Forty miles," I said. "We can make it before dark if we go now."

Juniper closed the journal and held it against her chest, the way she carried everything that mattered.

"Then let's find out what my father didn't want anyone to read."
