## 8. What Elias Buried

The fuel cache was where the half-map said it would be: a rusted drum sunk into the shingle at the mouth of a creek that didn't have a name on any chart Sebastian owned, just a penciled *E.V.* and a date eleven years old. The drum's lid had been soldered shut against bears and weather, and it took Juniper twenty minutes with a pry bar and a rock to work it open while the tide sucked gravel out from under her boots in long, patient pulls.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark: two jerry cans of aviation fuel, a box of .303 rounds that had survived dry, a tin of matches, a coil of copper wire — and a journal.

She knew it before she touched it. The cover was green canvas, soft-cornered, the kind survey companies handed out by the crate in the years when the interior still believed itself full of promise. Her father had carried one like it all through her childhood. He'd filled three of them with soundings and bearing lines and, once, a pressed flower he'd never explained — juniper, of course, because Elias Vann was not a subtle man about the things he loved and was a master of subtlety about everything else.

This one wasn't his handwriting.

That was the first wrongness, and she felt it in her sternum before her brain caught up. The entries were in a careful draftsman's print, initials *R.M.* in the corner of the first page, dated the spring of the year Juniper turned nine. She almost put it back in the drum. Fifteen hundred miles from anywhere, sitting on a cache her father had hidden and never mentioned, she did not want to read another man's account of whatever this was.

But the last third of the book was in Elias's hand. She knew the slanted, impatient letters, the way he crossed his sevens. She'd learned her own numbers by tracing them.

Sebastian was fifty yards off, checking the tie-downs on the plane where he'd nosed it up the shingle above the tideline. He'd barely spoken since the channels began to narrow, and when he did speak it was in the clipped voice of a man rationing himself. She understood rationing. She decided not to share hers.

She sat down on the drum lid with the journal in her lap and the wind coming off the water cold enough to make her eyes water, which was convenient.

* * *

R.M. wrote like an engineer, which meant he wrote like a man who expected to be believed. The early pages were survey notes: core samples, depth soundings along a river system marked only as *the Kestrel drainage*, assay values for zinc and copper that were good but not remarkable. Routine work. A company called Corvane Minerals had contracted the survey; R.M. was their senior geologist, and he was proud of his numbers.

Then, in midsummer, the print changed. Tighter. Faster. The margins filled.

*Water samples from below the tailings outfall are not consistent with the discharge permits filed in Victoria. Arsenic at forty times permitted levels. Lead beyond scale. I have re-run the assays three times on three separate instruments. There is no error.*

*I took my concerns to the site manager, D. Halloran's office forwarded me to legal. Legal has asked me to sign a revised sampling protocol. I have declined.*

Juniper looked up. The light was going long and bronze across the water, and somewhere inland a loon was calling in that descending, mournful way that always sounded like a question nobody wanted answered. Down the beach, Sebastian straightened from the plane's strut and watched her, and she watched him back for a moment — then went on reading, because whatever was between the two of them, it could wait. This couldn't.

*They have moved the outfall. Not closed it — moved it, upstream, into a side channel that drains toward the village at Kestrel Narrows. I asked why. I was told the new location "improves operational efficiency." The village draws its drinking water from that channel. There are children there. I have seen them hauling water in buckets.*

*I have begun keeping duplicate samples. Sealed cores, chain-of-custody documented, stored aboard the Meridian Star. If anything happens to me, the evidence is in the hold, under the forward cargo deck, in crates stenciled C-7 through C-19.*

Her mouth had gone dry. She read the next entry twice because the first time her eyes refused to slow down.

*Corvane has chartered the Meridian Star as a supply vessel for the winter. This is either carelessness or opportunity, and I no longer believe in their carelessness. I have told Vann. He understands what the samples are worth — not in dollars. In weight. Some truths are too heavy to carry and too heavy to sink. We are going to find out which.*

*Vann.* Her father. In another man's journal, in the middle of someone else's dying, like a character walking onstage in a play he hadn't auditioned for.

* * *

The last entry in R.M.'s hand was dated the twenty-ninth of October, and it was short.

*They know. Halloran came aboard tonight with two men I didn't recognize and asked me, very reasonably, whether I had considered how isolated we all are out here, and how slowly help travels. I said I had considered it. She smiled. I am writing this by lamplight in the chart room while she waits on deck, and I want whoever finds this to understand: I am not afraid of dying. I am afraid of being wrong about the water, because if I am wrong, the village drinks for nothing and my death buys nothing.*

*I am not wrong about the water.*

Below that, nothing. The next page was Elias's.

His first entry was written the same night, and the handwriting was different from every sample of it she'd ever seen — steadier, slower, a man writing like he was carving stone because paper felt too temporary.

*Ray Marchetti gave me his journal tonight and told me to keep it where no one would look for it. Then he went up on deck to talk to Halloran's men, and I heard what a man sounds like when he has already decided. I stayed below like he told me. That is the thing I will have to live with, if I live.*

*They scuttled her at dawn. Not where the charts say — Ray and I moved the markers weeks ago, both of us, without saying to each other why. We sank her in the shoal basin past the second narrows, deep water over shallow, so that any salvage crew working from the official wreck position would drag their hooks over bare gravel forever. The Meridian Star sits under a shoal that only shows at low tide, in a channel you cannot find unless you were standing on her deck the night we walked her markers out.*

*In her hold are nineteen crates of sealed cores and water samples and assay records and photographs of the outfall and of the children at Kestrel Narrows. Corvane thinks they went down with a routine supply ship in a routine grounding. They let her sink because they thought they were drowning a problem. They were drowning the proof.*

*Ray is dead. They say he fell. I signed the incident report. I signed it, God forgive me, because I have a daughter who is eight years old and a wife who is sick, and Halloran made it very clear what happens to men who complicate things out here. I took Ray's journal and I kept my mouth shut and I told myself I would come back for the crates when it was safe.*

*It has been eleven years. It is never safe. But I am older now than Ray got to be, and I find I care less about safe.*

Juniper stopped reading.

She didn't decide to stop. Her hands simply wouldn't turn the page, and the loon was still calling, and the tide had turned sometime during the last hour without her noticing, and the whole bay had gone quiet in that particular way water goes quiet when it starts to run the other direction — as if the country itself were holding its breath to see what she'd do.

Eleven years. She'd been eight when he left the coast the final time. She remembered the leaving — the truck, the duffel, her mother crying in a way that frightened her more than shouting would have. She remembered being told he'd taken contract work up north. She remembered the postcards with no return address. She remembered, later, learning the word *estranged* and understanding that it applied to her father the way a diagnosis applies to a patient: accurate, clinical, useless.

She had spent her adult life believing Elias Vann had chosen the wilderness over her. It was a clean belief. It fit in the hand. You could be angry at it, and anger was fuel, and fuel kept a person moving.

Now she sat on a fuel drum at the edge of the map with her father's confession in her lap, and the clean belief was gone, and what was underneath it was worse and better and much heavier.

He hadn't left her. He'd been bought — cheaply, with fear — and he'd spent fifteen years paying the debt back in exile, moving marker buoys in the dark, building cairns in the rain, hiding a dead man's journal inside a fuel cache like a man burying a body he intended someday to exhume.

And the treasure. God, the treasure. Half a map, fought over, flown through storms for, hunted by a salvage fleet with a permit clock ticking down — and it wasn't gold. It wasn't claim-stones. It was arsenic and lead and photographs of children, sealed in crates under a shoal, and every pound of it was a liability wearing a fortune's clothing. Corvane hadn't sent a fleet north to recover wealth. They'd sent a fleet north to drown a witness a second time.

Dree Halloran. The name was in the journal twice, and it was also the name of the fixer who had stood on a dock two days ago and offered Sebastian money for their silence with the calm of a woman quoting a freight rate. Eleven years ago she'd smiled at a man on a deck and waited while he decided to die.

Juniper became aware that Sebastian had crossed the shingle and was standing ten feet away, hands in his pockets, looking at the journal with an expression she couldn't read — or could, and didn't like.

"You knew," she said.

"I suspected." His voice was low, even. "Your father told me the wreck held records, not riches. He didn't tell me whose records, or what kind. I thought maybe survey fraud. Skipped royalties." He paused. "I didn't think it would be this."

"Did you read it? While I was checking the tie-downs?"

"No." And then, because he was apparently incapable of letting a hard moment stay hard: "I don't read other people's mail. Occupational habit."

She almost laughed, and the laugh died somewhere in her chest and came out as something else. She held the journal out to him instead, open to Ray Marchetti's last entry, and watched him read it standing up in the wind, his face doing very little and his eyes doing a great deal.

"Halloran," he said finally, closing the cover gently, like it might bruise. "She was there. On the deck."

"She was running it."

"Which means the fleet two coves over isn't salvaging." He looked north, up the darkening channel, where the water ran black and smooth toward the narrows. "It's erasing. And if they find those crates before we do, there won't be an inquiry or a permit violation. There'll just be a barge that reports empty holds, and a village that keeps drinking."

"The village," Juniper said. "Kestrel Narrows. Is it still there?"

"It's there." Something moved behind his face, some current too deep to name. "People are still there."

She looked at him a long moment. There was a story in that pause — she'd known the man four days and she could already tell he was a book of pauses — but the light was failing and the tide was making and somewhere north of them a dive barge was running generators over bare gravel, dragging hooks through the wrong water, getting warmer.

"We should fly the last leg tonight," she said. "While the weather holds. While they're anchored."

Sebastian nodded slowly, but he didn't move toward the plane. He was looking at the journal in her hands.

"There's a page you haven't turned," he said.

She frowned. She'd read to the end of Elias's entry — she was sure of it. But when she opened the back cover, there it was: a single loose sheet, folded in quarters, tucked behind the binding where the spine's shadow hid it. She unfolded it with fingers that had suddenly gone clumsy.

It was a chart fragment. Hand-drawn, recent — the ink was barely faded. The shoal basin, the second narrows, and the Meridian Star's true position marked with a small, precise cross. And beneath the cross, in her father's slanted hand, five words:

*If you're reading this, Juniper —*

The rest of the line had been torn away. Torn cleanly, deliberately, along the fold — the rest of the sentence was somewhere else, in someone else's pocket, and every possibility of what it said hit her at once: *turn back*, or *forgive me*, or *sell it*, or *sink it*, or simply *I'm sorry*, and she stood there in the last of the light holding half a sentence the way she'd been holding half a map, half a father, half a truth, while down the channel, faint but unmistakable, the generators of the Corvane dive barge changed pitch — engines spinning up, anchors lifting, the fleet done waiting out the weather and coming to finish what it started eleven years ago.
