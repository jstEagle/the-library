## 9. Corvane on the Horizon

The bay had a name on the charts—Kestrel Sound, which Sebastian had always taken as a private joke the country was playing on him—and like most names on those charts it described nothing you'd recognize from the water. What it actually was: a long gray thumb of sea pushed eleven miles into the coast, guarded at its mouth by a shoal that showed itself only twice a day, ringed by timbered slopes that ran down to the tide line in curtains of fog. There were two beaches worth landing a plane on, one dock that had been built by a cannery company sixty years dead, and no reason for anyone to be there.

Which was why, when Sebastian brought the Cessna off the gravel bar at first light and swung north along the channel, and Juniper said, "Smoke," he felt the word land in his chest like a stone dropped down a well.

"Where?"

"Two points off the bow. Shore side." She was leaning against the window frame, binoculars up. She'd slept in the plane's back seat with the rifle across her knees and woken without a sound, and she hadn't spoken more than forty words since they'd lifted off, but she was awake now in every way that mattered. "Not a campfire. Too steady. Diesel generator."

Sebastian took the stick in his left hand and looked where she was looking. Through a rift in the morning fog, maybe four miles out, a smudge of woodsmoke rose off the shoreline, straight and unhurried, the smoke of people who expected to stay a while.

"Nobody camps in Kestrel Sound," he said.

"Nobody flies into it either." She lowered the binoculars. "Your friend Iles know we came this way?"

"Iles doesn't need to know anything. She just needs to wait." He banked gently south, putting a ridge of black spruce between them and the smoke, and dropped toward the water. "Corvane filed wrong coordinates at the registry. Sixty miles east of here. If their lead captain is any good, he'll have figured that out by now."

"And if he's any good," Juniper said, "he'll have started looking west."

Sebastian didn't answer. He throttled back and let the plane settle onto the water at the head of the channel, behind an island thick enough to swallow them, and killed the engine, and for a moment there was nothing but the tick of cooling metal and the slap of small waves against the floats and somewhere far off, so faint it might have been imagination, the low animal drone of a diesel generator working through the fog.

They sat there listening to it.

"Two coves over," Juniper said finally. "Maybe three. Sound carries funny over water."

"You've got good ears."

"My father says—" She stopped. Corrected herself, flatly, the way she'd been correcting herself all morning, all yesterday, every time the present tense slipped out of her mouth. "Said I hear everything and listen to half of it."

Sebastian looked at her profile against the gray glass of the window. Fifteen years ago he would have told her something kind. Now he just nodded and started checking his fuel state, because kindness was a currency he'd stopped carrying, and because there was work to do.

* * *

The plan was simple, which meant it was probably stupid. They'd come into the sound to find the first marker on Elias's map—a cairn on a granite point above the second beach, according to the joined halves of paper that Juniper now carried folded inside her jacket, against her heart, like a letter. The cairn was a day's walk from where they'd landed if the terrain cooperated, and the terrain never cooperated. But the dive barge—if that's what the smoke was—anchored two coves south meant Corvane was already working the water, and every hour they spent on the ground was an hour the fleet spent getting closer to the wreck.

So they went anyway. That was the whole plan.

Sebastian beached the plane on the shingle below the island's lee shore and they hiked the spine of it on foot, single file through devil's club and salal, until they could see down the length of the sound. And there it was.

The tender sat at anchor in the third cove, a hundred-foot steel workboat with a crane on her aft deck and a dive barge lashed alongside, floodlights still burning pale against the daylight because nobody aboard had bothered to shut them off. A RIB ran back and forth between tender and shore, ferrying crew. On the beach above the tideline someone had pitched a canvas camp, and the smell of frying bacon came across the water with obscene clarity.

"Salvage tender," Juniper said. "Class B. That crane's rated for lifting engines off wrecks." She passed the binoculars back. "That's not a survey outfit. That's a stripping outfit."

"I know the type." Sebastian worked the glasses. Crew count: eight visible, maybe twelve total. Two men on the dock of the old cannery, smoking. One woman standing at the rail of the tender, not smoking, not talking, just watching the water with the stillness of somebody whose job was watching. Even at this distance something about her posture snagged his attention—the absolute economy of it, no wasted motion, weight settled, hands loose. He'd seen that stance before. He'd worn that stance before, in another life, on other decks.

"That one," he said. "At the rail. Mark her."

"Why?"

"Because she's the only person on that boat who isn't doing anything, and the ones who aren't doing anything are the ones being paid to think."

Juniper took the glasses back and studied the woman for a long moment. "Fixer," she said at last. It wasn't a question. Her father's vocabulary, coming out of her mouth.

"Probably."

"So what do we do? Go around? Come back after dark?"

Sebastian weighed it. The cairn lay north; the tender lay south; the fog was burning off, which meant by noon anyone on that water could see for miles, including anyone flying a spotter plane, including a yellow Super Cub with a distinctive dent in its port float. Every option ended with them exposed. The only variable was when.

"We go now," he said. "Fast and quiet, while the fog's still on the north end. We find the cairn, we read what your father left, and we're gone before their RIB makes a morning run."

"And if it doesn't?"

He didn't have an answer for that, and she knew he didn't, and she shouldered her pack and started walking anyway, which he was beginning to understand was how she answered most questions.

* * *

They found the cairn at midday, exactly where the joined map said it would be: a waist-high stack of flat granite stones on the point above the second beach, weathered silver, older than it looked and younger than it seemed. Elias had built things to last. Under the capstone, wrapped in oilcloth and tarred twine, was a tin box, and in the tin box was a notebook and a brass fitting stamped with a number sequence that made Juniper go very still.

"This is a hold fitting," she said. "Off a survey vessel. This is off *her*." She turned the brass over in her fingers like it might burn. "He was here. He stood right here and he took this off the wreck himself."

"Or he put it there to prove he'd been." Sebastian kept his voice level, but his eyes weren't on the tin. They were on the beach below them, on the sand and gravel between the tideline and the grass, where the light had come around enough to show what the morning fog had hidden.

Footprints. Boot prints, fresh, a dozen sets or more, coming up from the waterline in a loose fan, converging on the cairn, milling around it, and then going back down to the water where a RIB had obviously been pulled up. One set of prints was smaller than the rest, lighter-heeled, and it stood apart from the others, slightly uphill, in the position of someone who watched while other people worked.

"They've been here," Juniper whispered. "They found it already."

"No." Sebastian crouched, touched the edge of one print. The sand crumbled cleanly; the walls hadn't slumped. "Tide's been through since. This is last night's, maybe this morning's. And look—they dug around the base, here, and here." He pointed to the disturbed gravel where someone had probed with a shovel or a rod. "But the capstone's undisturbed. The tin's untouched. They didn't find what they were looking for because they didn't know what they were looking for. They found a rock pile and got angry about it."

"But they know the route now. They know the marker exists."

"They know a marker exists." He stood, brushing sand from his knee, and looked south down the sound, where the fog was almost gone and the tender sat at anchor in plain view. "Which means the next place they dig will be the next marker, and the one after that, until they run the route themselves. Your father gave us a head start of exactly one marker."

Juniper put the brass fitting in her jacket with the map. When she spoke again her voice had changed—harder, flatter, the voice of someone rearranging furniture inside herself. "Then we don't go back for the plane yet. We keep moving. Right now, today, ahead of them."

"The tide turns in four hours. The shoal at the mouth of the sound won't let us out by water until evening, and the plane can't lift from the second beach in this wind."

"Then we walk out the logging spur to the north beach and fly from there."

"It's nine miles of blowdown."

"I grew up walking worse than blowdown." She was already moving. "You can keep up or you can explain to my father why I did this alone."

* * *

They didn't make the north beach.

They were two miles along the spur, in a corridor of alder and rotting corduroy log road, when Juniper stopped so abruptly Sebastian nearly walked into her. She raised one hand—not for silence, though she got it, but flat-palmed, the gesture of someone who had grown up around rifles and knew what a shot sounded like versus what a branch cracking sounded like.

Down through the trees, faint but unmistakable, came the whine of an outboard motor. Then a second one. Running fast, running together, running north.

Sebastian did the math he didn't want to do. Two RIBs, running north up the sound at speed, while the tender stayed anchored south. Not a supply run. A sweep. Somebody aboard that tender had looked at the same arithmetic he had—the north beach, the only other landing, the obvious exit—and had sent boats to close the door.

"They're cutting us off," Juniper breathed.

"Or they're just patrolling."

"At thirty knots, in formation?" She shook her head. "No. That's a net."

Sebastian stood in the green gloom of the spur and felt the old familiar cold move up through him, the cold he'd spent fifteen years learning to live without, the cold of being hunted by people with more boats than you. He thought about the plane on the shingle at the head of the channel, four miles back the way they'd come, behind the island, possibly already seen, possibly already reported. He thought about the yellow Super Cub and how distinctive that dented port float was, and how many people in this country had been paid to remember things.

And then, through the trees, through the sound of the outboards, he heard something else. Something that stopped his breath.

A loudhailer. Cracking and echoing across the water, words shredded by distance and wind—but one word came through whole, repeated twice, and the word was his name.

Not Oyelaran. Not Thackeray, the name on his license, the name stitched into the careful fabric of his fifteen quiet years. His *other* name. The name from the south. The name from the testimony and the burned safehouse and the witnesses who died. The name he had buried so deep he sometimes went days without remembering it belonged to him.

Someone on that water knew it. Someone on that water wanted him to know they knew it.

Beside him, Juniper had gone rigid. "Was that—" she started.

"Yes."

"How do they—"

"I don't know." Which was true, and insufficient, and they both knew it. He closed his eyes for one second, one full second, and let the fear finish arriving, because fighting it early only made it last longer. Then he opened them. "New plan. We don't make the north beach. We go back to the plane, we go out the way we came, and we deal with whatever's waiting at the channel mouth."

"And if the whole fleet is waiting at the channel mouth?"

"Then we deal with that too."

She looked at him then, really looked, the assessment raw and unhidden, the same look she'd given him on the airstrip the day she landed uninvited with half a map and a rifle. Deciding, again, whether he was worth trusting. Deciding, again, with less information than she wanted and more than he'd ever given anyone.

"You know who that woman is," she said. "On the tender. You recognized her. I saw your face."

"I recognized the *type*."

"Bullshit." No heat in it. Just fact. "You know her."

Sebastian looked down the spur, at the water glimpsed silver through the alders, at the wake-lines of the RIBs fading north. He thought about lying to her, and found, somewhat to his surprise, that he didn't want to. Not because honesty had become easy—it hadn't, it might never be—but because lies were ballast, and they were about to need every ounce of lift they had.

"Her name is Dree Halloran," he said. "Fifteen years ago she worked claims security for Corvane's predecessor, out of the southern offices. She was the one they sent when people wouldn't sign. And I know her because—" He exhaled. "Because I'm the reason she's out of a job in the south. Because I testified, once, under a different name, and the company fell, and she rebuilt herself up here, and I ran. And now she's found me, and she's using my real name over open water, which means she doesn't want to arrest me. She wants to *talk*."

Juniper absorbed this the way she absorbed everything—completely, silently, and then all at once. "Talk about what?"

"About what I saw. About what I know. About what it would cost to make me forget it again." He shouldered his pack and turned back down the spur, south, toward the plane, toward the water, toward the voice that knew him. "I've had this conversation before, Juniper. That's the problem. I've had it before, and last time I took the money."

She fell into step beside him. "And this time?"

Ahead of them, through the thinning trees, the sound lay open and gray, and the tender sat at anchor with her floodlights burning, and a RIB was already turning toward the island where a yellow Super Cub sat on the shingle with its dented float gleaming, patient as a promise.

"This time," Sebastian said, "I'm going to go find out what she thinks I'm worth."
