## 10. Tide Two

The channels began an hour after we left the fuel cache, and within twenty minutes I understood that the map my father had drawn was not a map at all. It was a confession.

Sebastian flew low over a braided labyrinth of water—channels splitting and rejoining like veins in a wrist, islands that were nothing but gravel bars wearing crowns of alder, tide flats the color of old pewter stretching out to meet a sea that hadn't decided yet whether it was coming or going. The sun sat low and hard off our left wing, throwing every channel into either blinding glare or absolute shadow. There was no middle light up here. Things were one thing or another.

"Tell me again what it says," he said. He'd asked twice already. He wasn't testing me. He was testing himself.

I held both halves of the map on my knees, Elias's brittle paper and mine, edges aligned where they'd been torn. My half had come to me in a lawyer's envelope four years ago, no note, postmarked from a town I'd never heard of. I'd kept it because it was the only thing he'd ever sent me, not because I understood it. A child's scrawl of coastline. Numbers along the margin. A symbol like a bird's footprint repeated three times.

Now, joined to his half, the footprint symbols resolved into something else: bearings. And the numbers weren't distances. They were depths.

"He didn't chart a route," I said. "He charted a tide table."

Sebastian glanced sideways at me, then back at the water. "Say more."

"The numbers only work if you read them against the tide. Six tides, the registry clerk said. The channels he marked—the shallow ones—he could only have run them at specific stages. Which means whoever follows this has to follow it *in order*. You can't skip ahead. The water won't let you."

"That's not navigation," Sebastian said. "That's a lock."

"That's my father," I said.

He didn't answer that, but his hands moved on the yoke in a way I was starting to be able to read—a small settling, like a man shifting weight onto ground he's decided to trust. We'd been flying together for two days now, and I had learned that Sebastian Oyelaran spoke with his hands before his mouth caught up.

* * *

The first channel was exactly as wide as the map promised, which frightened me more than if it hadn't been. There is a particular horror in watching a dead man's arithmetic prove accurate—every figure correct, every bearing true, the whole thing assembled by someone who knew he might never see it used. Elias had drawn this the way you write a will. Not hoping it gets read soon.

"Gravel bar at two o'clock," Sebastian said. "Map shows it?"

"Shows it. He wrote *green stick* beside it."

"Green stick." Sebastian banked, and there it was—a single alder sapling planted upright in the shingle, impossibly green against all that gray, visible from a mile off once you knew to look. "Your father had a sense of humor."

"No," I said. "My father had a system. The joke would've required him to expect company."

We ran the channel at two hundred feet, then lower, Sebastian's eyes moving between the water and the instruments and the map in my lap with a rhythm so steady it was almost musical. Once, when a crosswind shoved us toward a ridge of standing waves, he corrected without comment, without even a change in breathing, and I realized I had stopped bracing. Somewhere over the last two days my body had decided that when this plane lurched, it would recover. That was either trust or stupidity, and I genuinely could not tell which, and I had decided I didn't want to know.

The second marker was a split rock shaped like a shark's fin. The third was nothing—a bare stretch of water that matched the map only if you counted the depth soundings, which said nine feet over a shelf, and Sebastian read the shallows by eye and confirmed it. Nine feet. Give or take.

By the fourth hour the sun had swung around behind us and the channels had narrowed until the alders brushed our wingtips, and I stopped keeping track of where we were relative to anything except the paper. That was the trap of a good map. It replaced the world. You stopped seeing the country and started seeing only the lines, and somewhere in there you forgot that the lines were drawn by a man who bled out on a dock while a stranger held his hand.

Not bled out. That was the word from the hospital relay, passed through a fish buyer with a radio who charged Sebastian five dollars for the privilege: *stable, guarded, asking about his daughter.* Asking about me. I turned that over in my chest the way you turn a stone over to see what's underneath, and put it back down, because whatever was under it I couldn't deal with at two hundred feet in a maze of channels with Corvane's clock running.

Six tides. This was Tide Two. The concession expired with the sixth. Four left after today, and every one of them the water would be somewhere different, and so would we.

* * *

We put down on a long lake at midafternoon—a lake on the map, which meant Elias had known it, which meant it was safe, or as safe as anything got up here. Sebastian set the plane down so gently I felt the hull kiss the water rather than hit it, and we taxied to the far end where a creek fed in, cold and glass-clear over orange gravel.

"We walk from here," he said, killing the engine. "Map shows the cairn a quarter mile up the creek, on the east bench. He labeled it *first stone.*"

"First stone," I repeated. "So there are others."

"There are always others."

We took the rifle—I carried it; Sebastian had learned early that arguing with me about the rifle cost more energy than it saved—and walked the creek bench through alder and devil's club, the air smelling of rotting salmon and glacier melt. I went first. Sebastian let me, which told me something about him too: he wasn't performing protection. He was watching my back because that was the job he'd assigned himself, and he did it without commentary.

I found the cairn before he did.

It stood on the bench above the creek where the trees opened—a stack of flat stones maybe waist-high, deliberate, each rock fitted to the one beneath it with the patience of a man who had all the time in the world or none of it. Moss grew on the north faces. Lichen had begun its slow yellow work on the south. It had been here years. My father had built this and walked away and let the country start absorbing it, and somehow that hit me harder than the hospital message had. He'd expected to be gone a long time.

Then I saw the boot prints, and everything in me went cold and quiet.

They were pressed into the soft ground at the base of the cairn, two sets, overlapping. Fresh—the edges still sharp, the mud in the tread impressions still dark, not yet dried to the pale gray of the surrounding soil. One set large, one smaller. Both newer than last night's rain, because there was no rain-spatter inside them.

"Juniper." Sebastian's voice, low, behind my left shoulder. He'd seen them too.

I crouched without touching anything. "Two people. Within—" I looked at the sky, did the math on the dampness, the shade on this side of the bench. "Twelve hours. Maybe less."

Sebastian circled the cairn at a distance, reading the ground the way I'd watched him read water. "They came up the creek same as us. Stood here." He pointed without touching. "One of them moved stones. See the scuff on the third tier? They checked inside it."

"Inside it?"

"Cairns like this aren't just markers. Not his style. Look at the capstone—it lifts."

I looked. The top stone sat slightly proud of the stack, a gap beneath it the width of two fingers. My father had built a hollow into the heart of it and disguised the lid as part of the wall.

Sebastian glanced at me. "You want to do it, or me?"

"I'll do it."

The capstone came up heavier than it looked, grinding on grit. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff with age, lay a tin box, and inside the tin—nothing. A clean rectangle of dry dust where something had rested for years, recently removed. And tucked into the corner, folded once, a strip of paper that hadn't been there long enough to stain.

I picked it out with two fingers. Block letters, pencil, pressed hard:

*WE HAVE THE REST. THE WATER WILL TELL YOU WHERE.*

No signature. No threat spelled out. It didn't need to be.

* * *

We stood back-to-back at the edge of the bench for a full minute, both of us scanning the treeline, before either of us spoke. The creek talked the whole time, indifferent. Somewhere across the valley a raven made its dry wooden sound.

"They were here last night or this morning," Sebastian said finally. "Which means they're ahead of us, not behind. They've had the route longer than we have."

"How?" My voice came out flatter than I intended. "Half the map was in a lawyer's office four hundred miles south. The other half was sewn into my jacket. Who else—"

"Elias." He said the name carefully, the way you handle something with a broken edge. "Think about it. He built this cairn years ago. He's been running this route since before you ever got your half of the map. Anyone he guided in here, anyone he traded with, anyone he trusted even once—"

"He didn't trust anyone."

"Everyone trusts someone once." Sebastian's face did something complicated. "Believe me."

I wanted to argue. I wanted to say my father was a fortress, that I'd spent my whole childhood banging on the walls of him and never found a door. But the empty box was a door, and it had been opened from the outside, and arguments don't weigh anything against evidence.

"So they have the rest of the markers," I said. "And we have—" I stopped. I looked at the cairn. At the fitted stones, the moss, the patient engineering of it. "No. Wait. Look at it again."

Sebastian looked.

"This isn't just a marker. It's a *lock*, same as the channels. The tide numbers, the order—you can't skip ahead. If these two went straight for the wreck—if they pushed past the intermediate stones—they're guessing. And my father didn't build guesses. He built a sequence." I pulled both halves of the map from my jacket and held them up. "There are five footprint symbols. Five stones. Each one probably holds a piece—a bearing, a correction, something you need for the next leg. You can't jump to the end any more than you can run the shallow channel at the wrong tide."

"You hope."

"I *know*," I said, and heard my father's stubbornness come out of my own mouth, and didn't apologize for it. "This is how he thought. Everything in order. Everything earned. He wouldn't leave a treasure chest. He'd leave a test."

Sebastian was quiet a moment. Then: "And if the people who emptied this box figured that out too?"

"Then they're backtracking to the stones they skipped, and we'll see their prints at every one, and we'll know exactly how far ahead they are." I set the capstone back in place, fitting it the way Elias had fitted it, leaving no sign we'd opened it. "But right now, they've shown us their hand. Two people. On foot or by boat, not by air—we'd have heard a plane. Moving fast enough to beat the rain. And confident enough to leave a note instead of an ambush."

"That confidence worries me more than an ambush would."

"It should worry you," I agreed. "Come on. Tide's turning. We've got a channel to run and it won't wait."

* * *

Back at the plane, Sebastian didn't start the engine right away. He stood on the float with one hand on the strut, looking north up the lake toward the next line of channels, and I watched him do the thing he did—weighing, always weighing, a man running arithmetic on danger the way other men ran arithmetic on money.

"If they have the route," he said, "they work for Corvane. Or they sold to Corvane. Either way, Halloran knows roughly where we're headed, and her dive barge doesn't need the fine detail. She needs the bay. One bay. She can sit on a bay."

"Then we find the wreck before she stops waiting out the weather."

"And if we can't?"

I climbed into the right seat—which was mine now, unspoken, since the Long Water—and buckled in and laid the maps across my knees. "Then we lose," I said. "But we lose in the right order. That's all he ever asked."

Sebastian looked at me for a long moment, and whatever he saw in my face seemed to settle something in his, because he swung the prop, dropped into his seat, and brought the engine up smooth and strong.

We lifted off the lake into the long gold light, and ahead of us the channels braided away north into the haze, five stones hidden in a hundred miles of water, and somewhere in front of us—close enough now to leave messages, close enough to touch the things my father had built—two sets of boot prints were walking the same confession we were.

Tide Two was ebbing. Four tides left.

The water would tell us where. The question, the one I carried into the maze with the rifle across my knees and the maps on my lap, was whether the water would tell *them* first—or whether it had already.
