## 12. Iles Catches Up

The ranger station at Two Rivers sat where the Kettle River bent against the coast road like a man pausing to reconsider—a squat timber building with a green roof gone gray, a wind sock drooping on its pole, and a diesel generator muttering behind it. Juniper saw it first from the air, a half hour out, and said nothing. She'd learned by then that Sebastian read her silences the way other people read weather reports.

"Fuel stop," he said finally, not looking at her. "We're light on everything."

"We're light on time, too."

"Tide three doesn't start until tomorrow's dawn. We have the night." He banked the Cessna toward the strip, and she watched his hands on the yoke—steady, economical, hands that had done this ten thousand times—and thought about how little she actually knew of the man flying her father's map north. She knew he flew like a confession. She knew he flinched at the name Kestrel. She knew that when she'd asked him, somewhere over the Long Water, why he'd disappeared, he'd given her one piece—one—of a story with too many rooms in it, and then flown the rest of the day without speaking.

The landing was smooth as poured water. That was the thing about Sebastian Oyelaran: everything about him was smooth as poured water, and she had started to suspect that was exactly the problem.

* * *

The station keeper was a woman named Odile who looked at their mud-caked gear, their unshaved faces, and the rifle slung across Juniper's pack with the weary neutrality of someone paid not to notice things. She sold them fuel, coffee, and two cans of stewed tomatoes, and told them the radio phone worked if they fed it quarters.

Juniper was feeding it quarters when the floatplane came in.

She heard it before she saw it—a De Havilland Beaver, twin-bladed, government white with a black stripe, dropping out of a sky the color of wet slate. It taxied to the dock with the unhurried authority of a machine whose expenses were never questioned. The woman who climbed out wore a concession inspector's field coat, gray wool, brass buttons dulled by salt air, and carried a leather document case the way a surgeon carries instruments: close to the body, no wasted motion.

Marguerite Iles. Juniper recognized her from the registry office at Corvane Landing—the same upright carriage, the same eyes that went through you and itemized what they found. What struck her now, watching the inspector cross the gravel toward the station, was how deliberately she walked. Not fast. Not slow. Like a woman who had calculated exactly how much urgency would serve her and permitted herself precisely that much.

Sebastian came out of the washroom drying his hands and stopped dead when he saw her through the window.

Juniper watched him stop. Watched something go out of his face—not fear exactly, though there was some of that—but more like a man watching a door he'd braced shut swing open anyway. Then he set down the towel, squared his shoulders, and pushed outside, and she followed because following was what she did now, apparently, and because she wanted to see this.

"Inspector Iles," Sebastian said.

"Mr. Oyelaran." Iles stopped six feet away, which Juniper understood was a professional distance, chosen. Her gaze moved from him to Juniper and back. "You've been busy. Fuel logs show you topped off at Corvane Landing four days ago, then went dark. No flight plan filed since."

"I filed one for today."

"You filed one for *here*. Where you're going after is blank." She opened the document case. "I told you three days. This is day two."

"And you spent your day two flying to find me."

"I spent my day two doing my job, which happens to intersect with yours." She drew out a folder, held it but didn't offer it. "I'm going to say something once, Mr. Oyelaran, and then we can conduct whatever theater you prefer. I know who you are. Not the name on your license—I've known that for years, it's clean work, whoever built it. I mean the name underneath. The callsign. Kestrel."

The word landed on the gravel between them like something dropped from height.

Juniper watched Sebastian's hands. They didn't move. But the tendons stood up along the backs of them, and she thought: *there it is.* There was the man under the water-smooth surface, and he was terrified, and he was standing in it anyway.

"That name hasn't been mine for fifteen years," he said quietly.

"No. You gave it up. Along with the testimony, the deposition, the whole record—you let the company lawyers bury it in a settlement nobody signed and a file nobody kept." Iles's voice didn't rise. If anything it got softer, which was worse. "Three witnesses died inside eighteen months of that hearing, Mr. Oyelaran. You were supposed to be the fourth. Instead you vanished so thoroughly that even the people hunting you concluded you were dead. Do you know what that makes you, in the eyes of anyone who reads the old files?"

"A ghost."

"A *witness*. The only living one." She extended the folder at last. "And that changes what I can offer you."

* * *

They took it inside, because the rain had started, fine and needling, and because Iles said the rest of it should be said over paper rather than gravel. Odile retreated to the generator shed with the practiced tact of frontier people everywhere, and the three of them sat at a scarred pine table beneath a wall map of the whole concession country, all those blue channels and brown ridges, the Meridian Star's bay a smudge of coastline among hundreds.

Iles laid out the terms the way she probably laid out everything: flatly, completely, once.

"Immunity. Full statutory immunity, retroactive, covering anything you did inside the concession boundary in connection with the Vann claim—including, if it comes to it, the destruction of salvaged materials. In exchange, sworn testimony. Your real identity, your real history, on the record, under oath. What you saw fifteen years ago. What Corvane's predecessor did to the watershed at Blackwater Creek. What happened to the witnesses." She folded her hands. "And whatever you find at the wreck. If you find it."

"And if I don't sign?"

"Then I file my report on schedule. Day three. Your license gets flagged pending review, Corvane's legal department learns that a pilot matching your description is operating in the exclusion zone, and you spend the next year proving you exist while their fleet does whatever it likes in that bay." She paused. "I'm not threatening you. I'm describing a schedule."

Juniper sat very still. Part of her was listening to the words—immunity, testimony, exclusion zone—and part of her was watching Sebastian, because she had learned that the important information in any room was usually in the person saying least.

He was looking at the folder. Not reading it. Looking at it the way a man looks at a well he fell down once.

"The last time I signed my name to something about that company," he said, "two people died before the ink dried. Tomas Reyes. And a hydrologist named Ansel Bright, who found what I found and was brave enough to say it louder." His voice stayed level; that was the eerie part. "You're offering me immunity from prosecution. There isn't a statute that immunizes anybody from what that company does to witnesses."

"There is now," Iles said. "Because enough people like you stayed ghosts long enough that the rot got structural. That's why the inquiry has teeth this time. That's why I'm here instead of a company man. The world changed while you were hiding, Mr. Oyelaran. Slowly. Imperfectly. But it changed." She turned the folder around and slid it across the table. A pen followed it, black, heavy, official. "I need a signature. The name you were born with. Not the license name. The real one."

And Juniper watched him reach for the pen.

It wasn't dramatic. That was what she remembered afterward, what she kept turning over: how small it was. His hand just moved, the way hands do, toward a pen lying on a table, and for two seconds—maybe three—he held it, thumb finding the cap, and his face did something she had never seen it do. It came apart. All that poured-water smoothness, gone, and underneath was a young man in a hearing room fifteen years ago, signing his name because he believed paper could protect people, and learning otherwise in the worst way there is.

His hand started to lower toward the page.

"Don't," Juniper said.

She hadn't planned to speak. The word came out of her like a round coming out of a chamber—she felt the recoil of it in her own chest. Both of them looked at her. Iles with cool interest. Sebastian with something rawer than that.

"It's not your signature," Iles said mildly. "This is his decision."

"I know whose decision it is." Juniper kept her eyes on Sebastian. "I'm asking him to make it out loud instead of halfway."

"Juniper." His voice was careful. Careful the way ice is careful. "You don't know what that name costs."

"No," she agreed. "I don't. Because you've never told me. You gave me one piece of it over open water and then went quiet for six hours, and I let you, because I figured a man's past is his own business." She leaned forward, put her forearms on the table, felt the pine's grain press cold lines into her skin. "But here's what I do know. My father took a gut wound getting that evidence out of the ground, or getting to it, or getting away from the people who want it—and he's dying in a hospital bed right now partly because he spent thirty years believing names on paper wouldn't bring back a river. And you're sitting here holding a pen over the only thing that might bring the whole rotten structure down, and you're shaking, and you want me to believe that's principle. That it's *safety*. That staying invisible is some kind of discipline."

"It kept me alive."

"It kept you *absent*," she said. "There's a difference, and I think you know it, and I think that's why your hand is on that pen right now. Some part of you wants to sign. Some part of you has wanted to sign for fifteen years and couldn't find a door. And now there's a door, and you're scared, so you're going to tell yourself the fear is wisdom."

The rain ticked against the window. Somewhere behind the station the generator changed pitch. Iles sat motionless, watching them both with the expression of a woman who had seen negotiations come apart before and knew better than to touch the machinery while it was moving.

Sebastian set the pen down.

Not slammed. Placed. He placed it on the table parallel to the folder, precise as a man racking a tool, and when he spoke his voice had gone quiet in a way that made the hair stand up on Juniper's arms.

"You want to know what I saw? At Blackwater Creek?"

"Yes," she said.

"I saw a river running gray. Not silty—*gray*, chemical gray, for eleven miles, and every living thing in it belly-up, and men in company coveralls raking the banks at dawn before the survey flight came over, raking fish into pits and covering them with lime like they were burying a battlefield." His eyes had gone somewhere else entirely. "I testified to that. I signed my name. And eight months later Tomas Reyes's skiff washed up empty under the trestle bridge, and Ansel Bright's house burned with him in it, and the fire marshal's report said electrical, and everyone who wasn't dead agreed it was electrical, because agreeing was cheaper." He looked at her then, and his eyes were dry and terrible. "That's what my name bought, Juniper. That's the exchange rate. So don't stand there and tell me my fear is cowardice. My fear is *arithmetic*."

"Then be afraid and sign anyway," she said. "That's what courage is. Nobody told you?"

For a moment she thought he'd hit the table, or walk out into the rain, or both. His jaw worked. His hands flattened on the wood.

"You weren't there," he said, low. "You don't get to spend my blood."

"No," she shot back. "But I get to spend Elias's. He bled for what's in those crates—or whatever's in them—and if you sign, maybe it means something. Maybe it means the thing he bled for outlives him. If you don't sign, then all we're doing is a treasure run, and when it's over we'll be exactly what Halloran thinks we are: two scavengers who can be bought." She stood up, because sitting felt wrong suddenly, felt like surrender. "Sign it or don't. But decide which man you are before the tide turns, because I'm diving with one of them, and I need to know which."

The silence after that had weight, physical weight, like deep water.

Iles broke it, gently, which somehow made it worse. "The offer stands until I file. Day three, dawn, at the Corvane Landing office." She gathered the folder but left the pen. "You don't have to answer now. But understand me, Mr. Oyelaran—Ms. Vann—whatever you carry out of that bay, it will end up in front of a tribunal eventually. The only question is whether it arrives attached to names or floating loose, deniable, worthless. Evidence without a witness is just salvage." She buttoned her coat. "Think about which one you are."

At the door she paused, half-turned. "For what it's worth—and I know it isn't worth much—Ansel Bright had a daughter. She testified two years ago. She's alive. The world changed slowly, imperfectly, but it changed." And then she was out into the rain, and the Beaver's engine coughed, caught, and hauled her back up into the slate sky, and the sound faded south, and the station was just the two of them and a pen on a table and the generator muttering its one note.

* * *

They didn't speak for an hour.

Sebastian went out and preflighted the plane in the rain, thoroughly, unnecessarily, checking surfaces that needed no checking. Juniper cleaned her rifle by the stove, though it was clean. Odile came back in, brewed coffee, sensed the weather in the room, and left again. Outside, the light went from slate to iron; the tide was turning somewhere out in the dark channels, and tomorrow was tide four, and the shoal that hid the Meridian Star would only bare itself for an hour, maybe less, and Halloran's dive barge was two coves over with its generators running.

Finally Sebastian came in, dripping, smelling of avgas and rain, and sat down across from her. He didn't look at the pen. He looked at his own hands, turned palm-up on the table, as if checking them for damage.

"My name," he said, "is Sebastian Adeyemi Oyelaran. Born Lagos. Raised on the delta flights. I haven't said the middle name aloud since I was nineteen." He said it now steadily, like a man setting down something heavy on a marked spot. "There. That part's free. The rest—" He exhaled. "The rest I'll give Iles. After. Not before. If I sign before we dive, and Corvane learns of it, they won't send lawyers to that bay, Juniper. They'll send the tender with the winch and the work lights, and they'll take those crates out of the water and shred them in a furnace before any tribunal convenes. Order matters. Truth first, then the name. Not the other way around."

Juniper studied him for a long moment. The anger hadn't left her—it had just settled, the way silt settles, into something denser at the bottom.

"After," she said. "You swear it. Not to me. To the table. To the pen."

He picked the pen up, weighed it, and set it in his breast pocket, where it made a small hard line against the canvas.

"To the pen," he said.

She almost laughed, and didn't, and the not-laughing felt like the truest thing between them yet. She got the maps out instead, spread them over the scarred pine, weighted the corners with coffee mugs, and put her finger on the channel maze where the combined halves of her father's map converged on a blank stretch of coastline labeled only, in Elias's cramped hand, *the sleeping place.*

"Tide four," she said. "Dawn plus one hour. The shoal shows for sixty minutes if the swell's under two meters."

"And if it's over?"

"Then we dive anyway." She met his eyes. "Because Halloran's barge is waiting out the same weather, and when it lifts, she dives too. We go when it's ugly or we don't go at all."

Sebastian looked at the map, at the blank coast, at the small hard line of the pen in his pocket. Outside, the wind sock lifted, tested the air, and swung north.

"Ugly it is," he said.
