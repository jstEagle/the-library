## 13. Tide Four

The tide chart said low water at 11:40, and the tide did not care that we were late.

We came around the last headland with the engine throttled back to a murmur, the Cessna's floats kissing a sea gone flat and pewter-colored under a sky the color of wet slate. Juniper sat in the right seat with Elias's half of the map flattened against her knee, one finger tracing the coastline as it unrolled beneath us.

"There." She didn't point. She'd learned not to—pointing made you lean, leaning made the plane drift, and drifting over open water in sight of nothing was how pilots died. She just said it, quiet and certain: "The shoal."

I saw it before I believed it. A long, pale seam running parallel to shore, maybe two hundred meters out—a bar of gravel and broken shell lying just under the surface, visible only because the tide had dropped four meters and kept dropping. From the air, at any other hour, it would have been invisible. Just another stretch of cold green water. That was the genius of it. That was Elias Vann all over: hide the thing where nobody would think to look twice, then make looking possible only for twenty minutes a day, twice a day, to people who already knew.

"Circle once," I said. "Slow."

I banked us out over the water, wide and lazy, my eyes doing what they had learned to do fifteen years ago and never unlearned: reading the coast for other eyes. A cove to the north. A river mouth silted brown. Kelp beds drawing dark calligraphy on the shallows. Nothing else. No masts, no smoke, no wakes.

That should have comforted me. It didn't. Comfort is a thing the wilderness offers only right before it takes something.

* * *

We put down in the cove south of the shoal, a narrow slot of deep water between two granite shoulders, sheltered enough that the floats barely slapped. The moment the engine died, the silence came down like a lid. No wind. No birds. Just the tick of cooling metal and, somewhere far off, the long exhale of the sea working the outer rocks.

Juniper was out on the float before I'd finished tying off, rifle slung, binoculars up.

"Shoal's dry-topping in places," she said. "You can see the break in the swell where it runs. He marked it with something—look, at the east end. Dark line in the gravel."

"A mooring chain," I said. "Or what's left of one."

She lowered the glasses and looked at me, and I saw her doing the arithmetic she'd been doing since the fuel cache, since the journal, since the night dive that hadn't happened yet but was coming whether either of us admitted it or not. The wreck wasn't a rumor anymore. It was a place. Places could be reached. And anything that could be reached could be taken.

"Low water window's maybe forty minutes," she said. "Then it turns and covers everything again."

"Then we don't waste it standing here."

"Sebastian." She said my name the way she always did when she wanted me to stop moving—the false ease of it, the borrowed name sitting between us like a passenger neither of us had invited. "If someone else found the route. The boot prints at the cairn. If Halloran's people are ahead of us—"

"Then we'll see their boat," I said. "Water doesn't hide much."

But even as I said it, I was listening past her voice, out through the granite walls of the cove, toward the north. Toward the other cove. The one the chart showed as a good anchorage, deep and round, with a creek at its head.

Two coves over. Close enough that on a still day—

There it was. Faint. So faint I might have invented it. A low, rhythmic throb underneath the silence, more felt than heard, the way you feel a truck on a road miles away through the soles of your boots.

A generator.

Juniper saw my face change. "What?"

"Listen."

She went still. The silence stretched. Ten seconds. Twenty. Then her jaw tightened, and I knew she'd caught it too—that patient diesel heartbeat, steady as a metronome, coming from somewhere beyond the northern shoulder of rock.

"Somebody's anchored in the next cove," she whispered.

"Dive barge, if I had to guess. Big enough to need power for lights, compressors, a winch." I looked at the sky, at the flat gray ceiling pressing down. "Weather's coming in behind us. They're not diving in this. They're waiting it out."

"Waiting for what?"

"For the same tide we are."

* * *

We spent the next hour doing nothing, which is the hardest work there is.

The plan, such as it was, required patience we didn't have and couldn't manufacture. We couldn't approach the shoal by water without passing within a few hundred meters of the barge's anchorage—we'd be silhouetted against the sky line of the channel, engine noise carrying across flat water like a shout in a church. We couldn't fly over it; the spotter plane that had harried us since the registry office was somewhere under this same weather front, and Corvane's fleet had radios and we had a radio that worked about half the time. We couldn't wait them out, because the concession clock didn't wait for anybody. Two tides left after this one. Maybe three, if you counted generously, and I had stopped counting generously somewhere over the Long Water.

So we sat in the plane, in the cove, and listened to a generator breathe.

Juniper cleaned her rifle. Not because it needed it—because her hands needed it. I watched her strip it with the economy of someone who'd been taught young and never allowed to forget, each piece laid on a cloth in order, wiped, oiled, reassembled blind. Her father's teaching, probably. Or somebody's. There were a lot of things about Juniper Vann I was still learning, and most of them arrived sideways, in the space between actions.

"You keep looking north," she said, without looking up.

"I keep thinking."

"About what?"

"About Dree Halloran." I turned the word over. "You meet a lot of fixers in this business. Most of them are loud. They want you to know who they are, because half their job is theater. Halloran isn't loud. When she talked to us at the dock, she never once raised her voice, never once threatened us directly. She just described the world as it would be after we made the wrong choice, and let us live in it for a minute."

"And now she's two coves away."

"Now she's two coves away, waiting out weather she can read better than we can, on a barge with a crane and a dive crew and a permit that expires when ours does." I rubbed my eyes. "Elias said the fleet filed wrong coordinates. Miles wrong. But wrong coordinates don't matter if they've got someone walking the shoreline with the other kind of map—the kind you make with your feet."

"The boot prints."

"The boot prints."

She set the rifle down and looked at me straight on. In the gray light coming through the windshield her face was all planes and shadows, younger than she pretended and older than she deserved. "Say what you're actually thinking, Sebastian. You've been chewing it since the cairn."

I had. And I'd been swallowing it, because saying it out loud would make it load-bearing, and things that are load-bearing break under weight.

"All right." I took a breath. "I think Elias didn't sink those crates alone. I think he had help—someone who knew the channels, knew the shoal, knew how to make a wreck disappear. And I think whoever helped him kept a copy of the route, the way he kept his half and gave us his half. People who do dangerous things together keep insurance on each other. I've done it. You've done it."

"You think his old partner sold him out. Sold the route to Corvane."

"I think a gut wound is a strange thing to get from a fall," I said quietly. "And I think the person who put it there knew exactly where to find him, and exactly why he mattered."

Juniper was silent for a long time. Out on the water, the tide kept falling, patient as arithmetic. The pale seam of the shoal grew longer, higher, more naked, as if the sea were slowly opening its hand.

"If that's true," she said finally, "then whatever's under that shoal has been Corvane's for days. They've had divers down. They may have the crates already."

"No."

"How do you know?"

"Because the barge is still here." I nodded north, toward the sound of the generator. "If they'd raised what they came for, they'd be running for the registry office with the tide, not hiding from weather in a cove. Whatever's down there, they haven't got it yet. Either the diving's bad, or the wreck's deeper than they thought, or—" I hesitated.

"Or Elias made sure it couldn't be raised easy," she finished.

"Elias buried it once," I said. "He was good at it."

* * *

By mid-afternoon the light had gone the color of old tin, and the tide stood at its bottom, and we ran out of reasons to wait.

We went in the inflatable—the little rubber dinghy I kept lashed inside the float compartment, the kind of thing that looks like junk until it's the only thing between you and the water. Oars, not the motor. The generator's rhythm covered a lot, but an outboard carries, and I wasn't going to announce ourselves to a cove full of professionals because I was impatient.

We paddled.

It took the better part of an hour to work the long way around, hugging the southern shore, slipping through a gap in the kelp where the water went suddenly black and cold, then out into the open reach where the shoal lay exposed like the spine of some drowned animal. I kept us low in the boat, Juniper forward with the rifle wrapped in oilcloth across her knees, both of us paddling in a rhythm we'd never practiced and somehow matched anyway.

From the water, the shoal was a country of its own: ridges of shell gravel and rounded stone, tide pools holding their own private skies, ribbons of bladderwrack stinking of sulfur and iodine. And at the eastern end, exactly where the two halves of the map agreed, a length of rusted chain ran down from the gravel into a slot between two boulders—a mooring, ancient and deliberate, the kind of thing you only put in place if you planned to come back.

Or if you planned to make sure no one else could leave.

Juniper found the marker first. She'd gone quiet the way she went quiet when something mattered, and then she touched my arm and pointed—not at the chain, but at the gravel beside it.

Boot prints. Several sets, hours old at least, pressed into the drying mud at the edge of a tide pool. Heavy tread. Work boots. And beside them, half-filled with seep water, the clean rectangular scar of something that had been dug up and carried away.

My chest went tight. "They've been here."

"No." Juniper was shaking her head slowly, her eyes moving over the prints, reading them the way I read weather. "Look at the edges. Slumped. Rained on. These are days old—before the storm system, before the barge. This is the route-walker. The one from the cairn." She pointed further along the shoal, to a second cluster of marks near the slot where the chain vanished. "These are fresher. Different sole. Narrower. Dive boots. They came at the last low tide, in the rain, and they tried the slot."

"Tried?"

She handed me the binoculars and pointed at the water pooled in the depression around the chain anchor. At first I saw nothing. Then I saw it: a scatter of debris on the bottom, pale in the dark water. Rope ends. Cut cleanly. And a steel plate, bolted across the mouth of the slot, furred with fifteen years of growth except where fresh tool marks gleamed silver.

"They got to the plate," I said. "Couldn't get through it."

"Not in one tide." Juniper's voice was flat, but I could hear the current running under it. "Not with hand tools, in the dark, in the rain, with the water coming back up. They cut the lifting points and they quit. And now they're sitting two coves over with a crane and a cutting rig, waiting for the weather to lift so they can come back and take their time."

The generator throbbed faintly across the water, patient as a pulse.

I lay back in the rocking boat and stared at the underside of the sky and did the math I'd been avoiding. One tide window today, nearly spent. Weather closing from the west—twelve hours of blow, maybe eighteen, enough to pin a barge and ground a spotter plane and keep everyone honest. Then Tide Five, tomorrow evening. Then Tide Six, and the concession dead, and every claim on this water turning to paper ash.

Which meant Corvane had exactly two windows left. And so did we. And they had a crane, a crew, a cutting rig, and a barge, and we had a rubber boat, a rifle, a dying radio, and a plane shot full of holes sitting in a cove that they would find the moment they flew a search pattern.

"We can't beat them to it," Juniper said. It wasn't despair. It was inventory.

"No."

"So we don't try to beat them." She was looking at the slot, at the bolted plate, at the chain running down into the dark. "We let them open it."

I turned to look at her. Rain had started—not real rain yet, just the fine cold mist that comes ahead of the front, beading on her hair, on the oilcloth over the rifle. Her face was absolutely calm, and that calm frightened me more than the generator did.

"Juniper—"

"Elias didn't want the crates raised. He wanted them sunk forever. Every day we spend trying to get there first is a day we spend fighting for something he died trying to bury." She said it evenly, like a course she'd plotted. "Let Halloran's divers crack the plate. Let them bring up what's in there. And the moment those crates break the surface—in Corvane's custody, on Corvane's barge, with Corvane's name stamped on the operation—" she finally looked at me, and her eyes were bright and hard and terrible, "—that's not evidence anymore, Sebastian. That's a confession. Caught red-handed, on the record, with witnesses. Iles gets everything she needs without your signature. And we don't have to touch the thing Elias swore we shouldn't touch."

For a long moment I didn't answer. Because part of me—the part that had survived fifteen years by letting other people's plans fail first—wanted to kiss her for it. And another part, older and less honest, remembered what it was to stand in a room while men decided that a confession was cheaper than a witness, and knew exactly what happened to the people standing next to the crates when the shooting started.

"It works," I said slowly, "right up until Halloran realizes we're watching. She knows our faces. She knows the plane. If she spots us anywhere near that barge—"

"Then we watch from far away."

"Nothing is far away in these channels. You know that. Sound carries. Radar doesn't care about rain as much as you'd hope. And she's got a spotter plane we haven't seen since yesterday, which means it's either grounded—" I stopped.

Juniper had gone very still.

"What?" I said.

"Listen," she whispered.

I listened. The generator was still there, steady, patient. But underneath it now, threading through the diesel throb like wire through cloth, came a new sound. Higher. Thinner. Rising.

An engine. An aircraft engine, turning over, coughing, catching, settling into a climb.

Out of the northern cove, through the rag of the weather, a small shape lifted off the water and hauled itself into the gray—wheels on floats, high wing, the unmistakable silhouette of a spotter plane climbing into the murk. Heading west. Heading into the front, where no sane pilot would go.

Heading, unless I badly missed my guess, toward a certain cove where a shot-up Cessna sat tied to the rocks.

Juniper met my eyes, and neither of us said it, because saying it would start the clock, and the clock was already running.

The tide had begun to turn.
