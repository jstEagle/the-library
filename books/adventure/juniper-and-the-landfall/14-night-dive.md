## 14. Night Dive

The tide turned at 11:40, and Juniper knew it before she saw the water move. She felt it in her teeth, in the way the cove seemed to hold its breath—the whole bay exhaling one last time before it began to fill.

"You're not serious," Sebastian said.

She was kneeling at the edge of the gravel bar with the dive bag open between them, checking the seals on the waterproof cases for the third time. The plane sat behind them on the shingle, patched and ugly, its port wing still wearing the scorch from the chase two days ago. Above the tree line the sky was doing something theatrical—green and violet curtains of light rippling over black water, the kind of aurora that tourists paid thousands to see and that up here just meant *cold front coming*.

"I'm completely serious."

"It's a night dive in a tidal narrow on a wreck nobody's charted in thirty years." He said it flatly, like he was reading cargo weights off a manifest. "In water that's maybe four degrees. With a dive barge anchored two coves over, running generators we can hear from here."

"That's why tonight," she said. "Halloran's crew is weathered in. They won't put divers down until this front passes—they'd be idiots to try. We'd be idiots not to."

"We are idiots," Sebastian said. But he was already pulling his drysuit out of the kit.

* *

The tide was the whole argument, and she'd made it to him twice already, once with the tide tables spread on the wing of the plane and once again at midnight when he pretended to be asleep and she knew he wasn't. The shoal that hid the Meridian Star only broke surface—and only exposed the wreck's upper structure—at the bottom of a spring low. Tomorrow's low would be higher; the day after, higher still. By tide six the site would be drowned past reach, and Corvane's permit would expire with it, and everything Elias had bled for would sit under twelve feet of gray water forever, which might have been fine if they were going to sink it anyway—but they couldn't decide what to do with it until they knew what *it* was.

That was the part she kept circling back to. They were diving blind. Her father's journal told them where the crates were stowed—forward hold, beneath the collapsed deck plating, sealed against saltwater with wax and tar because Elias Vann had been the kind of man who did things properly even when the things he did were terrible—but the journal didn't say what was in them. Records, cores, *proof*, it said, in her father's cramped hand. Proof of what, exactly, it never quite came out and said. She'd read those pages four times now, by headlamp, by cookfire, once standing hip-deep in the shallows because she couldn't stand to sit still. Every reading left her colder.

Her father had helped sink this ship. That fact sat in her chest like a swallowed stone. Not wrecked—sunk. Deliberately, with intent, with the engines flooded and the seacocks opened and a man named Aldous Wren—no relation, the coincidence had made her laugh once, bitterly—signing the insurance claim while her father rowed away in a skiff with the truth in oilcloth bags. He'd been twenty-six years old. He'd carried it for thirty years without telling anyone, least of all her, and then he'd gotten himself gut-shot in a derelict camp three hundred miles from anywhere rather than tell the rest.

She zipped the dive bag shut. "Ready?"

Sebastian looked at the water for a long moment. In the aurora light his face was all planes and shadow, older than fifteen years should have made him. "My instructor used to say there are old divers and bold divers," he said, "but no old bold divers."

"Your instructor never met my family."

"No," he said quietly. "He met worse."

* *

They launched the inflatable from the lee side of the bar and ran it out into the channel with the engine at half throttle, because full throttle made noise and noise traveled over cold water like a shout down a stairwell. Two coves east, Halloran's dive barge sat at anchor with its work lights doused, only the green glow of its generator exhaust stack visible through the trees—a single ember floating in the dark, patient as a cat's eye.

Juniper steered by the bearing Sebastian gave her, dead reckoning across water that had no features, counting seconds in her head the way he'd taught her on the flight north: *you always know where you are if you know where you started, how fast you went, and how long it's been.* It was strange, being taught things by him. Stranger still how much of it stuck.

The shoal showed itself as a long seam of white water where the ebb met the flood, the sea confused at the turning, and beyond it the black hump of the reef that marked the wreck. They shut down the engine fifty meters out and paddled, and the silence that came down over them was so complete Juniper could hear her own pulse in her ears and, faintly, absurdly, the aurora itself—a crackle like static on a dead channel, though whether it was real or imagined she couldn't have sworn to.

"Anchor here," Sebastian murmured. "We drop on the seaward slope, follow it down to twenty meters, run the line east until we hit the hull. Current will be slack for maybe forty minutes. After that it turns and starts pushing us back into the narrows, and I am not swimming us out of a riptide by feel in the dark."

"Forty minutes," she said. "I've waited thirty years. Forty minutes is nothing."

He didn't answer that. He checked her gear instead—her regulator, her gauges, the knife strapped inside her calf the way their father had taught her when she was twelve, back when the lessons were about fish and not about anything else. His hands were steady. She noticed that she trusted them, and the noticing frightened her more than the dive did.

"Juniper." He said her name the way he almost never did—plainly, without irony or deflection. "Whatever's down there. Whatever your father buried. Once we see it, we can't unsee it. You understand that? There's a version of you that surfaces from that hold and gets to keep being the person who didn't know. This dive kills her."

Juniper looked down through the black water, where the beam of her dive light died after a meter and a half like a match dropped in a well.

"She was already dying," she said. "The day I read his journal."

* *

The cold hit her like a door slamming.

Even through the drysuit it found her—first the face, naked in the hood's gap, then the slow seep through wrist and neck seals, the water pressing in around her body with a weight that felt less like physics than like intention. She equalized her ears and dropped through layers of dark: the green-black of the surface zone, then nothing, then the grey-brown bloom of her own light reflecting off silt, then nothing again. Below her the seabed sloped away into a darkness that her lamp could not argue with.

Sebastian was a pale shape beside her, tethered to her by two meters of line, moving with an economy she recognized from watching him fly. No wasted motion. A man who had spent fifteen years practicing invisibility moved underwater exactly the way he moved through the world.

The slope steepened. Kelp gave way to bare rock furred with white anemones that glowed faintly in her beam like frost on a window. A lingcod hung motionless in the water column, regarding them with prehistoric indifference. At twenty meters her depth gauge confirmed what her ears already knew, and she leveled off and swam east along the contour, counting kicks.

Thirty kicks. Forty.

On the fifty-first, the wall rose out of the dark in front of her.

It shouldn't have affected her the way it did. She'd known it was there; she'd read the dimensions off her father's sketches. But knowing a ship is ninety feet long and seeing its hull rise above you like a cliff face, furred in plumose anemones and dripping with dead men's rope, are different things entirely. The Meridian Star lay heeled over on her port side, her starboard rail buried in sand, her superstructure collapsed into a tangle that might once have been a wheelhouse. Thirty years of the sea had dressed her in softness—anemone and sponge and drifting curtains of hydroid—but underneath the dressing she was still iron, still a wound in the seabed, still *wrong* in the way that sunken things are wrong, the way a tooth knocked out of a jaw is wrong.

Juniper hung in the water ten feet from the hull and felt her father's hands on the ship's rails, thirty years gone, and grieved for him in a place she hadn't known grief could reach.

Sebastian touched her arm. Pointed forward, to where the hull was split—not torn, *split*, a clean seam where the plates had been opened deliberately, the way her father's journal described: cut with a torch from the inside, then let the sea do the rest. The entry to the forward hold.

They went in.

* *

Inside, the ship was a cathedral.

That was the word that came to her and would not leave: cathedral. Their lights swept across ribbed steel arching overhead into darkness, deck plates fallen into slopes of sediment, a rain of particles hanging in the beams like dust in the shafts of some drowned church. A boiler wrench lay on the silt where a hand had set it down and never picked it up. A boot, leather gone to lace and lace gone to lace. Everything was the same brown-grey, the color of patience, the color of time doing its slow arithmetic.

The hold was aft of the split, behind a collapsed section of plating that Sebastian cleared with careful, incremental pulls, each piece set aside rather than shoved, because he understood—as she was learning to understand—that down here, carelessness killed you twice: once with a silt-out that erased the world, and once with whatever came next.

The gap, when it opened, was barely wide enough for a diver with gear. Sebastian went first. Juniper followed, and the ship closed around her, and for three full kicks she was in a space so narrow her tanks scraped steel on both sides, in the dark, inside a drowned ship, and every animal instinct she owned screamed at once.

Then the hold opened up, and her light found the crates.

There were nine of them.

They were smaller than she'd imagined—she'd been picturing treasure chests, something out of the stories her father used to tell badly, on purpose, to make her groan. These were survey crates, steel-banded, stenciled with the Meridian Star's registry number and the words GEOTECHNICAL SAMPLES — DO NOT OPEN IN FIELD. They were stacked in a deliberate arrangement against the inner hull, tarred, wrapped, lashed down with wire that had rusted to lace but still held, because her father had done it properly. Even at twenty-six, bleeding from a cut on his forehead according to the journal, rowing away from a murder he'd agreed to help cover up—he had done it *properly*.

Juniper's hands were shaking inside her gloves. She got the first crate free of its lashings and hauled it toward the gap, and it was heavier than it looked, dense, and she thought: *cores.* Sample cores, drilled from the watershed, sealed in liners. And documents. Her father's journal had said documents too—shipping manifests, lab results, correspondence. She'd assumed she understood what that meant.

Then her light caught the stencil on the second crate, the one beneath the registry number, the small line of text she'd have missed if the tar hadn't flaked away exactly there:

**KETTLE RIVER WATERSHED — CHAIN OF CUSTODY — CORVANE MINERALS LTD.**

And below that, in smaller letters, half obscured: **INCIDENT REFERENCE: ALDER CREEK.**

Alder Creek.

She knew the name. Everyone in the interior territories knew the name, the way you know the name of a grave. Eleven people dead in one winter, thirty years ago—three families, one whole homestead line, officially written off as bad water and bad luck. Officially. Her mother had grown up two valleys over. Her mother had stopped drinking well water for the rest of her life and had never once said why, and had died with the not-saying still in her mouth.

Juniper hung in the black water of the hold, breathing hard through her regulator, and understood.

It wasn't treasure. It had never been treasure. The claim-stones, the unregistered strike, the fortune everyone whispered about—it was misdirection, or it was leverage, or it was both, but underneath it was this: nine crates of proof that Corvane—or whatever Corvane had been called when it wore its earlier name—had poisoned a watershed, known it, shipped the evidence out on a survey vessel, and then sunk the vessel and its own paper trail together when the inquiry got close. Her father hadn't sunk a treasure ship. He'd sunk the truth, on orders, at twenty-six, because the alternative had been joining the eleven dead at Alder Creek.

And the truth had sat down here in the dark ever since, heavy as ballast, holding the ship's secrets the way the keel held the ship.

"Juniper." Sebastian's voice came through the comm unit, thin and distorted, stripped of everything except urgency. "Silt's moving. Tide's starting to turn early—we've got maybe fifteen minutes. How many can you take?"

She looked at the crates. Nine of them. Enough to bury a company. Too much to carry.

"All of them," she heard herself say.

"We can't lift nine crates on one dinghy."

"Then we take the ones that matter and we come back for the rest before tide six." Her voice sounded strange in her own ears, level and far away. "Sebastian. It's Alder Creek. It was always Alder Creek. My father didn't hide gold. He hid a massacre."

Silence on the channel. Then, very quietly: "I know. I read the manifest stencil on the way in." A pause. "Juniper—I testified about Corvane once. What I saw was small compared to this. Small enough that they killed four witnesses to bury it. This—" She heard him breathe, the regulator hissing. "This isn't evidence. This is a bomb with a thirty-year fuse, and we're standing on it."

"Then help me carry it."

* *

They took four crates.

It took eleven minutes that felt like eleven hours, passing them one at a time through the gap in the plating, Sebastian hauling, Juniper pushing, the two of them working in a rhythm that needed no words—which was good, because neither of them had any. The silt was rising around them in slow brown ghosts, stirred by their fins, erasing the hold behind them as they worked, so that by the end the cathedral was dissolving into cloud and her own light was bouncing back at her from a world with no walls.

On the last pass, wedging the fourth crate through the gap, Juniper looked back—one second, no more—into the collapsing brown murk where five crates still sat lashed in the dark, waiting, patient as bones.

*Come back for you,* she thought. *Before the tide takes the chance away.*

Then the current took her, and the ship let her go.

* *

Surfacing was like being born badly—cold and noise and the sudden enormous weight of air. The aurora had thickened overhead, green fire rippling bank to bank, and the cove was no longer slack: the flood was running, dragging white seams of current across the shoal, and the dinghy rode at its anchor line like a nervous animal. They got the crates aboard one at a time, muscles screaming, teeth chattering too hard to speak, and it wasn't until they were clear of the shoal and running for the gravel bar with the engine at full throttle—noise be damned, stealth be damned, the tide pushing at the hull like a hand—that Juniper finally got words out through her chattering jaw.

"The fifth crate," she said. "The top one. Did you see the stencil?"

Sebastian shook his head, eyes on the black water ahead.

"It said *chain of custody*," she said. "You know what that means? It means somebody signed for these. It means names. Dates. Signatures. It means somewhere in that tar and steel there's a paper trail with a company officer's name on it, and it isn't just proof of what they did—it's proof of who *knew*."

Sebastian was quiet for a long moment. The barge's green ember slid past behind them, two coves of black water away, and she saw him watching it, and saw something in his face she'd seen only once before—in the cockpit, over the Long Water, when he'd told her a piece of why he disappeared.

"They'll burn the river again before they let that surface," he said. "You understand that now. Not metaphorically. Halloran's people will burn whatever they have to."

"Then we'd better be faster than fire."

Ahead, the gravel bar rose out of the aurora light, and on it, small and impossible and absolutely real, a light was blinking.

Three flashes. Pause. Three flashes.

Someone was waiting at their camp.
