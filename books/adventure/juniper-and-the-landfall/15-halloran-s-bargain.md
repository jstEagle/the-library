## 15. Halloran's Bargain

Sebastian killed the engine a quarter mile out and let the plane drift down onto the water on idle, the way you do when you don't want anyone to know you're coming—and then he realized the silence was pointless, because whoever was blinking that light had already heard them land twice, take off once, and circle the bay like a gull with a question. He let the plane settle against the gravel bar with a grinding kiss of hull on stone and sat for a moment with his hands still on the yoke, listening to the tick of cooling metal.

Juniper had the rifle out of its case before the wheels stopped turning.

"Could be Iles," she said.

"Iles doesn't blink lights in threes." He unclipped his harness. "Iles knocks."

"Then who—"

"You know who."

He did not say the name. Saying it would make the night real before he had to live in it.

They came up the bar low and quiet, Juniper ten feet behind him and to the left, the way she'd learned to move somewhere he didn't ask about, the rifle held like an extension of her arm rather than a thing she was carrying. The camp was as they'd left it: tent staked against the wind off the water, stove cold, the crates of records and sample cores stacked under a tarp weighted with stones, because Elias's journal had taught them both that anything left loose in this country would be taken by weather or by men, and only one of those gave it back.

The light was a lantern hung from the ridgepole of their own tent. Underneath it, sitting on their own fuel drum, drinking from their own thermos, was a woman in a gray fleet jacket with her hair tied back and her boots crossed at the ankle like she'd been waiting for a train instead of squatting in a hostile cove at four in the morning.

Dree Halloran looked up at them without hurrying.

"Your coffee's terrible," she said. "No offense. Fleet issue?"

"Put the rifle down, Ms. Vann," she added, pleasantly. "If I wanted trouble, there are eleven men on that barge two coves over who'd have come with me, and you'd have known about it before your floatplane finished taxiing. I came alone because this is a conversation, not an operation. There's a difference. I'd appreciate it if we could keep it that way."

Juniper did not put the rifle down. She lowered it six inches and kept her finger indexed along the guard, which was more courtesy than Sebastian expected and less than Halloran wanted, and somehow that arithmetic suited all three of them fine.

Sebastian stood at the edge of the lantern light, deliberately outside it, the way he'd stood outside every pool of light for fifteen years. It wasn't strategy anymore. He understood that now, standing there. It was just where he lived.

"You're a long way from your barge," he said.

"I'm exactly where my barge isn't, which is the point." Halloran set down the thermos. "Weather window closes tonight. Tide five tomorrow. After that, Corvane's permit is a piece of paper worth framing and nothing else, and everyone on that fleet knows it, and half of them are furious about it, and one of them—the one whose name you don't want to know—is already talking about what happens to contractors who come back empty. So. Here I am."

"Here you are," Sebastian said. "At our camp. Drinking our coffee."

"Telling you first." She tilted her head. "That's the part you should be paying attention to, Mr.—"

She stopped. Smiled. And said the name.

"—Oyelaran."

The word went through him like a fishhook through the hand: small entry, enormous drag. Fifteen years since anyone had said it aloud to his face. He'd heard it secondhand twice—a newspaper clipping mailed to him by a lawyer who shouldn't have had it, a whisper through a dispatcher in Fort Reliance that turned out to be nothing—and both times he'd spent weeks afterward checking mirrors in parked cars and sleeping with his boots on. But spoken, aimed, delivered by a woman holding his own thermos—it landed differently. It landed like weather coming down.

Juniper's eyes flicked to him. She'd heard the name too. She didn't ask. Not yet. That restraint was the most frightening thing in the whole cove.

"That's not my name," Sebastian said.

"No," Halloran agreed. "It's the name on a deposition from nineteen years ago. Kestrel Six. The pilot who flew the survey team up the Meridian watershed, saw what was in the tailings ponds, and put it under oath when the company's lawyers offered him a number with enough zeroes to buy a life. You took the money anyway—don't look surprised, it's in the file—but then the witnesses started dying, one by one, in ways that were never quite provable, and you did the arithmetic everyone does eventually. You counted. You got out. New coast, new registration, new name painted on a new airplane, and fifteen years of flying medicine runs so far below the radar of your old life that even you stopped believing the old one happened." She spread her hands. "Did I get it right? I hate telling a story wrong."

The aurora had faded toward gray false-dawn over the ridge. The bay lay flat as poured tin. Somewhere across the water, faint as a heartbeat through a wall, he could hear the generators of the dive barge, and beneath them, lower, the thrum of a tender's engines idling at anchor. Close enough to hear. Close enough to cross the distance between hearing and arriving in nine minutes at cruising speed. He knew because he'd flown that distance yesterday and timed it without meaning to.

"You've been carrying my file around for fifteen years?" he said.

"I've been carrying it for three years. Since Corvane bought the salvage concession and I started reading everything the predecessor companies ever touched, looking for leverage, and found you instead." She shrugged. "You were a footnote. Footnotes are where the dangerous things live. Everyone reads the body text."

"And now you're here to read me the rest of it."

"Now I'm here to make you an offer, and I wanted to deliver it in person because—" she paused, and for the first time something moved behind the pleasantness, something almost like respect, or its counterfeit, "—because I know what it costs to hear your own name said out loud by someone from the other side of it. I've had mine said. Different context. Same sensation. Like being read your own obituary while you're still warm."

Juniper shifted her weight. "What offer."

Halloran looked at her with mild interest, the way you look at weather. "The daughter. Of course. Elias Vann's girl. You have the journal; I assume you've read what he helped bury. Then you already understand the situation better than most of the people signing Corvane's paychecks." She turned back to Sebastian. "Here it is. Plain, no theater, because theater wastes everyone's morning."

She laid it out in four sentences, each one landing harder than the last.

"One: Corvane will pay you both enough money that neither of you ever files a flight plan again unless you feel like it. Two: the inquiry Iles is building—I know about the affidavit, I know she has your voice on tape, I know what you broadcast on the open channel yesterday, Mr. Oyelaran, and yes, I know why you did it—that inquiry goes away. Not delayed. Gone. Witnesses withdraw, documents misfiled, the concession lapses quietly, and no one is ever charged, because charges require paper and paper can be made to disappear. Three: your records. All of them. The deposition, the witness list, the settlement, the photographs of the tailings ponds that exist in exactly two places in the world—one of which is currently under a tarp behind you. Erased. Scrubbed. Your old name becomes a dead file in a dead archive, and you go back to being nobody, permanently, with money this time. Four: safety. Which is the one I'd weigh heaviest, if I were you, because the man I answer to has already lost this permit and knows it, and men who have already lost stop calculating and start burning. He burned the river once. He burned witnesses once. He is not going to let two bush pilots and a box of cores end him."

She let it sit. The generators hummed across the water. A loon called somewhere north, one long note, unanswered.

"In exchange," she said, "the crates come with me. This morning. Before tide five. And you two fly south, rich and clean and forgotten, which—" and here her voice did something careful, something that sounded almost gentle, "—is the only version of this story where either of you is guaranteed to see next winter."

* * *

Sebastian walked to the tarp and lifted it and looked at the crates for a long time.

He hadn't opened them again since the dive. He didn't need to. He knew what was inside the way you know what's inside a wound: sealed sample cores in waxed tubes, labeled in Elias Vann's cramped hand with dates and station numbers; ledgers wrapped in oilcloth, the ink ghosted but legible; photographs in paper sleeves, gray and grainy, of a river running the color of battery acid past a camp where children had lived. And the deposition copy. His own signature, nineteen years younger, on a page that had once been the bravest thing he'd ever done and had become, within eighteen months, the reason three people he'd eaten breakfast with were dead.

He had testified because it was right. Then the dying started, and he'd taken the settlement—not because he was a coward, though he'd called himself one every day since, but because the third death was Marta Okonkwo, who had a daughter, and he'd stood in the back of that church and understood with total clarity that staying in the fight meant watching more funerals and changing nothing, because the machine that killed witnesses did not lose hearings. It outlived them. So he'd taken the money and buried the name and told himself invisibility was survival, and then told himself it was wisdom, and then stopped telling himself anything at all, and flew.

And now Dree Halloran was standing in his camp offering him the exact same door, polished, rehung, with better hinges.

That was the thing about the dark mirror. It didn't show you a stranger. It showed you the man you'd already been, holding out his hand again, patient, certain you'd shake it, because you had before.

"The witnesses," Sebastian said quietly. "In your version. In the erased version. What happens to the river?"

Halloran didn't flinch. Credit where due. "Nothing happens to the river. Nothing has happened to the river in nineteen years except what already happened. The poison's in the sediment. It'll be there whether the paper exists or not. What you're deciding isn't whether the river gets justice. It's whether two people who could still fly anywhere they want get to keep doing it." She stepped closer, boots crunching on gravel, and lowered her voice as if the bay might repeat it. "You've read the same history I have, Mr. Oyelaran. Names on paper don't bring rivers back. They don't bring anyone back. They just give the living something to die for, and the dead don't collect."

"That's the speech," Sebastian said. "The one from nineteen years ago. Almost word for word. Different lawyer, same cadence. *You can't win, you can only choose how much it costs.*"

"Yes," Halloran said. "Because it works. Because it's true. That's the obscenity of it—it's true, and it works, and every idealist who hears it calls it a lie right up until the funeral, and then signs." She smiled, thin. "I'm not asking you to believe me. I'm asking you to count. You're good at counting. You proved it."

Juniper spoke from the edge of the light, and her voice was flat and cold as the water. "My father took a gut wound getting that map to us. He's dying in a hospital two hundred miles south, and the last thing he asked for wasn't money."

"He asked you to sink it," Halloran said. "I know. We intercepted the call."

Of course they had. Sebastian closed his eyes for half a second. The satellite phone. He should have assumed. Assume the enemy hears everything; fly accordingly. Fifteen years of discipline and he'd let a dying man's voice go out over an open sky because grief made sloppy radio operators of them all.

"Then you know the answer's already been given," Juniper said.

"I know what a dying man asked for. I also know dying men don't have to live with consequences." Halloran turned back to Sebastian, and now she dropped the pleasantness entirely, and what was underneath was worse, because it was honest. "One hour. The tender comes at first full light—which is soon, you can see the gray coming up over the ridge—and if the crates aren't on the dock, I go back to the barge, and I report that the material exists, and where, and who's holding it. And then it stops being a conversation forever. I won't lie to you about what happens after that. I've seen it. I was there for some of it. The predecessor company trained me too, in its way."

Sebastian looked at her for a long moment. Then he said, "You said you came alone because this is a conversation."

"I did."

"Was any of it true? The part about the contractor who comes back empty?"

Halloran was quiet for a moment. When she answered, she looked at the water instead of at him. "His name was Teodor Brandt. He ran a survey boat for the predecessor company the year after your deposition. He found something he shouldn't have. He tried to sell it back to them instead of going public—he thought he was being practical. Smart. They paid him. Then they paid the sea." She picked up the thermos, set it back on the drum, precise as a clerk. "I tell you that not as a threat. As a courtesy. You're the only person in twenty years I've told it to straight, because you're the only person positioned to understand it. You were there. You saw the same machine eat the same kind of man."

"I was there," Sebastian said. "I watched it eat three."

"Then you know I'm not bluffing, and I know you know, and that's why I drove the bargain hard instead of dressing it up." She pulled a folded paper from inside her jacket—an actual paper, typed, unsigned—and set it on the fuel drum between them. "Terms are on there. Account numbers, the scope of the scrub, the withdrawal language for Iles's inquiry. No signature required yet. Read it. Talk. I'll be at the waterline."

She walked down the gravel to where the gray water met the gray stones and stood looking north with her hands in her pockets, giving them the fiction of privacy, which was itself a negotiation tactic, and a good one.

* * *

Juniper came and stood beside him at the tarp. Neither of them touched the crates.

"Well?" she said.

"She's right about the counting," Sebastian said. "That's what makes her dangerous. Everything she said is true except the conclusion. Money is real. Safety is real. The inquiry could collapse—Iles is one inspector with a recorder and a permit clock, and Corvane has lawyers like the river has water. If we trade, we probably live. Comfortably. Forgotten." He laughed, and there was no humor in it, just old rust falling off something. "Fifteen years ago I would have signed before she finished talking. I'd have told myself it was the smart play. The survivable play. I'd have been right, too. Right up until the next time somebody needed a witness and there weren't any, because guys like me kept taking the door."

Juniper watched him with those level eyes that missed nothing. "And now?"

"Now I keep thinking about what's actually in the boxes." He crouched and drew back the tarp fully, letting the false dawn fall on the waxed tubes, the oilcloth, the gray photographs. "It's not treasure. It never was. It's a confession with a serial number. And here's the thing Halloran's offer proves, Juniper—she wouldn't be here at four in the morning offering us a fortune if the paper were worthless. Companies don't buy garbage. They buy the things that can kill them. This kills them. Slowly, maybe, through some inquiry that takes years and convicts middle managers—but it kills them, and they know it, and that's why the offer is this big."

"So we refuse."

"We refuse the offer." He stood. "But listen to me, because this is the part I got wrong last time, and I won't get it wrong twice. Refusing isn't the same as winning. Last time I refused nothing—I signed, remember, I took the money and stayed quiet, and the witnesses died anyway. The mistake wasn't cowardice. The mistake was thinking my choice was private. That I could decide alone what the truth was worth and live with it alone." He looked at her. "Whatever we do with these crates, we don't decide it alone, and we don't decide it in the next fifty minutes because a fixer set a clock. Clocks are leverage. Leverage is what she brought instead of guns."

Juniper's jaw tightened. "Elias asked us to sink it."

"Elias asked us to sink it because names on paper won't bring back the river. And he's right." Sebastian crouched again, lifted one of the sealed cores, turned it in the gray light—station fourteen, date stamped, the sediment inside dark as tar. "But he also spent thirty years building cairns and drawing maps to lead someone here. You don't build a trail to a thing you want erased. You build a trail to a thing you want *found*—by the right person, at the right time, by someone strong enough to carry it. He couldn't carry it himself anymore. That's what the wound was. That's what the map was. He wasn't hiding the truth from the world, Juniper. He was handing it off."

Down at the waterline, Halloran turned and checked the eastern ridge, where the gray was paling toward the color of steel. First full light was close. The tender would be warming up.

Sebastian folded the typed terms once, precisely, and put them in his jacket—not accepting, not refusing. Filed. Then he walked down the gravel toward her, and Juniper fell in beside him with the rifle slung, and the three of them met at the waterline with the bay flat and silent and the generators humming across the dark water like a held breath.

"Halloran," he said. "Before you go back to your barge, there's something you should hear. Both barrels, no theater."

Her eyebrows rose, wary, professional.

Sebastian reached into the plane's emergency kit where he kept the satellite phone, thumbed it on, and set it on speaker on the fuel drum between them. The screen glowed in the half-dark. One new message had come in overnight, flagged urgent, from a coastal hospital exchange number he didn't recognize—a number he'd given to exactly one person, weeks ago, in another life.

He pressed play.

A nurse's voice first, tired, clipped: *"He insisted. Says it's for his daughter. Go ahead."*

Then a voice like gravel dragged over gravel, thin and wet with pain, but unmistakably alive:

*"Juniper. If this reaches you—don't sink it. Don't sell it either. I've had nineteen years to think about what I buried, and I was wrong about half of it. The river's gone, girl. Sinking the paper doesn't change that. But there are people downstream—Kwetha village, the families on the estuary—they're still drinking that water, and Corvane's new concession upstream means they'll drink worse. The cores prove the source. The source is still leaking. Get the records to Iles. Not for punishment. For an injunction. Stop the discharge. That's the promise, Juniper. Not the sinking. The stopping."*

A long breath, crackling with static and pain.

*"And tell the pilot—tell Kestrel—his name's good for something after all."*

The message ended. The bay was absolutely silent. Even the generators across the water seemed, for a moment, to hold their hum.

Halloran stared at the phone. Then at Sebastian. And Sebastian watched her recalculate everything—the offer, the clock, the tender warming up two coves over—because the dying man had just changed the price of every crate in the camp, and she knew it, and dawn was coming up over the ridge like a verdict.
