## 16. Elias's Promise

The satellite phone came out of the bottom of Sebastian's kit like something he'd been hoping to lose.

He'd gone down to the tideline to check the anchor line on the skiff, and Juniper had been left alone with the crates for the better part of an hour—long enough to inventory them properly, long enough to feel the weight of what they'd hauled up out of the dark. Eleven sealed document tubes. Four sample cores in waxed sleeves, each one labeled in her father's cramped, slanted hand: *Watershed C-3, depth 40m, DO NOT DRY*. And a ledger bound in oilcloth that read, when she finally worked the knot loose, like a confession written by a company man who had stopped believing in companies somewhere around page forty.

She was sitting on a fuel drum with the ledger open across her knees when Sebastian came back up the shingle, and she watched him see it—the phone in his hand, the way his thumb moved over the casing as if checking whether it still remembered how to be a telephone.

"Satellite's spotty this far north," he said. "But there's a gap in the ridge line. If we climb to the cairn and stand on one leg facing southeast, we might get thirty seconds of signal."

"That's not funny."

"I wasn't joking about the ridge." He turned the phone over. "I've been carrying this for six years and I've made four calls on it. All of them were mistakes."

"Then why carry it?"

Sebastian looked at her for a long moment, and she saw him decide something—not whether to answer, but how much of the truth the answer could hold without breaking.

"Because the day you need to make a call you can't take back," he said, "you'll want it to be possible."

* * *

The climb to the cairn took forty minutes through wet alder and old snowmelt channels, the ground soft as bread underfoot. The sky had cleared after two days of weather, and the light came down hard and clean off the glaciers, turning every pool they passed into a sheet of hammered tin. Juniper carried the phone. Sebastian carried the rifle and said nothing about it, which was its own kind of courtesy.

At the cairn—the first marker, Elias's stones stacked with a surveyor's precision, the ones with fresh boot prints beside them now three days cold—they found the signal bar flickering like a moth against glass. One bar. Maybe two if the wind held still.

Juniper dialed the number from memory. She'd had it memorized since she was nine years old, back when it was just *Dad's work number* and not the last thread connecting her to a man who had walked out of her life with a duffel bag and a lie about being back by Christmas.

It rang four times. Five. Long enough for her heart to start climbing into her throat, long enough for her to start composing the voicemail—

"This is Vann."

Her father's voice. Thin and scraped raw, but alive. Alive in a way that made her knees go strange, because she had spent eleven days building a coffin for him in her head and fitting all her grief into it like a garment, and now here he was breathing on the other end of a satellite link, and none of the grief fit anymore.

"Dad."

A silence. On the line she could hear hospital sounds compressed to almost nothing—a beep, a murmur, the vast institutional quiet of a ward at shift change.

"Junie." He said it the way he used to say it when she was small, when the word was less a name than a hand reaching for hers. "They told me—Iles told me you were north. With the pilot." A wet sound that might have been a laugh. "Kestrel. Of all the bush pilots in all this country, you found Kestrel."

"You know him?"

"I know who he used to be." Another pause, longer. "We'll come to that. Junie—how much time do we have on this thing?"

She looked at the signal bar. It held. "Thirty seconds if we're lucky. Less if the wind moves."

"Then listen. Don't talk yet. Just listen." His breath caught, and she heard the effort it cost him to keep talking, the way each sentence seemed to be paid for out of some account running low. "I'm at St. Brendan's. Coast guard picked me up off a logging float two days after your pilot left me. Gut wound went septic. They cut half of me out and the other half's arguing about whether to stay." The laugh again, worse this time. "So I don't have long, and I have exactly one thing to say, and I need you to hear all of it before you decide anything."

"I'm listening."

"The Meridian Star. You found it. I know you found it because Halloran's barge moved into the Sound the same week you did, and Corvane doesn't spend money on weather they haven't already bought." He drew a breath that whistled. "Whatever's in those crates—you've seen it by now. The cores. The ledger. You know what they put in the river."

Juniper closed her eyes. She could see it even now, behind her lids: the sample cores in their waxed sleeves, the assay numbers that meant nothing until you understood what they were assaying, and then meant everything. Arsenic compounds, processing tailings, a solvent Corvane's predecessor had pumped into the upper watershed for six straight years while telling the concession board the drainage was pristine. Forty miles of river. Three villages. The fishery her mother's family had worked for four generations, dead in a single season, officially attributed to "natural fluctuation."

"I know," she said.

"And part of you wants to carry it out. Put it in front of a judge. Make them say the names out loud in a courtroom where there are microphones." His voice hardened, or tried to; mostly it just cracked. "I know because that's what I wanted too, Junie. Fifteen years ago I sat in a room with lawyers and I thought: *this is it, this is how it ends, truth wins.* And do you know what happened? Two of the men who signed affidavits with mine died inside a year. One in a vehicle accident. One drowned with his boots on in three feet of water. And the company settled quietly, paid fines it wrote off as operating costs, and the river stayed dead, and nobody's name ever got said out loud anywhere that mattered."

"Dad—"

"I'm not finished." But his voice was fraying, and she heard him gather himself the way you gather yourself before a cold crossing. "Names on paper won't bring back the river. That's the whole lesson, Junie. That's the only thing I learned in fifteen years of carrying this. Justice isn't a courtroom. Justice is making sure it can't happen again—and those crates, in evidence, in a system Corvane owns more of than the government does, will sit in a warehouse until the statute runs out and then they'll be destroyed legally instead of buried honestly. Or worse. They'll be *lost*, the way evidence gets lost, and Corvane goes back to the watershed with a clean record and a new permit, and everything I did—all of it, the wrecking, the hiding, the fifteen years—was for nothing."

The wind shifted. The signal bar dropped to one.

"Dad, the signal's going—"

"Sink it," he said, fast now, spending words like a man spending his last coin. "Take it back down. Bury it under the wreck where I put it, where it can't be sold and can't be stolen and can't be *used*, and let the river be the witness. Not a court. The river remembers, Junie. Rivers remember better than judges do." A pause, one heartbeat long. "And tell Kestrel—" The line crackled. "—tell him the debt runs the other way. Tell him I never blamed him for flying out. Tell him—"

The bar vanished. The call died mid-sentence, and the silence that replaced it was so complete that Juniper could hear her own blood moving.

* * *

She stood at the cairn for a long time after the screen went dark.

Below her, the bay opened south toward the open water, gray-green and patient, and somewhere under that water, under the shoal that showed itself only at low tide, lay the hold of the Meridian Star with its eleven tubes and four cores and one terrible ledger—evidence enough to hang a company, if companies could be hanged, and heavy enough to drown anyone who tried to carry it alone.

Sebastian waited ten feet away, at a distance he seemed to have measured precisely, the rifle slung and his hands in his pockets. He didn't ask what Elias said. He'd heard most of it anyway; the wind had carried his father's voice down the slope in fragments, and from the look on Sebastian's face, the fragments had been enough.

"He wants us to sink it," Juniper said finally.

"I figured."

"You knew. Before the call. You already knew that's what he'd want."

Sebastian came a few steps closer, boots grinding on the frost-shattered stone. "Elias Vann spent fifteen years keeping that wreck secret at the cost of everything—his claim, his reputation, his family. Men like that don't change their minds at the end. They just run out of strength to keep holding the door shut." He paused. "What did he say about me?"

Juniper looked at him. In the hard northern light she could see the whole architecture of the man now—the careful distances, the name worn smooth from disuse, the way he stood always slightly angled away from things, as if perpetually preparing to leave. Her father had called him Kestrel. Her father had known him. There was a story there, and it was a long one, and right now she didn't have the room for it.

"He said the debt runs the other way," she said. "He said he never blamed you for flying out."

Something moved through Sebastian's face—there and gone, quick as a fish under ice. He nodded once, and didn't ask anything else, which told her the sentence had landed somewhere deep and old.

"So," he said. "Do we sink it?"

And there it was. The question she'd been circling since the night dive, since she'd come up gasping into the black water with a document tube in her hands and her heart going like a trapped bird. Because her father was right about the courts—she'd grown up watching the aftermath of that first betrayal, watching her mother write letters to agencies that answered with form letters, watching the settlement checks arrive like apologies from someone who wasn't sorry. She knew exactly how justice worked when the defendant could buy the scale.

But God, she wanted them to *pay*. She wanted names in headlines. She wanted Halloran's face on the news. She wanted the villages on the upper watershed to get the apology that had never come, the restitution, the acknowledgment that forty miles of river hadn't simply *fluctuated*—and every one of those wants lived in those crates, and her father was asking her to drown them.

"It's not fair," she said, and heard how young her voice sounded.

"No," Sebastian agreed. "It isn't."

"They killed a river. They killed people—maybe not with their hands, but they killed them, and they'll kill again if the watershed ever gets valuable enough, and sinking this doesn't stop them. Sinking this just means they win quietly instead of loudly."

"Sinking this means they win *blind*." He crouched, picked up a flat stone, turned it in his fingers. "You're thinking about punishment. Your father's thinking about protection. Both of you are right, and that's what makes it hard." He set the stone on the cairn, deliberate, adding to the pile the way Elias had. "I testified once. Did I tell you that? No—I didn't, and I won't tonight. But I'll tell you the ending: the truth went in, the machine absorbed it, and the only people who got hurt were the ones who told it. Truth isn't a weapon, Juniper. It's a burden. Whoever carries it decides what it's for. Corvane would use it to bury the past. A court might use it to bury the men who signed it. Your father wants it to bury nothing—to just *rest*, where no one can dig it up and sell it."

"And what do you want?"

Sebastian was quiet for a moment. Below them, far out in the Sound, a shape that might have been a tender or might have been a rock moved along the horizon and resolved into nothing.

"I want to stop carrying things," he said. "That's not wisdom. That's just tiredness. Don't take my vote."

* * *

They descended in silence, and the silence lasted all the way to the camp, through the rekindling of the stove, through the boiling of water for coffee neither of them drank. Juniper sat with the ledger on her knees again and read it cover to cover in the failing light, every entry, every date, every initials-and-numbers notation that mapped six years of poisoning onto forty miles of water. At the back, in the final pages, her father's handwriting changed—looser, drunker with rage—and became a narrative: dates, names, the order to dump, the meeting where the board voted to classify the drainage data, the two witnesses who signed alongside him. She read their names twice. She made herself memorize them, because whatever happened to the paper, she decided, the names would live in her. Not as ammunition. As remembrance. Someone had to be the river's witness, and the river couldn't testify.

Full dark came down. Out on the water, the tide had begun its long withdrawal, and she could hear the shoal revealing itself—water peeling off stone in long, sighing sheets, the wreck's roof coming up toward the air inch by inch, patient as everything else in this country.

Sebastian stepped out of the tent and stood looking south. Then he said, quietly, without turning:

"Tide five starts in six hours. After that, Corvane's permit has one tide left on it, and they know it, and they're not going to wait out weather anymore." He glanced back at her. "Whatever you decide, decide it soon. And Juniper—if it's the sink, we dive at slack water tonight, both of us, and we're done by dawn. If it's the trade—" He stopped. Started again. "If it's the trade, I'll help you carry it. Even though I think it's wrong. That's what a partner does."

Juniper looked down at the ledger. Her thumb rested on the last page, on her father's final line, written in a hand shaking with fury or fear or both: *Let it rest where the water can keep it.*

She thought about her mother, who had died still writing letters. She thought about the two witnesses with their boots on in three feet of water. She thought about the river itself—cold, green, empty, running the way it had always run, carrying nothing now but the memory of what had been put in it.

Justice as punishment needed a courtroom, and the courtroom was bought. Justice as protection needed only deep water and a promise kept.

"Get the gear ready," she said.

Sebastian nodded and moved toward the tent, and Juniper stayed outside a moment longer, looking south across the black water to where the shoal breathed in and out with the tide. Somewhere out there, under forty feet of sea, her father had buried the truth fifteen years ago, and the world had kept spinning, and the river had kept running, and maybe that was the whole of it—maybe some truths weren't meant to be raised. Maybe they were ballast. Maybe they were what kept a country upright.

She was still standing there when Sebastian's hand closed on her shoulder—brief, hard, the first unprompted touch he'd ever given her.

"Slack water's at 0300," he said. "Sleep if you can."

She couldn't. But she lay in the dark listening to the tide go out, and at 0230 she rose without being called, and together, in the cold and the wind and the last of the darkness, they began to carry the truth back down to the water that had kept it safe all these years—

—and that was when Juniper saw the light.

Not a star. Not the moon. A single white light, low on the southern horizon, moving with the steady, unhurried confidence of a vessel that knew exactly where it was going.

Two coves over, where the dive barge had been waiting out the weather, the generators had gone silent.

Halloran was coming.
