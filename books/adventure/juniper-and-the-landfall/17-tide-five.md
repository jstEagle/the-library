## 17. Tide Five

The radio woke him before the light did.

It was the wrong kind of static—not the soft hiss of open water and empty sky but a stuttering, rhythmic crackle, like someone keying a transmitter in bursts. Sebastian lay in the tent with his eyes open for a full minute before he moved, listening, cataloguing. Juniper was still asleep across the cold fire ring, her rifle within reach of her hand even now, even after everything. The tide was turning. He could hear it working against the gravel bar below the camp, that long slow exhale of the channel draining out toward the sea.

Fourth tide. They had two left.

The bursts came again. Three short, one long. Then silence.

He knew the pattern. Fifteen years ago he'd invented half the shorthand the coastal pilots used to keep their traffic off the concession company's ears, and three-short-one-long had been *position report, unencrypted, listen if you want*. Nobody honest used it anymore. Nobody honest was flying at four in the morning in weather like this.

Sebastian crawled out of the tent into a world the color of wet slate. Fog sat on the channel like a lid, thick enough to taste—kerosene and salt and the green rot of the estuary mudflats. Visibility maybe two hundred meters. Which meant whoever was out there transmitting couldn't see much either, and neither could he.

That should have been comforting. It wasn't.

* * *

Juniper took the news standing at the edge of the bar, arms crossed against the damp, watching the fog the way she watched everything—as if it owed her money.

"You're sure it's Corvane."

"I'm sure it's not a fisherman. Fishermen don't run burst transmissions on concession bands." He crouched by the crates they'd hauled up from the hold two nights ago, running his thumb along the seal of the nearest one. Inside: sample cores wrapped in waxed canvas, ledgers sealed in oilcloth, photographs Elias Vann had spent twenty years keeping dry and dark. Proof that Corvane's predecessor had poisoned the Kestrel watershed and buried the evidence under forty feet of cold seawater. Proof worth killing for, if you were the kind of company that killed.

"We move today," Juniper said. "Now, before this fog lifts. Fly it out while they're blind."

"Fly it where? Iles is at the ranger station. Halloran's barge is two coves south with a crane and a dive team. Every registry office on the coast has our flight plan—or thinks it does." He stood, knees cracking. "There's nowhere this plane goes that someone isn't already looking."

"Then we go somewhere nobody looks." She said it flatly, without drama, the way she said everything. "You told me once every pilot has places like that. Places that aren't on any chart because the chartmakers gave up."

He had told her that. Somewhere over the Long Water, in the dark stretch when the radio died and she'd navigated them through dead reckoning with nothing but a watch, a pencil, and a memory for coastline that frightened him a little—he'd told her things he hadn't meant to tell anyone. That was the trouble with her. She flew the way she listened: close, patient, and impossible to shake off your tail.

"The old survey strip," he said slowly. "Kettle Inlet. Abandoned since before the war. There's a gravel bed wide enough for the Cessna if the water's down, and there won't be a soul within fifty miles."

"And from there?"

"From there we wait out tide six, and Iles comes to us, or Halloran does, and either way it ends." He looked at the crates. "Elias wanted these sunk, not sold. But he also wanted them *found first*. On the record. If we just make them disappear, Corvane wins by default—the river stays poisoned on paper forever."

Juniper was quiet a moment. He'd learned to read her silences the way he read weather: there were silences that meant storm coming and silences that meant it had passed. This one was the second kind.

"My father," she said finally, "spent twenty years being a coward about this. I'm not going to spend twenty minutes more than I have to. Load the plane."

* * *

They were wheels-up at 0540, climbing into fog so dense the propeller seemed to be chewing it, the floats' shadow invisible beneath them, the whole world reduced to engine noise and instrument needles. Sebastian flew by feel and by the clock, counting seconds between known points, holding a heading he didn't entirely trust toward a channel mouth he couldn't see.

Juniper rode in the right seat with the map fragments spread across her knees, calling corrections in a voice pitched just above the engine. "Half a mile. Bear left at the fork—there should be an island, tall pines."

"There's no island I can see."

"You'll see it."

He saw it. A black shoulder of trees materializing out of the gray off the left wingtip, close enough that he flinched, and then they were past it and into the wider channel and the fog began, grudgingly, to thin.

That was when the sound reached them.

Not a transmission this time. An engine—a big radial, the heavy throb of something built for hauling, not hunting. It came from ahead and above, hidden in the last ragged ceiling, and Sebastian's stomach dropped before his mind caught up, because he knew that sound the way you know a voice in a dream. Twin-engine. Turbocharged. Corvane's aerial tender, the one that ran supplies out to the dive barge.

Except supply planes didn't fly at dawn in fog, and they didn't fly *here*, twenty miles off the barge's station, exactly astride the only route between the wreck site and anywhere else.

"They're not searching," Juniper said, and her voice had gone very quiet, which was worse than shouting. "They're waiting. They knew the route."

The registry leak. He'd suspected it since Iles told them the concession coordinates had been filed wrong—wrong on purpose, wrong in a way that only made sense if someone inside the office wanted the real route kept clear for private traffic. Someone had sold their flight plan. Maybe not even for money. Maybe just because Corvane asked, and asking was usually enough.

"Strap in tight," he said. "I'm going down."

"Down where?"

"Anywhere that isn't sky."

He rolled the yoke and put the Cessna into the channel's throat, dropping toward the water until the floats were kissing their own reflection, threading the narrows where the cliffs came together and no twin-engine in the world would follow. Rocks slid past below them, pale and fanged, close enough to count barnacles. Behind and above, the radial engine circled, patient as a gull, then gave up its altitude and settled into a trailing position—too high to touch them, too close to forget.

"He can't shoot," Juniper said. "Halloran wants the crates intact. She said so."

"Halloran wants them intact. Halloran isn't flying that plane." Sebastian's hands were steady on the yoke, which surprised him; somewhere in the last two weeks steadiness had stopped being a performance and started being true. "Corvane didn't send a negotiator this time. Negotiation's over. We're past tide four and they know what we found."

The channel bent east, then south, then doubled back on itself in a hairpin around a shoal that only existed at low water—and the tide was low, lower than he liked, the shoal showing its spine like a beached whale. He took the hairpin at an angle that made Juniper grab the door frame, the floats skimming foam, and for thirty seconds the plane behind them lost sight of them against the cliff shadow.

Thirty seconds was enough to make a decision.

"Kettle Inlet's gone," he said. "If they have our route, they have every route. We need a place that isn't a place." His eyes swept the water ahead, reading it the way Elias must have read it, the way every pilot who'd ever survived this coast read it: not as scenery but as options. Gravel bars. River mouths. The hundred anonymous shoulders of land where a floatplane could sit down and become a piece of the landscape.

"There." Juniper pointed. Not a bar—a river delta, braided channels spreading across a mile of tidal flat, silt and gravel and dead snags, ugly country, featureless country, the kind of place charts marked with a single dismissive word: *flats*.

"It'll tear the floats apart."

"It might. Or it might not. Better than the alternative." She looked at him sideways. "You always land where you're supposed to, Sebastian?"

"No," he said. "That's how I got here."

He put it down on the third channel he tried, touching gravel with a grinding shudder that went up through the airframe and into his teeth, the plane slewing, correcting, slewing again, and then rumbling to a stop in eight inches of water with the engine ticking and both of them breathing like they'd run the last mile themselves.

Behind them, high and receding, the radial engine turned north. Searching the routes. Searching everywhere except the one place on the coast too miserable to matter.

* * *

The damage was worse than he'd hoped and better than he'd feared.

The left float had taken the worst of it—a seam opened along the keel, weeping in a thin bright thread, and a strut cracked clean through at the weld. On land it would be a day's work with a torch and plate steel. On a tidal flat with no tools beyond what lived in the Cessna's survival kit, it was a question of whether the plane would float long enough to take off, and whether it would stay in one piece when it did.

Sebastian lay half in the cold water under the port float, wire and epoxy putty in his fists, and let himself think clearly for the first time since the radio wakes.

Tide five was turning. When it came back in, it would come in fast, and it would lift the plane, and the cracked strut would bear the whole weight of the airframe rocking on swells—and if it let go on the water, they would swim, and the crates would sink, and everything Elias had buried and Juniper had bled for would end as litter on the bottom of a nameless slough.

Or they could wait here. Sit out tide five and tide six on the flats, watch the concession expire, and let Iles come find them—if Iles ever did, if the inspector hadn't already been bought or buried in paperwork, if the leak in the registry office hadn't poisoned that well too.

Allies. He counted them on one hand and had fingers left over. Iles, maybe. Elias, unconscious in a hospital two hundred miles south. And Juniper, crouched on the gravel beside him with her sleeves rolled, holding the float steady against the current without being asked, her face set in the expression he was beginning to understand was her version of hope: jaw forward, eyes narrowed, expecting the worst and daring it to arrive.

"The strut's the problem," he said. "I can seal the seam. The strut I can splint, but a splinted strut is a promise, not a guarantee."

"What kind of promise?"

"The kind I've broken before." He worked the wire around the crack, looping it, cinching it with pliers gone slippery in his wet hands. "Fifteen years ago I promised a federal attorney my testimony would hold. Two witnesses died before the trial started. I stopped making promises after that. Promises are just predictions you haven't been proven wrong on yet."

Juniper was quiet. Then: "You're going to fly it anyway."

"Tide six is tomorrow morning. If we're not airborne before the fleet figures out where we are—" He sat back in the shallows, water streaming off his forearms, and looked at her. "Your father asked me to finish what he started. I refused. I told him I don't finish other people's promises." He held up the splinted strut, absurd, heroic, doomed. "Turns out I just needed the right one."

Something moved behind her eyes—not softness exactly. Recognition, maybe. One survivor recognizing another's scar tissue.

* * *

They heard the boat an hour later.

Not an engine—a oar, or a paddle, the rhythmic knock of wood against a gunwale carrying flat across the water in the evening calm. Sebastian froze with the epoxy gun halfway to the seam. Juniper was already moving, low, rifle coming off her shoulder without ceremony, sliding into the deadfall at the top of the bank.

The fog had burned off with the afternoon and the light was going gold and long, and out at the mouth of the third channel a canoe slid into view. One paddler. Small. Moving with the economy of someone who had done this ten thousand times.

An old woman in a rain hat, gray braid down her back, and Sebastian knew her before he could put a name to her—knew her the way you know a face from a photograph on a ranger station wall. Marta Okonkwo. The last homesteader on the flats, the woman every bush pilot knew and none of them talked about, because knowing Marta meant you flew the illegal runs, the medicine drops, the quiet favors the coast ran on underneath its official skin.

She brought the canoe alongside the gravel bar and looked at the wounded plane, and at Sebastian, and at Juniper rising out of the deadfall with the rifle, and she was unimpressed by all of it.

"Elias Vann's girl," she said to Juniper. "You have his chin." Then, to Sebastian, in the tone of a woman delivering a bill: "Radio at the ranger station says a blue-and-white Cessna on floats went missing this morning. Inspector Iles has been asking after it since noon. Asking loud." She handed a bundle across the gap between canoe and gravel: bread, smoked fish, a coil of copper wire, a length of aircraft-grade aluminum stock that made Sebastian's breath catch. "And she says to tell the pilot—her words—that tide six comes at 0612, and that after tide six she stops answering telephones."

"She knows where we are," Juniper said.

"She knows *everything*, girl. That's what inspectors are for." Marta was already backing water, unhurried, the gold light catching the lines of her face. "Question is what she does with it. Question is always what anybody does with what they know." She paused, one paddle blade dripping. "Corvane's tender landed men at the river mouth an hour ago. Four of them. Walking. They'll reach you before midnight if you're still here."

The canoe slid away into the gold water, and the flats went silent except for the tick of the cooling engine and the far-off mutter of the turning tide.

Sebastian looked at the aluminum stock in his hands. Enough to replace the strut—if he had a torch. He didn't. He had epoxy, wire, a hacksaw, and eleven hours.

"Get the crates loaded," he said. "Everything that doesn't fly, we bury. Everything that does—" He looked south, toward the river mouth, where four men were walking through the dusk toward them with the patience of a company that had never once been in a hurry. "Everything that flies, we take over the mountains tonight, in the dark, on a splinted strut, and we pray the wilderness forgives us for asking."

Juniper shouldered the rifle and started for the plane without a word.

Above the eastern ridge, the first star came out, hard and white, and Sebastian thought: *tide five is almost done. One left.*
