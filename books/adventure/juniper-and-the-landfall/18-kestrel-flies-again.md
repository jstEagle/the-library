## 18. Kestrel Flies Again

The plane sat on the gravel bar like a wounded bird, one wingtip dipped toward the water, three fresh holes stitched through the fabric of the fuselage where the Corvane tender's gunner had walked his fire across them as they'd lifted off. Sebastian had put her down hard on the shingle with the river running shallow under the wheels and Juniper shouting coordinates in his ear, and now, in the gray hour before dawn, he knelt in the cold with a patch kit and a headlamp and tried to remember how to be invisible.

He couldn't. That was the problem. Invisibility was a discipline, and disciplines failed when your hands shook.

Behind him, up on the bar among the alders, Juniper had built a windbreak of driftwood and tarps around the crates—eight sealed boxes of records and sample cores that proved everything, that named names, that weighed less than a moose quarter and more than a cathedral. Elias's life's work. The river's autopsy.

"You can't fix that spar," she said. She'd come down without him hearing her, which bothered him more than it should have. Fifteen years of listening for footsteps, and this woman moved through his blind spots like she'd been born there.

"I can fix it enough."

"Enough for what? Where are you even going to fly?"

Sebastian set down the rivet gun. The river talked to itself over the gravel, patient, indifferent. Somewhere south, two coves back, Halloran's dive barge would be waking its generators. Somewhere east, the fleet was moving early because someone in the registry office had sold them the tide tables and probably the color of Juniper's jacket. Six tides had become five. Five were nearly four.

"They're looking for a floatplane running low and quiet," he said. "Two people aboard, hugging the coast, headed for deep water or a ranger strip. That's what their spotters will be told to find."

"And?"

"And I'm not going to be that plane." He stood, knees cracking, and looked north up the valley where the fog was peeling off the ridgelines in long white rags. "I'm going to be loud."

* * *

She fought him for an hour, and she was right about most of it, and he let her win exactly none of it because there was nothing to trade. The crates stayed with her. The rifle stayed with her. The second half of the map—the half drawn in Elias's careful dying hand—stayed folded in her breast pocket, and if the plane went down, if the fleet caught him, she was to take the skiff cached at the mouth of the Kettle and be gone before the echo faded.

"That's what my father did," she said. "Left. Made himself small. Look how that ended."

"Your father got gut-shot holding onto something worth killing for. There are worse endings."

"There are better ones."

"Yes," Sebastian said. "There are. I've been avoiding all of them for fifteen years, and it turns out they keep anyway, like meat in a cache. You can't outrun a thing by standing still forever. Eventually you just starve in place."

Juniper crouched by the crates, arms wrapped around her knees, and for a long moment she was so much her father's daughter that it hurt to look at her—the same stubborn set of the jaw, the same way of staring at a man as though reading fine print.

"What name?" she asked finally.

He knew what she meant. Not Oyelaran. Not the name on the registration, not the name stenciled on the fuel receipts from Whitehorse to Fort Providence. The other one. The one Marguerite Iles had said across the airstrip like a knife laid flat on a table: *Kestrel.*

"Kestrel," he said. "It was my callsign, back when I flew survey for the company that taught Corvane everything it knows. It's the name the witnesses knew me by. It's the name on the testimony I never finished giving." He picked up the rivet gun again, mostly to have something in his hands. "Iles has been waiting three days for me to say it out loud. Halloran's known since the dock. The whole time we've been running, I've been running from two audiences at once, and only one of them wants me dead."

"The other one wants you on a witness stand."

"The other one wants me *alive*, which is worse, some days." He almost smiled. "But today it's useful."

* * *

The radio in the old Cessna had taken a round through the antenna lead, and he'd spliced it with wire and prayer, and when he keyed the mic at first light the transmission came out rough but it came out. He climbed onto the wheel strut with the handset and the long-wire antenna draped over his shoulder like a stole, and Juniper stood below him with her arms crossed against the cold, watching him do the thing she'd stopped arguing about an hour ago.

He didn't key up right away. He made himself wait. Below, the river ran brown and fast with last night's rain. Above, the ceiling was lifting off the peaks in stages, and somewhere in that lifting sky a Corvane spotter was already hunting, and somewhere further out, on a frequency Iles monitored the way other people monitored weather, a concession inspector was drinking bad coffee and waiting for the world to make sense again.

Sebastian thought about the man he'd been. Twenty-nine years old, callsign Kestrel, flying water-sample runs up the Alder watershed for Meridian Consolidated, believing in permits and assays and the general decency of paperwork. He thought about the night the cores came back hot and the order came down to log them clean, and he said no, and for eleven days he believed the system would hold. Then the lab tech who'd run the samples drowned in two feet of water. Then the junior geologist drove his truck into a cutbank at a speed no investigation could explain. Then Sebastian understood that the system held—for whoever built it—and he took a copy of the real logs, and he ran, and he had been running for fifteen years, changing strips and changing provinces, sanding his name down thinner every year until there was almost nothing left of it to grip.

Almost nothing. But Iles had gripped it. And Halloran. And now the river itself wanted it back.

He keyed the mic.

"Any station, any station, this is Kestrel." His voice came out steadier than he felt. "Kestrel, former Meridian Consolidated flight operations, Alder watershed program. I am transmitting in the clear on 123.45 and will repeat on guard. To any Corvane Salvage aircraft or vessels monitoring: I have what you're looking for, and I don't have it anymore. It's already gone where you can't buy it back."

Beside the plane, Juniper went very still.

"To Marguerite Iles, concession inspection, if you're listening—and I know you're listening—you'll want this logged. My name is Sebastian Adeyemi Oyelaran. I was the pilot on the Alder program in the spring of the incident year. I hold copies of unaltered sample logs, chain-of-custody records, and dive documentation regarding the vessel *Meridian Star*. I am willing to testify. Fully. Under my own name. Which is why—" and here he made himself breathe, made himself look at the ridgeline where the fog was burning off, made himself say the rest of it fast before courage failed— "which is why Corvane is going to want me very dead in the next six hours. So I'm inviting them to try. Openly. On the record. Any aircraft that fires on an unarmed plane whose pilot has just declared intent to testify—that's not salvage security anymore, ladies and gentlemen. That's murder with witnesses. Come and make a martyr of me. I dare you."

He released the key. The static hissed back at him, enormous, oceanic.

Then, faintly, threaded through the noise: a spotter pilot's voice, clipped and startled, calling a vector home. And beneath that, farther down in the band, another voice—dry, unhurried, unmistakably Marguerite Iles—saying words he couldn't make out to someone he couldn't hear, followed by a single clear transmission:

*"Kestrel, this is Iles. Copy your declaration. Log entry timestamped. Keep flying, Mr. Oyelaran. Every minute you stay on this frequency, they can't touch you without writing my report for me."*

* * *

He flew the way he hadn't flown in fifteen years: high, bright, transponding, chattering position reports into the open air like a man ringing a bell through a forest full of wolves. The Cessna's patched skin buzzed. The wounded spar sang a note he didn't love whenever the gusts came down off the divide, but she held, and he held, and below him the channels braided silver through the muskeg while the sun came up the color of brass.

They came for him inside forty minutes.

First the spotter—a fast little taildragger with corporate paint and too many antennas—pacing him at a respectful distance, photographing. Then, ten minutes behind, the twin-engine workhorse that Halloran used for crew runs, riding his wing like a debt collector. Neither fired. He could feel why. He could feel it the way you feel weather: every radio he was squawking on was a room full of listeners, and Iles was in all of them, logging, timestamped, and Corvane's fixers understood arithmetic better than anyone alive. A dead martyr cost more than a lost wreck.

So instead they herded him. The spotter slid ahead and turned back across his nose, forcing him south. The twin dropped behind and boxed the tail. They were walking him, gentle as sheepdogs, away from the northern channels where Juniper and the crates were already moving, away from the deep water, toward the barge, toward Halloran, toward a dockside conversation with no radios and no witnesses.

Sebastian smiled at the windshield, alone in the cockpit, and did the thing they wouldn't expect, because it was the thing no invisible man would ever do.

He turned north. Straight at the spotter, climbing, ugly and slow, broadcasting the whole way: *"Kestrel is turning north, Kestrel is turning north, note the time, note the heading, Iles, are you getting this?"*

The spotter broke left, startled. The twin overshot trying to follow. And for ninety seconds—one hundred and eighty feet per minute of climb, a wounded spar groaning, the whole gray country opening up ahead of him—Sebastian Oyelaran flew through the gap between them like a man stepping through a door he'd been afraid of his entire adult life.

On the open channel, Iles's voice, dry as gravel: *"Noted. Heading north. Mr. Oyelaran, you are either the bravest fool or the luckiest one I've met in twenty years of inspections."*

"Neither," Sebastian said. "Just done hiding."

Ahead, the fog bank over the Kettle estuary stood up like a wall, and behind it, somewhere in the maze of channels, a skiff with a woman and eight crates was running for deep water while the sixth tide began to turn. He pointed the nose at the wall of cloud, keyed the mic one last time, and told the whole listening world exactly where he was going.

Let them chase a ghost who'd finally given them his name. By the time they worked out what the name had bought, it would be the next tide, and the next tide belonged to Juniper.
