## 19. Landfall

The tide had turned against them an hour before dawn, and Juniper felt it in the way the water moved through the channel—no longer running out to sea in long brown ribbons but slackening, hesitating, gathering itself for the turn back in. Six tides. The registry clerk's words had lived in her head like a splinter since the coastal office: *the concession expires on the sixth tide, and not one minute after.* She'd counted them the way her father had taught her to count everything—by water, by light, by the feel of the air on her face.

This was the sixth.

She stood at the bow of the leaking skiff they'd borrowed from the fuel cache, watching the shoal come up out of the dark. The sky hadn't decided yet whether to be night or morning; it hung between, a bruised grey that made no shadows and gave no cover. Somewhere south of them, Sebastian's plane was climbing into that same grey with his real name spilling out of him over an open radio channel—*Oyelaran, Sebastian Oyelaran, calling Corvane fleet, do you copy*—and every salvage tender in the bay would be turning toward that voice like iron filings toward a magnet.

Toward him. Away from her.

"Come on," she whispered, though there was no one to hear it. "Come on, Dad."

The Meridian Star's grave showed itself the way it always did, only when the water let it: a pale seam of broken water where the current dragged across the drowned hull, a dark suggestion of structure beneath, like a body under a sheet. At low tide you could see the shoal's spine. At high tide it vanished entirely, and with it any chance of finding the hold without killing yourself on the wreckage.

The tide was coming back in. She had maybe forty minutes of workable water, and then the sea would close the door for good—and this time it wouldn't be for six hours. It would be forever, if she did what she'd come to do.

Juniper cut the engine and let the skiff drift the last hundred meters, shipping oars when the hull kissed the shoal's edge with a grinding whisper. She'd anchored here once already, two nights ago, in the dark, with Halloran's dive barge humming its generators two coves over and every nerve in her body screaming that she was a lit match in a powder room. She'd found the hold then. She'd brought up one crate, cracked its seal with her knife, and read enough by flashlight to understand what her father had spent fifteen years and a gut wound protecting.

Not gold. Not claim-stones. Paper, cores, sealed sample vials—the watershed's autopsy, conducted by the company's own surveyors and then buried with their ship so it could never testify.

Her father had helped sink it. Her father had been dying of it, in his way, ever since.

And last night, from a hospital bed three hundred miles south, he'd asked her to put it back.

*Names on paper won't bring back the river, June-bird. I've read those pages a hundred times in my head. Every name, every number. It doesn't unpoison anything. But if Corvane gets that hold open, they don't just erase the evidence—they buy the whole story. And then the river stays dead and everyone forgets why.*

She'd argued. God help her, she'd argued—justice, tribunals, Iles with her recorder and her immunity offer, all of it. Elias had listened the way he listened to weather reports, patiently, already knowing what was coming.

*Iles has your testimony. That's hers now, not mine. What's in the hold is mine to give away, and I'm giving it to the sea. Do it while the tide's low, June-bird. Do it while they're chasing Kestrel.*

Kestrel. The name still sat strangely in her chest—not the callsign, but the man who'd finally claimed it, broadcasting himself like a flare over open water so that she could have these forty minutes. She'd known Sebastian Oyelaran for less than two weeks and she trusted him more than anyone alive, which should have frightened her and instead just felt like gravity.

She checked her gear by feel: tank, regulator, weight belt, the crate lashed to the skiff's bench with the remaining seals intact—she'd learned her lesson about opening things. The second and third crates were already down there, weighted and waiting where she'd staged them on the wreck's deck plates. All she had to do was take this one down, set all three in the deepest part of the hold, and collapse what remained of the bulkhead overhead onto them. Elias's journal had described exactly how they'd done it the first time—blasting charges, a controlled fall of plating, *like burying a coffin*. She didn't have charges. She had a pry bar, a come-along winch, and the wreck itself, rotten and eager to fall.

It would have to be enough. The sea would finish the job; the sea always finished jobs.

Juniper rolled backward off the gunwale into water so cold it rang through her like a struck bell.

* * *

Down was a country she was learning to read.

The first dive, two nights ago, had been terror and fumbling—her light swallowed by silt, her hands finding the wreck's geometry by bruise and guesswork. This time she dropped through the broken cargo hatch feet-first, following her own marker line, and the Meridian Star opened around her like a cathedral she'd already memorized. Survey vessel, ninety meters of her, lying on her port side in thirty feet of green-black water, masts gone, superstructure furred with kelp. Fifteen years of tide and cold had been working on her the whole time, patient as anything in this country.

Her father had walked these decks as a young surveyor. Had helped load the crates that proved what the company had done upstream—tailings seepage, benzene in the spawning channels, test results too damning to survive an audit. Then he'd watched them scuttle their own ship rather than let the findings surface, and he'd kept the secret because keeping it was the only thing keeping him alive, and he'd hated himself in that quiet, corrosive way that eventually drove him into the interior to die alone among cairns and half-maps.

Juniper understood it now in her bones, moving through the drowned corridors with her light carving a small yellow room out of the dark. Some truths you carry because carrying them is the penance. He'd wanted to hand her a fortune. What he'd actually handed her was the weight.

The hold yawned ahead, half-collapsed, its ceiling a lacework of rusted plate and hanging cable. Her staged crates sat where she'd left them, furred already with a fine grey silt, looking less like evidence than like something the wreck had grown. She clipped her light to a stanchion, took the third crate off the lift bag she'd rigged, and began walking it down the list of the hull toward the deep end—the old ballast section, lowest point, where the mud was already claiming everything.

Fifteen feet. Twenty. The crate weighed nothing underwater and everything at once; it caught on a pipe flange and she had to wedge herself under it and lift with her legs, her regulator rasping, bubbles climbing past her face like a string of small silver arguments for going back up. She thought of her father's hands drawing the map, the left half and the right half, splitting it deliberately so that no one person could ever find the wreck alone. He'd built distrust into the treasure. Maybe he'd known that whoever came looking would need each other, and that needing each other was the only inheritance worth leaving.

She got the crate to the ballast section, laid it against the other two, and lashed all three together with the nylon strap she'd brought, cinching it until her gloves creaked. Then she looked up at the ceiling of rusted plate above them—the collapsed section of the hold's overhead, tons of it, hanging on a handful of corroded frames like a jaw held open.

Time to close the mouth.

* * *

The come-along winch was the hardest part, because it required both hands and most of her attention, and the water around her kept insisting on being noticed. Cold worked inward. Her air gauge ticked down. And twice she stopped entirely, frozen, because she heard—through the water, through her own bones—a sound she couldn't identify. Engine noise? The barge? Sebastian's plane, somewhere far above, leading a fleet of wolves across the bay?

Both times it resolved into nothing. Both times she made herself keep working.

She rigged the winch to a surviving frame beam, ran the cable under the hanging plate section, and anchored the far end to a deck cleat that had held for fifteen years and would hold for five more minutes. Then she climbed clear—up and out of the collapse zone, back along the corridor to the cargo hatch, where she could reach the release line she'd run earlier.

She paused there, hanging in the green dark with her light off, letting the wreck hold her.

This is the part nobody writes songs about, she thought. No chase, no explosion, no fortune lost in a storm. Just a woman in cold water, pulling a cable, putting something back where it belonged. Sacrifice without spectacle. Her father would have appreciated that—he'd spent his whole life after the sinking practicing invisibility, and she'd spent the last two weeks learning it from Sebastian, and maybe that was the family trade after all: how to disappear so that something true could survive.

But it wasn't quite disappearing, was it. Not anymore. Iles had the testimony. Sebastian had given his name to the air itself. The truth wasn't going into the dark—it was going into the record, into the inquiry, into whatever came next. Only the physical evidence was being returned to the sea, and it was being returned not to hide it but to keep it from being *bought*. There was a difference, and the difference was everything. You could drown a document. You couldn't drown what people knew.

Juniper pulled the release line.

Above and behind her, the winch pawls ratcheted free, the cable sang once like a struck wire, and the hanging plate section came down with a sound she felt more than heard—a vast, soft, final concussion, a whale falling asleep. Silt exploded outward and swallowed her light even through her closed eyes. The water became brown chaos, then brown calm, then slowly, slowly, green again.

When she dared shine her lamp, the hold's deep end was gone. Buried. Three crates of Corvane's worst secrets sealed under fifteen tons of their own ship, in thirty feet of black water, at the bottom of a bay that forgot nothing and forgave more than it should.

The sixth tide was turning. She could feel it beginning to move past her, the faint new push of the flood, water sliding back into the channels, filling the day's wounds. By full high tide the shoal would vanish. By tomorrow the charts would show nothing but depth numbers. Concession expired. Salvage rights void. Whatever Corvane sent north after this would be trespassing on a graveyard with nothing in it.

Juniper hung there a moment longer, regulator humming, and said goodbye to her father properly—in the place he'd never been able to return to, doing the thing he'd never been able to do.

Then she went up.

* * *

She broke the surface into a world that had chosen morning while she was under.

Low gold light lay flat across the water, and the sky to the east had gone the color of hammered tin. The skiff bobbed where she'd left it. She hauled herself over the gunwale, stripped her mask, and sat breathing hard in the sudden enormous silence, listening.

Far to the south, faint as a memory, she caught it: an engine note, high and thin, climbing. A plane. His plane—or what was left of it, shot up on a gravel bar yesterday, patched with jury-rigged fabric and prayer, flying anyway because Sebastian Oyelaran had apparently decided that invisible men make poor decoys. She couldn't see him. She could see, further south still, the shapes of the salvage tenders strung out across the water like beads on a wire, all of them pointed away from her, all of them chasing a voice.

Let them chase. Let them find an empty man in a broken plane and discover that the martyr they might have made of him had already testified, already signed, already handed Inspector Iles everything on tape. Let Dree Halloran stand on the deck of her barge and read the tide tables and do the arithmetic and understand, with the particular bitterness of a professional, that she'd been beaten by a dying prospector's daughter and a bush pilot with a buried name.

Juniper started the engine and turned the skiff north.

There was a gravel bar twenty miles up-channel where a shot-up Cessna was supposed to be waiting, and a man beside it who'd bet his life, his name, and his last good secret on her being able to hold her breath long enough to keep a promise. She intended to prove him right.

The flood tide carried her north, swift and sure, and behind her the Meridian Star settled another inch into the mud of the bay, holding her father's secret the way the country held everything—

—and ahead, rounding the first point, she saw the smoke.
