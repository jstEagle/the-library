## 20. The Reckoning Tide

**Iles**

Marguerite Iles had spent nineteen years in the concession service learning that the truth, in this country, was like permafrost—present everywhere beneath the surface, and almost never worth the cost of digging for it.

She sat in the ranger station at Kettle Narrows with a portable recorder between her elbows and a cup of coffee gone cold an hour ago, and she listened to Sebastian Oyelaran say his own name into the microphone as if he were setting down something heavy he'd carried too far.

"My name is Sebastian Oyelaran. Fifteen years ago I flew survey runs for Meridian Consolidated out of Fort Alder. I testified before the territorial inquiry about what they were doing to the Ashwater watershed. My callsign then was Kestrel. After the third witness died, I stopped using the name."

The recorder's little wheel turned. Rain ticked against the window. Outside, the tide was dropping out of the channel, exposing the black ribs of rock that had wrecked better boats than any of theirs.

Iles had come north expecting to file a report about an unlicensed pilot flying under a false identity. That report would have taken her twenty minutes. It would have ended a man's livelihood and changed nothing else in the world.

Instead she had this. Four hours of testimony, recorded on the open channel while Corvane's fleet listened and could do nothing about it but chase a decoy across the water. Names, dates, manifests. The dumping orders Oyelaran had seen with his own eyes. The sample cores Juniper Vann had described from the hold of the *Meridian Star*—sealed crates of them, logged, dated, signed by men whose signatures still appeared on Corvane letterhead.

And underneath all of it, the thing that made her hands unsteady on the coffee cup: Elias Vann's confession, relayed through his daughter's voice on the satellite phone, that he had helped sink the ship deliberately. Not to hide treasure. To hide evidence. To keep the river's poisoning from being quietly erased along with everything else.

Iles pressed stop. She sat for a long moment in the lamplight, listening to the rain and the tide.

Then she took out the incident report form—the one that would have ended Oyelaran's license—and wrote across the top of it, in block letters: **SUPERSEDED — SEE INQUIRY FILE 44-C.**

Nineteen years of learning when not to dig. This was the other thing she'd learned, the rarer lesson: how to know, finally, when the ground itself was asking to be opened.

She keyed the radio to the regional band, the one every registry office, ranger post, and salvage captain in the territory kept tuned out of habit and self-interest.

"All stations, all stations. This is Inspector Marguerite Iles, Concession Authority, badge four-one-one. Effective immediately I am opening a formal inquiry into the sinking of the survey vessel *Meridian Star*, the conduct of Meridian Consolidated and its successor entities, and the circumstances surrounding concession permit CV-7734. Recorded testimony has been secured. All parties holding relevant materials are instructed to preserve them. Corvane Salvage is directed to suspend all operations pending review."

She let the silence sit for three seconds, long enough for every operator on the band to understand she meant it.

"I say again: recorded testimony has been secured."

Somewhere out in the dark water, she knew, Dree Halloran was listening. And somewhere south, in a glass tower with the company's old name still ghosted on the lobby stone, men who had slept soundly for fifteen years were not sleeping anymore.

Iles poured the cold coffee out the window onto the gravel and started writing.

**Halloran**

Dree Halloran heard the broadcast from the wheelhouse of the tender *Charon's Ferry*, and she did not swear, which surprised her. She just stood there with the mug of tea going cold in her hand, watching the rain crawl down the glass, and felt something in her chest unlock that she hadn't known was locked.

Recorded testimony. Secured. An inquiry.

She thought about the file folder in the safe in her cabin. Her orders. Signed by a vice-president whose name she'd never been allowed to speak aloud on any channel, instructing her—in the careful, deniable language of such documents—to "ensure the recovery site yields no materials of evidentiary character." She'd read that sentence forty times over six years and told herself each time that it meant what she wanted it to mean: protect the company's interests. Recover assets. Standard salvage work.

It meant burn the evidence. It had always meant that. And she had always known it, the way you know a current under calm water.

Halloran went below, opened the safe, and took out the folder. There were three documents in it. The order itself. A payment schedule bearing her own signature against figures that would have bought her a house on the coast and a boat better than this one. And a list—she had never let herself read past the first two names—of people who had "impeded prior recovery efforts," with annotations in a neat corporate hand about outcomes.

She fed them into the woodstove one page at a time. The paper caught reluctantly, curled, went orange, went grey. The payment schedule burned last and longest, her own signature glowing briefly like a brand before it came apart.

She wasn't destroying evidence. She understood that clearly, standing there in the stove-light. Whatever inquiry Iles opened wouldn't need Halloran's copies; Oyelaran's voice and Vann's daughter's dive logs and the sealed cores themselves would bury the company without her help. What she was burning was her own leash. The moment those pages became ash, no one could prove she'd ever been offered the money—and no one could prove she'd ever have taken it.

That distinction mattered to her more than she could have explained, and she didn't try.

At dawn she called her deck crew together—six men and two women who'd followed her through three companies and one near-sinking—and told them the truth, or the shape of it. "The permit's dead. The job's dead. The company that hired us is going to be eaten alive by its own paperwork, and I'm not staying to watch." She paid them out of the float, every cent, and told them the *Ferry* was theirs if they wanted to run her north as a working tender, honest charters, honest wrecks.

"What about you?" asked Okonjo, her bosun, who had fished with her father and owed her nothing and stayed anyway.

"Me?" Halloran looked out at the channel, where the tide was turning, running gold-grey in the early light toward the open water. "I was never here."

By noon the *Charon's Ferry* was a rumor moving north. By evening she was a story salvagers would tell each other for years—the fixer who walked away from the biggest score on the coast the same week it collapsed. Nobody would quite believe it. That suited her fine. She had spent her whole career making herself useful to powerful people, and she found, with the coastline sliding past and the stove still smelling faintly of burnt paper, that she was looking forward to being useless to everyone.

**Juniper**

Juniper Vann watched the sixth tide turn from the cockpit of a shot-up Cessna parked on a gravel bar, and it was the strangest hour of her life, because nothing happened.

The concession expired at slack water. She'd written the time in her father's journal, in the margin beside his last entry, because it seemed right that the ending should live in the same book as the beginning. At 4:17 in the afternoon, permit CV-7734 died quietly somewhere in a registry database, and the bay where the *Meridian Star* lay under its shoal became, legally speaking, nobody's business again.

Overhead, far off to the southeast, she could hear it—a drone of engines, several engines, Corvane's fleet running down the sound after a decoy flight broadcasting a dead man's name. Sebastian had flown straight and loud and slow on purpose, daring them, and they had taken the bait like fish after a spinner, because rage makes fleets stupid. The last she'd heard on the handheld, Iles's voice had cut across their pursuit channel like a blade: *suspend all operations pending review.* And then, unbelievably, they had.

Nothing happened. The water lay flat. The generators two coves over had gone silent sometime in the night, and the dive barge sat dark on its anchor like a carcass. Gulls worked the tideline. Somewhere inland a loon called, that sound like grief and laughter at once, and Juniper sat on the wing strut with her father's journal open on her knees and let herself cry for the first time since the dock at the derelict camp—not for the fortune at the bottom of the bay, because there had never been one, but for the man who had spent twenty years building cairns and memorizing channels to guard a river he couldn't save and wouldn't stop trying to.

The evidence was down there. Two hundred feet of green-black water, and beneath the shoal, in the hold she'd swum through by torchlight with her heart hammering, the crates sat in the dark where she'd sent them—sunk deeper, wired to the wreck's own ribs, past the reach of any permit, any salvage clock, any company. Names on paper won't bring back the river, her father had said through the static. But neither will letting the paper be sold.

She had chosen protection over punishment, and she would spend years wondering if it was right, and she already knew she'd choose it again.

Her radio crackled. Sebastian's voice, ragged with adrenaline and low fuel and something that might have been joy: "Kestrel inbound, Juniper. Keep your light on. I'm coming home."

**Sebastian**

He put the Cessna down on the gravel bar with the fuel gauge showing fumes and three fresh holes in the airframe, and when his wheels stopped rolling he sat for a moment with his hands shaking on the yoke, doing the arithmetic of survival. Engine: good. Wings: intact. Self: fifteen years of hiding, spent in ninety minutes of broadcasting, and still breathing.

Juniper's lantern came up the beach toward him, bobbing, and he climbed out into the cold and met her halfway, and they stood there in the wind off the water not saying anything, because there are moments language isn't built for.

"You're insane," she said finally. "You know that. You flew at a salvage fleet with your name hanging out of the plane like a flag."

"It worked," he said.

"That's not the same as sane."

"No," he agreed. "But it's the first time in fifteen years I've been both things at once—alive and myself. I'd forgotten they could share a body."

Later, wrapped in blankets in the cabin of the ranger station where Iles had cleared a bunk for each of them, he gave his formal statement. Iles recorded it, timestamped it, sealed it, and then did something no official had done for him since Fort Alder: she shook his hand and used his real name, plainly, in front of witnesses, as though it were the most ordinary thing in the world.

"Oyelaran. Your license status will need to be regularized—there'll be hearings, paperwork, the usual machinery. But for the record: the Authority considers your testimony an act of public service, not a confession."

"The two aren't always different," he said.

"No," Iles said. "But tonight they are."

**Iles**

The inquiry opened on a Tuesday, which pleased Iles obscurely—beginnings should happen on ordinary days, so no one can claim the weather conspired.

She held the first session in the registry hall at Landfall, a room built for land disputes and fishing quotas, now filled beyond its benches with salvagers, reporters, and two lawyers from Corvane who arrived with the expressions of men attending the reading of a will that had gone badly wrong. She read the charges into the record: the dumping orders, the witness deaths, the deliberate scuttling, the six-year salvage operation conducted under false pretenses. She played the recordings. When Oyelaran's voice said *my callsign was Kestrel*, she watched the older of the two lawyers close his eyes as if in prayer.

The permit lapsed with nothing salvaged. Not a crate, not a core, not a gram of anything from the *Meridian Star*. In the ledger of the Concession Authority, the entry read, in her own handwriting: *Recovery yield: nil. Site status: closed.*

Nil. She had written the word slowly, liking the weight of it. Six years of fleet, fuel, and menace, and the entire enterprise had yielded nothing but its own exposure.

Corvane's counsel requested a stay. She denied it. They requested the identity of the informant who had tipped the fleet's premature move on the fifth tide—the leak in her own registry office. She slid a single sheet across the table: an internal audit, already complete, naming the junior clerk who had sold the coordinates for less than a month's wages. Small corruptions, she told the room, are how big ones get in. The clerk was escorted out past the reporters, and Iles noted the way the Corvane lawyers looked at him—not with anger but with recognition, the look of men seeing their own method in miniature.

By the end of the week the company's stock had shed a third of its value, three executives had retained personal counsel, and a prosecutor in the capital had requested the full file. Iles filed it herself, hand-delivered, and stood in the marble corridor afterward feeling the strange lightness of a woman who has spent nineteen years carrying a shovel and has finally, at the end, set it down.

* * *

**Juniper**

They let Juniper see her father on the ninth day.

Elias Vann lay small in the hospital bed at the coastal station, stitched and tubed and yellowed, but his eyes tracked her the whole way across the room, and when she took his hand his grip still had iron in it.

"Tell me," he rasped.

"The permit's dead. The inquiry's open. The evidence is under the shoal, wired to the ribs, where no dredge will ever find it unless the river wants it found." She leaned closer. "You kept your word, old man. So did I."

His breath rattled—laughing, she thought, or trying to. His eyes went past her shoulder to the doorway, where Sebastian stood holding his cap like a schoolboy, and Elias beckoned him in with two fingers.

"Fifteen years," the old prospector said, "and you end up keeping my promise better than I kept my own." He squeezed Sebastian's wrist. "Kestrel. Fly her north. She doesn't know the channels yet."

"She navigates by dead reckoning with a dead radio," Sebastian said. "She knows them better than I do."

"Good." Elias's eyes were closing. "Then you're both hired."

Juniper stayed until the nurses made her leave, and in the corridor she found Sebastian waiting, and outside the windows the tide was coming in over the flats, patient and enormous, erasing and revealing in the same motion.

"He's going to live," she said. "Broke. Disgraced in some circles. Famous in others. Absolutely impossible."

"Yes," Sebastian said, and there was something in his voice she was only beginning to learn how to read—relief with roots in it, deep ones.

"So," she said. "The plane's patched. The permits are expired. We're penniless, wanted as witnesses, and famous for all the wrong reasons." She looked up at him. "When do we leave?"

Sebastian smiled—the real one, the one that reached all the way up—and said, "As soon as the weather breaks."

* * *

**Iles**

On the last evening, Marguerite Iles walked out alone to the end of the Landfall dock. The tide was high and slack, the water like poured pewter, and far to the north a single engine droned—climbing, heading into the interior, growing smaller.

She raised her binoculars out of habit and caught it for a moment against the last light: a battered Cessna, two silhouettes in the seats, flying steady and true into the country that knew their names.

Iles lowered the glasses. In her coat pocket was a copy of Inquiry File 44-C, and clipped inside it, unofficially, a note she had written and rewritten four times and finally left plain:

*Witnesses relocated north. No further action recommended. Some names are worth the cost of knowing.*

Out on the water, the tide had begun, almost imperceptibly, to turn.
