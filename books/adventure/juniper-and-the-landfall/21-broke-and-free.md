## 21. Broke and Free

The plane looked worse than it flew.

That was the thing Sebastian kept coming back to, kneeling on the gravel bar with a rivet gun borrowed from a ranger station forty miles south, the morning light coming up gray and forgiving over the water. He'd seen aircraft come back from worse. He'd flown one out of the Muskeg country in '09 with a wing spar wrapped in friction tape and a prayer, and that prayer had held for eleven hundred miles. This was nothing. A punctured cowling, two holes through the port side you could put a fist through, a fuel line he'd spliced with hose clamps from a fishing tackle box. The bullet that had done the worst damage had gone through the fuselage where Juniper's shoulder would have been if she'd been sitting where she usually sat.

He didn't let himself think about that for longer than a breath.

The gravel bar was quiet now. The Corvane tender had gone north two days ago, running with its lights off, its permit dying by the hour, its captain presumably reading the same telexes everyone else was reading. The channels were empty of everything but tide and birds. Somewhere out past the headlands, the Meridian Star lay under her shoal with her hold full of poisoned truth, sealed under six fathoms of cold water, and no one was going to dig her up. Elias's wish, kept. The river's name, protected.

Sebastian set another rivet and thought about how strange quiet felt when you'd spent three weeks listening for engines.

* * *

Iles found him at the strip at midmorning.

She came up the beach on foot from the float she'd chartered, coat buttoned to the throat against the wind, carrying a leather folder the way other people carried weapons—carefully, and with a clear sense of what it could do. She stopped a few feet away and looked at the plane, at the raw aluminum patches, at Sebastian's hands black with sealant.

"You did this yourself," she said.

"Couldn't afford anyone else."

"That's not what I meant." She crouched, examined a patch seam, straightened up. "Fifteen years ago you couldn't have done this yourself. You had people for this."

"People leave," Sebastian said. "Or they die. Or they sign things they shouldn't and end up in the water." He wiped his hands on his thighs. "You came all this way to watch me do sheet metal?"

"I came because you asked me to." She opened the folder. "And because I don't take affidavits over the radio. Not ones like this."

The wind moved through the grass behind the strip. Out on the water, a loon called once and got no answer.

Iles laid the papers on the plane's wing, weighted the corners with four stones from the beach. It was old-fashioned and she knew it was old-fashioned; she did it anyway, so there would be no question later about screens and servers and who had touched what. The affidavit ran nine pages. Sebastian read every one of them standing in the wind, and Iles waited without hurrying him, which was its own kind of courtesy.

His name was in it eleven times. Not Kestrel. Not Oyelaran-the-bush-pilot-with-no-history. *Sebastian Adeyemi Oyelaran*, born Lagos, licensed 2004, witness for the prosecution in the matter of Meridian Consolidated Resource Group, 2010. The name he'd buried in the south was printed there in a clerk's font, plain as a road on a map.

He picked up the pen.

It should have been harder than it was. For fifteen years the signature had been the wall—the thing he would never do, the line between the man who testified and the man who vanished. He'd built a whole life on the far side of that line, a life measured in weather windows and fuel caches and airstrips where nobody asked questions. And now here was the pen, and here was the paper, and the line turned out to be made of nothing at all.

He signed. The letters came out steady. His hand remembered the name better than he did.

Iles took the pen back, blotted the page, and slid the copy across the wing to him. "Inquiry opens in three weeks. Corvane's counsel will say you're a disgraced pilot with a grudge and a forged map."

"They'll be half right."

"They'll be entirely wrong, and it will take them a year to admit it." She gathered her stones, tucked the folder inside her coat. "For what it's worth—I filed my report on your callsign the day after I met you. Before any of this. It was already in the system when the crates surfaced."

Sebastian looked at her. "Then why—"

"Because I wanted to see what you'd do when you knew I knew." She almost smiled; it didn't quite make it. "Most men run. You flew north into a salvage fleet. That told me more than your testimony will." She started down the beach, then paused. "Vann's awake, by the way. Woke yesterday evening. Asking for his daughter and, apparently, for you. In that order, though he'd deny it."

She went down to her float and was gone, and Sebastian stood alone on the strip with a patched airplane and a piece of paper with his real name on it, feeling lighter than he had any right to feel, and poorer than he'd been since he was nineteen years old.

* * *

The hospital was in Tern Harbour, three hours south in the Cessna, and it smelled like antiseptic and old paint and, underneath it, the sea.

Elias Vann occupied a corner room with a window facing the water, because Juniper had bullied the charge nurse into it and then, when that failed, had simply stood at the desk until it happened. She was there when Sebastian came down the corridor—sitting in the window chair, boots off, rifle nowhere in sight for the first time since he'd known her, a paperback facedown on her knee. She looked like she'd slept, finally, or at least stopped not sleeping.

"He's been asking for you since six," she said. "I told him you were fixing the plane."

"How much of that is true?"

"All of it. He just doesn't believe the plane comes before him." She studied Sebastian's face. "You signed it."

"I signed it."

"How does it feel?"

Sebastian considered the question honestly, the way he was learning to consider most questions now. "Like taking off into weather you can't turn back from. Bad right up until the wheels leave, and then—" He shrugged. "Then it's just flying."

Juniper nodded slowly, as if filing that away somewhere it mattered. Then she stood, put her boots on, and said, "Go on. He's been insufferable. He keeps doing arithmetic on the whiteboard about what the claim-stones would've been worth, and crossing it out, and doing it again. I think it's how he prays."

* * *

Elias was thinner than the man on the dock. The gut wound had taken something out of him that the surgeons said might grow back and might not, and his hands, those broad grubbing prospector's hands, lay quiet on the blanket like tools hung up for the season. But his eyes were the same—pale, patient, the eyes of a man who had spent forty years reading water and rock and had learned to wait out anything.

"Took you long enough," he rasped.

"You were unconscious."

"A man makes allowances." He coughed, waved off the offer of water until the coughing passed, then took it anyway. "Iles was here. Told me you signed your name on her paper. Real name."

"It's public record now. You could look me up."

"Don't own a computer. Don't intend to start." Elias looked at him for a long moment. "Kestrel. That was you. On the radio, all those years. The pilot who pulled the Wexler party out of the ice in '11. I heard that call go out. Everyone in the interior heard it." His mouth bent, not quite a smile. "I always wondered what happened to him. Turned out he was hauling my mail."

"Small country."

"Big sky." Elias shifted against the pillows, winced, settled. "Juniper tells me the stones are under the water. All of them. That you sank the crates too."

"The crates are staying down. The stones were never coming up."

"And Corvane?"

"Gone. Permit lapsed with nothing salvaged. Iles has the inquiry. Halloran—" Sebastian hesitated. "Halloran burned her orders and disappeared. I don't know if that makes her an enemy or something else. Ask me in a year."

Elias absorbed this, staring at the ceiling, at the window, at the gray line of the sea. When he spoke again his voice was lower, scraped down to the grain.

"Fifteen years I worked that wreck. Fifteen years telling myself I'd bring it up someday, get justice on paper, make them pay in a courtroom." He shook his head slowly. "An old man bleeding out on a dock will tell you the truth faster than a judge ever will. Names on paper don't bring back the river. I know that now. I knew it when I gave you the map, and I was ashamed of it, because giving it to you meant I wanted the treasure after all. Some part of me always wanted it." His eyes came around to Sebastian, sharp and clear. "You did the thing I couldn't do. You let it stay buried."

"You asked me to get you out alive," Sebastian said. "Or finish what you started. I refused the promise, remember."

"I remember."

"Well. I'm keeping it anyway. Both halves. You're alive, and it's finished." He sat down in the chair Juniper had vacated, leaned forward, elbows on knees. "That's the whole of it, Elias. The promise is kept. Letter and spirit. You can stop doing arithmetic now."

Elias Vann looked at him for a long time. Then he laughed—a dry, cracked sound, half cough, that went on until a nurse poked her head in and left again, deciding whatever she decided. When it was done, the old man's eyes were wet, and he didn't wipe them, and neither of them mentioned it.

"Broke," Elias said at last. "Both of us. Broke as church mice."

"Free," Sebastian said.

"That too." Elias closed his eyes, and for a moment Sebastian thought he'd gone to sleep. Then: "The plane. How bad?"

"Cowling, skin, one fuel line. She'll fly."

"She always flies. That's what I liked about her." A pause. "Where are you two pointed?"

Sebastian glanced toward the window, where Juniper had appeared on the walk below, looking out at the harbor with her hands in her pockets, waiting without waiting. "North," he said. "There's a strip outside Fort Meridian needs a supply run twice a month. Nobody else wants it. Weather's bad, pay's worse."

Elias smiled, properly this time, and it changed his whole face, made him briefly the man from the photographs Juniper kept folded in her journal. "Take my daughter with you," he said. "She navigates better than you fly, and she'll keep you honest. God knows somebody has to."

"I was going to ask her anyway."

"No, you weren't. You were going to fly north alone and feel noble about it." Elias's eyes drifted shut. "Ask her out loud. That's the whole lesson, boy. Everything else I learned in sixty-eight years, you can figure out yourself."

* * *

Sebastian walked out through the corridor, past the nurses' station, down the stairs, and out into the wind off the harbor. Juniper fell into step beside him without either of them arranging it, and they walked the length of the seawall together while the tide came in below them, filling the channels, covering the flats, erasing and rewriting the shoreline the way it always had and always would.

"So," she said eventually. "North."

"If you want."

"I want." She kicked a stone off the seawall, watched it drop. "But I'm navigating. And we're stopping at the cairn on the way up. Tide Four marker. I want to build it higher."

Sebastian looked at her—at the set of her jaw, so like her father's, and at the rest of her face, which was entirely her own—and understood that the wilderness had done what it always does. It forgets less than people do. But it also forgives more.

"He's awake," Sebastian said. "He's asking for you."

"I know. I've been sitting with him for two days." Juniper smiled, small and real. "He means you should come back in and say goodbye properly. He'll pretend he doesn't care. He cares enormously."

They turned back toward the hospital together, two broke people in a rich country, and above the harbor the sky was clearing from the north, fair flying weather all the way to Fort Meridian, and somewhere out on the water the sixth tide was turning, and turning, and would not stop turning for anything—neither for Corvane nor for grief nor for the past—but only ever forward, toward landfall, toward home.
