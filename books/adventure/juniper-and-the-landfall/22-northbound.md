## 22. Northbound

The hospital smelled of carbolic and old paint, and Elias Vann had never looked so much like himself.

He was propped against the pillows with the particular ferocity of a man who has been told he nearly died and refuses to be impressed by it. The gut wound had closed to a red seam under fresh dressings. His hands—those enormous, cracked, map-drawing hands—lay folded on the blanket like tools set down for the night. When Juniper came in he did not smile, because Vanns did not smile at arrivals; they simply made room.

Sebastian stood in the doorway a moment longer than he needed to. Fifteen years of practice said: don't be seen in rooms like this, don't leave names on visitor logs, don't give the country anything it can hold. But the country already held everything now. His name sat on Iles's affidavit in his own handwriting, legible as a scar. There was nothing left to protect by standing in doorways.

So he came in.

"Oyelaran." Elias's voice was gravel dragged over gravel. "You look terrible."

"You should see the other guy."

"I heard about the plane." Elias's eyes went to the window, where the harbor glittered under a north wind scrubbed clean. "Shot up on a gravel bar by Corvane's gunner. And you flew her out anyway."

"She complained the whole way."

"They do that." A silence opened between them, the kind that in another life would have been a wall. Now it was just a room they both stood in. "Iles told me what you signed."

"Did she."

"Said you wrote your whole name out like a man signing a confession and a birth certificate at the same time." Elias almost smiled. It cost him something, and he paid it gladly. "Took you long enough."

Juniper pulled the chair up beside the bed and sat, and Sebastian took the one by the window, and for an hour they talked about nothing—the weather, the price of avgas, the nurse who confiscated Juniper's rifle at the door and returned it with an apologetic note about regulations—and it was the easiest hour Sebastian had spent in fifteen years, because there was nothing left to hide behind and nothing left to hide.

When they rose to go, Elias caught Sebastian's sleeve. His grip was weaker than it should have been and stronger than anyone expected.

"The river," he said. "It'll come back. Maybe not in my time. Maybe not in yours. But water forgives faster than people do. Remember that, when you're deciding what kind of man to be tomorrow."

"I'm not deciding," Sebastian said. "I already signed."

Elias let go of his sleeve. "Then fly north," he said, "and stop looking over your shoulder. There's nothing back there but your own shadow, and it's tired of following you."

* * *

The airstrip at dawn was a strip of silver frost and gold light, and the Cessna sat on it like a patient after surgery.

Sebastian had worked three days on her. The bullet holes along the port side were patched with aluminum and rivets borrowed from a cannery welder who asked no questions except whether the coffee in the thermos was for him. The new fabric over the wing spar was a slightly different shade of white, so that from ten feet away the plane looked like a quilt stitched by someone in a hurry—which, Sebastian thought, was exactly what she was, and exactly what he'd been, and maybe that was honest in a way factory-fresh skin never could be.

Juniper was already there when he arrived, loading the right-hand seat.

She had packed the way she did everything: without ceremony and without waste. One duffel. Her father's journal, wrapped in oilcloth. The rifle broken down in its case, because Fort Meridian was a long way and the interior was longer. And on top of it all, squared carefully against the seat frame, half a map—her half—creased soft as cloth from handling.

She saw him looking at it.

"I keep thinking I should throw it away," she said. "The wreck's sunk. The stones are down where they belong. The coordinates are worthless now."

"Keep it."

"Why?"

"Because it's not a treasure map anymore." He ran his thumb along the fold. "It's a record of how far a man walked to bury something that mattered. That's worth more than claim-stones. That's the only kind of inheritance that doesn't rot."

Juniper looked at him for a long moment, and then she put the map back in the duffel, on top, where she could reach it.

They ran the preflight together, and it felt like a conversation. She checked the fuel samples while he drained the sumps; she worked the flaps while he watched the surfaces move; she read the checklist aloud in a voice that had learned, somewhere over six tides and two hundred miles of channels, to trust the person holding the other end of it. When he waggled the ailerons she called out their travel like a woman reading scripture she'd finally decided to believe.

"Clear prop," she said, and he started the engine, and the old girl coughed twice and caught, settling into that rough harmonic that every bush pilot knows is either music or a warning, and today it was music.

They taxied out past the fuel drums, past the registry office with its dark windows and its new notice taped to the door—CONCESSION 44-C EXPIRED, PENDING INQUIRY—and out onto the runway with the whole harbor spread below them, gray-green and turning.

"Fort Meridian direct?" Juniper asked, hand on the throttle.

"Direct." He looked north, where the mountains stood up white out of the morning haze, range behind range behind range, the whole stacked interior of the country waiting with its old indifference. "Weather's fair all the way. Iles says the inquiry won't need us for a month, maybe more. Halloran's gone to ground. Corvane's lawyers are busy explaining to their board how they lost a salvage permit, a fixer, and a public-relations war to a broke pilot and a prospector's daughter."

"And Elias?"

"Elias," Sebastian said, "told me this morning that if we're not back inside two weeks he's checking himself out of the hospital and walking home, and that we'd both deserve whatever happens to us."

"That sounds like him."

"He also said—" Sebastian paused. This part he'd turned over all night, and it still didn't sit easy, which meant it was true. "He said the Meridian Star was never mine to carry and never yours either. We carried it long enough. Time to put it down."

Juniper nodded once, slow, the way she nodded at weather reports she intended to test personally.

"Then let's go north," she said, and pushed the throttle forward herself.

* * *

The wheels left the ground at 7:14 by the strip clock, and the country came up to meet them.

Sebastian climbed out over the harbor, banked once—not waving goodbye to anything, just showing the town the airplane, showing the town the pilot, letting anyone who cared to look see Sebastian Oyelaran flying openly in daylight with his name on the tail and his face in the window—and then set course north, straight across the sound, into the light.

Juniper settled into the right-hand seat like she'd been born to it. Maybe she had; she was her father's daughter, and Vanns belonged in moving things—boats, planes, arguments. She flew the radio first, working through the frequencies, and when the Fort Meridian FSS answered her call with a crisp acknowledgment of *Cessna Seven-Four-Bravo, squawking visible, report overhead*, she glanced sideways at Sebastian with something that was almost laughter.

"They know who we are."

"They know who I am." He leveled off at four thousand five hundred, in the smooth cold air above the haze. "That was the whole point."

Below them the coastline unspooled—the channels where they'd run from Halloran's tender, the bay where the dive barge had sat with its generators muttering, the shoal that showed itself only at low tide, and beneath it, forty feet down in the green dark, sealed crates full of poison and proof, sinking slowly into the silt that would hold them better than any court ever could. The tide was high now. You couldn't see the shoal at all. In a season the sand would shift over it. In a decade the kelp would take it. The wilderness forgets less than people do—but it also forgives more, and it had already begun forgiving, quietly, the way water does, without being asked.

"What happens to you now?" Juniper asked, over the engine's drone. "After the inquiry. After everything."

Sebastian considered it honestly, watching the mountains come on.

"Fifteen years ago I would have told you I'd disappear again. New name, new strip, somewhere south where nobody reads northern newspapers." He shook his head. "But Iles said something to me when I signed the affidavit. She said, *you can't outrun a name forever, Mr. Oyelaran—you can only decide which one you're running toward.*"

"And which one are you?"

"Mine." He said it plainly, testing the weight of it, finding it lighter than expected. "Kestrel was a good callsign. It kept me alive. But Kestrel was a bird that only ever flew away from things." He looked over at her. "I'm done flying away."

Juniper was quiet for a while. Below, a river delta braided itself silver across the tundra, and a herd of caribou moved along a ridge like a slow brown river of their own, going wherever caribou have always gone, indifferent to permits and concessions and the names of men.

"My father used to say the interior keeps books on everybody," she said finally. "That you can lie to a judge and lie to a priest, but you can't lie to a country—it watches too long." She tapped the window, down at the land rolling past. "He said that like it was a threat. I think he meant it as an invitation."

"It is," Sebastian said. "It always was. I just wasn't ready to accept."

The flight north took four hours, and they talked most of it—not about the wreck, not about Corvane, not about the crates sleeping in the silt, but about small things. Where the best fishing was above Fort Meridian. Which strips flooded in spring. Whether Elias would actually stay in the hospital or turn up at the fuel cache one morning with a rifle and a grudge and a healed-over scar and no explanation. Juniper laughed twice, really laughed, and each time Sebastian felt something in his chest unlock a little further, some bolt that had been drawn tight since a courtroom in the south and a graveyard with too many fresh mounds in it.

Over the last range, the Fort Meridian valley opened up before them—the strip, the town, the smoke rising straight in the still air, the whole small human settlement tucked into the vast green shoulder of the country like a stitch in leather.

"Cessna Seven-Four-Bravo, Fort Meridian traffic, cleared to land, wind calm," the radio said, and Juniper read back the clearance in a steady voice, and Sebastian brought the plane down through the last thousand feet with his hands light on the yoke and his heart doing something it hadn't done at a landing in fifteen years.

It wasn't relief. Relief was for escapes, and this wasn't an escape.

It was arrival.

The wheels touched, rolled, slowed. They taxied in past the fuel drums, past the little terminal with its peeling paint, and shut down, and the silence after the engine was enormous and friendly, filled with ravens and wind and the tick of cooling metal.

Juniper unbuckled and looked around at the valley, at the mountains holding it, at the sky going on forever northward over terrain no map had ever finished describing.

"So," she said. "What do we do first? We're broke."

"We've got a plane, a full tank, and two weeks before my brother-in-law-to-be—" he stopped, corrected himself, grinned—"before Elias discharges himself and comes looking for us. There's a medicine run to the Kettle River post that pays in fish and gratitude. And after that, I hear the interior needs pilots who aren't hiding from anybody."

Juniper slung her duffel, and the half-map rode on top of it, and she stood there a moment in the northern light with the wind pulling at her hair, and she looked—Sebastian thought—not like a woman who had lost everything, and not like a woman who had won, but like a woman who had finally landed.

"The country knows our names now," she said.

"All of them," Sebastian agreed. "Every one we ever buried."

"Good." She started up the dock toward the terminal, then paused and looked back at him, and the sun was full on her face and there was nothing between her and the day. "Then let's give it something worth remembering."

Sebastian Oyelaran locked the plane, shouldered his bag, and followed her north into the country that had watched him for fifteen years and waited, patient as water, for him to come home.

Behind them the tide turned, and turned, and turned—forgetting nothing, forgiving everything—and ahead the mountains stood open, and the fair weather ran on all the way to the edge of the map, and past it.
