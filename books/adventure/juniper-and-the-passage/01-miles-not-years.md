## 1. Miles, Not Years

The fog came off the water before dawn, the way it always did in Corrow Bay, rolling in slow and gray as something breathing. Ivo Pemberton stood at the rail of his boat with a mug of coffee gone cold in his hand and watched it eat the harbor whole — the masts, the breakwater, the crooked line of rooftops along the quay — until there was nothing left of the world but the sound of water slapping hull and the gulls crying somewhere above him like souls that hadn't found their way down.

He had been awake since four. He was always awake by four. Sleep had stopped being a thing he did and become a thing he endured, an hour or two of dark he passed through like a diver passing through cold strata, and then he surfaced into the same gray morning as the one before it. He'd stopped counting the mornings years ago. That was the trick of it, he'd learned. You didn't count the miles if you wanted to keep walking.

*Vantage*, he thought, and drank the cold coffee anyway.

The navy freighter lay in forty fathoms two miles off the point, down where the light went from green to blue to a darkness that swallowed the beam of any lamp you cared to carry. She'd gone down eleven years ago with a crew of thirty-one and a cargo manifest stamped sealed by the Admiralty, and for eleven years she had lain on the shelf undisturbed while the lawyers argued about salvage rights and the families argued about pensions and the sea argued with nobody at all, because the sea never needed to argue. It just waited.

Now the courts were settled, the consortium held the paper, and the consortium had hired Ivo Pemberton to strip her. Simple work. Catalogue the hold, cut free what was worth cutting, mark the rest for the barge crews. Three weeks, maybe four, weather permitting. Good money by Corrow standards, and money was the only language Ivo still spoke fluently.

He checked his gauges without looking at them, out of habit more than need, then hauled the anchor and let the little boat drift out through the fog toward the point. The engine muttered under him, a low animal complaint. He knew every sound she made the way he knew the sounds of his own body, which was to say intimately and without affection.

* * *

Forty feet down, the water turned cold enough to ache. Eighty feet, the green died and the blue began. At a hundred and twenty, Ivo leveled out and hung in the open water column with his lamp beam cutting a cone through the murk, and below him, vast and patient as a sleeping animal, the *Vantage* rose up out of the dark.

She was three hundred feet of rusted steel lying on her starboard side, her superstructure furred with plumose anemones, her hull streaked orange and black where eleven years of corrosion had painted their own history over the navy's gray. A forest of dead men's wire rigging trailed from her rails like kelp. Schools of rockfish moved through her shadow in slow silver rivers. She looked less like a wreck than like a reef that had once been a ship, and Ivo felt the old familiar tightening in his chest — not fear, exactly, and not awe either, but something between them that had no clean name. The feeling of standing in a church built by something that did not care whether you prayed.

He descended along her port rail, following the shot line, and paused at the breach.

It was the first thing they always showed you in the survey photographs: the gash amidships, six feet across, edges peeled outward like petals. Torpedo damage, the official report said. Enemy action in the last year of the war, the official report said. Ivo had read the report twice, and both times he had looked at the photographs afterward and thought the same thought he thought now, hanging in the cold water with his lamp playing over the torn steel: *petals peel outward.*

Torpedoes pushed in. Explosions inside a hull blew outward, yes — but this tear had the wrong geometry, the wrong lips, the wrong everything. He'd seen blast damage on a dozen wrecks. This wasn't it. This looked like a door opened deliberately from within, or a seam split by charges set against the inside of the plating, placed by someone who understood ships well enough to know exactly where to put them so she'd go down slow and level and quiet.

He shook the thought off the way you shake off cold water finding a seam in your suit. It wasn't his problem. His problem was cargo, and cargo was what paid for coffee and diesel and the roof over his head, and a man who asked too many questions about other people's cargo in Corrow Bay tended to find his mooring fees doubled and his work drying up, when he found anything at all.

He swam the length of the hull to the main cargo hatch, worked the dogs loose — they turned easier than he expected, someone had been down here recently, or the sea had been kinder than usual — and dropped through into the hold.

* * *

Inside, the *Vantage* was a cathedral of silt and silence.

His lamp swept across the deck: collapsed derricks, a tangle of chain, the pale bones of crates long since rotted open. Silt lay over everything like gray snow, softening the angles, erasing the details. Fish scattered ahead of his light in nervous flickers. His own bubbles rose past his face and vanished into the black overhead, and in the silence between breaths he could hear the ship herself — that deep, faint groan and tick of a steel hull settling by fractions, the sound of a thing dying so slowly it would take another century to finish.

He started forward, working methodically, the way he did everything. Slate in hand, lamp in the other, marking positions. Forward section: crated machinery, mostly stripped of value by rot, some brass fittings worth recovering. Midships: the manifest said baled cotton and tin ingots, and indeed there were bales — or what had been bales, now just shapeless masses of fiber fused with silt — and there were ingots, stacked in neat pyramids against the starboard bulkhead, each one furred with oxidation, each one exactly where the paperwork said it should be.

That was the first thing that bothered him. Everything was exactly where the paperwork said it should be.

Eleven years underwater, a sinking, whatever chaos attended a ship going down — and the hold looked arranged. Tidied. The cotton bales sat in rows too straight for cargo loaded in wartime haste. The tin pyramids were spaced with a regularity no stevedore ever managed. It was as if someone had staged the hold to match the manifest, the way a man tidies a room before company arrives.

Ivo hung in the water beside the tin ingots and let his lamp travel slowly along them. Then he reached out, hooked his fingers under the top ingot, and lifted.

It came away too easily. And it was wrong in his hands — the balance of it, the density. Tin is heavy, heavier than a man expects, but this had the wrong heaviness, a denser, colder weight, and when he scraped his knife across the furred surface, the bright metal underneath was not the dull gray-white of tin but something harder, paler, with a faint warm undertone to its shine.

He turned it over twice in the lamplight. He was not a metallurgist. But he had stripped enough wrecks to know what common metals felt like, and this was not tin, and it was not lead, and it was not anything that belonged in a navy freighter's declared cargo.

He set it back precisely where it had been, aligned with the pyramid's angle, and wrote nothing on his slate. Some things you didn't write down. Writing made them real, and real things had consequences, and Ivo Pemberton had spent eleven years arranging his life around the principle that consequences were for men who expected something from tomorrow.

He moved aft.

* * *

The aft hold was where the discrepancies lived.

The manifest — the copy the consortium's clerk had handed him with a smile and a handshake, the copy he'd read by lamplight in his cabin until he could recite it — listed the aft sections as mail, personal effects, and general stores. Light cargo. Nothing worth the cost of recovery. The clerk had actually told him he could skip it if time ran short.

The aft hold was full.

Not mail. Not effects. Crates — heavy-gauge, steel-banded, stenciled with numbers instead of names — stacked three high and lashed to the deck rings with new-looking cable, cable that had no business looking new after eleven years of immersion. Ivo played his lamp along the nearest stack and counted. Forty crates visible, and the stack ran back into the dark beyond his light. Each crate maybe four feet by three. Each one heavy — he could tell by the way the lashings bowed, by the way the lower crates had bitten into the deck plates beneath them.

Stenciled numbers. He copied three of them onto his slate, working slowly, the characters emerging under his glove like something surfacing. They meant nothing to him. But they weren't naval stock numbers — he knew those formats, everyone in the salvage trade did — and they weren't shipping codes either. They were something else. Something private.

And here was the second thing, the thing that made the cold move from the water into his chest: the crates were dry.

Not dry-dry; nothing down here was dry. But the silt lay thin on their tops, thinner than on the deck beside them, and the steel bands showed almost no rust, and when he brushed his glove across the nearest stencil, the paint came away on his fingers in flakes — fresh flakes, bright under the lamp.

Somebody had been in this hold. Recently. Somebody had cleaned these crates, or replaced them, or — and the thought arrived fully formed, unwelcome, patient — somebody was still using this hold. Eleven years after the ship sank. Using a drowned freighter forty fathoms down as a warehouse, as a dead-drop, as a place to store whatever these numbered things were, in the one place in the world where no honest man would ever think to look.

Ivo backed away from the crates, slowly, the way you back away from a dog you've caught watching you. His bubbles streamed up and he barely noticed them. His heart was going harder than the depth warranted, and he made himself take long, deliberate breaths, because a man who panicked at a hundred and forty feet died at a hundred and forty feet, and he had no intention of dying today. Dying required expecting something from tomorrow, and he had given that up years ago, and he was surprised — genuinely, absurdly surprised — to discover how much he apparently still wanted to keep not doing it.

He finished the survey. That was the discipline of him, the thing that had kept him alive and solvent when better men had washed out: you finished the job. He catalogued the forward holds, marked the brass, noted the condition of the plating for the cutting crews. He avoided the aft hold after that, except once, at the end, when he let himself drift past the hatch opening for one last look.

The lamp beam touched the crates one final time. Forty of them, lashed and waiting, patient as the sea itself.

Then he went up.

* * *

The surface was gray and ordinary and smelled of salt and diesel, and Ivo sat on the deck of his boat stripping his gear with hands that were almost steady, and told himself the story he intended to live by: tin ingots, cotton bales, mail sacks rotted to pulp, a hold full of nothing. He would write the report tonight. He would write it clean and boring and exactly as detailed as the contract required and not one word more. The consortium got its salvage list. The *Vantage* kept her secrets. Everybody stayed paid, and nobody stayed curious.

It was a good story. He'd been telling himself stories like it for eleven years, ever since the day the telegram came about his father, and he'd gotten good at believing them for hours at a stretch.

But sitting there in the fog with the wreck's buoy bobbing behind him, he kept seeing the geometry of that breach. Petals peeling outward. And the dry crates. And the numbers, marching down his slate in his own handwriting like a confession he hadn't written yet.

He fired up the stove and made coffee he wouldn't drink and pulled the contract papers out of the chart drawer, and there it was, the manifest copy, the consortium's seal crisp in the lamplight. He read it again, line by line, the way a man reads a letter from someone he suspects of lying. Baled cotton. Tin ingots. Mail and effects. General stores.

Thirty-one men had gone down with this ship. He'd read their names too, the first night, the way he read all crew lists — a habit, a penance, he'd never decided which. Captain Reyes. Chief Engineer Okafor. Able Seaman Hale.

Hale. He remembered pausing on that one, for no reason he could name. There had been a Hales in Corrow as long as there'd been a Corrow. Prospectors, boatmen, troublemakers. He'd wondered idly if the sailor was kin to any of them, and then he'd poured another drink and forgotten it, the way he forgot most things, the way a man forgets things when forgetting is the load-bearing wall of his whole life.

The fog was lifting off the water now, thinning into rags, and through it he could see the far shore taking shape — the quay, the taverns, the crooked town where everybody knew everybody's business except the business that mattered. He had a report to write and a week of easy work ahead and then payment, and payment meant another season, and another season meant more miles, and miles were all he'd ever asked for. Miles, not years. You didn't count the miles if you wanted to keep walking.

He was coiling the anchor line when the other boat came out of the fog.

It came fast, a hard-chined launch with its canvas rigged down and two men in it, neither of them wearing the bored faces of fisheries inspectors. It swung wide around his mooring, slowed, and the man at the wheel looked at Ivo — looked at him the way you look at a mark, or a problem — for three full seconds before opening the throttle again and running on toward the point. Toward the *Vantage*'s buoy.

Ivo stood in his own wake with the wet rope in his hands and watched them go, and the cold that had left his chest on ascent came back and settled in for good.

He thought: *they know I've been down there.*

He thought: *those crates were dry.*

And beneath both thoughts, older and quieter, in the voice of a man who has stopped expecting anything from life and is therefore never quite prepared when life expects something from him:

*You should have written it on the slate.*
