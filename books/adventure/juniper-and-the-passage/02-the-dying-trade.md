## 2. The Dying Trade

The Saltglass Tavern sat at the end of the harbor road like something the sea had coughed up and refused to take back. Its timbers were black with salt rot, its windows fogged from within by decades of pipe smoke and wet wool and the breath of men who had nothing better to do between tides than drink. Ivo Pemberton had been coming here since he was old enough to pass for eighteen, and in all those years the place had never once surprised him.

Tonight it did.

He came in smelling of the Vantage still — brine and rust and the cold iron sweetness of deep water — with his logbook under his arm and three weeks of salvage figures inked in his careful hand. The room was half full, which for a Tuesday in the dying days of autumn meant full enough. He took his usual stool at the far end of the bar, where the lamplight didn't quite reach, and ordered whatever was in the jug.

That was when he noticed her.

She sat alone at a corner table near the hearth, and she was wrong for the room in every particular. Too old for the company, too clean despite the dust on her, and dressed like no woman Ivo had ever seen in the Saltglass: canvas coat, men's boots worn through at the heel, a wool cap pulled low over hair gone white as gull feather. A prospector's kit lay at her feet — pick head wrapped in oilcloth, a coil of rope, a leather satchel that she kept one hand on even as she drank, as though the table might steal it if she blinked.

She was coughing when he first looked at her. Not the shallow bark of a cold. This was deep, wet, structural — the sound of a body coming apart from the inside. She bent over it, shoulders shaking, and when she straightened she wiped her mouth with the back of her glove, and in the firelight Ivo saw the red there plain as paint.

He looked away. It wasn't his business. Nothing in this town was his business except the wreck offshore and the money it would put in his pocket, and he'd learned long ago that looking too closely at other people's suffering was a habit that got a man drowned. You saw a drowning swimmer, you went after them, and then there were two bodies instead of one. His father's voice, or maybe just the shape of a voice he'd invented because he couldn't remember the real one. Either way, the lesson held.

He drank. He ate the stew, which was mostly barley and regret. He opened his logbook and began transcribing the day's figures into the ledger he kept for the naval survey office, because the navy paid by the item catalogued and did not pay for sloppy arithmetic.

"You're the diver."

The voice came from behind him, roughened by the cough but steady underneath, the voice of someone used to being heard across canyons. He turned. The old woman had come to stand at his elbow without making a sound, which should have been impossible in boots like hers on floorboards like these. Up close she was smaller than she'd seemed, and older — seventy if she was a day — but her eyes were pale gray and absolutely level, and they looked at him the way he imagined he looked at a hull breach: measuring, deciding whether it could be patched or whether the whole thing was going down.

"Ivo Pemberton," he said. "Salvage and recovery."

"Juniper Hale." She said it like a name that had once meant something and knew it didn't anymore. "You've been out at the Vantage three weeks."

"That's public knowledge. Harbor office posts the contracts."

"And you've found things down there that aren't in your manifest."

The ledger under his hands suddenly felt very heavy. "Ma'am—"

"Don't ma'am me, boy, I'm dying, not delicate." She pulled out the stool beside him and sat, slowly, wincing, and set her satchel on the bar between them. Her hand stayed flat on its lid. "I watched you row out this morning. Watched you come back. Three weeks, same hours, same little boat. A man that methodical notices things. Cargo in a hold that shouldn't be there. Compartments sealed from the inside."

Ivo kept his face still. It was a skill he'd practiced most of his life, keeping his face still, and it served him now. But his pulse ticked up against his collar. *Compartments sealed from the inside.* Nobody had said that aloud to him. He hadn't said it aloud to himself, not exactly — he'd written *discrepancy* in his notes and left it at that, the way you leave a door shut in a house you don't own.

"I catalogue what I find," he said. "I report what I catalogue. What the navy does with the report is the navy's business."

"The navy." She smiled, and it was a terrible smile, thin as a knife edge, with blood on the teeth of it somewhere though he couldn't see where. "Boy, I've been in these mountains and along this coast longer than that ship was afloat. I know what the navy is. I also know what it isn't. And what it isn't is honest about the *Vantage*."

The tavern noise went on around them — dice clattering, a fiddle tuning badly, the landlady shouting at her boy about the wood — but Ivo felt the space around their two stools go quiet, the way water goes quiet over something deep.

"Why are you telling me this?" he asked.

"Because you're the only man in this room who's been inside her." She leaned closer. Up close he could smell her — pine pitch and woodsmoke and underneath it something sweetish and wrong, the smell of a body eating itself. "And because I need a courier, and you have a boat, legs that work, and—" her eyes flicked over him, cataloguing the way he catalogued wrecks, "—no family to ask questions. Am I right? No wife. No mother living. Nobody."

It wasn't a question, so he didn't answer it. But the accuracy of it landed somewhere below his ribs, in the hollow place he kept meaning to fill and never did.

"I need a package carried," Juniper Hale said. "Over the Serrat Range. To my daughter."

"The passes close in—" He stopped himself doing the arithmetic aloud, but she caught it anyway.

"Three weeks. Yes. I know exactly how long. I've been counting since summer." She coughed again, shorter this time, and pressed her glove to her mouth, and when she took it away there was fresh red on the leather, dark in the lamplight. She didn't hide it. She let him see it, deliberately, the way you show a buyer the flaw in a stone before naming your price. "I won't make the crossing myself. We both know that. I might not make the week."

"Then take it to the magistrate, or the mail coach, or—"

"The mail coach goes to Corren's Ford and stops. My daughter lives past Corren's Ford, up the Kestrel valley, and the only way to her before snowfall is on foot, over the Serrat, and the only people who know the way are dead or dying." She tapped the satchel. "Or drew the map themselves."

"You're a prospector."

"I was a lot of things." For a moment something moved behind her eyes — grief, or memory, or both wearing the same coat. "I was married to a man who worked the high country. I raised a daughter in a cabin at four thousand feet. I have crossed the Serrat in every month God saw fit to invent, and I drew every route I ever survived onto paper because I don't trust my own memory anymore and I trust other people's less." She unlatched the satchel — Ivo noticed her fingers shook, not from age but effort — and drew out an oilskin packet, tied with waxed cord, and laid it on the bar between his ledger and his cup.

It was smaller than he expected. That was the thing he remembered afterward, in all the long bad nights that followed: how small it was. An oilskin packet, hand-tied, lighter than a loaf of bread. Everything that followed came out of that.

"What's in it?" he asked.

"A map. A letter. Some papers." She said it evenly, but he heard the weight she was putting on *some papers*, the way a mule driver says *grain* when the packsaddle holds silver. "You don't open it. You don't read it. You carry it to Mara Hale, Kestrel valley, past Corren's Ford, and you put it in her hands. Not under her door. In her hands. And you tell her—" and here, for the first time, the old woman's voice cracked, a fine hairline fracture running through the bedrock of it, "—you tell her it's from her mother, and that her mother is sorry, and that she'll understand when she reads it. Even if she hates me. Especially if she hates me."

Ivo looked at the packet. Then at the woman. Then at the packet again.

"How much?" he said, because that was easier than everything else he was thinking.

"Nothing."

"Nobody hires a courier for nothing."

"This isn't hiring. This is asking." She fixed him with those gray eyes. "Though I'll pay your passage, your gear, and a hundred crowns on delivery, if it salves your pride to call it wages. I've got gold in the hills and no use for it where I'm going."

A hundred crowns. Half again what the navy contract would pay him, and the navy contract was nearly done. He should have said yes immediately. Instead he heard himself say, "Why me? You've known me ten minutes. I could be anyone. I could be working for whoever you're afraid of."

"Are you?"

"No."

"Then we agree on something." She almost smiled. "I watched you dive, boy. Third week, second day — you came up fast, too fast, off the stern quarter, and you hung on the gunwale a minute getting your breath, and then you went back down. Most men don't go back down. Most men decide the job will keep till tomorrow." She touched the packet once, lightly, the way you'd touch a sleeping child. "You went back down. That's the whole of my reasoning. A man who goes back down keeps his promises whether he means to or not."

The fire popped. Somewhere behind them the fiddle found its key and began a tune about a girl drowned in a millpond, because coastal taverns only knew two kinds of songs and this was the wetter one.

Ivo turned his cup slowly on the bar. He thought about the hold of the *Vantage*, the crates stenciled with markings that matched nothing on any manifest he'd been given, the compartment bulkheads with their locking wheels spun tight from the inside. He thought about the survey officer in Corren's Ford who'd taken his last report without reading it, eyes sliding sideways, and told him to finish the job and file the rest in duplicate. He thought about the hollow place below his ribs, and how a hundred crowns wouldn't fill it, and how nothing would, probably, and how that had stopped bothering him sometime in the last few years without his noticing.

"I'm not a good bet," he said finally. "I want that understood. I don't make promises I intend to keep. I make promises to get people to stop talking."

"I know," Juniper Hale said placidly. "Your kind always says that. It's how I know you'll keep it."

"That makes no sense."

"Neither does scuttling your own freighter in six fathoms of honest water, but the navy managed it." She pushed the packet an inch toward him. "Take it or leave it. But decide tonight. I haven't got many nights, and I'd rather spend the ones I've got left writing to my daughter than shopping for couriers."

He should have left it. He knew that even then, some animal part of him knew it, the way a diver knows the current has shifted before his instruments tell him — a pressure change, a coldness in the water, a wrongness. Take the job, deliver the map, collect the coin, walk away. Simple. Clean. The kind of job he'd built his whole life around: miles, not years; depth, not depth of anything else.

Instead he heard himself say, "If I take this, I take it my own way. I choose the route. I choose the timing. If I decide the pass is closing too fast, I turn back, and you get your map returned and your hundred crowns stay in the hills. Agreed?"

"Agreed," she said, too quickly, and he understood that she would have agreed to anything, that the terms were theater, that she had chosen him the way you choose a plank in a shipwreck — not because it's good wood, but because it's floating and you're not.

He picked up the packet. It weighed almost nothing. He untied nothing, opened nothing, just felt the oilskin cool and slick against his palm, and felt the small absurd weight of another person's dying settle into his hands.

"One more thing," Juniper said. She leaned close, and her breath was thin and smelled of copper. "When you give it to her — tell her I crossed the Serrat eleven times and came home every time. Tell her the twelfth doesn't count against me."

"I don't even know what that means."

"She will." She closed his fingers over the packet with both of her hands, and her grip was stronger than it had any right to be, the grip of a woman who had swung a pick at altitude for fifty years. "Thank you, Ivo Pemberton."

"I haven't promised anything."

"Yes, you have." She released him, gathered her satchel, and stood, swaying slightly, catching herself on the bar. "Come by the Widow Marsh's boarding house tomorrow evening. I'll draw you the approach to Kestrel valley and tell you what to say. And bring your questions, boy. You're full of them. I can hear them rattling around in you like ballast stones."

She walked toward the door, slow, upright, her boots striking the boards with the deliberate rhythm of a woman who intended to reach wherever she was going under her own power or not at all. At the threshold she paused, silhouetted against the fogged glass and the blue dark beyond it, and coughed once, hard, into her glove. Then she pushed out into the night, and the wind took the sound of her going.

Ivo sat a long while with his untouched drink and the packet in his pocket, listening to the fiddle saw through the millpond song. Around him the tavern went on being the tavern, warm and dim and stupid with ordinary life, and none of it felt real anymore. He kept seeing the blood on the glove. Kept hearing *compartments sealed from the inside*, spoken in a dying woman's voice as casually as a weather report.

Finally he opened his ledger, and beneath the day's neat columns of salvage figures, in the margin, he wrote the only thing worth writing:

*Mara Hale. Kestrel valley. Before the snows.*

Then he closed the book, left coin on the bar, and stepped out into the wind, where the harbor lights burned low over black water and the Serrat Range stood up against the stars like the spine of something sleeping — something that had been sleeping a long time, and was, he would come to understand, beginning to wake.

Behind him, in the window of the Saltglass, the fiddle started the song again. Ahead of him, three weeks of mountain waited, and a daughter who didn't know yet that her mother was dying, and a packet in his coat that weighed almost nothing at all.

He went back to his boat. He always went back.
