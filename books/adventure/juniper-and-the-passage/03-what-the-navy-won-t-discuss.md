## 3. What the Navy Won't Discuss

The fog came in off the water before dawn, and Ivo Pemberton went out into it because the work was there and he was a man who did what was in front of him and nothing more.

He had spent the night at the tavern longer than he meant to, Juniper Hale's map folded inside his coat like something stolen. He had told himself he would leave it on the table. He had told himself a lot of things, sitting across from that ruined woman with her blood-flecked handkerchief and her eyes that burned like coals banked under ash — that he would refuse, that the whole business was madness, that a promise made to a dying prospector was worth exactly what it cost to make it: nothing.

Then she had pressed the oilskin packet across the table with both hands, the way you'd hand over an infant or a live coal, and said, "Not for me. For Mara," and something in his chest had shifted half an inch, the way ballast shifts when a hull takes water, small and wrong and irreversible.

So now he was rowing out through the fog with the map against his ribs and the Vantage's buoy line in his hands, telling himself this was just another day of salvage. Catalog the hold. Mark the recoverable items. Log the discrepancies — God, the discrepancies — and get paid by the ton like any honest vulture.

The Vantage sat in forty fathoms of cold green water two miles off the point, and even before he reached the buoy Ivo knew something was wrong.

* * *

It was the tender.

His own skiff, the *Magpie*, rode at the buoy where he'd left her, but beyond her, half-shrouded in fog, lay a second vessel — a hard-chined workboat with a diesel engine idling low, exhaust curling off the water in a pale ribbon. No name painted on her bow. No registration numbers. A canvas dodger over her cockpit, and on her deck, stacked neat as coffin lids, three dive tanks he didn't recognize.

Ivo shipped his oars and drifted, listening.

Under the diesel's mutter he heard the other sounds: the clank of a weight belt set down on decking, the hiss of a regulator being tested, a voice — low, clipped, professional — giving some instruction he couldn't make out. Then silence, and the particular quality of silence that means someone has stopped talking because they've heard *you*.

"Magpie," the voice called across the water. Not a question. They knew his boat's name. "Mr. Pemberton. Come alongside, please."

There are moments on open water when a man understands how alone he is — no witnesses, no law closer than the horizon, nothing between him and whatever happens next except his own judgment. Ivo sat with his oars in the locks and felt that aloneness settle over him like the fog itself.

He could run. The fog would cover him for a mile, maybe two, and then what? They had a diesel; he had oars and a small aux sail. They'd already found him once. And running told them everything: that he had something worth running with.

He thought of Juniper's map against his ribs. He thought of her saying *deliver it*, and how lightly he'd taken the word, how cheaply he'd imagined keeping it.

He rowed alongside.

* * *

There were four of them. Three stood in the cockpit of the workboat, and one — the talker — was a lean man in his forties with grey going through a black beard trimmed close, the kind of beard a man grows when he's spent years in dry suits and doesn't want whiskers caught in the seal. His gear was expensive and well-used. His eyes were the flat grey of slate underwater.

"You're diving the Vantage," the man said. It wasn't a question either. These people didn't seem to deal in questions.

"I'm contracted for salvage," Ivo said. "Navy freighter, lost last spring. Wreck's fair game."

"Wreck's fair game." The man repeated it without inflection, tasting the words. "That's what the paperwork says." He nodded at one of the others, a heavyset man with a face like a dropped crate, who stepped onto the *Magpie* without asking and began to go through her.

Ivo's hands wanted to close into fists. He kept them loose on the oar handles. "You don't have a right—"

"No," the bearded man agreed pleasantly. "We don't. But we're doing it anyway, so let's not waste each other's morning. You went down yesterday. What did you find?"

"The hold. Cargo netting, dunnage, crates gone to mush. Standard wreck."

"What was in the crates?"

"Nothing worth logging. Paper stock, mostly. Rotted through."

The bearded man watched him with the patience of someone watching a fish take a hook it hadn't noticed yet. Below Ivo's feet, the heavyset man was opening the *Magpie*'s stern locker, then the lazarette, then the shallow compartment under the bow boards where Ivo kept spare line and his father's compass — the brass one, the only thing he owned from Commander Aldous Pemberton besides his own face and his own blood.

The heavyset man came up with the compass in his palm and turned it over, and Ivo felt something move behind his sternum that he refused to name.

"Nice piece," the heavyset man said. "Old navy issue."

"It was my father's."

Something flickered across the bearded man's face then — quick, there and gone, like a shadow under clear water. He took the compass from his companion, weighed it in his hand, read the engraving on the back that Ivo had never bothered to have removed because he'd never imagined anyone would care: *A.P.*, and beneath it, smaller, the date of a commissioning ceremony twenty-six years gone.

"Pemberton," the bearded man said softly. "As in—"

"As in nobody," Ivo said. "My father was a clerk in the naval stores office. Give it back."

The bearded man gave it back. That frightened Ivo more than anything else that morning — the easy way he handed it over, the little smile that went with it, the sense of a ledger being balanced somewhere behind those slate-grey eyes. They weren't looking for a compass. They weren't even really looking for a map, though the heavyset man went through Ivo's coat pockets with thorough, impersonal hands while the other two watched the water and the horizon both.

They found nothing, because the map wasn't on him. It was wrapped in oilcloth inside the hollow of his lead weight belt, which he'd rigged at four in the morning with hands steadier than he'd expected them to be, and which hung now on its hook in plain sight, dumb and innocent, thirty pounds of dead metal with a dead woman's secret poured into its core.

"Last thing," the bearded man said, when the search was done. "Advice, freely given. The Vantage is a closed file. Whatever your contract says, the contract is void as of this morning. Dive her again and you'll have an accident. Cold water, old wrecks — they kill men all the time. Nobody will ask why." He smiled, and it reached his eyes without warming them. "You seem like a careful diver, Mr. Pemberton. Stay careful."

"Who do you work for?" Ivo asked.

"Salvage consortium," the man said. "Private interests. We recover things the navy misplaces." He turned away, dismissal complete, and then paused, half-turned, and added — almost gently, which was the worst part — "Your father would understand, I think. Men who know when a file should stay closed."

They cast off. The diesel note deepened, and the workboat slid away into the fog toward the point, taking its three spare tanks and its four professionals and its terrible courtesy with it, and Ivo sat in the *Magpie* with his heart going like a hammer on sheet copper and the fog closing in behind them until the sea was empty in every direction, as if none of it had happened.

Except that his coat smelled of another man's hands, and his father's compass had been weighed in a stranger's grip, and the words *your father would understand* were still ringing in his skull like a struck bell.

* * *

He anchored over the wreck anyway.

Later he would tell himself it was stubbornness, or greed, or the simple momentum of a job half done. All true, in their ways. But underneath all of it was the thing he couldn't say even to himself yet: that the bearded man had been afraid. Professionals like that didn't waste a morning threatening a coastal salvager unless there was something down there worth the morning, and fear, Ivo had learned long ago, always pointed at the truth the way a current points at the surface.

He kitted up in the grey light, checked his gauges twice, spat in his mask, and went down the buoy line into the green dark.

Forty fathoms. The water went from grey-green to bruise-purple to black, and his lamp carved a cone out of the darkness, and the Vantage rose to meet him out of it like a cathedral roof collapsing upward — the great freighter lying keel-down in the silt, her superstructure furred with white plumose anemones, her hull plates weeping orange rust in slow curtains that moved like the hair of drowned women.

He had dived her six times now. He knew her the way you know a house you've worked in: the torn gash along her starboard side where she'd struck the shelf rock, the cargo hold with its collapsed hatch coaming, the engine room where the telegraph still stood at FULL ASTERN, frozen in the order nobody had obeyed in time.

But today he went somewhere he hadn't gone before. Today he went aft, past the hold, into the lower compartments the manifest had listed as void space — empty tanks, ballast, nothing of value.

The manifest said void space. The manifest, which Ivo had copied from the provincial records office three weeks ago, listed the Vantage's cargo as paper stock, machine parts, and general naval stores. Four hundred tons of bureaucratic nothing.

The lower compartments were not empty.

He swam the first one slowly, lamp sweeping. It was lined with crates — not the soft rotted pulp of the upper hold but hardwood, tarred, strapped in steel, stacked and shored with a care that spoke of cargo that mattered. He hooked his pry bar under the nearest lid and levered, and the wood gave with a groan of trapped air, and inside, packed in oiled straw, lay rifles. New ones, grease still bright on the actions, dozens of them, and below the rifles, in sealed tins stamped with a shipping mark he recognized from Juniper's tavern ramblings and from nowhere else — a circle broken by a vertical bar, the mark of the Meridian Shipping Consortium — ammunition, and beneath that, cases stenciled with numbers that matched nothing on any manifest Ivo had ever seen.

He hung there in the black water, breathing his own bubbles back into his own face, and understood.

The Vantage hadn't been carrying paper stock. She'd been carrying arms — hundreds of tons of them, consigned in secret, marked as nothing, bound for buyers the navy would never admit to. And she hadn't been wrecked. Ships don't tear their own hulls open on shelf rock in calm weather with their telegraphs at full astern unless someone has put them there, and someone had put the Vantage there, deliberately, with her secret cargo aboard, because a drowned ship keeps no ledgers and dead crews tell no tales.

And Juniper Hale — coughing her life out across a tavern table — had known. Had known what the ship carried, had known why men would cross mountains and burn cabins to keep a map from surfacing, had looked at Ivo Pemberton, son of the officer whose signature was somewhere on the scuttling orders or very near them, and had chosen him anyway. Chosen him precisely. The way you choose a tool. The way you choose a weapon.

*Your father would understand,* the bearded man had said. Which meant they knew who Ivo was. Which meant they had known before they ever let a salvage contract drift down to a small-town diver with a cheap boat and no questions — or worse, they had given him the contract *because* of who he was, a name to hang an accident on if one proved necessary.

The cold came through his suit then, real cold, the kind that gets into the marrow, and Ivo realized his breath was fast and shallow and his gauge showed he'd burned twenty minutes standing still in the dark among the rifles of dead men.

He kicked free of the compartment, rose along the superstructure, and hung for a moment at the edge of the gash in the starboard hull, looking back into the wreck's lit interior. From here, framed in the torn steel, the hold looked almost peaceful — the drifting sediment, the pale anemones, the slow rain of rust. Forty-one men had died in this ship. The record said grounding and weather. The record was a lie with a naval letterhead.

He ascended slowly, watching his depth gauge, doing everything correctly, everything carefully, a careful diver, and all the while a voice in his head that sounded unpleasantly like the bearded man kept repeating: *you'll have an accident. Cold water, old wrecks.*

* * *

The fog had thickened while he was under. When Ivo broke the surface, the world had shrunk to a circle fifty yards wide, the *Magpie* a pale shape above him, the sky and sea indistinguishable. He climbed aboard, stripped his gear with numb fingers, and sat in the bilge water and the fish smell with the engine of his own thoughts flooding.

They knew his boat. They knew his name. They knew his father's name, and they had said it like men holding a card they intended to play later. His contract was void. His presence on the wreck was now, officially, an accident waiting for a convenient place to happen.

And the map — Juniper's map, the dying trade, the promise he'd accepted the way you accept a drink you don't want, meaning to set it down untouched — that map ran through the Serrat Range, inland, overland, along a route that suddenly made obscene sense. Because if you were moving arms by sea and needed a land route for the rest of it, a route no magistrate's clerk would ever see, you'd want a map of the passes too. And if a prospector's husband had been aboard the Vantage — Juniper had said *my Tom sailed on her*, though Ivo hadn't understood until now what she'd been telling him — if a prospector's husband had carried papers ashore before she sailed, or known the route, or simply seen too much—

Then the map wasn't a map. It was evidence. It was the missing manifest, drawn by hand, hidden in the hills, and Juniper Hale had spent her last weeks keeping it alive the way you keep a coal alive in wet weather: cupped, guarded, passed hand to hand.

She had passed it to him. To the son of the man who might have sunk the ship that killed her husband. Either she hadn't known who he was — unlikely, he thought now, remembering the precision of her eyes — or she had known exactly, and decided that the best person to carry a truth was the one with the most reason to bury it.

Ivo sat in the fog until his hands stopped shaking, and then he unwrapped the oilskin from the weight belt and looked at the map for a long time. Juniper's hand was strong despite the dying: firm lines, surveyor's marks, elevations noted in a prospector's shorthand, and along one margin, in smaller script, a single sentence he had read last night without understanding:

*Tom said the mountain route is the only record they can't drown.*

The only record they can't drown.

He folded the map away, hauled up the anchor, and rowed for shore through a fog that seemed to press against his skin like a held breath. Behind him the Vantage lay in her forty fathoms with her secret cargo and her forty-one dead, and ahead of him the coast was materializing out of the murk in grey silhouette — the tavern, the harbor, the road inland toward the Serrat Range and a daughter named Mara who wanted nothing to do with any of it.

He had promised a dying woman he'd deliver a map. He had assumed, in the casual arithmetic of a man who had stopped expecting anything from anyone, that the promise would cost him a week's travel and a little embarrassment.

Now he knew better. Now he knew the price list included armed men, a voided livelihood, and quite possibly the cold-water accident the bearded man had promised him so courteously. Now he knew that Juniper Hale had known exactly what she was asking, and asked him anyway, and that the only honest thing left in his life was a promise made to a stranger over a table sticky with spilled beer.

The harbor lights came up out of the fog one by one, yellow smears in the wet dark.

Ivo rowed toward them, and for the first time in years — since his father's name had become a door slammed in his face, since the navy had closed its files and the coast had closed its ranks — for the first time in years, Ivo Pemberton was afraid. Not of drowning. Drowning was simple; drowning was honest work.

He was afraid of what he'd do next, and of how much of it, he already suspected, he was going to mean.
