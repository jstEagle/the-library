## 4. Juniper's Last Night

The rain came in off the water around dusk, the kind of thin, needling rain that the coast specialized in — too soft to be called a storm, too steady to ever end. It ticked against the window of Juniper Hale's rented room above the chandlery like a clock counting down something no one wanted named.

Ivo climbed the outside stairs with a paper sack of coal in one arm and a bottle of laudanum in his coat pocket that the apothecary had sold him without a word, only a look. The stairs groaned under him. Everything in this town groaned eventually — the piers, the boats, the men who worked them. Salt got into everything and made it sing a low, complaining note until it gave out.

He knocked once and let himself in, because she had told him the night before that knocking was a waste of what little strength she had left.

Juniper Hale lay propped against two pillows in a bed that was too narrow for her, as if the room itself had already begun reclaiming its space. She was a big woman gone suddenly small, the way old prospectors went — weathered down from the outside in, like stone in a river. Her hands rested on the blanket, knuckles swollen, nails still rimmed with gray dust that no amount of washing would ever lift. That dust had been in her skin for forty years. It would go into the ground with her.

"You're late," she said. Her voice was gravel rolled in honey, or what remained of both.

"The apothecary was closing."

"The apothecary is always closing." She turned her head toward the window, toward the sound of the rain. "Sit down, diver. And light the lamp before you do anything else. I'm done talking to shadows."

He lit the lamp. The flame caught and steadied, throwing warm light across the room: a sea chest with brass fittings gone green, a coil of climbing rope coiled neat as a sleeping snake, a rifle on pegs above the bed cleaned so recently the oil still shone. On the wall, pinned by four rusted tacks, hung the map.

He'd seen it last night across a tavern table, half-hidden under her forearm while she coughed blood into a rag and talked fast, faster than a dying woman should have been able to talk. In lamplight it looked different. Hand-drawn on oiled canvas, ink faded to the color of weak tea, annotated in at least three different hands — hers, he guessed, and an older, more careful script, and a third hand that pressed so hard the pen had torn through in places. The Serrat Range ran down its length like a spine. Passes were marked with small crosses. One valley bore a symbol he didn't recognize: a circle with a line struck through it, drawn over and over, obsessively, until the paper wore thin.

"Coal," he said, setting the sack by the stove. "And this." He put the laudanum bottle on the table.

She looked at it the way a sailor looks at a chart of waters he has no intention of sailing again. "Later. Maybe later. First you'll earn your supper. There's bread in the tin and cheese going soft beside it. Cut some for yourself. I can't eat, but watching a man eat is nearly as good."

It wasn't a request. He cut bread and cheese and ate standing up, because sitting felt like settling in, and settling in felt like agreeing to stay, and staying meant witnessing something he wasn't sure he could carry afterward. But he stayed anyway. He ate slowly. Outside, the rain kept its patient arithmetic.

* * *

"Tell me about the ship," she said when he'd finished.

He wiped his hands on his trousers. "You know about the ship."

"I know what everyone knows. Navy freighter, went down in the March storm off Corren Point, eleven souls lost, salvage rights auctioned off to whoever bids lowest and asks nothing." Her eyes found his. They were pale eyes, almost colorless, the eyes of someone who had spent decades squinting into snow glare. "I also know what nobody knows, which is why we're having this conversation instead of you being asleep in your bunk like a sensible man."

Ivo pulled the room's single chair to the bedside and sat. "The manifest lists general stores," he said. "Rope, salt pork, boiler plate, mail sacks. What's in the hold doesn't match."

"Go on."

"Boiler plate doesn't come packed in crates stenciled with provincial customs marks. Mail sacks don't get chained to the keel frames. And there's a compartment aft — welded shut from the inside, which is a thing you don't do to a cargo hold unless whatever's inside matters more than the men working next to it." He paused. "I've stripped nine wrecks. I've never seen a navy vessel loaded like a smuggler's barge."

Juniper smiled. It transformed her face for a moment — showed him the woman she must have been at twenty, laughing on a trail somewhere, snow to her knees. Then the smile cost her a coughing fit, deep and wet, and he held the rag for her because she let him, and the blood on it was darker than it had been the night before.

"Scuttled," she said, when she could speak again. "Not lost. Scuttled. Opened her own veins and went down quiet, in weather that should have kept any sane captain ashore."

"How would you know that?"

"Because my husband was aboard her," Juniper said, "and because Elias Hale did not die in a storm. Elias Hale died holding a door shut."

The lamp flame leaned in a draft from the window seam and righted itself. Ivo found that his hands had gone cold, though the stove was lit and the room was warming.

"She was carrying something the consortium wanted moved without paperwork," Juniper went on. "Something that had to disappear — not stolen, understand, disappeared, with a wreck and a storm and eleven names read out in church to make it respectable. Only it didn't all go to the bottom the way they planned. Some of it came ashore. Some of it went overland, through the Serrat, before the ship ever sailed. My husband knew the route. He helped build it." Her fingers moved on the blanket, tracing a line Ivo recognized — the same line struck through the circle on the map. "And when they decided the route had to die with the ship, Elias decided otherwise."

"Mrs. Hale—"

"Juniper." She waved the correction with two fingers. "I've been Mrs. Hale to a magistrate and a minister and a man selling bad whiskey. You'll be Ivo to me, so it's only fair."

"Juniper." The name felt strange in his mouth, intimate and heavy. "Why are you telling me this? Why me?"

She studied him for a long moment. The rain filled the silence, ticking, ticking.

"Because you dive like a man looking for something," she said finally. "I watched you work the Vantage from the point yesterday morning. Through the glass. Most salvagers strip a wreck like crows on a carcass — quick, greedy, gone. You went slow. You touched things. You counted the dead man's boots in the galley and set them straight before you left." Her voice roughened. "A man who straightens a drowned stranger's boots is a man who remembers there were people aboard. That's rare. That's rarer than honesty, even."

"I catalogued them for the record," Ivo said, and heard how hollow it sounded.

"Mm." She closed her eyes. For a moment he thought she'd slipped away from him entirely, and panic rose in his throat, sudden and absurd — he'd known her three days; he had no business panicking. Then her eyes opened again, fixed on the ceiling as if reading something written there.

"I'm going to tell you pieces," she said. "That's all I've got left in me, pieces. A whole story takes breath I don't have. So you'll take the pieces and carry them, and someday, if you're stubborn enough and lucky enough, you'll find the rest of the story yourself, and you'll wish you hadn't, and then you'll be glad you did. That's how truth works. Like a hard crossing. Nobody thanks you mid-traverse."

* * *

She slept a little after midnight. He fed the stove, checked the lamp oil, and sat in the chair with his coat on, listening to her breathing — a sound like waves drawing back over shingle, each retreat a little longer than the last.

In the small hours she woke and spoke without opening her eyes, and he understood that some of what she said was meant for him and some of it was meant for a man thirty years dead, and that she herself might not have known which was which.

"Elias hated the cold," she murmured. "Complained every mile of the Serrat. Said a man ought to live where things grow. And then he goes and dies in the coldest water on the coast." A pause. "He'd laugh at that. He had a laugh like a rockslide. You'd hear it coming long before it arrived."

"Tell me about the route," Ivo said quietly.

Her eyes opened. In the lamplight they were clear, terribly clear, the fog of pain burned off by something harder underneath.

"No," she said. "Not the route. The route is on the map, and the map is for Mara. You don't get the route, diver. You get the promise." She pushed herself up on her elbows, and the effort shook her, and she did it anyway, because there are things a dying woman will spend her last coin of strength to say face-to-face. "Swear it again. I want to hear it in this room, in this light, with my ears working. None of your tavern nods."

Ivo leaned forward. "I'll take the map to your daughter. Before the passes close. Into her hands, or into the ground trying."

"Not good enough." Her voice cracked like ice shifting. "Say the rest."

He looked at her. She waited, unblinking, and he understood that she knew — had known since the tavern, probably — exactly how much of himself he'd withheld behind the promise. Dying people develop a terrible sight. They see through hull plating.

"I won't sell it," he said. "I won't give it up. Not to the men who'll come asking, and there will be men asking. I won't open it. I won't copy it. I deliver it whole, or I don't come down off the mountain."

"And Mara?"

"I'll find her. However far she's gone."

"And you'll tell her—" Juniper's breath caught. He started forward, but she gripped his wrist with a strength that astonished him, her fingers like rigging wire. "You'll tell her I loved her. Whatever she believes. Whatever I let stand between us all these years because I thought distance was the same thing as safety. Tell her the distance was mine. All mine. The love was always hers."

"Yes," Ivo said. "I swear it."

She held his wrist another moment, reading him, weighing him, the way he weighed salvage in his hands before committing to a lift. Then she released him and sank back against the pillows, spent, her chest working.

"There's a second thing in the case," she said, almost too quietly to hear. "Under the map lining. When the time comes — and it will come, because men like the ones who took my Elias don't stop at a coastline — when the time comes, she'll need to find it herself. Don't show her. Don't tell her. Let her find it." Her mouth twisted. "Mothers are fools, but we're consistent fools. We always think our children deserve the finding, not the telling."

"What's in the lining?"

Juniper smiled again, that rockslide laugh gathering somewhere behind her eyes though she hadn't the wind left to loose it. "The rest of the story," she said. "The part that will make her hate me properly, first. And then forgive me. In whichever order she chooses." She turned her face to the window, where the rain had begun to slacken toward the gray suggestion of dawn. "Now cut me a slice of that cheese after all. A thin one. If I'm crossing tonight, I'll cross chewing."

* * *

She ate perhaps a third of the slice, letting it soften against her palate, watching the window lighten from black to iron to the dull pewter of a coastal morning. The rain stopped. Somewhere below, the chandlery's boy began dragging crates across the dock, and gulls started their arguments, and the town resumed its ordinary noise as if the world were a place where women did not die in rented rooms above boat shops.

Near six, she asked him to open the window. Cold air poured in, clean and salt-sharp. She breathed it like a diver surfacing.

"My mother crossed the Serrat seven times," she said. "Carried mail and medicine and once, God help us, a piano's worth of tuning forks for a church in the valley. She taught me the range the way other mothers teach prayers. Every pass has a temper. Every glacier keeps score." Her voice was fading now, not with drama but with simple economy, words rationed like food on a long traverse. "Mara has her grandmother's head for it. Better than mine. She reads country the way you read water. She'll hate that I know that. She'll hate that I'm saying it. Say it anyway."

"I will."

"She'll test you. Let her. Fail honestly rather than pass falsely — she'll forgive the first and never the second." Juniper's hand found the edge of the blanket and gripped it. "And diver — Ivo — when she learns what you've been hiding, and she will, because I chose you partly for the hiding, God forgive me, because a man with buried things knows how to carry them—"

Her breath hitched. Stopped. Started again, thinner.

"—when she learns it, don't you dare lie to spare her. She's had a lifetime of merciful lies. They're heavier than the truth. They sink a body faster."

"Juniper—"

"Map's in the sea chest," she whispered. "Oilskin case. Combination's her birthday, which you'll have to earn, which is the point." Her eyes were closing. "Elias. Elias, the light's wrong on the ridge, we should—"

The sentence never landed. Her breathing drew back over the shingle one more time, and then further, and then it was just the sea doing what the sea does, and Juniper Hale was elsewhere — on a ridge somewhere, maybe, in better light, walking toward a man with a rockslide laugh.

Ivo sat without moving for a long time. The gulls argued. The boy dragged crates. The lamp burned down to a blue nub and guttered out, and he let it.

Then he stood, opened the sea chest — her birthday, he'd learn it, he'd sworn to — lifted out the oilskin case, and felt the weight of it, and the weight beneath the weight, the stiff rectangular shadow sewn into the lining that she'd told him not to touch.

Through the window, far down the harbor road, a horse stood saddled and motionless in the early light, and a man beside it was looking up at the chandlery windows.

Ivo closed the chest, tucked the case inside his coat, and reached for his pack.
