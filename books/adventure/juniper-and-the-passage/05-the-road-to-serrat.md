## 5. The Road to Serrat

The road inland was a scar of mud and gravel that followed the old survey line north, and Ivo Pemberton walked it because his boat was no longer safe and neither, he suspected, was he.

He had left Greyhollow before dawn, before the harbor fog lifted, with a canvas pack, Juniper's map pressed flat in its oilskin case against his spine, and four days of hard travel between himself and the men who had searched his boat. They hadn't found the map. He'd watched their hands move through his charts, his spare lines, the false bottom of his tackle chest, and their disappointment had been quiet and professional and terrifying. Professionals didn't search a salvager's boat for nothing.

Juniper Hale had known what the Vantage carried. That was the sentence he kept turning over like a stone in his pocket, feeling for the edge of it. A dying prospector in a tavern, coughing blood into her sleeve, and she had known.

The land changed as he climbed away from the coast. The salt smell gave way to resin and cold earth, then to something thinner, higher — the smell of rock that had never known anything but weather. The Serrat Range stood up ahead of him the way a wall stands up, white-crowned even in late summer, and he found himself studying it the way he studied water before a dive: not with fear, exactly, but with the honest arithmetic of a man counting what could kill him.

Three days he walked. On the fourth, he came down into a valley where a river ran milky with glacial flour and a scatter of cabins clung to the slopes like burrs. The largest building was a trading post with a tin roof gone rust-orange, and outside it a hand-painted sign read KESTREL LANDING — POP. 214, though someone had scratched out the number and written, beneath it, *fewer*.

Inside, the post smelled of kerosene, cured meat, and woodsmoke. A heavyset woman with forearms like dock rope looked up from a ledger.

"Looking for work?" she asked.

"Looking for a guide."

"Season's ending."

"I'm told there's one who'll take the high route. Hale."

The ledger pen stopped moving. The woman looked at him for a long moment, and whatever she saw in his face made her set the pen down entirely.

"Mara Hale doesn't take passengers this time of year," she said. "Mara Hale barely takes clients at all. And if you're the third man this month to come asking after her—" she leaned forward, "—I'd think hard about whether you're the kind of trouble she's already had her fill of."

"Third man?"

"Two came through in the last week. Well-dressed. Asked the same question you just did, only they weren't asking to hire her." She straightened. "They were asking where she slept."

The kerosene lamp hissed. Somewhere out back, a dog began to bark, then thought better of it.

"Her cabin's up the north fork," the woman said at last, quieter. "Half a day, if you know the trail. Which you don't." She tore a page from the ledger, sketched three lines on it, and slid it across the counter. "You didn't get this from me. And when you see her — tell her Bess says the pass will close early this year. The birds already went."

* * *

Mara Hale heard the dog stop barking before she heard the man on the trail, and that was how she knew it wasn't one of hers.

Her dogs barked at everything — deer, wind, her own shadow in the dusk — but they went silent for things that worried them, and this silence had a texture to it, a held breath. She set down the axe she'd been using to split kindling, took up the rifle instead, and moved to the window with the unhurried economy of a woman who had done this before.

Two strangers in a week. The first pair had been polite in the way of men reading from a script, asking about her mother with soft voices and hard eyes, and she had told them the truth: Juniper Hale was nothing to her. They had smiled like they didn't believe her, because they didn't, and left, and Mara had spent that night packing a go-bag and sitting by the window with the rifle across her knees.

Her mother. Dead a month now, or near enough — word traveled slow out of Greyhollow — and still reaching out with her long gray fingers to pull Mara into whatever fevered business she'd been about at the end. The prospecting, the obsessive letters, the last one, delivered by a mail rider who wouldn't meet her eye, full of a daughter's name written like an apology and a sentence Mara had read a dozen times and still couldn't make sense of: *If a man comes carrying my map, hear him out. I chose him. Trust that if you can't trust him.*

*I chose him.* As if Mara were a piece of property to be willed. As if twenty years of silence could be settled by a dying woman's arithmetic.

The man came up the trail at an even pace, not hurrying, not hiding. That was either honest or very good. He stopped at the edge of the clearing, well outside the porch light she'd left burning, and raised both hands — an old gesture, deliberate, showing them empty.

"Mara Hale?" His voice carried in the cold air. Low, roughened, a coastal accent worn smooth. "My name's Ivo Pemberton. Your mother sent me."

Everything in Mara went still and cold, like water going to ice.

"My mother," she called back, "is dead."

"Yes," he said. "I know. I was with her."

That was the wrong thing to say. Or the right one. She couldn't tell, and the not-knowing made her finger tighten on the rifle stock. He stood in the blue dusk with his pack and his salt-bleached coat and his hands open, and he looked — tired. Not performing tired. The kind of tired that lives in a man's bones.

"The letter said to hear you out," she said. "So. Say it. You have until full dark."

He glanced at the sky, where the last light was draining off the peaks. "That's not much time."

"It's what you get."

So he talked. He talked about a tavern in Greyhollow and a woman coughing blood into a red-stained sleeve. He talked about a map, hand-drawn, through the Serrat Range, traded for a promise. He talked about men on his boat with professional hands and empty eyes, and about a navy freighter called the Vantage, and at that name something moved through Mara that she refused to name, because her father had gone down on a ship by that name when she was nine years old, and her mother had never once, in twenty years, said his name aloud.

"Your mother knew what that ship carried," Pemberton said. "I think she knew her whole life. And I think the men looking for the map are the same men who've been looking for you."

The full dark came. Mara stood in her doorway with the rifle and the lamp at her back, and she thought about the go-bag by the door, packed and waiting, and hated her mother a little more for being right.

"You can sleep in the woodshed," she said. "The door latches from your side only. If I hear you move before dawn, I'll assume you're one of them, and I won't ask questions after."

"Fair," he said.

She watched him cross the clearing to the shed. She did not sleep.

* * *

Ivo woke to screaming dogs and the smell of smoke that wasn't woodsmoke.

He came out of the shed at a dead run, and the clearing was wrong — lantern light swinging wild, a horse he didn't know tied at the tree line, and two men at Mara's door, one holding the door off its hinges and the other holding Mara by the arm with a knife at her throat. She had a rope burn of red on her cheek where she'd fought and lost, and her eyes over the blade were not afraid. They were furious. Ivo understood, in a distant, clinical way, that he was looking at the most dangerous thing in the clearing, which was not the knife.

"Map," said the man with the knife. He was soft-spoken, well-dressed under a trail coat, and his voice had the flat courtesy of a man reading terms. "And the salvage diver, if he's here. Then we leave, and no one is inconvenienced."

Ivo stood in the shed doorway, and something old and cold woke up in him — something he kept in a flooded room at the bottom of himself, behind a door he'd sealed years ago. He had been a boy on navy bases. He had been a young man trained by men whose names were never written down. He had spent fifteen years underwater because down there, nothing asked him to be anything but competent.

"The map's not here," Ivo said.

"We know it's not on her." The knife man's eyes flicked to him, cataloguing. "We've been through this cabin twice while you slept. Sloppy of you both." He smiled without warmth. "So it's on you. Hand it over."

Ivo looked at Mara. Mara looked back, and in one glance they conducted an entire negotiation: *do you have it, no, do they know that, no, then we lie.*

"It's in the shed," Ivo said. "Under the floor. Let her go and I'll get it."

"You'll get it now, or I'll open her throat and get it anyway."

"You won't." Ivo took one step forward, and his voice changed — dropped into a register that came from somewhere below the coastal salvager, from the flooded room, from the sealed door. "Because if you wanted us dead you'd have done it in our sleep, and if you kill her you lose your only leverage, and men like you don't spend leverage. You spend other people. So here's what happens. I walk to the shed. You watch. The map comes out. And your knife goes away, or the next thing that happens in this clearing happens very fast and very badly for you, and I promise you I've had a worse month than you have."

The clearing held its breath. The dogs had gone silent again.

It was Mara who broke it — not the men, not Ivo. She drove her heel down onto her captor's instep, twisted, and drove her elbow back into his ribs with the practiced violence of a woman who had broken up trail fights in mountain towns her whole adult life. The knife man staggered; the blade opened a line along her arm, shallow, spraying red; and Ivo crossed the clearing in four strides and put the soft-spoken man into the mud with an economy of motion that surprised them all, most of all himself.

Then they were running. Dogs loosed and howling behind them to confuse the trail, Mara's hand slick with blood and gripping his, the go-bag already on her shoulder because she had packed it a week ago because her mother had taught her, in the end, the only lesson she'd ever taught her well: *be ready to leave.*

They went up. Not down, not along the valley — up, into the black shoulders of the foothills, on a trail Mara knew by feel in the dark, and behind them the lantern light shrank and the shouting fell away and the mountain took them in like water taking a stone.

* * *

They stopped at dawn in a hollow of granite and pine, where a seep of snowmelt ran cold over blue stone. Mara bound her arm in silence, efficient, not wasting a motion on pain. Ivo watched her and offered nothing, because he understood the set of her jaw: any kindness offered now would be taken as a debt, and she was not in a mood to owe.

"Third man this month," she said finally. Not a question.

"According to the trading post."

"Then they weren't looking for me before you came." She turned, and her eyes were gray as her mother's, and just as unreadable. "They were waiting. You led them here."

"Or they followed me and I lost them, and they found you anyway, and we'd be having this conversation regardless." He crouched by the seep, drank, kept his voice level. "I can't prove which. Neither can you."

"No," she agreed. "I can't." She pulled the bandage tight with her teeth. "So here's how this goes, Pemberton. You have a map my mother drew and a promise she bought from you, and I have twenty years of reasons not to care what my mother wanted. But those men will come back, and they'll come back better, and alone we're both dead. So we go together — as far as the high country. That's not trust. That's arithmetic."

"Arithmetic I can work with."

"And there are terms." She stood, slinging the go-bag, and the morning light came up the valley behind her and caught the blood on her sleeve and the fury still banked in her face. "You'll tell me everything. Not today, maybe. But before the high traverse, you'll tell me what my mother was into, what that ship carried, and why men kill over a dying woman's map. All of it. I don't cross a pass with a stranger holding secrets. I've seen what that does to people." Her voice didn't rise; it went flatter, which was worse. "I grew up with one."

Ivo looked at her — at the rope burn and the knife line and the gray eyes — and thought about the flooded room at the bottom of himself, and the sealed door, and how far away the high traverse was.

"Before the traverse," he said. "Agreed."

Behind them, far down the valley, a dog barked once and went silent, and Mara's head came up like a deer's, and without another word they turned uphill, into the Serrat Range, climbing.
