## 6. Terms of Passage

Mara Hale had buried her mother twice by the time the sun cleared the ridge above the cabin — once in the ground behind the church at Cold Harbor, where the coffin had been too small for all the things left unsaid, and once in the ashes of the hearth, where she'd burned every letter Juniper Hale had ever sent her without opening more than three.

The stranger sat across from her at the scarred pine table with his hands wrapped around a mug of tea he hadn't touched, watching steam rise off it as if it might tell him something useful. His name was Ivo Pemberton. He'd said it the way men say names they've worn out — flatly, without expectation that it would mean anything to her. He was broad through the shoulders in the way of a man who worked with rope and weight, but there was a stillness in him that didn't match salvage work. Divers she'd known were restless on land, always shifting, always listening for water. This one sat like a man who had learned, somewhere, that moving first got you killed.

He wanted her to guide him over the Serrat Range. In late autumn. Carrying a map her dying mother had pressed into his hands like a debt being called in.

"No," she said again, because he seemed to be waiting for a different answer, and she wanted him to understand there wasn't one coming.

"You haven't heard my terms."

"I don't need terms. The answer's no." She stood, took his untouched tea, and poured it out the window into the frost-killed garden. "You can sleep in the barn if the road's drifted shut tonight. You can't have my season."

"Mara—"

"Don't." The word came out sharper than she intended, and she let it. "My mother spent thirty years walking away from me. Letters I answered. Letters I stopped answering. And now she dies and sends a stranger — a *diver*, a man who smells of bilgewater and other people's wrecks — to hand me a folded piece of paper like it settles something." She turned back to face him. "It doesn't settle anything. It doesn't even come close."

Pemberton didn't flinch. That was the thing about him she kept noticing against her will: nothing landed on him visibly. It all went somewhere inside, into some hold below the waterline, and sealed itself there.

"Your mother was coughing blood into a cloth," he said quietly. "She held that map like it was the last warm thing she owned. She made me swear an oath before she'd let go of it. I don't know what's in your history with her. But she believed this mattered more than her own dignity, and she was not a woman who spent belief cheaply."

"You knew her one night."

"One night was enough."

Mara looked at him for a long moment. Outside, wind moved through the larches with a sound like slow water, and somewhere down-valley a dog barked twice and gave up. She thought about the three letters she had opened before burning the rest. One had been about weather. One had been about a mule. The third had said only: *I know what I did, and I know what it cost you, and I'm not asking forgiveness, only that you remember the mountains are honest even when people aren't.*

She had burned that one last.

"Sit down," she said.

* * *

They negotiated the way she imagined two merchants would haggle over a damaged cargo — each naming a price the other couldn't pay, then circling toward something neither liked but both could live with.

"I'll take you across," she said, "on conditions. First: we go by my route, my pace, my judgment. You don't argue terrain with me. Second: you carry your own weight, literally, and if I say drop something, you drop it. Third—" she leaned forward, put both palms flat on the table, "—you tell me everything. What's on that map. Why men might follow us. What my mother was into at the end of her life that required a sworn oath from a stranger. All of it, Pemberton. I don't walk blind into the Serrats for anyone."

Something moved behind his eyes then — the first unguarded thing she'd seen in him. It was gone fast, shuttered like a hatch.

"Everything," he repeated.

"Every word."

He looked at the table. He looked at the window, where the light was going thin and gray. When he spoke, his voice was level, and she would spend many nights afterward turning that levelness over in her mind, wondering how much craft had gone into it.

"The map is a route. Your mother drew it herself — passes, camps, water sources, places to shelter. It goes over the range to a town called Passage, on the far side. There are people who don't want that route known. They've already been to the wreck I was salvaging, and they searched my boat this morning while I watched from the tree line. So yes — someone may follow us. I won't pretend otherwise."

"That's not everything. That's a shipping forecast."

"It's what I have."

"You're lying."

"I'm telling you what I know," he said, and met her eyes, and there it was again — that sealed-hold look, pressure behind plating. "Your mother told me pieces. Not all of them. She ran out of time, or she chose not to spend her last hours on explanations. Either way, I can't give you what I wasn't given."

Mara sat back. She was good at reading people; guiding rich climbers and frightened pilgrims through country that killed the careless had taught her to weigh a face like a pack before a crossing. Pemberton was holding something back — that was certain as weather. But he wasn't lying about the shape of it. The fear in him was real, and it wasn't fear of her.

"Then here's my price for the part you're hiding," she said. "When we're on the mountain, and it comes up — and it will, because everything comes up on the mountain — you tell me. No more half-truths after the snowline. Swear it."

A long pause. Long enough that she counted her own heartbeats.

"I swear it," he said.

"And the map. I want to see it."

His hand went, very slightly, toward his coat — and stopped. She watched him decide. "Not here," he said finally. "Not until we're past the foothills. If those men come back and find it on you, knowing you have it makes you a target whether you guide me or not. Ignorance is armor, Mara. Let it be yours a little longer."

"Convenient."

"Yes," he agreed, without shame. "It is."

She should have walked him out then. She knew it the way she knew loaded snow slopes — a flat, cold certainty in the chest. Instead she heard herself say: "The passes close in three weeks. Maybe less, if the jet stream drops early like the almanac says. We leave day after tomorrow. That gives me one day to provision and one day to set my affairs, such as they are."

Relief crossed his face so openly that it almost made her sorry for him. Almost.

* * *

The town of Lowbridge straddled the river road forty minutes' walk downhill from her cabin, and it consisted of a trading post, a farrier, a chapel, a tavern, and eleven houses that had learned to hunch against the wind. Mara went down alone — partly because Pemberton's coastal accent would raise questions, and partly because she wanted an hour without him in it.

The trading post smelled of lamp oil, dried fish, and canvas. Old Sef, who had run it since before Mara could read, weighed out hardtack, salt pork, tea, a brick of sugar, candle stubs, and a new tin of matches while she tallied on the counter with a pencil stub.

"Heard the navy lost a ship off the coast," Sef said, conversationally, as he bagged the pork. "Freighter. Big one. Salvage men crawling all over the bay like flies on a wound."

Mara's pencil paused. "Since when do you read coast news?"

"Sergeant from the garrison came through Tuesday. Buying blankets." Sef tied the bag with a practiced twist. "Said the manifest was classified. Said it twice, like once wasn't going to scare anybody properly." He looked at her over his spectacles. "You heading up?"

"Over."

Sef's eyebrows climbed. "Over the Serrats. In what — October?"

"Early November, if I'm slow."

"Almanac says the jet drops early this year. Could be white by the third week." He began adding items to the pile unprompted — a second blanket, a coil of good rope, a storm lantern. "Who's the man?"

Mara looked at him. Sef had watched her grow up; he had earned one true sentence. "He's carrying something my mother gave him. People want it. I don't know yet how bad the people are or how big the something is."

Sef nodded slowly, the way old men nod when the world confirms what they already suspected about it. "Take the rope," he said. "And Mara — if it's bad enough that strangers cross a range to bury it, it's bad enough that strangers'll cross a range to dig it up. Don't be proud on the ice."

She paid, and carried the weight home, and hated how familiar the weight felt.

* * *

That evening she found Pemberton in her yard, splitting wood she hadn't asked him to split. He'd found the axe leaning by the door and fallen into a rhythm that was too clean for a dockworker — each stroke landing exactly where the last had aimed, the rounds coming apart in two and three pieces, no wasted motion, no second strikes. She stood at the corner of the cabin and watched him for a full minute before he sensed her.

"Supplies are bought," she said. "We leave at first light the day after tomorrow. There's a road as far as the old toll station; after that it's trail, then scree, then the high traverse. Fourteen days if the weather holds. Sixteen if it argues."

He set the axe down carefully, head against the chopping block, the way a soldier racks a rifle. She pretended not to notice that too.

"There's something else," she said. "Garrison sergeant came through buying blankets. Navy freighter sank off the coast — the one you were diving, I'd guess. Manifest classified." She let that sit. "Classified manifests don't usually need divers warned off by professionals, Pemberton. Classified manifests get handled by the navy itself. Unless whatever was on that ship isn't the navy's anymore."

For a moment he said nothing. Wind came down off the ridge and flattened the grass along the fence line, and the first real cold of the season arrived inside it, smelling of stone and distance.

"Three weeks," he said instead. "You said the passes close in three weeks."

"Two and a half, if the almanac's right. Which is why we leave when we leave, and why we don't stop for anything short of blood." She stepped closer. "I'm going to ask you one question tonight, and you can refuse to answer, and I'll still go with you, because I swore to Sef I'd see this through and because my mother spent her last breath on you and I want to know why. But I'll only ask it once."

He waited.

"What did my mother drag our family into, Pemberton? Because my father died in those mountains when I was nine, and she never once told me what he was doing up there, and now I find out she was drawing maps of the same country on her deathbed. I'm not asking for secrets. I'm asking if I'm walking my father's road."

The wind moved again. Somewhere in the trees, a branch cracked under its own accumulating cold.

"I don't know," Pemberton said — and this, at least, she believed wholeheartedly, because it cost him visibly to say it. "But I think she did. And I think that's why she sent me to you specifically, and not to anyone else in the world."

Mara held his gaze a moment longer, then turned toward the house.

"Sleep in the barn," she said. "First light, day after tomorrow. Eat before you come to the door — I don't cook for men who lie to me by omission."

"And after the snowline?" he called after her, quietly. "You said—"

"I keep my terms," she said, without turning around. "You'd better learn to keep yours."

Inside, she took down her mother's third letter from the drawer where she'd hidden it from herself, and read it again by lamplight, and this time she noticed what grief had kept her from seeing before: the ink was fresh. Juniper Hale had written it within the month. Within the month of her death, she had written to a daughter she hadn't spoken to in six years — *the mountains are honest even when people aren't* — which meant she had known, well in advance, that honesty was about to become dangerous.

Outside, the stranger's firelight glowed faintly through the barn boards, steady as a held breath.

Two and a half weeks, Mara thought, and blew out the lamp.
