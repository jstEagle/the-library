## 7. First Ascent

The foothills began where the road gave up pretending.

Mara felt the change before she saw it — the ground tilting beneath her boots, the pines thickening from scattered wind-bent things into a dark standing sea, the air taking on the cold mineral smell of snowmelt running somewhere beneath stone. For three days they had climbed away from the coast, and the land had been testing them gently, the way a mother tests a child's grip before letting go of the hand. Now the gentle part was over.

She stopped where the trail narrowed against a granite shoulder and let Ivo come up to her. He was breathing harder than she was. Not much — but enough. She filed it away the way she filed everything about him: in the drawer she kept for facts that didn't yet agree with each other.

"From here it's real," she said. "The trail's only a trail because people have walked it. After the saddle tomorrow, it stops being even that."

He looked up at the mountain the way a man looks at a ledger he's been told he can't afford. "And after that?"

"After that, the mountain decides."

He nodded and said nothing else, which was becoming his habit, and she was becoming suspicious of it. A man who talked too much you could read like shallow water. A man who talked this little was holding something under the surface, and she'd spent enough years guiding rich fools and frightened pilgrims through the Serrat to know that the quiet ones were the ones you watched.

She turned and led on.

* * *

She had built the morning's route as a test. She told herself it was simply good practice — you didn't take a stranger into the high country without learning his limits — but she knew the truth of it, and the truth was that she wanted to see him fail. Not cruelly. Not so that he'd be hurt. She wanted it the way you want a bad tooth pulled: because the uncertainty was worse than the answer.

The first test was the scree field below the old survey cairn, a slope of broken slate that moved like a living thing underfoot, each step a small negotiation. She took it fast, deliberately, choosing the line a guide would choose and letting him find his own. Behind her she heard the hiss of sliding stone, a grunt, a pause. When she glanced back he had found the trick of it — weight forward, steps short, moving with the slope instead of fighting it — and was thirty feet below her, steady, unhurried, reading the ground the way she read it.

Salvage divers, she reminded herself. They worked on wrecks. They knew how to move over unstable surfaces.

It was a reasonable explanation. She turned away and climbed faster.

The second test was the river. Not the glacial torrent that waited higher up — that one she was saving, and not out of malice but because there was no way around it and she wanted to know, before they reached it, what she was working with. This one was smaller, a meltwater stream running fast and clear over granite, maybe eight feet across where the trail had once had a bridge and no longer did. The water was the temperature of a held breath. A fall into it would mean numb hands and shaking legs for an hour, and an hour of shaking legs on the next section was a dangerous arithmetic.

She crossed on the stones without slowing, the way she'd done it a hundred times, and turned on the far bank.

Ivo stood at the edge. He didn't hesitate — that was the first thing she noticed. He studied the water for perhaps three seconds, and she watched his eyes move: the current, the stones, the far bank, the angle of the flow against his hip if he slipped. Then he stepped in.

The cold hit him. She saw it hit him, saw his whole body clench around it, saw his breath go short and white. But his feet kept moving, each placement deliberate, and halfway across, where the current pushed hardest, he did the thing she did — turned slightly downstream, let the water take some of his weight rather than shoving against all of it — and came out on her side soaked to the thigh and gasping, but standing.

"God," he said, shaking water out of his boot. "That's — that's a kind of cold I don't have a word for."

"Most people don't." She was already walking. "It's the mountain's way of asking if you're serious."

"Am I?"

"We'll see."

But she had turned her face away so he wouldn't see what was in it, which was not the disappointment she'd expected. It was something closer to unease. Because she had guided bankers and poets and a bishop once, and every one of them had hesitated at that crossing — hesitated, argued, tested the first stone twice, looked back at her for permission. Ivo Pemberton had read the water like a page he'd read before.

* * *

They made the tree line by early afternoon, and the world opened.

The forest fell away below them in dark green terraces, and above, the peaks of the Serrat stood up into a sky so clean it looked rinsed. Snow clung to the high couloirs in long white scars. Somewhere beyond those peaks, three weeks' hard travel away, was the pass that would close, and beyond it the daughter of a woman Mara had spent eleven years refusing to think about, and the promise Mara had made for reasons she still couldn't fully explain.

Ivo stopped beside her and stood with his hands on his hips, looking at the range.

"You've crossed it before," he said. It wasn't a question.

"Four times. Twice guiding, twice alone." She pulled her flask from her pack and drank, then held it out to him without looking. He took it, and their fingers didn't touch, and she was aware of that fact in a way that annoyed her. "The Serrat doesn't care that you're in a hurry. It doesn't care that you're being chased, or that a dead woman's map is in your pack, or that the passes close when they close. It cares about one thing, and that's whether you respect it every single hour. The moment you stop, it collects."

"Collects what?"

"Whatever you were careless with. Usually fingers. Sometimes everything."

He handed the flask back. "You talk about it like it's alive."

"It is alive. It just doesn't like us." She shouldered her pack. "Come on. I want the ridge before the wind comes up."

The ridge trail was the third test, though she hadn't planned it as one — it simply was what it was, a spine of broken rock with the valley dropping away on the left and a scoured gully on the right, exposed to the wind that came up the western face every afternoon like a tide. Halfway along it, where the path crossed a slab of bare granite polished by centuries of weather, she heard him stop behind her.

When she turned, he was crouched at the edge of the slab, looking down.

Not at the drop. At the rock itself. At the scuff marks in the lichen, and the flattened grass at the base of the gully, and — she came back three steps and saw it — the dark ring of a cold fire pit, stones stacked with the particular neatness of men who had built fires in a hundred places and left no argument behind.

She crouched beside him. The ashes were days old. The grass was bent where bedrolls had been. There were boot prints, at least four sets, and a tin that had held sardines, and a single horsehair caught on a gorse bush.

"They're not behind us," she said quietly. "They're ahead."

Ivo didn't answer right away. He was looking at the prints the way he'd looked at the river — reading, not reacting. "They camped here two nights ago, maybe three. They're moving slower than us. Heavier. Horses, at least one." He touched the fire pit's stones without touching the ash. "But they know this route. They camped on the ridge line, not the valley, because the ridge gives you sightlines both ways."

"You know a lot about how men hunt other men for a salvage diver."

He looked at her then, and for a moment something moved behind his eyes — that same flicker she'd seen on the dock at Sorrow's Reach, the one that came and went like a fish under ice. "I read a lot," he said. "It's a quiet trade. You get through books."

"Mm." She rose. "Well, salvage diver. Your books should tell you what this means. If they camped here three days ago moving slower than us, and they're ahead of us now—"

"Then they didn't come up this way." He stood, brushing grit from his knee, and his voice had gone flat and careful. "They came up another route. They knew where we were going before we did."

The wind arrived then, right on schedule, coming up the western face in a long cold breath that set the gorse hissing. Mara pulled her collar up and made herself think it through, because fear made people fast and fast was how people died in the Serrat.

Four sets of prints. Horses. Professional camp discipline — she'd guided enough military survey parties to know what tidy ashes meant. And ahead of them, somewhere between here and the saddle, moving with the patience of men who had done this before.

"They're not chasing us," she said. "They're waiting for us. There's a difference, and it matters. Chasers make mistakes. Waiters don't."

"So we don't go where they're waiting."

"We don't go where they expect." She turned west, toward the gully, and studied the country beyond it — a broken slope of tumbled rock descending into a fold of the mountain where a tarn lay like a coin at the bottom of a purse. It was ugly ground. Slow ground. Ground that would cost them half a day and every ounce of patience Ivo had. "There's a route through the tarn basin. Old shepherd track. It's not on any map worth printing, which is why it's still worth having."

"You know it."

"I grew up knowing it." She heard the edge come into her own voice and didn't soften it. "My mother drew half these routes. You're carrying her handiwork in that oilskin case, if you've forgotten. The Serrat is the one thing she left me, and I know it better than anyone alive, including the men waiting on the other side of this ridge."

Ivo was quiet a moment. The wind pulled at his collar. "Mara."

"Don't."

"You should know I'm not—"

"I said don't." She turned and started along the slab, away from the fire pit and its tidy stones, away from whatever sentence he'd been about to build. "Save your confessions for a night we're not standing in a wind tunnel with four sets of boot prints behind us. Right now you're my client, I'm your guide, and the only thing I need from you is that you do exactly what I say for the next six hours."

Behind her, after a moment, his boots followed. Steady on the stone. Unhurried. The boots of a man who had walked away from things before.

* * *

The tarn basin took them the rest of the day and all of what she'd promised it would take. The shepherd track was less a track than a rumor — a line of cairns, some fallen, some stolen for other walls, threading through boulder fields where the light came slantwise and gold and made every shadow look like a person. Twice Mara stopped and backtracked and found the cairn she'd missed. Twice Ivo said nothing, only watched her work, and she could feel him learning her, the way she'd been learning him, and she hated how much she didn't mind it.

They came down to the tarn as the sun went behind the western peaks, and the cold came with the shadow, dropping on the basin like a lid. The water lay still and black, holding the first stars. Mara picked the campsite the way her mother had taught her: wind shadow of the big rock, stone behind, stone below, sightline to the track, two ways out.

Ivo built the fire small, the way she'd have built it, feeding it dead wood that burned clean, and cooked the trail food without being told how, and when she took first watch he didn't argue, just rolled into his bedroll with his boots on and was asleep in what seemed like four breaths.

That was the last entry in the ledger. She sat with her back to the warm stone and added it up in the blue dark.

He moved like a man who'd carried weight over broken ground. He read water. He read fire pits. He fell asleep instantly, which soldiers and sailors did and bankers never did, and he woke — she'd noticed this morning — already oriented, already listening. And when the wind had come up on the ridge and she'd snapped at him, he had gone quiet the way men go quiet who are used to being shouted at by someone with the right to do it.

Salvage divers worked wrecks. Wrecks didn't teach you to sleep with your boots on.

The tarn lay black and still, and the stars wheeled slowly over the saddle they would have to cross tomorrow, and Mara sat with her mother's map folded in the oilskin case against her ribs and understood, with a cold that had nothing to do with the altitude, that she was guiding a stranger through the mountains, and that the stranger was the least knowable thing on them.

She woke him for second watch. He surfaced without a sound, without a flail, eyes open and fixed on her face before his body had finished moving — and then he saw where he was, and who she was, and something in him deliberately stood down, and she pretended not to have seen it.

"Anything?" he asked.

"Stars," she said. "And four sets of boot prints somewhere east of us, waiting."

He nodded and took the watch, and she lay down and did not sleep, because through the gap between her hood and the stone she could see him sitting against the rock with his knees up, motionless, facing not the fire but the darkness to the east, watching the way a man watches when he knows what's coming for him and has decided, for reasons of his own, to walk toward it anyway.

* * *

Dawn came up the color of pewter, and with it the wind, and with the wind — Mara, climbing the last rise above the tarn with Ivo a rope's length behind her, stopped so suddenly that he nearly walked into her.

Below them, in the next valley east, where the main trail bent around the shoulder of the mountain toward the saddle, a thread of smoke stood up straight in the still morning air. Not a shepherd's smoke. Too much of it. Too deliberate. And as the light strengthened she picked out the shapes around it: canvas, pack frames hung from a dead pine, the dark motion of horses grazing a line.

Four men. Camped exactly where the trail would bring them. Exactly where she would have brought a client, if she hadn't known better.

"They're not following us," she breathed. "They're herding us. Everything we've done since the ridge — they've been pushing us west, into worse ground, keeping us moving, keeping us tired." She looked at Ivo, and his face in the gray light was doing something she'd never seen it do. Not fear. Not quite. Something older and more tired than fear, the look of a man reading a letter he'd been dreading for years.

"Ivo," she said. "Who are these people?"

He didn't answer. Below them, one of the four shapes detached from the camp, climbed onto a boulder, and stood looking west — looking, it seemed to Mara, directly up at the two small figures on the skyline, at the two people who were supposed to be a day behind and were instead a valley away, watching.

The man raised one hand. Not a wave. A salute.

Ivo's hand found her arm. "Mara," he said quietly. "We need to be gone from this ridge. Now."
