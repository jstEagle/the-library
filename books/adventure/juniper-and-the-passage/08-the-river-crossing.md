## 8. The River Crossing

The river announced itself a mile before we saw it — a low, grinding roar that came up through the soles of our boots, through the granite of the trail itself, as if the mountain had a pulse and it was failing. Mara stopped at the bend where the pines broke apart and looked down into the valley cut, and I watched her face do something complicated.

"That's the Serrat Fork," she said. "It shouldn't sound like that."

"It's melt."

"It's *early* melt." She said it the way a doctor says a word like *malignant* — flat, precise, refusing to dress it up. "The high snows aren't due to let go for another month. Something's opened up there." She squinted toward the white shoulders of the range, still days away, blue and indifferent above the timberline. "My mother drew this crossing on her map for a reason. There was a bridge."

"There isn't now."

"No," she said quietly. "There isn't."

We came down to it through alder scrub that clawed at our sleeves, and the Serrat Fork filled the world. I had spent my working life inside water. I knew its moods the way Mara knew weather — the brown patience of harbor water, the green glass of deep anchorage, the black slap of night swells against a hull. This was none of those. This was milk-green and insane, braided with white, carrying whole trees sideways like matchsticks, slamming them against the far bank with a concussion I felt in my teeth. The cold coming off it was a physical thing, a wall you leaned against just standing near it.

Ivo Pemberton, who could hold his breath four minutes in thirty-degree seawater, stood on the bank of a river and felt his stomach drop.

"Upstream," Mara said. "There's a bar of gravel maybe half a mile up where it splits around an island. If the channel's narrow enough—"

"And if it isn't?"

She didn't answer. She just shouldered her pack and started walking, and I followed, because following Mara Hale uphill had become, over six days of trail, the closest thing I had to a plan.

* * *

The gravel bar was worse than the bank. The island she'd remembered was a strip of stones no wider than a wagon, and the two channels split around it unevenly — the near one broad and fast, the far one a compressed throat of white water barely ten feet across but moving like something with intent. On the far side, the trail climbed away through scree, visible and mocking.

Mara crouched at the edge and studied the water with the same attention she gave maps, head tilted, lips slightly parted. "We rope together. Cross the near channel to the island, then the throat one at a time, belayed from the stones. If anyone goes in—" She stopped. She didn't finish it, and I understood why. Nobody finishes that sentence honestly.

"I'll go first," I said.

"Of course you will." There was no warmth in it. Six days of trail had taught me her tones: this one meant she'd already decided I was being stupid and had decided, equally, to let me. It was a kind of permission. It was also, I was beginning to suspect, a kind of test.

We roped up. Her knots were quick and exact, the rope biting across my chest under my arms, and when she cinched the last hitch her knuckles brushed my sternum and neither of us moved for a second longer than the work required. Then she stepped back behind a boulder, took three turns around it, and said, "Go."

The first three steps were nothing. Gravel, knee-deep push, the current talking against my legs. The fourth step the bottom fell away.

Not gradually. Not the way harbor shallows ease you down. The river simply removed the ground, all of it, and the cold hit me like a blow to the skull — a cold beyond cold, a cold that wasn't temperature anymore but violence, that closed my hands and emptied my lungs in one convulsive spasm. The current took me before I finished understanding I'd been taken. It rolled me. My pack became an anchor dragging me down; my boots filled and swung heavy; the rope snapped taut across my chest and spun me downstream like a hooked fish, and above the roar I heard — or dreamed I heard — Mara shouting my name, one syllable torn into pieces by the water.

Here is what twenty years underwater teaches you: not calm. Everyone imagines divers are calm. What it actually teaches you is procedure. Panic is air; air is life; spend it wisely. So some cold clerk in the back of my skull opened his ledger while the rest of me tumbled in the green chaos, and the ledger said: *rope — follow the rope hand over hand, it leads to the island. Pack — release it.* And my fingers, dead as wood, fumbled the buckle once, twice, and the river ripped the pack off my back and I felt it go down like a stone taking a piece of me with it — the map, Juniper's map, the whole reason for—

No. Not the map. The map was in the oilskin case against my ribs, under my shirt, where I'd carried it since the lake country. The pack was food, rope, the tent, everything else. The ledger noted the loss without comment. The clerk did not note that my heart had stopped anyway.

I hauled along the rope. The current fought every foot, peeling my grip open, shoving my face under, and the water was so full of rock-flour I couldn't see my own hands, couldn't see anything but churned jade and white, and somewhere in it my shoulder struck a submerged boulder hard enough that I heard it — felt it — a deep wrong bell through the bone. My right arm went half-numb. I kept hauling with the left. Ten feet. Five. My knees found gravel, then my hands, and I crawled out onto the island stones on my belly like something the river had decided, at the last moment, not to keep.

I lay there gasping, cheek against wet stone, the sun shockingly gentle on the back of my neck. My whole body shook. Not fear — I told myself it was the cold, and mostly believed it. Across the throat of white water, Mara was already moving, paying out rope, re-anchoring, her face set in lines I couldn't read at forty feet and didn't want to try.

"Can you stand?" she shouted.

I got to my knees. The island tilted. "Yes."

"Then get off my rope and throw it back. You're going to belay *me*, and if you drop it I will personally come over there and drown you."

Despite everything — the numb arm, the hammering heart, the pack somewhere in the deep green dark — I laughed. It came out cracked and ragged, but it was a laugh, and I saw her hear it even across the roar, and something in her stance eased by a degree.

She crossed the throat like it was a staircase. That's the only honest way to describe it: the water that had handled me like a toy took her sideways shove and her planted pole and her absolute refusal to be moved, and she came across in long deliberate angles, hip-deep, then thigh-deep, then out, streaming, standing on the stones beside me with her chest heaving and her eyes blazing.

"You went in on step four," she said.

"The bottom went."

"The bottom always goes. You read the water first or you don't cross." She was wringing out her braid with both hands, glaring at me, and I realized distantly that she was more frightened than angry — that the anger was the coat she'd thrown over the fright because the fright was too big to wear in public. "You dove straight into the deepest line like you were diving. Like the water would hold you. Rivers don't hold you, Ivo. Rivers don't *want* anything. That's what makes them worse than the sea."

"I know," I said. "That's what makes them honest."

Something flickered across her face at that — almost a smile, killed quickly. She turned away and started rigging the far anchor, and I sat on the stones dripping in the sun, holding my numb arm, watching the water that had nearly kept me slide past green and glittering and utterly indifferent, and thought about how the last person who'd pulled me out of water had been my father, when I was nine, in the harbor at Corren's Landing. He'd hauled me up by the collar, furious with fear, and said, *Never make someone dive in after you.* I hadn't thought of that in thirty years. Memory is like that — it waits under the surface until something drowns near it.

* * *

We made camp late, in a hollow above the river where the wind couldn't find us, having lost the pack and gained nothing but distance and a lesson. We had the emergency rations from Mara's kit, a fire of driftwood that burned hot and smelled of resin, and between us, one dry blanket each and a silence that had changed texture since the crossing.

Mara dried my shoulder without being asked. She'd stripped my shirt back and was working salve into the bruise blooming there — the boulder's signature, a great purple-black continent spreading from the joint — and her hands were brisk and clinical until they weren't. Somewhere in the middle of it they slowed. Her thumb traced the edge of the bruise, light as reading braille, and I sat very still and watched the fire and did not breathe the way a man doesn't breathe when breathing might end something.

"It'll be stiff tomorrow," she said, and her voice was normal again, and her hands wrapped the joint in linen with efficient turns.

"Mara." I'd been turning it over since the island, all afternoon, the way you worry a splinter. The honesty arrived in my mouth before I'd voted on it. "I have to tell you something, and you're going to wish I'd drowned instead."

She sat back on her heels and looked at me. Firelight moved on her face. "Say it."

"The map." I touched my ribs, where the oilskin case rode. "Your mother's map. I told you it was a route through the Serrats, a prospector's track, nothing worth the men who burned your cabin and followed us up the valley. I told you that because it's what I wanted to believe. But I've been lying to myself longer than I've been lying to you." I made myself look at her. "Juniper didn't trade me a trail. She traded me a destination. The map marks more than passes — I've seen the marginal markings, survey bearings, elevations that don't belong to any mining claim. They belong to a supply line. An overland route. And I think — I'm beginning to think — it matches the Vantage."

Her jaw tightened. "The wreck you were stripping."

"The wreck I was hired to strip. Except the manifest says grain and machine parts, and the hold is full of crated machinery stamped with consortium foundry marks, and there's a sealed compartment aft that isn't on any drawing of the ship. Somebody paid to have that freighter sink exactly where she sank, with exactly what she carried, and somebody else has been killing people ever since to keep the story under the water." I heard my own voice go quiet. "Your mother knew. Your father knew. Whatever he died guarding on that route — it's written on the edges of her map, and I've known since the foothills, and I let you walk into this thinking it was a dying woman's sentimentality."

The fire popped. Down in the dark the river went on roaring, patient as time.

Mara didn't move for a long moment. Then she said, "The men on the ridge. The ones who searched your boat."

"Yes."

"They weren't after a map through the mountains."

"No."

She looked into the fire, and I watched her take it in — not the information; she was too much her mother's daughter for the information to surprise her. She was taking in the fact of my lie, weighing it, deciding what it cost and whether she'd pay it. When she finally spoke, her voice was low and level and worse than shouting.

"My mother chose you. Out of everyone on that coast, coughing blood in a tavern, she put this in your hands. So either she was a fool at the end—" her eyes came up and pinned me, gray and unblinking, "—or she saw something in you worth the risk. I haven't decided which yet. But Ivo — if there's more you're not telling me, more about *you*, you'll tell me before we cross the high pass. Because I've buried one parent who kept secrets to protect people, and I watched what it did. I won't walk blind into a second one."

"I know," I said. And because I owed her at least the shape of the truth, if not yet the thing itself: "There is more. About me. And you'll have it before the pass. I swear it."

She held my eyes a moment longer, then nodded once, and began banking the fire for the night.

* * *

I slept badly, which was usual, but woke before dawn to a sound and found Mara awake too, sitting wrapped in her blanket at the lip of the hollow, looking down at the river. I joined her. The water had dropped a hand's width overnight — the melt pausing, gathering itself — and in the first gray light the Serrat Fork ran quieter, deeper-colored, almost dignified, as if embarrassed by yesterday's violence.

"We lost the tent, most of the food, and the spare rope," she said, without turning. "By rights I should turn us back."

"By rights."

"But the pass closes in twelve days, and going back means the men on the ridge, and staying here means starving politely." She glanced at me sidelong, and in the half-light there was something in her face I hadn't seen before — not softness exactly, but the absence of armor, brief as a gap in cloud. "You hauled yourself out of that river on one arm. Half-drowned, pack gone, arm dead, and you kept the map dry against your ribs the whole way. My mother would have liked that. She'd have called it stubbornness with good posture."

"She'd have charged me extra."

That earned me the ghost of a real smile, there and gone. Mara stood, shouldered her lightened pack, and looked up the valley to where the trail climbed toward the white shoulders of the range, still days away, still blue and indifferent.

"Then we go up," she said. "And Ivo — tonight, when we camp, you start teaching me those marginal markings. Every bearing. Every elevation. If your life is going to depend on my mother's map, I want to read it better than you do."

She started walking. I followed, as I had followed for six days, up out of the river's reach — and behind us the Serrat Fork went on sliding past, green and cold and keeping, I suspected, the rest of my secrets for now.
