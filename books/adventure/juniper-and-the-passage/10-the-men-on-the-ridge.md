## 10. The Men on the Ridge

The whistle came again — three notes, repeated — and this time it was closer, threading up through the pines from the switchbacks below the camp like something following a scent.

Ivo had been awake for an hour. He'd heard the first call in his sleep and surfaced from it slowly, the way he used to surface from deep water, rising through cold layers of dark until the world above resolved into sound and grey light. Now he crouched by the window of the old assay office with Mara's breathing behind him — quick, shallow, controlled — and counted.

Three notes meant a signal. Repeated meant confirmation. The silence between repetitions was the thing that mattered: long enough to carry, short enough to time. Whoever was down there wasn't calling to the mountains. They were calling to each other.

"They'll come at first light," he said quietly. "It's what I'd do."

"You've done this before." Mara's voice was flat in the way that flat things are sharp. She was already moving through the dim room, gathering their packs, cinching straps she'd cinched a dozen times now, her hands sure and fast while her eyes stayed on him. "You said you were a salvage diver. Divers don't know what three notes mean."

Ivo didn't answer. There was no answer that wouldn't be a lie, and he wasn't ready yet to choose which lie, or whether he was done lying altogether. Outside, the sky over the eastern ridge had gone the color of pewter, and beneath it the ruined camp lay in shadow — the collapsed headframe, the rusted ore carts half-swallowed by scree, the long low bunkhouse where they'd spent four days listening to ash-fall tick against the roof like slow rain.

Four days pinned by weather. Four days of Mara deciphering her father's map by firelight, of learning the shape of her silences, of almost saying things and then not saying them. And all the while, apparently, someone had been climbing toward them through the same storm, patient as winter itself.

He heard them before he saw them — boots on gravel, deliberate, spaced like men who knew how to move in a line. Then shapes detached from the treeline at the lower edge of the camp: five of them, maybe six, fanning out with a discipline that made his stomach tighten. Not prospectors. Not bandits. Bandits were loud and greedy and stupid in predictable ways. These men moved like a crew working a wreck site, each one covering water the others couldn't see.

"Back door," Ivo said. "There's a gully behind the bunkhouse. It climbs to the saddle west of the headframe. If we're over the ridge before they work out where we went—"

"And if we're not?"

"Then we're not."

Mara slung her pack and looked at him for a long moment, and whatever she saw in his face made her nod once and stop asking questions. That frightened him more than the men outside. She was starting to trust the part of him he least wanted her to see.

* * *

They went out through the rotted rear wall where the boards had fallen away years ago, into the gully, into cold so clean it felt like cutting themselves on it. Frost furred the stones. Their breath hung and drifted. Above them the saddle was a black notch against the lightening sky, maybe four hundred feet of loose rock and frozen scrub, and Ivo took it at a pace that made Mara curse him under her breath — a diver's pace, steady, metronomic, the pace of a man who had learned that panic burns air faster than fear ever could.

Halfway up, the whistle sounded again, directly below them now, and closer. A voice carried up through the still air, distorted but intelligible:

"—saw movement on the slope. Two of them. Cut the saddle."

Ivo's blood went very quiet.

They reached the notch as the sun broke the far ridge, and the whole basin of Ashfall Camp lit up below them gold and terrible, every shadow suddenly defined, every one of their own shadows suddenly visible — two long dark figures stretched across the snowfield above the camp like signatures written on a page.

"There," someone said. Not shouting. Just stating it, the way you state a bearing.

Ivo pulled Mara down behind a shelf of granite and looked east along the ridgeline, because that was where he would have sent men if he were the one running this: along the crest, using the skyline, fast, while the two fugitives were silhouetted against their own sunrise. He was right. He could see them — three dark shapes moving along the crest with the easy, horrible economy of men who did this often, spread wide enough to flush, close enough to converge.

"Mara." He kept his voice level. "How good are you on scree?"

"Better than you."

"Then run when I say run, and don't look back no matter what you hear."

She looked at him then — really looked — and he watched her arrive at the edge of the question she'd been circling for days. *What are you?* He could see her deciding whether to ask it now or later, and choosing later, because later was a luxury the next thirty seconds didn't offer.

"Now."

* * *

What happened on the ridge, Ivo would spend the rest of the day trying not to remember, and the rest of his life failing to.

It began simply. They ran the spine of the ridge northward, boots punching through the wind-crusted snow, the world reduced to breath and burn and the slap of frozen ground. Behind them the pursuers spread wider, herding, patient — they didn't need to catch them on the open crest; they only needed to bend the ridge toward the cliffs on its western face and let the terrain do the work. Ivo saw the geometry of it the way he saw everything now, all at once, without wanting to: the funnel, the pinch point at the rock tower ahead, the two men who would already be cutting ahead below the crest to close it.

He should have told Mara. He should have said *we're being funneled, we need to drop off the line, take the eastern chute.* Instead his body simply acted, the way a body acts when it has done a thing many times in another life, in another name.

He stopped. Turned. Went *down* the slope instead of along it, straight at the two men cutting the corner, and Mara's cry behind him — *Ivo!* — cracked across the snow like ice giving way.

The first man came around the boulder with a short club already raised, and Ivo was inside the swing before it finished beginning. He didn't think about it. That was the horror of it, the thing he would lie awake turning over: there was no thought. His forearm took the man's wrist, his heel swept the man's stance, and the club went one way and the man went the other, down hard onto stone with a sound Ivo felt in his teeth. The second man had a knife — a real knife, a working blade — and came in low and serious, and Ivo's hands answered him in a language he hadn't spoken since he was nineteen years old: deflect, turn, elbow, throw. The man hit the scree and rolled and came up slower, holding his arm at a bad angle, and Ivo was already moving, already kicking the knife away down the slope, already putting himself between the fallen men and the ridgeline where Mara stood frozen with her hand over her mouth.

Above, the other three had seen. One of them fired — a pistol, the crack enormous in the thin air — and the bullet struck stone somewhere near Ivo's shoulder and whined away into the valley. He grabbed Mara's sleeve. They ran. Down the eastern chute, through the scrub, across a frozen seep that cracked but held, until the ridge was behind them and the pursuers' shouts had thinned into the wind, and then farther, much farther, until Mara finally stopped at a stream-cut and bent double, hands on knees, breath tearing out of her in white rags.

Ivo stood apart from her. His hands were shaking. He looked at them with a kind of distant disgust, these hands that had known rope and wreck and the patient work of salvage for twenty years, and that had remembered, in the space of six heartbeats, exactly how to break a man.

"Ivo." Mara straightened. Her face was pale, and not from the altitude. "Who taught you to do that?"

"No one. It doesn't matter—"

"Don't." Her voice shook, and it wasn't fear in it, or not only fear. "Don't tell me it doesn't matter. I have spent nine days telling myself stories about you. That you're a diver who got in over his head. That my mother trusted you because you were decent and lost and she could see it. And just now I watched you put two armed men on the ground in less time than it takes to say their names, and you want me to believe nobody taught you?"

He opened his mouth and nothing honest came out.

Behind them, faintly, a voice called up the chute — weak, ragged, human. "*Wait.* Hey — *wait*, damn you—"

They both turned. Down among the rocks, dragging himself upright against a boulder, was the second man — the one with the knife, the one with the badly folded arm. He'd followed them, or crawled after them, and he'd gotten maybe three hundred yards before his body quit. He wasn't reaching for a weapon. Both hands were occupied: one pressed to his collarbone, the other raised, empty, palm out.

"He's alone," Mara said. "The others went back along the crest."

"They'll circle." But Ivo found himself moving down the slope anyway, and hated himself a little for the softness of it even as he did it.

* * *

The man was younger than he'd looked at distance. Thirty, maybe, with a beard going grey early and frostbite reddening his ears, and when Ivo crouched beside him and ran practiced fingers along the injured arm, he hissed through his teeth and said, "Not broken. Separated. Shoulder." Then, wryly, painfully: "You fight like a navy man."

Ivo's hands went still.

"Say that again."

The man laughed, and it cost him. "Easy. I'm not with the navy. Nobody's with the navy — that's the whole joke of it." He sagged back against the boulder, sweat freezing at his hairline, and looked from Ivo to Mara standing above him with her mother's map strapped to her back, and something in his face shifted from professional hostility to a tired, bitter candor. "You really don't know who hired us."

"The navy lost a freighter," Ivo said. "The navy wants its salvage secured. That's the contract I signed."

"That's the contract they let *you* sign, so your hands would be on the wreck when we needed a fool to blame." The man spat to the side, carefully, favoring the shoulder. "My name's Corren. I work for the Meridian Shipping Consortium. Have for eleven years. The *Vantage* never belonged to the navy — not really. She was chartered. Hull leased, cargo owned outright by Meridian, manifests written twice. Once for the navy's books and once for ours. When she went down off Serrat Point, she wasn't lost. She was *put down*. Scuttled in deep water with everything aboard, because what was in her hold was worth more sunk than delivered — insurance, silence, and no witnesses." His eyes came up to Ivo's, and there was something almost gentle in them, which was somehow worse than cruelty. "You've been inside her. You know the holds don't match the manifest. So ask yourself who wrote the manifest, and why a dying prospector gave you a map through the mountains that follows the overland route the cargo took *before* it ever went to sea."

Mara made a small sound, like something in her chest cracking along a seam.

Corren watched her, then Ivo, and shrugged with his good shoulder. "We're not soldiers. We're retrievers. There's a document — a report, a confession, something — that came off that ship's route and into the hills, and if it reaches a magistrate, eleven years of very comfortable people go to prison, and some of them would rather two more bodies froze on this ridge than let that happen." He paused. "Your friend fights like a navy man. I said it because it's true, and because you should ask him why. Ask him his father's name."

The wind moved through the stream-cut, lifting the frost smoke off the stones.

"Get off this mountain," Corren said quietly. "Both of you. Not because I'm merciful. Because the next team won't talk to you first."

Ivo rose. For a moment he considered leaving the man there — the sensible thing, the safe thing — and then he unslung his pack, took out the tin of heat paste and the spare wrap, and set them on the rock beside him without a word. Corren looked at the supplies, then up at him, and nodded once, like a man acknowledging a debt he hadn't expected to be paid.

They climbed away from him toward the high traverse, the only route left — the exposed one, the one Mara had said no guide takes in early winter, the one that would pin them against the sky for two days with no cover and no retreat.

Mara didn't speak for an hour. When she finally did, her voice was stripped down to bare wire.

"You're going to tell me," she said. "Up there, on the traverse, with nothing else to do — you're going to tell me everything. Who you are. What your father's name is. Why a consortium retriever looked at you and saw the navy." She glanced sideways at him, and her eyes were wet and furious and unbearably steady. "Because I swore to my mother I'd get this map across the range, and I will, even if I have to do it alone. But I won't do it with a stranger. Not one more mile."

Ahead of them the traverse ran out along the cliff face, a thread of broken stone stitched across a thousand feet of nothing, and beyond it the passes waited, white and closing.

"Yes," Ivo said. "You're right. It's time."

And the words he would have to say rose in him like water filling a flooded hold — cold, dark, and rising past the point where any man could keep his head above them.
