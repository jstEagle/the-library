## 11. What Ivo Buried

The storm had chased them up out of the river valley like a dog after a wounded thing, and by the time the trees gave out they were climbing through cloud, boots crunching on old snow that had never quite learned to melt at this altitude. Mara Hale went first. She always went first now. It was not a decision she had made so much as one her body had made for her, somewhere between the ambush on the ridge and the night in the ashfall camp when she had read her father's route off her mother's map and understood that everything she'd believed about her family was a story told to children.

The high traverse began where the ridge narrowed to a spine of gray stone no wider than a cart track, with air on both sides and nothing below but a thousand feet of scree field glittering faintly through the mist. The wind came off the icefields to the north with a sound like someone breathing through their teeth.

"Rope," Ivo said behind her.

"I know."

She could hear him working — the soft rasp of cordage through his hands, the careful clatter of a piton being seated into a crack. He was good at this. That was the thing she kept circling back to, the thing she couldn't stop noticing even now, even after what she'd seen him do on the ridge. He moved over broken ground the way she imagined he moved underwater: unhurried, economical, a man who had made peace long ago with the fact that the world would kill him if he gave it an opening. She had thought, in those first days, that it was just salvage work. Divers learn patience. Divers learn to be calm in the dark.

But divers didn't set pitons like that. Divers didn't read a rock face the way sailors read weather. And divers — ordinary hired divers, men who stripped copper from drowned freighters for consortium wages — did not move the way Ivo Pemberton had moved at dawn three days ago, when the men came off the ridge with knives drawn, and he had turned into something else entirely.

She hadn't asked yet. She had meant to. Every night since, lying awake in whatever shelter they'd found, she composed the question in her head, arranged it carefully, and then watched it dissolve against the wall of his silence. He would answer anything else. Ask him about tides, about rope, about the Vantage's hold, and he talked freely, almost gratefully, as if every answered question bought him another hour of not answering the real one.

The rope came taut between them. She tested it, nodded, and stepped out onto the spine.

* * *

They walked the traverse for two hours before the mountain forced the conversation.

It happened because of the snow bridge. A drift had built itself across a gap in the ridge, a smooth white hump like something sleeping, and Mara stopped at its edge because stopping was what kept guides alive. She probed it with her staff. Solid enough, probably. Probably was a word she hated.

"We go around," she said. "Down and across the slab, then back up. It'll cost us an hour."

"It'll cost us more than an hour." Ivo looked past her, down the traverse, to where the weather was stacking itself against the peaks behind them — a bruised, boiling wall with lightning buried somewhere inside it like a coal. "That's coming our way. We don't have an hour."

"So we dig steps through the bridge and go fast, one at a time."

He shook his head slowly, and there was something in his face she hadn't seen before — not fear exactly, though fear lived in it, but a kind of grim arithmetic. "If it holds, we cross in ten minutes. If it doesn't—"

"Then I anchor you and pull you out. That's how it works."

"On ice that steep?" He gestured down the gap, where the mist simply ended, swallowed. "You'd be pulling a dead man, Mara. Or dying next to me. You know that."

"I know that," she said, and heard how flat her voice went. "I've pulled men out of crevasses. I've buried two clients in eleven years of guiding. So yes, I know exactly what the odds are, and I'm telling you we can make this crossing, and you're telling me we can't, and one of us is wrong."

"And if it's me?" He crouched at the edge of the bridge, laid his palm flat on the snow, and stayed there a moment, reading it the way he read water. When he looked up at her, something had gone out of his face — the careful blankness, the diver's mask. What was underneath was tired and older than his years. "If I'm right, then the only way across is the way I know. The way my father taught me."

The words landed strangely. My father. In all their weeks together, in all the half-truths traded over cook fires, he had never once said those words with weight behind them. His father had been a shape in the fog of his stories — a navy man, gone early, spoken of the way you speak of weather that ruined a harvest. Nothing more.

"My father taught me snow bridges," Ivo said. "Glacier travel. How to read a cornice from below. How to move on ice without crampons when you have to." He stood, and the wind pushed at him, and he let it. "I wasn't going to say any of this. I was going to get you over the pass and hand you your mother's map and disappear, and you'd never have to know. But you're standing on a mountain asking me to trust your life to my judgment, and I can't keep doing that while I'm lying to you. It's not fair to you. And honestly—" a breath, ragged at the edges, "—it's not something I can carry anymore either."

Mara felt the temperature drop. Not the wind. Something else.

"Say it," she said.

* * *

He said it in pieces, the way men do when the truth has been packed away so long it comes out in layers, each one colder than the last.

"My name isn't Pemberton by accident." He said it facing the gap, not facing her. "Commander Elias Pemberton. Vantage. You've seen the name — it's in the salvage writs, in the loss report. Commanding officer of record. The man who, according to the official account, lost his ship to a storm off Serrat Point eight years ago."

"The man who scuttled her," Mara said. It wasn't a question. She had known since the ashfall camp, since she traced the overland cargo route and understood what her father had died guarding. The Vantage hadn't been lost. She'd been murdered by her own captain, run onto the rocks in a controlled flooding, carrying a hold full of things the shipping consortium needed at the bottom of the sea — and a crew who hadn't been told.

"Yes." Ivo's voice didn't waver, but his hands, wrapped around the rope, had gone white-knuckled. "Elias Pemberton was my father. I was sixteen when the Vantage went down. I spent the next eight years learning everything I could about wrecks, about salvage, about the sea — because I needed to know what he'd done. Whether he'd done it. Whether he was even alive." A muscle worked in his jaw. "And then Juniper Hale walked into a tavern on the coast and put a map in my hands, and I understood, all at once, why the universe has a sense of humor. Because the daughter of the man whose evidence could burn the consortium is standing in front of the son of the man who burned the ship."

The words hung in the thin air between them, and Mara discovered she was shaking.

Not with cold. With something hotter and uglier. Betrayal has a specific taste, she thought distantly. Like copper. Like blood in the mouth at altitude. She had trusted this man with her mother's promise. She had slept beside him in the ashfall camp with her back against his pack. She had let him take the lead on the ridge, let him decide which way they ran, and every hour of every day since her mother died she had been building something fragile out of the wreckage of her family — and he had let her build it on top of a lie.

"You knew," she said. Her voice came out low and terrible. "From the tavern. From the first night. You knew who my mother was, what the map was, what the Vantage carried — and you said nothing."

"I said the truth I could afford."

"You said *nothing*. You sat across from me at a hundred fires and let me think you were a stranger. You let me believe the only liar in this story was my mother." She took a step toward him, and the rope between them went slack, and she didn't care. "Do you know what I did with your silences, Ivo? I filled them with trust. I decided you were the one honest thing left. The one man who wanted nothing. And the whole time you were—"

"What?" He turned to face her fully now, and his eyes were wet and furious, mostly at himself. "What was I? Say it."

"The son of the man who killed my father."

The words came out of her like something torn loose, and the moment they did she saw them land — saw them go into him the way a piton goes into bad rock, deep and crooked and irreversible. He flinched. Actually flinched, this man who hadn't flinched at knives on the ridge or at the river trying to drown him.

"He didn't kill anyone," Ivo said quietly. "That's the part I've spent eight years trying to prove. The official record says storm damage. The consortium's record says nothing at all. But my father kept papers — I found them after he vanished. Draft testimony. Names. Dates. A schedule of what was really in the hold, and orders that came down from people who never signed anything." He stopped. Breathed. "He scuttled the Vantage because he was ordered to, and he testified because he couldn't live with it, and then he disappeared, and I have spent half my life not knowing whether that means he's dead or whether they have him. Either way, Mara — either way — he tried to tell the truth. Whatever else he was, he tried."

"Then where is he?"

"I don't know."

"Where are the papers?"

"Gone. Burned with everything else when they came for him. What's left is in my head and in your mother's map, and in whatever your father hid along that cargo route." He held her gaze, and there was no armor left anywhere on him; she could see straight through to the exhausted, stubborn core of the man. "I should have told you in the foothills. I should have told you the first night. I told myself I was protecting you. That was a lie too. I was protecting myself, because I knew what you'd see when you looked at me, and I couldn't bear it. And now you're seeing it anyway, at ten thousand feet, on a rope, with a storm coming, and I'd give anything to have made a different choice."

Below them, far down the gap, the mist shuddered. Thunder, muffled, like artillery on some distant front.

Mara turned away from him. She looked at the snow bridge instead, smooth and white and treacherous, and she made herself think like a guide and not like a woman whose whole architecture of trust had just come down. The bridge would hold, probably. Probably. The detour around the slab would cost them the hour they didn't have. And the man on the other end of her rope — the man she had been tied to, literally tied to, for days — was the son of Commander Pemberton, and he had just handed her the truth like a man laying down a weapon, and she could not un-know it, and they still had to cross this mountain.

Her mother had chosen him. That was the thought that kept rising, unwelcome, insistent, like a body floating up from deep water. Juniper Hale — who had missed nothing in forty years of mountains and men — had put this map in *his* hands. Had made him swear. Had sent her own daughter to walk beside him. Did her mother know? Had she known who he was when she chose him?

And if she had known — what did that make her choice? A mistake, or the sharpest kind of wisdom?

Mara didn't have an answer. She only knew that the betrayal in her chest was no longer simple, and simple was what she needed it to be, because simple things could be cut away. This couldn't. He was on her rope. They were on her mountain. The storm was coming, and the pass was closing, and the evidence her father had died guarding was strapped to her back in an oilskin case, and none of it — not one piece of it — cared how she felt about Ivo Pemberton.

"Anchor in," she said finally, and her voice sounded like someone else's. "We're crossing the bridge. Your way. Fast, one at a time, full tension on the rope." She drove her staff into the snow at the lip and began setting the anchor herself, refusing his help with a silence that was louder than shouting. "And when we're across, you're going to tell me the rest. All of it. Everything you've buried. Because I'm done learning who you are by accident."

He moved to obey, and for a moment they worked side by side without touching, without speaking, two people bound by a rope and a dead woman's promise and a truth that had just made the rope feel very thin.

Thunder again, closer. The first flakes came spinning out of the dark wall behind them, dry and small, the vanguard of something enormous.

"Mara," Ivo said quietly, as she finished the anchor. "There's one more thing. About my father. About what happened after he testified."

She looked up at him, snow gathering on her shoulders, her hands stilling on the rope.

"What?"

His eyes went past her, down the traverse, toward the gap — and then fixed on something beyond it, in the failing light, and the blood left his face.

"Mara," he said again. "Don't move."
