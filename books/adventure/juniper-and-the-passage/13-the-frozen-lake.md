## 13. The Frozen Lake

**IVO**

The lake appeared out of the white like a held breath.

They had climbed two days through the high col, and now the range opened onto a basin of blue ice a mile wide, ringed by scree slopes that funneled the wind down onto its surface in long, singing gusts. Mara stood at the edge of it, arms crossed against the cold, studying the crossing the way Ivo had once studied tide tables — reading what was written there and what was not.

"Two hours to cross," she said. "Three if we're careful. The far shore has an overhang where we can shelter before dark."

"And if we're not careful?"

She didn't answer. She didn't have to. He had been under ice before. Once, off the Halden shoals, when a net had dragged him down and he'd spent ninety seconds learning exactly how much air a man could waste on panic. Ninety seconds was a long time. It was long enough to compose your own eulogy and find it wanting.

The lake's surface was not uniform. Wind had scoured it bare in places, glass-dark and terrifyingly clear, so that he could see down through ten feet of water to stones lying on the bottom like sleeping animals. In other places snow lay in drifts, soft as flour, hiding whatever the ice beneath was doing. Mara chose their line along the eastern shore where springs fed in from the scree — thin ice there, she said, avoid the margins — and then out across the middle where the cold had done its deepest work.

Ivo carried the map inside his coat, against his chest, the oilskin case strapped flat over his sternum where he'd worn it since the ridge. Since the ambush. Since the wounded man had looked up at him from the snow and said *consortium* in a voice like a confession. The case rode against his heartbeat, and he had caught himself, more than once, pressing a hand to it the way a man touches a wound to confirm it still hurts.

They were a hundred yards out when the wind changed.

It came off the western peaks in a single long exhalation, and the temperature dropped so fast his breath cracked in the air. Mara stopped, head tilted, listening to the ice the way other people listened for footsteps. Then he heard it too — a sound underneath the wind, deep and structural, like a whale song played through stone. A groan that traveled the whole length of the lake and died somewhere behind them.

"That's normal," Mara said. Too evenly. "Ice talks when the temperature swings."

They went on. The light was failing by degrees, the sky going the color of pewter, and Ivo found himself counting his steps without meaning to. Forty-one. Forty-two. Beneath his boots the ice was eight inches thick here, maybe ten — he could tell by the sound of it, the dense dull knock of good ice versus the hollow ring of bad. He had spent half his life reading water through a hull. He knew its moods. That was the joke of it, the ugly joke: all his skill, all his years of diving wrecks, and the thing most likely to kill him was water in its simplest form, wearing armor.

At sixty yards the ice began to speak again, closer this time, a cracking whisper directly underfoot, and Mara spread her arms wide — weight distribution, he knew, she didn't have to say it — and they moved apart, each walking their own line, ten feet between them.

Then the map case shifted.

It was nothing. A strap slipping, the oilskin sliding an inch against his ribs as he reached to steady it, one boot lifting and setting down a half-step out of rhythm. But the ice heard it. There was a report like a rifle shot, and the crack ran out from under his heel in a white lightning-bolt, branching, racing ahead of them toward the far shore faster than any man could run.

"Mara—"

"I see it." Her voice had gone flat and fast, the guide's voice, no room in it for anything but instruction. "Don't stop. Don't run. Keep coming, even weight, come to me—"

He kept coming. The crack widened as he crossed it, a seam opening in the world, black water showing through like a vein cut into marble. Three feet wide. Four. He stepped across it where it narrowed, felt the far edge flex under his boot, heard the ice take his weight and hold — hold —

And then the case strap snapped.

Not slipped. Snapped, the buckle sheared clean through, whether by cold or by wear or by some malice in the metal, and the oilskin case dropped from his chest, hit the ice, skated, and slid straight into the open seam.

He did not think. That was the truth he would carry afterward, the fact he would turn over in the fevered nights to come: there was no decision in it at all. Juniper Hale's coughing voice in a smoky tavern. *Before the snows.* Her hand closing around his wrist with a grip that had no business being that strong in a dying woman. The map was the promise. The promise was the only thing left of her.

Ivo went into the black water after it.

* * *

**MARA**

The hole where he'd gone under was small and obscene, a ragged mouth in the white, already beginning to steam in the killing cold.

She was moving before her mind finished the word *no*. Down flat, arms spread, boots hooked back against solid ice, sliding on her belly toward the breach the way her father had taught her when she was nine years old and thought frozen lakes were toys. *The water takes you in four minutes,* Elias Hale had said, crouched beside a fishing hole with her mittens in his fist. *Your body quits in three. So you do everything twice as fast as you think you can.*

"Ivo!" Nothing. The hole breathed mist. "IVO!"

Bubbles. Then a hand — his hand, glove black with water, breaking the surface and slapping at the ice lip and finding nothing to grip because the edges were rotten, crumbling, slick as oiled glass. His head came up after it, gasping, hair plastered to his skull, eyes wide and white-rimmed with shock, and the sound he made was not a word. It was the sound a body makes when it understands, all at once, what cold water is.

"The case—" he got out. "Mara, the case, it's—"

"Forget the case." Her voice came out iron. "Grab my wrists. Now."

He tried. That was the terrible thing, she saw it immediately — he tried, and his hands wouldn't obey. The cold had already taken them, fingers curled into claws that scraped the ice and slid off, and each failed attempt pushed him lower, his chin going under, coming up, going under again. His breath was coming in whoops, huge involuntary gulps, the gasp-reflex, and every gulp was a minute stolen from him.

She flattened further, worming out until her shoulders hung over the weak ice, until she could feel it bowing under her chest, and grabbed his forearms above the useless hands. Skin through soaked wool. His arms were already rigid, shaking with a fine violent tremor that traveled up her own bones.

"On three. Kick. KICK, Ivo, don't pull, kick your legs — one —"

He kicked. The ice under her groaned and sagged and held, and he came up six inches, enough to get his ruined hands over the lip, not enough to get his chest out. The second try gained less. By the third he wasn't kicking properly anymore; his legs were answering some slower signal, delayed, drunken, and his eyes had begun to lose their panic and go somewhere else, somewhere calm, which was worse. Much worse. She had seen that look once before, on a client who'd fallen into the Threlkeld torrent, twenty minutes before they pulled his body out at the weir.

"No," she said aloud, to him, to herself, to the lake. "Look at me. Look at my face. You are not doing this. Juniper gave you to me. Do you hear me? She traded her last days for you, and I will drag you out of this water by your teeth if I have to."

Something moved behind his eyes. Recognition, maybe. Or just the animal refusal she was shouting into him.

She hooked her ankle around a pressure ridge behind her, took his forearms in a cross-grip, and threw herself backward with everything her shoulders had. The ice screamed. Cracks radiated out around her like frost ferns on a window, and for one suspended moment she was certain the lake meant to take them both — and then his torso came up over the lip, dead weight, streaming, and she hauled and hauled and hauled until his hips cleared, his legs, until he was out and she was rolling with him away from the breach, away, away, until the ice under them rang solid and true.

He was making a sound. Not words. A chattering, clicking rattle deep in his chest, and his lips had gone the color of slate, and frost was already forming in his lashes.

"Up," she gasped. "You have to stand. I can't carry you and I won't dig you out of the ice. Up, Ivo. Please."

He stood. God help him, he stood — swaying, legs buckling, rising and falling and rising again while she braced him under his arm, and together, lurching like drunks, they covered the last forty yards of the lake without either of them looking down.

* * *

**IVO**

There was fire, and there was not-fire, and for a long time those were the only two things in the world.

The not-fire was the worst of it. After the shore, after Mara had gotten him into the overhang and stripped his soaked clothes and wrapped him in both their sleeping bags with her own body heat pressed against his back because she remembered, she said, that alone was not enough — after all that, the cold had moved inside him and set up housekeeping. His extremities burned and ached and then, treacherously, warmed, and he knew from wreck-site first-aid drills what that warmth meant. Tissue dying politely. Nerves signing off.

But there was also fire: a handful of sticks Mara had coaxed alight with trembling hands and a quarter hour of blasphemy, feeding it twig by twig inside the shallow cave, the smoke crawling along the ceiling and out a crack in the rock. He watched it the way drowning men watch lights.

He surfaced fully sometime in the night, shivering at last — real shivering, violent and rhythmic, and Mara actually laughed, a cracked wet sound, when it started.

"You're shivering. Thank God. Shivering means you're losing the argument slowly instead of quickly."

"How long?"

"Six hours, give or take. You surfaced twice. Said something about a winch both times." She fed the fire another stick. "I've decided not to ask."

He tried to sit up and his body filed a formal objection. Every muscle had been wrung out; his hands, when he brought them into the firelight, were red and swollen and clumsy as mittens. And the space against his chest where the case had ridden for eleven days was empty. Cold. He put his palm there anyway, an old habit, and found nothing, and the loss went through him like a hook.

"The map," he said. "Mara. The map went down with me. I had it — I almost had it, I saw it sinking, I couldn't—"

"I know."

"It's gone. Juniper's map is at the bottom of a frozen lake and I am the reason—"

"Ivo." She said his name the way you'd strike flint. "Stop."

He stopped. She was sitting across the fire with her knees drawn up, hair drying in wild copper waves, and there was soot on her cheekbone and exhaustion carved under her eyes, and she was looking at him with an expression he hadn't seen on her before. Not anger. Not pity. Something steadier than either.

"You went under the ice for a piece of oilskin," she said. "You couldn't swim, you couldn't feel your hands, and you went back down for it anyway. I watched you make that choice." She paused. "I've been trying to decide since sundown whether it was the bravest or stupidest thing I've ever seen. I've settled on both."

"The promise—"

"Is not lost." She reached into the pocket of her parka and drew out a sodden leather notebook — her trail journal — and set it deliberately on her knee. "While you were snoring like a landslide, I sat here and wrote down every detail of that map I could remember. The route through the Serrat cols. The cairn markings. The spring symbols. The notation at the top margin in her handwriting — the numbers, the dates, the shipping references." Her jaw tightened. "I've had it memorized since Ashfall Camp, Ivo. I've had all of it memorized since the night I realized what the route actually was. The lake took the paper. It didn't take what's in my head."

He stared at her. The firelight moved on her face. "How much do you remember?"

"All of it." Quietly. "Every line. I redraw it tomorrow when my hands work, and we compare it against yours, and between the two of us we'll have it whole. That's what my mother intended, I think. She didn't draw a map for one person to carry. She drew it so it could be broken into pieces and survive." Mara looked into the fire. "She was always three moves ahead of everyone. It used to drive me insane."

The wind came down off the peaks and moaned across the lake, and out on the ice, far away, something groaned and settled. Ivo pulled the sleeping bag higher and let the shivering take him, because it hurt and because hurting meant living, and because Mara Hale was sitting three feet away keeping watch over him with a fire and a memory and a fury she had converted, somehow, into care.

"Mara." His voice came out rough. "Why did you come out onto the ice? The section you crawled over was rotten. You knew it. One crack and we'd both—"

"I know what I knew." She didn't look at him. "Do you want a speech? Because I don't have one. I had a man going under a frozen lake and about ninety seconds to decide how much he was worth to me, and I spent exactly none of that time deciding, and I've been angry at myself about it ever since. How's that."

"That's not an answer."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't."

The fire ticked and spat. He should leave it there. Every instinct honed across a lifetime of guarded silence told him to leave it there, to bank the moment like the fire and let it burn low. Instead he heard himself say:

"When I was under — the second time, before you pulled me — I stopped fighting. For a few seconds. It was quiet down there, and part of me thought, well. This is easy. This is finally easy." His swollen hands flexed uselessly in his lap. "Then I heard your voice through the ice. Muffled. Like hearing it from underwater, which I suppose I was. And it made me furious. That you'd be up there in the cold because of me. That I'd done that to someone else." He looked at her. "So I fought. Not for the map. For that."

Mara was very still. The firelight caught the wet brightness in her eyes before she blinked it away, and when she spoke, her voice was low and stripped of all its armor.

"You absolute fool," she said, and her hand crossed the space between them and closed around his ruined, swollen fingers, and neither of them pretended, anymore, that it meant anything less than everything.

Outside, the wind shifted, and beneath its voice the lake groaned again — and this time, faint but unmistakable, from the direction of the breach, came a different sound. A knocking. Rhythmic. Deliberate.

Mara's head came up. Her hand tightened on his.

"Somebody's out on the ice," she whispered. "And they know exactly where we went in."
