## 15. Snowbound

The storm came down on us like a lid.

We had seen it building all afternoon — a bruised wall of cloud stacking itself against the western peaks, the light going strange and yellow beneath it, the way it does before the mountain decides to keep you. Mara read the sky the way I read water: not with her eyes but with her whole body, some animal arithmetic running behind her face. By the time the first flakes came sideways out of the white, she had already found us the hollow.

It was a seam in the mountainside above the tree line's last ragged fingers, where an overhang of granite leaned out like a shelf and the wind had scoured a chamber beneath it, deep enough for two people and a fire if they didn't mind sharing each other's breath. She'd marked it on the way up, days ago, without saying so. That was the kind of guide she was. She planned for the storm even while hoping past it.

"Get everything inside," she said, and there was no argument in her voice, only the flat certainty of someone who had watched weather kill before. "Everything. If the wind takes the tarp we're done."

I hauled the packs, the rope, the oilskin case with its ruined map and its hidden letter, everything we owned in the world now, into the stone throat of the hollow. The first real gust hit as I dragged the last bundle in, and it was like being struck by something alive. Snow didn't fall after that; it flew, horizontal, hissing past the entrance in white rivers. Within an hour the world outside had ceased to exist. There was the hollow, the small orange island of our fire, and beyond the lip of rock a whiteness so total it made a sound — a pressure, almost, like the sea pressing on a hull.

Mara built the fire low and disciplined, feeding it sticks no thicker than her thumb, banking coals the way a miser counts coins. We had fuel enough for four days if we were careful. We had food enough for three. Neither of us said that part aloud.

"So," she said finally, settling back against the pack she'd propped against the wall, her knees drawn up, her hands wrapped around the tin cup I'd filled with melted snow. "Four days."

"If it's four."

"You've been in storms like this?"

"On the water." I watched the flames bend and straighten, bend and straighten. "Different rules out there. You can run before a storm at sea. Here it runs you down."

She nodded slowly, and for a long time neither of us spoke, and the silence wasn't hostile anymore. It hadn't been hostile since the lake. Something about dragging a man half-drowned out of black ice water rearranges the furniture between two people. She had held my shaking body against hers for warmth through a night I mostly didn't remember, and you don't get that back, and you don't pretend it away either. But we hadn't yet done the other thing — the harder thing. We hadn't talked.

The storm gave us nowhere to hide from it.

* * *

It started, as these things do, sideways, with the small stuff.

"My mother hated the mountains," Mara said, staring into the fire. "Did you know that? Everyone assumes, because of my father, because of the range, that she was born to it. She wasn't. She was a harbor girl. She used to say the Serrat Range was just the sea standing on end, and she never learned to love anything that couldn't move."

"She crossed it twice," I said. "You told me."

"For him. Both times for him." Mara turned the cup in her hands. "Once to meet him coming down, once to bury what he left behind. And she told me, when I was young and stupid and romantic about it, that she'd have crossed it a hundred times. Then he died and she never went higher than the headlands again. Thirty years looking at those peaks from below like they owed her money."

"What did she tell you about how he died?"

"The official story. Survey party caught in a slide." Her mouth twisted. "I believed it until the map. Until your wreck." She glanced at me then, quick and sharp. "Your turn. Fair trade. Tell me about growing up Pemberton."

I fed a stick to the fire and bought myself a moment with it.

"There isn't much to tell," I said. "That's the truth of it. My father was gone by the time I could form memories of him. My mother didn't talk about him. There were photographs — a man in uniform, always slightly out of focus, or facing away, or half-turned at the edge of the frame. I used to think that was the camera. Later I understood my mother had turned them. She couldn't bear to look at his face and she couldn't burn them either."

"And you?"

"I built him out of spare parts. A diver builds a picture of a wreck that way — a plate here, a fitting there, and you imagine the ship between them. I knew he'd been an officer. I knew there had been disgrace, though nobody would name it. I knew my mother flinched at the sound of certain words. *Navy* was one. *Vantage* I wouldn't learn until years later, and by then I'd already signed aboard a salvage crew working her hull, and felt something move in me that I couldn't name." I looked at my hands. "I spent my whole life diving wrecks, Mara. Do you understand that? Of all the trades in the world. I told myself it was because I liked the work. The quiet of it. The order of things sunk and settled. It took Juniper Hale dying in front of me to make me see I'd been circling one wreck my entire life, trying to find my father in the cargo."

"And did you?"

"No." The word came out rawer than I intended. "I found what he did instead. Or what was done around him. I still don't know which. That's the thing I can't — " I stopped, started again. "Every night since the ridge, I lie awake and try to build the scene. The Vantage taking water that shouldn't have been there. My father giving an order, or failing to give one, or being ordered by men above him who needed a hole in a hull more than they needed thirty-one sailors alive. I build it and build it and I can't finish it, because I don't know if he was a murderer or a martyr or just a weak man in a strong current. And I am his son, and I have spent forty years terrified that whatever he was, I'm carrying it too."

Mara was quiet a long moment. The storm pressed its shoulder against the mountain and the firelight shivered.

"When I pulled you out of the lake," she said, "you weren't carrying anything. You were just a man drowning. I've thought about that a lot these past days. Whatever you inherited, it doesn't live in your blood. It lives in what you choose when the ice breaks." She set down her cup. "So choose out loud, Ivo. What would you sacrifice for this? For the map, for my father's report, for all of it. Name it plainly. Because I named mine already, back on the traverse, and you owe me yours."

* * *

The fire burned down to a red eye and I fed it again, and outside the wind rose to a register that seemed less like weather than like grief — long, sustained, noteless. Somewhere above us snow loaded onto the overhang with a slow granular whisper, and both of us tracked that sound with our ears, because we knew what it meant if the load let go.

"I'd sacrifice the salvage," I said at last. "The boat. The work. Everything I was before Juniper put that map in my hands. I've already lost most of it — the consortium will see to the rest. And here's the strange thing: it weighs nothing. I stood on the deck two nights ago and tried to mourn it and couldn't find the grief anywhere. Forty years of a life, and it packed into one bag." I shook my head. "What I can't sacrifice — what I'd fail at sacrificing, if it came to it — is knowing. If we come down off this mountain and the report burns or the magistrate laughs or the consortium buries it all again, I could live with that, barely. What I couldn't live with is turning back. Not knowing how it ends. That's the coward's door and I've walked through it before and I won't again."

"That's not a sacrifice," Mara said. "That's an appetite."

"It's the truest answer I have."

She considered that, head tilted, and I saw her deciding whether to be angry about it, and then deciding not to be. "All right. Then here's mine, plainly, since we're doing this. I would sacrifice him." She said it flatly, watching the fire. "My father. The myth of him. If it comes down to his good name or the truth of what killed those sailors — what got him killed keeping quiet, or killed talking, whichever it was — I'd hand over every scrap of it. I spent twenty years building a shrine to a dead man who chose a secret over his family, and shrines are cold places to live, Ivo. I know. I lived in one." Her voice cracked, just once, and she steadied it. "My mother crossed those mountains twice for a man who wouldn't cross a hallway to tell her the truth. I won't do the same. Even if the truth costs him. *Especially* if it costs him."

"Even if it costs you?"

"Especially me. I'm the one who has to carry it down."

The honesty of it landed in me like cold water. I thought of my own half-answers on the traverse, the careful architecture of omission I'd built between us, and felt the shame of it fresh.

"Mara. When I lied to you — "

"You didn't lie. You arranged things." She almost smiled. "My mother did that too. It must be something about people who carry secrets; they develop beautiful manners about them." She drew a breath. "But you told me the worst of it yourself, on the traverse, when you didn't have to. Nobody forced you. So we're even. Or close enough for a snowbound cave."

"Close enough," I said, and meant it, and something in my chest unclenched that had been clenched since the ridge.

We sat with that for a while. The storm howled. The fire ticked. And then, because the dark makes confessors of us all, she asked the question I'd been waiting for and dreading since the lake.

"Do you remember your mother telling you anything? Anything at all, from before?"

"One thing." I hadn't spoken it in decades. "One night — I was six, maybe seven — she'd been drinking, which she rarely did, and she sat me on her knee by the window overlooking the harbor, and she said: *your father loved the sea more than he feared it, and that was his whole problem.* And I said, what does that mean? And she kissed the top of my head and said, *someday you'll meet a thing you fear more than you love, and then you'll understand him.*" I looked at the fire. "I think I met it in that lake. And I think what I feared more than I loved was losing you before — " 

I stopped. The sentence hung there, unfinished, trembling like the flame.

Mara didn't rescue it. She let it hang, which was its own kind of mercy, and then she reached across the space between us and took my hand, scarred palm against scarred palm, and held it, and said nothing, and the nothing was the fullest answer anyone had ever given me.

We slept that night closer than the cold required. Neither of us pretended otherwise in the morning.

* * *

The second day we memorized the map.

It was Mara's idea, born of the fever nights in the cave — the understanding that paper fails, that oilskin leaks, that the mountain takes what it wants. We had the damaged chart spread on the flattened crate we used as a table, its ink bloomed and ghosted where the lake water had gotten in, and we took turns reading sections aloud while the other closed their eyes and repeated them back. Ridges, cairns, elevations, the strange little symbols Juniper Hale had drawn in a hand that shook and didn't shake at once. Mara's father's route. The overland spur that matched, mile for mile, the discrepancies I'd cataloged in the Vantage's hold — the cargo route the manifests swore didn't exist.

By the second evening she could walk the whole crossing blindfolded, reciting it like a psalm. I could manage the northern third and fumbled the rest, and she teased me for it, gently, the first teasing either of us had managed in weeks, and I understood we had passed some border that didn't appear on any map.

The third day the food ran to its dregs — a handful of oats, half a brick of tea, the heel of hardtack we split like communion. Hunger made the hours long and the talk short, and we conserved ourselves the way we conserved the fire. But in the afternoon she said, apropos of nothing, "Tell me what you'll do. After. If it works."

"After."

"When the magistrate has the report and the consortium is burning and your name is in every paper on the coast attached to the word *Pemberton*. What then?"

I honestly didn't know. I turned it over and found it weightless. "I've never planned past a job before," I said. "There was always another wreck. Now there isn't. Maybe I'll dive. Just — dive. Not salvage. There are things down there worth visiting that nobody's paying to recover."

"Like the Vantage."

"Like the Vantage." I glanced at her. "Would that horrify you? Laying markers for the dead. Including, eventually, maybe, one for my father, wherever he ended up."

She reached over and touched the back of my neck, where the hair had grown back unevenly since the fever. "I grew up in a shrine, I told you. I'd rather have a marker. Markers you can visit and leave."

The fourth morning, the sound changed.

I woke to silence — that enormous, ringing silence that follows a storm the way thunder follows lightning, the whole world holding its breath under new snow. I crawled to the lip of the hollow and pushed my arm out into blue, blinding morning. The wind had died in the night. The sky above the peaks was hard and clean, the color of a blade.

"Mara," I said. "Mara, it's broken."

She came up beside me and we stood together in the entrance, blinking, two creatures emerging from a burrow, and the sight of the range nearly stopped my heart. Four feet of new snow lay over everything, smooth and featureless, the mountains remade overnight into something soft and murderous. But the sun was up, and the air was still, and high to the east the far pass — Juniper's pass, the pass that should have shut days ago — showed a thin line of open sky between its shoulders.

"It broke early," Mara breathed. "Three days early, at least. High pressure's moved in — look at the color up there, that's stable air." She was already calculating, her eyes moving along the route, her lips counting. "If we move today, travel light, push the descent — we could reach the far saddle before the next system. The pass might still be open. It *might*."

"Then we go today."

"We go today." She turned to me, and the hope in her face was so naked it hurt to look at, and underneath the hope, plain as bedrock, the dread we shared and hadn't said: that the same open sky that let us through would let the consortium's men through too. They had lost us in the storm. Storms end.

We broke camp fast, silent, practiced, two people who had become a single machine without noticing when it happened. As I cinched the last strap, Mara paused at the edge of the hollow and looked back at the little den of stone that had held us for four days — the fire ring, the flattened crate, the place where we had finally told each other the truth.

"Come on," I said. "The pass is open."

"For now," she said, and stepped out into the blinding white, and we began to descend.
