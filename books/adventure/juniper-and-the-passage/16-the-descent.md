## 16. The Descent

The mountain let them go grudgingly.

Mara felt it in the way the trail narrowed and then widened, the way the wind that had screamed across the high traverse for two days dropped to a mutter, the way the air thickened with each switchback until it seemed almost warm against her cracked lips. Below them, the Serrat foothills unrolled in long brown waves stitched with pine, and beyond those, hazed blue by distance, lay the valley — and the town called Passage, where the road forked toward the magistrate's seat or downriver toward the coast.

Below them, too, was the agent. She could feel him the way she felt weather coming: a pressure behind the eyes, a certainty without proof. He had tracked them since the ridge. Perhaps since before the ridge. Men like him did not lose people on mountains; they simply waited for mountains to finish the work and then walked in to collect what remained.

She stopped at the lip of a granite shelf and looked back the way they had come. The high traverse was a white scar across the shoulder of the range, already filling with fresh snow, erasing their passage as though the mountain itself were complicit. Somewhere back there was the frozen lake, the hole in the ice, the case that had gone under and come up again water-stained and half-legible. Somewhere back there Ivo had nearly died, and she had dragged him out by his collar, screaming at him, crying without admitting she was crying because the snow made it impossible to tell tears from anything else.

He was twenty feet below her on the trail now, moving carefully, favoring the knee he'd wrenched on the descent but refusing to say so. He caught her looking and lifted one hand — *still here* — and she turned away before her face could tell him anything else.

* * *

They camped that night in a hollow of boulders where a stream ran black under thin ice, and Mara built the fire small and screened it with their packs, out of habit now, out of four weeks of habit, and sat turning the oilskin case over in her hands while Ivo slept.

The map inside was ruined. She had known it since the cave, since the fever nights, when she'd held it close to the flame and memorized line after line — the contour marks Juniper Hale had drawn with a steady hand thirty years ago, the route overland from the coast to the high valley, the symbols that weren't mining claims at all but waypoints along a cargo road the navy had never admitted existed. Her father had died guarding it. Her mother had carried it alone for three decades, coughing her life out in a coastal tavern, waiting for a stranger with a salvage boat to walk through the door.

But ruined wasn't useless. That was the thought that had been forming in her all day, crystallizing with every mile of descent, and now, in the firelight, with Ivo breathing slow and even beside her, it finished forming.

She took out her field notebook — the one she'd kept since her first season guiding, waterproof paper, grease pencil — and began to draw.

Not a copy. A copy would be checked against what the consortium already knew, and any error would damn them. No. Something better. She drew the route accurately as far as the second waypoint, the one the consortium surely knew about, the one their dead man on the ridge must have reported before Ivo put him down. Then she deviated. She moved the third waypoint east, into a box canyon she knew from her guiding years — a canyon with no exit, a canyon that would take a search party two days of wasted climbing to disprove. She shifted the cache symbol off the true bearing by a full compass point. She added a false annotation in a fair imitation of Juniper's cramped hand: coordinates that placed the manifest's overland terminus at an abandoned stamp mill on the wrong side of the watershed.

It was a lie built out of truth, which was the only kind of lie that held weight. And when she finished, she sat back and looked at it and felt something she hadn't felt since the ridge ambush, since the ice, since the fever: not fear. Not the animal, scrambling fear of being hunted. Something colder and more deliberate. They had spent a month running. This was the first thing she had done that made the running into something else.

"You're going to burn a hole in that notebook," Ivo said quietly.

She hadn't heard him wake. She didn't startle; she'd stopped startling around him somewhere on the traverse, and she suspected he'd noticed, and she suspected neither of them would say so.

"I'm making a decoy," she said.

He sat up, wincing, and leaned over the notebook. The firelight moved on his face — still thinner than it should be, the fever's tax not yet repaid, a new scar of chapped skin across one cheekbone. He studied her drawing for a long time. She watched him read it the way he read water, looking for what moved underneath.

"The canyon's real?" he asked.

"Real and dead-ended. I guided a client group through there six years ago chasing a rumor of silver. Three days to get in, three days to get out, nothing at the bottom but scree and disappointment."

"And the mill?"

"Real. Abandoned forty years. If they go there, they'll find exactly what we want them to find — old machinery and their own stupidity." She closed the notebook. "They've tracked us since the ridge. Which means they know our pace, our camp pattern, probably our faces by now if the agent has eyes in the valley towns. We can't outrun them to the magistrate. So we don't try."

Ivo was quiet. In the firelight his eyes had that flat, considering look she'd learned meant he was actually listening, actually working the problem, rather than agreeing to make peace.

"They'll intercept us before Passage," he said. "Or in it."

"Yes."

"And you want to be intercepted."

"I want to *choose* the interception." She heard her own voice come out level, hard, and realized how long it had been since it sounded like that — since before the cabin ransacking, since before everything. "Think about it. They expect fugitives. Frightened people clutching the thing they killed for. What do frightened people do when cornered? They run for the pass, or they bargain, or they break." She tapped the notebook. "We give them a corner. We let them take something. People stop hunting what they've already caught."

"That's a risk."

"Everything is a risk. Walking into Passage with the real report is a risk. Burning it is a risk. Doing nothing is just dying slowly at someone else's convenience." She found she was angry now, a clean anger with nowhere to go but forward. "My mother carried this thing for thirty years and never once got to use it. My father died holding it and never once got to choose how. I'm not carrying it another mile as prey, Ivo. From here down, we hunt."

The word hung between them. He looked at her for a long moment, and then something shifted in his face — not a smile, exactly, but the ghost of one, the expression of a man recognizing a current he knew how to swim in.

"All right," he said. "Hunt. How?"

* * *

They talked until the fire burned to coals, and then they talked past that, whispering in the dark like conspirators, and Mara found that planning suited them both — gave their hands and minds work to do that wasn't grief or fear or the other thing they weren't naming.

The plan, by the time dawn greyed the sky, had three moving parts.

First: the false map. It would travel in the oilskin case, visible, guarded, argued over. When the agent took it — and he would take it, because men who had chased something across a mountain range did not walk away empty — he would believe he had won. Belief was the whole weapon. A suspicious man verified; a victorious man celebrated.

Second: the real evidence. Her father's final report, the pages she'd found sewn into the map's lining, water-spotted but legible, naming names, dates, tonnages — the consortium's crime laid out in her father's careful naval hand. That would travel folded small, inside the lining of her coat, against her body, in pieces split between two hiding places so that no single search could take all of it. Ivo would carry the pages of the crew list and the scuttling order; she would carry the report proper. Neither of them would know both halves' exact locations. "So if they break you," she said, and couldn't finish, and he said, "Good thinking," in a voice that told her he'd finished it for her.

Third: timing. The magistrate's seat was a day and a half beyond Passage, over the low col that stayed open most of the winter. The passes above them would close within days — maybe hours, given the cloud stacking up over the western peaks. Which meant the consortium's window was closing too. A desperate man made mistakes. They intended to be standing next to him when he did.

"There's one flaw," Ivo said, near morning.

"One?"

"He knows my face. Maybe not yours, but mine. If he's been fed descriptions from the coast — and he'll have been, the salvage office would have sold me out the day I left port — then the moment he sees me, this stops being about the map and starts being about me." He said it evenly, but Mara heard the undertow in it: the father, the consulate records, the name Pemberton that Ivo had only begun to say aloud without flinching. "I'm not bait you can spend."

"You're not bait at all." She pulled her knees up, looking at the first grey light on the peaks above them. "You're the distraction. There's a difference. Bait doesn't know it's being used." She glanced at him. "And you don't have to do any of it. I can go into Passage alone."

"No," he said, too fast, and then, more carefully: "No. We do it together or not at all. I promised your mother a delivery, not a martyr."

"My mother chose you," Mara said. "I keep coming back to that. Out of every diver in every tavern on that coast, she picked the son of the man who sank the ship. She wasn't sentimental. She was precise." She stood, stamped feeling back into her boots, and looked down the trail toward the valley, where the town called Passage waited somewhere under its woodsmoke haze. "Maybe she knew we'd need each other exactly this way. Or maybe she just knew you'd keep the promise once it cost you something. Either way — we're past the point where either of us gets to be noble alone."

* * *

They descended all that day, and the mountain changed around them as they went.

The pines came back, tall and resinous, and with them the smell of living green after weeks of stone and snow. The trail widened to a pack road, then to something with wheel ruts frozen hard in the mud. They passed a shrine at a bend — a cairn with a tin cup and a faded ribbon — and Mara touched the ribbon without breaking stride, an old guide's courtesy to the dead of the range. By late afternoon they could hear the valley: a river running loud and unfrozen below, a dog barking somewhere, the far iron clank of a sawmill keeping its own time.

Passage sat where the river bent, a scatter of slate roofs and lamplit windows strung along one street, honest about its own purpose. It existed to provision people going over the pass and to take their money doing it. Every building in it was a threshold.

Mara stopped on the last rise above town as the light failed, and Ivo stopped beside her, and for a moment neither spoke. Down there, somewhere, was a man who had spent a month and other men's blood trying to take a piece of paper from them. Down there was a telegraph office, a stable, a magistrate's road, a pass that might close tomorrow. Down there was the end of the running, one way or another.

"How do we play it?" Ivo asked.

"We come in separately. You take a room at the inn — loudly, visibly, paying in coin, letting anyone watching form exactly the picture they expect: the diver, tired, spending his last money, guarding his gear. I come in an hour behind you as a guide off the range, which I am, and I book passage information at the stage office for the magistrate road, which I'll genuinely need." She watched the town lights steadying as full dark came on. "The agent will move on you tonight or tomorrow morning. He won't be able to help himself — the pass closing means his window is closing. When he does, he finds the oilskin case. Let him work for it. Make him commit men, make him be seen. And then—"

"And then we're gone over the col before he opens it," Ivo finished.

"Before he opens it," she agreed. "Because the moment he opens it, he knows. And the moment he knows, every man he has moves onto that magistrate road, and we have to already be a half-day ahead with the real thing." She turned to look at him. In the last light his face was drawn and resolute and, she thought, not afraid — or afraid and standing in it anyway, which was worth more. "Can you sleep in that room knowing they're coming?"

"I slept four years on a boat over a wreck full of dead men," he said. "I can sleep anywhere."

But his hand found hers in the dark, brief and hard, and let go, and she understood it as the whole conversation they weren't having yet — the one about what came after, if there was an after, if the magistrate listened, if the pass held, if they walked out of this valley with the truth delivered and their skins intact. Later. All of it later. First the town. First the trap.

They went down the last mile in the dark, single file, quiet as the dead they carried with them, and the lights of Passage rose to meet them like a held breath.

At the edge of town, where the pack road became a street, Mara paused by the first lamp post and adjusted the oilskin case inside her coat, feeling the false map's edges through the fabric, and the true pages beneath it, thinner than a prayer.

Somewhere ahead, a door closed. Footsteps crossed a boardwalk and stopped.

A voice, pleasant and unhurried, spoke out of the shadow between two buildings:

"You'll be the Hales, then. And the diver. I've been hoping you'd come down before the snow."
