## 17. The Town Called Passage

The town announced itself the way a fever announces itself — first a smell, then a heat, then the certainty that something was wrong with the world.

Woodsmoke and tallow and the sour sweetness of a distillery working overtime against the cold. Then the sound: hammers on the palisade crews were throwing up along the north road, driving stakes into frozen ground with the desperate rhythm of men who knew the calendar better than they knew their own names. Then, at last, the town itself, spilling down the shoulder of the valley in a clutter of slate roofs and yellow windows, all of it crouched under the black shoulder of the pass like a congregation waiting for a verdict.

Passage. They had named it honestly, at least. Every building in it existed for one reason: people trying to get from one side of the Serrats to the other, and the money that changed hands while they failed or succeeded.

Ivo stood at the edge of the last field, breath smoking, and felt the weight of the oilskin case against his ribs the way he'd once felt a tank on his back — familiar, necessary, and quietly trying to kill him.

Behind him, Mara came down the trail with the rolling, economical stride she never lost, not on flats, not on scree, not on the last hundred yards of a crossing that had taken eleven days and most of their luck. Her face was wind-scoured to a dull red, her eyes narrowed against the low sun, and when she stopped beside him she didn't look at the town. She looked at him.

"Rules," she said.

"You have rules."

"I have three rules. You're going to repeat them back to me."

Ivo almost smiled. It hurt his cracked lips, so he didn't. "One: we don't split up. Two: the magistrate's office first, before we eat, before we sleep, before anyone sees our faces long enough to remember them. Three—" He paused.

"Three," Mara said, "you let me do the talking."

"That's four rules."

"Three rules and a condition. Say it."

"You let me do the talking."

"Because you look like a man who's been dragged behind a horse for a week, which is true, and men who look like that say stupid things to officials. I've watched you do it. Twice."

He had, in fact, said stupid things to officials twice — once to a customs clerk in the river town whose questions about their cargo had been answered with a lie so lazy Mara had kicked him under the table, and once to a gate guard who'd asked if they were armed and Ivo had answered honestly. She'd spent a quarter hour smoothing that one over with a story about a hunting party scattered by weather that had been so detailed and so boring the guard had waved them through just to make it stop.

"Fine," Ivo said. "You talk."

She shouldered past him toward the road. After eleven days of sharing fire, rope, rations, and the small brutal economy of two people keeping each other alive, she still shouldered past him like he owed her money. He followed, and the town took them in.

* * *

Passage in the last week of the season was a town holding its breath. The trading floor of the Waystation House — part inn, part freight office, part courthouse of last resort for disputes between caravan men — roared with the particular noise of people trying to get things done before the mountains said no. Freight clerks shouted figures across counters. A woman argued with two teamsters over the price of moving a piano, of all things, up a pass that would be a wall of ice in nine days. Somewhere a fiddle was being tuned, badly, by someone who clearly intended to keep tuning it all night.

Ivo kept his hood up and his eyes moving. Old habit. In the salvage yards you learned to read a room for the man who wasn't looking at the merchandise but at the people looking at the merchandise. He did it now without deciding to, the way he still tied certain knots without deciding to — knots his father had taught him, on a boat, in another life, before the word *Vantage* became a gravestone.

Mara got them a room at the back of the Waystation, paid in the mixed coin they'd scraped together, and asked the clerk — a narrow man with ink-stained cuffs and the manner of someone perpetually behind on his own paperwork — where a person went to file a formal complaint with the provincial magistrate.

The clerk looked at her the way men in towns like this looked at any woman asking about official channels: with a flicker of curiosity and an immediate calculation of what the answer might cost him.

"Magistrate Corren sits the office on Tanner's Row," he said. "But he's sitting the assize at Harlow until Thursday."

"Thursday." Mara's voice didn't change, but Ivo saw her hand flatten on the counter. "And today is?"

"Friday."

The word sat between them like a stone dropped into still water. Nine days until the pass closed. Six until the magistrate came back.

"Is there no deputy?" Ivo asked, before he could stop himself. Rule three, broken in under a minute.

The clerk's eyes came to him — to the hood, the scabbed lip, the way he held his left arm a little close to his body, the ribs Mara had strapped after the traverse. "Deputy Voss is here. He handles... matters of urgency." A pause, delicate as a scale weighing something. "What's the nature of the matter?"

"Salvage fraud," Mara said smoothly. "A shipping concern, a falsified manifest. We have documents."

Something moved behind the clerk's eyes. Ivo watched it move and felt the cold slide down his spine that had nothing to do with the wind off the pass. The man hadn't asked what documents. He hadn't asked which concern. He'd heard *documents* and gone very still, the way a man goes still when he hears a sound in a house he thought was empty.

"Tanner's Row," the clerk repeated. "Third door past the chandlery. Deputy Voss keeps morning hours."

They walked out into the street and Ivo took Mara's elbow and steered her into the lee of a woodpile, out of the traffic of sledges and freight carts.

"You saw it," he said.

"I saw it."

"He went still."

"Everyone goes still when you mention the consortium's name in a town like this." But she said it without conviction, and she knew he knew it, and for a moment they stood there in the smoke and the noise, two people who had learned to read each other's silences the way they'd learned to read weather, and neither of them liked what the silence said.

"The documents don't leave your body," Ivo said. "Not in that office. Not until you've looked Voss in the eye and decided."

"I know how to carry paper, Ivo."

"I know you do. I'm saying it anyway." He heard how it sounded — not instruction, but the other thing underneath it, the thing they'd been circling for a hundred miles and had only ever named once, badly, in a snowbound cabin with the storm screaming hard enough to make confessions feel deniable. He let it go. "Tanner's Row. Morning hours. It's nearly evening now."

"Then we eat, we sleep, and we're first in line when Voss unlocks his door." Mara pulled her hood forward. "And Ivo — the Waystation bar. Keep your back to it. There was a man at the corner table, gray coat, city boots, drinking nothing. Men who drink nothing in a bar are working."

* * *

They ate in the kitchen passage, standing, from bowls the cook handed them without comment. Ivo slept three hours in the narrow room with his boots on and the oilskin case strapped under his shirt, and woke at the gray hour before dawn with his heart going like a diver's heart at depth, that slow enormous pounding that meant the body knew something the mind hadn't admitted yet.

He lay in the dark listening to Mara breathe across the room — she'd taken the chair, refused the second bed with a look that had ended the discussion — and tried to name what the body knew.

It was the clerk. It was the gray coat. It was the fact that they had come down out of the mountains in eleven days, faster than any reasonable party, faster than anyone could have tracked them over the high traverse in this season — and yet the whole town had felt wrong from the first smell of it. Not hostile. *Prepared.* The palisade crews on the north road. The extra lanterns at the freight office. The sense, which he couldn't source and therefore couldn't dismiss, of a stage that had been set before the actors arrived.

His father had used a word for that. *Arrangement.* You didn't stumble into an arrangement, the Commander used to say, standing at the rail of a navy cutter with his hands clasped behind his back like a man at his own trial. An arrangement is made. The only question is whether you were consulted.

Ivo got up in the dark and dressed without waking her, which was a skill, and went down into the blue cold of the empty street.

* * *

Tanner's Row at first light was a lane of shuttered workshops smelling of hides and lye. The chandlery was third from the corner, and the magistrate's office was third past that — a squat stone building with a green door and a brass plate worn nearly blank. A lamp burned behind the frosted window. Someone was already at work.

Mara arrived beside him before he heard her, which still unsettled him. She was carrying the report — her father's final report, the pages she'd memorized by firelight in the cave and then read so many times since that Ivo suspected she could recite it in her sleep, in the dark, bleeding. She'd copied it out twice on bought paper in a hand that wasn't hers, in case. The originals rode against her skin. The copies rode in the map case, which rode against his, because they had agreed that the two of them should never carry the same thing in the same place.

"Morning hours," she said. "It's morning."

"It's barely morning."

"Then we're early. Early is honest. Honest is what we're selling." She looked at the green door, and Ivo watched her do the thing she did before every hard thing: a single slow breath, eyes half-closed, like a woman standing at the edge of cold water. Then her hand was on the latch.

The door opened before she pulled it.

The man in the doorway was forty, maybe, neat in a deputy's gray coat, with a clerk's soft hands and a clerk's careful face. "Miss — Hale, is it?" he said.

The world went very quiet, the way it goes quiet underwater.

Ivo saw Mara's stillness and knew she felt it too. They had used no name in any town. They had paid in mixed coin and told the customs clerk a story about a hunting party. No one knew the name Hale. No one could know the name Hale.

"Deputy Voss," the man said, stepping back to admit them, and his eyes — mild, apologetic, professionally kind — went past Mara to Ivo and stayed there. "And Mr. Pemberton. Come in, please. Both of you. We have a great deal to discuss, and the pass closes in nine days, which I'm told concerns you more than it concerns me."

* * *

The office was warm. That was the first obscenity of it — the iron stove ticking, the kettle on the plate, the whole room heated and lit and arranged at an hour when no honest clerk was at his desk. There were three chairs before the desk and a fourth man in the corner, and the fourth man was the gray coat from the Waystation bar, and he was drinking nothing.

"Sit," said Voss, who was not a deputy, or if he was, it was the smallest true thing about him. He poured tea from the kettle with the unhurried grace of a host. "You'll want to hear this before you decide what to do with your papers. I'm told you're reasonable people. Your mother was reasonable, Miss Hale. Difficult, at the end, but reasonable. And your father—" a small pause, a small sorrow, expertly placed, "—your father was the most reasonable man the consortium ever employed, right up until he wasn't."

Mara had not sat. Ivo watched her stand there with the copies in her coat and the originals against her heart and a face like the north face of a mountain, and he stood too, because standing beside her was the only thing in the room that made sense.

"You have the wrong office," she said. "We're here for the provincial magistrate."

"You're here because the provincial magistrate is the one man in this province the consortium can't yet buy, and you walked six hundred miles to put a document in his hands. Yes. We know." Voss set down the teapot. "We've known since the ridge, Mr. Pemberton. Since before the ridge, if I'm honest. We knew when you took the salvage contract on the Vantage, because we wrote the contract. We knew when Juniper Hale walked into a tavern and gave you a map, because she'd been trying to give someone that map for six years, and we'd been watching her for six years, waiting to see who'd be fool enough to take it."

The kettle ticked. The gray coat in the corner watched Ivo with the flat patience of a man who had done this before and would do it again.

"Here is what I don't understand," Voss went on, and his voice changed — softened, dropped, became the voice of a man confiding rather than negotiating. "You. Pemberton. We ran your name the day you signed the salvage papers. It came back clean. Too clean. A salvage diver with a dead father and no history — no debts, no record, no family. We had to dig, and when we dug, Mr. Pemberton, we found something that frankly made my week." He smiled. It was a good smile. It was the worst thing Ivo had seen in eleven days of bad things. "Commander Elias Pemberton did not vanish. That's the story the navy let stand because it was cheaper than the truth. The truth is that he testified. Fully, voluntarily, with documents — the same documents, I'd wager, that you've been carrying across the Serrats in an oilskin case. He stood in a hearing room in the capital and told the truth about what the Vantage was carrying and who ordered her scuttled and who was paid to look away. And then—"

"Then he disappeared," Ivo said. His own voice sounded far away to him, as if he were hearing it through water, through a hull, through twenty years.

"Then he was remanded into private custody," Voss said, "pending resolution of certain commercial matters. That was the legal language. *Private custody.* He was taken to a consortium facility and held as a material witness, because a witness who can be produced is worth more than a witness who is dead — for a while. And then the matter was resolved, and the witness was—" the small sorrow again, the expert hand placing another stone, "—no longer required. That was nineteen years ago, Mr. Pemberton. I tell you this not as a kindness. I tell you because you're about to hand those papers to a magistrate, and when you do, the consortium will burn every facility, every ledger, every record of Elias Pemberton's existence, and whatever chance there is — and I'm not saying there is a chance, I'm saying *whatever* chance — that he is still alive in one of them will burn with it."

The room was silent. The stove ticked. Somewhere outside, the hammers on the palisade went on driving stakes into frozen ground, steady as a heartbeat, men building a wall against the winter as if a wall would be enough.

Ivo became aware that he was looking at his own hands. They were steady. Divers' hands, steady at depth, steady when the line goes slack, steady when the light dies and the thing you came for turns out to be something else. His father's hands. He had his father's hands, and his father's habit of standing with his weight even on both feet, and — apparently — his father's talent for surviving the consortium's attention, at least for a while.

Nineteen years. He did the arithmetic the way you do arithmetic in cold water, fast and without feeling, because feeling would stop your heart. Nineteen years ago Ivo had been a boy on a navy dock being told his father's ship was lost and his father was lost with her, and there had been a funeral with no body, and a pension, and a flag folded into a triangle, and a mother who had died inside the folded flag within two years. Nineteen years of a grave that wasn't a grave. Nineteen years during which the man might have been — might still be — in a room somewhere, with a lamp, waiting for someone to come.

"Miss Hale." Voss's voice again, gentle now, turning from him like a man turning from a finished transaction. "Your father wrote that report. Your mother carried it. They both paid for it. You can pay for it too, or you can be the Hale who finally, at the end of the whole sad business, acts in the family's actual interest. The consortium will pay for the papers. Well. The consortium will pay a great deal for the papers. And the magistrate—" he spread his soft hands, "—the magistrate will give you justice. A finding. A name in a record. Justice is a wonderful thing, Miss Hale. It's just very slow, and very cold, and it doesn't open doors."

"You're wrong about one thing," Mara said.

Ivo heard the change in her voice and knew it, the way he knew the change in the wind off the ice — the note that came before the whole surface moved.

"You said we've been carrying the papers in an oilskin case." She smiled, and it was Ivo's turn to see the worst thing in the room. "You've been watching us for eleven days and you still don't know what we have. You know what Juniper gave him. You don't know what my father hid in the lining. You don't know the names on the second page. So before you talk to me about burning facilities, Deputy — or whatever you are — you should understand that the document you want doesn't exist in any facility of yours. It exists in my head, and in his, and it will exist in the provincial record, and there is no price and no fire that reaches it."

Voss looked at her for a long moment. Then at Ivo. Then he did the thing that told Ivo more than everything else in the room: he stopped performing. The warmth went out of his face like a lamp turned down, and what was left underneath was just a man doing arithmetic.

"Nine days," Voss said. "The pass closes. And Mr. Pemberton — the offer regarding your father stands for nine days as well. After that, the winter takes the passes and we take the records, and in spring there will be nothing left to find. Think about which dead man you owe." He stood. The gray coat stood with him. "The Waystation will keep your room. Consider it a courtesy of the office."

They walked out into the blue morning, past the green door, down Tanner's Row, through the smell of hides and lye, and neither of them spoke until the chandlery was behind them and the crowd noise of the Waystation swallowed the sound of their footsteps.

Then Mara stopped, in the lee of the woodpile where they'd stood the night before, and turned to face him, and Ivo saw that her eyes were wet and furious and that she was holding herself the way she held herself on bad ice.

"Say it," he said. "Whatever it is. Say it now."

"He's alive." Her voice cracked down the middle. "Ivo. Your father might be *alive*, and you've spent eleven days keeping my mother's promise, and I watched you just now — I watched your face — and I know what you're going to do, and I need you to say it to me before you do it, because if you leave me to find out at the crossroads—"

The hammers drove their stakes. The pass stood open, nine days wide, a black notch in the white shoulder of the Serrats, and at the bottom of it, six hundred miles back, a dead woman's promise and a living man's father were pulling him apart like two teams on one rope.

"Mara," Ivo said. "I have to tell you something about what I'm going to do."

And the worst of it was that he didn't know yet which thing he was going to tell her.
