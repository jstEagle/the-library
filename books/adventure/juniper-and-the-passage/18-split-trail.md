## 18. Split Trail

The crossroads announced itself before they saw it: a leaning post with three arms, its paint scoured to bare wood by a century of weather, standing where the valley road forked like a river deciding which sea to die in. To the left, the track climbed toward the consulate town of Verrin Hollow, where the consortium kept its records office behind iron shutters and polite clerks. To the right, the road dropped through stands of larch toward Passage itself, and the magistrate's court, and whatever justice might still be reached before the snows sealed the high country like a lid on a jar.

Ivo stood at the post a long time without touching it.

The morning was cold enough that his breath hung around him in slow rags. Below them the valley lay in blue shadow, frost silvering the fence lines, woodsmoke rising straight as plumb lines from the chimneys of farms that had no idea what two half-frozen travelers were carrying between them — or rather, what Mara carried, sewn now into the lining of her coat, her father's final report pressed flat against her ribs like a second heartbeat.

"Verrin Hollow is four hours' walk," he said. "Passage is six."

"I know how to read a map, Ivo." Mara's voice was hoarse from the storm, from four days of speaking only in necessities. She had not yet forgiven him. He understood that she might never, entirely. But she had read Juniper's letter again last night by the fire, all of it this time, aloud, and something in her face afterward had told him the ground between them had shifted — not to forgiveness, but to something harder and more useful. Purpose.

He turned to look at her. Frost had gotten into her eyebrows; there was a crack at the corner of her mouth that the cold kept reopening. She looked, he thought, like everything his life had failed to be until three months ago.

"My father may be alive," he said.

* * *

IVO

He had rehearsed the argument all night while she slept, and it still came out wrong — too fast, too raw, stripped of the careful neutrality he'd worn for thirty years like a diver's suit.

"The agent said custody. Not dead. Custody." Ivo heard his own voice crack on the word and hated it. "Twelve years I've believed he abandoned us. Walked out of my mother's life and mine and never sent so much as a letter. And now you're telling me—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "Now I'm telling myself he didn't choose to go. He was taken. And he might still be sitting in some room in Verrin Hollow waiting for someone to come."

Mara said nothing. That was the worst of it. She had learned from him, somewhere on the traverse, how to let silence do the cutting.

"He testified," Ivo went on. "That's what the agent said. He testified against them and then disappeared. You don't keep a man alive twelve years unless he's worth something alive. Unless they're afraid of what happens if he isn't."

"And if he is?" Mara asked. Her voice was level, almost gentle, which somehow made it worse. "If he's alive, Ivo, what then? You walk into their office and ask politely? The man who tracked us down the ridge is in that town. The one whose friends we left bleeding on the ice. You'd hand yourself over to get to a cell next to your father's?"

"I'd hand over anything," he said. "You don't understand because your parents are—"

He stopped himself, but the damage was done. He watched it land. Mara's jaw tightened, and for a moment he saw her mother in her face — the same stubborn architecture of grief.

"Don't," she said quietly. "Don't tell me what I understand about losing parents to that ship."

* * *

MARA

Her father had been aboard the *Vantage* when it went down. She had learned that in the mining camp, in the ashfall, reading her mother's map by lamplight while the storm tried to bury the world. She had learned it the way you learn a bone is broken — not all at once but in a single sickening shift, everything above and below the fracture suddenly wrong.

And now Ivo wanted to walk away from the thing her father had died guarding.

She made herself breathe. The cold helped; it always did. Cold simplified. You breathed or you didn't. You walked or you froze. Everything else — love, fury, the terrible tenderness she felt watching this man argue himself into ruin — could wait until the pass was crossed.

"If we split up," she said, "we both fail."

"No. If we split up, one of us—"

"One of us *might* succeed, if nothing goes wrong, and something always goes wrong." She pulled off a glove and touched the coat lining, feeling the stiff rectangle of oilskin and paper beneath the wool. It had become automatic, this checking, the way other people touched talismans. "Listen to me. My father wrote that report knowing he would probably die for it. He hid it in a map and gave the map to my mother, and my mother carried it for twenty years, coughing her lungs out in taverns, waiting for someone she could trust with it. She chose *you*, Ivo. Out of every diver on that coast, she chose the son of the man who sank the ship. Do you think that was an accident? Do you think she didn't know exactly who you were?"

"She didn't tell me."

"No. Because you'd have refused. Because you were so busy being nobody's son that you couldn't have carried anybody's promise." She saw him flinch and did not soften it. "The report names the crime. It names the men who ordered the scuttling. It names the cargo they murdered forty sailors to hide. If it reaches the magistrate, the consortium falls. Their records office burns — legally, officially, every page of it. Including any record of what happened to Commander Pemberton after he testified."

"That takes months. Years, maybe. Court proceedings—"

"And if you go to Verrin Hollow today, it takes nothing. They take the report off your body and put you in the room next to your father, and in ten years some other fool gets a map and starts walking." She stepped closer. Their breath mingled in the frozen air, hers and his, indistinguishable. "I am asking you to let the dead defend themselves for six more hours. Six hours, Ivo. Then do whatever you have to do."

"It might be twelve years," he said, barely audible. "It might be twelve years too late already."

Mara looked at him — at the frost in his beard, the exhaustion carved under his eyes, the way his hands hung at his sides like a man who had forgotten what they were for except holding rope and holding on. She loved him. She had known it since the lake, since she'd dragged his weight across the ice with her arms screaming and thought, absurdly, *not this, not him, I've lost enough.* Love had arrived uninvited and unwelcome and it changed nothing about what was right.

Which was, she was beginning to understand, the whole cruelty of it.

* * *

IVO

He walked to the signpost and put his hand on it. The wood was wet and freezing and utterly indifferent.

Six hours. She was right — he knew she was right the way you know a current is stronger than you are, by the pull of it against everything you want. The report had to reach Passage. Forty names on a manifest that never officially existed. His father's name, maybe, among the testimony. And if the consortium fell, the walls around his father — if his father lived — fell with them.

But twelve years. Twelve years of believing himself abandoned, and the man might be four hours away, breathing the same valley air, and Ivo was supposed to walk in the opposite direction?

"You could come with me," he said. "To Verrin Hollow. We take the office together tonight, find what's there, and still make Passage by tomorrow night. The passes won't close that fast."

"They'll close faster than anyone thinks. The storm broke early — that cuts both ways. And the agent knows your face now. The moment you set foot in that town, every man he can buy is looking for you." She shook her head slowly. "I'm delivering the report. That's not negotiable. My father died for it. So the only question is whether you're walking beside me or away from me."

There it was. Laid out on the frozen road like a blade.

He thought about his mother, who had died still setting a place at the table some nights, out of habit, out of a hope she'd been ashamed of. He thought about the *Vantage*'s flooded hold, the crates that matched no manifest, the silence the navy had wrapped around the wreck like a shroud. He thought about Juniper Hale's hand in his, light as a bird's, making him swear.

*I'm not asking you to be brave,* she'd said. *I'm asking you to be true.*

"Mara." He said her name and found he had nothing to put after it. All the arguments had run out and left only the fact underneath: that he loved her, that she was walking into a town full of hired men carrying the most dangerous paper in the province, and that he could not be in two places at once, and the universe had never once, in all his life, cut him a kindness on that particular terms.

"I know," she said. Her eyes were bright and furious and dry. "I know. Go."

* * *

MARA

They divided the gear in silence, and it was the silence that undid her more than anything — the practiced economy of it, two people who knew each other's kit well enough to divide it without a word. His rope went to her pack because she'd need it on the descent. Her spare socks went to him because his had frozen solid in the lake crossing and never fully dried. Small intimacies, handed back and forth like coins at a parting.

At the last, he took the map case — the empty one, the oilskin ruined by the lake, the original report already hidden in her coat. He held it a moment, turning it over in his scarred hands.

"For luck," he said, and gave it back to her.

"That's not luck. That's garbage."

A ghost of a smile crossed his face, there and gone. "Then we match."

She laughed — a short, broken sound, startled out of her — and then hated herself for laughing, and then decided she didn't care. If these were the last minutes, let them be human ones.

He cupped her face in both hands. His palms were cold and cracked and they trembled slightly, and she understood that the trembling wasn't cold. "Passage," he said. "The magistrate's office. Don't stop for anything. Don't talk to anyone. If the agent finds you before you reach the door—"

"I know what to do."

"Tell me anyway."

"Run," she said. "Scream fire. People come running to fires even when they won't come for strangers."

"Good." His thumb traced her cheekbone once, memorizing it, and she let him, and then she stepped back because if she didn't step back now she wouldn't, and the morning was burning, and the passes were closing, and the dead were waiting on both roads.

"Ivo." He paused, halfway turned toward Verrin Hollow, a dark figure against the pale slope. "Your father testified. Whatever else he was, whatever he did — he tried to tell the truth at the end. So did my father. So did my mother." She lifted her chin toward the magistrate's road. "Let's finish what they started."

He nodded once. Then he turned and walked, and she watched him go — up the left-hand track, growing smaller against the white shoulder of the mountain, not looking back, because looking back was a luxury neither of them could afford.

When he passed out of sight behind the ridge, Mara Hale shouldered her pack, pressed her hand once against the paper over her heart, and started down toward Passage alone.

* * *

IVO

He did not look back. He had counted the steps instead — a diver's habit, marking distance in numbers when the heart got too loud — and by the time he reached the first bend in the track he had counted past the point where turning around would mean anything except undoing them both.

The road to Verrin Hollow climbed through silent larches, needles gold and falling, and Ivo Pemberton walked it with an emptiness in his chest that was not entirely grief. Part of it was lighter than grief. Part of it was the strange, terrifying freedom of a man who has finally chosen, and chosen wrong or right he could not yet say, but chosen — wholly, irreversibly, with his whole buried self.

Four hours to the consulate town. Four hours to find out whether his father was a prisoner or a memory.

In the valley below, faint with distance, he could just make out the roofline of Passage, and a church bell ringing the hour — six bells, the sound carrying clean across the frozen air.

Six hours, Mara had said. He lengthened his stride.

Behind him, far up the trail they had descended together, a rider crested the ridge — a single figure, motionless against the sky, watching the valley where the two roads divided.

Watching him go.
