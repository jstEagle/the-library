## 19. The Consulate Records Office

The consulate office in Passage sat on Cordwainer Street like a man pretending to be asleep — shutters drawn, brass plate polished, a clerk's window that opened at nine and closed at noon whether or not anyone had come. Ivo had watched it for two days from the loft of the saddlery across the way, learning its rhythms the way he'd once learned the rhythms of tide and current: when the lamplighter passed, when the agent Vess came and went, when the night watchman made his rounds and how long he lingered at the cookshop on the corner, where the proprietor kept a stove going and opinions flowing.

Two days was long enough. The pass would close soon. Mara was already gone, three hours ahead of him on the magistrate's road with her father's report sewn into her coat lining, and every hour he spent here was an hour she traveled without him between her and the men on the ridge.

But his father might be behind that door.

The thought had carried him through the split at the crossroads, through the cold night walking back down-valley, through the morning of watching and waiting. *Testified, then disappeared.* The agent's words had lodged in him like a splinter working toward the heart. Not dead. Disappeared. Men who disappeared could be found. His whole life had been built on the arithmetic of salvage — what sank could be raised, what was lost could be recovered, given enough line and enough bottom time. He had never once considered that some things were lost on purpose.

He went in at dusk, when the lamplighter had passed and the watchman was deep in his stew.

* * *

The lock on the back door was old work, older than the consortium's presence in Passage, and it gave to Ivo's picks with the small complaining sounds of metal remembering its age. He'd learned locks in the navy yards as a boy, running errands for officers who never asked where their sons went after dark, and the knowledge had sat in his hands all these years like a coin in a pocket, waiting.

Inside smelled of paper and lamp oil and something faintly medicinal — ink preservative, maybe, or the tonic of a man who worked too many hours. He stood in the dark of the entry hall and let his eyes adjust, counting doors by the gray light leaking under them. Three. A stairway rising to a second floor. The clerk's front room would be to his left; records kept elsewhere, if they were kept at all.

He tried the nearest door. Locked. The second opened onto the clerk's room — ledgers in ranks on shelves, a desk with its drawers neatly closed, everything squared away with the particular tidiness of a man who had something to hide and hid it in order rather than in darkness. Ivo lit no lamp. He'd spent enough hours in black water to work by feel, and his fingers read the ledger spines one by one: shipping schedules, tariff filings, harbor dues. Nothing but the honest face of the consortium's business.

The third door was the stairwell door, and the stairs went up to a landing with two rooms. The first was a bedroom, recently used, the bedclothes thrown back, a valise open on the chair. Vess's quarters, then. He gave it thirty seconds and nothing more.

The second room had a lock worth respecting — newer, oiled, the kind of mechanism that cost money because it protected something that cost more. Ivo knelt before it in the dark and worked it slowly, listening past his own pulse to the tumblers, and when it gave he felt no triumph at all. Only the sensation, familiar from the wreck, of passing through a hatch into a compartment where the water had been waiting a long time for someone to open the door.

* * *

The room beyond was cold. No stove had ever been piped in here; this was not a room where a man sat and worked. It was a room where papers were kept the way a body is kept — out of sight, preserved against decay, visited rarely.

He risked the shuttered lantern, turning the wick down to a bead of flame. Filing cabinets in two rows. A map table with weights on its corners. And on the table, laid out flat beneath the weights, a single folder of heavy gray stock, stamped with the consortium's seal and marked in a clerk's careful hand:

**PEMBERTON, C. — MATTER CLOSED.**

Ivo did not move for a long moment. The flame trembled in its glass. Outside, far off, a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

He lifted the weight. His hands were steady — they were always steady, hands that had worked lines in freezing water — but behind the steadiness something was coming apart quietly, the way ice comes apart on a spring lake, without sound, without drama, all at once.

*Matter closed.*

He opened the folder.

The first page was a deposition summary. His father's name at the top — Commander Callum Pemberton — and beneath it, in compressed bureaucratic language, the shape of a testimony: the scuttling of the freighter *Vantage*, ordered under consortium instruction, the cargo manifest falsified before departure, the true manifest — munitions, and worse, materiel sold to a party the summary declined to name — routed overland through the Serrat Range under separate cover. His father had signed the false manifest, then un-signed it, then sailed the ship to the coordinates and opened her seams himself, staying aboard until the last boat pulled away.

Ivo had known most of it since Ashfall Camp, since Mara had read the fragments aloud by firelight while he pretended to study the map. Knowing it secondhand had been bearable. Reading it in the consortium's own hand — seeing his father reduced to a file number, a liability assessed and managed — was another thing entirely. Grief, he discovered, had layers, like a wreck settling into silt. You thought you'd reached the bottom and found there was another deck below.

He turned the page.

A surveillance report, dated eleven years back. *Subject located in the coastal settlements south of Greywater. Employed as a harbor pilot. No indication subject has contacted family. Recommend continued observation.*

His father had been alive. Eleven years ago, alive, working tides within a hundred miles of the coast where Ivo had grown up diving wrecks, close enough that on some clear day they might have stood on opposite shores of the same bay. And the consortium had watched him the way Ivo watched a promising wreck site — patiently, professionally, waiting for the value to surface or the structure to collapse.

The next pages told him which it had been.

An incident report. A fall from the pilot's staging at Greywater harbor, ruled accidental by the local magistrate — a magistrate whose name appeared, Ivo noticed with a stillness that felt colder than the Frozen Lake, on a list of retainer payments filed three months later. The report was dated nine years ago. Nine years. Mara had been a girl. Juniper Hale had still been walking the high country, coughing blood into the snow, carrying a map she couldn't deliver herself because the men it named were still looking for anyone connected to the *Vantage*.

And at the bottom of the incident report, clipped with a brass fastener gone green with age, was a final memo, one line long, initialed by a hand Ivo didn't recognize:

*Subject deceased. File to be retained pending resolution of outstanding claims. Matter closed.*

Ivo set the folder down on the map table with exaggerated care, the way he'd handle a decompression bottle at depth, because his hands wanted to shake and he would not let them.

* * *

He had come up the mountain for a living man. Every hard mile, every frozen camp, every hour of fever — he had told himself the story straight through the worst of it: *find your father, make it right, choose him over the promise if you must.* Love against duty, and he had chosen love, and the love had turned out to be a ghost wearing a harbor pilot's coat, drowned nine years before Ivo ever saw Juniper Hale dying in a tavern that smelled of tar and cheap gin.

He sat down on the filing cabinet and put his face in his hands and let it come.

It came the way grief comes to men who have practiced silence — privately, brutally, without witness. His shoulders shook. His breath tore. Somewhere in the middle of it he heard his own voice, very low, saying *Da* the way he hadn't said it since he was twelve years old, since the morning the naval courier had come to the house and said *missing, presumed lost at sea*, and his mother had begun the long fading that ended four winters later, and Ivo had signed articles aboard a salvage ship because a man who could hold his breath for four minutes had no need to breathe at home.

Nine years. The man had been alive when Ivo was twenty-two, diving the coast, making his name pulling cargo out of drowned ships. Alive, and a hundred miles away, and watched. If Ivo had looked — if he had ever once believed his father might have walked away from the sea instead of sinking into it —

No. He caught the thought and held it, the way you hold a line when the current wants it. This was the consortium's arithmetic too: the son who stopped searching, the widow who drank herself quiet, the file gathering dust in a cold room. They had counted on exactly this. Grief was their cheapest employee. It had worked for them for nine years without wages.

He wiped his face on his sleeve and stood up.

Because there was one more thing in the folder. He'd seen it as he turned the pages and made himself go back for it now, though his whole body argued otherwise. A cross-reference slip, filed loose at the back: *Matter Pemberton — see also Matter HALE, J., active. Subject's daughter believed in possession of cartographic material originating with deceased crew member R. Hale. Surveillance authorized.*

Active. Not closed. Mara's name wasn't written there, but it didn't need to be. *Believed in possession.* The men on the ridge hadn't been following the map. They'd been following her — and him — all along, and somewhere ahead on the magistrate's road, alone, sewing her father's report against her ribs, Mara Hale was walking into a net whose knots Ivo had just seen documented in the consortium's own gray folders.

He took the cross-reference slip. He took the deposition summary — his father's own words, compressed but his, the only testament the man had left, and Ivo would not leave it in this cold room to be burned when the matter finally resolved itself. The rest he photographed in memory, page by page, the old diver's trick: look, look again, surface with it intact.

Then he closed the folder, replaced the weights, locked the door behind him, and stood a moment in the dark of the stairwell with his hand flat against the wall, steadying himself the way you steady yourself on a ladder before the last few fathoms.

One door had closed. He felt it shut — felt the specific, physical finality of it, like a hatch dogged down against the sea. His father was dead. There would be no reunion, no reckoning across a table, no answer to any of the questions Ivo had carried since he was twelve. That account was settled, and settled badly, and no amount of bottom time would raise anything from it.

But grief, he understood now, standing in the dark with his father's testimony folded against his chest, was not the same as an ending. Juniper had taught him that — dying by inches in a tavern, trading her last possession for a stranger's promise. The dead did not ask you to stop. They asked you to carry.

His father had testified. Mara's father had died guarding the truth of it. Two men, one crime, and one woman on a road ahead with the evidence and no idea that the men hunting her had a file with her mother's name on it, marked *active*.

Ivo blew out the lantern, went down the stairs, and let himself out the back door into the blue cold of evening. Somewhere north, the pass was closing. Somewhere on the magistrate's road, Mara was walking alone into a surveillance net with a date on it.

He turned his collar up against the wind and started running.
