## 20. The Magistrate's Door

The town of Passage smelled of woodsmoke and wet wool, and to Mara it had never smelled so much like the end of something.

She came down off the last shoulder of the range with her boots soaked through and her lungs burning, and below her the valley opened like a held breath finally let go — a scatter of slate roofs, a church spire crooked as a broken finger, and at the far end of the main street, squat and gray behind its iron fence, the provincial magistrate's office. She could see the flag on its pole, hanging limp in the windless cold. She could see the lamplighter working his way down the street with his long pole, unhurried, a man whose evening contained nothing worse than frost.

*Three hundred yards,* she thought. *Three hundred yards of open street.*

After everything — the river that had tried to drown Ivo, the ice that had nearly taken them both, the fever, the ambush on the ridge, the four days pinned in the snowbound cabin where they had said things to each other that could not be unsaid — after all of it, it came down to three hundred yards of open ground, and somewhere behind her, closing fast, the men who wanted what she carried never to be read.

Her hand went to the inside of her coat, where the oilskin packet lay against her ribs. The map was ruined — water-bled, half its ink dissolved into ghost-shapes — but she no longer needed it. For six nights in the cave, by firelight, while Ivo shivered and burned and fought whatever war fever wages in the blood, she had copied every line of it into her memory, and folded into its lining she had found the thing that mattered more: her father's final report, written in a hand she almost didn't recognize because she had been nine years old when he died, naming names, dates, tonnages — the consortium's crime laid out in her father's careful, dying arithmetic.

*Deliver it before the passes close.* Her mother's voice, thin and bloody and certain, saying it across a tavern table to a stranger. *Promise me.*

Mara had spent most of her life being angry at Juniper Hale for choosing the mountains over her, for choosing maps over a daughter. It had taken a frozen lake and a man's near-death for her to understand that her mother had chosen her too — chosen her the only way a dying woman with enemies could: by finding someone stubborn enough and lost enough to carry the truth the whole distance.

*Ivo.* She glanced back up the trail. Empty. Snow squalls moving through the pines like ghosts changing rooms.

He had left before dawn, when the storm broke early and the far pass showed a seam of blue sky, and he had not told her his plan so much as let her read it in his face. *They'll follow me,* he'd said, lacing his boots in the dark. *I'm the one the agent knows. I'm the one they want.* And when she had started to argue, he had put his hands on either side of her face, cold fingers against cold skin, and said, quietly, the way men say things they've already decided: *You're the one who can finish this. I'm just the one who can run.*

She had kissed him then — hard, furious, memorizing him — and gone down the mountain alone.

Now she stood at the tree line, watching the street, and made herself think like a guide instead of a grieving daughter. The magistrate's office had one public entrance, iron-fenced yard, a side gate for clerks. Lamplighters meant dusk, and dusk meant shadows pooling between buildings. The pursuers would expect her to come in from the pass road, from the north. So she wouldn't. There was a drainage cut behind the tannery — she'd marked it on the way into the valley years ago, on a trip she'd taken with her mother, back when there were still trips to take — and it ran behind the chandlery and came out two doors from the magistrate's fence.

She moved.

The snow squeaked under her boots. Her breath tore out of her in white rags. She kept to the shadowed side of the buildings, past the closed market stalls with their awnings sagging under new snow, past a tavern where laughter leaked warm through shuttered windows — a whole town going about its evening, none of them knowing that the last hours of an open pass were ticking down around them, that somewhere above the valley men were descending with guns and grievances.

At the mouth of the drainage cut she stopped, pressed her back to the tannery wall, and listened.

Hoofbeats. Not on the pass road — in the street. Coming fast.

She flattened herself into the cut's black throat as three riders swept past the end of the alley, and in the light of the corner lamp she saw the lead rider clearly: gray coat, gray eyes, the agent from the consulate office, the man who had looked at Ivo across a table in Passage and smiled like a man opening a ledger. Voss. He was shouting orders she couldn't catch, wheeling his horse toward the north end of the street — toward the pass road, toward where they expected her.

*Toward where Ivo is.*

Her chest clenched. She made herself breathe. Made herself count to five, the way her mother had taught her to count out a bearing. Then she ran.

Behind the chandlery, over a fence slick with frost, across a yard where laundry lines hung dead and white with ice. The magistrate's iron gate rose in front of her, and beyond it the office windows glowed yellow, and a clerk was just stepping out to light the lamp over the door.

"Sir!" Her voice cracked on it. "Sir — please — I need the magistrate. Tonight. Now."

The clerk turned, took in the frostbitten woman with the pack and the wild eyes, and began the sentence every bureaucrat is born knowing — *"Office hours are—"*

"The Vantage," Mara said. "The navy freighter. I have Commander Hale's final report."

Something changed in the clerk's face. Small, but real — the flicker of a man who has heard a word he wasn't supposed to hear. He looked past her at the emptying street, and then, quickly, unlocked the gate.

Inside, the office smelled of ink and coal smoke. A junior magistrate — young, tired, spectacles fogged — met them in the corridor, and Mara pulled off her gloves with stiff fingers and unwrapped the oilskin packet, and for one suspended moment she simply held it, feeling the weight of it, the weight of everyone who had died to move it those few inches from her ribs to a government desk. Her mother. Her father. The crew of the Vantage, thirty-one souls the manifests said were carrying grain and were actually carrying evidence of a crime that had bought and buried a fleet's worth of silence.

And Ivo — who had carried it too, though it cost him his name, his trade, nearly his life; who was at this moment riding north into the teeth of it so that her hands could stay free.

"Read it," she said, and her voice did not shake, and she was proud of that. "All of it. Before anyone tells you not to."

The junior magistrate broke the seal. His eyes moved down the page. Stopped. Moved again, slower. When he looked up, the tiredness was gone from his face and something older and harder had replaced it.

"Where," he said carefully, "did you get this?"

"It was hidden in a map my mother carried for eleven years." Mara heard her own voice go quiet. "My name is Mara Hale. My father wrote that report. My mother kept it alive. A salvage diver named Ivo Pemberton got it across the Serrat Range — and the men who killed my father are in your town right now trying to make sure you never read it."

As if the words had summoned them, hooves sounded again outside — closer now, turning, coming back. The clerk blew out the corridor lamp on instinct, and in the sudden dark Mara heard the riders slow at the gate, heard the creak of leather, a voice low and clipped giving an order.

Then, from the north end of the street, cutting through the cold air like a blade:

A gunshot.

* * *

Ivo had never been much of a rider, and the horse knew it.

They had come down off the pass together in the gray hour before dawn, man and animal equally resentful, equally afraid, and he had talked to the horse the whole way down because talking kept the fear in a shape he could carry. Below them Passage had lit itself window by window, and he had watched Mara's small dark figure detach from the tree line and flow toward the magistrate's office like water finding its level, and he had felt something in his chest that was not quite relief and not quite terror and was, he understood finally, simply love — love with nowhere left to hide.

Then he had turned the horse north, toward the pass road, and made himself visible.

It hadn't taken long. Voss's men were professionals; they'd tracked them since the ridge, since the ambush, since the frozen lake where Ivo had gone under the ice and come up a different man. They spotted him within the hour, and he led them on a long, cold dance along the valley's northern rim — close enough to be seen, far enough to stay free — until the light failed and the riders grew impatient and the game became something else.

Now he sat his blowing horse at the edge of the lamplight at the north end of the main street, and Voss sat twenty yards away with two of his men flanking him, and the other two were already moving south, toward the magistrate's office, and Ivo understood with cold clarity that he had failed — that he had drawn their attention and not their numbers, and that Mara had perhaps ninety seconds of safety left.

"You should have taken the money, Pemberton." Voss's voice carried easily in the still air. Pleasant. That was the worst of him — the pleasantness, the ledger-keeper's calm. "Your father did. At first. Did you know that? Your father took consortium money for two years before his conscience got expensive."

"I know what my father did," Ivo said. "I read his report."

"He was a coward at the end. Ran to testify instead of settling it privately. Cost us a great deal." Voss shifted in the saddle, and Ivo saw his hand drift toward his coat. "You're a coward too. All this way, and you send the girl in alone."

"Yes," Ivo said. "That's rather the point."

He fired from the saddle — not at Voss, but at the lamp bracket above the chandlery door, and the glass burst and the street plunged into a pocket of darkness, and in that darkness he wheeled the horse and shouted, loud, ragged, unmistakable: *"Mara! Run!"* — a lie shaped like a warning, because she was already safe behind iron and law, and every man on that street needed to believe otherwise.

The return shot cracked past his ear. The horse screamed and Ivo went off its back, hit the packed snow, rolled, came up behind a water trough with his pistol out and his heart going like a stamp mill. He fired twice more, blind, buying seconds, spending them recklessly because seconds were the only currency he had left and he had never in his life spent anything so gladly.

Boots on snow. Shouting. Through the gap beneath the trough he saw shapes converging on him — and then, from the direction of the magistrate's office, a sound he would remember for the rest of his life: a bell. The office bell, rung hard, rung in alarm, and a voice amplified by a speaking trumpet, official and furious:

*"In the name of the provincial magistrate — drop your weapons! You are surrounded!"*

Lanterns bloomed in windows up and down the street. Doors opened. The town of Passage, which had slept through the whole war coming down its mountain, woke all at once, and the arithmetic changed. Four men with guns against a street full of lights and rifles and righteous inconvenience.

Voss's men melted backward into the alley by the tannery. Voss himself hesitated one moment too long, and Ivo, rising from behind the trough with both hands raised and blood running warm down his temple where a splinter of lamp glass had caught him, looked the agent full in the face.

"They'll want a name," Ivo said. "Give them yours. My father's is already spoken for."

They took Voss at the corner by the church, three constables and a dozen townsfolk, and he went quietly, adjusting his cuffs, already composing whatever version of events he would carry to whatever power still claimed him. Ivo watched from the trough, swaying, and found he felt nothing at all — no triumph, no satisfaction. Just the enormous, ringing emptiness that follows when a thing you have carried so long is finally set down.

"Mister Pemberton."

The junior magistrate stood over him, spectacles fogged, breath steaming, holding out a hand. Behind him, flying down the middle of the street with her coat open and her hair half-loose and her eyes blazing, came Mara.

Ivo had time to stand fully upright before she reached him. He had time to notice that she was crying without any noise at all, tears freezing on her jaw. He had time to say, "Did you—"

She hit him like a rockslide, arms around him, face buried in his shoulder, and he held on, and the cold, and the blood, and the fear, and the eleven years of his father's silence, and the whole white weight of the Serrat Range — all of it receded, just slightly, just enough.

"You absolute fool," she said into his coat. "You beautiful, impossible fool."

"Your mother hired the right diver," he managed.

Behind them, the junior magistrate was shouting for the office to be sealed, for the telegraph line to be checked, for witnesses. Above the rooftops, far to the north, the pass stood black against the last violet band of twilight — and even as they watched, a lantern swung out from the signal station on the heights, and the closure flag climbed the pole: red, red, red.

The passes were officially closed. No one else was coming. No one else could follow.

Whatever happened next would happen here, in the valley, in the light.

Mara pulled back far enough to look at him, and touched the bleeding cut on his temple with two cold fingers, and said, "There's something you need to know. Something I found in the report's lining — a second page, in my father's hand. Ivo... there's a name on it I don't understand. A witness list. And your father's name isn't crossed out."

The bell above the magistrate's office was still ringing, and Ivo felt the ground shift beneath him all over again.
