## 1. The Thief Who Stole Nothing

Nine days out from the registry office at Cindermark, Astrid Oyelaran had learned three things about the man he was chasing.

He walked at night. He drank his water warm, because he never stopped long enough to build a fire for anything but tea, and even the tea he made in a dented tin cup that caught the starlight and threw it back like a coin at the bottom of a well. And he was afraid — not the ordinary fear of a man crossing empty country, which every traveler carried like a second shadow, but a specific, gnawing, forward-looking fear, the kind that made a man check his backtrail too often and sleep in hollows with two exits.

Astrid knew that fear. He had worn it himself, once, years ago, before the ranger's oath had settled over him like a coat of armor that never quite fit right. Fear like that meant the man wasn't running *from* something so much as running *toward* something he believed would kill him anyway.

The flats stretched out ahead of him, white as a scalded sky, cracked into hexagonal plates by a sun that had been squeezing this country dry for eleven years. The heat came up off the salt in waves that bent the horizon into liquid, so that the distant mesas seemed to float, unmoored, islands in a sea of shimmer. There was no shade. There was no wind worth the name. There was only the crunch of salt crust underfoot — a sound like walking on old porcelain — and the small, persistent voice in Astrid's head asking him, for the ninth consecutive day, what exactly he thought he was doing.

Because here was the thing. Here was the thing that had kept him on this trail past the point where any reasonable officer of the law would have turned around.

He didn't know what was stolen.

* * *

The report had come to him at the end of a season that had already gone badly. His contract with the Cindermark district was expiring; his pay purse held less than it should have; his boots were down to their last resoling. He'd been sitting in the shade of the registry office's awning, waiting to file his quarterly report, when the clerk — a nervous young woman named Pell who alphabetized her ink bottles — came rushing out with her hands pressed to her cheeks like a woman who had seen a ghost.

"Someone's been in the records," she said. "Someone's been in the death rolls."

Astrid had looked at her the way he looked at most people who interrupted his paperwork: patiently, and without much hope that it would amount to anything. "What's missing?"

"That's just it," she said, and her voice went thin and strange. "Nothing's missing. Everything's still there. But someone copied out an entry. A whole entry. Name, date, claim number, last known location." She swallowed. "And then they burned the copy paper in my waste bin, and left the ash."

An entry copied and then destroyed. A theft of nothing. A crime with no object, no victim, no loss that anyone could name or measure or prosecute. The dead prospector whose record had been consulted — one Juniper Hale, registered deceased twenty years past, claim number 4-4-1-9, last known location a stretch of the eastern salt flats that appeared on no map Astrid had ever trusted — was exactly as dead and exactly as registered as he had been the day before. Nothing had changed except that somewhere out there, a stranger now carried the shape of a dead man's life in his head.

Pell wanted it investigated anyway. Her supervisor wanted it forgotten. The magistrate's office, when Astrid dutifully sent word, sent back a reply so fast and so dismissive that it felt less like indifference than like a door being closed before he could look through it: *No public interest served. File and forget.*

File and forget. Two words that should have ended the matter, and would have, if Astrid Oyelaran had been built for forgetting. He wasn't. He was built the way the desert builds its rivers: shallow, stubborn, and incapable of stopping until something gives.

So he'd pulled the original record himself, read it twice, and gone out onto the flats after a man whose face he had never seen, whose crime he could not name, and whose motive was a locked room with no windows.

* * *

On the third day he'd found the first campsite — a ring of stones arranged with a neatness that spoke of either military training or deep, compulsive habit — and beside it, half-buried in the salt, a scrap of copy paper, burned at the edges, on which survived a single legible fragment: *...claim number 4-4-1...*

On the fifth day, at a dried seep where the crust gave way to damp clay, he'd found a boot print a half-size smaller than his own, left-leaning, the heel worn on the outside edge. A man who favored his right knee. A man who had walked a long time on bad ground.

On the seventh day, the dust storm had come up out of the southwest like the end of the world, a brown wall two miles high that ate the sun and turned noon to dusk, and Astrid had spent six hours wrapped in his cloak with his bandana over his face, listening to the sand hiss against the salt and thinking, with a clarity that felt almost holy: *I could die out here for a piece of paper that says nothing happened.*

But the storm had passed, and the trail had held, and now it was the ninth day, and the trail was leading him somewhere.

That was the other thing he'd learned. The thief wasn't wandering. Every campsite, every water stop, every choice of route — they all pointed the same direction, east and slightly south, toward a stretch of the flats that the survey maps marked with a single word in faded government ink: *UNPRODUCTIVE.*

Nobody crossed the flats toward *unproductive*. Nobody walked nine days through the worst country in the district to reach a place where nothing grew, nothing lived, and nothing waited. Unless what they were looking for wasn't the kind of thing that showed up on maps.

Astrid crested a low rise of crusted salt at dusk and saw, far below, a pinprick of light. A fire. Small, shielded, the flame barely taller than a hand — the fire of a man who wanted warmth but not witnesses.

He stood there a long moment, watching it, feeling the day's heat drain out of his bones and the night cold rise up through the salt to take its place. Somewhere behind him, forty miles back, was the registry office, and the case file, and the world where this all made sense. Somewhere ahead was the fire, and the man, and whatever truth had driven him across the end of the earth carrying nothing but a dead man's name.

Astrid started down the slope.

* * *

He came in low and quiet, the way the rangers taught you — weight on the outside of your feet, breath timed to the wind, your silhouette folded into the dark until the dark forgot you were there. The fire's glow reached him before its warmth did, painting the salt crust amber, throwing long shadows from a scatter of gear: a bedroll, a water skin, the dented tin cup, a pack that sat slumped against a rock like a tired animal.

The man sat with his back to a boulder, his right leg stretched out stiff in front of him, and he was reading. Not by the firelight — by a tiny shuttered lantern, the kind surveyors used, its beam narrowed to a blade that fell across a folded page. Even at thirty paces Astrid could see the paper was old, soft with handling, covered in cramped handwriting and lines that might have been a map.

The man heard him at fifteen paces. Of course he did. You didn't survive nine days alone on the flats by being deaf.

"Don't," the man said, without turning around. His voice was hoarse, scraped thin by dust and disuse. "I've got nothing worth taking."

"I'm not a robber." Astrid stepped into the firelight, hands loose and visible at his sides, the way you did when you wanted a frightened man to stay frightened instead of becoming a desperate one. "Astrid Oyelaran. District ranger, Cindermark."

The man turned then, slowly, and Astrid got his first good look at him. Younger than the trail had made him seem — thirty, maybe thirty-five — with a face all sharp angles and exhaustion, dust worked so deep into the lines around his eyes it looked like age. His right knee was wrapped in a rag soaked in something dark. His hands, resting flat on his thighs, were trembling. Not from cold.

"You've been following me," the man said.

"Since Cindermark. Nine days."

"Nine days." The man laughed, a dry, airless sound with no humor anywhere in it. "For what? What do you think I took?"

"That's the question I've been asking myself since I started." Astrid crouched down at the edge of the firelight, keeping his weight off his heels, keeping everything easy and open. "Pell at the registry says nothing's missing. The magistrate's office says file and forget. So you tell me. What does a man walk nine days into the flats to do with a dead prospector's name?"

Something moved across the man's face — quick, involuntary, like a fish turning under ice. He looked down at the folded paper in his lap, and his thumb moved over it once, gently, the way you'd touch a wound.

"You read the record," he said. "Juniper Hale."

"I read the record."

"Then you know he's dead. Twenty years dead. Registered, witnessed, sealed." The man's voice had gone quiet, careful, each word set down like a foot on thin ice. "You can't steal from a dead man, ranger. That's the whole point of being dead. Everything's already been taken."

"Then why copy his entry?"

The silence stretched. The fire popped. Out on the flats, the night wind had finally risen, moving across the salt with a sound like slow breathing, and somewhere far off a coyote said something brief and bitter to the moon.

The man looked up, and in the firelight his eyes were wet, though his face stayed perfectly still.

"Because names are the last thing we own," he said. "And somebody out here is about to need one more than Juniper Hale does."

Astrid didn't move. Didn't blink. Twenty years on the flats had taught him that the moments that mattered always arrived dressed in stillness, and that the worst thing you could do was fill them with noise.

"Tell me your name," he said quietly.

The man smiled then — a small, broken, astonishing smile, the smile of a man setting down a weight he'd carried so long he'd forgotten it was heavy.

"That's the funny part," he said. "That's the whole funny part, ranger. I don't have one anymore. I gave it away." He tapped the folded paper against his knee, once, twice, like a man knocking on a door he knew wouldn't open. "The name I was born with is buried under a debt I couldn't pay, back in Saltmere, and the collectors own it now, same as they own everything else in that town. So I went to Cindermark and I found myself a dead man with no family, no friends, no grave anyone visits — and I took his name. Fair trade, near as I can figure. He's done with it. I need it."

"And the map?" Astrid nodded at the paper in the lantern light. "Dead men don't draw maps."

"No," the man agreed softly. "They don't."

He held the paper out then, across the fire, and Astrid took it, and the moment his fingers touched it he understood that the case he'd been chasing for nine days — the absurd, impossible, file-and-forget case of the stolen name — had just become something else entirely. Something older. Something with teeth.

The paper was a map. Hand-drawn, faded, annotated in two different hands, and marked at its center with a symbol Astrid had never seen but recognized instantly, the way you recognize a face from a dream: a circle bisected by a line, with water flowing through it.

At the bottom, in ink gone brown with age, a single line of text:

*The Rivergate. Where the water went. Where it's been ever since.*

Astrid looked up. The thief was watching him with an expression of terrible calm.

"Now you know why I ran," the man said. "And why they'll follow. And why, ranger, you have a choice to make — same as I did, nine days ago, sitting in that registry office with a pen in my hand."

The fire burned low between them. Out on the flats, the wind changed direction, and Astrid smelled it coming before he understood it: dust, and distance, and somewhere behind them both, faint but unmistakable, the sound of horses.
