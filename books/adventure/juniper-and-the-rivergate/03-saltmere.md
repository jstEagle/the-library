## 3. Saltmere

The town announced itself by smell before it showed itself at all — brine and rust and the faint chemical bite of alkali ponds, carried on a wind that had crossed two hundred miles of nothing to reach him. Astrid Oyelaran reined up on the last rise of the causeway road and looked down at Saltmere, and thought: *so this is what Juniper Hale's daughter calls home.*

It was not much. A company town in its second act of dying, laid out in a grid too ambitious for its water, streets that had been named for prosperity — Founders' Row, Meridian Avenue, the Corn Exchange — now serving as drainage channels for the pumps' runoff. The buildings were salt-bleached timber and corrugated iron, patched with patches over patches, and everything wore the same pale crust, as if the whole settlement were slowly being candied. Beyond the last row of houses, the desalination works squatted against the flats like a wounded animal: a sprawl of boiler sheds, settling tanks, and pump-houses, all of it steaming gently in the late-afternoon heat, all of it fed by a canal that came in from the coast thin as a vein in an old man's wrist.

Above it all, on the one hill the town could claim, stood the magistrate's hall — new timber, fresh white paint, a flagpole with a banner that hadn't faded because it hadn't been up long enough. The taxes had been coming to *that*, Astrid understood. To paint and banners and a roof that didn't leak, while the pump-houses below sweated and groaned and leaked from every seam.

He rode down into the town slowly. There was no point hurrying. He'd been carrying Juniper Hale's map for six days now, wrapped in oilcloth inside his coat, against his chest like a second heartbeat, and he had learned on the ride from the waystation that a thing you carry for a dying man does not get lighter with distance. It gets heavier. Every mile added weight to it, the way grief does.

*Saltmere,* Juniper had said, his voice already more air than sound, his hands — ruined hands, prospector's hands, scarred and split and trembling — pressing the oilcloth bundle flat against Astrid's sternum. *My daughter. Wren. Tell her — no. Don't tell her anything. Just give her the map. She'll know. Promise me.*

Astrid had promised. It had seemed a small thing at the time, there in the waystation's dim lamplight, with the old man's breath rattling like wind through a broken window. Promises made to dying men always seem small. They are never small. He knew that better than most.

Saltmere's main street was half-empty at the hour when other towns would have been filling. A water queue stretched from the public cistern halfway down Founders' Row — women mostly, and children too young to be ashamed of waiting, each with vessels ranked in order ahead of them, buckets and jerricans and clay jars standing sentinel in a line like patient soldiers. Nobody talked much. In towns where water was rationed, Astrid had noticed, talk got rationed too. Conversation cost moisture. Even the dogs lay flat in the shade with their tongues out, silent.

He dismounted at the livery-that-was-no-longer-a-livery — the sign said FEED & TACK, but the stalls held stacked crates and a family's entire household goods, and a boy of maybe ten watched him from atop the crates with the flat appraisal of a customs officer.

"Looking for work?" the boy asked.

"Looking for someone."

"Everyone's looking for someone." The boy shrugged with a whole philosophy in it. "Costs a coin to ask me questions."

Astrid flipped him a worn copper. "Wren Hale. Works the pumps."

The boy caught the coin out of the air without looking at it. His face did something complicated — not quite pity, not quite warning. "Pump-house three," he said. "Night shift starts at dusk. You'll find her before then if you're quick. And mister—" he hesitated, coin disappearing into some pocket, "—she don't like surprises."

"Neither do I," Astrid said. "We'll get along fine."

They didn't.

* * *

Pump-house three was the largest of the works, a cathedral of iron ribs and steam, and the noise inside it was a physical thing — a rhythmic, colossal *chug-groan, chug-groan* that Astrid felt in his back teeth before he was ten steps through the door. The air was wet and hot and tasted of salt and machine oil. Men and women moved along the catwalks in shifts, feeding the boilers, greasing the pistons, hauling the great leather gaskets that sealed the press heads and which lasted, judging by the pile of cracked ones by the door, perhaps a week apiece.

He found her at the far end, under the biggest of the press arms, alone with a maintenance job nobody else wanted: re-seating a valve collar the size of a wagon wheel, lying on her back beneath it with her arms buried to the elbow in the mechanism, steam drifting across her in slow white ribbons.

She was smaller than he'd expected. That was the first thing. Juniper had described his daughter in fragments between coughs — *tall like her mother, stubborn like me, good with her hands* — and Astrid had built a picture from the pieces, and the picture was wrong. She wasn't tall. She was compact, dense with muscle, built low like something designed to withstand weather. Her hair was cut brutally short, practical as a tool handle, dark with sweat and spray. Her sleeves were rolled above the elbow, and her forearms were a topography of scars: burn lines from steam, thin white nicks from metal edges, one long puckered ridge along the right forearm that could only have come from a scald — the kind of injury pump-workers got when a press let go and the boiling brine came out sideways.

She worked without wasted motion. Every turn of the wrench meant something. Astrid had spent nine days tracking a thief across empty country and had learned, in that emptiness, to read people the way he read ground — and this woman read as bedrock. Hard strata. Nothing loose on the surface.

He waited until she backed out from under the press arm, sat up, and reached for the rag hanging off her belt. Only then did he step into the light between them.

"Wren Hale?"

She didn't startle. That was the second thing he noticed. Her eyes came up and took his measure in one pass — face, hands, the ranger's badge at his collar, the dust of the road on his coat, the weight distribution of his stance — and dismissed whatever she found, all in about a second and a half.

"Who's asking?"

"Astrid Oyelaran. Desert ranger, out of Cordera station." He gave her the pause people expected after a name like that, the pause that said *legitimate, checkable.* "I'm here about your father."

Something happened behind her eyes. Not softening — the opposite. Something drew shut, like a shutter going across a window before the storm hits. She stood, unhurried, wiped her hands on the rag with terrible thoroughness, and hung it back on her belt.

"My father," she said, "is dead."

"He was alive six days ago."

That landed. He saw it land — a small contraction around her mouth, a stillness in her hands. But her voice, when it came, was level as poured concrete. "Then you're here late, and slow, and I'm sorry for it. Was there anything else? Because this shift runs till midnight and the foreman docks pay for idle talk."

"Juniper Hale died at a waystation called Two Wells, four days east of here. Bad lungs, bad heart, and twenty years of hard living catching up all at once. He knew it was the end. He had time to put his affairs in order." Astrid kept his voice even, unhurried, a ranger delivering a report rather than a man delivering a blow. "His affairs consisted of one item. He asked me to bring it to you."

"I don't want it."

"You don't know what it is."

"Don't care." She picked up her wrench. "Whatever it is, sell it. Drink the proceeds. Bury it. I've got a valve to seat before the night shift comes on and finds it weeping brine all over their boots."

"It's a map, Wren."

The wrench stopped moving. For a moment — just a moment — the shutter behind her eyes trembled. Then it slammed again, harder than before, and she turned on him with a fury so sudden and so complete that two workers down the catwalk paused and looked over.

"A map." She laughed, and it was the worst laugh Astrid had ever heard, dry as the flats outside, a laugh with nothing alive in it. "Of course it's a map. Twenty years, and the man sends a stranger with a *map.*" She stepped closer, close enough that he could see the salt dried in the creases of her knuckles, could smell the steam and sweat of her. "Did he say anything else? Did he happen to mention where he was sending maps *from*? Did he say why it took him two decades to remember he had a daughter?"

"No," Astrid said honestly. "He asked me to say nothing else. Those were his words. Just give her the map, and say nothing."

"Well, isn't that convenient." She spat past him, into the drain channel, with an accuracy that suggested long practice. "Say nothing. That was always his best skill. He could say nothing for years at a stretch. Champion of it."

"Wren—"

"Don't." The word cracked like a whip, and the nearest worker found somewhere else to look. "You don't get to use my name. You knew him for what, a day? An hour? You got the deathbed scene, the big request, the noble errand. I got a door closing when I was eleven years old and nothing since. Not a letter. Not a coin. Not a word passed through a traveler saying *he's alive, he thinks of you.* Nothing. So you can take your map and your sad duty-face and your ranger's oath and you can walk back out the way you came in, and you can tell the dead man's ghost that Wren Hale said—" her voice caught, just once, a hairline fracture in the concrete, instantly plastered over, "—said thank you for thinking of me at the very end, when it was free."

The press arm chose that moment to cycle, and the whole floor shuddered, and steam screamed briefly from a joint overhead, and in the noise and the shaking she turned away from him and went back to her knees beside the open valve, arms plunging into the machinery as if she could drown her own words in it.

Astrid stood there a long moment, feeling the map against his chest like a coal.

He had delivered death notices before. It was part of the work, out on the flats — riders who didn't come home, prospectors who walked into the sand and stayed there. He knew the grammar of grief, the way it came out sideways as anger more often than tears, especially in people who couldn't afford tears because tears cost water and dignity both. Everything she'd said was armor. He understood that. Understanding it didn't make it hurt less to stand in front of it.

But something else was bothering him, and it took him until he was halfway down the catwalk toward the door to name it.

*She didn't ask how he died.*

Not really. She'd asked where, and why-now, and rhetorical questions aimed like knives — but she hadn't asked the question underneath all the others, the one a daughter asks even furious, even armored: *was he alone? did he suffer? did he say my name at the end?*

She hadn't asked because asking would have been surrendering to hope, and hope was the one thing her armor wasn't built to stop. Astrid had seen soldiers do the same thing. Refuse to look at the wound because looking might mean bleeding.

He stopped at the head of the stairs. Turned back.

"The waystation keeper's wife sat with him his last hour," he said, pitching his voice under the machinery's rhythm, just loud enough. "He wasn't alone. And he went easy, at the end, once he'd made me promise. Like he'd been holding on for the errand and then he could finally set it down." He paused. "He said your name. Once. Right at the end. Not to me — to the ceiling. Like he was telling somebody who'd listen."

Her shoulders had gone rigid under the press arm. She didn't turn around. Her arms kept working, wrench turning, turning, turning, with a precision that had gone mechanical, automatic, all her attention clearly somewhere else entirely.

"I'll leave the map with the waystation keeper at Two Wells," Astrid said. "Under your name. It'll wait there as long as it takes. No charge, no conditions, no dead man's errand hanging over you. If you want it, it's yours. If you never come for it, it'll sit in a drawer until the desert takes the building, and that'll be that, and I'll consider my promise kept either way." He pulled his coat straight. "Fair enough?"

Nothing. Just the wrench, turning.

He went down the stairs and out into the evening, and the door boomed shut behind him, and the noise of the pumps cut off like a held breath released.

* * *

Outside, Saltmere had lit its few lamps, and the water queue had dissolved into supper-time quiet, and the magistrate's hall on its hill glowed with more light than the rest of the town combined. Astrid walked his horse along the canal path toward the town's single boarding house, and tried to feel like a man whose promise was kept, and failed.

Because it wasn't kept. Not really. Leaving a map in a drawer two hundred miles away was a lawyer's promise, a technicality, and Juniper Hale hadn't struck him as a man who dealt in technicalities. *She'll know,* the old man had said. *She'll know what it is.* Which meant the map wasn't paper. It was a message. And messages left in drawers were just cowardice with extra steps.

He was still arguing with himself about it at the boarding house counter, and still arguing over a bowl of gray stew, and still arguing when the boarding house keeper — a wide, silent woman with forearms like Wren's — set down his plate and said, without preamble:

"You found her, then. At pump three."

Astrid looked up. News traveled fast in thirsty towns; there was little enough to drink and less to talk about.

"I found her."

"She throw anything at you?"

"Only words."

"Hm." The woman collected the next table's dishes, stacking them with a clatter. "Her father used to come through here, you know. Years back. Before your time in these parts, maybe. Big talker, when he drank. Talked about rivers under the salt, gates, old water older than any well. Folks called him a liar." She paused, dishes balanced on one arm. "Called him worse than that, near the end. Then he stopped coming, and folks forgot him. Except her. She'd sit by the window on rest days, some years, facing east. Never said why. Never said anything." The woman's eyes, small and shrewd in her broad face, rested on Astrid a beat too long. "People say nothing loudest when they've got the most to say. You being a ranger, you probably know that."

Astrid said nothing, which proved her point.

He paid for his room, climbed the outside stairs to it, and sat on the narrow bed with the oilcloth bundle in his lap, unwrapped at last, the map spread across his knees in the lamplight. It was beautiful work — survey-grade linework, elevation hatching, watercourses marked in a hand that knew what it was drawing, and annotations in faded ink that made his skin prickle: depths, seal-points, a symbol repeated at intervals that looked like a gate drawn in cross-section. Whatever Juniper Hale had been running from, he had run carrying this, and had kept it hidden for twenty years, and had spent his final breath making sure it reached his daughter.

And his daughter wouldn't take it from his hand. But she might take it from a drawer, alone, unwatched, where pride couldn't see her do it.

Or she wouldn't. And the map would molder, and the promise would rot, and the last thing a dying man had trusted to him would fail.

Astrid sat with it a long time. Below, the pumps chugged and groaned through the night shift, keeping Saltmere alive one gallon at a time. Somewhere out in the dark, a dog began to bark and thought better of it.

He was rewrapping the bundle, deciding — drawer, or door — when he heard the outside stair creak.

One set of footsteps, light, deliberate, stopping outside his door. A pause long enough to contain an entire argument with oneself. Then a knock: two raps, hard, like a challenge.

Astrid opened the door.

Wren Hale stood in the lamplight, still in her work clothes, steam-scalded and salt-crusted, her scarred hands jammed in her pockets as if to keep them from doing something. Her jaw was set like a foundation stone. But her eyes — those hard, shuttered eyes — were red-rimmed, and fixed on the oilcloth bundle in his hands with an expression that grief wears when it has run out of anger and hasn't yet figured out what to wear instead.

"That offer," she said, in a voice sanded raw. "The drawer at Two Wells. Does it still stand?"

Astrid stepped aside and held up the map.

"It stands," he said. "But I'll tell you what I told myself all the way here: I don't think your father sent you a map at all. I think he sent you a question. And I don't think it's the kind that waits politely in a drawer."

Wren looked at the bundle for a long, long moment. Then she reached out and took it — not gently, not gratefully, but the way you take hold of something that has been pulling at you for twenty years — and turned it over in her scarred hands.

"What kind of question?" she asked.

Astrid glanced past her, down the dark stairwell, toward the glow of the magistrate's hall on its hill — and at the figure standing at the edge of that glow, watching the boarding house with the stillness of a man who had been watching for some time.

"The dangerous kind," he said quietly. "Come inside. I think we're already being asked to answer it."
