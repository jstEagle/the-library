## 4. The Map That Shouldn't Exist

The boarding house room was narrow as a coffin and twice as warm, the walls sweating salt through their whitewash like they'd been weeping for years. Wren had lived in worse. She'd lived in worse this month. The pump-house dormitory smelled of brine and machine oil, of forty women sleeping in shifts around the same three fans, and at least here there was a window — one window, cracked and clouded with mineral bloom, that looked out over Saltmere's crooked main street toward the magistrate's hall glowing on its hill like a lantern someone had forgotten to snuff.

She set the bundle on the bed and did not touch it.

That was the discipline of it. Twenty years of working the pumps taught you things about pressure: you didn't open a valve all at once when the line might be fouled, you didn't trust water that came too easy, you didn't put your hands on anything until you knew what it wanted from you. Everything in Saltmere wanted something. The company wanted her labor. The magistrate wanted her taxes. The desert wanted her sweat, eventually her life, and it would take both without malice or hurry, the way a well takes a dropped coin.

So she sat on the edge of the cot with her back straight and her hands flat on her knees, and she watched the bundle the way you watch a stranger who has asked to share your fire.

It was wrapped in oilcloth, tied with waxed cord, and the cord was knotted in a way she almost recognized before she made herself look away. There were habits in it. Her father's habits. The double loop he used to tie off the tent flaps when they camped out past the marker stones, back when she was small enough to ride his shoulders and the world was nothing but heat and his voice telling her the names of stars. She had spent twenty years teaching herself not to remember those knots. Now here one was, sitting on a borrowed mattress in a rented room, patient as a debt.

Astrid Oyelaran had gone downstairs. She could hear him below, talking to the landlady in that low ranger's voice, arranging something — rooms, maybe, or just the fiction of respectability. He had said he'd give her the night. He had said it like a man keeping a promise to a dead friend, which, Wren supposed, was exactly what he was doing. Juniper Hale trades a map for a promise; the promise walks two hundred miles of flats to keep its word; and the word is *no*, because some promises deserve to fail.

She would tell him no in the morning. She had already told him no tonight, standing in the street with the pump-house grit still under her nails, and he had taken it without argument, which annoyed her more than arguing would have. Men who argued you could hate cleanly. This one just stood there absorbing her refusal like sand absorbing rain, and then he'd said *the dangerous kind* and looked at the hill, and now there was a watcher at the edge of the lamplight and everything had gotten complicated, and the map was still sitting on her bed like a question she'd already answered wrong.

Outside, the watcher hadn't moved. She checked twice. A silhouette against the glow of the hall, hat brim low, weight settled the way men settle when they're being paid by the hour. Not leaving. Whatever Astrid Oyelaran had carried into Saltmere, it had been noticed, and noticed things in this town did not stay private long.

Wren unbound her hair, pulled off her boots, and lay down on top of the blanket fully dressed, because that was another habit the pumps taught you — sleep ready to move.

She didn't sleep.

* * *

At some hour past midnight the lamp-oil smell got to her, or the silence did, or maybe it was only that twenty years is a long time to hold a breath and her body finally mutinied. She sat up. Struck a match. Lit the stub of candle on the windowsill, cupping the flame from the draft, and the room leapt into being around her — the water stain on the ceiling shaped like a hand, the hairline crack in the plaster running floor to lintel like a vein, the bundle, still waiting.

"Fine," she said aloud, to no one, to the room, to him, wherever he was — alive in some claim shack or dead in a waystation bed with a ranger holding his hand. "Fine."

She cut the cord with her belt knife. The knot gave the way his knots always gave, all at once, like it had only been waiting for permission.

Inside the oilcloth: a leather case, cracked at the corners, smelling of tallow and dust and something faintly green, like a memory of plants. Inside the case, a map. Folded in eighths, the creases gone soft as cloth from handling, and when she opened it across the bed the candle flame leaned toward it as if reading over her shoulder.

It was beautiful. That was the first betrayal of the evening — that it was beautiful. Ink on treated hide, drawn with the sure, spare hand of a man who had surveyed half the territory for companies that never paid him fairly. The salt flats rendered as fine stippled white. The marker stones as small patient circles. And at the center, out where every official chart in Saltmere printed *UNPRODUCTIVE — NO WATER*, a valley drawn in careful cross-hatch, and beneath it, in her father's blocky capitals: **THE RIVERGATE**.

Beneath an empty place. Beneath the lie every map in the territory agreed on.

Wren's hands were steady. She noted that about herself, distantly, the way you note weather. Her hands had learned steadiness the hard way, holding wrenches against torque that wanted to take fingers, and they stayed steady now while the rest of her began quietly coming apart.

Because she knew this hand. Of course she knew this hand. But knowing it wasn't the thing. The thing was lower down, near the valley's southern rim, where the survey notes crowded in small and dense, elevation figures and bearing marks and little annotations in a second handwriting — a child's handwriting, round and uneven, the letters leaning like they were tired:

*the water sings here. papa says don't tell.*

Her own handwriting. Age eight, maybe nine. The summer before he left.

She sat down on the floor. Not decided to sit down — her legs simply ended their arrangement with her, and she was sitting, back against the bed frame, map spread above her like a sky, candle guttering, and she read it again. *The water sings here.* She remembered singing. She remembered the sound — a thin harmonic rising out of a crack in the rocks out past the markers, so high and pure she'd thought it was a bird until her father crouched down and put his palm flat against the stone and said, *feel that? That's water moving where it shouldn't be. That's a secret, Wrennie. Big ones sing.*

Big ones sing.

She had helped him draw this. She had held the other end of the measuring cord while he sighted bearings. She had inked the little circles for the marker stones because he said her circles were better than his, rounder, more honest. She had written that note in the margin with the stub of pencil she kept on a string around her neck, and he had laughed — she could still hear the laugh, low and surprised, like the sound itself was a discovery — and then he had folded the map away, and within the year he was gone, and the map had gone with him, and she had spent twenty years learning to be a woman whose father was a story other people told badly.

And here it all was. In her own hand. Evidence, filed in ink, that once upon a time he had not left her out. That he had brought her *in*. Past the markers, into the singing place, into the work, into the secret — trusted her, at eight years old, with the biggest secret in the territory.

The anger didn't collapse. Anger like hers doesn't collapse; it's load-bearing. But something shifted behind it, some internal wall she'd built so long ago she'd mistaken it for terrain, and through the shift came a cold draft of older grief, the original grief, the one underneath: not *he abandoned me* but *he took me with him and then he left anyway*, which was worse, which was so much worse, because abandonment you can survive by deciding you were never loved. But this — this proved she had been loved. Proved it in her own childish letters, in the care of the drafting, in the fact that he had kept the map for twenty years through every hard mile of a hard life instead of selling it, because it had her handwriting in it, because it was theirs.

He had run. Everyone agreed he had run. But a man who runs doesn't carry a map with his daughter's heart drawn in the margins. A man who runs lightens himself. He had carried it the way you carry a wound — because it was yours, because letting go of it meant letting go of something true.

Unless he'd come back for it someday. Unless he'd always meant to come back, and dying in a waystation two hundred miles from home was just the desert collecting its usual fee, and the last thing he'd done with his last strength was hire a stranger to finish the trip he couldn't.

Wren pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes until the candle became a smear of orange. She thought about the pump-house. About the foreman's ledger where her name was entered like a quantity. About the way the town dried a little more each year while the magistrate's gardens on the hill stayed green, fed by pipes nobody would explain, and how she had learned to stop asking about those pipes because the last girl who asked had lost her shift, then her lodging, then her place in the record books entirely, as if thirst could be administered like punishment.

If there was water under the flats — real water, singing water, enough to make a whole valley lie about itself — then everything she had ever endured had been unnecessary. Every scar on her hands. Every shift. Every person she'd watched fade in the drought years, politely, officially, of *natural causes*. It hadn't been the desert's cruelty. It had been arithmetic. Someone had done sums and decided the water belonged elsewhere, and the empty places on the maps weren't empty, they were *edited*.

Her father had known. Her father had known and left her inside the lie.

Or — the draft again, colder — her father had known and left her *outside* what came next. Because if the Rivergate was hidden, it was hidden by people who drowned a valley to do it, and people who drown valleys do not tolerate witnesses, and Juniper Hale had been a witness, and he had run, and everything he'd done since — the drifting, the silence, the twenty years of not writing — might not have been cowardice at all. It might have been a wall between her and a bullet, built out of her hatred, mortared with her grief. Let her believe the worst. Believing the worst kept her small, and small things don't get visited in the night.

She didn't know. That was the truth of where she stood at three in the morning: she didn't know, and the not-knowing had teeth.

What she knew was the map. She bent over it again, tracing the route with one finger — the markers, the dry washes, the claim shack marked with a tiny square and labeled in his hand: *home, such as it was*. Days out, past the deep flats, into country she'd only seen from the tops of derricks. And along the margins, everywhere, the annotations. Some in his hand. One in hers. And one more, tucked into the corner by the valley's northern approach, in a third hand entirely — smaller, fussy, precise, the handwriting of a clerk:

*Survey ref. 44-C. Sealed by order. Do not copy. Do not discuss.*

Someone else had handled this map. Someone in the government's employ had seen it, known what it showed, and let Juniper Hale walk away with it anyway — or failed to stop him, or marked it and moved on, assuming a lone prospector with a dead man's name for a daughter would never dare the flats.

But someone was watching the boarding house right now. Someone had watched Astrid arrive. The seal on the survey reference wasn't old ink. It wasn't faded. It was fresh enough to shine slightly when the candle caught it, the way new wax shines.

They know it's here. Or they will by morning.

Wren folded the map along its soft old creases, and her own handwriting disappeared inside the fold, and she found she couldn't quite breathe until it was hidden, as if the note — *papa says don't tell* — were a living thing that could be arrested.

She sat a long time with the case in her lap. Then she did the thing she'd sworn she wouldn't do, the thing she'd rehearsed all evening on the walk from the pump-house: she made a decision. Not about her father — that verdict was still locked away with the evidence still arriving. About the map.

In the morning she would tell the ranger yes. Not because she forgave anyone. Not because the map was beautiful or the desert was calling or any of the songs said it should be. But because her own handwriting was in the margins of a map to stolen water, and Wren Hale had spent twenty years learning that in Saltmere, whatever was written about you became the truth, and she would be damned — she would be *drowned* — before she let the last thing her father ever wrote about her be a secret somebody else got to keep sealed.

She pinched out the candle. In the dark, the watcher on the street was a darker shape against the hall's glow, and it had drifted closer to the boarding house steps.

Below, faintly, she heard Astrid's boots on the stairs, unhurried, stopping outside her door. A soft knock. Two beats.

"Wren." Low, even. "You need to see this. There's a rider coming up the hill road — and he's carrying magistrate's colors."

She was already reaching for her boots.
