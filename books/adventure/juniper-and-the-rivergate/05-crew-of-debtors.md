## 5. Crew of Debtors

The charter office in Saltmere occupied the ground floor of a building that had once been a granary and now smelled of dust and old ink, which was to say it smelled like every government office in the territory. Astrid stood at the counter while a clerk with ink-stained cuffs explained, for the third time, why he could not cross the flats alone.

"Expedition charter," the clerk said, tapping a ledger with one finger as though the gesture lent the words weight. "Minimum crew of four. Certified water plan. Bond posted against rescue costs. That's the law since the Merrow disaster."

"The Merrow disaster was eleven years ago."

"And we learned from it." The clerk smiled the way men smile when they enjoy their small authority. "You want to walk out onto the salt, Ranger, you do it legal or you don't come back to file a report about it."

Astrid looked at the map folded inside his coat — Juniper Hale's map, drawn in a dead man's careful hand, its margins crowded with notes in two different scripts, one of them a child's. He had promised to deliver it to Wren Hale, and Wren Hale had refused it, and then she had spent three days refusing it again in increasingly creative ways before finally unrolling it on her kitchen table at midnight and saying, *If I'm going to be a fool, I'll be a fool who knows where she's walking.* Which was, Astrid supposed, how partnerships began.

They needed a crew of four. They had two names, no bond money worth mentioning, and a destination that appeared on no survey ever filed.

"We'll need people," he'd told Wren that first night, watching her trace the route with a cracked fingernail. "People who can work the flats."

"People who won't ask questions," she'd corrected him. "Which means people who've already asked all the wrong ones and paid for it."

She was right, of course. She usually was, in the sharp-edged way of someone who had learned early that being wrong cost more than being hard. And so Astrid Oyelaran, who had spent nine years as a ranger enforcing the territory's rules, set about assembling a crew made entirely of people the rules had chewed up and spat back out.

* * *

He found Odile Vasseur in the records annex behind the magistrate's court, reshelving documents by candlelight because the territory couldn't afford lamps and wouldn't pay for candles after sundown unless someone's neck was on the line.

She was a narrow woman, forty or fifty, impossible to pin down, with gray threading through dark hair she kept pinned so tightly it looked painful. When Astrid came through the door she didn't look up from her armload of folios.

"Annex is closed to public inquiry after four," she said. "Come back tomorrow and fill out form eleven-C."

"I'm not here for records. I'm here for you."

That got her attention. She turned, and Astrid saw the particular stillness of a woman deciding whether the man in front of her was a threat or merely an inconvenience. "I don't know you."

"Astrid Oyelaran. Territory ranger, currently between contracts." He set his badge on the shelf beside her — not presenting it, just letting it sit there where she could ignore it if she chose. "I'm told you were Magistrate Corvane's senior clerk for six years. Filed everything. Read everything."

Her face did something complicated and quick, like water finding cracks in stone. "I'm told a lot of things. Most of them are wrong, and the rest are none of your business."

"You were dismissed eight months ago. No cause given. No reference permitted. You've been shelving paper in a room without windows ever since, on half wages, waiting for something."

"Waiting for my shift to end," she said flatly. But her eyes went to the map case under his arm, and Astrid watched her catalog it — the size, the oilcloth wrapping, the way he held it slightly away from his body like something alive — and he knew he had her, or nearly.

"Odile Vasseur." He said her name slowly, letting it land. "I need someone who can read a survey mark and tell me whether it's real. Someone who knows what the government seals look like, what the filing conventions are, what a forged document smells like next to an honest one. I'm told there isn't anyone better in Saltmere."

"Who told you that?"

"Does it matter?"

"It matters enormously." She set down her folios, one by one, precise as a ritual. "Because whoever told you that either wanted me hired or wanted me watched, and I've had enough of both to last two lifetimes."

"Nobody sent me. A dying man gave me a map, and the map has marks on it I can't read, and every instinct I have says those marks aren't supposed to exist." He let that sit. "I want to know what the territory buried, Ms. Vasseur. I'm told you're the person who'd know where to start digging."

For a long moment the only sound was the candle guttering and, somewhere above them, the creak of the courthouse settling into the cold. Then Odile laughed — a short, dry sound with no humor in it, only surprise.

"A dying man's map." She pulled the pin from her hair, shook it loose, repinned it tighter, all in the space of a breath — a nervous habit she clearly hated having. "Do you know what I did for six years? I filed things. That's all. Documents came across my desk with classifications I wasn't cleared to understand, and I filed them, and I forgot them, because forgetting was the job. Forgetting was *the whole job*, Ranger. And then one day I remembered something I shouldn't have, and eight months later I was shelving paper in the dark." Her eyes came up to his, and they were steady now, and very tired. "What happens to me if I help you?"

"Honestly? Probably nothing good."

"Good." Something almost like relief crossed her face. "I've had enough of nothing bad happening to careful people. When do we leave?"

* * *

Brannic was easier and harder. Easier because he said yes within ten minutes; harder because he said yes while drunk, and Astrid had to come back the next morning and get the yes again from the sober man underneath.

He found him at the Drowned Rat — the only tavern in Saltmere that hadn't been repossessed by the water company — a broad, slow-moving man with forearms like dock rope and hands so calloused they left faint prints on the glass he held. He'd been a well-diver for thirty years, which meant he'd gone down into the earth's throat after water the way pearl divers go down after light, feeling along stone in the dark for the seep and whisper of an aquifer. It also meant his hearing was shot in one ear and his balance was off in a way he compensated for with a walk like a ship in calm weather.

"Rivergate." Brannic said the word the way other men said a woman's name they weren't over yet. "My grandfather dove for it. Swore there was a river under the salt big enough to drown the whole territory twice. Everyone called him a liar. Called my father one too. Called me one." He drank. "Maybe we're a family of liars. Or maybe we're a family of men who could feel water through forty feet of rock and nobody wanted to hear it."

"Can you find it?"

Brannic set down the glass and pressed his palm flat against the tavern table, and for a moment he closed his eyes, and Astrid had the strange sense of a man listening to something very far away and very deep. "There's water under this town right now," Brannic said quietly. "Under the pumps. Under the salt crust. I can feel it like a pulse in my teeth. Whether it's *the* water—" He opened his eyes. "Take me out there and I'll tell you. That's the only honest answer anybody's going to give you about the Rivergate, Ranger. Everything else is stories."

"That's all I'm asking for. Honest answers."

"Then you're asking the wrong territory." Brannic grinned, and it transformed his face into something younger and rougher. "But I'm in. Fair warning: I drink, I argue, and if we find it, I'm going down first, and you can't stop me."

"Noted," Astrid said. "And Brannic — tomorrow, when you're sober, I'm coming back, and you're going to say yes again."

Brannic considered this. "Fair," he admitted. "The drunk me agrees to everything. The sober me needs convincing." He leaned in, breath like raw spirit. "Tell the sober me one thing: does the daughter know?"

Astrid went still. "Know what?"

"Juniper Hale's girl. Whole town knows the old man had a daughter he never wrote to. If we're chasing his ghost, does she know we're doing it?"

"She's leading us," Astrid said, and Brannic's grin faded into something more solemn, and he raised his glass an inch off the table — a toast, or an apology, or both.

* * *

Tam Voss was the hardest, because Tam Voss was the reason Astrid had come to Saltmere in the first place, and because finding him required no effort at all.

Astrid spotted him on the fourth evening, sitting on the pump-house steps eating bread and onions like a man with nowhere better to be, and the absurdity of it hit Astrid like a fist: nine days of pursuit across open flats, tracking a thief by spoor and campfire ash and the particular way he broke trail around ridge lines, and the man had simply walked into the same town Astrid was headed for and sat down to wait.

"You knew I'd follow you here," Astrid said, stopping three steps below him.

"I hoped." Tam looked up. He was younger than Astrid had expected — late twenties, maybe, with a lean face and eyes the color of weak tea, and the unhurried calm of someone who had been chased before and survived it. "You strike me as a man who finishes what he starts. Rangers usually are. It's the ones who give up halfway you have to worry about."

"You stole a name out of the registry at Candler's Post. A dead prospector's name. Juniper Hale's name." Astrid watched his face carefully. "Nine days I tracked you for a piece of paper. You want to tell me why?"

Tam tore his bread in half and offered the larger piece upward, an offering, or a joke, or both. "Sit down, Ranger. It's a long story and you look like a man who hasn't eaten."

Astrid didn't sit. Tam ate his half anyway, unhurried, and talked with his mouth half full like a man who'd grown up hungry and never unlearned it.

"There was a boy at Candler's Post," he said. "Sixteen, maybe. Came in on a freight wagon with no papers, no name anyone trusted. His own name got burned with the rest of his family's records in the Kessel fire — you remember the Kessel fire?" Astrid did; everyone did; three years ago, a company warehouse, forty families' documents gone in an afternoon, and the magistrate's office had ruled reissuing them *an administrative burden disproportionate to need*. "Without papers he couldn't draw rations, couldn't take work contracts, couldn't exist. The company police picked up paperless boys every week and shipped them to the drainage camps. Nobody asks questions about drainage camps."

"So you stole him an identity."

"I stole him a *good* identity. That's the trick of it. Not a fresh name — fresh names get audited. A dead man's name, one with history, with a claim record, with a pension file nobody's touched in years. Juniper Hale had been missing presumed dead for a decade. Perfect. My friend signs a work contract as Juniper Hale, draws against Hale's old claim, becomes a boring middle-aged prospector on paper, and lives." Tam shrugged. "It's not even really stealing. It's more like — inheritance."

Astrid stood there a long moment, working through the shape of it, and found that the shape was worse than the crime. Nine days. Nine days of water rationing and wind-scoured nights, chasing a man across the emptiest country in the world for an act of mercy committed with a fountain pen.

"You could have told the registry clerk that," he said finally. "Explained the circumstances."

"To a territory clerk?" Tam smiled, and there was no warmth in it at all. "Ranger, the clerk would have filed a report, the report would have gone to the magistrate, and my friend would have been in a drainage camp inside a month. Some truths are only safe in one pair of hands at a time."

"And now you're telling me."

"Now I'm telling a ranger who's standing on the steps of a pump-house in a town that's dying of thirst, holding a map to a river that officially doesn't exist." Tam brushed crumbs from his knees and stood, unhurried, until they were eye to eye. "Wren Hale told me. Three days ago. She cornered me outside the assay office and asked if I was the thief who took her father's name, and then she asked if I wanted to make it right." His tea-colored eyes glinted. "She's terrifying, by the way. You should keep her."

"I intend to hire her."

"Too late. She already hired me. Said a man who could steal a name could probably steal other useful things, and that she'd rather have me inside the expedition than following it." He spread his hands. "So. Arrest me, or shake my hand. Either way, I'm coming."

Astrid looked at him for a long time. The sun was going down behind the pump-house, and the shadows of the pipes lay across the yard like bars, and somewhere in the town a child was crying and a dog was answering and the desalination pumps thudded on and on, dragging brackish water up from the earth drop by drop, a town drinking itself toward the end of its patience.

"You understand," Astrid said, "that if you run out there, I will find you. I've proven that."

"You really have," Tam agreed cheerfully. "It was honestly impressive. I lost you twice and you just — kept coming. Like weather." He extended his hand. "Partners, then. Until the flats decide otherwise."

Astrid took it. The hand was thin and strong and calloused in ways that didn't match any honest trade, and it gripped like it meant it.

* * *

They met that night in Wren's rooms above the pump-house, five people and a lamp, and Astrid looked around the table and understood exactly what kind of expedition this was.

Wren Hale, who hadn't spoken to her father in twenty years and was carrying his map like a splinter working toward her heart. Odile Vasseur, who had chosen the windowless room over the comfortable lie and was now choosing the dangerous truth instead, her hands quiet on the table but her eyes moving constantly, cataloging exits. Brannic, sober today, smelling faintly of last night anyway, turning his palm up and down as though he could already feel the water through the floorboards. Tam Voss, who had stolen a name to save a life and was grinning like a man who'd gotten away with something even as he signed on to the most dangerous thing any of them had ever done.

Debtors and drifters. People with nothing to lose, which was another way of saying people who had already lost it, and kept going anyway.

"Charter papers are filed," Wren said, tapping a document in front of her. "Four-person minimum, we have five. Water plan says we're prospecting the eastern salt margin — true enough to pass inspection, vague enough to buy us time. Bond's covered by Brannic's claim stake and whatever's left of my father's pension account, which, it turns out, he never drew a coin from. Twenty years of accrual." Her voice caught, just barely, and she pushed past it. "We leave in three days."

"Corvane's office countersigned the charter this afternoon," Odile said quietly. Every head turned. She kept her gaze on the table. "Standard procedure — expeditions over four persons require magistrate review. I saw the routing stamp when I filed. His office will have a copy of our manifest. Our stated route. Our names."

Silence. The lamp hissed. Outside, the pumps thudded their patient, endless count.

"Then we assume he's reading it," Astrid said at last. "And we assume a man like Corvane doesn't countersign anything without a reason."

"He'll send riders," Odile said. "Not to stop us — to follow us. Let us do the dangerous work, find whatever's out there, and then step in and take it, legal and clean, with warrants." She looked up, and her face in the lamplight was pale and absolutely resolved. "I've seen him do it before. Twice. I filed the paperwork both times."

Wren's hand flattened on the map case. Brannic's jaw tightened. Tam, of all of them, smiled — a small, sharp smile, the smile of a man who had spent his life one step ahead of exactly this kind of trouble.

"Well," Tam said, leaning back, "it's a good thing we're used to being followed."

Astrid met Wren's eyes across the lamp, and in them he saw the same thing he felt in his own chest: not fear, exactly. Something closer to certainty. Whatever waited out on the salt, they would not be walking toward it alone — and they would not be walking toward it unwatched.

Three days, he thought. Then the flats.

And somewhere behind them, already, the sound of riders gathering.
