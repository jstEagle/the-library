## 6. The Magistrate's Interest

Odile Vasse had filed away eleven thousand documents in her nine years as the Magistrate's clerk, and she had been ordered to forget four of them.

She remembered every one.

That was the trouble with a mind like hers — the kind that could recite tariff schedules in its sleep, that kept ledgers of other people's debts the way other women kept pressed flowers. Forgetting was a skill. She had never acquired it. So when Wren Hale unrolled her father's map across the scarred table of the Saltmere boarding house and Odile leaned over it with her tea going cold in her hand, she did not see a prospector's scrawl. She saw a survey mark — a small, precise glyph like a closed eye with a vertical lash through it — stamped twice along the western edge of the flats.

She had inked that glyph herself. Once. In a basement office that smelled of lamp oil and mildew, on a document whose cover bore a wax seal the color of dried blood, and whose final instruction, in Magistrate Corvane's own hand, read: *File under sealed water rights. No copies. No index entry. Speak of this to no one, including yourself.*

She had spoken of it to no one for six years. Including, mostly, herself.

"Odile?" Wren's voice came from far away. "You've gone white as the flats."

"It's nothing." Odile set down her cup. Her hand was steady — she made it steady, the way she made all things steady, by force of will and long practice. "A cramp. From the tea."

But her eyes went back to the map, and the map looked back at her, and between them passed the whole silent history of a thing she had been paid handsomely to pretend did not exist.

* * *

The Rivergate appeared in exactly one document Odile had ever handled, though the document had never named it so plainly. It had been called *the diversion project*, *the lowland reclamation*, and once, memorably, *the correction*. It was a ledger of water rights purchased from a town that no longer appeared on any map — a town whose name had been struck from the registry with the same black bar Corvane used to redact inconvenient line items. The purchase price had been listed as *settled in full*, which was a strange way to describe a transaction when you knew, as Odile knew, that the sellers were dead.

She had asked about it once. Only once, in her first year, when she still believed the government was an institution rather than a man.

Corvane had looked at her over his desk with those pale, patient eyes — eyes like winter light on ice, beautiful and utterly without warmth — and said, "Clerk Vasse, what is the first duty of records?"

"To preserve them, Magistrate."

"The first duty of records," he had corrected gently, "is to serve order. A record that destabilizes order is not a record. It is a weapon in the wrong hands. You would not leave a loaded rifle in a schoolyard, would you?"

"No, Magistrate."

"And yet you would leave a dangerous truth lying about where any fool might pick it up." He had smiled then, and the smile had been worse than the eyes. "Forget the file, Clerk. That is also a form of preservation."

She had forgotten it. Outwardly. For six years she had stamped his warrants, transcribed his tax levies, watched Saltmere's pumps run drier and drier while the lowland farms below the escarpment grew green as emeralds, and told herself the same story every clerk tells: *I am small. The machine is large. My hands are clean because they are only hands.*

Then Juniper Hale's map lay on a table in front of her, and the closed-eye glyph stared up out of the salt-stained paper, and Odile understood with terrible clarity that her hands had never been clean. They had merely been gloved.

* * *

She walked back to the records office through the evening streets of Saltmere, and the town looked different to her now, the way a room looks different after you find the body in it.

Saltmere at dusk was a study in thirst. The desalination towers stood against the reddening sky like the ribs of some enormous beached creature, and beneath them the pump lines ran into the town in iron veins, sweating condensation, each joint wrapped in rag to catch the precious drips. Children queued at the public tap with clay jars, and the queue was orderly because disorder cost water, and everyone knew the arithmetic of thirst the way coastal towns know tides. On the magistrate's wall — his wall, the one he'd built with the same taxes that had priced private wells out of reach — the proclamations fluttered:

*CONSERVATION IS PATRIOTISM.*
*THE LOWLANDS FEED US ALL.*
*WATER FINDS ITS PROPER LEVEL.*

Odile stopped at that last one. Water finds its proper level. She had typed it herself, three years ago, from Corvane's dictation, and thought it a harmless platitude. Now she heard it for what it was: a confession wearing the clothes of a proverb. The river had found its proper level once — in a valley east of here, beneath the salt, feeding a town that had the misfortune to sit on top of the only reliable freshwater source in three hundred miles. And men like Corvane had decided the proper level was elsewhere.

She let herself into the records office. The night clerk, a boy of sixteen with chapped lips and ambitions of a posting to the capital, looked up from his novel.

"Burning the midnight oil, Clerk Vasse?"

"Just checking an index cross-reference." Her voice was smooth. Nine years of practice. "Go home, Tamwell. I'll lock up."

He left gratefully. She waited until his footsteps faded, until the street noise settled into its evening hush, and then she stood in the middle of the archive she knew better than her own heartbeat and felt the full weight of what she was about to do.

There was no copy of the sealed file. Of course there wasn't; she had made sure of that herself, following orders. But clerks are human, and humans keep talismans. Six years ago, on the night she filed it, she had copied a single page — not the whole, just the survey summary, the page bearing the closed-eye glyph and the coordinates of the capped wells — and she had hidden it inside the spine of a bound volume of obsolete grazing ordinances, because no one in the history of the territory had ever voluntarily opened a volume of obsolete grazing ordinances.

Her fingers shook as she cut the stitching. Not from fear, she told herself. From the cold.

The page came free. The glyph looked up at her, identical to the one on Juniper Hale's map, and beside it, in the neat hand of a surveyor twenty years dead, the notation she had never been able to forget: *Flow redirected per contract. Original channel sealed. Town site inundated. Recommend permanent closure of record.*

*Inundated.* As if the town had flooded itself. As if the water had wandered off its path on a whim and drowned a community by accident, and the surveyor had merely tidied up the paperwork afterward.

Odile sat down on the floor between the shelves, in the dust and the lamplight, and held the page, and wept — briefly, furiously, without sound, the way she did everything forbidden — not for the drowned town, though God knew they deserved tears, but for herself. For nine years of clean gloves. For the eleven thousand documents she had served faithfully into a machine that ate towns and printed proclamations.

When the tears were done she folded the page into her bodice, restitched the volume's spine with thread from her sewing kit, trimmed the loose ends even, and locked the office behind her.

It was the first dishonest act of her career. It felt like breathing after a long dive.

* * *

The summons came the next morning, before the sun had cleared the towers.

Not a summons, precisely. Summons were for debtors and smugglers. What came was a note on cream paper, delivered by a rider in magistrate's gray, written in a secretary's hand but signed — unusually, pointedly — by Corvane himself:

*Clerk Vasse — Attend me at your earliest convenience regarding matters of archival integrity. — M.C.*

*Archival integrity.* Odile read the phrase four times. It could mean nothing. It could mean everything. Corvane did not summon clerks casually; in nine years she had been called to his office perhaps five times, always for matters of protocol, always leaving with her position strengthened and her soul slightly smaller.

She dressed with care. Gray dress, high collar, hair pinned without ornament. Armor, of the only kind available to a woman whose weapons were penmanship and memory. In the bodice, over her heart, the stolen page lay flat and warm against her skin, and she was aware of it the way one is aware of a coal.

The magistrate's residence occupied the highest ground in Saltmere, naturally. Everything Corvane owned occupied the highest ground. His office windows faced south toward the lowlands, and Odile had long suspected this was deliberate: he liked to look at what he owned.

He was standing at those windows when she was shown in, hands clasped behind his back, watching the green country below the escarpment shimmer in the morning heat. He did not turn immediately. He never did. Making people wait was the first lesson of power, and Corvane had learned all the lessons.

"Clerk Vasse." He turned at last. He was perhaps sixty, silver at the temples, with the lean, well-kept look of a man who had never carried anything heavier than a pen. "Thank you for coming so promptly."

"You wrote *earliest convenience*, Magistrate. This was mine."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth — appreciation, or its counterfeit. "Sit, please."

She sat. The chair across from his desk was positioned lower than his, a fact she had noticed years ago and resented quietly ever since. Today she noticed it and felt nothing but contempt, and the contempt was so clean and so new that it frightened her more than the summons had.

"I'll come directly to the matter," Corvane said, settling behind his desk. "Yesterday evening, a woman named Hale — Wren Hale, daughter of the prospector Juniper Hale — was seen in company with certain persons reviewing certain documents. Among the persons observed was a former ranger, one Oyelaran, currently off-contract and, I'm told, recently involved in a curious matter of theft from the registry office." He paused. "And among the documents reviewed, according to my observer, was a map. A survey map of the eastern flats, marked with official glyphs."

The room was very quiet. Through the window, heat shimmered off the lowlands like a mirage of everything the world was supposed to be.

"A map," Odile said.

"Marked with official glyphs, Clerk. Glyphs that appear in sealed files. Files which, I remind you, have no copies, no index entries, and no witnesses beyond the clerk who filed them." His pale eyes rested on her with the gentleness of a trap closing. "You filed such a file once. Do you recall it?"

Here it was, then. The moment her whole life had been bending toward without her knowledge, the way a river bends toward the sea.

Every instinct of nine years said: *deny, deflect, survive.* Tell him you don't recall. Tell him the observer was mistaken. Tell him maps circulate, prospectors copy surveys, glyphs degrade into decoration. Give him nothing, give him less than nothing, give him the bland competent face of Clerk Vasse, who has never given trouble to anyone.

And underneath that instinct, new and singing, another voice: *he killed a town. He is hunting a dying man's daughter over a dying man's map. And you are the only person in this room who knows what the glyph means, and if you lie badly you will die uselessly, and if you lie well you might live to do something.*

Terror and exhilaration arrived together, twins, indistinguishable. Her heart hammered so hard she was certain he could hear it. Her hands, folded in her lap, were perfectly still.

"I recall the file," she said.

His eyebrows rose a fraction. Honesty was the last thing he'd expected, which meant it was the first thing he hadn't planned for. Good. Let him improvise. Improvisation was the one art he'd never mastered, because he'd never needed to.

"Do you," he said softly.

"I file eleven hundred documents a year, Magistrate. I recall most of them. It's why you keep me." She let a small, dry pause develop. "I recall that it concerned water rights in the eastern district. I recall that it was sealed on your authority. I do not recall — because I was ordered not to retain, and I am an obedient servant of this office — any details of its contents."

"An obedient servant." He smiled. "And yet my observer reports that yesterday, in the company of the Hale woman, you reacted to a map with visible distress. Pale as salt, he said. He has an eye for these things."

"Your observer reports that I was pale." Odile raised her chin. "Magistrate, I am fifty-one years old. I have worked in a lamp-lit basement for nine years. I am pale at noon, pale at midnight, and pale at festivals. If pallor is evidence, arrest half the town."

The silence stretched. Somewhere below, a pump coughed and caught, coughed again — the old north tower, failing by inches, another item on the maintenance ledger she maintained and Corvane ignored. She held his gaze and thought, absurdly, of her mother, who had once told her: *the best lie is a true thing said slantwise.* Everything she had just said was true. That was the exhilaration of it — she was lying with nothing but truth, building a fortress out of accurate statements arranged to conceal their meaning, and it was the finest craftsmanship of her life.

"The map," Corvane said at last. "If such a map exists — and I make no accusation, Clerk, I am merely a man who worries about archival integrity — it would constitute a breach of seal. A serious one. The original survey materials were destroyed, you may recall."

*They were not,* Odile thought. *I filed the destruction certificate myself, and I remember noticing that the certificate was dated three days before the date of destruction listed within it. Someone kept copies. Perhaps you. Perhaps the surveyor. Perhaps a dying prospector who saw too much.*

"I may recall that as well," she said aloud. "Or I may not. Sealed files blur, Magistrate. That's rather the point of sealing them."

Another silence. Outside, a wind had come up, worrying the proclamation on the wall — *WATER FINDS ITS PROPER LEVEL* — into a soft dry rattle, like something trying to get out of a cage.

Corvane studied her for a long moment, and Odile had the sense of being weighed on scales she couldn't see, of numbers being tallied behind the winter-light eyes. Then he inclined his head, a small precise motion, and the audience was over.

"Thank you, Clerk Vasse. Your discretion, as ever, is noted." He returned to the window. "One more thing. Should the Hale woman or her associates approach the archives — should anyone inquire after eastern district records, however obliquely — I would want to know. Immediately. Consider it a routine reporting duty."

"Of course, Magistrate."

"You're dismissed."

She rose, bowed the exact depth required by protocol, and walked to the door with her spine straight and her lungs burning. Her hand was on the latch when his voice came again, mild as milk:

"Oh — Clerk. One small correction to your earlier statement. You said you recall no details of the file's contents." A pause, delicate as a knife laid on silk. "I never told you what the file contained. Only that it was sealed."

The room tilted. Odile stood with her back to him and felt the trap she had walked into so confidently close around her ankle — not sprung, not yet, but set, patient, waiting. He had fed her the word *water rights* himself, minutes ago. Hadn't he? She searched her memory, that flawless, cursed memory, and found the moment: *it concerned water rights in the eastern district* — his words first, or hers? The sequence blurred. He was good at this. He had decades of practice at making honest people doubt their own minds.

"Water rights in the eastern district," she said, turning her head just enough to show profile, voice bored to the point of insult. "Magistrate, this is a desert. Every file in this building concerns water rights in some district. If that constitutes a leak, the whole archive is a sieve, and you should dismiss us all."

Another pause. Then, softly, almost warmly: "Quite so. Good day, Clerk."

She walked out. She descended the stairs. She crossed the courtyard under the eyes of two riders in gray who pretended not to watch her, and she passed through the gate into the ordinary dusty morning of Saltmere, and only when she reached the first corner, out of every sightline, did she stop and press her hand flat against her bodice, over the stolen page, over her hammering heart.

She had lied to Corvane. To his face, in his own house, and walked out whole.

Her knees nearly buckled — not from fear anymore, or not only. There was a feeling rising in her chest she hadn't experienced since girlhood, since the day she'd first understood that ink and paper were levers long enough to move the world: terror and joy in equal measure, braided tight, unmistakable.

She had chosen a side. There was no undoing it. The observer would report her pallor, her pauses, her insolence; Corvane would weigh them; sooner or later the scales would tip. Every day she remained in the archives was a day spent inside a closing fist.

Which meant the time for filing was over. The time for acting had begun.

She straightened her collar, composed her face into the bland competence of Clerk Vasse, and set off through the thirsty streets toward the boarding house where a prospector's daughter sat on a map that could drown a magistrate — moving quickly, upright, pale as salt, and for the first time in nine years, entirely unafraid of what she was about to write next.
