## 7. Out Past the Markers

They left Saltmere an hour before dawn, when the magistrates' lamps still burned low in their windows and the only witnesses were the pump-hands changing shift, who asked no questions because questions cost more than silence.

Astrid rode point with the map folded against his chest, inside his coat, where the wind couldn't take it and neither could a casual glance. Behind him came Wren on a dun mule she'd named nothing at all — "Names are for things you expect to keep," she'd said, and he hadn't argued. Then Odile on a borrowed horse that was too fine for her threadbare clerk's clothes, sitting it like a woman who had spent twenty years being carried by other people's decisions and was only now learning to hold reins of her own. Brannic drove the water cart, a creaking, sun-bleached thing pulled by two oxen with the patience of stones. And Tam Voss rode last, always last, the way a man rides when he wants to see everything coming.

The flats began three miles out, past the last survey marker — a leaning post of salt-crusted iron stamped with the magistrate's seal, the official edge of the world. Beyond it, the charter said, they traveled at their own risk.

Astrid looked back once from the marker. Saltmere sat gray and small against the darkening sky, its desalination stacks bleeding thin smoke into a wind that smelled of brine and dust. He thought of Juniper Hale dying alone at the waystation, trading the last thing he owned for a promise to a daughter who didn't want it delivered. He thought of Corvane's riders, whom Odile swore would come within two days if the magistrate's clerks were half as thorough as she remembered being.

"Keep up," he said, mostly to himself, and turned east into the empty.

* * *

The first day taught them the flats' first lesson: distance lies.

What looked like an hour's ride took four. The air itself conspired — dry as kiln-dust, so clear that the horizon seemed close enough to touch, and yet every landmark receded as they approached it like a rumor. By noon the salt stretched white in every direction, cracked into hexagonal plates the size of dinner tables, and the heat came up off it in visible waves, bending the light until the oxen seemed to walk through water.

Brannic called a halt at what he swore was a buried seep. He was a broad, slow-moving man with forearms like mooring rope and a face weathered down to essentials — eyes, nose, mouth, all of them built for squinting. Well-divers were a dying trade in the lowlands; the aquifers had dropped beyond the reach of hand-dug wells, and men like Brannic had gone from indispensable to ornamental in a single generation. He claimed he could feel water through forty feet of stone.

He knelt, pressed both palms flat to the salt crust, and closed his eyes. The crew waited. The sun climbed. A fly walked across Odile's boot and she let it.

"There's nothing here," Brannic said finally, and there was something wrong in his voice — not doubt but bewilderment, the tone of a man who has reached for a stair that isn't there. "There should be something here. There's always something under the flats. Even dead water leaves a taste."

"You're sure?" Astrid asked.

"I've been sure my whole life." Brannic stood, wiped his hands on his thighs, and looked out at the white expanse with an expression Astrid had never seen on a well-diver's face before. It looked like grief. "This whole place is quiet. Not dry — quiet. Like something's holding its breath."

Wren said nothing, but Astrid saw her hand drift toward the satchel where the map rode, and rest there, and stay.

They filled their bottles from the cart instead and moved on. That night they camped in the lee of a salt ridge, and Tam Voss — who had spoken perhaps thirty words since leaving Saltmere — surprised everyone by producing a bundle of dried fish and hardtack from his saddlebag and distributing it without comment, portion by portion, each share exactly equal.

Odile eyed hers suspiciously. "Poisoned?"

"If I wanted you dead," Tam said, "I'd have saved the food."

It wasn't quite a joke. It was close enough that Brannic laughed, one short bark, and after that the camp felt fractionally less like five strangers sharing a fire and fractionally more like a crew.

* * *

The storm came on the third day, and it came without mercy or warning worth the name.

Astrid had seen dust storms before — brown walls rolling off the high desert, ugly but honest, visible for hours. This was different. One moment the horizon was clean white; the next, a smudge appeared in the southwest, and Odile, who had grown up in the flats country before her family sold her ambitions to the magistrate's office, went pale and said a single word: *whiteout.*

"Get the tarps," she shouted, and her voice cracked into the register of someone giving orders for the first time in years and finding she remembered how. "Down! Everything down! Animals' eyes covered, mouths covered if you can!"

The smudge became a wall became the world. The wind arrived first, hot and screaming, carrying grit that found every seam in clothing and every crease in skin. Then the dust itself — not brown, not gray, but a blinding bone-white churn of powdered salt and silica that erased the sun, erased the ground, erased the difference between up and down. Astrid got his kerchief over his face and the tarp stakes driven by feel alone, and then he simply held on to the cart wheel and existed inside a roar so total it felt less like sound than like the absence of any world that had ever contained quiet.

Time stopped meaning anything. He counted his breaths because counting was the only structure left. Two hundred breaths. Four hundred. The tarp snapped above him like something alive trying to escape. Once, through a gap in the white, he glimpsed one of the oxen standing perfectly still, eyes wrapped, enduring with the patience of its kind, and he thought: *that's the whole lesson, isn't it. Stand still. Breathe shallow. Wait.*

When the storm passed — late in the day, or possibly early the next morning; none of them could say — the world had been rearranged. Salt ridges had migrated. Their tracks were gone. The cart sat half-buried to its axles. And one member of the expedition was missing.

"Wren," Astrid said, and heard the name come out of him stripped of everything but fear.

They found her within the hour, because Tam Voss found her — which was the second surprise of the day, and by far the larger.

She had been thrown from the mule when it panicked, and the storm had rolled her and buried her, and by the time the air cleared she was lying in a shallow depression half a mile from camp, dazed, her ankle twisted, unable to stand. Worse: the depression was a sink crust, a hollow where brine had eaten the salt plate thin from beneath, and her weight was already flexing it, hairline fractures spidering out around her body with soft, wet cracking sounds. Beneath the crust lay the brine sink — a slurry of supersaturated salt solution deep enough to swallow a person and dense enough, once they went under, to hold them there.

Tam reached her first. Astrid watched from thirty yards out, moving as fast as the treacherous ground allowed, and watched the thief do the one thing a ranger is trained to do and most people never learn: he did not run to her. He dropped flat, distributed his weight onto his forearms and knees, and crawled — slow, deliberate, reading the crust by sound, tapping ahead of each movement with the flat of his knife.

"Hold still," he said to Wren, calm as a man discussing the weather. "Don't struggle. The more you move, the faster it goes."

"I can't—" Her voice was thin. The cracks had reached her boots now. Brine welled up through them, dark and glistening.

"Yes you can. You're going to lie there and be bored while I bore myself getting to you. That's the whole job."

He reached her in what felt like hours and was probably six minutes. He slid a coil of rope under her arms, backed away along his own crawl path, and anchored the line to the mule, which had recovered its composure and now regarded the entire proceedings with equine disdain. Three pulls, steady, and Wren came free of the crust with a sound like a cork leaving a bottle, and the depression behind her sighed and slumped inward, swallowing the shape she'd left behind.

She sat on solid salt afterward, shaking, her boot full of brine, and Tam knelt and unlaced it for her without asking, poured it out, and bound her ankle with strips of his own shirt because he said — and this was true — that bandages were for people who planned to bill you later.

"Why," Wren said. Just that. She looked at him with an expression stripped raw, anger and gratitude fighting for the same territory. "You've lied to us since Saltmere. Why save me?"

Tam tied off the knot, tucked the end flat, and considered the question with the seriousness it deserved.

"Because I spent a year working for men who'd have watched you go under and written it down as a loss," he said. "I'm trying to be a man who doesn't." He stood, shouldered his pack, and added, almost too quietly to hear: "Also your father paid me once, years ago, to guide him to the edge of these flats and no further. He told me if I ever met his daughter, I'd know her by the way she asks *why* instead of *thank you.*"

He walked back toward the camp. Wren stared after him, and then at the collapsed sink, and then at nothing at all.

Astrid helped her up. She leaned on his shoulder the whole way back, and neither of them spoke, because some silences are fuller than speech.

* * *

That evening the desert finished what the storm had started.

They made camp in the shelter of a salt ridge taller than the rest, and the wind had died to nothing, and the cold came down off the stars with the suddenness the flats were famous for. They huddled near the fire — small, conserving, more idea than warmth — and something in the day's arithmetic changed the group's chemistry. Fear does that. Shared fear is the fastest solvent of suspicion known to humankind.

Brannic spoke first, staring into the flames. "My sense is broken," he said. "Has been since we crossed the marker. Forty years I've felt water under stone like you feel a floor under your feet. Out here it's just — nothing. Silence all the way down." He rubbed his palms together as though they'd failed him. "I keep thinking it's me. Age, maybe. But it started exactly at the marker, and I don't believe in coincidences that neat."

"The government sealed the surveys," Odile said slowly. She had her knees drawn up, her clerk's precision reasserting itself around the edges of exhaustion. "I filed the sealing orders myself, years ago. Hydrological surveys of the eastern flats, classified under water-rights statutes. I never read the contents — clerks don't — but I remember the volume. Thousands of pages. Someone mapped every drop of water under this salt and then locked the knowledge away." She paused. "I used to think it was bureaucratic hoarding. Now I think maybe they were hiding something that could be *felt.*"

"Like a river," Wren said.

Nobody answered, but everybody heard it.

Astrid took out the map then — the first time he'd shown it in open country — and weighted its corners with stones. In the firelight the old ink lines glowed amber, and Wren leaned over it, tracing routes with a finger still stiff from the cold, and Astrid watched the others watch her, and understood that somewhere between the storm and the sink crust, the question had stopped being *can we trust each other* and become *what are we walking toward.* Trust, he knew from long experience, was rarely given. It was usually just the residue left behind when everything else had blown away.

"We're two days from the coordinates," he said. "Corvane's riders will have left Saltmere by now, if Odile's right about the clerks. We can't outrun them across open salt. So we stop being chased." He tapped a wrinkle in the map's western margin, a jagged line Juniper had drawn without label. "We go through the badlands instead. Slower. Harder. But no rider follows tracks into rock."

"Badlands eat expeditions," Brannic said.

"So does the magistrate." Astrid rolled the map and looked around the fire, at each of them in turn: the diver who had lost his oldest sense, the clerk who had lied to power for the first time, the thief who had crawled across breaking ground to save a stranger, the daughter who hadn't thanked anyone yet but had stopped flinching. "We vote at first light. But I'll tell you how I'm leaning."

Before he could say, Odile raised her head sharply, listening, and put two fingers to her lips.

Out past the firelight, far across the dark salt, a sound carried on the dead-still night air: the thin, high note of a signal whistle. Three notes, repeated twice. Ranger code — but not any ranger call Astrid knew, and not the pattern of any legitimate patrol.

Then, fainter, from the opposite quarter, a second whistle answered it.

They were not behind the expedition. They were on both sides of it.

Astrid killed the fire with a sweep of sand, and the darkness took them all.
