## 9. Riders on the Horizon

Tam Voss had spent his whole life learning how to be looked at without being seen, and so he was the first to notice that they were being watched.

It was a small thing, at first. A shimmer on the eastern horizon where no water was, no heat-haze either — the flats had gone cool with evening, and heat-haze needed heat. Then it resolved, briefly, into three dark specks moving in formation before the light swallowed them again. He counted the spacing between them out of habit. Riders. Spaced like men who'd been taught to ride abreast for searching, not traveling.

He said nothing. Not yet. He let the camp go on around him — Brannic hammering a tent stake with more anger than force, Odile reading by the last grey light, Wren sitting apart with her father's map spread across her knees like something she was trying to memorize or forgive — and he did what he always did when trouble came: he measured it.

Nine days out of Saltmere. They'd crossed the marker line two days past, out past the survey stones where the charter's legal protection thinned to nothing and the desert stopped pretending to belong to anyone. The dust storm had cost them half a day and most of their tempers. Brannic's well-sense had failed him twice since, and the man walked now like someone who'd lost his hearing — flinching at silences only he could feel. And Astrid Oyelaran, ranger, oath-keeper, the one honest man Tam had met in eleven years of dishonest work, trusted him.

That was the problem. That was the whole problem, folded up small enough to fit behind his ribs.

Because Tam knew who rode on the eastern horizon. He'd known since Saltmere. He'd known since before Saltmere, if he was honest, which he was trying very hard lately not to be.

* * *

The fire that night was small — Brannic's rule, fuel being what it was out here, dung and scrub and the occasional bleached branch — and they huddled close around it because the cold came off the salt like a blade drawn slowly from its sheath. Tam sat with his hands out to the flames and felt them shake, and hid the shaking by turning his palms as though warming the backs of them.

"Astrid." He kept his voice level. It took everything. "How far back do you figure Corvane's riders are?"

The silence that followed had texture. He felt every head turn toward him the way you feel weather change.

Astrid looked up from cleaning his rifle — he cleaned it every night whether it needed it or not, a ritual Tam recognized from soldiers and other men trying to hold themselves together with repetition. "I didn't say they were riders."

"You didn't say anything. That's the point." Tam smiled, and hated himself for smiling, because smiling was the oldest trick he had and he could feel it working even now, even on people he'd started — gods help him — to respect. "Three of them, east ridge, keeping pace with us since midday. They're good. Better than good. They've got a tracker who reads sign like Odile reads ledgers."

Odile closed her book. "How would you know that?"

"Because I trained him."

The words came out before he could choose them, and once they were out there was no choosing anything else. That was the thing about truth, Tam had learned late and expensively: it wasn't a door you opened. It was a dam. You touched it and the whole wall came down at once.

"My name isn't Voss," he said. "Well. It is now. It's been mine longer than my real name ever was. Two years ago I was broke and hunted in Saltmere, and Magistrate Corvane's people found me, and they made me an offer I was in no position to refuse. Find a map. Follow a thief who stole a dead man's name out of a registry office — yes, Astrid, your absurd case, the one that gnawed at you, that was me — and recover what he carried. There was a bonus for the thief's head. There always is."

Wren had gone very still. She still held the map, but her knuckles had whitened around its edges, and Tam couldn't look at her, so he looked at the fire instead, which was easier, fire having never trusted anyone.

"I found the map," he went on. "Or rather, Juniper Hale's map found me — I tracked the registry theft to a waystation clerk who'd sold copies to three different buyers, because that's what clerks do, and I bought the best copy back. And then I read it. Survey marks I shouldn't have understood, but I'd spent two years inside Corvane's offices, filing things, carrying things, listening to things men say when they think the help is furniture. I knew what those marks meant. Sealed records. Government cisterns. A river that officially doesn't exist, under a town that officially never did." He paused. "And I knew what Corvane intended, because I'd heard him intend it. Not the map itself. The *people* who'd come looking for it. 'Let them find it,' he told his captain, 'and then let the desert find them.' He wasn't going to recover the Rivergate. He was going to use it as bait, and bury everyone who bit, the way his predecessors buried a town."

Brannic spoke first, low and gravel-rough. "So you ran."

"So I ran. With the map. Which is why Corvane hired a better tracker than me to follow me, and why that tracker is currently sitting on the east ridge counting our fires." Tam finally made himself look up. "Ask me why I stole a dead man's name. Go on. It's the question that brought you all here."

"Juniper Hale," Astrid said quietly. "You stole his name because—"

"Because names are the only inheritance some of us get. Because there was a boy in the debt-farms outside Saltmere, sixteen years old, no papers, no name the law would recognize, and the farms work children until they die unnamed and unrecorded and nobody's ever the wiser. My brother died that way. So when I saw a registered name sitting unused in a government office — a prospector marked deceased, no next of kin on file — I took it. Gave it to the boy. A name is a small thing to steal. It's also the only thing I've ever stolen that I'm proud of." He laughed, short and bitter. "And it's the crime that put me in Corvane's path, because the registry office reported the theft, and the registry office reports to the magistrate, and the magistrate saw in one stolen name exactly the kind of deniable, desperate, disposable man he likes to own."

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the dark, the flats ticked and settled, contracting against the cold, and Tam thought: *they're moving now.* Night rides. Of course. His tracker — Sella, her name was, a woman who could follow a ghost across glass — would push through the dark hours and hit their trail at dawn.

"So," he said. "There it is. All of it. I was Corvane's man. I might still be, as far as any of you can prove. I led a magistrate's agent straight to this expedition by being too slow to spot her tail, and in about eight hours she's going to bring riders down on this camp, and the only reason I'm telling you now instead of running tonight is—" He stopped. Started again, plainer. "Is that I'm tired of being the kind of man who runs. And because somewhere between Saltmere and the sink crust where I pulled Wren Hale out of the ground, this stopped being a job."

Nobody moved. The firelight made masks of their faces.

* * *

It was Odile who broke the silence, and Tam would remember that afterward — not the ranger, not the daughter with every right to hate him, but the clerk, the woman whose entire career had been built on knowing which lies were safe.

"The tracker," she said. "Sella. Describe her."

"Tall. Grey streak in her hair, left side. Carries a crossbow she restrings every night, same as your ranger cleans his rifle. Drinks her coffee bitter enough to strip rust. And she's loyal to Corvane the way I used to be — which is to say completely, right up until the moment she learns what he actually pays in." Tam shrugged. "I don't know if she's learned yet. I hope not. I liked her."

"That's not reassuring," Brannic muttered.

"It's not meant to be. Nothing about me is reassuring. I'm a liar with a confession habit; ask anyone."

"Enough." Wren's voice cut across the fire, flat and hard as the salt under them. She stood, the map still in her hands, and looked down at Tam with eyes that had twenty years of abandonment sharpening them. "You sat across from me at that waystation table. You ate our food. You watched Astrid hand me my father's map and you watched me take it, and you knew — you *knew* — that Corvane wanted that map badly enough to kill for it, and you said nothing."

"Yes," Tam said.

"Why?"

"Because saying it out loud would have made it true in front of witnesses. Because I thought I could lose her — Sella — in the flats. Because I'm a coward with excellent instincts." He held her stare, because looking away would have been worse than anything he could say. "And because you'd just lost your father twice over, once to the desert and once to the truth of him, and I didn't want to be the third loss that week. That's not an excuse. It's just the order things happened in."

Wren's jaw worked. For a moment Tam thought she'd strike him, or walk into the dark, or both. Instead she said, "My father traded a map for a promise. One promise, to a stranger. And you—" her voice cracked, and she hated it, he could hear her hating it, "you traded *us* for a head start you weren't even sure you could use."

"No," Tam said softly. "I traded myself. That was always the plan, Wren. If Sella caught me, she'd catch a rogue courier with a copy of the map and a story about selling it in Lowmark. She wouldn't follow the expedition, because the expedition would already be a rumor behind her. I was the decoy. I just—" he exhaled. "I just forgot to tell anyone I was volunteering."

The wind came off the flats then, long and low, carrying salt grit that hissed against the canvas. Above them the stars were merciless, the desert sky's old cruelty — beautiful and utterly indifferent, showing every soul below exactly how small they were.

Astrid set down his rifle.

He did it deliberately, Tam noticed. Set it down, within reach but set down, and stood, and came around the fire, and stood over Tam with the firelight behind him so his face was unreadable shadow.

"When I arrested men," Astrid said, "back when I still believed arrest meant something, I learned one test. Not confession — everyone confesses when they're caught. Not remorse — remorse is cheap, it costs nothing but performance." He crouched, bringing his face into the light. "The test is what a man does when he could still run. You had a horse, Tam. You had a night, and a head start, and a tracker who thinks she knows your habits. You could be thirty miles gone by dawn and none of us would ever know you'd been here. Instead you told us. In front of the map. In front of her." A glance at Wren. "That's the test. That's the whole test."

"He vouched for me in Saltmere," Tam said. "With his oath. When Odile wanted me shackled and Brannic wanted me buried. Astrid staked his ranger's oath on a thief he'd chased nine days across open desert. So no — I'm not asking for trust. Trust gets earned, and I haven't earned a damn thing. I'm asking for one night. Give me until dawn, and if Sella's riders come down on this camp, you'll know everything I told you was true, because I'll be standing between you and them."

"That's a terrible plan," Brannic said.

"It's the only plan I have."

"It's a terrible plan," Brannic repeated, "and I've dug wells my whole life, so I know a hole when I see one. But I've also watched you sleep with one eye open every night since Saltmere, boy, and I know what that costs. Fine. One night."

Odile nodded slowly, already calculating — Tam could see the ledger turning behind her eyes, weighing odds and outcomes, the clerk's reflex that had kept her alive in Corvane's offices. "If we're expecting riders, we should break camp now and move in the dark. Cold march, but a live one."

All of them looked at Wren.

She hadn't spoken since her accusation. She stood at the edge of the firelight with the map pressed to her chest, and Tam watched her wrestle with it — he knew that wrestling, had done it his whole life, the fight between the wound and the arithmetic. Her father had run to protect her. This man had lied to protect her. Both of them had decided what she could bear without asking her, and she was only now, out here on the flats, learning to be asked.

"If you're lying," she said finally, "I will find you. Not the rangers. Me. And I grew up working desalination pumps, Master Voss — I know exactly how much pressure a man can take before something gives."

"Understood," Tam said, and meant it, and was — genuinely, unexpectedly — reassured.

* * *

They struck camp by starlight, fast and silent, and rode west into the cold with no fires and no talk. Tam took point, because he knew the ground here — a hard pan shelf that would hold hoofprints shallow, a dry wash that would confuse a tracker reading sign at speed. Behind him he heard the crew settle into the rhythm of the march: Brannic's mule complaining, Odile's steady breathing, Astrid riding drag with his rifle across his knees, and Wren beside him, silent, watchful, not forgiving him but not leaving him to the dark either.

Dawn came up the color of an old bruise, and with it, on the ridge behind them, the riders.

Three, as he'd said. Moving fast now, no longer bothering to hide. And Tam reined in, squinted against the rising light, and felt his stomach drop through the salt crust into whatever lay beneath the world — because there were four.

Not three. Four.

The fourth rider rode ahead of the others, small and slight, and even at this distance Tam knew the set of those shoulders, the way she sat a horse like she'd been poured onto it. Sella. But she wasn't leading a recovery team anymore. The fourth rider wore magistrate's colors — deep blue and iron grey — and carried a banner-pole with a sealed warrant case glinting at its tip, and behind the four riders, cresting the ridge in a long dust-plume, came a dozen more.

Corvane hadn't sent trackers after a map.

Corvane had come himself.

"They know," Tam breathed. "Gods — they know about the vault. They're not chasing us, they're *escorting* us. Right to the gate." He turned in the saddle, and found Astrid beside him, and Wren on her other side, all of them watching the dust-plume swallow the horizon. "Every word I told you, and worse. He's not offering amnesty this time. He's done offering."

Below them, far out across the white flats, the land began almost imperceptibly to slope downward, toward a valley every map called empty.

Toward the drowned town.
