## 10. The Drowned Town

The map said *empty*, and the land agreed with it for three days.

Astrid had learned long ago to distrust that kind of agreement. The flats lied the way a still pond lies — by showing you only yourself. White salt to every horizon, cracked into polygons like the scales of some buried serpent, and above it a sky so wide and blue it made a man feel like an insect pinned under glass. Nothing moved out here but heat shimmer and the occasional mirage of water that dissolved when you walked toward it, patient as a con artist.

"Maps call this the Hollow Reach," Odile said, riding beside him with the survey copy spread across her saddle-bow, weighted at the corners with flat stones she'd collected for the purpose. She'd been consulting it more often as they went east, and her voice had developed a new tightness over the last two days, like wire drawn through a die. "No settlements. No registered claims. No wells within forty miles. The government surveys mark it as geologically sterile."

"And you believe the government surveys now?" Brannic called from behind, where he rode with his dowsing rods lashed across his back like a pilgrim's cross.

"I believe in documents," Odile said. "It's what's written in them I've stopped believing."

Astrid let them argue. He was watching Juniper Hale's map instead — the original, which Wren had finally allowed out of its oilcloth wrapping on the second day, after Tam swore on his mother's grave he hadn't memorized it. The old prospector had drawn in details no honest surveyor would bother with: a curl of shading here, a notation in that same childish hand Wren kept going quiet over, a small inked sun in the corner of one panel as if whoever drew it had been thinking about morning when they made it.

And in the center of the Hollow Reach, where every official map showed nothing at all, Juniper had drawn a valley.

Not marked as a valley. Marked as a wound. A long gouge in the salt rendered in heavy strokes, with a single word beneath it in cramped letters: *LOW*.

They crested the last rise at midafternoon on the third day, and Astrid reined up so hard his horse stumbled.

* * *

It was there. Of course it was there. But seeing it was different from believing it, the way hearing a name is different from meeting the person.

The valley ran north to south for perhaps three miles, a shallow bowl sunk maybe sixty feet below the plain, and it was not empty. Salt had drifted into it over the years — decades, centuries — filling it the way snow fills a plowed field, softening every edge. But beneath that pale shroud, shapes. Lines too straight for nature. A grid of low walls emerging from the crust like the ribs of something enormous that had lain down here to die and been slowly swallowed. Streets. You could see streets, running true as a carpenter's level, and along them the stubs of foundations, and here and there the black mouths of doorways that the salt had not quite reached.

At the valley's center stood a structure that had resisted burial better than the rest: a stone tower, or the stump of one, rising twenty feet above the drifts, its top crowned with a corroded metal cap that caught the sun and threw it back in a dull orange flare.

Nobody spoke for a long time. The wind came down off the plain and stirred the salt, and fine white dust ghosted across the ruins and settled again, and Astrid thought: *it looks like the town is breathing.*

"That's not possible," Odile said finally. Her voice had gone very quiet, which from Odile was worse than shouting. "I filed the clearance surveys for this quadrant myself. There was a standard form. *Sterile basin, no structures, no water table.* I remember because it was one of the few easy ones." She was gripping her saddle-bow hard enough that her knuckles had gone the color of the salt below. "There were four clerks on that filing rotation. We processed six hundred quadrants that season."

"You're saying someone forged your paperwork," Tam said.

"I'm saying someone forged *everyone's* paperwork," Odile said. "This isn't a hidden valley. This is an erased one."

Wren hadn't said anything at all. She sat her horse at the lip of the rise with the wind pulling at her scarf, staring down into the bowl, and Astrid watched her father's face looking out of hers — the same set of the jaw, the same way of going very still when something mattered. When she finally spoke, her voice was flat as the flats themselves.

"He drew this before I was born. The valley. The tower." She tapped the oilcloth roll against her thigh. "He knew. Twenty years ago, he already knew this was down here."

"Then let's go find out what," Astrid said, and led them down.

* * *

The descent took an hour, though it was less than a mile, because the salt crust would not be hurried. It held under the horses' hooves with a sound like breaking crockery heard through a wall — a deep, resonant crunch that carried farther than it should have, so that Astrid kept glancing at the horizon expecting the sound to summon something. It summoned nothing. That was somehow worse.

Near the bottom the crust thinned and gave way to something stranger: ground that rang hollow underfoot. Brannic dismounted first, knelt, and struck the surface with the butt of his knife. The note that came back was clear and sustained, a bell-tone that traveled outward through the ground until Astrid could feel it humming in the soles of his boots.

"Well-diver's ground," Brannic murmured. He was a big man gone soft around the middle, with forearms like mooring rope and a face weathered past guessing at age, and Astrid had never seen him look like this — not afraid exactly, but the way a man looks entering a church he doesn't belong in. "There's space under us. Voids. Cisterns, maybe, or cellars. Whole streets of them." He pressed his palm flat against the ringing earth and left it there, eyes half-closed, like a physician taking a pulse. "And water. Deep. Moving."

"Moving?" Astrid asked.

Brannic opened his eyes. "Rivers don't sit still, ranger. Even buried ones."

They made camp that night in the lee of the tower, because it was the only windbreak the valley offered, and because none of them could bear to camp anywhere else. They picketed the horses on the rim where the salt gave way to scrub, and Astrid posted watches, and the fire they built was small and blue-flamed from burning scrub-wood heavy with mineral, casting shadows that jumped wrong.

Tam took first watch, and Astrid couldn't sleep anyway, so he walked.

That was how he found the well.

It stood fifty yards east of the tower, a circular stone housing waist-high, and unlike everything else in the valley it had been maintained — or rather, it had been *sealed*. A slab of dark metal covered its mouth, bolted down with eight bolts, and across the slab ran a stamp pressed deep into the metal, large enough to read by starlight:

**PROPERTY OF THE PROVISIONAL AUTHORITY — SEAL INTACT UNDER PENALTY OF LAW**

Below it, a registry number, and a date. Astrid crouched and traced the date with one finger. Forty-one years ago. Before Odile's forged surveys. Before Juniper's flight. Before any of it.

He walked the valley then, carefully, following the ghost-lines of the streets, and found another well. And another. Eleven in all, each capped with the same dark slab, the same stamp, the same registry number. Not different seals for different wells — the *same* seal, stamped from the same plate, laid down in a single campaign by men who had come here with a job to do and done it thoroughly.

Eleven wells in a town that officially did not exist.

Astrid stood in the middle of a dead street between two sealed wells and felt the awe he'd carried since the rim curdle slowly, sourly, into something else. Wonder was for landscapes. This was a crime scene. He'd spent nine days chasing a man who stole a name, and he'd thought that was the strangest theft he'd ever investigate. But this — this was the theft of a town. Not its wealth; its wealth was still here, buried and useless. Its *existence*. Someone had taken several hundred lives' worth of homes and wells and streets and filed them out of the world, and the instrument of the theft wasn't a crowbar but a stamp, and the thieves had signed their work because they'd known no one would ever come to read the signature.

Except Juniper Hale had come. And run. And spent twenty years drawing maps in margins for a daughter he couldn't face.

Astrid heard footsteps behind him and didn't reach for his sidearm, because he knew the stride — quick, deliberate, heels striking hard.

"You should be sleeping," Wren said.

"So should you."

She came and stood beside him at the nearest well, and for a while neither of them touched anything. The stars were ferocious out here, away from even Saltmere's lamps, and their light lay on the salt like frost.

"Counted eleven," she said.

"You counted too."

"I grew up with a prospector. You learn to count things." She crouched, studying the stamp without touching it. "Sefa's people," she said — the name from Juniper's letters, the descendants he'd written of in code and half-sentences, *the ones who stayed, the ones who keep what's kept* — "if they're real, if they're down here somewhere, they'll know what these are."

"If they're down here," Astrid said, "they've been watching us since we hit the rim. Maybe since the flats."

"Good," Wren said.

Astrid looked at her. In the starlight her expression was unreadable, but her hands weren't — they were steady, and that told him enough. Grief wearing anger as armor, he'd thought when he first met her at the pumps. But this was something else. This was a woman standing in the ruin of whatever had broken her father, deciding to walk all the way into it.

"They sealed the wells," he said. "All of them. Same day, same stamp. You don't do that to stop people drinking. You do it to stop people *remembering* there was ever anything to drink."

Wren was quiet a moment. Then: "My father's letters said he saw the cisterns. And something else, under them. Something he wouldn't write down even in letters he never sent." She rose, brushed salt from her knees. "Tomorrow we find out what scared him enough to spend twenty years running."

* * *

Dawn came up the color of old bone, and with it came the discovery that ended the argument about whether anyone lived in the valley.

Astrid woke to silence — the wrong kind. The horses, picketed on the rim, were screaming.

He was up with his sidearm before his mind finished the thought, boots crunching, and the others were close behind him, and what he saw at the picket line stopped him cold: nothing. No riders, no raiders, no predators. The horses stood shivering in a ring, whites of their eyes showing, and beyond them the plain stretched empty to every horizon.

But the ground was ringing.

Beneath their feet, beneath the whole valley, a deep tone had begun — felt more than heard, rising through the salt crust and up through boot-soles and shinbones, a slow pulsing hum like the heartbeat of something vast turning over in its sleep. Brannic dropped to his knees and pressed both palms to the ground, and his face went through wonder and terror in the space of a breath.

"It's the water," he whispered. "Something moved it. Down deep — gates, valves, I don't know — but the river just *turned over*. Like it heard us."

"The Rivergate," Odile breathed. She had her documents clutched to her chest like a shield. "Juniper's map. It's not a place on the river. It's a *door* in it."

Then the light changed.

It came from the direction of the tower — a gleam, a flash, deliberate, the way a mirror catches the sun when someone angles it on purpose. Once. Twice. Three times.

Astrid turned slowly, hand resting on his holstered sidearm, and scanned the base of the ruined tower, the shadowed doorways along the buried street, the long drifts of salt that lay over everything like a sheet drawn up to the chin.

From the darkness of a doorway thirty yards away, a figure stepped out onto the salt. Then another. Then a dozen, rising out of the ruins along the whole length of the street as if the drowned town itself were giving up its dead — lean, sun-darkened people in dust-colored robes, old and young, moving without hurry, surrounding the camp in a loose and practiced circle. At their center stood a woman older than the others, straight-backed, with a face seamed like the salt crust and eyes that had seen everything the valley had to hide.

None of them carried visible weapons. All of them carried the certainty of people who had been waiting a very long time.

The old woman looked at Astrid, at Wren, at each of them in turn, and when she spoke, her voice carried across the ringing ground as clearly as the bell-tone of the buried cisterns.

"You came down the road Juniper Hale drew," she said. "So you'll answer for it. Who told you the way — and what do you mean to take?"
