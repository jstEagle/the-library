## 12. Descent

Brannic had been listening to water his whole life, and he had never heard anything like this.

Not heard with his ears — though there was that too, a low thrum beneath the salt crust, below the ruins, below even the dead town's capped wells. He heard it in his teeth. In the soles of his boots. In the old scar along his ribs where a well-cable had snapped and nearly taken him down a shaft in his twenties. The scar ached before rain; every diver's did. But this was not an ache. This was a pulse.

*The Rivergate*, Sefa had called it, standing among her people in the drowned town's upper streets like a woman made of salt and patience. *You will not go down without one of us.* And then she had looked at Brannic — looked at him the way no one had ever looked at him, not as a laborer or a tool or a man who could be lowered into dark places on a rope — and said, *But you will lead. You are the only one of them who can hear it.*

He hadn't told her he could hear it. He hadn't told anyone.

They gathered at the mouth of the descent at first light, if light it could be called — a gray seepage through the salt ceiling far above, filtered down through forty feet of crusted brine until it came out the color of old bone. The Keepers' town did not have mornings. It had gradations of dim.

"Say what you like about the view," Tam Voss muttered beside him, "but I've seen nicer chapels."

"Chapels don't hold water," Brannic said.

"No," Wren Hale said quietly. "That's rather the point."

She stood at the edge of the flooded stairwell with the map unrolled across her forearm, Juniper Hale's careful lines glowing faintly in the bone-light. Brannic had watched her study that map for three days now, watched her trace her father's handwriting with a fingertip like a woman reading a letter from the dead. He knew that look. It was the look of someone standing at the lip of a shaft they'd spent their whole life being lowered into.

"The underlevels run dry to the third landing," she said. "After that—" She stopped. Looked up at him. "My father's notes say after that, the water rises. That's why he needed a diver."

"That's why you have one," Brannic said.

Astrid Oyelaran crouched at the stairwell's edge, testing the air the way rangers did, palm out, fingers spread, reading temperature and movement like other men read tracks. "Cold," he reported. "Moving. Slow, but moving." He glanced back at Odile, who was checking the seals on their lamp-oil for the fourth time, and at Tam, who was pretending not to be afraid and doing a poor job of it, and last at Sefa's chosen guide — a young Keeper named Ilo with river-stone eyes and a voice like a closed door.

"You don't have to come," Astrid said to Brannic. Not a question about courage. A ranger's courtesy: the offer of an exit.

Brannic almost laughed. Forty years of wells. Forty years of rope-burn and black water and the particular loneliness of being the man who went down where others wouldn't, and this ranger thought he might want an exit now, at the one place on all the flats where the water was singing.

"I've been coming my whole life," he said. "I just didn't know the address."

* * *

The stairs were cut stone, pre-Company work, older than any charter or magistrate's seal — each step dished in the middle by feet that had worn them smooth centuries before anyone thought to drown a town. Brannic went first because that was the order of things. Behind him came Ilo, then Wren, then Astrid, then Odile with the lamps, then Tam bringing up the rear because Tam had explained, with great seriousness, that someone trustworthy needed to guard against pursuit, and everyone had understood that what he meant was that he didn't trust himself not to bolt if the dark got too close, and everyone had let him keep his dignity by agreeing.

The dry levels smelled of salt and stone and time. Their lamps threw long shadows up walls carved with figures — people, Brannic realized slowly, hundreds of them, processions of them, carved walking toward a central motif that repeated on every landing: a circle bisected by a vertical line. A gate. A wheel. Water held and water released.

"They built their whole lives around it," Odile murmured behind him. Her clerk's voice had gone soft, reverent, the way it did when she handled old documents. "Look at the lintels. Every doorway has the mark. It wasn't hidden here. It was *worshipped*."

"It was *water*," Brannic said. "Same thing, if you're honest about it."

At the second landing they found the capped wells. Seven of them, sunk through the floor itself, each sealed with a disc of gray metal stamped with the government seal Brannic had seen on survey markers all his life and never once questioned. The Company seal. The Magistrate's office. Here, in a town that officially did not exist, on wells that officially held nothing.

Wren knelt at one and pressed her palm flat against the cap. "Cold underneath," she said. "You can feel it."

Brannic knelt beside her. Put his own hand down. And there it was again — stronger now, unmistakable, rising through forty feet of stone and seal and lie: the pulse. Slow. Enormous. Patient as geology.

*Thrum.*

He pulled his hand back like a man touching a sleeping animal.

"You feel it too," Wren said. Not a question.

"My whole life I've found water by listening," Brannic said. "Dry country teaches you. You put your ear to stone, you learn the difference between hollow and full, between a seam that weeps and an aquifer that *waits*. I've felt water move under a hundred miles of flats." He shook his head slowly. "I never felt it *beat*."

"What does that mean?" Tam asked from the rear, and his voice had lost its flippancy entirely. "Water doesn't beat. Water doesn't—"

"Pressure," Brannic said. "It means pressure. It means somewhere below us there is more water than this whole desert has seen in a hundred years, and it is not sitting still. It's *pushing*. It's been pushing against these caps, these doors, this gate, for longer than any of us have been alive." He stood. His knees cracked. He found that he was smiling, and couldn't stop. "I've spent forty years begging the ground for a cupful. Down there is a heart the size of a sea, and it's been waiting for someone to open the door."

Ilo spoke for the first time since the surface. "Sefa says the founders believed the gate should never open. That what was kept below was kept below for a reason." The young Keeper's eyes caught the lamplight. "But Sefa also says the reason changed, when the magistrates came. Ask yourself, diver: when you reach the bottom — who is the lock keeping out? And who is it keeping in?"

No one answered. Below them, faint as memory, the water pulsed.

* * *

The third landing ended at water.

Not a flood — a stillness. Black, glass-flat, filling the stairwell from wall to wall and descending into darkness that their lamps could not negotiate. The air above it was cold enough to see your breath. Brannic crouched and touched the surface and felt his skin prickle: cold, but not killing-cold. Moving, but barely. Fed from below.

"Tide," he said. "Or the closest thing to it. The gate breathes." He looked back at the others. "From here it's diving. Full kit. Ropes anchored at every junction. We go slow, we go together, and nobody — *nobody* — leaves the line. Black water eats people. It doesn't do it fast and it doesn't do it loud. It just takes them, and you find out three days later when they don't come up."

"Cheerful fellow," Tam said, but he was already checking his gear with hands that knew the work.

They dressed in silence. Brannic helped Wren with her harness straps, and she let him, which told him how frightened she was — the hard-eyed pump-girl from Saltmere, who flinched from every offered hand. When he cinched the last buckle he said, low, "Your father picked me out of a waystation crowd and trusted me with his map. I've been trying to work out why ever since."

"Because you're the best diver on the flats?"

"Because I'm the only diver who'd do it broke and off-contract," Brannic said. "He wanted someone with nothing to lose except the promise." He met her eyes. "We're going to find what he ran from. And when we do, whatever it is — you won't carry it alone. That's not the promise. That's just me talking."

For a moment her face did something complicated, grief and gratitude fighting over the same ground. Then she nodded once, and turned to the water, and became again the woman who worked the pumps.

They went down.

* * *

Black water is its own country. Brannic had told them this on the surface and now he showed them: a place where direction dies, where up and down are rumors, where the only truths are the rope in your hand and the breathing in your ears and the count. He led them down the flooded stairwell hand over hand, lamp beams swallowed within arm's reach, the world reduced to stone and cold and the soft percussion of their own hearts.

And beneath everything, growing with every fathom, the pulse.

*Thrum.*

He felt it in his chest now. Felt it set up a sympathetic rhythm in his own heartbeat, so that for long stretches he could not tell which beat was his and which belonged to the water, and he understood — suddenly, completely, the way you understand things underwater that you can never explain on land — why the founders had carved their god into every doorway. This was what worship was. This was what it had always been. Not belief. *Resonance.* Something vast below you, and your small self trembling in sympathy, and the choice — the terrible, holy choice — of whether to answer.

They passed through a submerged corridor where the carvings continued, and Brannic's lamp swept across them: the procession of figures again, but changed. Now they walked *away* from the gate-mark. Now some of them carried children. Now the stone showed hands raised, mouths open, and Brannic did not need Odile's scholarship to read what happened in this corridor a very long time ago.

He heard Wren make a sound through her regulator. He gripped her wrist. Held it. *Later,* the grip said. *Grieve later. Breathe now.*

The corridor opened. Their lamps, pushing desperately outward, found a space so large the beams simply gave up — a cavern, a chamber, a hall, something that had been a building once and was now a cathedral of dark water. Far above, impossibly, a shaft of gray light: a crack in the salt ceiling, letting down one thin thread of the surface world like a hair fallen into a well.

And ahead, at the far wall, where the water shallowed onto a shelf of ancient masonry —

The door.

Brannic surfaced first, dragging the line, and hauled himself onto the shelf, and the others came up gasping around him, and none of them spoke, because the door did not permit speech.

It was round. Thirty feet across, maybe more, ringed in stone so old the tool-marks had gone glassy. Across its face ran the great wheel — the bisected circle from every carving, rendered in bronze gone green-black with age, its spokes thick as a man's waist. Around the rim, in a script older than the Company, older than the magistrates, older maybe than Saltmere itself, ran an inscription that Odile would read aloud later, her voice shaking, but that Brannic did not need translated, because Ilo read it aloud right there, kneeling, in the old tongue, and then again in the common speech:

*WE DROWNED OUR TOWN TO KEEP THIS DOOR SHUT. WE DROWNED OUR NAMES SO NO ONE WOULD COME LOOKING. FORGIVE US. THE WATER IS NOT THE TREASURE. THE WATER IS THE WITNESS.*

Behind the door, against the bronze, against thirty feet of engineered stone, the Rivergate pushed.

*Thrum.*

Brannic laid both palms flat on the wheel. The metal was cold and alive. The pulse came up through his arms, through his shoulders, into his chest, and his heart answered it, beat for beat, diver and water keeping time like two instruments tuned to the same impossible note. Forty years of dry holes and dying towns and men paying taxes for water the magistrates sold back to them at famine prices — all of it, all of it, pressing on the far side of this door, patient as forgiveness.

"Well-diver," Ilo said softly. "Can you open it?"

Brannic did not answer at once. He listened — really listened, the way he had listened to a thousand wells, asking the ground its one question: *what do you want?*

Every well had answered the same way. *Take me. Take me up. Give me to the thirsty.*

This one answered differently. This one said: *Remember first.*

He turned to look at his crew — Wren with her father's map clutched to her chest like a shield, Astrid with his hand already near the wheel, Odile pale and lit up from inside, Tam staring at the inscription with the expression of a man reading his own sentence. Behind them, the black water of the drowned corridor lay still as held breath.

"Yes," Brannic said. "I can open it."

His hands found the spokes. The bronze groaned — a sound like a word held back for a century, finally clearing its throat.

And from somewhere beyond the door, faint but unmistakable, came a sound none of them expected: footsteps.
