## 13. What the Vault Holds

Odile had spent eleven years filing other people's secrets, and she had learned to read a room by its locks.

The vault door told her everything before Brannic's lamp ever touched it. It was not the door of a treasury — no gilding, no boastful crest, no iron meant to impress. It was the door of a registry. Plain steel gone brown with salt-bloom, banded twice, and stamped in the upper left corner with a seal she knew better than her own signature: the double-branch and closed fist of the Magistracy of the Lowlands, third iteration, pre-Corvane. She had catalogued forty-one documents bearing that seal during her years in the Records Annex at Corvane's seat. She had filed them under *Hydrographic Survey, Restricted*. She had been ordered to forget them, and she had been very good at obeying orders, right up until she wasn't.

"Odile." Wren's voice came low through the flooded gallery behind them, echoing off stone that had been underwater for two generations and still smelled of it — cold iron and river silt and something older, something like a church. "You've gone pale."

"I've seen this seal," Odile said. Her voice sounded strange to her own ears, flattened, official. The clerk's voice. The one she'd sworn off in Saltmere when she signed the charter and burned her commission papers in Brannic's cookfire. "On paper. In boxes. I never once saw it on a door."

Brannic set his shoulder against the wheel. The well-diver was a mountain of a man gone soft at the edges, and he had been reverent the whole descent — crossing himself with muddy fingers at each capped well they passed, murmuring to the water like it could hear him. Now he spat into his palms, gripped the wheel, and said, "Somebody lock this from the outside, or we turning?"

"From the outside," Sefa said.

The old Keeper had come down with them, though her people had begged her not to. She moved through the drowned underlevels of her ancestors' town like a woman walking her own kitchen, stepping over collapsed lintels without looking, ducking beams she must have struck a thousand times as a girl. When Brannic hauled on the wheel and the mechanism groaned — a sound like a glacier clearing its throat, salt cracking out of the works in long red flakes — Sefa did not flinch. But her hand found the wall, and stayed there.

The door swung inward six inches and stopped on its own weight.

Cold air came out. Not dead air — Odile had expected dead air, the preserved nothing of a sealed tomb — but moving air, faintly sweet, carrying the mineral breath of deep water. The Rivergate itself was somewhere below them; Brannic had felt it since the second landing, pressing up through the stone like a pulse, and he kept touching the floor with his palm the way a physician touches a wrist.

"Lamp," Odile said, and took it from Tam without waiting, because if she waited she would lose her nerve, and she had not clawed her way out of the Annex and across four hundred miles of salt to lose her nerve at a doorway.

She went in first.

* * *

The room was smaller than the door promised. That was her first thought, and her second thought was that whoever built it had understood something about scale: you did not need a cathedral to hold a truth. You needed shelves.

They ran floor to ceiling on three walls, cut directly into the living rock, and every shelf was full. Not gold. Not plate, not coin, not the plundered wealth of a drowned town — though there were strongboxes, and Odile noted them the way she noted everything, automatically, in the back-catalogue of her mind. The strongboxes were pushed to the corners like afterthoughts. What filled the shelves was paper.

Ledgers. Folios. Map tubes sealed with wax. Bound volumes of council minutes, their spines hand-lettered in a careful civic script: *Riverton Township, Year One of the Diversion*. Year Two. Year Three. A whole run of them, forty volumes or more, marching down the shelf like witnesses called to stand.

"Riverton," Wren said behind her. "That's what it was called."

"That's what it was called," Sefa confirmed. Her voice had changed in here. Smaller. "Before they took even the name off the maps."

Odile lifted the first volume down and opened it on her knee, and eleven years of training took over whether she wanted it to or not. She read the way clerks read — fast, structural, hunting the anomaly — and within three minutes she stopped breathing normally.

It was all here.

Not an inference. Not a rumor dressed in a prospector's paranoia. It was here in the town's own hand, dated, witnessed, countersigned. The correspondence between the Riverton town council and the Magistracy's Office of Water Rights, beginning fourteen months before the flood. The surveyor's reports establishing the river's course and the town's dependence on it. Then the letters changing tone — polite, then pleading, then furious — as the Magistracy announced its intention to construct "improvements" upstream. Then the engineering memoranda, stamped RESTRICTED in the same violet ink Odile had mixed herself a hundred times in the Annex copy-room: the diversion channel specifications, the dam site at the narrows, the projected inundation levels.

And then the last folder, and Odile's hands were shaking so badly she nearly dropped it.

It contained the requisition orders. Not for the dam — for the evacuation that never happened. A full census of Riverton, nine hundred and forty souls, with columns for resettlement allotments that had been drawn up, approved, funded — and then struck through, every line, in a single decisive hand, with a marginal note in that same hand: *Cost exceeds benefit. Recommend alternative disposition.*

Alternative disposition.

Nine hundred and forty souls. They had computed the cost of moving them, found it inconvenient, and turned the water loose instead. And then they had capped the wells, sealed the survey, purged the maps, and written a town out of existence so cleanly that twenty years later a magistrate's clerk could file the whole affair under *Hydrographic Survey, Restricted* and forget it, because forgetting had been made easy, forgetting had been made a career.

"Odile." Astrid's voice. The ranger stood in the doorway with his hat in his hands, which he did when he entered rooms he considered holy. "What is it? You look like—"

"It's all here," she said. Her voice cracked on the second word and she let it. "Astrid. Everything. The orders, the census, the signatures. Corvane's predecessor — Aldous Vane-Marrec — his seal is on the striking-out himself. And underneath—" she turned pages with wet eyes, hunting, confirming, because a finding without citation was worth nothing, she had built her whole life on that, "—underneath, the internal review. They investigated themselves. They found the deaths 'regrettable but fiscally justified.' It's minuted. It's *minuted*, Astrid. Somebody wrote that sentence, and somebody else approved it, and both of them went home to dinner."

Nobody spoke. The lamp hissed. Somewhere far below, through forty feet of rock, the Rivergate pressed against its gate, patient as water always is.

Tam crouched by the strongboxes and pried one open with his knife, and inside were not coins but personal effects — wedding rings strung on cord, a child's christening cup, letters bundled and tied. He looked up at Odile with an expression she had never seen on the thief's quick face. It was the face of a man who had picked a hundred locks expecting coin and found, instead, the reason he'd been hired.

"They didn't hide treasure," Tam said quietly. "They hid evidence."

"And Juniper Hale found it," Wren said.

* * *

They found his traces an hour later, and Odile would remember the finding for the rest of her life, because it was the moment the history in the room stopped being history.

Wren found it — of course she did; she had her father's eye for marks the way Odile had a clerk's eye for seals. On the inner face of the vault door, low, where a man kneeling would leave it: a pencil notation in a cramped, familiar hand. Survey coordinates. A date. And beneath them, three words:

*Wren — if you're reading.*

There was more, tucked into a gap in the shelving where a folio had been removed and replaced with a packet wrapped in oilcloth. Juniper Hale's handwriting covered both sides of thirty pages, and Odile read over Wren's shoulder because Wren's hands were not steady enough to hold the pages flat, and someone had to be.

He had come here nineteen years ago. A young prospector chasing a rumor of water, guided by an old Keeper — Sefa's sister-in-law, Sefa said from the doorway, in a voice like ash — who had decided the secret should not die with her generation. He had seen the vault. He had read what Odile was reading now. And he had done the arithmetic that Odile, standing in the same room, had also done: that a man who carried this proof out would carry a target on his back, and that everyone who helped him would wear the same target, and that the Magistracy had already demonstrated precisely what it did to inconvenient populations.

So he had run. Loudly. Ostentatiously. A prospector with a map, selling pieces of it, drinking too much, telling tall stories in waystations from Saltmere to the coast — making himself the legend, making the Rivergate a treasure story, a fool's errand, so that anyone who followed him followed *him*, and never traced the thread back down through the flooded town to the quiet Keepers who had kept the truth alive for two generations in a valley the maps called empty.

"He wasn't running away," Wren said. Her voice was terrible and steady. "He was running *toward*. Every year. For twenty years. Toward anything that wasn't us."

"He was running between," Sefa said. "Between them and us. All this time." The old woman came forward and laid her palm flat on the shelf of minutes, on the volume marked *Year Three*. "We hated him for a while, some of us. Thought he'd abandoned his post. My sister-in-law died before she could tell the rest of us what she'd shown him. It took me twenty years to understand what he understood in one night: that the only thing keeping my people alive was nobody looking. And so he made sure everybody looked at him instead."

Odile turned away, because she was a clerk, and clerks know that some documents are not meant to be read over another person's shoulder while they weep.

She busied herself instead with the thing she was made for. She began a catalogue. She had no forms, no annex, no violet ink — she used the back of Tam's forged charter and a stub of pencil — but her hands remembered their discipline, and item by item the room became legible: census, correspondence, engineering memoranda, internal review, evacuation requisitions, and — her pencil stopped — a final folder, unmarked, containing a single sheet bearing the current seal. Corvane's seal. Dated eight years ago.

A receipt. For one (1) recovered folio, surrendered by one J. Hale under interview, condition: coerced. And below it, in Corvane's own small precise hand, a routing note: *Retain. Subject remains at large. Reopen file upon recovery of remainder.*

He had known. Eight years, Corvane had known exactly what lay under the flats, and he had let Juniper live — had let him wander and drink and tell his stories — because a live legend was a compass needle pointing away from the vault, and because someday, when the drought got bad enough and the monopoly needed new water rights badly enough, he would reopen the file and take the rest.

Which was why riders had followed them from Saltmere. Which was why amnesty had been offered at gunpoint. Which was why, somewhere above them right now, Corvane's camp sat on the rim of the valley like a held breath.

"Astrid," Odile said, and her voice came out in the flat official register again, the clerk's register, the one that made men sit up in hearing rooms. "You need to see this last page. And then we need to decide — tonight, before he comes down — how much of this room we can carry, and what we do about the rest."

Astrid crossed the vault and read. His jaw tightened; she watched the ranger's oath move behind his eyes like weather.

"Copy what matters," he said finally. "All of it, if we can. Wren copies, you verify, Tam finds us satchels. We don't burn anything. We don't destroy anything." He looked around at the shelves, at the nine hundred and forty names sleeping in their ledgers, at the old woman with her hand on the past. "This room has been buried long enough. Whatever happens next — it doesn't get buried again."

Above them, muffled through stone and salt and sixty feet of drowned town, came a sound none of them mistook for thunder.

Hooves. Many hooves, coming down into the valley in the dark.
