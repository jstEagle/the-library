## 14. Corvane Arrives

They came at the hour when the light went thin and gold, when the salt valley held its breath between day and dark.

Astrid saw them first from the rim of the drowned town — a line of riders where no riders should be, threading down through the pass that Sefa's people called the Needle. Dust rose behind them in a long gray banner, and beneath it, catching the last of the sun, the dull gleam of government-issue rifle barrels. He counted without meaning to. Fourteen. Then fifteen, lagging with a limping horse. Then a wagon, heavy-tired and slow, drawn by two mules that had been whipped too often to fear anything but the whip.

"Odile," he said, and his voice came out flat and quiet, the way it did when his body knew trouble before his mind agreed to it. "Company."

She was beside him before he finished the word. She had a clerk's eyes, made for scanning columns of figures, and she found the riders faster than he had. "That's not a patrol," she said. "Patrols ride in pairs. That's a column." A pause, and then, softer: "That's Corvane."

Below them, in the ruins of the town that maps called empty, the Keepers were lighting their evening fires. Smoke rose thin and blue from chimneys that hadn't breathed in twenty years until this expedition arrived. Wren was at the well-head with Brannic, hauling water for the mules. Tam sat on a fallen lintel sharpening a knife he barely needed, because sharpening was what his hands did when his mind was working. Sefa stood in the doorway of the old meetinghouse, watching the fires as if they were children she was counting home.

Astrid watched the riders come and felt the strange stillness that had lived in him since the flats — nine days tracking a man who stole nothing, a bargain struck too fast, a promise carried like a stone in his chest — all of it narrowing now to a single point, the way water narrows going through a gate.

"They tracked us from Saltmere," Odile said. "I told you he would."

"I know you did."

"He'll have warrants. Real ones, stamped and sealed. He'll have amnesty papers too. He always travels with both." She said it like a woman reciting her own autopsy. "Astrid. When he offers — and he will offer — everyone here needs to know what refusal costs. Because I've seen the ledger of what Corvane pays people who say yes, and I've seen the other ledger too. The one nobody files."

Astrid turned from the rim. "Then we tell them ourselves. Before he does."

* * *

He gathered them by the largest fire, in the square where the drowned town's founders had once stood to make announcements to neighbors who were already dead or dying under the water they'd diverted. It was a bad place for good news and a worse place for lies, which was why Astrid chose it. The Keepers drifted close, silent, wrapped in their salt-bleached robes. Sefa did not stop them. She stood at the edge of the firelight with her arms folded, an old woman carved out of patience, and waited to see what these strangers would do when the wolf finally showed its teeth.

"Riders coming through the Needle," Astrid said. "Fifteen, maybe sixteen, plus a supply wagon. Magistrate Corvane's colors on the lead rider — I can see them plain from the rim. They'll be here within the hour, maybe less if the light holds."

Brannic spat into the fire. The well-diver was a mountain of a man gone soft at the edges, with hands so calloused they clicked when he flexed them. "Sixteen guns," he said. "Against what? Us and a town full of grandmothers?"

"The Keepers have ways of making sixteenth guns very expensive," Sefa said from the shadows, without heat. "But expensive is not impossible. Speak your piece, ranger."

Astrid looked around the fire. At Wren, whose hands were scarred from the pumps and whose face was doing what it always did — showing nothing while everything behind it moved like weather. At Tam Voss, the thief, watching Astrid with those quick gray eyes that never quite settled anywhere. At Odile, straight-backed, hands folded, a woman who had spent ten years filing away other people's crimes and had only just learned what it cost to keep one of her own.

"Corvane won't come shooting first," Astrid said. "Not here, not yet. He wants the vault intact and the records unburned, and bodies make questions. So he'll talk. He'll offer each of you something — amnesty, money, a clean slate, whatever he thinks you want most. And I need every one of you to hear me now, before he says it, because things said after sound different than things said before."

He paused. The fire cracked. Somewhere in the flooded underlevels below their feet, Brannic had told him once, the Rivergate's pressure sang through the stone — a note too low for ears, felt in the teeth, in the ribs. Astrid felt it now, or believed he did.

"What's down there ends him," he said. "The records in that vault are the death of everything Corvane built and everything his patrons farm off the backs of dying towns. He knows it. Juniper Hale knew it twenty years ago, and it broke his life in half, and he ran anyway rather than let it be buried with him. We are standing on the reason Juniper left his daughter. And Corvane is coming to take it back into the dark."

Wren spoke without looking up. "You're asking us to refuse him."

"I'm telling you what refusing means. Amnesty refused is a warrant accepted. If we stand together tomorrow, every one of us becomes a criminal by nightfall — except the Keepers, who were criminals the day they were born, according to men like Corvane." He met her eyes then, and held them. "I'm not asking. I'm giving you the hour to decide. Anyone who walks down to that wagon and takes his paper, I'll consider it smart, not shameful. I've been poor enough to know the price of a clean slate. I won't think less of anyone who buys one."

Silence, except the fire.

It was Tam who broke it, and he broke it laughing — a short, dry sound with no humor in it, like a stick snapping. "You're a strange man, Oyelaran. Most rangers would've chained us to the mules by now and called it duty."

"Most rangers aren't standing on a massacre."

"No," Tam said. "No, they're not." He looked into the fire for a long moment, and Astrid watched something move behind his face — the thing Tam kept locked up, the debt he'd hinted at on the trail and never named. "I'll hear him out," Tam said at last. "Fair's fair. But I'll tell you now so there's no surprise later: whatever he offers, I've already got what he gave me, and it wasn't worth the carrying."

* * *

They came down through the ruins at dusk, and Corvane's riders came up to meet them, and the two companies stopped forty paces apart in the wide salt pan where the town's market had once stood — though nothing remained of it above ground but a suggestion of walls, like a memory pressing up through a bedsheet.

Magistrate Aldous Corvane dismounted last, which was itself a performance; Astrid had seen the type before, men who entered rooms by letting rooms notice them. He was tall and dry as a fence post, with a magistrate's gray coat brushed immaculate despite nine days of hard riding, and a face built for portraits — even, reasonable, faintly sorrowful, as though the world's disappointments were a personal weight he bore for everyone's benefit. He walked forward alone. His riders stayed mounted, rifles across their thighs, casual as men waiting for a race to start.

"Astrid Oyelaran," Corvane said. His voice carried beautifully, trained on courtrooms. "Ranger, second charter, Saltmere district. On leave, I believe. Or perhaps deserting — the paperwork was unclear." He smiled to show it was a joke. Nothing about him suggested it was.

"Off-contract," Astrid said. "Which means the only law out here is mine, and I'm exercising it."

"Spoken like a man who's spent too long alone with his own opinions." Corvane's gaze traveled past him, cataloguing: Wren, Odile, Brannic, Tam. It rested on Tam a half-second longer than the others, and something passed between the two men — not warmth, but recognition, the way two blades recognize each other. "I'll be brief, since the light is going and my men have ridden hard. There is a document beneath this valley. Stolen government property. I have a warrant for its recovery, signed and sealed, and I have warrants besides for the arrest of one Tam Voss, fugitive, and one Odile Ferran, clerk, absent without leave and suspected of records theft."

"You forgot me," Wren said.

Corvane turned to her with genuine, practiced regret. "Miss Hale. No. You're not wanted for anything. You're wanted *by* something — your father's map, your father's mistakes. I'd hoped to spare you this." He reached into his coat and drew out a folded paper, heavy stock, wax seal glinting red in the last light. "So let me do it properly. Amnesty. Full and unconditional, for every person standing here — the thief, the runaway clerk, the off-contract ranger, all of it wiped clean, backdated, filed and forgotten. In exchange: the map, the document, and your silence. You walk away rich in the only currency that matters out here, which is a past nobody can hold over you." He let the paper catch the light. "Refuse, and I'm afraid the warrants do what warrants do. There's a gallows frame in Saltmere that hasn't been used in eleven years. My clerks assure me it's still sound."

Astrid heard Odile breathe once, slow and controlled, the breath of a woman steadying herself against a number she'd already calculated.

"You first, Master Voss," Corvane said pleasantly. "Since we have history."

Tam stepped forward. Up close, in the failing light, Astrid could see how thin he'd gotten on the trail, how the desert had pared him down to wire and intent. "You hired me in Saltmere," Tam said. "Back room of the assayer's office. You said find the old man's map, bring it to you, and my brother's debts would vanish. All of them. Every marker he owed in every town from here to the coast." He paused. "You remember saying that?"

"I remember being generous."

"And then I read the file you had me copy as proof of authorization. Sealed survey records. Rivergate hydrology, diversion schedules, compensation ledgers for a town that isn't supposed to exist." Tam's voice stayed level, almost gentle, which made it worse somehow. "You weren't going to pay my brother's debts, Magistrate. You were going to bury them in the same hole you buried this valley. So I took the map and ran, and I've been running ever since, and I want you to know—" he smiled, and it was the first honest smile Astrid had ever seen on him, crooked and tired and free— "running's been the best work I've ever done."

Corvane's face did not change. That was the terrible thing about it; it simply absorbed the blow and remained portrait-ready. "Your brother's debts don't vanish because you've developed principles," he said. "They're held by men who answer to me. Walk away tonight and they're paid by morning. Stay, and I'll have them collected in front of you, slowly, as instruction." He extended the paper. "Last kindness I'll offer."

Astrid braced himself. This was the moment — the whole expedition balanced on it, and he had no right to tip the scales, and every instinct screamed to speak, to vouch, to spend his oath like a coin. He kept his mouth shut. Some choices had to be owned to mean anything.

Tam looked at the paper for a long time. Then he reached out — and pushed it gently back against Corvane's chest.

"My brother died in the spring," he said quietly. "Fever. Never knew what I was doing or why. So the debts are just debts now, and debts are just numbers, and numbers—" he glanced at Odile— "she tells me numbers can be survived. Ask me again when I'm starving. Tonight, no."

Something moved through the group behind Astrid — not words, just a settling, like sand finding its level. He felt it in his shoulders. One refusal, spoken aloud, made the next one cheaper.

"Master Brannic," Corvane said, turning smoothly, as if Tam had merely declined a drink. "A simple man's terms. A well-drilling license of your own. Your name on the charter, your rig, your crew. Every man in the lowlands dreams of it. Say yes and it's yours by season's end."

Brannic scratched his jaw. "Been diving wells thirty years," he said. "Found water under ground nobody thought had any. You know what I learned?" He didn't wait. "Water doesn't care whose name's on the license. It goes where it's going. Always has." He nodded down at the salt beneath their feet, at the heartbeat Astrid could almost feel through his boot soles. "There's a river under here that's been patient a long, long time. I aim to see where it goes. That's my license."

"Clerk Ferran." Corvane's voice cooled by a degree, the first crack in the performance. "Ten years of faithful service. I signed your certification myself. Whatever sentiment has taken hold of you, I remind you that sentiment does not survive unemployment. Refuse me and you'll never file a document in this territory again. No office will touch a traitor to the registry."

Odile stepped forward, and Astrid saw her hands were shaking — and saw her plant them at her sides anyway, forcing them still, a woman holding herself upright by main strength. "You're right," she said. "I'll never file anything again. Do you know what I filed for ten years? Diversion orders. Compensation denials. Death certificates for drownings listed as accidents, in towns listed as empty. I filed the cover-up page by page, in triplicate, and I told myself it was just ink." Her voice caught, steadied. "It was never just ink. So no, Magistrate. No amnesty. I'm done filing your version of events. From tonight I'm testifying to mine."

Corvane looked at her for a moment longer, and Astrid saw — just for a flash, quickly buried — something that might have been genuine bewilderment. Men like Corvane understood greed and fear completely. Loyalty freely given, to strangers, over advantage — that was a language they'd never learned to read.

Then there was only Wren, standing slightly apart, and Corvane saved her for last the way a butcher saves the cut he knows will bleed.

"Miss Hale," he said, and softened his whole body toward her, grief worn like cologne. "Twenty years ago your father stood roughly where you're standing and I offered him nearly the same terms. He chose pride. It cost him everything — his claim, his name, his family. I watched him throw his life away rather than accept help, and I have regretted my part in his ruin ever since." He held out the paper a final time. "Don't inherit his mistake. Take the amnesty. Go home to Saltmere. Let the dead keep their secrets — they've kept them this long."

And Wren Hale, who had refused her father's map for three days because refusing was the only armor she owned, looked at the paper, and then at Corvane, and Astrid saw the anger drain out of her face and leave behind something far more dangerous: clarity.

"You killed his life," she said, "and you're offering to forgive me for noticing." She didn't raise her voice at all. "My father ran from you for twenty years and never once stopped paying for what he saw here. You want to know what I choose? I choose to finish what he started. Keep your paper. I've seen what your signature's worth — there's a valley full of graves under my feet with it on every deed."

The silence afterward was enormous. Out beyond the ruined walls, the first stars were surfacing over the Needle, and somewhere below them the hidden river pressed against its gate, patient as it had been for twenty years.

Corvane folded the amnesty paper along its crease, unhurried, and put it away. When he looked up, the portrait-face was gone. What remained was smaller and much more honest: a man doing arithmetic.

"So be it," he said. "Warrants stand. Sergeant — torches. If they won't come out, we'll see how long the grandmothers' cisterns last." He turned to mount, then paused, and gave Astrid one last look over his shoulder. "You could have been smart, ranger. I do hope the view from the wrong side of history is worth it."

"It's got a river coming," Astrid said. "Should be lovely."

The riders withdrew to the mouth of the valley, and within the hour their campfires bloomed there — fifteen of them, ringed and watchful, sealing the Needle like a wound closed with hot iron.

Astrid stood in the dark among his people — a thief, a clerk, a well-diver, a daughter, and a town of ghosts — and listened to Sefa give quiet orders in the old tongue. The siege had begun. And below their feet, faint but unmistakable now, the Rivergate sang.
