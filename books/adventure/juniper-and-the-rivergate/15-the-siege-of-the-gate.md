## 15. The Siege of the Gate

The Keepers sealed the vault at dusk, and the sound it made was nothing like a door.

Wren stood in the drowned town's central square — a square that had been underwater for sixty years and still remembered being a place where people gathered — and listened to the gate close behind them. It was not stone grinding on stone. It was deeper than that, a note felt in the teeth and the breastbone, as if the whole valley had swallowed and settled. Brannic had gone pale when he heard it. He was a man who had spent thirty years listening to water through rock, and he said only one word: "Sealed."

Sefa's people worked with the unhurried efficiency of those who had rehearsed a catastrophe for generations. They had names for everything: the long gallery, the dry stair, the cistern hall, the room they called the remembering place, which was the vault, which was now shut. Within an hour of Corvane's riders appearing on the southern rim, the Keepers had withdrawn into the ruins' deep levels, collapsed two choke points behind them, and begun rationing water from stores that had been laid down before Wren was born.

She had expected panic. What she found instead was routine, and that frightened her more.

"They've done this before," she said to Sefa, watching an old man mark tallies on a slate by the cistern hall.

"Twice in my lifetime," Sefa said. "Riders come, we seal, they grow bored or they die of thirst on the rim, and we open again." She said it the way a farmer talks about frost. Then her eyes went to the south, where the last light was catching dust plumes, and something moved behind them that was not routine at all. "But never this many. And never one who knows what he's looking at."

Corvane had brought forty riders, two supply wagons, and a surveyor's kit. Wren had watched through a crack in the ruined customs house as his men staked out the valley mouth with the patience of men who intended to stay until winter. By full dark, fires ringed the southern rim like a necklace of orange beads, and the first sentries were already walking the perimeter of the salt crust above the town, boots crunching on ground that had once been rooftops.

The water cache was the first thing he took.

It happened on the second day, and Wren didn't understand what she was seeing until it was done. A Keeper named Ilo — young, quick, trusted with the surface runs — had gone up through the dry stair to the hidden cistern on the northern slope, the one that fed the ruins during a siege. He came back at a dead run, blood on his temple, and the news came with him like cold air through a broken window: Corvane's men had found the cistern, drained it into their own barrels, and left a warrant nailed to the wellhead.

*By order of Magistrate Corvane, this water is seized as evidence in the matter of unauthorized excavation.*

Evidence. Wren read the copy Odile had transcribed three times, looking for the joke in it. There wasn't one. That was the horror of magistrates; they could make theft sound like paperwork.

"He's not trying to starve us," Odile said that night, in the lamplight of the cistern hall, surrounded by the Keepers' children who were pretending not to listen. "He's trying to make us thirsty enough to negotiate. Thirst makes people reasonable. That's in the training manuals — I filed them."

"How long can we hold?" Astrid asked.

Brannic answered before anyone else, because it was his question to answer. He knelt with his palm flat against the floor, the way he did when he was reading water, and his face was terrible with concentration. "There's a vein under the east gallery. Small. Cold. It'll give us drinking water if we're careful, but it won't give us comfort, and it won't give us enough to wash a wound." He looked up. "We're careful, we last a month. We're careless, we last eight days."

"A month," Sefa repeated. "Then we are in a race between his supplies and our discipline." She turned to the assembled Keepers, and her voice changed — became the voice of someone who had given orders across sixty years of sieges real and imagined. "Rationing begins tonight. Half measures. No one draws without a tally. And no one," and here her eyes swept the room and landed, briefly, on each of the outsiders in turn, "no one speaks of what is behind the sealed gate. Not to each other. Not in sleep. The gate holds because we hold."

Wren lay awake that night on a pallet in the remembering place's antechamber, listening to the town breathe. Sixty years dead, and it still breathed — air moving through flooded galleries, water ticking against stone, the great sealed gate holding its note below everything like a held breath. Somewhere above, forty men sat around fires on the bones of the people whose secrets they'd been paid to keep.

She thought about her father, and for the first time since Saltmere, she thought about him without anger.

* * *

The days found their shape, and the shape was thirst.

Morning: tally marks, the east gallery vein, Brannic teaching two Keeper children to listen for water the way other children learned letters. Midday: heat pressing down through the salt, the ruins' upper rooms becoming ovens, everyone retreating downward into the cool wet dark where the drowned town kept its old temperature like a grudge. Evening: council, arguments, the same arithmetic performed with different numbers of hope.

Wren learned the ruins the way you learn a wound — intimately, unwillingly, until it was part of your map of yourself. She learned that the flooded streets below the third level were clear as glass and lit by nothing, that fish no larger than a fingernail lived in the old chapel, white and blind and patient. She learned that the Keepers sang when they worked, low songs with no words she knew, and that the songs were older than the flood. She learned that grief, given enough time and enough company, becomes architecture.

On the fifth day, Corvane escalated.

They heard it before they understood it — a rhythmic booming through the stone, distant and deliberate, like a heart learning to knock. Tam, who had taken to sleeping near the choke points with a knife under his hand, was the first to name it. "Picks," he said. "They're not waiting us out anymore. They're looking for another way in."

Sefa's face when she heard it was the face of a woman watching a fire start in a house she'd already lost once. "The surveyor," she said. "Corvane has government surveys. Old ones. Sealed ones." Her eyes went to Odile, and there was no accusation in them, only the weary certainty of someone who had always known this day would come. "Your magistrate knows where the soft stone is."

"I never saw those surveys," Odile said quickly, and then stopped, because the lie was useless here, and everyone knew it, and Odile's whole life had been built on knowing which lies were worth telling. She sat down slowly on a dry-stone step. "I filed the index. I never read the documents themselves. But the index had entries. Survey expeditions, 9th year of the old charter. *Site assessment, submerged structures, southern basin.* I remember thinking it was odd, that anyone would survey something underwater." She laughed, and it was a bad sound. "I was twenty-two. I thought the odd thing was the survey."

The picks worked for two days and then stopped, and no one knew whether that was better or worse. Silence, Brannic said, could mean failure. It could also mean they'd found what they wanted and were being quiet about it.

It was during those two days of terrible silence that Wren found the letters.

Not Juniper's letters — she'd found those in the claim shack, weeks ago, and carried them against her ribs ever since like coals she couldn't put down or hold. These were different. She'd been assigned to help catalog the Keepers' stores in a side chamber off the cistern hall, a room of salt-crusted shelves and careful labels, and tucked behind a row of preserved grain jars she found a bundle wrapped in oilcloth, tied with wire, labeled in a hand she knew better than her own.

*J. Hale — correspondence, unsent.*

Twenty years of letters. She had read some of them by lantern light in the claim shack, the ones from the early years, the ones full of excuses and self-accusation tangled together. But these were later ones, and they were addressed differently. Not to *Wren*. To *Sefa*.

Her hands shook so badly she nearly dropped the bundle.

She took them to Sefa directly, because she was her father's daughter and had inherited his inability to sit with a question overnight. The old woman was in the gallery above the chapel, mending a net that hadn't been used in sixty years, and when Wren held out the oilcloth package without a word, Sefa set down her work and looked at it for a long time.

"You knew," Wren said.

"I knew he wrote." Sefa untied the wire with fingers that were steady and slow. "Once a year, sometimes twice, a letter would come by ways I will not describe with Corvane's ears everywhere. Never a return address. Never a request. Just — reports." She drew out the topmost letter, yellowed soft as cloth. "Of the men following him."

Wren sat down on the wet stone beside her, because her legs had decided the matter without consulting her.

"Read it," Sefa said gently. "You should hear it from him. It's why he wrote them. He told me once — *if anything happens to me, she'll need to know I wasn't running away. She'll need to know what I was running toward.*"

Wren unfolded the letter. Her father's handwriting, cramped and slanting, pressed hard enough to emboss the page — a man writing fast, writing scared, writing anyway.

*Sefa — Two more of Corvane's men reached me at Redstone Crossing. I led them east, away from you, three days hard, and doubled back west once they'd committed. They know I'm alive. That means they know the Rivergate is real, and that means everything I do from now on is worth doing badly rather than not at all. Tell your people the seals are holding. Tell them a stranger in the eastern towns drinks his water slow and thinks of them. Tell my daughter —*

And there it stopped. The ink faded where the pen had hovered, the paper worn thin at that exact spot from being touched, over years, by a thumb worrying at a sentence that wouldn't come.

*Tell my daughter.* Twenty years of letters, and every one of them broke at the same place.

Wren read them all. She read them through the afternoon while the siege ground on overhead, while the water ration shrank, while Corvane's men walked the salt above the bones of the drowned. Letter after letter, year after year, the same architecture of devotion: *led them north, led them south, spent the winter letting myself be seen in towns I'd never otherwise visit so they'd believe the trail ran east.* He had made himself the lure. He had spent two decades being hunted on purpose, keeping the hunt alive and pointed away from Sefa's people, because a live hunt needed a quarry and he had volunteered to be it.

And the letters to Wren — the ones that had stopped coming, the silence she had built her anger around like a wall — the letters explained that too. *If I write to her, they will watch her. If they watch her, she is leverage. Every year I don't write is a year she stays worthless to them. I am buying her safety with her hatred, and it is the only currency I have.*

She was crying before she noticed, the way rain starts before you hear it, and Sefa put a hand on her shoulder and let her, saying nothing, because some things you cannot be talked through, only accompanied.

"He wasn't a coward," Wren managed finally.

"No," Sefa said. "He was the bravest coward I ever knew. He was afraid of everything, and he did all of it anyway, for sixty years' worth of strangers and one girl he couldn't even say hello to." She folded the letters back into their oilcloth with ceremony, the way you fold a flag. "He came here the last time, four years ago. Older than his age. He stood where you're standing and he asked me to promise that if he died out there, someone would tell you the truth gently." Her old eyes were dry and very bright. "I promised. I'm sorry it's arriving during a siege. Truth keeps poor hours."

Wren laughed — a cracked, wet sound that startled both of them — and wiped her face with a sleeve that smelled of salt and lamp oil. Above them, faint through sixty feet of stone, the picks started again.

* * *

On the ninth day, the water went critical.

Brannic had warned them the vein was small, but veins lie, or the earth does, and the seep in the east gallery thinned to a trickle and then to a dampness, and then to a dark stain that mocked them. The tally board told the story in chalk: eleven days of reserves at half measures, and forty men on the rim with full barrels — their barrels, stolen from the hidden cistern, and Corvane knew it, and was content to wait.

The council met in the cistern hall by lamplight, and for the first time, the argument wasn't about whether to hold. It was about what holding cost.

"We open the gate," said a young Keeper named Dara, whose father had died on the surface runs. "We take the water beneath it. It's right there. It's been right there for sixty years while we drank rainwater and buried our dead of thirst."

"The gate opens outward," Sefa said, "and what's behind it isn't just water. You know what's behind it."

"Then let it all come out!" Dara's voice cracked. "Let the records drown, let the truth drown, let Corvane have his empty vault — at least we'd live to hate him!"

"And the lowlands?" Odile asked quietly. "The towns dying of thirst beyond the flats? If the river releases, it floods — yes — but it feeds. My father's village has been buying Corvane's water at ruinous prices for fifteen years. Open the gate and either we save them or we bury them under a flood. We don't get to choose how it goes."

"That's not a choice," Dara said. "That's a gamble."

"It's the only kind we have left."

Astrid had been silent through all of it, standing with his back to the wall like a man guarding a door that wasn't there. Now he spoke, and his ranger's voice filled the hall the way cold fills a cellar. "There's a third option nobody wants to say out loud, so I'll say it. We don't have the water to run, and we don't have the walls to hide. But Corvane doesn't actually want a massacre. Massacres make inquiries, and inquiries make clerks nervous, and Corvane lives and dies by his clerks." He looked at Odile. "What he wants is the map, the crew, and silence. Which means every day we hold without breaking is a day he has to wonder what we've already sent out of this valley."

"Nothing's been sent out of this valley," Tam said flatly.

"Not yet," Astrid said, and something passed between him and Odile that Wren couldn't read — or could read, and chose not to look at directly, because hope was a rationed substance too.

The council broke up past midnight. Wren stayed behind, sitting on the steps of the antechamber with her father's letters in her lap, listening to the sealed gate hold its long note below her. Love reassembled, she thought, is not the same as love intact. You could piece a bowl back together and it would hold water, but you would always see the lines, and you would always handle it gently, and maybe that was its own kind of devotion — a vessel that knew it had been broken and was grateful, every day, to be holding anything at all.

She was still sitting there when Ilo came down the dry stair at a dead run for the second time in a week, and this time he wasn't bleeding. This time he was laughing, wild and disbelieving, and the words he brought down into the dark rearranged the whole siege in a sentence:

"There's a rider coming up the valley from the north — alone, carrying a white rag on a spear — and she's shouting that she has papers signed by Corvane's own clerk, and—" he stopped to breathe, grinning like a madman, "—and she says the execution order for the prisoner is forged, the diversion's already started, and somebody in this room had better tell her where to put a wagonload of gunpowder."

Wren was on her feet before he finished. Somewhere above, faint and rhythmic through the stone, the picks had stopped — and in their place, far to the south, came the first confused shouts of Corvane's camp waking up to find itself on fire.
