## 16. Tam's Bargain

Tam Voss had spent his whole life learning the shape of lies — how they sat in the mouth, how they weighed in the hands, how a good one felt almost like truth if you carried it long enough. He'd stolen names, faces, histories. He'd worn other men's lives like borrowed coats. But he had never once walked into an enemy's camp and offered himself up as merchandise.

The salt flats at dusk were the color of old bone, and Corvane's camp sat on the rise above the drowned valley like a wound that wouldn't close. Tam could see the cookfires from here, small orange eyes blinking through the haze of dust and woodsmoke. Somewhere down there, men who had followed him across half the territory were sharpening knives with his name on them.

He checked the forged pages one last time. Odile had outdone herself — the seal was perfect, the ink aged with tea and ash, the clerk's hand copied so faithfully it made Tam's skin crawl to look at it. The documents claimed the vault held nothing but tax ledgers from a settlement that had failed forty years ago. Nothing worth a siege. Nothing worth dying for.

"You don't have to do this," Wren said behind him. Her voice was low, roughened by days of dust and siege-water. "There are other ways."

"There aren't," Tam said. "Not anymore. Corvane's got riders on every approach, and Sefa's people can't hold the vault forever. They need time. I'm the only thing cheap enough to buy it."

"That's not true."

"It's the truest thing I've said all year." He tucked the papers inside his coat, against his chest, where the lie would sit closest to his heart. "Besides — I owe more than I can pay. This is how men like me settle accounts."

Wren looked at him for a long moment. In the failing light her face was all angles and shadow, her father's stubbornness carved into every line. "Come back," she said finally. "That's not a request."

Tam smiled, and it hurt his face. "I've never been good at keeping promises. Ask anyone."

"Then be bad at it one more time."

* * *

Walking into Corvane's camp was like walking into the mouth of something that had been waiting for him all along.

The sentries knew him. Of course they did — Tam had spent three months in Corvane's employ before he went rogue, long enough to learn the magistrate's habits, his appetites, his particular way of looking at men like they were arithmetic problems waiting to be solved. The guards took his weapons without ceremony, patted him down with hands that lingered on the coat where the forged papers sat, and marched him between the tents toward the large pavilion at the center, where lamplight spilled gold onto the trampled salt.

Corvane received him seated, which was its own kind of message. The magistrate was older than Tam remembered — thinner, grayer, but the eyes hadn't changed. They were pale and patient and utterly without warmth, the eyes of a man who had never once doubted his own right to take what he wanted.

"Voss." Corvane's voice was soft, almost gentle. That was always worse than shouting. "You've cost me a great deal of money."

"I've brought you something worth more." Tam kept his voice steady, kept his shoulders loose, kept the smile of a man selling rather than begging. "The vault. Its contents. Everything you've been bleeding riders into this valley to find."

"And why would you sell me what you refused me before?"

"Because I've seen what's down there now." Tam let a beat pass, let disappointment curdle visibly across his face. "Tax ledgers. Land surveys. Forty years of dead men's bookkeeping. There's no treasure, Magistrate. No water rights, no deeds, nothing your patrons would cross the street for. The Rivergate story is exactly what it looks like — a prospector's fever dream. I went rogue over nothing."

Corvane studied him the way a butcher studies a carcass. "And you expect me to believe you crawled back to offer me nothing?"

"I expect you to be angry about it," Tam said. "And then I expect you to realize that the ledger pages I'm offering prove the claim is worthless, so you can pull your riders out before winter takes them. I'm saving you money, Magistrate. It's the most honest work I've ever done."

He produced the papers. His hands didn't shake. Twenty years of thieving had taught him that much, at least — the moment of handover was sacred, and trembling ruined everything.

Corvane didn't reach for them. Instead he nodded once, and a thin man stepped out of the shadows behind the pavilion's central pole — a small, precise man with ink-stained fingers and spectacles that caught the lamplight like coins.

Clerk Merrick. Corvane's shadow, his memory, his living archive. The man who had trained Odile, back when Odile still filed things and forgot them on command.

"Merrick," Corvane said mildly. "Look at these."

Merrick took the pages with reverence, holding them up to the lamp. For a long moment there was only the sound of wind against canvas and Tam's own heartbeat, loud as a drum in his ears.

Then Merrick frowned.

It was a small frown. Barely a crease. But Tam had spent his life reading faces, and he saw it the way a sailor sees the first dark line of a storm on the horizon.

"This survey mark," Merrick said slowly, turning the page. "The river gauge notation. It uses the pre-reform format. But the date stamp—" He looked up, and his eyes behind the spectacles were terribly kind, which was the worst thing about him. "The date stamp uses post-reform numbering. Someone has married two eras of bureaucracy, Magistrate. These documents were made by someone who learned the old system secondhand."

The silence that followed was very long.

Tam had prepared for suspicion. He had prepared for anger, for torture, for the slow unraveling of his story under questioning. What he had not prepared for was being undone by typography.

"They're genuine enough," Tam said. "Records get recopied. Systems change—"

"Records get recopied by clerks," Merrick said gently, "who use one system consistently. This was forged by someone clever. Cleverer than most. But cleverness leaves fingerprints too." He set the pages down on Corvane's table, aligning their edges with a small, compulsive precision. "The tea-staining is well done, though. Almost convincing."

Corvane rose. He crossed the pavilion slowly, and when he reached Tam, he did not strike him. He simply stood close, close enough that Tam could smell the sandalwood oil in his beard, and studied him with that patient, bottomless gaze.

"The vault holds something," Corvane said quietly. "Or your friends believe it does. Otherwise you wouldn't have risked your neck carrying me a forgery." He tilted his head. "What is it, Voss? Proof? Records? Something with my predecessors' names in it?"

"I told you the truth—"

"You told me a story." Corvane's voice never rose. It never needed to. "Here is what will happen now. You will tell me the real contents of that vault, or you will die tomorrow at dawn, publicly, painfully, somewhere your friends can watch. And we both know which of us is better at enduring pain."

Tam laughed. He couldn't help it — something in him cracked open, some final chamber of fear flooding through, and what came out was laughter, ragged and real.

"You think I haven't thought about that?" he said. "You think I haven't run the arithmetic? Here's mine: whatever's in that vault, it ends you. Not wounds you — ends you. Your monopoly, your patrons, your name. And every hour I keep silent, your riders sit in the cold eating through supplies they'll need for the ride home. So go ahead. Dawn works fine."

Something flickered across Corvane's face — not anger. Respect, maybe, or its ugly cousin, calculation. He turned away.

"Bind him," he said. "Dawn, then. Let the valley watch."

* * *

They put him in a supply tent with his wrists lashed to a tent pole, under guard of two bored soldiers who played dice by lamplight and ignored him, which suited Tam fine.

He should have been afraid. Some part of him was — a small animal part, huddled in the corner of his chest, counting the hours until dawn. But beneath the fear was something stranger. Lightness.

All his life, guilt had ridden him like a second skeleton. The names he'd stolen. The friend whose identity he'd taken — Joren, dead of fever in a waystation ditch, whose name Tam had worn for six years because a dead man doesn't mind and a living thief needs one. The jobs he'd done for Corvane before he understood what the magistrate's arithmetic added up to. Every lie had been a stone in his pockets, and he'd grown so used to the weight he'd forgotten he was drowning.

Now the stones were gone. Not because he'd been forgiven — no one had forgiven him, least of all himself. But because he'd finally paid something down with the only currency he had left. Skin. Time. Tomorrow morning, the last installment.

He thought about Wren's face when she said *come back*. He thought about Astrid's oath-sworn vouching, the ranger who had staked his own honor on a thief's word. He thought about Brannic's big gentle hands and Odile's ink-stained courage and Sefa's people singing low in the ruins at night, songs in a language the flood hadn't drowned.

He thought about Joren, and whispered an apology into the dark, and for the first time in six years it felt like it landed somewhere.

The dice clicked. The lamp burned low. Outside, the wind moved over the salt flats with a sound like the whole world breathing.

Near midnight, one of the guards shifted, and Tam spoke to the darkness, casual as a card game.

"Your sergeant's skimming the payroll. Has been since Saltmere. Check the muster rolls if you don't believe me."

One guard glanced over. "Why tell us that?"

"Because dead men don't gossip," Tam said, "and I figure someone ought to know the truth before dawn. It's the only thing I've ever been good at — knowing things, and telling them too late."

The guard snorted, but he wrote it down. Everyone wrote things down eventually. That was the whole disease of the world, and Tam Voss had caught it worse than anyone.

He settled back against the pole, wrists burning, and watched the gap in the tent flap where the stars wheeled slowly westward. Dawn was four hours off. Somewhere across the valley, Astrid was watching for his signal — or mourning its absence. Either way, the ruse had bought what it needed to buy: a day, maybe two, while Corvane chewed on the question of whether the vault was empty.

It wasn't empty. It was full of the truth, and the truth was a flood waiting for a gate.

Tam closed his eyes. If this was the price, he'd pay it standing — or hanging, as the case might be. But gods, he hoped someone would tell Wren he'd gone out grinning.

He was still smiling when the tent flap moved, and a shadow slipped inside — a slim silhouette with ink-stained fingers and a knife already drawn.

Odile.

"Don't make a sound," she breathed, crouching to saw at his bonds. "We have until the watch changes. And Tam — the forgery failing was the best thing that could have happened. Corvane believes the vault holds something worth killing for now. Which means he's going to storm it at first light."

The bonds parted. Tam rubbed his raw wrists and found, to his surprise, that he was still smiling.

"Then we'd better move faster than dawn," he said.
