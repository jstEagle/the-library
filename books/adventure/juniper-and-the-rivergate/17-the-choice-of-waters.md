## 17. The Choice of Waters

Astrid found the mechanism by accident, which was how he found most things that mattered.

He had gone down alone before dawn, while the Keepers slept in their salt-hollowed chambers and the siege above ground ground on like a toothache. Nine days Corvane's riders had sat in the valley mouth, nine days of thin rations and thinner hope, and the vault below the drowned town held its breath around them all. Astrid had told himself he was only walking the flooded galleries to clear his head, lantern held high over black water, boots finding the old cobblestones by feel. But some part of him — the ranger part, the part that could not leave a trail unfollowed — had been circling the gate since the day Sefa first showed it to them.

It stood at the end of the lowest gallery, where the town's founders had cut their cisterns into the living rock: a wheel of green bronze taller than a man, crusted white with salt, set into a wall of fitted stone that predated every map Odile had ever filed. Juniper's marks were scratched beside it still — the prospector's shorthand, a triangle and a slash, twenty years old and sharp as the day he'd cut them.

Astrid had looked at that wheel a dozen times and seen only a door.

Tonight, with the lantern low and the water still as poured glass, he saw what the door was for.

The gallery was not a gallery. He understood it all at once, the way you understand a lie you've been telling yourself — completely, and all at the wrong time. The flooded street he stood in was a channel. The cisterns were not storage; they were *ballast*, chambers built to be filled and emptied in sequence, and the great wheel did not open a vault. The vault had been opened to them days ago, its records laid out under lamplight while Odile copied pages until her fingers bled. The wheel opened something else. The wheel stood at the head of a staircase of stone that descended into darkness below even the drowned streets, and when Astrid held his lantern over the edge he saw water moving down there. Not standing. Moving. A slow, muscular turning, like the shoulder of something enormous rolling over in its sleep.

The river. The actual river, the one the maps called dry, the one that had fed three towns before the founders of this one diverted it and drowned their own streets to hide the crime. It was still there. It had been there all along, running in the dark beneath the salt, patient as a debt.

And the wheel, if turned, would let it out.

Astrid crouched at the rim and did the arithmetic the way he'd been taught at the ranger academy, back when he'd believed the desert rewarded honesty. He could feel the fall of the land in his knees — he'd crossed the flats enough times to read grade through his boots. Below this valley, days to the south and east, lay the lowlands. Corvane's patrons' country: the walled farms, the green circles visible from the ridge lines at dusk, the only green for two hundred miles, green because they alone drank from the diverted water. If the gate opened, the river would take its old course. It would run through the lowlands whether the lowlands wanted it or not. It would drown the cistern farms, the aqueduct heads, the terraced fields that fed the magistrate's monopoly — and it would keep running, out past the walls, into the drought-stricken towns that had been drinking rationed brine while Corvane's masters watered orchards.

It would also drown everything here. The vault. The records. Twenty years of Juniper's guilt and a century of the Keepers' vigil and the proof, in ledgers and testimony and a dead town's own hand, that the men who built the lowlands had built them on a massacre. All of it, under water, forever.

You could not have the river and the truth. The stone did not care about justice. Water did not read.

Astrid sat down on the wet cobblestones and did not touch the wheel.

* * *

He was still there when the light changed, which was how Wren found him — though he suspected she'd been looking for a while. She came down the gallery with a lamp of her own and her sleeves pushed up, the way she always did, as if she were about to work a pump even standing still. She stopped when she saw his face.

"You've been down here all night."

"I found something."

"I can see that." She set her lamp beside his and crouched, following his gaze to the wheel, to the moving dark below. He watched her read it the way he had — the channel, the ballast, the turning water — and watched the color leave her face in stages, like a tide going out.

"It's not a vault door," she said.

"No."

"It's a spillway."

"Yes."

She was quiet for a long time. Down in the deep, the river turned and turned. Somewhere above them, muffled by forty feet of salt and stone, a rider's horn sounded the hour, thin as an insect.

"Odile's copied everything that matters," Astrid said. It came out flat, and he hated the flatness, because it sounded like an argument already won, and nothing about this was won. "The diversion orders. The massacre ledger. Corvane's family seal on the original writ. She says it's enough to hang him in any honest court."

"There are no honest courts in the lowlands," Wren said. "There are courts Corvane's patrons own, and courts too far away to matter. You know that. I know that. Odile knows it better than anyone — she *filed* for those courts for eleven years." She wrapped her arms around her knees. "Paper doesn't drown a monopoly, Astrid. Water does."

"Paper doesn't drown a town either." He heard his own voice crack on it. "Sefa's people. This is their whole history down here. Their grandmothers' houses. The graves. You flood it, and Corvane's story wins by default — *there was never a town, there was never a crime, look, there's only water.* That's how he'd tell it. That's how his patrons have told it for a hundred years."

"And if we don't open it?" Wren's voice rose, and the gallery took it up, gave it back doubled off the wet stone. "Saltmere gets another summer of ration brine. The well-divers keep dying at the bottom of holes for a cup a day. Brannic's own sister is out there somewhere drinking dust. You've seen the lowland towns, Astrid. You've walked them. You know what another year of Corvane costs in bones." She stopped. Breathed. When she spoke again it was barely above the sound of the water. "My father walked these galleries. He stood where you're standing. And he ran. Twenty years, I thought he ran because he was a coward. Now I think he stood right here and couldn't turn the wheel, and couldn't walk away, and so he ran in a circle for two decades, carrying a map he couldn't give anyone."

"Maybe he was right not to turn it."

"Maybe he was a coward after all." She said it like she was spitting blood. "I don't know. That's the hell of it, Astrid. I don't know, and he's not here to ask, and you're asking me to be sure about a thing my father couldn't be sure about in twenty years."

"I'm not asking you to be sure." Astrid got to his feet. His knees ached; the cold of the stone had gotten into them during the night without his noticing. "I'm telling you I can't do this alone."

That stopped her. He saw it land — saw the ranger's oath-keeper, the man who'd crossed nine days of empty flat on the trail of a stolen name because he could not abide an unfinished thing, admit the unfinished thing was bigger than him. Wren looked up at him, and for a moment the hardness in her face was just grief wearing a different coat, and then it was just grief.

"Who else is there?" she said. "Tam's in chains waiting for dawn. Odile would tell you to turn it and then spend the rest of her life filing the paperwork for the drowned. Brannic —" she almost laughed, wet-eyed — "Brannic would weep with joy. He'd turn it himself if we let him. He says the river's been calling him for a week. And Sefa —" Wren shook her head. "Sefa swore her people to silence. She'd never let it open. But her people are forty old souls in a salt hole, Astrid. If we ask her to choose between the graves and the living, we're asking her to bury her town twice."

"Then we don't ask her to choose."

"Then what do we do?"

Astrid looked at the wheel. Green bronze, salt-crusted, patient. Juniper's mark beside it like a signature on a contract twenty years overdue. He thought about the promise he'd made a dying man at a waystation — *carry it to her, say nothing else* — and how every promise since had grown out of that one like roots out of a stone, and how all of them together still didn't tell him which way to turn.

"I don't know yet," he said. "But nobody turns this wheel alone. Not me. Not you. Not Corvane, if I can help it. Whatever we decide, we decide it together, and we live with it together, and we tell it together after. That's the only thing I'm sure of. That's the whole of what I've got."

Wren stood. For a moment she put her hand flat against the bronze, the way you'd touch a door you weren't ready to knock on. The river turned beneath them, patient as a debt, patient as grief.

"Dawn's coming," she said. "And Tam runs out of time at dawn."

* * *

They climbed back up through the drowned streets as the first grey light came down the well-shafts, and the weight came up with them, settled across Astrid's shoulders like a pack he'd be carrying the rest of his life. Above, in the Keepers' hall, the others were already awake. He could hear Odile's voice, low and fast, and Brannic's rumble answering, and he knew before he reached the top of the stair that the question had gotten out of him somehow — that his night at the wheel had shown on his face, or that Wren had gone ahead, or that the truth under the salt simply leaked upward the way all truths did, given time.

Sefa stood at the head of the stair, waiting. Old, straight-backed, eyes like chips of the salt itself. She looked at Astrid, and then past him, down into the dark where the water moved.

"You found the spillway," she said. It was not a question.

"You knew it was there."

"I have known it was there since I was six years old, when my grandmother brought me down and showed me the wheel and told me: *one day, someone will stand there and have to choose, and we will have to live with what they choose.*" Sefa's voice was dry as the flats. "She was wrong about one thing, ranger. She thought it would be one of us. The Keepers were meant to choose. Instead the choice has walked into our valley wearing a ranger's coat and carrying my grandfather's river in his hands."

"Then help me carry it," Astrid said. "I won't turn it alone. I won't leave it alone either. But I'm not deciding the fate of your dead and the lowlands' living in one night with a lantern and a bad conscience."

Sefa studied him for a long moment. Behind her, dawn was coming up the valley mouth, and with it, faintly, the sound of Corvane's camp waking — hammers, hooves, the ring of a whetstone on steel. Dawn. Tam's dawn.

"No," Sefa said at last. "You won't decide it alone. But you'll have to decide it soon. Because my scouts say Corvane's brought up the big drills in the night. He means to come through the gate wall within two days — and when he does, ranger, the choice stops being yours."

She turned toward the sound of the hammers, and the light of the rising sun caught the salt crusting the ruined town, and far below them all, in the dark, the river went on turning, patient as a debt, waiting to be either a grave or a deliverance — and Astrid understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water, that whatever he chose, he would be choosing it for everyone who had ever drunk, and everyone who ever would.

And dawn was already breaking over the valley, and Tam Voss had one hour left to live.
