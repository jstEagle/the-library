## 18. Loyalty vs. Truth

The vault's lamplight turned everyone the color of old paper.

Wren stood among the shelves of the drowned town's memory — ledgers bound in oilcloth, survey charts rolled tight as fists, a child's arithmetic primer with its corners gone soft from damp that had never quite reached it — and listened to her crew come apart at the seams.

It had started quietly, the way most disasters do. Odile had been reading aloud from a diversion order dated sixty-one years back, her clerk's voice flat and precise, translating bureaucratic cruelty into plain speech: *by authority of the Office of Water Rights, the channel of the Alder River is to be closed at the structure known locally as the Rivergate, effective immediately, compensation to affected parties to be determined.* Compensation had never been determined. The affected parties were under forty feet of salt-crusted stone.

Then Brannic had said, in the voice of a man setting down something heavy: "There's enough water behind that gate to green the whole lowlands. Enough for Saltmere. Enough for every pump-town between here and the Verge." His hands, always moving when he spoke, went still against his knees. "And we're standing here reading."

"We are standing here *witnessing*," Odile corrected, and there was an edge on the word that hadn't been there before. "If we open that gate, we drown all of this. Every page. Every name. The proof that Corvane's office murdered a town dies with the flood, and he walks free, and in ten years his successors do it again somewhere else because no one ever wrote down what happened here."

"And if we don't open it," Brannic said, "the people who are dying of thirst right now — not sixty-one years ago, *right now*, today, this season — keep dying while we wait for a court to read paper." He looked around the lamplit circle. "You ever watched a well go brackish? I have. You ever carried water forty miles because the pumps ran dry? I have. Paper doesn't fill a cup."

"Paper hanged men at Caster's Ford," Odile said. "Paper put Corvane's own predecessor in irons when the grain monopoly broke. It can be done. It has been done. But only if the record survives."

Wren said nothing. She had been saying nothing for a long time, and she could feel the silence becoming its own kind of argument, the kind people start speaking over.

Tam sat apart on an overturned crate, arms wrapped around his shins, watching the argument like a man watching weather roll in. He'd been quiet since dawn — quieter than usual, which for Tam was saying something — and when Brannic appealed to him directly, "You've seen what thirst does, Voss, you grew up out past the markers," Tam only shrugged and said, "I've also seen what happens to towns that get famous. Ask me how I know." And wouldn't say more.

Astrid was at the far end of the vault, near the gate mechanism itself, one hand resting on the great iron wheel as if he could feel a pulse through it. He'd been touching it on and off since they'd descended, the way you touch a horse's neck to calm it, or yourself. When the argument crested again — Odile citing statutes, Brannic citing bodies, their voices climbing the shelves like water rising — Astrid finally turned.

"Wren."

Just her name. The room went still with the particular obedience of exhausted people handed a reason to stop talking.

"You knew him best," Astrid said. "What would Juniper want?"

*There it is*, Wren thought. *The question I've been carrying since the claim shack.*

She thought about the letters. Twenty years of them, unsent, stacked in a tin box beneath a floorboard in a shack that smelled of rust and juniper smoke. She had read them twice through by lamplight while the others slept, and then a third time slowly, the way you drink the last water in a skin. In them her father was neither the abandoning ghost of her anger nor the martyr of Sefa's stories. He was a man writing to a daughter he didn't dare face, explaining and re-explaining, circling the same wound: *I saw the cisterns, Wren-girl. I saw the names in the ledger. If I stay, they will make me part of the silence, or they will end me, and either way you lose a father. So I am going to be lost instead. It is the only shape of leaving that keeps you safe.*

But nowhere — she had checked, going back through them with her finger under each line, twice — nowhere did he say what should be done about it. Not once. A man who had spent twenty years composing arguments had left the verdict blank.

"I don't know," she said, and heard how it landed, how Brannic's shoulders dropped and Odile's chin lifted, each of them hearing in her uncertainty the victory of their opponent. "But I know where he kept his maps."

* * *

The map was where she'd left it, wrapped in oilcloth in her pack, and she unrolled it on a crate between the two factions like a woman laying out cards. The margins were dense with her father's hand — and hers, the small round letters of a nine-year-old helping hold the chain and copy down numbers, *W.W. was here* scratched in one corner beside a drawing of a bird that might have been a wren or might have been nothing.

"Look at the gate chamber," she said, and pointed.

Her father had drawn it in section, cutaway, the way prospectors draw things they intend to dig. The wheel mechanism. The counterweights. And below the wheel, in ink darker than the rest, added later — she could tell by the pen stroke, heavier, more deliberate — a set of marks that weren't measurements at all. They were words, abbreviated almost to nothing, in the shorthand surveyors used for caveats: *UNRESOLVED. SEE BOTH. DECIDE.*

"He annotated everything," Odile murmured, leaning close. "Every chart I ever filed from him came back covered in notes. He couldn't leave a line alone."

"Except this one," Wren said. "He didn't resolve it. He drew the question into the map itself. *See both. Decide.*" She looked up. "He wasn't hiding the answer. He was leaving the question to whoever came after. He trusted us with the not-knowing."

"That's a coward's trick," Brannic said, but without heat; it sounded more like grief wearing accusation's clothes. "Twenty years running and he leaves the hard part to strangers?"

"To his daughter," Wren said. "To whoever his daughter brought with her." She let that sit. "Maybe it took him twenty years to understand that he shouldn't be the one to answer. Maybe that's not cowardice. Maybe that's the hardest thing a man who knows too much can do — refuse to make his knowledge a weight on other people's necks."

Odile had gone very still, the way she did when a document told her something its writer hadn't intended. "Or," she said softly, "he understood exactly what we're only now learning. That this choice can't survive being made alone. Look at us. Two days in this valley and we're already split down the middle. If Juniper had written *open it* or *keep it sealed*, half of us would have obeyed and half of us would have hated him, and whichever way it went, the ones who lost would have spent the rest of their lives believing they'd been betrayed." She tapped the margin note. "*Decide.* Plural. He meant it to be argued over. He meant it to cost."

"It's costing," Tam said from his crate. First words in hours. Everyone looked at him. He picked at a fraying seam on his sleeve. "You want to know what I think? I think both of you are right, and that's the trap. The water saves lives now. The record saves lives later. And Corvane is coming regardless, so whatever you choose, you'd best choose fast." He glanced up, and for once there was no performance in his face, just the flat honesty of a man too tired to lie. "I stole a name once because someone needed one more than I did. This is bigger than a name. But it's the same shape. Somebody's got to decide who needs it more."

* * *

They climbed out of the vault as dusk came down over the drowned valley, and the Keepers' settlement glowed against the darkening salt like embers banked for the night — low fires, low voices, low buildings built to be overlooked. Sefa met them at the edge of the terraces, as she seemed to meet everything, upright and unhurried, her hands folded in front of her like a woman waiting out a long sermon.

"Well?" she said. "Have you decided what my town died for?"

The bluntness of it knocked the wind out of the argument. Brannic opened his mouth; Odile caught his arm.

"Not yet," Wren said.

Sefa studied her for a long moment. Around them, Keeper children were hauling water jars up from the deep cisterns, small figures bent under loads sized for adults, and Wren watched Sefa watch them, and understood that every hour of indecision was an hour the old woman had been paying for sixty-one years.

"Do you know what we are, girl?" Sefa said. "We are the answer nobody wanted. Our grandparents drowned so that a handful of families could own rain. We survived by agreeing to be forgotten. Every Keeper alive took a vow of silence before they could talk — I took it at seven years old, my grandmother's hand on my head, *you will not speak of the water, you will not speak of the town, you will not speak of the names.*" Her voice didn't rise; it compressed, like stone under pressure. "I have kept that vow for seventy years. And now you stand in our valley holding the proof of everything we buried, and you ask for time."

"I'm asking for one night," Wren said.

"One night." Sefa almost smiled. "I'll give you until the second watch. After that, if you haven't chosen, my people will seal the lower ways and you can argue on the surface until Corvane arrives, which won't be long. My scouts say his riders made the north rim at midday."

The word moved through the group like cold air through a cracked door. Astrid's hand went to the compass at his belt — an old habit, Wren had noticed, a thing he touched the way other men touched talismans — and Tam stood up off his crate without appearing to decide to.

"One night," Sefa repeated. "Spend it well."

* * *

Wren spent it at the mouth of the vault, sitting on the steps with the letters in her lap, listening to the mountain breathe.

That was the thing about the Rivergate that no one had warned her about: it breathed. Brannic had felt it first, down in the flooded underlevels — the pressure of the water behind the gate coming through the stone like a pulse, slow and immense, the heartbeat of a river held prisoner for sixty-one years. Now, sitting close to the sealed door, she could feel it faintly through the rock under her palms. *Thum. Thum.* Patient. Enormous. Indifferent to statutes and to thirst alike.

Behind her, footsteps on the stairs. She didn't turn; she knew the weight of them. Astrid settled onto the step beside her, close enough to share warmth, far enough to leave her the silence if she wanted it. He'd gotten good at that, these last weeks. Good at the geometry of a grieving person's space.

"My mother used to say," he offered eventually, "that a promise is just a debt you take on willingly. And that the worst debts are the ones where you promised before you understood the price."

"She sounds like she'd have liked my father."

"She'd have called him a fool and fed him anyway." A pause. The gate pulsed beneath them. "For what it's worth — I don't think there's a right answer down there. I think there's a right *way* to answer. Odile's right that truth dies in water. Brannic's right that people die waiting for truth. Both of those are true at once, and the desert doesn't care which one you pick."

"That's not comforting, Astrid."

"I'm not trying to comfort you. I'm trying to tell you you're not wrong for not knowing." He turned the compass over in his fingers, not opening it. "When I was tracking Tam across the flats, nine days, I kept telling myself it was duty. It wasn't. It was that I couldn't stand an unfinished case. An unanswered question itched in me like sand in a boot. And then I found him, and the answer was — nothing. He stole a name to give away. There was no closing it. There was only deciding what to do next." He finally looked at her. "I think your father understood something I'm only learning. Some questions aren't meant to be closed. They're meant to be carried."

"*See both,*" Wren quoted. "*Decide.*"

"Plural," Astrid said. "He wrote it plural."

She looked down at the letters in her lap, at the topmost page, the last one he'd written, the ink faded to the brown of dried blood. She had read it four times now and each time it ended the same way, mid-thought, almost mid-sentence, as though he'd been interrupted — or as though he'd stopped on purpose:

*And so, Wren-girl, if you are reading this, then the map found you, which means I trusted you with the one thing I could never finish. Don't look for my answer in these pages. I checked. It isn't there. What's there is everything you need to make your own —*

And nothing after. For twenty years she'd assumed the letter was damaged, or that he'd died writing it, or simply failed. Sitting on the breathing stone with the lamplight trembling and Astrid quiet beside her, she suddenly saw it differently. The sentence didn't trail off. It *stopped*. Clean break, clean margin, the pen lifted with intention. Her father, who annotated everything, who could not leave a line alone, had left this one deliberately broken — the way you leave a door unlocked for someone you're expecting.

"He finished it," she whispered.

"What?"

"The letter. It's not unfinished. It ends where he wanted it to end. *Everything you need to make your own—*" She pressed her palm flat against the vault door, against the pulse coming through the iron. "He left the sentence open because the sentence was the point. He wasn't giving us an answer. He was giving us the *making*. The choosing. Us, specifically — not a magistrate, not a court, not the Keepers' vow. Whoever followed the map."

Astrid was silent a moment. Then: "So what do we make?"

Above them, faint through the layers of stone and salt, came a sound that didn't belong to the mountain's breathing: hoofbeats, many, on the north rim. Or perhaps only the wind. Perhaps only the wind.

Wren stood, and folded the letters away, and felt the question settle into her bones not as a burden but as an inheritance — the first true thing her father had ever given her.

"Get Odile," she said. "Get Brannic. Get everyone. We decide together, tonight, before the second watch." She looked up toward the sound on the rim, and her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "Because I don't think Corvane's riders care which answer we pick. And I think we're about to run out of night."
