## 20. Opening the Rivergate

The water came for them before Corvane did — or rather, the sound of it did.

Astrid heard it through the stone first: a low percussion beneath the vault floor, like a heart buried in the mountain's chest, beating faster now that the seals were broken. Brannic had felt it for days, kneeling on the flagstones with his palms flat, his face gone strange and young. *She's awake,* he kept saying. *She knows we're here.* Astrid had no gift for wells, but even he could feel it now, a tremor in the soles of his boots, in the roots of his teeth, as if the whole drowned town were holding its breath.

He stood at the vault door with Wren's copied pages wrapped in oilcloth against his ribs and listened to the riders come down the valley.

They came the way Corvane did everything — in order, with paperwork. Astrid watched from the ruined bell tower of the submerged chapel, where the salt crust had eaten the bell to lacework, and counted lanterns strung along the causeway road: thirty, maybe forty men, torchlight doubled in the shallow floodwater that still pooled across the old plaza. Behind them, mules carried pumps and blasting powder. Ahead of them, riding a gray horse too fine for the terrain, came Magistrate Corvane himself, in a dust cloak over magistrate's white, looking less like an invader than an inspector arriving late to his own appointment.

"He brought the whole ledger of his sins," Odile said beside him. She'd stopped flinching at her own jokes two days ago, which Astrid took as either progress or surrender.

"He brought enough guns to not need the ledger," Astrid said.

Below, in the vault antechamber, Sefa's Keepers were moving with terrible calm. Old women and young men, children carrying lamp-oil and the town's remaining stores down into the deep galleries. They had lived under this valley for three generations knowing what slept beneath them, and they moved now the way people move in a house they have always expected to burn. Sefa herself stood at the vault door, small and straight as a fence post, watching the torches descend the switchback road.

"They'll breach within the hour," she said when Astrid came down. "The outer doors are salt-eaten. Powder will do the rest."

"Then we're out of time." He turned to Wren, who sat cross-legged on the vault steps with the last bundle of copies, checking pages by lamplight. Her hands — scarred from twenty years of pump-work — moved over the paper with a tenderness he'd only ever seen her give her father's letters. "Is it done?"

"It's done." She tied the oilcloth with a cord, pulled the knot tight, tested it. "Every page that matters. The diversion orders. The survey that proved they knew the town was inhabited. Corvane's grandfather's signature on the cistern seals. Three sets — one for Saltmere, one for the lowland towns, one for whoever's left standing if the other two don't make it."

"And the originals?"

Wren looked at him. In the lamplight her eyes were her father's eyes, and that had stopped hurting somewhere on the flats and become simply true, the way the north is true.

"The originals stay with the town," she said. "Sefa's people buried their dead under those records. We don't carry their grief out of the valley like loot."

* * *

Corvane breached the outer doors forty minutes after midnight.

The explosion rolled through the ruins like thunder learning to walk. Dust sifted from ceilings that hadn't seen sky in sixty years; somewhere below, Brannic swore with the fluency of a man who had spent his life listening to stone and hated hearing it hurt. Then boots — many boots — splashing through the flooded corridors, lantern-light swinging wild across walls furred with salt bloom, and voices calling orders in the clipped cadence of men who had been promised this would be easy.

Astrid met them in the great hall of the archive, because there was no sense pretending otherwise and every reason to buy minutes.

He stood alone in the center of the chamber, between shelves that rose into darkness, holding a lantern, and let them find him. Ten riders filed in behind Corvane, then ten more, rifles slung but hands near. The magistrate walked past all of them, unhurried, and stopped six feet away, and looked around the vault with an expression Astrid would remember the rest of his life: not greed, exactly. Recognition. A man coming home to something he had been told about since childhood, the way other men are told about God.

"There it is," Corvane said softly. "Sixty years my family paid for the upkeep of this valley's silence. Do you know what that costs, ranger? Every year. Every single year. And here it all is, and it cost me one thief and one washed-up lawman." He smiled. It was almost warm. "Where are the others?"

"Gone."

"No one is gone. This valley has one road." He unbuttoned his gloves, finger by finger, a man settling in. "Let me save us both the theater. I've read your file, Oyelaran. Desert ranger, nine years, no family, no property, no debts worth the name. You're the last honest man in the territory, which is why you're standing here instead of running. So hear me: amnesty. Full pardon for yourself and everyone under your charter — the clerk, the well-diver, the thief. The girl too, though she's committed nothing but grief. Walk away tonight and in the morning none of you exist in any record I control. Which is all of them."

"You'll take the records," Astrid said. Not a question.

"I'll *preserve* the records." Corvane drifted along the shelves, trailing one gloved finger through the dust, reading spines. "That's the difference between me and you idealists. I don't want to burn any of this. Burn it, and the water rights get contested in open court, and some other family buys the monopoly, and nothing changes except the name on the seal. I want it *kept*. Catalogued. Sealed again, properly, under my authority. The truth is safest in the hands of the man with the most to lose from it." He turned. "That's not cynicism, ranger. That's stewardship."

Behind him, one of the riders shifted, and Astrid saw the man's face in the lantern light — young, sunburned, uncertain — and understood that Corvane's soldiers were not monsters. They were men who had been told a story about order, and order sounded so much like justice that most people never learned the difference.

"You know what's in these shelves," Astrid said. "You've known since you were a boy. Your grandfather drowned a town."

"My grandfather *secured* a territory through a difficult decade." Corvane's voice didn't rise, but something behind it did, a hairline crack in porcelain. "Four thousand people drink because of what happened here. Would you trade four thousand living mouths for eight hundred dead ones? Because that's the arithmetic, and I do the arithmetic, and you—" he pointed one bare hand at Astrid, and for the first time his composure showed its seams— "you do *sentiment*."

"That's the thing about arithmetic," said a voice from the gallery above.

Everyone looked up. Wren Hale stood on the iron balcony with a lantern in one hand and the gate wheel's chain in the other, and she was smiling, and Astrid's stomach dropped through the floor.

"You never count the water itself," she said.

* * *

Later — much later, when he had time to be angry, when he had time to be grateful, when he had years to turn over the moment and never once resolve whether he'd have been brave enough to do it himself — Astrid would reconstruct it piece by piece.

Wren had not waited for his plan. She had taken the copy bundles down to Brannic and Odile at the river-door while he stood talking, because she had read her father's letters until she knew them better than he did, and Juniper Hale had written, in the last unsent one, a sentence she'd recited to Astrid on the third night of the siege: *The gate doesn't keep the water in. It keeps the lie in. Open it and the lie drowns first.*

She had climbed the gallery ladder while Corvane monologued, because Corvane always monologued — Odile had said so, weeks ago, filing him in absentia among the men who loved the sound of their own legitimacy — and she had found the chain.

So when Corvane shouted *stop*, and ten rifles came up, and Astrid stepped sideways into the line of fire without deciding to, what he saw was Wren's hands closing on the wheel-chain, and what he heard was her voice, steady as a pump-stroke:

"Wren Hale, daughter of Juniper Hale, witness for the drowned town of Cael Ford. Everything in this vault is copied. Everything that matters left this valley an hour ago in three bundles, carried by three people, going three directions. Kill us and you kill witnesses to your own grandfather's signature." She hauled the chain slack through her fists. "Open the gate yourself, Magistrate. Or I will."

"Don't." For the first time, Corvane's voice cracked clean through. He took a step forward, and it was not a magistrate's step; it was a man's, sudden and afraid. "Girl — Wren — listen to me. The release channels haven't been maintained in sixty years. The surge will take the lowlands before it takes the farms. You'll kill more people than my family ever—"

"You've had sixty years to maintain them," Sefa said, emerging from the dark of the side gallery with her people behind her, silent as tide. "You chose the farms."

Corvane turned to his riders. "Shoot the chain."

And here was the thing Astrid would carry home like a stone in his boot: not one of them raised a rifle. The young sunburned one actually lowered his. They had ridden down the valley behind warrants and lanterns, but they had spent five days camped outside a drowned town, drinking water hauled from a drought-rationed cistern while their magistrate drank from private stock, and somewhere in those five days the story Corvane told had quietly died in them, the way stories die — not with argument but with thirst.

"Shoot the *chain*," Corvane said again, and his voice was alone in the vault, and he knew it.

Wren looked down at Astrid. Not for permission — she had never once asked his permission — but the way you look at someone across a decision you're making together, so that afterward neither of you carries it alone.

"Together," Astrid said.

They turned the wheel together, her hands and his, the chain running through the ancient pulleys with a groan like a waking animal, and for one long second nothing happened at all, and Astrid thought: *it's rusted shut, sixty years, of course it's rusted shut, we'll die in this room for a broken machine—*

Then the mountain exhaled.

* * *

There was no language Astrid knew for what came next, and he had made part of his living on words.

The inner seals went all at once — not an explosion but a *release*, a sound like the sky tearing along a seam — and the Rivergate opened, and the river remembered which way was down.

It came through the vault in a brown-gold wall taller than a horse, and it hit the flooded plaza below and rose again, and the whole drowned town lit up with the roar. Shelves went over. Lanterns died. Astrid was lifted off his feet and slammed against a shelf-column and held there, pinned by water that moved like a living thing, and through the roar he heard the archive — sixty years of sealed lies, diversion orders and death lists and the neat clerical handwriting of respectable men — dissolving into pulp, ink blooming off the pages in black clouds that the river took south like smoke.

*Good,* he thought, absurdly, drowning-adjacent, clinging to iron. *Take it. Take all of it.*

The water found the old channels the founders had cut and abandoned, and the channels held — mostly. Brannic had spent the siege up on the rim with Sefa's engineers, clearing the surge-cut of sixty years of salt and stone, gambling everything on the theory that the river wanted to go home if anyone would let it. The theory held. Mostly. The flood took the valley floor, took the causeway, took Corvane's advance camp with its mules and pumps and powder stores and swept the wreckage out onto the flats in a brown fan visible, Odile would later swear, from the Saltmere towers. It took the lower galleries of the town, the ones already empty, already grieved. It did not take the high road, where the Keepers stood with lamps, singing — actually singing, low and wordless, a sound Astrid felt in his sternum more than heard.

And it did not take Corvane.

Astrid found him at dawn, or what passed for dawn under the storm-light, clinging to the upper balcony of the archive with his fine gray coat torn and his white cuffs gray with silt, alive because the water had, with a kind of contemptuous precision, declined to bother with him. His riders had scattered to the rim. His monopoly lay under twelve feet of new river. His family's sixty-year secret was mud.

He looked at Astrid across the ruined hall, and there was nothing left in his face to perform with.

"You've killed the territory," he said hoarsely. "When the courts sort this out—"

"The courts," Astrid said, "are going to be very busy reading."

He held up the oilcloth bundle — Wren's third copy, the one for whoever was left standing — and let Corvane see it, and watch it go into his coat, and understand that it was already beyond his reach.

Then the water rose another foot, gently, like a tide closing a ledger, and Astrid climbed toward the light.

* * *

They stood on the rim at sunrise, all of them — the crew, the Keepers, the soaked and shivering riders who had chosen not to shoot — and watched the river run.

It ran down the old channel toward the lowlands, wide and brown and loud, and the drought country received it the way cracked earth receives rain: greedily, audibly, with a sound like the ground itself drinking. Somewhere down there were towns that had been rationing spoonfuls. Somewhere down there, by tomorrow or the day after, children would stand at a dry wash and watch water arrive, and none of them would know the names of the people on this ridge who had opened it for them.

Except that they would. That was the point. That was the whole point.

Wren stood next to Astrid with her father's last letter in her hand, unread, saved. Her sleeves were soaked to the shoulder. There was silt in her hair and she was laughing, or crying, or both, in the way of people who have just set down something enormous.

"He knew," she said. "All those years walking the flats, letting them chase him — he wasn't running *from* it. He was keeping the road warm." She wiped her face with a wet sleeve. "He left the choice unfinished because he knew he couldn't trust himself to make it. And he trusted us to."

Astrid looked out at the water going south, and thought about the case file waiting in Saltmere — *theft of one name, value negligible* — and about how he would write it up, and how no report in the territory's history would be less adequate to what had actually happened.

"We should get ahead of the flood," he said. "Saltmere will want to see it coming."

They were halfway down the ridge trail when Tam Voss came pelting after them, hatless, breathless, grinning like a man reprieved — and behind him, limping up from the wreckage of the camp with two surviving clerks and a warrant that was now the most dangerous piece of paper in the territory, came Odile, holding it aloft against the sunrise.

"You'll want to be in Saltmere for this," she called. "Corvane's own registrars just signed his arrest. And Astrid—" she caught up, breathless, triumphant, terrified— "they're asking for the woman who read the charges. They're asking for *us*."

Below them, faint but unmistakable, the first bells of the lowland towns began to ring.
