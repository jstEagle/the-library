## 1. The Smiling Men

The rain had been falling on Meridian City for nine days, and Yara Iversen had spent all of them in the same diner booth, watching the same man smile at her from across the table.

His name was Dane Okafor, though he'd introduced himself as *Dane, please, Mr. Okafor is my father's name*, and he was drinking tea he never touched. The cup sat before him going cold, a little island of steam that had long since stopped steaming. He wore a gray suit the color of the sky outside and a tie the color of nothing at all, and his smile was the kind you'd trust with your luggage.

"Nine days of rain," he said, as if they were old friends catching up on weather. "You must be itching to get out there. I've read your file, Ms. Iversen. You're very good in bad weather."

"I'm good in weather," Yara said. "Bad or otherwise. It's the paperwork I'm bad at."

"Ah." The smile widened by a millimeter, no more. It was like watching a door open onto a room you weren't sure you wanted to enter. "Yes. The paperwork."

The paperwork. Fourteen months of it, stacked in a shoebox under her rig's passenger seat: the loan against the truck, the second loan against the instruments, the consolidation that wasn't, the deferment that expired the same week her mother's medical bills came due, the interest recalculated at rates she'd needed a calculator and two aspirin to verify. The Meridian Trust had made it all so easy. Their offices smelled like cedar and fresh coffee. Everyone who worked there smiled. She'd thought, once, that this was what kindness looked like when it had money behind it.

Now she knew it was what patience looked like.

"The Trust isn't unreasonable," Okafor said. He turned his teacup a quarter-turn, aligning the handle precisely with the table edge. "We don't want your truck, Ms. Iversen. Honestly. A storm-chasing rig is a specialized asset; liquidating it would take months, and we'd both lose money on the transaction. What we want—" he spread his hands, palms up, an open book, "—is to be paid back. That's all anyone wants."

"And if I can't?"

"Then we take the collateral. It's in clause eleven. You initialed it." He said it gently, the way a doctor says *it's malignant*. "I'm not here to threaten you. I'm here because the Trust believes in second chances. We believe people are more than their debts." He leaned forward, and for the first time his eyes did something other than smile—they went still and attentive, like a bird watching a worm. "Which brings me to why I asked to meet."

Outside, the rain came down in ropes. A delivery truck hissed past, throwing up a fan of gutter-water. In the booth behind them, two old men argued cheerfully about baseball. The ordinary world, going on. Yara felt it receding from her like a shoreline.

"There's a contract," Okafor said. "Salvage work. Government-adjacent. A survey team went into the Kestrel Range eight months ago—six people, hydrology and geothermal instrumentation—and stopped transmitting. The agency that hired them has gone quiet, which happens, budgets being what they are. But their equipment is still up there. Tens of thousands of dollars of it. Seismic arrays, water gauges, core samplers, a full comms suite." He slid a folder across the table. It was heavy. "Recover what you can carry out. Document the site. Bring back anything that looks like records—field notes, data drives, logs. Standard salvage terms. And here's the part that should interest you." He tapped the folder once. "Completion fee covers your balance. All of it. Every cent. You walk away owing the Trust nothing."

Yara didn't touch the folder. "Nobody walks away owing the Trust nothing."

Okafor laughed—a real laugh, warm, surprised, the laugh of a man genuinely delighted. It was somehow worse than the smile. "You'd be amazed how often people say that. And yet." He shrugged. "Contracts are contracts. Even ours. Especially ours—we're very proud of our contracts. Read it yourself. Take it home. I'll give you until Friday."

"It's Wednesday."

"Yes," he agreed pleasantly. "One week, Ms. Iversen. That's the other thing I came to say, and I do wish it didn't have to be said at all. If the salvage contract isn't signed by next Wednesday, the Trust begins seizure proceedings on the collateral. The rig, the instruments, the trailer. Everything with a serial number." He finally lifted his teacup, took a sip of the cold tea without flinching, and set it down again, aligned. "I'd hate that. Genuinely. I've seen your vehicle assessments. That rig of yours is a beautiful machine. It would break my heart to see it on a repo lot."

"You don't have a heart."

"Oh, everyone says that too." He stood, buttoned his jacket, and his smile returned to its factory setting—pleasant, patient, bottomless. "But I do. It just belongs to the Trust now. They were very generous about it." He put a card on the table, white with a single embossed line. "Friday. Or Wednesday. Your choice, Ms. Iversen. We only ever offer choices."

He left. Yara watched through the rain-streaked window as he crossed the street without hurrying, without opening an umbrella, smiling at the weather as though it owed him money instead of the reverse.

* * *

She read the contract that night in the cab of her rig, parked in the lot behind Halvorsen's Garage where she rented a slot by the month and was three months behind on that too. The dome light flickered. Rain drummed the roof like fingers asking to be let in.

The contract was forty-one pages. She read the first twelve carefully, and by page thirteen the language had begun to move beneath her like river ice—*salvage rights revert*, *undisclosed hazards*, *contractor assumes all risk of disappearance*—and she kept reading anyway, because that was the thing about drowning: you'll grab anything, even something pulling you under.

*Assumes all risk of disappearance.* She read that phrase four times. Six people had gone into the Kestrels eight months ago and stopped existing. The contract didn't say dead. It said *disappeared*, in italics, as if the word itself were a warning label. And there was a rider about records—anything resembling documentation recovered from the site was to be surrendered *unopened and unexamined* to the Trust's representatives upon return. Unopened. Unexamined.

She thought about that. She thought about Okafor's teacup, aligned to the table edge. People who needed everything aligned were people afraid of something being out of place.

Her father had worked surveys too. Kestrel-adjacent ranges, twenty years ago—she'd grown up on his radio calls relayed through crackle and static, *good snowpack today, kiddo, found a spring that sings*. He'd died on one, a whiteout on a ridge with a map case strapped to his chest, and the official report had used the word *unfortunate* twice and *anomaly* once, and Yara had been seventeen and had believed reports because she hadn't yet learned that reports were written by whoever paid for them.

She looked at the signature line. It waited at the bottom of page forty-one, above a line of fine print she could barely read by the dying dome light, and it waited the way all Meridian documents waited—patiently, confidently, certain of her.

The rain kept on. Somewhere across the lot, Halvorsen's dog barked twice and gave up.

Yara got a pen from the dash. Her hand hovered. One week. Seven days, and then the rig—the sixteen years of her life welded and rewelded into that chassis, the anemometer mast her father had helped her build when she was nineteen, the radar unit she'd bought with her first grant, the cot she slept on and the stove she cooked on and the whole small sovereign nation of herself that fit inside a truck bed—all of it towed away by men who smiled.

They only ever offer choices.

She signed. The pen scratched loud in the quiet, and when she finished, the letters of her own name looked strange to her, like a stranger's handwriting in her journal.

Then she packed. Rope, pitons, the ice axe, freeze-dried everything, spare batteries, the med kit, her father's old compass with the cracked face that still pointed true. She loaded until the rig sat low on its springs, and then she sat in the driver's seat with the engine off and the wipers off and let the rain speak for a while.

North. Two days' drive to the Kestrel headwaters, following a river up into country where six people had walked out of existence. To recover equipment. To document a site. To bring back records unopened and unexamined.

She turned the key. The engine caught on the second try, the way it always did, grumbling awake like an old dog that loved her anyway.

"One week," she said to the windshield, to the rain, to the smiling man somewhere in the city behind her. "Fine."

The headlights carved a tunnel through the wet dark, and Yara Iversen drove north, carrying a debt, a contract she hadn't finished reading, and a feeling she couldn't name yet—something between dread and recognition, like hearing your own name called from a room you've never entered.

The river would explain. Rivers always did, eventually.

She just had to follow it upstream.
