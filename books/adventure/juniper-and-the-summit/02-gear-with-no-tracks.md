## 2. Gear With No Tracks

The river told her she was close before anything else did.

Yara noticed it as a change in the water's voice—a steadiness beneath the usual argument of rapids, as if the river had made up its mind about something. She'd been following the Kestrel's headwater fork for two days now, through a canyon that narrowed and widened like a throat swallowing, and the water here ran clear and cold and oddly patient, sliding over stones the color of old bruises.

She smelled woodsmoke next.

That stopped her cold. Woodsmoke meant people, and the survey team had been missing eleven months, and no one else had any business this far up. She killed the engine of her rig—the battered six-wheeled crawler she'd named *Vespera*, the only thing she owned that the Meridian Trust hadn't yet figured out how to price—and stood in the shallows listening. The smoke smell was faint, ghosted, the kind that clings to stone and canvas long after the fire dies. Days old. Weeks, maybe.

She should have turned around then.

She didn't. She told herself it was the contract—the salvage terms were specific, gear recovery only, coordinates locked to the basecamp site—but the truth was uglier and simpler. She was thirty-one thousand credits deep, her rig was collateral, and a week was all the smiling man in the gray coat had given her. Desperation smells like woodsmoke too, if you breathe it long enough. It makes everything downwind look like salvation.

She hiked the last two kilometers on foot, *Vespera* tucked into a stand of black-barked pines, her pack heavy with rope and the salvage scanner she'd rented against her better judgment. The canyon opened without warning, the way canyons do—one step it's walls, the next it's sky—and there, on the gravel bench above the flood line, sat the basecamp.

Intact.

That was the first wrong thing. Not damaged, not scattered, not the sad archaeology of a camp overtaken by weather. *Intact.* Three dome tents staked in a tidy row, guy lines taut, rain flies still cinched. A cook shelter with its table folded down. A solar array angled at the sky with the dumb confidence of a thing that expected to be needed tomorrow.

Eleven months of Kestrel winters, and the tents looked like the team had stepped out to check the river gauge and would be back before the tea went cold.

Yara stood at the edge of the bench for a long time, letting the wind move through her jacket, feeling the particular silence of a place that has been left in a hurry by no one in a hurry at all. Her breath fogged. Somewhere upstream, water fell over stone with a sound like slow applause.

"Okay," she said aloud, because saying things aloud kept the fear at the size of words. "Okay."

She circled the camp before she entered it, the way you'd circle a downed animal. The perimeter showed no signs of predators—no scat, no claw-raked bark, no bones. The food stores were the second wrong thing. She unzipped the cook shelter's storage crate and found it full: vacuum-sealed ration bricks stacked in neat columns, fuel canisters sealed, a case of electrolyte tabs, even a tin of the terrible spiced tea that government survey crews always seemed to carry. Nothing eaten. Nothing spoiled. The crate had been closed against the weather with a care that felt, in that empty place, almost like a message.

*We didn't leave because we had to.*

The third wrong thing was the boots.

They were lined up outside the largest tent—six pairs, seven, she counted twice—placed heel to toe with the precision of soldiers at inspection. Mountaineering boots, insulated, broken in, laces tucked inside. Not tossed. Not paired hastily and abandoned. *Arranged.* And beside them, folded into a weatherproof sack, were the team's gloves. Every pair.

Yara crouched there a long time, her own boots planted in the gravel, and felt the cold come up through her soles like a hand closing.

Nobody walks into a Kestrel winter barefoot. Nobody walks anywhere barefoot. The river valley dropped below freezing by midafternoon; the passes above were white nine months of the year. Seven people—six, she corrected herself, the manifest said six, plus Dr. Voss made seven—had taken off their boots, folded their gloves, and walked out into a landscape that kills the unprepared in hours.

And left no tracks.

That was the thing she kept circling back to, the thing that made her scanner hand tremble when she finally made herself sweep the bench. The ground told a story of nothing. No footprints, though the fine gravel here held prints beautifully—she could see her own, sharp-edged, every lug pattern crisp. No tracks older than hers. No drag marks, no animal sign, no disturbance at all. The snow line started four hundred meters up the canyon wall, but the bench itself had been scoured by wind and rain and should have held *something*—a boot heel, a knee-print, a dropped and half-buried glove.

Nothing. As if seven people had been lifted off the earth by the collar, like kittens.

The scanner chirped its useless green confirmation: no organic remains within detection range. Yara shut it off. The silence afterward was enormous.

* * *

She made herself work. That was the discipline her father had taught her, back when the lessons were about storms and the discipline was simpler: *when the sky goes strange, you do the next small task, and then the next, until the strangeness passes or you do.* So she did the next small task. She inventoried the gear against the manifest on her contract—transceiver array, present; core samples, present, still sealed; geological hammers, present, lined up beside the boots like everything else, handles worn to the shape of hands.

The river gauge was the last item, and the strangest.

It stood at the water's edge on a galvanized post, a government-standard flow monitor, and it was *ticking*. Not running—ticking. Its little anemometer cups spun in a wind she couldn't feel on her face. Its data light blinked in a rhythm too regular to be random. She knelt beside it and pulled her scanner out again, and the readout made her sit down in the gravel.

The gauge was still transmitting. Low-power, burst-mode, a whisper of signal aimed downriver at relay stations that had stopped listening months ago. But the data stream was intact, and it was current, and it was wrong in a way she had to check three times before she believed it.

The gauge was recording water levels that didn't exist.

The Kestrel fork in front of her ran knee-deep. The gauge insisted it ran four meters deep, and rising, in a smooth exponential curve that had begun—the timestamp said—eleven months and six days ago. The day after the survey team's last scheduled check-in. The curve hadn't plateaued. It was still climbing, one impossible centimeter at a time, a flood being measured in a river that had never flooded, a drowning recorded in a place where nothing had drowned.

Unless the gauge wasn't measuring the river.

Yara sat with that thought while the light faded off the canyon walls, and the cold came down off the peaks like something being poured. She ate a ration brick she'd brought—she wasn't touching the camp's stores, not after the boots—and she did not make a fire, though she wanted one badly. Fire meant light, and light meant being seen, and she could not have said, even to herself, who she was afraid of being seen by.

The wilderness was watching her back. She'd felt it since the canyon mouth, that prickle between the shoulder blades that every storm chaser learns and every storm chaser denies—the sense of an attention too large to be an animal, distributed through the wind and the water and the standing dark of the pines. Her father used to say that big weather had a mind, and she'd thought it was a figure of speech. Out here, with seven pairs of empty boots at her back and a machine counting a flood that wasn't there, it didn't feel like a figure of speech. It felt like being *read*.

She got into her bivvy sack inside *Vespera*—she was not sleeping in those tents, contract or no contract—and lay listening to the river. Around midnight, the gauge's data light changed rhythm. She noticed it because she'd been staring at it through the windshield, unable to sleep, telling herself she was keeping watch.

The light had been blinking in steady intervals. Now it blinked in a pattern. Short-short-long. Short-short-long. Pause. Short-short-long.

She knew the pattern. Every licensed operator in the northern territories knew it. It was the oldest distress cadet code there was, the one they taught you before they taught you anything else, the one that meant, in three pulses any child could tap out on a table:

*Here. Here. Here.*

Yara sat up in the dark with her heart going like a drill motor and her breath fogging the glass, and she watched the little light speak its impossible sentence to a canyon full of nothing, over and over, patient as a heartbeat.

The river ran knee-deep in the moonlight. The gauge said four meters and rising. Seven people had walked barefoot into a blizzard and left no tracks, and something at the headwaters was still counting them, still calling for them, still—

A sound. Behind her. Not the river, not the wind. A footfall on gravel, one, deliberate, at the edge of the firelight she hadn't built.

Yara went for the flare gun in her door pocket and turned.

At the treeline, just past where the moonlight quit, stood a figure. Watching her. Not moving. Not smiling.

She'd seen that silhouette before—in the missing-persons file the Trust's contract had attached as an afterthought. The survey team's comms officer. The one body the manifest listed as *status unconfirmed*.

Petra Lindqvist had come back to the place she'd supposedly never left.
