## 3. Upstream Rules

The canyon began where the map ended.

Yara noticed it first as a change in the river's voice. Downstream it had babbled, friendly almost, the way water sounds when it's going somewhere you could follow on foot without thinking. Here, squeezed between walls of gray granite that rose like the ribs of something buried, it spoke in a lower register—a continuous growl, the sound of a machine left running in a basement. She stood at the mouth of it with her pack straps biting her shoulders and understood that she was about to walk into a throat.

The salvage contract had said *headwaters*. It had said *recover equipment of value*. It had not said anything about the canyon, because the survey team's own maps—what few pages Yara had found at basecamp before burning through the rest of the file on her rig's cracked screen—showed a blank space above the last gauge station. A blank space with one word penciled in the margin in handwriting that pressed hard enough to emboss the paper: **UPSTREAM RULES APPLY**.

No explanation. Just that.

"Fine," she said to the canyon, because talking to empty places was a habit from storm work—you narrated your position so that if you stopped narrating, someone would know when to worry. Nobody was listening now. She narrated anyway. "Upstream rules apply."

She checked the river gauge one more time before leaving it behind. The little brass instrument ticked in its housing like an insect in a box, recording a rise of four centimeters overnight. Snowmelt, probably. Probably.

* * *

*Day 9 — Voss*

*The canyon narrows past the second tributary until a man with a full pack must turn sideways. Okonkwo measured it at eleven meters wall-to-wall and laughed, said it was like walking down the aisle of a church built by someone who hated people. I did not laugh. The rock here is fractured along three axes and I have counted forty-one fresh scars on the walls where slabs the size of trucks have recently let go. This terrain does not forgive. It does not even remember forgiving. We rope up tomorrow.*

*Halvard says he can hear the water changing pitch at night. Says it hums. I told him it's resonance, standing waves in a confined channel, perfectly explicable. He looked at me with that flat northern patience of his and said, "Then explain why it stops when we stop talking."*

*I could not.*

Yara read the diary pages by headlamp each night, propped against whatever stone she'd chosen for a pillow, and each morning she woke up angrier at Dr. Ansel Voss than she'd been the night before. Not because he was wrong about anything. Because he was right, and precise, and careful—and careful men with careful notes still vanished, boots and all, into a canyon that kept no record except his.

She was on day two of the gorge when the rockfall came.

There was no warning she could have used. That was the thing she'd understand later, lying bruised under an overhang with grit in her teeth: the mountain didn't announce itself. One moment she was crossing a talus fan where the canyon widened just enough to pretend at mercy, testing each block with her boot like her father had taught her—*weight, then commit, never both at once*—and the next the whole slope above her exhaled. Not a crash. An exhalation, a long grinding sigh of stone deciding to be somewhere else, and then the world was noise and dust and the taste of iron.

When it settled she was alive, which surprised her, and unbroken, which astonished her, and her route forward was buried under forty tons of new granite, which did neither.

She sat down on a boulder still warm from friction and laughed until it turned into something else, and then she drank water and ate half a ration bar and got up, because upstream rules applied to her too.

The detour cost her six hours. She climbed the debris field hand over hand, found a ledge above the slide, traversed it with the river snarling far below, and dropped back into the channel as the light went copper and then gone. In the dark she pitched the tent in a slot barely wider than herself, and she read Voss by headlamp with dust still in the creases of her knuckles.

* * *

*Day 11 — Voss*

*We lost the radio mast today. Not to weather—to the river. It came up thirty centimeters in an hour, brown and muscular, and took the mast off its moorings like a man plucking a hair. Lindqvist got the equipment cases clear but lost the spare battery pack downstream. She cried. I don't blame her. There is something about this canyon that makes loss feel larger than it is, as if the walls are keeping score.*

*The water is rising every day and the melt season hasn't started. Halvard has stopped humming. Okonkwo asked me directly today what we were doing here, and I told him the truth: the thermal anomaly doesn't make sense. The gradient is wrong. The heat is coming from somewhere it shouldn't come from, at a depth it shouldn't reach, and the government wants to know why before they sign away mineral rights. That is the job. But I will write here what I don't say aloud: the instruments behave strangely near the source. Yesterday the seismometer recorded a signal with no seismic event attached. A clean tone. 4.2 hertz. It lasted ninety seconds and stopped the moment Lindqvist switched off her transmitter.*

*Okonkwo says the canyon is haunted. I say the canyon is listening. I am not sure which of us is being scientific.*

Yara read that passage three times, and the third time she became aware of the silence around her—the specific, textured silence of the gorge at night, which was not silent at all but full of the river's low machinery, and beneath it, when she held her breath, something else. A hum. Faint. Below hearing and inside it at once, felt in the molars more than the ears.

She switched off her headlamp.

The hum continued. Then—it could have been the water, it could have been blood in her own ears, exhaustion playing tricks on a woman alone in a hole in the earth—but it seemed to pause. A gap, like a breath drawn. Like something waiting to hear if she would speak.

"Don't," she said aloud, to herself, to Voss, to whoever was keeping score. "Don't do this to me."

She slept badly, and dreamed of her father's radio, the old handheld he'd carried on surveys, the way he'd tap it twice before transmitting—two clicks, always two, *so you know it's me and not the mountain*, he'd joked, though she'd never understood what the mountain would want with his voice.

* * *

On the fourth day the water won.

Not all at once. The river had been rising since the rockfall—she gauged it against a marked boulder each morning, and each morning the mark drowned a finger-width higher—and by the fourth day the channel ahead closed entirely, a churning strait between walls too sheer to climb with a pack. So she climbed anyway, because the alternative was downstream, and downstream was Dane Okafor's smile and a week ticking toward zero.

The traverse above the flood line took the whole day. Ledges no wider than her boots. Granite polished by ten thousand years of meltwater, slick as glass where spray reached it. Twice her footing crumbled and twice the mountain caught her—once on a root grown impossibly from a crack in bare stone, once on a nub of rock that should not have held her weight and did. She stopped afterward, shaking, arms wrapped around a bollard of stone, forehead pressed to the cold grain of it.

"You're testing me," she told the canyon. Her breath fogged and hung there. "Fine. Test."

And the thought arrived—not spoken, not hers exactly, but arriving the way weather arrives—that the missing team had passed this same ledge. Had pressed their palms to this same stone. Had felt the same indifferent vastness underneath everything and kept going anyway, eight strangers roped together into a single organism, trusting each other with their lives because the terrain gave them no other choice. She had spent two days reading Voss's diary like evidence. Somewhere on this ledge it stopped being evidence and started being company.

*You lost your mast,* she thought, hauling herself up over the lip of the high shelf. *I lost my route. You kept going. So did I.* It wasn't conversation. It wasn't prayer. It was closer to marching in step with soldiers who'd marched here before her and never marched out, and finding that their pace fit hers.

That night, camped on the shelf above the flooded strait, she read the last intact page.

* * *

*Day 14 — Voss*

*The canyon opens tomorrow, according to Halvard's barometry—he swears he can smell the basin beyond, says the air changes. I believe him. I believe all of them now, every strange report, because the strange reports agree with each other and the official explanations don't.*

*Lindqvist recorded something tonight. She won't play it for me. She sat outside her tent for two hours with her headphones on and when she finally took them off her face was the face of a person who has heard a door open somewhere it shouldn't open. She said only: "It answers." I asked what answers. She said, "Whatever you bring to it."*

*Tomorrow we reach the source. I find I am writing this entry slowly, the way you speak carefully in a room where you suspect the walls have ears. Perhaps they do. Perhaps that is the whole discovery. The heat, the gradient, the tone at 4.2 hertz—all of it might be secondary to the simple fact that this place takes in sound and gives it back, and has been doing so for longer than there have been people to fill it with voices.*

*If we find what I think we'll find, the survey ends here and something else begins. I've instructed the team to log everything. Whatever listens to us, we will listen back.*

*Two clicks on the radio before every transmission. So you know it's us, and not the mountain.*

Yara lowered the page. Her hands weren't steady.

Beyond the shelf, the canyon walls fell away in the dark—she could feel the openness the way Halvard had smelled it, a loosening in the air pressure, the river's growl spreading out and softening into something wider. Tomorrow she'd reach the basin. Tomorrow, the source, or whatever remained of the place where eight people had decided the truth was worth staying for.

She was packing the diary away when she saw it: light. Not starlight. A point of pale green, far up-canyon, blinking with a rhythm she knew in her bones before her mind caught up.

Two flashes. Pause. Two flashes.

Someone was signaling on the survey team's emergency band—and they were close enough to see her fire.
