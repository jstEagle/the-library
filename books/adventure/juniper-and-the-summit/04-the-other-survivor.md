## 4. The Other Survivor

The river taught me its rules by the third day: never trust the quiet stretches, never camp below the high-water line, and never stop listening. The canyon had a voice of its own—water over stone, stone under water, the deep percussion of the range shifting somewhere beneath my boots—and I'd learned to sort its sounds the way a sailor sorts weather. Wind in the scree. Meltwater in the side channels. The tick and groan of frost letting go of the rock at dawn.

So when the rhythm changed, I knew before I knew why.

A footfall that wasn't water. A pause that wasn't wind.

I didn't turn around. Turning was what prey did. Instead I crouched at the riverbank as if checking my gauge readings, let my hand drift to the pry bar lashed across my pack, and watched the current carry a curl of foam past my knees. The canyon narrowed ahead into a slot of gray granite, walls close enough to touch with both arms outstretched, and the river went through it like a fist through paper. Whoever was behind me would have to follow me through it, single file, no room to maneuver.

Good. Let them.

I counted to thirty. Then I moved—not forward, but sideways, into the shadow of a fallen slab where the morning light hadn't reached yet, and I waited.

The figure came down the trail twenty minutes later, moving well. That was the first thing I noticed: whoever it was walked this terrain like they'd been born to it, weight low, steps placed rather than thrown. A hooded shell jacket, gray as the rock, a pack stripped down to almost nothing. They passed within four meters of my hiding spot without a glance.

I stepped out behind them and put the pry bar's cold length against their shoulder blade.

"Don't," I said.

They froze. Then, slowly, hands rising, they turned—and the hood fell back, and I was looking at a woman maybe ten years older than me, face burned brown and seamed, eyes the pale flat blue of glacier ice. She looked at the pry bar the way you'd look at an old acquaintance.

"Is that survey-grade titanium?" she said. "You're threatening me with salvage."

"Who are you?"

"Petra Lindqvist." She said it evenly, like a name on a form. "Comms officer, Kestrel Headwaters Survey, Meridian Trust Expedition Seven. Which makes you either very lost or very stupid, because nobody comes up this canyon by accident."

"I'm here on contract. Salvage recovery. The team's equipment."

Something moved behind her eyes—too fast to name, gone before I could read it. "Contract from whom?"

"The Trust."

She laughed. It was not a kind sound; it was the sound a rope makes when it parts under load. "Of course it is." Her hands were still up, but her weight had settled, coiled, and I realized with a cold slide in my stomach that she wasn't afraid of me at all. "Lower the bar, storm chaser. If I wanted you dead, you'd have died at the basecamp. You slept twelve hours next to a river gauge I could have opened your throat beside."

My skin crawled. "That was you? Watching the camp?"

"That was me making sure whatever followed you in wasn't following *me* out." She lowered her hands slowly. "You found the tents. The food stores. The boots lined up nice and neat."

"You know about that."

"I packed those boots myself." For the first time her voice cracked, just a hairline fracture, and just as quickly sealed. "Turn around. Go back downstream. Take whatever scrap you want off the lower caches and tell the Trust the site's washed out. They'll believe it. They *want* to believe it."

"Not until you tell me what happened to your team."

"What happened is none of your business."

"Six people vanished." My voice came out harder than I intended, and I let it. "No bodies. No tracks. Boots left behind like they walked into a blizzard barefoot. I've spent three days sleeping in their kitchen. I've read their logbook. Somebody owes those people more than a missing-persons file, and if it isn't going to be you, then it's going to be me."

The silence stretched. The river filled it, patient and enormous.

"You sound like Ansel," she said finally, and the name landed on her like a weight she'd been carrying so long she'd forgotten how to set it down. "Dr. Voss. Team lead. He said things like that. *Somebody owes.*" She shook her head. "Idealism is expensive up here. The exchange rate is everything."

"So tell me what it cost."

"Why?" She took one step closer, ignoring the pry bar entirely, and now I could see the fine tremor in her that had nothing to do with cold. "What did the Trust promise you? Debt clearance? A bonus? They're generous when they want something buried. I know because—" she stopped herself, jaw tightening.

"Because what?"

"Because they funded us." She said it flatly, watching my face for the reaction. "Your salvage contract, my expedition—same money, same smiling men signing the checks. Meridian Trust Geological Survey Initiative, grant number 44-Kestrel. Look it up when you get signal, if you ever get signal. We weren't mapping headwaters for science, girl. We were mapping them for *acquisition*. And we found something in the acquisition that the Trust will burn a canyon to keep quiet."

The word *burn* sat between us.

"You're lying," I said, but it came out weak, because it fit. Of course it fit. The cheerful enforcers, the contract I hadn't finished reading, the way Dane Okafor's smile had lingered half a second too long when he'd handed me the route map—*headwaters access, standard salvage corridor*—like he was handing me something pre-wrapped.

"If I were lying," Petra said quietly, "would I still be here? Six weeks I've survived alone in this range. Off their charts, off their satellites, eating lichen and snowmelt. All I had to do was walk south and disappear forever. Instead I'm standing here talking to a debt collector's errand girl because—" She stopped again. Pressed her lips together. Whatever lived behind her teeth, she wasn't ready to feed it.

"Because why?"

"Because you're going upstream anyway. I can see it in you. Same look Voss had." She stepped back, putting distance between us, and gestured at the slot canyon ahead with something close to grief. "Fine. Then hear the only warning I'm authorized to give, which is none: the river gets worse. The canyon gets worse. And at the end of it there's a place that—" Her breath caught. Actually caught, a hitch like a skipped heartbeat. "There's a place that listens. It takes sounds and it keeps them and it gives them back, and what it gave back to me, I wouldn't wish on the man who invented debt."

"That's not a warning. That's a ghost story."

"Yes," she said. "It is. And every word of it is true."

We stood there circling each other in the narrow light, two survivors of nothing yet, each certain the other was the trap. I thought about the boots lined up neat as soldiers. I thought about the diary pages I'd found tucked in the logbook, Dr. Voss's handwriting deteriorating page by page from surveyor's precision to something fevered and reverent: *the conduits sing back. Ansel heard his mother. Marta heard the drill rig from her first season. It remembers. God help us, it remembers better than we do.*

I hadn't told anyone about those pages. I hadn't told anyone about anything.

"How do I know you didn't kill them?" I asked. "Your own team. How do I know you're not the reason there are no bodies?"

Something terrible crossed her face—not guilt exactly, but its neighbor, guilt's exhausted roommate. "Because I ran," she said simply. "I'm the coward of the story, storm chaser. Ask anyone. Oh wait—you can't. There's no one left to ask." She shouldered her pack and turned toward the slot canyon, then paused, not looking back. "One more thing. Your contract. Read the indemnity clause. Section nine. It says the Trust assumes no liability for psychological injury sustained during recovery operations."

"So?"

"So why would a salvage contract need a clause like that?" She started walking. Over her shoulder, without turning: "Either come with me and see it yourself, or go home and spend the rest of your life wondering which of us was lying. Those are your options. I've had six weeks to think about mine."

I stood in the cold spray of the slot canyon with the pry bar heavy in my hand and the diary pages burning a hole in my pack, and I hated her, and I believed her, and I could not for the life of me separate the two.

She was fifty meters ahead when I noticed the second thing—the thing that made my blood go from cold to ice.

She was heading upstream.

The woman who claimed she'd escaped. The woman who'd begged me to turn back. Walking, sure-footed and unhesitating, toward the very place she swore she'd fled—like someone returning to a grave they couldn't stay away from, or like someone leading me exactly where she'd been paid to lead me.

And far above, thin as a needle through the canyon's throat, came a sound I'd know anywhere: the chop of helicopter blades, coming up the Kestrel.

* * *

I caught up to her at the mouth of the slot, where the river squeezed white and furious between walls the color of old bone.

"You're going back," I shouted over the water.

She didn't deny it. She stood looking up-canyon, and in the flat northern light I saw what six weeks of survival had carved into her—not hardness, but hollowness, the shape of a person who has emptied themselves out to keep moving. "There's something I left there," she said. "Something I should have taken when I ran."

"Your brother?" I don't know why I said it. Maybe the diary. Maybe the way she'd flinched at the word *listens*. Maybe just the arithmetic of grief—I knew its sums by heart.

Her head snapped around, and for one raw second all the hostility burned off her like fog, and underneath it she was just a woman standing in front of the place that had eaten everyone she loved. "How do you—"

"Lucky guess," I said, and lied badly.

The helicopters were louder now, echoing off the walls so they seemed to come from everywhere. Petra's eyes went up, tracking the sound with the practiced ease of someone who'd once run comms for a living, and when she spoke again her voice had gone flat and fast, professional, the mask slammed back down.

"Two rotors. Heavy lift. That's not rescue—that's a work crew." She grabbed my sleeve, and her grip was iron. "Listen to me. Whatever the Trust told you about salvage, forget it. They don't send helicopters to retrieve tents. They send helicopters to make sure no one else ever finds what's up there. Including you."

"Then we move faster than they do."

She stared at me. Somewhere in that glacier-blue stare a decision was being weighed, and I watched her make it the way you watch someone step off a ledge—half horror, half relief.

"Voss would have liked you," she said, disgusted. "Come on. There's a goat track above the slot that cuts two hours off the canyon. Stay off the waterline, do exactly what I say, and if you hear a voice that isn't mine—" she hesitated, and the tremor came back into her hands, "—don't answer it. No matter whose voice you think it is."

Above us, the first helicopter crested the ridge, and its shadow slid down the canyon wall like a hand closing.
