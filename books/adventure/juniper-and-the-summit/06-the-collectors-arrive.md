## 6. The Collectors Arrive

The river told her before the sky did.

Yara had learned to read water the way other people read faces—the way a current tightened when weather was coming, how trout went sullen and deep before a front rolled over the divide. So when the Kestrel headwaters went glass-still at midday, holding the canyon walls upside down in themselves like a held breath, she stopped walking and stood very still on the gravel bar.

Then came the sound. Not thunder—thunder had texture, a rolling weight to it. This was a flat mechanical pulse, rhythmic as a heartbeat with something wrong in it, bouncing off the canyon walls until it seemed to come from everywhere.

She knew that sound. She'd grown up under it, in the shadow of the Meridian Trust's regional office, watching their helicopters lift off from the pad behind the lending hall with the same cheerful yellow livery they painted on their collection vans. *We're here to help*, the logo said, a smiling sun rising over stylized mountains. Yara had spent twenty-nine years learning what happened after the sun arrived.

She killed her headlamp even though it wasn't lit, an instinctive, useless gesture, and pressed herself into the willow thicket at the bar's edge as the helicopter crested the eastern ridge.

It came down the canyon low and slow, rotors chewing the cold air into rings that flattened the river grass for fifty meters on either bank. The yellow sun on its flank caught the afternoon light and seemed to glow. Two more followed it, spaced like beads on a wire, and the sound of all three together filled the canyon so completely that Yara felt it in her sternum, in her back teeth, in the soles of her boots.

They passed directly overhead. Close enough that she could see a figure in the open side door, strapped in, scanning the banks with binoculars. The figure wore no uniform—just a good jacket, good boots, the kind of gear that cost more than everything Yara owned including her rig—and its face, when it turned briefly toward the willows, was smiling.

Not laughing. Not grinning. Just *smiling*, the way the Trust's people always smiled, with a warmth that never reached anything deeper than the skin, and Yara felt the old cold slide down her spine, the cold of being eight years old and watching two smiling men carry her mother's sewing machine out to their van while her mother thanked them. Thanked them. That was the part that had haunted her longest—not the taking but the thanking, the way the Trust made you complicit in your own dismantling, made gratitude feel like the only dignity left to hand over.

The helicopters banked around the bend where the canyon narrowed, toward the flat meadow two kilometers upstream where the survey team's basecamp sat empty and neat as a museum diorama.

Yara stayed in the willows a long time after the sound faded, listening to the river resume its ordinary voice, and understood with total clarity what she had known only abstractly since the contract: nobody sent three helicopters to recover camping equipment.

* * *

She approached the meadow the way she'd approach a storm cell—wide, circling, reading it from every angle before committing. The helicopters sat on the grass in a loose triangle, rotors drooping, engines ticking as they cooled. A camp had already bloomed around them: four expedition tents, a galley fly, folding tables bearing instruments she didn't recognize, antennas on masts, a portable generator humming its small stubborn song against the vast quiet of the range.

Men and women moved among it all with the unhurried efficiency of people who had done this many times in many places, and every one of them, as far as Yara could see through her binoculars from the tree line, was smiling.

It was worse than uniforms would have been. Uniforms you could hate cleanly. But these were field jackets and fleece beanies and steaming mugs, and they passed each other equipment with little nods and murmured courtesies, and they smiled at the mountains, smiled at the river, smiled at the work itself, as if sealing something shut forever were just another beautiful day outdoors.

Yara counted nine. She noted which two carried rifles slung across their backs with the casualness of men carrying fishing rods, and which one—the tall figure who had looked into the willows—was giving orders without appearing to give orders, receiving information with tilted head and widening smile, distributing decisions with a gesture, a word, a laugh she couldn't hear across the distance.

She should have run. Every survival instinct she possessed, honed by years of positioning herself relative to rotating walls of wind, told her to back away from the tree line, climb the western scree, and put the whole drainage between herself and those yellow suns. Her debt would survive. Debts were patient. She could disappear into the basin country for a season, let the Trust's interest calcify, come back—

But the vault was upstream. The recording was upstream. Her father's voice was upstream, still living in the stone, still saying whatever he had said in his last hour, and these smiling people with their instruments and their rifles had come to make sure no one ever heard it again.

She walked out of the trees into the open, hands visible, heart hammering a rhythm older than language.

* * *

Dane Okafor met her halfway across the meadow.

She knew his name before he offered it—everyone in debt knew Dane Okafor, the way farmers in tornado country knew the names of long-track storms. He was the Trust's closer, the one they sent when accounts grew complicated, and he was shorter than his reputation, compact and neat in a charcoal softshell, gray threading his temples like frost on a fence post. His smile was exactly as advertised: warm, genuine-seeming, tireless. It did not flicker when he saw her. It did not change at all. It simply was, the way the mountains were.

"Yara Iversen." He said her name like a gift he'd been keeping for her. "Contract holder, salvage provision seven-fourteen. You found the basecamp. You've been following the river." He gestured gently at her mud-crusted gaiters, her salt-stained pack, as if admiring craftsmanship. "Alone, I believe. Impressive. The Kestrel doesn't forgive solo travel this late in the season."

"You've been reading my file."

"I've been *respecting* your file." He turned slightly, opening his body toward the camp, the helicopters, the steam rising from someone's kettle—an invitation woven entirely of posture. "You must be exhausted. Please. Coffee's real, not freeze-dried. Sit. Eat. Let's talk like professionals."

"I know what you are," Yara said. "I don't need coffee to say no."

Okafor laughed—a real laugh, or such a perfect copy that the difference stopped mattering—and shook his head with something that looked, impossibly, like admiration. "There it is. Your father had the same look. Do you know I met him? Briefly. At a hearing, years ago. Tor Iversen, standing up in the gallery with his maps under his arm, telling a room full of suits that the numbers were wrong." The smile softened into something almost tender. "He was right, you know. About the numbers. Everyone in that room knew he was right. And everyone in that room also knew it wouldn't matter, and do you know why?"

"Because the Trust owns the rooms."

"Because the Trust *is* the rooms," Okafor agreed pleasantly. "And now here's his daughter, standing in a meadow, about to be offered the one thing he never had." He spread his hands, and Yara saw that they were steady, well-kept, the hands of a man who had never once had to grip anything for his life. "Walk with me a moment. Ten minutes. Then decide whether to hate me."

Against every instinct, she walked with him. Because ten minutes of his mouth was ten minutes of intelligence, and because some traitor part of her wanted to hear the offer spoken aloud, wanted to know its exact shape before she refused it.

* * *

He led her along the meadow's edge, away from the others, and spoke with the ease of a man reciting a favorite poem.

"The survey team found something in the headwaters," he said. "Something geological. A formation that preserves acoustic information—stone that remembers sound. The team's findings were... destabilizing. Not scientifically. Financially. The formation sits beneath land holdings whose valuations depend on certain surveys remaining authoritative. If the formation's existence became public, if its properties became public, the resulting litigation would take eleven years and cost the Trust everything it has built in this region." He glanced at her sidelong, smiling. "You've heard the recordings by now. I can tell. You have the look of someone carrying a voice in her chest."

Yara said nothing. The river said enough, talking to itself below the bank.

"So the Trust's position is simple. The source must be sealed. Permanently, thoroughly, with engineering rather than explosives alone—we brought specialists, we brought concrete, we brought patience. In six days there will be nothing up this canyon but rock, and in six months no one will remember there was ever anything else." He stopped walking and turned to face her fully, and for the first time his smile changed—it widened, warmed, became the smile of a man about to hand someone exactly what they'd been starving for. "And you, Ms. Iversen. Your account with us stands at forty-one thousand, six hundred and twelve marks, accruing daily. Your rig is collateral. Your rig is your life—I've seen the modifications, the anemometer array, the lightning cage you welded yourself. Beautiful work. It would be a tragedy to repossess it."

"Get to it."

"The Trust erases the debt. Today. All of it, plus the penalties, plus the flag on your credit registry—you walk out of this valley owing nothing to anyone, free to chase every storm between here and the coast for the rest of your life." He let that sit in the mountain air between them, let her feel its full weight, the way she'd feel a change in pressure before a cell dropped. "In exchange: you guide our team to the source. You know the route—the rockfall, the crossing, the high water. You'd save us days, and days matter now. And afterward, you say nothing. Not to journalists, not to investigators, not to whoever comes asking when the Trust's name surfaces in connection with a missing survey. You never heard voices in the stone. You never heard—" and here his voice gentled, became almost kind, and that kindness was the cruelest thing he'd done yet, "—your father."

The meadow seemed to tilt. Yara locked her knees and kept her face still, the way she kept her face still when hail started coming sideways, because movement invited damage.

Forty-one thousand marks. The rig. The open road. No smiling men on her porch, no yellow van idling outside her mother's building, no more arithmetic done at 3 a.m. with a calculator and a knot in her throat. Freedom, itemized and handed over with both hands, warm as fresh bread.

All it cost was silence. Just silence. Her father had died for a set of numbers, and she could sell his last words for less than the price of a used engine.

"That's it," she said quietly. "That's the whole offer."

"That's the whole offer." Okafor's smile held steady, patient, certain. Behind him the helicopters ticked as they cooled, and somewhere in the camp a radio murmured, and the river ran on toward the vault, carrying its cold freight of memory toward the sea. "Debt erased, freedom granted, and the past stays buried where the past belongs. Most people find it a very comfortable arrangement." He extended his hand. "I'll need your answer by morning. We move at first light—with you or without you."

Yara looked at the hand. Well-kept, steady, empty.

She thought of her father's maps under his arm in that hearing room. She thought of the neat boots at the basecamp, lined up facing the river, and the team that had chosen to stay in the dark with the voices rather than come back to a world that paid for silence. She thought of the way the stone had held her father's voice for eleven years, faithful as a grave, waiting for someone willing to listen.

She lifted her eyes to Okafor's smile—that tireless, sunlit, bottomless smile—and heard herself say:

"All right. I'll guide you in."

His smile bloomed wider, satisfied, victorious, and Yara smiled back, because she had grown up in the Trust's shadow and knew exactly how to wear one.

What Dane Okafor didn't know—what no one in that cheerful yellow camp knew—was that the route she intended to guide them by ran past the one person in the Kestrel Range who hated the Meridian Trust more than Yara did. And Petra Lindqvist had a rifle, a grudge, and nothing left in the world to lose.
