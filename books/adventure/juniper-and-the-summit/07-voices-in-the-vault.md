## 7. Voices in the Vault

They came at dusk, and the mountain told on them.

Yara heard it first as a wrongness in the river—a stutter in the water's voice, as if something upstream had swallowed a stone too large. Then the rotor-throb arrived, flattened by canyon walls into a pulse she felt in her back teeth. She killed her headlamp and pressed into the shadow of a fallen spruce, and through the black lattice of branches she watched the helicopter settle on the gravel bar below, its running lights painting the water red and green like some carnivorous Christmas.

Three figures dropped from the skids. Even at this distance, even in the dark, she knew them by their posture—the easy, unhurried confidence of men who had never once been refused anything. Dane Okafor stood last to leave the aircraft, and when his head turned toward the canyon wall where she crouched, Yara felt the smile reach her across two hundred meters of cold air. He couldn't have seen her. It didn't matter. Smiling was what they did instead of seeing.

Petra's hand closed on her shoulder—hard, warning—and pulled her back from the treeline.

"Not that way," Petra breathed. "There's another entrance. The survey found it. A fissure above the hot springs, where the hillside breathes."

"The hillside *breathes*."

"You'll see." Petra's face in the starlight was gray and set, the face of someone walking toward a thing she'd spent years outrunning. "And Yara—we go in quiet, we take nothing but sound, and we do not answer them. Whatever you hear in there." Her throat worked. "Whatever you hear—you don't answer it."

They climbed while the collectors made their camp below, cheerful fires blooming on the gravel bar like a second constellation. The fissure was exactly what Petra promised: a seam in the rock exhaling warm air that smelled of sulfur and old rain, wide enough for a body turned sideways, breathing against Yara's spine with a slow tidal rhythm. She went in feet-first, arms above her head, and the mountain swallowed her whole.

* * *

Inside, the dark was total and then it wasn't.

The walls themselves gave light—or rather, heat did, faint mineral shimmer along the stone conduits that ribbed the cavern ceiling like the insides of some enormous throat. Water ran somewhere below, and steam moved in slow banners, and the temperature climbed until Yara stripped off her outer shell and still sweated. But none of that was what stopped her.

It was the sound.

A murmur filled the vault—not water, not wind, but voices. Layered, overlapping, half-dissolved, rising out of the stone itself the way bubbles rise through amber. Snatches of radio chatter. A woman laughing, decades dead. A weather report for a day long past. A child counting to ten and losing her place and starting over. The cavern held all of it, patient as sediment, and now it was giving it back.

"It absorbs," Petra said quietly. Her voice sounded obscene here, too loud, too alive. "Geothermal convection through the conduits. The stone records pressure waves the way tape records magnetism. Everything loud enough, everything close enough to the vents—it stays." She wouldn't look at the walls. She looked at her boots. "I told you. Something that listens."

"How far back?"

"Voss thought sixty years. Maybe more." Petra finally raised her eyes, and there was terror in them, raw and unarmored. "Yara, I heard my brother in here. Eight years after we buried him. His voice, saying my name." Her jaw tightened. "I ran. I signed what they put in front of me and I ran, and I've been running ever since, so believe me when I tell you that whatever you're hoping to find in this place, it will cost you more than you have."

But Yara was already moving toward the sound.

She followed the voices deeper, over travertine terraces slick as wet glass, past pools that glowed a faint arterial orange. The murmuring thickened. And then, threading through the decades of strangers, she caught a cadence she knew.

Radio protocol has a rhythm. Four years of chasing storms with her father had taught her its music—the clipped call signs, the readbacks, the way he always said *copy* twice because once never felt like enough to him. She heard it now, ghost-faint, from a side gallery where the ceiling dipped low and the conduits ran thick as tree roots.

She crawled under the lip of stone before she let herself think about it.

"—Kestrel Base, Kestrel Base, this is Survey Three-One-Seven, Iversen, copy?"

Her whole body went cold in the furnace heat.

*Iversen.* Her father's call sign. Her father's name. The transmission was rough with static, thirty years compressed into mineral, but the voice was unmistakable—that low, careful baritone that had talked her to sleep through a hundred nights of tornado warnings over the radio when he was away and she was small. He sounded tired. He sounded frightened. He sounded exactly like himself.

"Say again your last, Three-One-Seven." Another voice, younger, strained. "You're breaking up."

"I'm saying the datum lines don't match." Her father's voice hardened, and Yara pressed her palm flat against the warm stone as if she could hold it still. "I've run the triangulation four times. The geothermal gradients Meridian filed with the ministry are fabricated. There's no stable field under the north basin—their maps show a reservoir that isn't there. They've been selling development leases on phantom heat. Someone at the Trust cooked the surveys, and I want it on record that I am *not* signing the certification."

Static. Long, ugly static. Then a third voice, smooth as poured cream, and Yara knew it instantly though she'd only heard it across negotiation tables and collection notices: Okafor's predecessor, or perhaps Okafor's teacher—the same smiling warmth stretched over steel.

"Dr. Iversen. Let's not be hasty. The Trust values accuracy as much as anyone—but consider what a correction would mean. Your team's funding. Your daughter's future. These mountains are unforgiving, and rescue is expensive. Accidents happen on surveys. Frequently, in fact."

"That sounds like a threat."

"It sounds like an observation. We're a lending collective, Doctor. We understand debt better than anyone. And you owe us a certification."

Silence on the recording—thirty-year-old silence, held in stone. When her father spoke again, his voice had gone very calm, the calm Yara had inherited without ever knowing its price.

"Then log this. I'm transmitting the true data on the emergency band tonight. Every listening station in the region will have it by morning. You can tell your principals the survey is finished—and so is their lie."

The transmission ended in a burst of interference, and beneath it, faintly, Yara could hear the next fragment already queued in the stone: her father again, hours later maybe, shouting into wind, calling a mayday that no one had ever played for her family, that the Trust had buried along with him—

Petra found her there on her knees in the steam, both hands flat on the rock, forehead nearly touching it, shaking.

"He told them," Yara said. Her voice came out in pieces. "He told them he'd expose them, and they—" She couldn't finish. The cavern finished for her, replaying the mayday, the wind, the rotors of a helicopter that never came, or came too late, or came to make sure.

"They killed him," she said. "For a map. For a *lie* on a map."

"Yes," Petra said simply. "That's what they do. That's what I helped them do—to Voss, to the others. They offered me my brother's debts cleared and a quiet life, and I took it, and I left them here." Her voice cracked down the middle. "They chose to stay, Yara. At the source. Because the source remembers, and they wanted the truth to have a witness even if no one ever came."

Yara lifted her head. Through the steam, the conduits pulsed their faint light, and the vault murmured on—her father's voice folded in among sixty years of the dead, waiting, patient as stone, for someone to finally listen.

Below them, faint through miles of rock, she heard the generators spin up. The collectors were moving.

And Yara Iversen, kneeling in the heat with her father's last words still ringing in her skull, understood suddenly, completely, what she had to do with them.
