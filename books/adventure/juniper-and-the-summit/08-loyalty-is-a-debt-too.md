## 8. Loyalty Is a Debt Too

The fire was small enough to hide in a cupped hand, and Petra built it anyway.

She told herself it was for warmth. The canyon walls held the night's cold like water in a basin, and Yara had gone down to the river to fill the bottles, her headlamp bobbing away into the dark like a firefly with somewhere better to be. But that wasn't why. Petra built the fire because she needed something to look at while she said what she'd been carrying for three years, and a flame was as good a confessor as any—it listened without judging, and it burned everything you fed it.

Her brother's voice was still in her ears.

*Petra. Peti. Come find me.*

Not really him. She knew that now, knew it the way you know a scar is only skin—the knowledge sat on top of the wound without ever closing it. The vault didn't hold ghosts. It held sound, absorbed into stone the way heat soaked into rock all afternoon and bled out all night, and what she'd heard in that cavern was a boy of nineteen replaying through mineral veins older than language. But grief doesn't care about geology. Grief heard *come find me* and answered, and she had run—up the shaft, out the mouth of the cave, into snow so bright it scoured her retinas—and she had kept running for three years, all the way into the arms of the only people willing to pay her not to stop.

Yara came back up the bank, bottles clinking, and crouched across the fire from her. The storm chaser's face was still wrecked from the vault. Petra recognized the expression because she'd worn it herself, alone, in the dark: the look of someone who has just learned that the dead can testify.

"You heard him," Yara said. Not a question. "Your father."

"He was mapping the Meridian corridor." Petra turned her hands toward the flames. They were shaking; she let them. "The Kestrel headwaters. Eleven years ago. The Trust wanted a route for their high-voltage line, and my father found—" She stopped. Started again. "He found that the surveys were wrong. That they'd always been wrong. The fault lines, the load ratings, the whole map the Trust used to justify the corridor—they'd drawn it from projections, not measurements, and the ground under it wouldn't hold what they planned to hang on it. He filed a dissent. He filed it twice."

"And then?"

"And then his survey team hit weather that no one had forecast, on a route no one should have approved, and the Trust paid out his life insurance before the search was even called off." Petra watched the fire eat a knot of pine, spitting, reluctant. "I was fourteen. I believed in weather for a long time. Weather is very convenient."

Yara said nothing. Above them the canyon rim cut the sky into a black blade, and somewhere upriver the vault breathed its faint geothermal warmth into the dark—a sound below hearing, like the earth murmuring in its sleep.

"I need to tell you what happened to us," Petra said. "All of it. Because after tomorrow there may not be an after, and because you deserve to know who you're standing next to."

* * *

"We reached the source on the ninth day," she said. "Voss led us up the same canyon you climbed. He'd found the surveyor's notes from my father's last expedition—the ones that were never filed, the ones that got him killed—and Voss was the kind of man who couldn't leave a question mark on a map. Six of us. Voss, Okonkwo, Reyes, the Halvorsen twins, me. We went in expecting a hot spring. Maybe a fumarole worth a footnote."

"The cavern."

"Stone conduits, branching like lungs. Geothermal vents that had been cycling air through the mountain for—God knows how long. And the stone remembers. Heat drives sound into it, and cold draws it back out. Every word spoken in those galleries over centuries, soaked into the rock and played back when the temperature swings right. We stood in the dark listening to avalanches that fell before anyone had a name for this valley. And then we started hearing voices."

Petra pulled her knees up, wrapped her arms around them. The fire had burned down to a red eye and she fed it another branch, needing the light.

"Reyes heard her mother. Okonkwo heard a man speaking a language his grandmother had died with. Voss—" she laughed, and it came out broken, "—Voss heard silence, which he said was the most interesting thing in the whole cave, because even the silence had texture. And I heard Mikkel. My brother. Drowned off Henningsvær when I was nineteen, body never recovered, and there he was in the stone saying my name in the exact cadence he used when he wanted me to come look at something he'd found on the shore."

"That's impossible."

"Yes," Petra agreed. "It's completely impossible. It happened anyway. You know it did. You heard your father."

Yara's jaw tightened. In the firelight her eyes were wet and furious at being wet.

"Voss understood what we'd found before any of us did," Petra went on. "Not the voices—he never cared about the voices—but the recordings. My father's last radio calls were made within range of these vents. His final transmission, the one where he named the falsified surveys, where he gave coordinates and readings and said, verbatim, *the corridor maps are fiction and the Trust knows it*—it's in the stone. It plays back every cold snap. The truth has been sitting in that mountain for eleven years, waiting for someone with a recorder."

"So you brought it out."

"No." The word came out flat, and she made herself look at Yara while she said the rest. "No. I sold it."

The fire cracked. Downriver, an owl asked its one question and got no answer.

"There was a satellite phone in our kit. Emergency use only. On the fourth morning, while the others were sleeping off the vault—none of us slept properly after it, we'd lie in our bags listening to each other breathe—I called the number I'd been given two years earlier by a man from the Meridian Trust who bought survey data. I told them where we were. I told them what the cavern held. And they offered me money to make sure it stayed there."

"You reported your own team missing."

"I reported us *dead*." Her voice cracked on it. "Weather event. Total loss. I gave them our transponder codes so they could spoof the wreckage, and I walked out barefoot so the story would hold—no tracks, no gear taken, a team swallowed by the range. The boots stayed lined up at basecamp because a survivor takes her boots. Everyone knows that. Everyone reads a scene the way they're taught to read it."

Yara had gone very still. When she spoke, her voice was low and careful, the way you talk near a trigger. "And the others? Voss, Reyes, the twins?"

"They knew." Petra's throat worked. "Okonkwo figured it out first—he saw the phone log. And instead of stopping me, they voted. All five of them. Voss said the vault was the only honest archive left, and if the world outside ran on purchased silence, then they would stay inside the honesty. They chose to stay at the source rather than come back and lie about it. Reyes kissed my forehead and told me not to freeze. The twins gave me their chocolate ration for the walk out, like I was the one going on the hard journey." She pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "They're probably still there. Listening to themselves. I've told myself for three years that they're dead, because dead is a thing I can carry, and *waiting* is not."

* * *

"They'll pay you too."

Petra said it plainly, because there was no other way left to say it. She took the folded contract from her inner pocket—the one she'd carried since the helicopter, the one Okafor had pressed on her with that smile that never reached anything—and laid it on the flat rock between them. The paper caught the firelight and glowed like something alive.

"Dane Okafor. You met his smile already. This is the same offer I took three years ago, updated for inflation. Your debt, erased. A number with enough zeros behind it that you never chase a storm for money again. All it costs is what it cost me: you walk out of these mountains and you say the vault is a legend, the recording is a forgery, and the survey team—including your father—died of weather." She pushed the contract an inch closer to Yara. "Take it."

"You're joking."

"I have never been more serious in my life." Petra leaned forward, and the firelight showed her own face reflected in Yara's eyes, and she hated how desperate she must look, how much like the smiling men. "Listen to me. You think broadcasting it will free you. I thought burying it would. Both of us were wrong in the same direction—the Trust doesn't end because of a recording. Recordings can be disputed, doctored, litigated into dust. The Trust ends things. It ended my father's dissent with a storm. It ended six people with a press release. If you put that transmission on an open band, Okafor won't send lawyers. He'll seal the canyon with everyone inside it, and the incident report will read *unauthorized salvage, tragic loss*, and your father's voice will be entombed under a hundred meters of rubble with the rest of the honesty in this world."

"And the alternative?" Yara's voice rose. "Sell it? Let them buy the truth twice?"

"The alternative is you live." Petra heard her own voice break and didn't bother repairing it. "You live, and you owe nothing, and someday—maybe ten years, maybe twenty—you find another way to hurt them. From outside. With your hands free. I'm begging you, Yara. Not for the Trust. God, no. For you. Because I know exactly what's in your chest right now, that white-hot certainty, and I know what it costs, because I've spent three years paying interest on mine."

Yara stared at the contract. Then at her. "You want absolution."

"I want company," Petra said. "That's worse, isn't it? I want to not be the only person alive who knows what that mountain holds and chose wrong. Take the buyout and we'll both be liars together, and maybe lying in pairs is almost like being human."

For a long moment the only sound was the river, patient as time, wearing the canyon deeper one grain at a time.

Then Yara picked up the contract—not to sign it. She held it by one corner, letting it dangle over the fire, and looked at Petra with an expression that was neither pity nor forgiveness but something harder than both: recognition.

"You're right about one thing," Yara said. "Okafor won't send lawyers."

Far up the canyon, muffled by miles of stone, came the whine of rotors.
