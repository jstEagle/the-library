## 9. The Source Seals

The vault was singing when the first charge went off.

Yara felt it through the soles of her boots before she heard it—a deep, tectonic shudder rolling up through the cavern floor, through the ribs of the mountain itself. The stone conduits overhead trembled, and the voices caught in them stuttered like a record skipping: her father's voice fracturing mid-word, *—coordinates are wrong, do you copy, the coordinates are*—

"No," she breathed, though there was no one to say it to. Petra had gone up the shaft twenty minutes ago with the survey team's old emergency transmitter cradled against her chest like something rescued from a fire.

Yara pressed her palm flat against the warm wall of the conduit. The stone hummed under her hand, alive with everything it had swallowed—the wind off the Kestrel headwaters in years no one living remembered, the crackle of a radio call from a man who had been dead for eleven years, the sound of boots on gravel that belonged to six people who had chosen to stay here rather than carry a lie downstream.

The second charge was closer. Dust sifted down through the bioluminescent haze, catching the pale green light like snow falling upward.

She should have been running. Instead she knelt beside Voss's equipment cache—the one the vanished team had left intact, packed and ready, as if they'd always known someone would come—and pulled out the backup recorder they'd built to duplicate the vault's output. It was the size of a bread loaf, heavy as sin, and it held everything. Every replayed hour. Her father's last transmission, clean and whole, copied straight from the memory of the stone.

*Coordinates are wrong.* She'd listened to it eleven times now. Each time she waited for it to hurt less. It never did.

Above her, faint but unmistakable, came the whine of a helicopter banking low over the canyon mouth.

* * *

She climbed the access shaft two rungs at a time, the recorder strapped to her back, its weight dragging at her shoulders like a passenger who didn't want to leave. The air grew colder as she rose, then warmer again near the surface, where the morning light fell in a slanted column through the fissure that served as the cavern's front door.

Petra was waiting at the top, crouched behind a shelf of fallen rock, the emergency transmitter open on the stone beside her. Her face was gray. Not afraid—Yara realized with a jolt—but resolved, the way weather looks before it breaks.

"They're setting charges along the canyon narrows," Petra said without preamble. "I counted four crates of shaped explosive. Okafor isn't sealing the source, Yara. He's burying the entire approach. No canyon, no trail, no reason for anyone to ever come looking."

"Then we're out of time." Yara shrugged the recorder off and set it beside the transmitter. "This is the survey band?"

"Emergency frequency 121.5. Every listening station between here and the coast monitors it automatically. Anything broadcast on this band gets logged, timestamped, archived." Petra's jaw worked. "It can't be unlogged. You understand what you're doing? Once this goes out, there's no version of your life where it stays quiet."

"That's the point."

"You could still take his deal." Petra said it fast, like pulling a splinter. "Okafor will honor it. He smiled at me for three years while I took their money; he'll smile at you too. Debt gone. Rig stays yours. You drive out of these mountains a free woman and nobody ever has to know what the river remembers."

Yara looked at her. This woman who had carried the truth like a stone in her boot for eleven years, limping on it, telling herself each step was the last.

"My father died mapping those mountains," Yara said quietly. "He died because he found out the surveys were false and wouldn't shut up about it. The Trust called it an accident. The Trust put a number on my life instead of a name on his grave." She touched the recorder. "You want to know what freedom costs? It costs exactly this. Everything else is just interest."

Somewhere below, muffled by a hundred meters of stone, the third charge detonated. The fissure above them spat grit. A crack ran up the canyon wall across from them with a sound like ice calving.

Petra exhaled slowly. Then she reached down and flipped the transmitter's power switch.

"Antenna's rigged to the old relay mast," she said. "Voss built it to survive anything short of a direct strike. Give me sixty seconds to establish carrier signal, then start feeding it the recordings."

* * *

They were forty seconds in when Dane Okafor stepped into the light.

He came around the bend of the canyon alone, hands visible, palms out, that smile fixed on his face like a mask bolted to a skull. Behind him, half-hidden in the shadow of the narrows, two more figures moved among the charge crates with unhurried competence.

"There she is," Okafor said warmly, as if greeting a niece at a wedding. "Yara Iversen. You know, I had such high hopes when you signed that salvage contract. Such a practical young woman. And yet here you are, at the top of a hole in the ground, about to do something very impractical."

"Here I am," Yara said. Her thumb rested on the transmitter's send key. The carrier tone was live—she could hear it, thin and steady, a heartbeat going out into the sky.

Okafor glanced at the equipment, and for just a moment the smile flickered—not into anger, but into something almost like disappointment, which was somehow worse. "That recording is eleven years old. It's the word of dead men and a cave. Do you know what a court does with evidence like that? Nothing. Do you know what a debt ledger does with sentiment? Also nothing." He spread his hands. "Walk away. Right now. I'll tear up your contract myself, on camera, with witnesses. You'll owe nothing. Isn't that what you've wanted since the day you signed?"

The lie of it was beautiful. That was the thing about the Meridian Trust—they never offered you anything ugly. Every trap they built looked exactly like the door you'd been praying for.

"I did want it," Yara said. "Right up until I heard his voice."

Below them, deep in the mountain, the fourth charge fired.

The world lurched. The canyon narrows behind Okafor folded inward with a roar like the throat of the earth clearing itself, and the column of dust that rose was less smoke than solid displaced stone, a brown pillar climbing toward the clouds. The relay mast swayed. The carrier tone warbled and held.

Okafor turned to look at the collapse, and in that half-second of turned attention, Petra moved.

Yara would remember it forever, the way you remember lightning: Petra Lindqvist, comms officer, survivor, traitor, walking past her without a word, picking up Voss's flare pistol from the equipment cache, and firing it directly into the nearest crate of shaped charges.

"No—" Okafor started.

The crate went up in a white bloom. The blast wave hit them all like a fist; Yara was thrown against the transmitter, ear ringing, vision full of sparks. When she could see again, one of Okafor's crew was down, the other scrambling for cover, and Okafor himself stood in the drifting haze with his smile finally, finally gone—his face naked underneath, and what was underneath was fear.

"Petra," he said. "Petra, think about what you're—"

"I thought about it for eleven years," Petra shouted over the rumble of the settling canyon. "You were right, Dane. It was easier. It was so much easier. And every night I heard my brother asking me why I wasn't there, and I told myself it was grief." She leveled the flare pistol at him. "It wasn't grief. It was testimony."

"Yara!" Petra didn't look back. "Send it! Send all of it!"

Yara pressed the key.

* * *

The recording went out into the morning like water finding its level.

Her father's voice first—she'd chosen that deliberately, made the first thirty seconds his, so that whatever station picked it up, whoever was on shift drinking bad coffee in some listening post in the flats, the first thing they logged was a dead man saying *the coordinates are wrong, the Trust's maps are wrong, check the headwater gauges, they falsified the—* and then the rest, hours of it, Voss's measured field reports, the team's final decision spoken aloud in the vault's green light, the whole buried confession of a mountain that never forgot.

Every station in the region would log it. Timestamped. Archived. Un-erasable. Somewhere, machines were writing her father's name back into the record.

The canyon was coming down around them. Not all at once—in great reluctant sections, the way a giant might lower itself to sleep. The relay mast groaned and bent but held, its antenna still kissing the sky, still feeding the signal out even as the rock beneath it sheared away. Yara fed the transmitter until the cache ran dry, until the recorder clicked empty, until there was nothing left to give.

Then Petra grabbed her arm. "The shaft. Now. The vault's the only shelter—"

They went down together, hand over hand, as the fissure above them narrowed, widened, screamed. Grit became gravel became stones the size of fists. Yara felt the mountain deciding, felt the whole weight of the Kestrel Range leaning into its choice, and in the last meter before the shaft mouth sealed she looked up and saw blue sky shrink to a coin, to a slit, to a thread—

—to nothing.

Darkness. Total, pressurized, absolute. The silence afterward rang louder than the collapse.

They were entombed with the voices. Yara pressed her back against the warm conduit wall and felt it humming beneath her shoulder blades, steady as a pulse, and understood with a clarity that felt almost gentle: the place that held the truth had held it long enough. It had given the truth to her, and now it was paying for the transaction with its own body. Somewhere above, tons of stone settled into permanence over the singing conduits, and the recordings that remained here would remain unheard, sealed like a heart inside a chest.

But not erased. Never erased. Just quiet now. Off duty. Its work done.

Petra was laughing—or crying, or both, breath hitching in the dark. "We're alive," she kept saying. "We're actually alive."

"For now," Yara said. Her ears were bleeding; her hands were shaking so hard she had to lock them together. But somewhere out beyond all that stone, on a frequency every listening station in the region was contractually obligated to monitor, her father's voice was still traveling—outward, outward, at the speed of light, unstoppable now as weather.

Then, through the dark, through the ringing, through the settling dust: a sound neither of them expected.

Knocking. Rhythmic. Deliberate. Coming not from the sealed shaft above, but from deeper in the mountain—from a direction where, as far as either of them knew, there was nothing left to make it.

Petra went rigid beside her. "Tell me you heard that."

Yara stared into the blackness, her heart hammering against the hum of the stone.

Someone else was in the vault.
