## 10. Downstream

The letter from the Meridian Trust arrived on a Tuesday, folded into a cheerful yellow envelope with a smiley-face sticker on the flap—the same stationery they'd used for every collection notice, every polite threat, every reminder that my rig, my truck, my life's whole architecture of debt belonged to them. I stood in the gravel lot outside the post office in Meridian Falls and held it for a long time before I opened it, because I had learned that everything the Trust touched came wrapped in warmth, and warmth was the most expensive thing they sold.

Inside was a single page. *We are pleased to inform you,* it began, in the same rounded font as always, *that in light of ongoing regulatory review, all outstanding obligations associated with account 4471-I have been voided effective immediately.* Voided. Not paid. Not forgiven—forgiveness would have required them to admit the debt was real, and the debt had never been real; it had been a leash dressed up as arithmetic. The word they chose was *voided*, and beneath it, smaller, almost shy: *The Meridian Trust thanks you for your continued partnership.*

I laughed. Right there in the parking lot, alone, holding a piece of paper that weighed nothing at all, I laughed until my ribs hurt and an old man walking his dog gave me a wide berth. Because that was the joke, wasn't it. They hadn't paid me off. They hadn't bought my silence—I'd spent their silence money on broadcast towers and bandwidth instead. The scandal had done what seven years of payments never could: it had made me worthless to them. A debtor under investigation is a liability. A witness is worse. They didn't erase my account out of kindness. They erased it because keeping it open meant keeping my name attached to theirs, and their name was currently burning down in hearings, subpoenas, and the slow, merciless arithmetic of federal auditors who did not smile.

Seven years of my life, priced at zero by the people who'd set the price.

I folded the letter into quarters and put it in the glove box of the rig, next to my father's old road atlas, its spine cracked white at the page for the Kestrel Range. Then I sat in the driver's seat with my hands on the wheel and let the morning happen around me—the smell of diesel and wet sagebrush, a magpie arguing with something invisible, the mountains north of town still wearing their winter like a coat they hadn't decided to take off.

I owed nothing. The thought kept arriving and kept refusing to land, like a bird circling a perch it didn't trust.

* * *

Petra called on Thursday. Her voice came through the truck's speaker thin and stripped, all the hostility sanded off it, which somehow made her harder to listen to than when she'd been pointing a rifle at me across a riverbank.

"They're offering immunity," she said. "For testimony. Full cooperation, full disclosure." A pause long enough that I could hear her breathing, hear the room around her—hard walls, somewhere institutional. "I'm going to take it."

"Good," I said.

"I told them about the others. About how they stayed." Another pause. "Yara, they stayed because the vault played them back to themselves, and they couldn't leave knowing what leaving would mean. Ansel wrote it in the diary—you read it. *Something that listens.* He meant it kindly. That was the worst part. He meant it kindly."

"I know."

"The canyon's sealed. Okafor's crew saw to that before anyone could stop them. There's a memorial going up at the trailhead—some bronze plaque, some names." Her voice caught, just barely, on the word *names*. "It'll say they died in service. It won't say they chose to stay. I don't know if that's a lie or not anymore."

"Tell the truth at the hearing," I said. "Let the plaque be wrong. Plaques are always wrong. It's the recordings that matter."

She was quiet for a while. When she spoke again, her voice had gone small in a way I hadn't heard from her, not even in the cavern, not even with the dead singing out of the stone. "My brother's voice. In there. You heard yours too."

"Yeah."

"Do you ever wish you hadn't?"

I looked out the windshield at the Kestrels, blue and distant and closed. "No," I said, and found that it was true. "I wished a lot of things hadn't happened. But not that."

She said she'd testify anyway, and hung up, and I sat with the engine ticking and understood that we would probably never speak again, and that this was its own kind of settlement—two survivors who had circled each other in the high country finally walking away in opposite directions, each carrying half the weight.

* * *

The drive south took two days. I took the long way, not because I needed to but because for the first time in seven years I could—no collection windows, no check-in calls from men whose smiles I could hear through the phone, no route planned around which counties had friendly courts. I stopped where I wanted. I ate bad diner food at midnight because I felt like it. In a motel outside Redstone I slept eleven hours and woke without the low hum of dread that had lived under my sternum so long I'd mistaken it for a heartbeat.

The cemetery was on a hill above a town so small it didn't appear in my father's atlas, only in mine. His stone was granite gone soft-edged with weather, the letters shallow now, the dates more legible than the name. I'd been here twice since the funeral—once too numb to feel anything, once too angry to stay—and both times I'd talked at him the way you talk at a locked door. This time I sat down in the grass beside him, cross-legged, the way I used to sit beside him on the tailgate of his survey truck while he explained contour lines to me with a stick in the dirt.

"Hey, Dad," I said.

A wind came up the hill and moved through the pines, and I let it say whatever it wanted first.

"You should see the news feeds. It's ugly and it's everywhere. The Trust, the falsified surveys, the whole rotten ledger of it. Your maps were right. Every line you drew was right, and they knew it, and they buried it because your honesty was worth more than their whole portfolio." I picked at the grass. "They killed you for being accurate. Isn't that the stupidest sentence in the world."

The pines again. Somewhere below, a dog barked twice and quit.

"I heard you," I said, and my voice went sideways on the words, and I let it. "In the vault. Your last call. I heard the whole thing—not the version they logged, the real one. You sounded calm. You sounded like yourself. You told them what you'd found and you told them you weren't going to change a single number, and then—" I stopped. Breathed. "Then the recording does what recordings do. But Dad, before that, you said something I never knew. You said, *Tell Yara the storms aren't running from anything. Tell her they're going toward.*"

I hadn't known he'd said that. I hadn't known anyone had heard it. For twenty years I'd built myself out of the shape of a man who drove into weather, and here was the voice itself, pulled out of forty-year-old stone, telling me I'd gotten him right.

"So that's what I'm doing," I said. "Going toward. Not for them. Not against them anymore either—they're finished, or they will be, and it turns out revenge is just another kind of debt and I'm done owing things." I put my palm flat on the granite, cold even in the June sun. "I'm doing it because it's mine. The chasing. It was always mine. You just got to it first."

I stayed until the light went long and gold, until the shadows of the stones lay across the hill like survey lines, all pointing the same direction, all true.

* * *

The storm was building over the basin east of the range by the time I crested the pass three days later—a big, organized cell with a rotating core, the kind of supercell that made civilians pull over and made me lean forward in my seat. The sky over it was green-black and enormous, stacked like architecture, and lightning moved inside it the way thoughts move inside a mind deciding something.

I pulled onto the shoulder at the overlook and got out. The air tasted of rain and ozone and dust from a hundred miles away. Wind shoved my hair back and pressed my shirt flat against me, and the whole atmosphere roared its low continuous roar, the sound of ten thousand tons of air figuring out which way to turn.

On the passenger seat, my phone buzzed. A message from a regional forecasting group I'd started consulting for—legitimate work, paid work, work nobody smiled about. *Cell's trending northeast. If you're positioned, now's the window.*

I looked at the storm. It looked back, the way the wilderness in the Kestrels had looked back, the way the vault had listened—but this thing wasn't listening and it wasn't watching. It was simply going, magnificent and indifferent, toward wherever it was going, and I could go with it or not, and either way the world would keep turning, and either way I would still be the woman who had walked into a mountain carrying nothing and walked out carrying the truth.

That was the difference. That was the whole difference. Everything I chased now, I chased free.

I climbed back into the rig. My hands were steady on the wheel. Outside, the first fat drops hit the windshield, one, two, then a drumming rush of them, and the storm leaned east and I turned east after it, throttle open, wipers slapping, the atlas in the glove box falling open on its own to a blank page at the back—the one with no roads drawn on it yet.

There was room on that page. There was room for anything.

Ahead, the lightning came down like a signature, and I signed after it.
