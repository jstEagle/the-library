## 1. Miles, Not Years

Cyrus Nystrom measured his life in miles, not years.

Years were unreliable things. They accumulated like snow on a cornice — silently, deceptively, until the whole mass of them threatened to slide and bury you. But a mile was honest. A mile was earned one bootfall at a time, paid for in sweat and blister and the particular ache that lived behind the knees after a thousand meters of gain. A mile could be trusted.

He had walked eleven thousand of them since the last time he'd spoken to his father longer than four minutes. He knew the number the way other men knew their golf handicap or their blood pressure. Eleven thousand, two hundred and six, to be exact, because he kept the tally in the back of his field notebook, small and neat, in a column he never showed anyone. Every contract, every survey line, every fire road and game trail and unnamed drainage between here and the ice fields of the northern ranges — logged, dated, totaled. It was not sentiment. It was accounting. Distance was the only currency he'd ever managed to keep separate from the family name.

The northern ranges in late summer smelled like stone warming in the sun and the green rot of meltwater meadows, and Cyrus loved them precisely because they didn't know who he was. To the marmots whistling from the talus and the ravens riding the thermals above the col, he was nothing but another two-legged animal moving through their country with a GPS unit and a roll of flagging tape. That was the whole point of this work. Out here, *Nystrom* meant nothing. The mountains had never read a monograph. The scree did not care about the Kestrel controversy or the twenty-year-old scandal that had split a discipline down the middle like a struck geode. The trail only asked whether you could read it, and Cyrus could read it better than anyone alive — a fact he intended to take to his grave, along with everything else.

"Yo, Nystr—" Rhea caught herself mid-syllable, the way she always did, and course-corrected with the ease of long practice. "Ossman. You got a minute?"

*Ossman.* His mother's middle name, repurposed as a surname, worn like a jacket turned inside out so the label didn't show. Rhea Okonkwo ran the crew the way she ran everything: efficiently, loyally, and with just enough curiosity to be dangerous. She knew there was something under the alias. She'd stopped asking three seasons ago, which was one of the reasons Cyrus kept coming back to her crews.

He jogged up the switchback to where she stood at the survey stake, her tablet glowing against the backdrop of the valley — a broad U-shaped trough of spruce and glacial till, the Kestrel River running silver through its throat. They were finishing a hydrology corridor assessment, ten days of tedium measuring flow rates and slope stability for a provincial water authority. Honest work. Forgettable work. Exactly the kind of work Cyrus had built his life out of, the way a man builds a wall out of stones too heavy for anyone to want to steal.

"What's up?"

"Client's been moved up." She handed him the tablet without looking at him, which was itself unusual; Rhea looked at everyone when she talked, watched their faces the way she watched weather. "New contract came through last night. Bigger money. We're being pulled off the corridor job."

Cyrus scrolled. The client name field held a holding company — *Meridian Heritage Group LLC* — which told him nothing except that someone wanted to not be found, and he felt the first cold thread of unease unspool somewhere beneath his sternum, the way you feel a storm front before you see it. "Heritage group. So, archaeology?"

"Salvage and recovery dig. Remote site." She paused. "High country. River source."

The words went into him slowly, like cold water into a boot. "Which river."

Rhea finally looked at him. "Kestrel."

The name landed in the valley between them and echoed off walls that hadn't heard it spoken aloud in twenty years. Somewhere below them the actual river ran on, indifferent, carrying snowmelt down from a place that appeared on no map older than two decades — scrubbed, redacted, sealed by court order and by something heavier than court orders. Cyrus had spent five years making sure he never heard that word in a professional context. He'd turned down work. He'd walked off a job in Alaska when a client mentioned it as a joke. And now it had come up the mountain to find him anyway, wearing a holding company's name like a false beard.

"That's not possible," he said. His voice came out flat, which took effort. "That valley's closed. Protected status. There's no permit process. There's no—"

"There's an advance team already up there." Rhea said it quietly, and now he understood why she hadn't been looking at him. "Sent ahead by the client. Two weeks ago. Before we even signed anything."

The cold thread became a wire, and the wire pulled tight. An advance team. On the Kestrel. Without permits, without oversight, without anyone who knew that route well enough to understand what the river did to people who treated it casually. Cyrus thought of the upper gorge — the slot canyons where the water rose eight feet in an afternoon if it rained on the divide, the scree slopes that moved like breathing things, and above all of it, at the top of everything, the glacier. The glacier that calved into the source lake every August like clockwork, closing the ice bridge on the east shore, sealing the upper valley until winter.

It was July. The window was open. Barely.

"Who is this guy?" Cyrus asked. "Behind Meridian. Who actually signs the checks?"

Rhea's jaw worked. She was deciding something, and he watched her decide it, and he understood with a sinking, vertiginous certainty what she was going to say before she said it, the way you understand a rockfall has started before the first stone moves.

"He's flying in today," she said. "To brief us personally. He insisted." She exhaled. "It's Vale, Ossman. Marcus Vale."

* * *

The helicopter came up the valley at four in the afternoon, beating the air into submission, and Cyrus stood apart from the crew with his arms crossed and watched it land on the gravel bar like a black beetle settling on a white page. He had seen photographs of Marcus Vale for thirty years — in his mother's office, in journals, in the background of conference photos where Vale always stood slightly apart, silver-haired even in his forties, immaculate even at base camp, smiling the smile of a man who had never once doubted himself in public.

In person he was smaller. That was the first thing. Smaller and older, with a climber's lean frame gone wiry and a face weathered into deep creases, and eyes that swept the crew the way a man checks his instruments — quickly, comprehensively, missing nothing. When those eyes reached Cyrus, they stopped. Held. Something flickered there, some recognition that had nothing to do with the alias, and Cyrus felt the past arrive the way weather arrives in the high country: not gradually, but all at once, a wall of it, dropping visibility to zero.

*Nystrom,* the look said. *I know exactly whose son you are.*

Vale shook hands down the line of the crew. When he reached Cyrus, his grip was dry and strong and lasted half a second too long.

"Mr. Ossman," Vale said. "I've heard good things. They tell me you're the best trail-finder working today." A pause, delicate as a scalpel. "They tell me you learned your craft from the very best."

"I learned it from maps," Cyrus said.

"Of course." Vale smiled. "Maps."

He briefed them on the gravel bar with the river talking behind him, spreading printed sheets on a folding table while the crew gathered around — site plans, logistics schedules, a compensation figure that made Tomas, the youngest of them, whistle low under his breath. Triple the corridor rate. Hazard pay on top. A completion bonus that would clear Rhea's debts and buy the crew a full winter of no work at all.

"The site," Vale said, "is a cultural deposit near the Kestrel headwaters. Pre-contact material. Exceptional preservation. My organization holds the recovery rights." He said it smoothly, and Cyrus watched the crew nod along, and he thought: *he's lying, and he's good at it, and none of them can hear it because they don't know what that valley cost.*

Then Vale distributed the contracts, and Cyrus read his twice, because reading carefully was the only weapon he'd brought to this conversation. Buried in clause fourteen, in language so dense it might have been poured rather than written: nondisclosure binding the crew to silence regarding the site's history. Not the location — the *history*. All references to prior expeditions, prior findings, prior personnel. Sealed. In perpetuity. With penalties that would bankrupt any of them twice over.

Prior expeditions. Prior personnel. As if the valley's dead were a liability to be managed.

"You have a question, Mr. Ossman?" Vale asked, without looking up.

"Just admiring the legal work."

"It's thorough." Vale rolled the maps closed. "We move in nine days. The window closes fast this time of year, and I intend to be standing at the source when it does." He folded his hands and looked downriver, toward the closed country, and for a moment — just a moment — the polish dropped and something rawer showed underneath, something that looked almost like hunger, or grief, or the two of them grown together so long they'd become the same thing. "Twenty years I've waited to finish this work properly. Nothing will stop me. I hope you'll all feel the same."

The crew dispersed to pack. Rhea lingered, watching Cyrus watch Vale's helicopter lift off and shrink upvalley, a black speck swallowed by the white shoulders of the range.

"We don't have to take it," she said.

"Yes, we do." Cyrus heard himself say it and hated how steady it sounded, because inside he was anything but steady. Inside, eleven thousand miles of careful distance were collapsing at once, and beneath them, exposed like bones in a melting snowbank, lay everything he'd buried: a brother's name, a mother's sealed journal, a valley that had eaten one Nystrom and spat out the rest of them broken. "There's already a team up there. Unpermitted. Unqualified. If the calving comes early and that route closes with people on the wrong side of it—"

He stopped. Rhea waited.

"If I say no," he finished quietly, "and somebody dies up there because nobody who knows that river would go, then it's mine. Whatever else is true, that part's mine."

Rhea studied him for a long moment. "You know who he's really racing, don't you? Up there. Twenty years waiting." She shook her head slowly. "This isn't archaeology, Ossman. This is a grudge with a budget."

She walked back toward camp, and Cyrus stayed on the gravel bar as the light went long and gold and the Kestrel ran cold and quick at his boots, hurrying down out of the forbidden country as if it, too, wanted to put the place behind it.

That night he sat in his tent with his headlamp off and dialed the number he had not dialed in five years. It rang once. Twice. On the third ring, a voice answered — older, rougher, but unmistakable, a voice that had narrated his entire childhood like a documentary.

"Cyrus." Dr. Ingrid Nystrom did not sound surprised. She sounded like a woman who had been sitting beside a phone for twenty years, waiting for exactly this call. "You've heard about Vale."

Not a question. The past, arriving like weather. Cyrus closed his eyes, and outside the tent fabric the wind came down off the glacier fields carrying the smell of ice, and somewhere far upriver, in a valley that had no business being open, men were already walking toward the source.

"How much do you know?" he asked.

His father was quiet for a moment. When she spoke again, her voice had changed — lower, urgent, stripped of everything except the truth.

"Everything," Ingrid Nystrom said. "And Cyrus — whatever Vale told you, whatever he's paying you — you cannot let him reach that spring. Do you hear me? Some things are buried for a reason."

The line went dead, and Cyrus sat alone in the dark with the river's voice in his ears, counting the miles between himself and the source.
