## 2. The Contract with Teeth

The contract arrived in a leather folio heavier than any survey agreement Cyrus had ever held, and it smelled of money the way old churches smell of incense — deliberately, expensively.

They met in the crew's staging tent at the edge of Kestrel Junction, a mud-flat town that existed only because the river did: two outfitters, a helipad worn to bare gravel, a bar with a stuffed wolverine above the till. Rain ticked against the canvas overhead. Rhea sat across the folding table with her arms crossed, and beside her, filling more space than his body strictly required, was Marcus Vale.

Cyrus had seen him before only in photographs — the ones his mother kept in a drawer she thought no one knew about, next to the ones she'd burned. In person Vale was taller than the pictures, silver at the temples, with the particular stillness of a man who had spent decades being waited on by landscapes. He wore field clothes that had never known a real field. His hands, though — those were working hands, scarred at the knuckles. That was the detail that unsettled Cyrus most. A man who dressed like a boardroom but climbed like a laborer.

"Nystrom," Vale said.

Not *Mr.* Not *your father's boy.* Just the name, laid on the table like a card turned face up.

"Vale." Cyrus didn't sit. "I'm here for the route briefing. The Kestrel headwaters survey. That's all."

"Of course." Vale smiled as if they were both in on something. He opened the folio. "Let's talk about what you're actually being paid for."

* * *

The numbers were obscene. Triple the standard rate for a high-altitude route assessment, plus hazard multipliers, plus a completion bonus that would let Rhea replace the crew's aging radio array, pay off the helicopter lease, and still winter comfortably. Cyrus watched Rhea's face as she read — the careful blankness of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't want anyone else to see her doing. Rhea Okafor had mortgaged her gear shed to keep the crew solvent through two dead seasons. Everyone at this table knew it. Nobody said it.

"Triple," Rhea said flatly. "For a route walk."

"For a route walk under conditions of discretion." Vale slid a single page free of the folio and turned it toward them. "Clause fourteen."

Cyrus read it twice. It was elegant, the way traps are elegant. The crew would be bound to silence not merely about the dig's findings but about the site's *history* — no discussion, publication, or disclosure of prior expeditions, prior claims, or prior incidents connected to the Kestrel source, in perpetuity. Breach triggered repayment of all fees plus damages.

"Prior incidents," Cyrus said. His voice came out level, which surprised him. "That's a strange phrase for a contract about rocks."

"It's a standard confidentiality instrument."

"It's a gag order about something specific." He tapped the page. "You don't write 'prior incidents' into a clause unless there's an incident you're buying silence about. Which one?"

Vale regarded him for a long moment. Outside, the rain thickened; someone's generator coughed and caught. When Vale finally spoke, his tone had shifted — warmer, almost confiding, which was worse.

"Dr. Nystrom ran an expedition up that valley twenty years ago. It ended badly. She sealed her notes, pulled every map from circulation, and has spent two decades pretending the source doesn't exist. I'm asking your crew not to gossip about old grief while they do honest work. Is that so monstrous?"

"You know it isn't the gossip I'm worried about."

"No," Vale agreed softly. "I imagine it isn't."

Rhea cut in before it could become a duel. "Clause fourteen aside — what exactly does the advance team need from us? You said route assessment. You've already got people up there?"

Something flickered across Vale's face — fast, quickly smoothed, but Cyrus caught it. The flicker of a man whose script had been jumped ahead of.

"A small reconnaissance party. Logistical scouting. They'll meet us at the lower gorge."

"When did they go up?"

"Last week."

"Last week." Rhea leaned back. "Before the calving forecast updated. Before the warm front. Marcus, the ice bridge window may close early this year. If your people are already above the gorge—"

"They're experienced men."

"Experienced men die on that bridge too," Cyrus said. "I've pulled bodies out of crevasses where the experienced men were. Experience just means they knew better when it happened."

The tent went quiet. Vale looked at him then without the smile, and for one unguarded second Cyrus saw what lived underneath: not arrogance but hunger, raw and patient, the look of a man who had wanted one thing for thirty years and could taste it now through the canvas walls, upriver, under ice.

"Then it's fortunate," Vale said, "that I'm hiring the best trail-finder in the northern ranges. Your mother always said you read water better than she did. She said it bitterly, mind you, but she said it."

"I'm not taking the job."

"Cyrus." Rhea's voice, low.

"I'm not." He straightened. "Find another router. There are three names in the Junction book who'd take triple pay to walk into a glacier's mouth, and good luck to them."

He was at the tent flap when Vale spoke again, unhurried, terrible in its mildness.

"Four men, Mr. Nystrom. My advance team. No permits filed — I'll be honest with you since you're leaving anyway. No satellite check-in for six days. And the last message they sent mentioned a meltwater channel running where their map showed solid ice." A pause, precisely measured. "Their map is Halloran's map. From 1987. I sent them up with a forty-year-old map because it's the only one that exists, and because the man who could correct it was hiding behind a false surname in the Junction registry."

Cyrus stopped with his hand on the flap.

"Yes," Vale said behind him. "I knew who you were before I walked in. I've known for five years. You think a man can bury a name like yours? You sign your field reports *C. Marsh*, and you hold your pen the way Ingrid holds hers, and you read scree like scripture. I could pick you out of a crowd of a thousand guides."

The rain filled the silence. Cyrus stood very still, and the past came up around him like water rising in a shaft — Anders's laugh, the sealed drawer, the word *Kestrel* spoken aloud for the first time in twenty years, and how it sounded exactly like it had sounded the night the satellite phone rang and rang and finally his mother answered it and never really spoke again.

"If I say no," he heard himself ask, "you'll send someone else up after them anyway."

"I'll send myself."

"And if they're dead when you get there, that's on me?"

"That," Vale said, "is entirely your construction. I'm merely noting the arithmetic."

* * *

He walked the riverbank until dark. The Kestrel ran brown and quick with snowmelt, shouldering past boulders, impatient, going somewhere it remembered. He'd guided forty rivers in five years and never once this one, had rerouted contracts, eaten cancellation fees, invented excuses — anything to keep the name of that water out of his mouth. Now he stood on its bank like a man visiting a grave he'd been avoiding, which, in every way that mattered, he was.

Rhea found him at the last light, sitting on a drift log with his boots in the shallows.

"Triple pay," she said, sitting down without invitation. "Tomas called his daughter tonight. Told her we might make rent and tuition both off one season. He cried a little. Don't tell him I told you."

"Rhea."

"I know what you're going to say. I can see clause fourteen written on your face." She picked up a flat stone, weighed it. "So don't take the job for the money. Take it because four men are up that valley with a map older than I am, and you're the only person in this hemisphere who knows that route well enough to bring them down alive. Or don't take it, and spend the rest of your life measuring miles instead of years, and call that living."

She skipped the stone. It went four times, which on brown water was luck, and they both pretended otherwise.

"Your mother swore off that valley," Rhea added quietly. "I read the file when Vale's lawyers sent the site history. She didn't just seal it — she burned bridges behind her. Permits revoked, access covenants, the whole architecture. A woman doesn't build a wall like that over a bad season. She builds it over a body."

Cyrus said nothing. The river said enough.

* * *

He signed at nine the next morning, in the outfitter's back room, with the stuffed wolverine watching and the rain gone soft as breath against the windows. He read every clause first, all nineteen pages, and made Vale strike one sub-section — the indemnity that would have made the crew liable for Vale's own advance team — and Vale struck it without argument, which told Cyrus the old man wanted him badly enough to bleed a little, and that frightened him more than anything else so far.

When it was done, Vale shook his hand. His grip was dry and strong and brief.

"The team launches Thursday," Vale said. "High water or low, we go up together."

"One condition of mine. Not in the contract." Cyrus held his eyes. "Anyone on my rope does what I say on that route. Including you. Including when it means turning back."

Vale smiled the boardroom smile, but the hunger underneath it had not gone anywhere. "Agreed," he said. "Though I'd caution you, Mr. Nystrom — the source doesn't turn back for anyone. I learned that from your mother, a long time ago."

Thursday was six days out. That night Cyrus unpacked the journal he had stolen from his father's office five years ago — Ingrid's handwriting, twenty years old, the spine cracked at the same three pages — and set it on the outfitter's bunk beneath the lamp, and did not open it, because he knew that once he did, he would read it the way a trail reads a man: all the way to the end.

Outside, the Kestrel ran loud in the dark, going up-country, going home.
