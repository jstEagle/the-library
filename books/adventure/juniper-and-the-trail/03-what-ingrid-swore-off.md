## 3. What Ingrid Swore Off

Cyrus didn't sleep in Vale's camp. He told Rhea he was checking the cache lines, and she let him go, because Rhea understood that some men need to walk their worry out through their boots.

He walked until the tents were fireflies behind him, then farther, up a bench of granite above the river where the Kestrel ran loud enough to drown his own thoughts. It didn't work. The river had his brother's voice in it tonight.

From the inner pocket of his pack — the pocket he'd sewn shut years ago and unsewn just as many times — he drew out the journal.

It was a bad binding, cheap canvas gone soft as cloth from handling, corners furred like an animal's ear. *Kestrel Expedition — Field Record — I. Nystrom.* Twenty-two years old. He'd stolen it from her office at nineteen, slid it out from under a monograph on Andean irrigation systems while she stood in the hallway talking to a donor, and he had never once given it back. She had never once asked. That was the shape of their whole estrangement: two people pretending not to notice what the other had taken.

He sat on the cold stone with his back against a boulder and opened it to the first page, because he always did, because there was no way into this book except through the beginning.

*June 3rd. We are nine days out from the last resupply and Anders has already named every marmot on the glacier. He wants to call the big one near Camp II "Professor," which Halloran says is flattery and I say is slander. Cyrus is four months old today. Ingrid, you sentimental fool, you brought his photograph and you look at it more than your maps.*

His father's handwriting changed over the pages — brisk and slanted early on, growing tighter as the summer went, as though the pen itself were climbing toward something.

He read the way other men prayed: without expecting answers, only proximity.

* * *

The journal again, later that night, by headlamp in his tent while the crew slept around him like cargo:

*July 11th. Halloran is certain the source lies under the eastern serac field. I am not certain of anything except that he is wrong about the route and right about the prize, which is the most dangerous combination in a man. Anders caught him moving survey markers yesterday. Halloran laughed and said the mountain doesn't grade your homework, Dr. Nystrom. I did not laugh.*

Cyrus had read that entry a hundred times and it had meant nothing to him at nineteen. Now, with Vale's advance-team markers fresh in his memory — cairns stacked wrong, flagging tape in colors that didn't match any permit protocol — the sentence landed like a boot on ice. History didn't repeat itself, his mother used to say. It recruited.

He turned pages. The entries shortened. The handwriting tightened further, letters crowded together like people pressing back from a ledge.

*August 2nd. The bridge below the source is rotten. I have said we wait for cold. Halloran says the season is closing. Anders says he'll scout it light. I said no.*

*I said no.*

That was all. Two words alone on the line, written and then struck through once, hard enough to tear the paper, and then left standing anyway. Cyrus put his palm flat over them. The paper was cool and slightly damp with river air, and underneath his hand he could feel the ghost of the pen's pressure, twenty-two years old, his mother's whole body pressed into two words.

He had been four months old when Anders died. He had grown up inside the silence his mother built around that August like a wall of river stones — beautiful, deliberate, load-bearing. He knew the facts the way the crew knew weather reports: crevasse collapse on the upper Kestrel, one fatality, expedition terminated, site sealed. What he knew of his brother came from a shoebox: a photograph of a boy of nineteen grinning on a glacier with a marmot-shaped shadow at his feet, a Boy Scout compass with a cracked bezel, a letter home that ended *tell Cy I'll teach him to read water when I'm back.*

Nobody had ever taught him. He'd taught himself, out of spite, and become better at it than anyone, and hated that it was the one inheritance he couldn't refuse.

* * *

Sleep took him finally near dawn, and the journal dreamed for him, which is to say the past arrived wearing its own voice:

*August 4th. We went up.*

*Halloran pushed. I let myself be pushed, God forgive me, because I wanted the source more than I wanted to be right about the ice. Anders scouted the bridge. It held for him. It held for me. It held for six men and then it held for the seventh, and the seventh was my son, and the mountain does not grade your homework.*

*The collapse took the whole shelf. They found the compass three days later downstream. I have ordered the valley sealed. Coordinates destroyed, permits burned, the access route collapsed behind us by our own hands. No one returns to the Kestrel Source. Not for treasure. Not for truth. Not for me.*

*Anders believed the source should belong to everyone or no one. I am making it no one. It is the only monument I know how to build him.*

Cyrus woke with the headlamp still burning and the journal open on his chest, and outside the tent the glacier spoke — a long, deep groan rolling down the valley like a door being dragged open somewhere underground.

* * *

In the morning he went looking for Vale and found him at the map table, freshly shaved, immaculate in a camp full of men wearing the same shirt three days running. Vale looked up with the pleasant expression of a man who has already won something.

"You slept badly," Vale observed.

"I slept fine."

"Your face disagrees." Vale tapped the map, the gorge, the serac field rendered in confident blue contour lines. "My advance team confirmed the approach. We move tomorrow."

"Your advance team stacked cairns wrong," Cyrus said. "Flagging tape's non-standard. Either they don't know the protocols or they don't care, and both options mean they're dead men walking if the weather turns." He watched Vale's face do something small and fast — a flicker, there and gone, like a fish under ice. "Where did you find them, Vale?"

"Contractors. Vetted." The pleasantness held, but it had gone thin, a coat of varnish over older wood. "You have concerns about the route?"

"I have concerns about men who send scouts ahead and then can't tell me what the scouts ate for breakfast." Cyrus leaned on the table. "I've read about expeditions like this one."

"Oh?"

"Twenty-two years ago. Same valley. A man pushing a schedule the ice hadn't agreed to." He let that sit. Vale's hands went very still on the map's edge, and Cyrus understood, with the cold certainty of reading water before seeing the drop, that Vale knew exactly which expedition he meant — and had chosen this valley anyway.

"My mentor died believing this site was his life's work," Vale said quietly. "Dr. Halloran. Perhaps you've heard the name. Your mother certainly has."

The glacier groaned again, long and low, and somewhere high on the serac field something calved — a crack like a rifle shot, then the long rumble of ice finding its new shape. Every man in camp looked up. Vale didn't. He kept his eyes on Cyrus, and Cyrus saw what lived behind the varnish now: not greed. Grief, fossilized into purpose.

"Tomorrow," Vale repeated, and rolled up the map.

* * *

That night Cyrus sat with the journal open to the last written page and read the final entry, the one he had never been able to finish at nineteen, the one that ended mid-sentence as though even his mother's discipline had failed her:

*They tell me grief gets smaller. It doesn't. You get bigger around it. I have sealed the valley and I will seal myself, and if my living son ever asks me why, I will tell him—*

Nothing after. The sentence hung off the page like a man off a rope.

Cyrus closed the book and held it against his sternum and listened to the river, which had carried his brother's compass downstream twenty-two years ago and had never once stopped moving since. *Tell Cy I'll teach him to read water when I'm back.*

He wasn't coming back. But he was going upstream, tomorrow, into the valley his mother had walled shut with her own hands — and for the first time Cyrus wondered, with a feeling like ground thawing underfoot, unstable and inevitable, whether she had buried the truth along with the coordinates. Whether the thing Vale wanted destroyed might be the same thing she had spent two decades making sure stayed hidden.

Whether, at the end of this trail, he would find a fraud — or find out who had committed it.

Above the camp, the serac field groaned its long note, and the glacier began, quietly, to let go of its edges.
