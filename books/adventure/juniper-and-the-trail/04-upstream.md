## 4. Upstream

The Kestrel gorge did not welcome them so much as tolerate them, the way a sleeping animal tolerates a fly.

Cyrus felt it in the first hour — the walls closing from a valley to a throat, granite rising on both sides until the sky was a bright ribbon overhead and the river filled the rest of the world with its voice. Not the polite murmur of lowland water. This was a sound with weight behind it, snowmelt and glacier-grind, cold enough that standing near it made your teeth ache. He'd been hearing that sound in his sleep for five years without knowing why. Now he knew. It had been waiting for him.

Rhea set the pace at the front, Tomas behind her with the rope coils, the two junior crew members — Priya and Okafor — in the middle where they'd be protected, Vale at the rear with his hired glaciologist, a thin man named Brandt who spoke mostly to his instruments. Cyrus took point by unspoken agreement, or perhaps by unspoken surrender. Nobody else could read this country. They all knew it, including Vale, which was the only reason Cyrus was still here instead of on a bus south.

He measured the morning in cairns. That was how he measured everything now — miles, not years. Five years since he'd spoken more than ten words to Ingrid Nystrom. Five years of contracts like this one, except none of them like this one, because none of them had ever led back here.

"Marker," Priya called, twenty minutes in.

Cyrus held up a fist. The column stopped. He climbed to the marker — a cairn of stacked stones, waist-high, deliberate — and stood over it while the river roared below.

It was wrong. That was the first thing. A trail-finder builds cairns the way a mason lays brick: flat stones, interlocked, sized to survive wind and meltwater and the occasional curious fox. This one was stacked round-on-round, lazy, the kind of pile a hiker makes who has never had to trust a cairn with his life in whiteout. And it was fresh. The lichen-crusted stones beneath had been disturbed; he could see the pale scars where new rock had been set on old, no more than two weeks old, maybe less. The moss crushed under the base hadn't begun to stand back up.

Vale's advance team. The one that had gone upriver without permits, without insurance, without telling anyone their route. They'd marked the trail — but badly, and worse than badly. Cyrus crouched and looked downstream along the line the cairns implied and felt his stomach tighten. The line didn't follow the high bench. It followed the waterline.

Nobody who knew this gorge would mark the waterline in June. By July the Kestrel would rise three meters on afternoon melt alone. Any route pinned to the riverbank was a route designed to drown someone.

* * *

They made eleven kilometers the first day, which was good going for loaded packs in broken ground, and camped on a gravel fan above a side creek where the water ran clear instead of gray. Cyrus built the fire last, after the tents, after the food was cached in bear barrels, after he'd walked the perimeter out of habit older than memory. Rhea sat down across the flames from him without asking. She'd earned the right somewhere in the afternoon, when she'd taken the lead across a talus field exactly the way he gestured her to, no questions, no hesitation.

"Those markers," she said. "You didn't like them."

"No."

"Talk."

He fed a branch to the fire. Across the camp, Vale's tent glowed faintly, and he could hear Brandt's voice, low and clipped, dictating readings into a recorder.

"Your client sent people up this gorge ahead of us. They marked a route along the riverbank." He looked up. "In June, that's not a route. It's a trap with good intentions. Come August, it's just a trap."

Rhea was quiet for a moment. "How far does the water come up?"

"Depends on the heat. Three meters is normal. Five if we get a warm front off the coast." He didn't say what he was actually thinking, which was: *and warm fronts are coming early this year, I can smell it in the snowline.* "Tomorrow I'll re-mark the route on the high bench. We follow my cairns, not theirs."

"And Vale?"

"Is paying you triple to get his team to a site, not to trust dead men's rock piles." He said it flatly, and watched her decide to accept it. She was a good leader. That was the thing about Rhea he kept underestimating and then rediscovering: she cared about her people more than about being right.

"Re-mark it," she said. "But Cyrus — if there's something you're not telling me about this valley, this is the trip to tell it."

The fire popped. Somewhere upstream, ice shifted in the glacier with a sound like a door closing in an empty house.

"I'll tell you everything I know when I know it matters," he said. Which was a lie of omission, and they both heard it, and she let it stand, and he hated himself a little for it, and went to check the bear barrels again anyway.

* * *

The second day the gorge narrowed and the country turned against them properly. The trail — Vale's trail, the advance team's trail — hugged the river through a slot where the walls leaned in and the water ran green and fast as liquid glass. Cyrus stopped the column at the mouth of it and read the rock the way other men read weather.

Scree tells you things. Fresh scree is sharp-edged and pale; old scree is rounded and darkened, stained with lichen, settled into itself. The slope above the slot was fresh. He could see the scar of a recent slide maybe sixty meters up, a raw stripe through the vegetation, and at its base a spill of blocks the size of trucks that had come down within the last season — some of them within the last month, their fracture faces still clean and unweathered. The freeze-thaw cycle was still working that slope. Every cold night pried it open a little wider. Every warm day shook something loose.

And the advance team's cairns ran directly beneath it.

"We go up," Cyrus said, and pointed at a ramp of stable bedrock angling toward the benchland, sixty meters of climbing with packs. "There's a shelf above the slide zone. We traverse high and drop back to the river past the narrows."

Brandt appeared at his elbow, consulting a folded map that Cyrus recognized with a sour twist of the chest: a Vale expedition reprint, based on survey work from twenty years ago. His mother's survey work. The lines were accurate. The country had moved on without them.

"The map shows the route continuing along the—"

"The map is twenty years old and the mountain doesn't care what it shows." Cyrus kept his voice level. "That slope is active. Your client's advance team walked under it because they didn't know how to look at it. We're not making the same mistake."

"You're the trail-finder," Brandt said, with the particular condescension of a man quoting a job title. "Fine."

They climbed. It cost them ninety minutes and a thousand curses from Okafor, whose pack straps bit, but from the bench Cyrus looked back down at the slot and watched, with a cold satisfaction that felt uncomfortably like grief, as a rock the size of a refrigerator detached from the slide scar, bounced twice, and shattered on the trail the advance team had marked. The sound arrived seconds later, a flat crack rolling up the gorge.

Nobody said anything. Priya looked at him. Okafor looked at him. Even Tomas, who trusted nothing he couldn't tie a knot in, gave him a long nod.

By evening, Cyrus had rebuilt four cairns on the high line and torn down two of the advance team's where they might mislead someone in fog. He worked fast and wordlessly, and he realized, somewhere in the third cairn, that this was the closest thing to prayer he knew: stacking stone against chaos, marking a safe line through country that wanted to kill you. Competence as armor. If he was busy reading the mountain, he didn't have to read himself.

* * *

On the fourth day they found the advance camp.

It sat in a hanging meadow above the river, two tents collapsed and weighted with stones, a fire ring, crates half-buried where someone had cached supplies for the return trip. Abandoned in haste or abandoned on purpose — Cyrus couldn't tell yet, and the difference mattered.

He circled the camp slowly while the others spread out to inventory it. The fire ring held ash gone gray with rain — a week old, maybe ten days. The crates were Vale Expedition stenciled. One had been forced open and left gaping, its contents gone: whatever had been inside had been carried onward, upriver, toward the source.

And there, at the edge of the meadow, half-hidden in the grass, a cairn that was different from all the others.

Cyrus knelt beside it. The others were sloppy; this one was precise. Flat stones, interlocked, sized to survive anything. Built by someone who had learned the craft the same place he had — the same hands-teaching-hands lineage, the same habits, the same instinct for weight and balance. He knew that style the way you know a handwriting. He'd grown up watching it.

His mouth went dry. He pulled the top stone free and found what he both expected and dreaded: a strip of surveyor's tape, knotted in a specific way, a signal code from an older expedition. The knot said, in a language only three living people could read: *wrong way — go high.*

Someone had come through here recently and left a warning. Someone who built cairns like Ingrid Nystrom taught them to be built.

"Cyrus." Rhea's voice, tight. "You need to see this."

She was standing at the far tent, holding something small between gloved fingers. He crossed to her and saw it: a boot print in the soft ground beside the tent stake, pressed deep, recent — days old at most. Large. A Vibram lug pattern he recognized, because he'd bought the same pair for his father once, years ago, before the silence.

No, he thought. No, that's not possible. She swore. She sealed the valley. She forbade—

But the knot in the tape said *go high*, and the cairn said *I know you'll find this*, and the print said *she is already here, ahead of you, the way she was always ahead of everyone.*

The glacier groaned somewhere above them, long and low, a sound like the mountain clearing its throat before it speaks.

Cyrus stood very still in the meadow, the surveyor's tape burning in his fist, and understood that the race had changed shape entirely. It wasn't just Vale racing the calving season anymore.

Someone else was already upstream.
