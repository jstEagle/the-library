## 5. The Woman at the Ford

The Kestrel turned mean overnight.

Cyrus heard it before he saw it — a change in the river's voice, from the low conversational mutter of snowmelt to something harder, a grinding note like millstones. By the time the crew reached the high ford where the gorge pinched to a throat of gray stone, the water had come up eight inches and gone the color of skim milk, heavy with rock flour ground off the glacier's belly. It moved with the confidence of something that had been moving for ten thousand years and intended to keep doing so.

"Too fast to cross," Rhea said. She stood at the lip of the ford with her hands on her hips, wind pulling at the hood of her shell jacket. "We wait it out or we find another line."

"There is no other line," Cyrus said. "Not before the bridge. The canyon walls go vertical for six miles upstream." He crouched, touched two fingers to the current just past the eddy line, and pulled them back stinging. Cold enough to stop a heart in four minutes. "It'll drop tonight when the sun goes off the icefields. We camp here."

They were making camp — Tomas arguing cheerfully with the stove, Vale's hired surveyor Okafor triangulating something that didn't need triangulating — when Cyrus saw the smoke.

One thread of it, thin and blue, rising from behind a spur of granite half a mile downstream. Not their smoke. He hadn't heard anyone pass them on the trail, and no one passed on this trail without him knowing; he'd been reading the ground since dawn, the way you read a face, and the ground had told him they were alone.

He said nothing. Just drifted toward the spur with his pack still on, the way you drift when you don't want your body language to announce anything. The habit of five years of keeping his name quiet: move like a man going to piss, and nobody follows.

The woman was already watching him when he came around the rock.

She sat on a boulder above a small fire, rifle across her knees, a park service badge on a lanyard around her neck and a cup of something steaming in one hand. Dark hair cut short and practical, a face weathered to the color of old leather at the cheekbones, eyes the pale green of glacial ice under strain. She looked at Cyrus the way he looked at rivers: measuring volume, reading speed, calculating what would happen if she let herself be carried.

"You're loud," she said. "For a trail-finder."

"I wasn't trying to be quiet."

"No," she agreed. "That's what made you loud. People who try to be quiet sound like animals. You sounded like a man thinking." She nodded at the fire opposite her. "Sit. Or don't. But if you're going to stand there hovering, I'm going to finish my tea first, and it's the last of a tin I've been rationing for eleven days, so I intend to enjoy it."

Something about the arithmetic of eleven days of tea loosened something in Cyrus's chest. He sat.

"Sela Vann," she said. "Warden, Kestrel drainage district. Which makes you, by my count, trespassers in protected wilderness engaged in unpermitted archaeological salvage." She sipped. "I should arrest all six of you. I've been composing the paperwork in my head for two days. It's very satisfying paperwork."

"We haven't dug anything."

"No. You've been very careful. That's how I knew you weren't Vale's people." Her eyes flicked up. "His advance team left a cairn on the moraine below the icefall with a plastic cache tube inside. Survey markers sprayed on bedrock in three places. A latrine trench dug forty feet from running water." She recited it flatly, but each item landed like a struck nail. "Your crew has buried its waste, crossed the ford twice instead of once to spare the bank vegetation, and re-stacked every cairn you found back to its original stones. So. Either you're the most conscientious criminals in the district, or you're being paid not to care and can't help caring anyway."

Cyrus said nothing, which was an answer, and she smiled like she'd gotten it.

"Marcus Vale," she said. "Big contract, triple rate, secrecy clause. Am I close?"

"How do you know about the clause?"

"Because I've been tracking Marcus Vale for two years." She set down the cup. The playfulness went out of her face the way cloud goes out of a sky, leaving something harder and more beautiful underneath. "Two years of permits filed and withdrawn, of helicopter charters booked through three shell companies, of salvage crews that materialize in protected drainages and evaporate before my reports clear the regional office. Someone upstairs keeps losing my memos, Mr.—?"

"Nystrom."

The name was out before he could catch it, the way a boot slips on wet scree. He watched it land. Watched her go still, the way water goes still over a deep hole.

"*Nystrom*," she repeated. "As in—"

"Yes."

"Huh." She studied him with new attention, and Cyrus felt the old familiar flinch, the bracing for *the Ingrid Nystrom? the Anders Nystrom?* — the name as a door everyone wanted to open. But Sela Vann only nodded slowly, filing it away rather than reaching for it. "That explains the cairns. Her crews used to rebuild them stone for stone. There are warden logs from the seventies that mention it. 'Nystrom party passed through, restored trail markers, left no trace.' Like a religion."

"It was."

"And you broke it."

"I'm working on it," Cyrus said, and was surprised to hear the honesty in his own voice. He hadn't meant to say it out loud. Something about her made the truth sit closer to the surface than usual.

Sela leaned forward, elbows on her knees, and for a while neither of them spoke. The river filled the silence, grinding its ancient argument against the gorge walls. Somewhere above, a chunk of the icefield let go with a sound like distant artillery, and both of them glanced up at the same instant, the same instinct, the same count of seconds until the echo died.

"Here's my problem," she said finally. "My duty says I radio in, detain you, and end this expedition today. My duty also says the reason I've spent two years eating cold food and writing memos into a void is that Vale's operations have already damaged three protected sites, and the only way I ever prove it is by catching him *in* it — with evidence, at the source, red-handed." She turned the cup in her fingers. "You're my best chance at that. And you're also, technically, my arrest."

"That's quite a fork in the trail."

"I'm a warden. Forks are my whole life." She looked at him directly then, and there was nothing soft in it, which paradoxically was the softest thing anyone had offered Cyrus in years. "So here's my offer. Information for information. I tell you what I know about Vale's advance team — and I know plenty, including that they went upriver nine days ago with equipment no legitimate survey carries. You tell me what's actually in his contract, because I promise you it isn't artifacts. Men who spray-paint bedrock aren't looking for potsherds."

Cyrus weighed it. Below them, faintly, he could hear Rhea calling his name, the syllables stretched thin by the wind.

"The secrecy clause binds us to silence about site history," he said. "Not artifacts. History. There's something at the source about what happened there twenty years ago, and Vale wants it before anyone else can read it."

Sela went very still. "Twenty years ago," she said. "The Kestrel expedition. The one that—"

"Yes."

"I'm sorry." And she said it plainly, without ceremony, the way you'd say *watch your footing* — a warning and a kindness in the same breath. Then: "You understand that whatever's up there, if it documents a crime in protected land, it belongs to the record. Not to Vale. Not to your family. To the record."

"I know what it belongs to."

"Do you?" She held his gaze. "Because I've met a lot of people up here who knew exactly what things belonged to right up until it cost them something. Duty's easy when duty and desire point the same direction. The valley tests whether they still point there when they don't."

"And if they don't?"

"Then you choose," Sela Vann said, "and live with it. Same as me." She rose, slung the rifle, and kicked dirt over her fire with three efficient motions. "Radio silence from me for seventy-two hours. After that, if you're still upriver, I come after you, and I won't be alone. Use the time well, Nystrom."

She paused at the edge of the rocks, half-turned, the light catching the green of her eyes.

"For what it's worth — the advance team's markers are wrong. Deliberately wrong. They route around the true line to the source. Whoever drew them wanted anyone following to end up on the wrong side of the icefall." A ghost of a smile. "You'd have seen it eventually. You see everything. It's very annoying."

Then she was gone, quick and sure-footed as water finding its level, and Cyrus sat alone with the river's roar and the strange new weight in his chest — part warning, part warmth, all of it dangerous.

When he got back to camp, Rhea was waiting with her arms crossed. "Where were you?"

"Talking to a warden."

Rhea's face did something complicated. "Are we arrested?"

"Not yet." Cyrus looked upstream, toward the icefall, toward the source, toward twenty years of buried truth and a woman who'd just promised to hunt them in three days' time. "But we've got seventy-two hours to get there first. Break camp. We cross at dusk."

Above the gorge, unseen behind the evening clouds, the glacier groaned — long and low, like something turning over in its sleep, beginning to dream of letting go.
