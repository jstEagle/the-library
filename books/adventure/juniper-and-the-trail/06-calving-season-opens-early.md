## 6. Calving Season Opens Early

The warm front came in like a rumor, then like a fact.

Cyrus felt it before he saw any sign of it — a softness in the air at dawn that had no business being there at four thousand meters, a smell of wet stone and green water where there should have been only iron and ice. He stood outside his tent with his coffee going cold in his hands and watched the light come up wrong. Not the hard, clean alpenglow of the past six mornings, but something diffuse, humid, the sky the color of an old bruise healing.

The glacier knew before the forecast did.

He'd been listening to it for days without admitting what he was hearing — a deep, arrhythmic groaning from up-valley, the sound of a thing shifting its weight in its sleep. This morning the sound had changed register. It was not sleeping anymore.

By the time Rhea crawled out of her tent, Cyrus had the barometer out of his pack and was watching the needle slide with the slow inevitability of something already decided.

"How bad?" she asked. She didn't ask *what*. She'd been on enough of his routes to know that when he went quiet and started measuring things, the measuring was the answer.

"Front's riding up from the southwest. Warm rain by tonight, maybe sooner." He turned the instrument so she could see. "That's the Kestrel tongue. That's our bridge."

Rhea looked up-valley, where the glacier poured down between the flanking walls like a river that had forgotten how to move, all except at its margins, where it moved constantly — shedding itself into the gorge in thunderclaps they'd been counting like sheep at night. Calving season. The window Vale's own geologists had charted: nine more days, give or take, before the meltwater swelled and the ice bridge across the outflow channel became a corridor of collapsing slabs.

"Give or take," Rhea said.

"The take just got bigger."

* * *

Vale received the news the way he received everything now — standing apart from the crew, hands clasped behind him, as if the valley were a lecture hall and the weather an unruly student. He listened to Cyrus's report with a courtesy that was worse than argument.

"I've seen warm fronts stall out on this aspect before," Vale said. "We proceed on schedule."

"On schedule is dead. The schedule died this morning." Cyrus kept his voice level, kept it crew-voice, kept it away from the twenty years of other things he wanted to say to this man. "We push to the advance camp today, cross tomorrow at first light, or we don't cross at all."

"You're recommending we rush my expedition because of one barometer reading."

"I'm recommending we rush your expedition because I can hear the glacier letting go of itself every forty minutes, and because your maps put the crossing two days later than it can safely happen." He let that sit. "Your maps, Mr. Vale. The ones you didn't want corrected."

Something moved behind Vale's eyes — not anger; Cyrus had expected anger. It was closer to recognition, the look of a man hearing a voice he'd heard before, long ago, saying words he'd heard before. For half a second Cyrus thought Vale might say *you sound like her*, and he braced against it like a man bracing against a door in wind.

"We move today," Vale said finally. "But we move in order. The equipment goes first, as planned. The heavy crates."

The heavy crates. Cyrus had watched those crates get packed at the trailhead — dense, sealed, their contents listed only as *survey instruments* — and he had watched Vale's two hired men handle them with a care that survey instruments had never earned from anyone. He looked at them now, lashed to the lead sleds, riding ahead of the people.

"Instruments go with the crew," Cyrus said. "People first. Always first."

"They're insured, Mr. Nystrom—"

The name landed between them like a dropped tool. Neither of them picked it up. Vale had known who he was since the trailhead; Cyrus had stopped pretending otherwise somewhere around the third day, when it became clear that pretending cost more energy than it saved. But Vale using the name aloud, casually, in front of Rhea and Tomas and the others — that was new. That was a door opened on purpose.

"People first," Rhea said quietly, and that settled it, because Rhea's word settled things. But Cyrus saw Vale's jaw work once, twice, like a man swallowing something that would keep.

* * *

They made eight miles by midafternoon, and the mountain began taking payment.

It started small — a hiss of gravel off the eastern wall, nothing, a shrug of scree. Then the wet air found the snowfields clinging to the couloirs above the trail, and the snowfields began to fail. Every twenty minutes there was a soft whump somewhere above them, and everyone flinched, and the flinching got worse instead of better, because that was how fear worked: repetition didn't dull it, it tuned it.

Sela appeared at the rear of the column as if she'd always been part of it, moving with that economy Cyrus had come to watch for — no wasted motion, her eyes doing constant arithmetic on the slopes. She fell into step beside him.

"Your client cut the switchbacks below the moraine," she said, low. "I walked his line this morning. He took the direct route through the runout zone to save half a day."

"He wouldn't—"

"He did. I've got photographs." Her jaw was tight. "Two years I've been building a case on this man, Cyrus. Permit fraud, salvage trafficking, and now he's routing human beings under active release slopes to protect a schedule he knows is fiction."

Cyrus looked back down the trail, at the neat, efficient line of their ascent cutting straight through the fan of debris below the couloirs. His stomach turned over slowly, the way ice turns over in spring water.

"He knew," he said. "He knew the front was coming. His geologists would have flagged it days ago — he had the same forecasts we did, better ones. And he sent us up here anyway, on a compressed route, through the runouts."

"Why?"

Because the crates mattered more than the people. Because whatever was in those crates had to reach the source before anyone else could reach it — before Sela could stop him, before the season closed, before Ingrid Nystrom, wherever she was, could—

The thought arrived whole, unbidden, and cold: *before my mother could.*

"Cyrus." Sela's hand was on his sleeve. "Whatever you just figured out, hold onto it. We're not through the runout yet."

Above them, the mountain agreed.

The release came off the western couloir with almost no warning — a crack like a rifle, then the long wet roar of a snowfield letting go, carrying a freight of rock with it. Cyrus was shouting before his mind finished the math, grabbing Tomas by the pack strap and hauling him toward the wall, but the fan was wide and Tomas was slow with fatigue, and the edge of it caught him anyway — a glancing blow of stone and slush that threw him sideways down the slope like a doll kicked off a shelf.

The world narrowed to rope and hands and blood. They reached him in seconds that felt like hours. His left leg was broken below the knee, the bone angulated wrong under his shell pants, and there was a gash over his ear that bled with cheerful enthusiasm, and he was conscious, cursing steadily and inventively, which Cyrus had learned long ago was the best possible sound an injured man could make.

"Splint," Rhea was saying, calm as a metronome. "Cyrus, airway. Sela—"

"I've got him." Sela's hands were already working, sure and fast, and Cyrus understood in one glance that she had done this before, in worse places, alone.

They splinted Tomas against a boulder while the mountain continued its argument overhead, smaller releases now, aftershocks. Tomas gripped Cyrus's wrist during the worst of it and grinned through the blood.

"Told you," he said. "Told you this job had teeth."

"Shut up and breathe."

"Triple pay," Tomas said, and laughed, and then stopped laughing because the leg objected, and Cyrus held his shoulder and looked up the trail — at the sled tracks of the heavy crates, already far ahead, already protected, already prioritized — and felt something in his chest close like a fist.

Vale came down the trail twenty minutes later. Cyrus watched his face as he took in the scene — the blood, the splint, the wrecked schedule — and what Cyrus saw there was not horror. It was recalculation. A man re-pricing a loss.

"You knew," Cyrus said. He didn't raise his voice. There was no need. The valley was quiet between releases, and the words carried. "You had the forecast. You cut the route through the runout to save time for those crates. You knew the risk and you hid it, and Tomas is paying for it."

Vale looked at him for a long moment. Around them the crew had gone still, the way crews go still, the silence itself a verdict pending.

"I have spent thirty years," Vale said at last, "watching the Nystrom family decide what the truth costs and who should pay it. Don't lecture me about hiding risks." He turned to Rhea. "Medevac can't fly in this front. He rides with the gear sleds back to the ford. Warden Vann can escort him down. We continue."

"We continue," Rhea said, "because the bridge closes in five days and you know it. Not because you said so." She looked at Cyrus. "Get him stable. We move in an hour."

* * *

Evening came down purple and cold after all that wet heat, the front stalling against the high peaks as if even the weather couldn't decide. Cyrus sat with Sela at the edge of camp, Tomas asleep inside on the good drugs, his leg strapped and elevated, stable enough. Below them the glacier ran silver-gray in the last light, and as they watched, a section of the serac field at its shoulder let go — a boom that rolled up the valley and came back changed, and a plume of powder rising like smoke from something burning.

"Five days," Sela said. "Maybe four. That bridge won't hold meltwater and traffic both."

"No."

"And you're still going across."

Cyrus didn't answer right away. Up-valley, faint as memory, the glacier groaned again — that deep, patient sound, the sound of a thing that had been waiting twenty years and could wait five more days.

"There's something at the source," he said finally. "Something Vale wants badly enough to spend Tomas's leg getting to. And I think—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "I think my mother spent her whole life making sure nobody ever got there. Including me. Especially me."

Sela was silent a moment. When she spoke, her voice had the careful flatness of someone setting down something fragile.

"When I come up that valley after him — after all of you, if it comes to that — I need to know which side of the line you'll be standing on."

"I don't know yet," Cyrus said. It was the most honest thing he'd said in five years. "Ask me again at the source."

Another calving boomed up-valley, closer now, the echo coming back shorter. The mountain wasn't scenery anymore. It hadn't been for days. It was a counterparty, an adversary with its own ledger, and it was calling in debts — and somewhere beyond the failing ice, at the forbidden spring beneath the glacier, Cyrus had the sudden, certain feeling that the largest debt of all was waiting to be collected.

Sela followed his gaze up the darkening valley.

"That sound," she said. "It's getting closer every hour."

"Yeah," Cyrus said, and stood, and began packing for a crossing they'd have to make at first light, days early, across ice that was already dying. "And it knows we're coming."
