## 7. The False Ledger

Sela Vann had learned to read camps the way other people read faces.

A camp told you everything if you knew where to look: how the tent lines were tensioned, whether the cook fire was fed with patience or haste, whether the gear was stowed like people who expected to stay or like people who expected to run. The advance camp in the hanging valley above the third gorge told her that Marcus Vale's men had expected to stay exactly as long as it took to find one thing and leave.

She crouched at the edge of the moraine with the wind pulling at her hood and studied it for a long time before she moved. Four tents — no, three tents and a storage fly pitched over something they didn't want seen from the air. A radio mast, cheap and tall. Boot tracks braided through the gravel, too many of them overlapping to count, but all of them heavy, deliberate, the prints of men carrying loads they hadn't logged.

Beside her, Cyrus Nystrom went very still.

"You see it," she said.

"The fly." His voice was flat, careful, the voice of a man holding a door shut against something on the other side. "You don't cover cargo unless the cargo is wrong."

They came down through the boulder field without speaking, because speaking was a habit for places where the land wasn't listening. Here the land listened to everything. The glacier spoke in its sleep below them, a long groaning syllable repeated every few minutes, and the meltwater ran silver over stones the color of old iron. Sela kept her hand near the bear spray on her hip out of habit rather than hope; against men like Vale's, it would be about as useful as a whistle.

The camp was empty. That was the first wrong thing. The second was the stove still warm when she laid her palm near it. The third was the smell — not fuel, not food, but paper. Old paper, foxed and sweet, the smell of an archive that had been carried into a place archives were never meant to go.

"They left in a hurry," Cyrus said. "Or they were made to leave."

"Vale went upriver." She nodded toward the ice bridge, a pale seam stitched across the gorge two miles on, thin as a scar this late in the season. "Two of his men went with him. The other two—" she tilted her head at the tracks leading back down-valley, fresh, fast-spaced, "—were sent down to meet the main crew. To keep them busy. To keep *you* busy."

Cyrus looked at her then, and Sela saw again what she'd been trying not to see since the ford: a man built entirely out of restraint. Everything about him was load-bearing. Nothing was decoration.

"My mother's name is going to be in whatever's under that fly," he said. It wasn't a question.

Sela didn't answer. She lifted the edge of the tarp instead.

* * *

The crates beneath were museum-grade — acid-free board, foam cutouts, desiccant packs, the whole liturgy of preservation — and they were empty except for one. In that one, wrapped in a wool shirt that had been washed so many times it had gone soft as skin, lay a ledger.

It was small. That was what struck her first, kneeling there with the wind worrying the tarp above her head. Two decades of rumor, two years of her own investigation, an entire valley sealed by federal order and grief, and the object at the center of it all was a field ledger scarcely bigger than a hymnal. Canvas-bound. Corner worn round. The spine cracked in a way she recognized from her grandfather's survey books, from a generation of scientists who wrote standing up in bad weather and pressed their notes flat under their knees.

The name stamped inside the cover had faded to a ghost: **E. HALLORAN**.

"Don't touch it," Cyrus said, though his hand was already halfway out, already wanting.

"I'm going to touch it." She pulled a nitrile glove from her jacket pocket — she'd stopped pretending, days ago, that she wasn't building a case — and worked it on. "This is evidence. Which means from this moment forward, I'm not your guide or your ally or whatever it is I've been. I'm a warden, and this is a seizure, and you're a witness. Do you understand me?"

"I understand you." He said it like a man agreeing to a sentence.

She opened the ledger on her knee.

Halloran's handwriting was beautiful. That was the obscenity of it — the looping, confident script of a man who never doubted himself, entries dated across a summer twenty-two years gone. Elevations. Barometric readings. Sketches of the gorge walls rendered with an artist's economy. And threaded through the science, casual as weather notes, the other thing.

*Recovered 14 items from spring chamber. Gold plaque, ceremonial. Two figurines, copper alloy. Textile fragment, remarkable condition.*

Sela turned pages with fingers that wanted to shake and refused to. The Kestrel Source artifacts. The discovery that had made Halloran's reputation and, by inheritance, the reputation of everyone who stood on it — the students he trained, the institutions he endowed, the rival whose own legend had been forged in opposition to his. She'd read the published accounts in three languages. Every one of them described the source cache as excavated, documented, provenance clean as glacial water.

The ledger said otherwise.

*Site disturbed prior to arrival. Tunnels cut, supports burned. Local removal evident — recent.* And beneath it, in the same lovely hand, the decision that had followed: *Reconstructed context for publication. Chamber photographed after rearrangement. No one will know the difference.*

"No one will know the difference," Sela read aloud, because some sentences needed air to be believed.

Cyrus had gone the color of the scree. He knelt beside her without touching the book, reading over her shoulder, and she watched him arrive at each entry a half-second behind her, watched his lips move slightly, watched the architecture of his restraint take weight it wasn't built for.

"He looted it," Cyrus said. "Before anyone ever saw it. He dug it out, sold half of it, and staged the rest—"

"And wrote the story everyone's been teaching for twenty years." Sela turned another page. There were shipping entries here. Consignments. A buyer coded only as *M.V. Antiquities, Zürich*. Her stomach tightened. "Your mother found this. Didn't she."

"She must have." His voice had dropped to almost nothing. "Twenty years ago. After Anders—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "After the collapse. She sealed the valley. She forbade anyone to go back. Everyone thought it was grief. Everyone said she was protecting the dead." His jaw worked. "She was protecting the living. If Halloran's fraud came out, it wouldn't just burn him. It would burn every career built on his work. Half the discipline. Maybe hers too, because she'd been on that expedition, because people would say she knew, because people always say that."

"So she buried it deeper than the glacier did."

"And now Vale wants it." Cyrus sat back on his heels, and Sela watched the last piece click into place behind his eyes, watched it land like a rockfall. "He doesn't want artifacts. There are no artifacts. He wants this book. Because if it surfaces, Halloran falls, and Halloran raised him — Halloran was the father his actual father never—" He pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes. "He's not here to destroy the evidence. He's here to destroy it *and replace it*. Forge entries. Date them differently. Make it look like Ingrid Nystrom falsified the record to bury a rival's honest work. Make her the fraud."

Sela closed the ledger gently, as if it could feel being handled roughly, as if the man who'd written it might still be embarrassed.

"That's why the secrecy clause," she said. "That's why the advance team came in without permits. That's why he needs the source itself — he needs to stage the scene to match the forgery. He needs your mother's valley to lie for him one more time."

The glacier groaned below them, long and low, and neither of them laughed at the timing, because it wasn't funny. It was the sound of a witness clearing its throat.

* * *

They worked through the gray afternoon while the light thinned and the wind swung around from the north, which was the wrong direction, which meant cold, which meant the calving season was thinking about arriving early. Sela photographed every page with the methodical patience her father had taught her — *the mountain rewards the slow, selam, the fast ones feed it* — while Cyrus searched the rest of the camp with a trail-finder's eye and found the things men hide when they think no one reads terrain.

A second set of documents in a waterproof tube: Vale's working copies, already annotated, already altered. Dates shifted. Entries composed in an imitation of Halloran's hand that was good enough to fool a committee and not good enough to fool paper chemistry. A letter of transmittal addressed to a journal editor, pre-written, awaiting only the final page numbers. And a photograph, creased soft from handling, of a young Marcus Vale standing beside a silver-haired Edmund Halloran at some long-ago dig, both of them squinting into the sun, both of them smiling like men who believed the ground would hold.

Cyrus held the photograph a long time.

"He was twelve," he said finally. "In this picture. My mother told me once — she remembered him, a kid trailing Halloran, carrying screens. She said he was the only person Halloran was ever gentle with." He set it down face-up on the crate, deliberately, the way you leave flowers. "And Halloran taught him that the record is whatever you're strong enough to make it. That's the inheritance. That's what he's trying to protect."

"Then we take both ledgers," Sela said. "The true one and the false one. Together they tell the whole crime — the fraud and the cover-up of the fraud. Your mother's silence included." She met his eyes and made herself say the hard part, because she'd learned that mercy delayed was cruelty compounded. "If I file this, Cyrus — and I have to file this — it won't just be Vale who burns. Ingrid concealed evidence for twenty years. However good her reasons, however much grief was sitting on top of it, a review board won't weigh that kindly. You know that."

"I know that." He said it steadily, and she realized he'd known it since the ford, maybe since the contract, maybe since the day he stole his mother's journal out of her office and read his brother's death written in her handwriting. "She'd want it anyway. That's the thing I spent five years refusing to understand. She buried it to protect us, but she never slept again. I used to think the silence was her choice." He looked upriver, toward the ice bridge, toward the source, toward whatever waited there that had taken his brother and his childhood in the same season. "It was her sentence."

The light was failing. Down-valley, faint as breath, Sela heard engines — the crew coming up, or Vale's men returning, or both, converging on this little valley of paper and lies. She sealed the true ledger in an evidence bag, and the false one in another, and stowed them against her spine where the pack rode closest to her body.

Then Cyrus straightened, sudden, his head cocked toward the glacier tongue below the camp, and all the blood left his face at once.

"Sela." His voice had gone quiet in the way that quiet means danger. "Look at the serac line."

Above the ice bridge, where the glacier poured over the shoulder of the mountain, a cornice that had hung there stable for a century had begun, in the last hour, in the warming wind, to shed. Even at this distance she could see the fresh scars of it, pale wounds on blue ice, and hear — felt in the sternum more than the ear — the deep percussion of calving blocks striking the valley floor.

The season hadn't come early.

It had arrived.

And somewhere between here and that failing bridge, alone with a ledger worth more to him than any life on this river, Marcus Vale was running out of ground to stand on — which meant, Sela thought, shouldering her pack, that every one of them was.
