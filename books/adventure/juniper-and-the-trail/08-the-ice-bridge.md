## 8. The Ice Bridge

The ledger was gone from the ammo box before dawn.

Cyrus knew it the way he knew weather — not by looking, but by the wrongness in the air. He'd slept with his back against the supply cache, the canvas cold through his jacket, and he woke to a silence that had a shape to it, a Vale-shaped hole where a man should have been. The box's latch hung open. Inside, where the leather-bound ledger had sat wrapped in a fleece shirt, there was only a folded note weighted with a carabiner.

*Don't follow. The source is mine. It was always going to be mine.*

Below it, in smaller script, almost apologetic: *The bridge won't hold two.*

Cyrus stood in the blue half-light and read it three times. Around him the advance camp slept on — Rhea's breathing, the tick of the stove cooling, somewhere a raven working at something frozen. Upvalley, the Kestrel Glacier filled the notch between the peaks like a held breath, and even from here he could hear it: a low, grinding complaint, the sound of ten thousand tons of ice remembering that it was water.

The calving had started early. Everyone knew it. The warm front had come through two days ago and turned the whole valley soft, meltwater running in braided silver threads down the glacier's face, and the ice bridge — that natural arch of compressed blue where the river went under and came out the other side — had been groaning like a door in a dying house ever since. Sela had measured it yesterday with a laser rangefinder and said, flatly, *a week, maybe less.* Tomas's shoulder was strapped and useless. Rhea had already begun marking the emergency descent.

A week. Vale had given himself hours.

Cyrus looked at the note a fourth time and then put it in his pocket, because some part of him — the part that had spent twenty years cataloguing other people's mistakes — wanted to keep the evidence.

*The bridge won't hold two.*

He thought about letting it be true.

He thought about it honestly, standing there with the cold working through his boot soles: let Vale go. Let the man walk himself out onto that arch of rotten ice with a stolen ledger and a lifetime of rot in his chest, and let the mountain do what mountains do. Cyrus could be downriver by the time anyone asked questions. He could be a hundred trail-miles away, nameless again, the whole ugly business of the Nystrom legacy dissolving behind him like breath off glass.

Then he saw it — the way he'd seen the fresh, wrong markers on the way up, the ones Vale's advance team had left pointing into a rockfall gully instead of around it. Vale didn't know this route. Vale had never read ice in his life. He had a map drawn by a dead man's ambition and a headlamp with dying batteries, and he was going to walk out onto a bridge that had been singing its own funeral for three days, and he was going to do it in the dark.

Anders had died on this river.

Cyrus pulled his boots on.

* * *

The ice bridge was worse in person than it had been in his head.

By the time Cyrus reached it, the sky had gone the color of skim milk, and the glacier above the bridge was shedding water in ropes, a hundred small waterfalls threading down the seracs. The bridge itself spanned the gorge where the Kestrel dove beneath the ice — a natural arch, maybe forty meters of it, blue at the core and white-rotted at the edges, sagging in the middle like a tired back. He could hear it. Not the groaning anymore. A finer sound, a crystalline ticking, like a clock counting down in a language only the mountain spoke.

Vale was a third of the way across.

He'd left his pack at the near abutment — smart, or half-smart, the one smart thing — and he was moving with the stiff, deliberate steps of a man who had read about ice in books. His headlamp beam wobbled. He was sixty-three years old and the wind up the gorge was cutting sideways and he was carrying the ledger inside his jacket, against his heart, like a man carrying a bomb he both wanted and dreaded to detonate.

"Vale!" Cyrus's voice cracked off the ice walls and came back at him doubled.

The man stopped. Didn't turn. His lamp swung, found Cyrus, held.

"The bridge won't hold two!" Vale shouted back, and there was something almost giddy in it, the hysteria of a man who had finally stopped pretending. "I read your note! I wrote it!"

"You wrote it because it's true, and you're still on it!"

"I've waited thirty years!" Vale's voice bounced and shattered against the gorge walls. "Thirty years since Halloran came down off this glacier with boxes of artifacts and a story, and everyone clapped, and Ingrid Nystrom — *Ingrid* — got to be the one who found the inconsistencies, the one who got to be righteous about it, the one who got to bury it all and be a saint for her mercy!" He took a step. The ice ticked. "The source is mine. The truth is mine. You Nystroms have had thirty years of other people's patience—"

The glacier answered him.

It wasn't a groan. It was a shot — a rifle-crack from somewhere high on the serac field, then a second, then a long tearing roar like the world's largest sheet of paper ripping, and Cyrus's body understood it before his mind did. He'd grown up on this sound. He'd been a boy the last time he'd heard it this close, and his legs had known it ever since.

"Off the bridge!" he screamed. "Vale, MOVE—"

Vale moved — wrong. He hurried, and hurrying on rotten ice is how it kills you, and Cyrus watched the man's boot find a lens of meltwater over blue glass and watched him go down hard, the ledger skidding from his jacket, sliding on the sloped surface toward the sagging middle of the arch, toward the thin white place where the ticking had become a hiss.

Vale lunged for it. The ice spoke — a deep, structural groan, the sound of a thing deciding — and a crack ran out from under the man like a lightning stroke drawn in ink, forking toward both abutments.

Cyrus was already on the bridge.

He didn't remember deciding. Later, he would understand that he had decided twenty years ago, on the day they'd carried Anders's body down this valley, that he would never again stand on safe ground while someone was on bad ice. His crampons found the rhythm his body had never forgotten — step, plant, weight, step — and the bridge bucked under him like a living thing, and the cold spray of the river came up through the cracks and hit his face like thrown gravel.

He reached Vale at the middle. The crack had opened into a seam, a black finger of void widening beneath them, and beyond it the far half of the arch still stood, for now, a bridge to a bridge that was already gone.

"Give me your hand." Not a request. He said it the way he said *rope's fixed, clip in* — the flat voice of the trail, the voice that didn't leave room for argument.

Vale looked up at him. In the flat grey light, with his hood back and his grey hair plastered with melt, he looked suddenly, terribly old. "Why," he said. Not *help me.* *Why.* As if the question mattered more than the answer, as if he had thirty years invested in the question.

"Because I'm not my mother's mistakes," Cyrus said, "and I'm not yours. Hand."

Vale handed it over.

Cyrus hauled him up and they went across the seam together, arm in arm, two men who hated each other in the oldest way — by inheritance — moving as one body because the ice allowed nothing else. Behind them the near half of the arch let go. There was no drama to it, no roar, just a sigh and a shift of weight and then forty meters of ten-thousand-year-old ice folding down into the gorge like a knee giving way, and the river took it, and the sound came up out of the earth through the soles of Cyrus's boots rather than through the air.

They stood on the far side, chest heaving, and the bridge was gone, and the source lay somewhere above them in the white, and there was no way back but the long way, if there was a way back at all.

* * *

They didn't speak for a long time.

There was a boulder field above the gorge, moraine dropped by the glacier in some older, angrier century, and they climbed to it because climbing was the only thing either of them knew to do. Cyrus got Vale out of the wind behind a boulder the size of a house, got his own stove lit in the lee, got water melting. Small mechanics. The liturgy of staying alive. Vale sat with his back against the stone and the ledger in his lap — Cyrus had retrieved it from the ice at the last second, and Vale hadn't thanked him, and hadn't tried to take it back either. It sat between them like a third man.

The light was failing. Upvalley, the glacier went on shedding its waterfalls, and every so often the mountain cracked its knuckles and both of them flinched.

"You could have let me go," Vale said at last. His voice had come down out of its hysteria and landed somewhere quiet, somewhere almost puzzled.

"Yes."

"You hate me."

"I don't think about you enough to hate you." Cyrus fed the stove another pinch of fuel. "That's the truth of it. You were a story my mother told with her jaw set. I thought you'd be bigger."

Vale laughed — a short, cracked sound with no humor in it, the laugh of a man hearing his own obituary read aloud. "Bigger." He looked at his hands. They were shaking, and not from the cold. "Do you know what Halloran was to me?"

Cyrus said nothing. He had learned long ago that silence was the best tool in the kit; people filled it with the truth.

"My father was a bank manager in Vancouver who died at his desk when I was nine. A man in a grey suit, dead of a grey life. Halloran was — " Vale searched for it, and Cyrus watched him fail and then settle. "He was color. He came to lecture at the university with glacier water still in his boots and stories that made the room feel too small, and he looked at me — a scholarship kid with no name and no money — and he said, *you'll do.*" Vale's jaw worked. "Do you know what that's worth? *You'll do.* Three years later he took me into the field. He taught me to read moraine and ice and light. He was the first person who ever acted like I was made of the same material as the mountains." He looked up, and his eyes were wet, and he didn't seem to know it or care. "He was the best man I ever knew. And he was a thief. And everything I am — everything I built, every dig, every paper, every inch of the reputation I spent thirty years sharpening against your mother — it's all load-bearing walls on a foundation of his fraud. And she knew. She *knew*, and she sat on it for twenty years, and let me build."

"She sat on it," Cyrus said quietly, "because exposing it would have buried you too. Halloran's students. Halloran's whole generation. She chose the living over the record." He paused. "I've spent five years being furious at her for that. I'm starting to understand it's the hardest thing she ever did."

"And now?" Vale's voice was thin. "Now the ledger exists, and I'm the one holding it, and if it comes to light — if it comes out that I came up here to destroy it — " He stopped. Looked down at the ledger in his lap. Looked, for a long moment, like a man looking down into the crevasse where his whole life had been standing all along. "I thought if I could just get to the source first. If I could stand where he stood. If I could understand *why* he did it, maybe — " He shook his head. "There's no *why* that holds. I've been carrying his crime for thirty years and calling it loyalty."

The stove hissed. Somewhere above them, the glacier groaned, a long tectonic complaint, and a raven crossed the milk-white sky like a signature on a document.

Cyrus looked at this old man — his mother's enemy, his brother's indirect executioner, a man built out of another man's theft — and found, to his own surprise, that the hatred he'd packed for this trip like a bedroll had come loose somewhere on the ice. What was left wasn't forgiveness. It was something smaller and worse: recognition. He knew what it was to be made by someone else's choices. He'd spent five years running from his mother's name the way Vale had spent thirty running toward his mentor's ghost. They'd both been carrying a ledger. His just happened to be written in grief instead of fraud.

"Vale," he said. "The bridge is gone. There's no way down this side except the route Sela mapped, and you don't know it. Which means from here on, you come with me, you do what I say, and when we get down, you tell the truth. All of it. To her, to the warden, to whoever asks. That's the toll."

Vale looked at him for a long time. The light was nearly gone now, and the glacier above the source — *Anders's* glacier, Cyrus thought, *my brother's grave-country* — glowed faintly blue in the last of it, like something alive holding its breath.

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you can explain to the mountain why you're still here when the calving starts in earnest."

Something moved across the old man's face — not a smile, but the place where a smile had been excavated out of him. He pulled his jacket tighter and looked upvalley, toward the source, toward whatever was waiting there that had pulled three generations of them into this valley like water finding its level.

"Your mother," he said quietly. "She'll be there, won't she. At the spring. I've known that woman forty years. She'd never let either of us reach it alone."

Cyrus stood, shouldered his pack, and looked up the white throat of the valley, where the glacier groaned and shifted in the dark like something turning over in its sleep.

"Then we'd better hurry," he said. "I think she's been waiting for both of us."
