## 9. The Source

The glacier's throat opened an hour before dawn, and Cyrus went in alone.

He had left word with Rhea — a lie dressed as caution: *scouting the route ahead, back by midmorning*. She'd looked at him too long, the way she did when she was deciding whether to let him have his secrets, and then she'd said, "Bring yourself back. The rest is negotiable." Sela had said nothing at all, only pressed her spare headlamp into his hand and held his wrist for one breath longer than necessary. He carried both of them with him now, along with everything else he couldn't set down.

The ice tunnel breathed. That was the thing no one told you about glaciers — that they were not silent, not dead. They groaned and ticked and sighed like enormous sleeping animals, settling their weight through centuries of compressed snow, and every sound came up through the soles of Cyrus's boots before it reached his ears. He moved through blue light the color of deep water, following the river itself — the Kestrel, young here, thin as a rope, running clear over glassy ice in a channel it had cut for ten thousand years.

His mother's journal had described this passage. He had read the entry so many times the words had worn smooth: *The river enters the ice at the third moraine and speaks all the way down. Anders said it sounded like it was telling jokes.* Anders had been nineteen. He had laughed at everything — at the cold, at their mother's obsession, at the absurdity of two brothers hauling survey gear through a valley their father had never lived to see. The journal entries from the last week were full of his voice, transcribed like music Ingrid couldn't stop hearing.

Cyrus walked into the mountain of ice after him.

The tunnel narrowed, widened, narrowed again. Meltwater dripped somewhere above in a rhythm almost like speech. His breath hung in the lamplight. And then the passage turned, and the ceiling lifted, and he stepped out into a chamber that stopped his heart and restarted it wrong.

It was a cathedral of ice. The walls rose forty feet, ribbed and translucent, glowing faintly with the first gray light filtering down through crevasses far overhead. And at its center, where the glacier had ground back against bedrock, water welled up out of the earth itself — a spring, small and furious and impossibly blue, boiling gently up through a seam in the stone. The source of the Kestrel. The place on no map. The place Ingrid Nystrom had sealed her own expedition report to keep the world away from.

And beside the spring, sitting on a crate with her parka unzipped and her silver hair loose down her back, was Dr. Ingrid Nystrom.

She didn't startle. She had heard him coming — of course she had; she could hear a glacier thinking. She looked up at him across twenty years and five thousand trail miles, and her face did something complicated: relief and terror and a grief so old it had become architecture.

"You followed my markers," she said. Her voice was rougher than he remembered. Or maybe he remembered it wrong. Maybe he'd been remembering a recording instead of a woman.

"Your markers were wrong," Cyrus said. "Deliberately. You wanted them found by someone who knew how to read wrong."

"I wanted them found by you." She said it plainly, without apology, which was the most Ingrid thing possible. "Vale's advance team would have missed them. Anyone else would have. But you'd know. You always could find what I buried."

*What I buried.* He felt the words go through him like cold water. "How long have you been here?"

"Four days." She gestured at the camp that wasn't a camp — a single tent pitched in the lee of the ice wall, a stove, two crates, a coil of rope. A woman alone under a glacier at sixty-three years old, four days' walk from help, because she could not stay away. "I flew in before Vale's people reached the lower valley. I've been watching them come upriver on the satellite feed like watching a slow-motion accident."

"And you didn't call anyone. You didn't warn us off."

"I called you eleven times in five years, Cyrus." Her voice cracked on the last word, and she pressed her lips together until it steadied. "You didn't answer once."

* * *

They sat on opposite sides of the spring, the way they might have sat at a kitchen table in another life, with the water talking between them — that same bright, running chatter Anders had loved. Up close, Cyrus could see the cache behind her: a recess in the bedrock, dry despite everything, sealed under a slab of stone that had been pried loose and set aside. Inside, the dark shapes of field cases. Aluminum. Old. Stenciled letters he could half-read even from here: **HALLORAN EXPEDITION — 1971**.

"He really brought it all back down," Cyrus said quietly. "After he looted it."

"He brought back what he wanted me to find." Ingrid drew her knees up, wrapped her arms around them. She looked suddenly smaller, older, a woman made mostly of stubbornness and scar tissue. "The artifacts were real. That was the genius of it. Halloran looted the site — took pieces that belonged in this valley, in this country, pieces the local families had tended for generations — and replaced them with forgeries he'd bought in Lima. Then he wrote provenance for the fakes. Published. Built his whole reputation on it. And when I got here twenty years ago and started finding seams in his work — tool marks that were wrong, alloy signatures decades off — he was already dead and celebrated, and his students were everywhere, and Marcus Vale was his brightest disciple."

"So you confronted the foundation."

"I confronted the foundation." Her laugh had no humor in it. "And I learned what you learn when you bring truth to institutions: the truth is a cost, and reputations are assets, and they will always choose the asset. They offered me a settlement. Quiet funding, a chair, anything — if the discrepancies stayed between us. When I refused, they made it clear the alternative was worse. They would say *I* forged the evidence. A grieving woman, a failed expedition, a dead son — they had a story ready, Cyrus. They'd been writing it since the crevasse."

The chamber groaned around them, long and low, ice shifting its weight miles away. Neither of them flinched. You didn't flinch at glaciers if you'd been raised inside one.

"Anders," Cyrus said. Because there was no way around it, and he was done going around things. "Tell me what actually happened. Not the journal version. The true one."

Ingrid closed her eyes. For a moment she was so still she might have been carved from the same material as the walls.

"The route was mine," she said. "Every decision on that expedition was mine. The weather window was closing and I pushed. Anders flagged the snow bridge over the east crevasse — he said it sounded hollow. I told him we were losing the season and hollow meant careful, not turn back." Her hands were shaking now; she watched them as if they belonged to someone else. "He crossed first to test it. Because he was nineteen and strong and he wanted to prove he was his mother's son. And it held for him, and it held for the next two, and it broke under Tomas Ferro, and Anders went back for him without waiting for anchors."

"Tomas Ferro." The name landed like a stone. "Rhea's father."

"Yes." She opened her eyes. "You understand now why Rhea's crew was the only crew I ever trusted you with, even from a distance. Why I paid her father's medical debts quietly for years and never signed my name. The ledger of what I owe that family is longer than any field book."

Cyrus sat with it. Twenty years of a story he'd built from fragments — the silence, the sealed reports, the way his mother never said Anders's name above a whisper — and underneath it all, not villainy, just a woman who had made one fatal call and then spent two decades paying interest on it. It didn't forgive her. It didn't need to. It just made her human, which was somehow harder to hold.

"And the fraud," he said. "You buried that. You let Halloran's name stand. You let Vale build his career on it."

"I let it stand because exposing it required the ledger, and the ledger was here, under the ice, and every route to it was a route that had already killed my son." Her voice finally broke fully, and she let it. "So yes. I chose. I chose the living over the truth. I chose your childhood over history. And I have regretted it every single day in ways I cannot count, and I would make the same choice again, and I don't know what that makes me."

"It makes you my mother," Cyrus said.

The words came out of him before he'd decided to say them, raw and unpolished, and they hung in the freezing air between them. Ingrid stared at him. Something in her face — something armored for twenty years — began, very slowly, to come apart.

"I'm not here to absolve you," he went on, because honesty was the only currency they had left, and cheap comfort would poison it. "You were wrong to shut me out. You were wrong to let me grow up thinking the silence was my fault. And I was wrong to run five years rather than pick up a phone. We're both cowards, Mom. We just picked different directions to be cowardly in."

A sound escaped her — half laugh, half sob — and then she was up and crossing the ice between them, and he was standing, and they held each other in the blue light of the source of the Kestrel River while the glacier sang its ancient, indifferent song overhead. She was thinner than he remembered. She smelled of woodsmoke and cold stone. His eyes burned and he let them.

"I couldn't stay away," she whispered into his shoulder. "This is where both my boys are. Anders in the ground downstream, and you — God, Cyrus, I lost you too, I just didn't get to bury you."

"You didn't lose me." He pulled back enough to look at her. "You just had to walk upstream to find me. Like everything else in this family."

* * *

The cache came open between them, the way it should have twenty years ago.

Ingrid's hands knew the work — she'd pried the slab herself days earlier — but she waited, and Cyrus knelt beside her, and together they lifted out the field cases of the Halloran Expedition, 1971. Inside: notebooks in a tight, arrogant hand. Photographs. Shipping manifests. And the ledger itself — a fat, water-stained volume whose pages documented, in Halloran's own meticulous script, the exchange of authentic pieces for forgeries, itemized with prices, dated, initialed. Proof that the founding discovery of an entire academic dynasty was theft wearing a published citation.

"He numbered everything," Ingrid murmured. "Thieves always do. They can't help themselves. The precision is the pleasure."

Cyrus photographed every page with the camera Sela had given him, and then they repacked the originals. Above them, the glacier had changed its voice. The groans were coming more often now, deeper, and somewhere far above, ice was calving — he could feel it in the floor, a shudder like a struck bell. The warm front. The early season. Hours, maybe less, before this chamber's architecture failed.

"We go now," he said. "Together. Downriver, the emergency route — Sela mapped it. There are people waiting, Mom. Rhea. Sela. A man named Vale who is currently holding a copy of what we're holding and doesn't know yet that the original is real and his is worthless."

Ingrid shouldered her pack with the economy of a woman who had never once packed badly. At the mouth of the tunnel she paused, one hand flat against the ice wall, and looked back at the spring — the small furious blue heart of everything.

"Goodbye, Anders," she said softly. "We're taking the truth home."

Then the glacier groaned, long and splitting, and a crack raced across the ceiling like lightning frozen mid-strike, and Cyrus grabbed his mother's arm and ran for the light.
