## 10. Downriver

The emergency route Sela mapped was not a route so much as a rumor of one — a goat track stitched along the eastern wall of the gorge, half scree, half prayer. She had found it two summers ago on a patrol that officially never happened, and she had drawn it from memory onto the back of a topographic sheet with a grease pencil, the lines wobbling where her hands had been cold.

"It descends past the second hanging valley," she told them at dawn, pointing with her chin because her fingers were busy rewrapping Tomas's splint. "There's a bench above the lower falls where you can traverse west and pick up the old survey cut. Two days if the weather holds. Three if it doesn't."

"And if it doesn't?" Rhea asked.

Sela looked up at the glacier, which had been calving all night — great concussive booms rolling down the valley like artillery, each one followed by the long white exhale of powder snow. "Then you'll hear about it," she said. "That's usually how weather announces itself here."

Cyrus took point. It was what he knew how to do, what he had always known how to do: stand at the front of a line of people and read the ground ahead of them like a sentence he'd already memorized. The trail-finder's gift his mother had put in him before he could walk, the one he'd spent five years pretending was just a job skill. But this morning, moving through the blue shadow of the gorge with twenty-two people strung out behind him trusting his feet, he felt no armor in it. He felt only the weight of everything they were carrying down — Tomas on a litter between two strong backs, Vale walking unroped at the rear like a man who no longer believed the mountain owed him anything, and in Cyrus's pack, wrapped in his father's old survey tent, the ledger.

Halloran's field ledger. The true record of the Kestrel Source, written in a hand that started confident and ended drunk with its own importance: dates altered, artifacts listed twice under different provenances, and on the final pages, in ink darker than the rest, the inventory of what had been taken from the spring itself — and sold, and cited, and taught to three generations of students as gospel.

His mother had found this truth twenty years ago and buried it. He understood now why. Not to protect Halloran's name. To protect the living — the families, the funding, the whole fragile architecture of a discipline built on a lie. And it had cost her Anders, cost her Cyrus, cost her everything except the truth itself, which had waited under the ice like a seed.

He tested a boulder with his pole, watched it hold, and waved the line forward.

* * *

They made the bench by midafternoon of the first day, and there Sela stopped them, because below the falls the route crossed a shelf of talus that needed crossing in silence. While the crew rested, she sat apart on a flat stone with her satellite phone cradled in both hands, and Cyrus went and sat beside her without asking permission, the way he had learned she preferred.

"You're going to file it tonight," he said.

"Tonight. From the bench camp, while I still have signal." She didn't look at him. Below them the river ran milky with rock flour, braiding and rebraiding around stones it would carry to the sea. "The illegal salvage. The advance team without permits. Vale cutting the anchor line above the moraine to save an hour — that's what dropped the rockfall on Tomas. All of it."

"Rhea signed a secrecy clause."

"Rhea signed a clause that doesn't survive contact with criminal law, and I'll tell her so myself, gently, before the report goes public." Sela finally turned to look at him, and her eyes were steady and tired and very clear. "You know what's strange? Two years I've chased Vale through paperwork. I imagined this moment like a door closing on him. Now it just feels like writing down what happened. Which is all justice ever is, I think. Writing down what happened, when everyone would rather you didn't."

"Even when it hurts people you—" He stopped.

"People I care about," she finished for him. "Yes. Even then. Especially then. That's the whole test, Cyrus. Justice that costs nothing isn't justice. It's bookkeeping." She looked back at the river. "Your mother understood that. That's why she buried it — she thought silence was the price of protecting people. She was wrong, but she was wrong for the right reason. Vale is wrong for the wrong ones. The report won't pretend otherwise."

Cyrus reached over and laced his cold fingers through hers. She let him.

"Where does that leave us?" he asked. "You, me. A warden and a guide whose family name is about to be in every journal in the hemisphere."

"Somewhere downstream," she said, and almost smiled. "Everything ends up downstream eventually. We'll find out what the water keeps."

* * *

Vale did not sleep that night. Cyrus saw him sitting upright against his pack through the small hours, silhouetted against the glacier's glow, and near dawn Cyrus gave up on his own rest and walked over and sat down across the fire ring from him.

"I'm turning state's evidence," Vale said, before Cyrus could speak. His voice was scraped raw, as if he'd been talking to himself all night and finally decided to include someone. "Full cooperation. Halloran's correspondence, the sale records, everything I have in storage in Zürich. My lawyer will hate it. Let him."

"Why?" Cyrus asked. Not cruelly. He genuinely wanted to know.

Vale was quiet a long time. Above the eastern ridge the sky went from black to iron to a thin, reluctant rose.

"Halloran raised me," he said at last. "My father died when I was nine. Halloran hired me at nineteen, taught me stratigraphy, taught me how to read a river valley, taught me that a man's name is the only thing he takes into the ground with him. Everything I am, I built on his foundation." He turned his hands over, palms up, as though weighing something invisible. "And the foundation is rotten. Every paper I've published, every lecture, every student I've trained — all of it stands on a fraud, and worse, on Ingrid Nystrom's silence, which means my whole career has been standing on her grief without knowing it." He laughed, a dry sound with nothing in it. "I came up this river to destroy a ledger. Instead it destroyed me. That's fair, I suppose. That's more than fair."

Cyrus fed a stick to the coals and watched it catch.

"My brother is buried up there," he said. "Under the ice by the source. My mother has carried that alone for twenty years because telling the truth felt like digging him up again. I spent five years running from our name because I thought distance was the same thing as peace." He looked at Vale across the dead fire. "It isn't. Neither is silence. You don't get to choose whether the past is heavy. You only get to choose who helps you carry it."

Vale's eyes were wet in the firelight. He didn't wipe them. "Your mother said something like that once, at a conference in '98. Before any of this. I called it sentimentality." He shook his head slowly. "I've been climbing the wrong mountain my whole life, Nystrom. I finally see the summit, and it turns out to be a grave."

When the sun cleared the ridge, Vale called his lawyer, and the call lasted forty minutes, and when it was done he sat with his head in his hands, lighter and broken open at once, like a man who has set down a load and discovered the load was holding him together.

* * *

They reached the trailhead on the third afternoon, under a sky rinsed clean by wind, the peaks behind them smoking with fresh snow. There were vehicles waiting — park service, and beyond them, at a distance, a single figure standing beside a mud-spattered truck with a roof rack worn silver by decades of expeditions.

Ingrid Nystrom had come down ahead of them, alone, by the high route, because of course she had. She stood with her hands in her pockets and her white hair loose in the wind, and when Cyrus came off the last slope she didn't call out or wave. She just watched him come, the way she must have watched him leave five years ago, and the way she had once watched two sons walk up a river valley, one of whom never walked back.

He stopped in front of her. For a moment neither of them spoke, and the wind filled the silence with the smell of pine and glacier melt.

"The corrected record," she said. Her voice was rough. "You're publishing it. Under your own name."

"Under both our names," Cyrus said. "If you'll stand beside me. Co-authors. Partners. Not rivals' pawns — ours. Nobody else's."

Something moved through her face like weather — grief and relief and fear and underneath all of it, faint but unmistakable, joy. She reached out and took his hand, and her grip was still the grip that had hauled him out of rivers when he was six, firm and certain and warm.

"Together, then," she said. "For Anders. For the truth. For whatever comes next."

* * *

The paper appeared in the spring, in the Journal of Northern Archaeology, under a title that would be quoted for years: *The Kestrel Source: A Corrected Provenance.* Cyrus Nystrom, first author. Ingrid Nystrom, second. In the acknowledgments, one line: *For A.N., who loved this river first.*

The fallout was everything they had expected — retractions, furious letters, three careers quietly ending, Vale's testimony unfolding in a hearing room in the capital while Sela Vann sat in the gallery in her uniform, having filed her report knowing exactly whom it would wound, including, in ways no regulation covered, herself. Rhea's crew took the scandal standing; the money had been clean, and Rhea said so loudly and often, and contracts arrived anyway, because competence outruns gossip eventually.

But none of that was the ending. The ending came in June, when Cyrus and his mother went back up the Kestrel — legally, permitted, accompanied — and stood together at the source, where the spring came up cold and impossibly blue beneath the retreating ice, and laid a bronze plaque at the edge of the meltwater with a name on it and two dates and nothing else, because Anders had never needed a title. He had just been the first one up the trail.

Cyrus knelt and touched the water. It was freezing and alive and going somewhere.

"Downriver," his mother said softly, beside him, her shoulder against his. "Everything goes downriver."

"Yes," Cyrus said. And for the first time in five years, maybe in his whole life, he wasn't running from anything. He was carrying it — all of it, the grief and the name and the truth — and it fit in his arms like something he'd owned all along.
