## 1. The Long Way Down

The pass had a name on the maps — Col de Serraille, elevation 2,847 meters, rated "moderate" by the alpine club that had never once paid for my rope — but the people who actually crossed it called it the Long Way Down, and they said it the way you'd say a curse or a prayer, depending on which side of it you were standing.

I was standing on the wrong side. The high one. With a client.

"Is this necessary?" Aldous Pemberton-Whitmore asked me for the fourth time that morning. He was fifty-one years old, worth more than the mountain we were standing on, and dressed in gear so new it still smelled like the shop in Zurich where he'd bought it two days ago. His boots had never touched anything but carpet before Tuesday.

"No," I said. "That's rather the point."

He looked at me the way wealthy men look at things they've purchased and don't fully understand — with a mixture of trust and suspicion, as though I might be overcharging him even now, mid-moraine, with no receipts in sight.

"The eastern route is three hours faster," he said. "Your own itinerary says so."

"My itinerary also says *subject to guide's discretion*. Read your contract."

"I read my contract. It's forty pages of you not being liable for anything."

"That's the part most clients underline." I checked his harness for the third time anyway, because his knots were decorative at best and because checking knots gives your hands something to do when your head is somewhere else. Somewhere five years back and four hundred kilometers east, which is exactly where it had no business being on a clear morning in September.

The truth — the truth I wasn't going to hand him along with his invoice — was that I'd stopped taking the easy routes around the same time I'd stopped having anywhere to be. The eastern route was a road. Roads have stations at the bottom, and stations have telegraph offices, and telegraph offices have clerks who know your face from the notices pinned up behind their desks. The Long Way Down had scree, a glacier tongue, one fixed line I'd placed myself eleven crossings ago, and absolutely nowhere for anyone to find you except by walking it after you.

I moved constantly because stopping lets the past catch up. That's not poetry; it's logistics. Grief is a slow animal but it has excellent endurance, and if you stand still long enough — one winter in Chamonix taught me this, one bad winter — it walks you down like wolves walk down something wounded. So I kept moving. New valleys, new names on the client manifests, new versions of myself with slightly different hair and a slightly different story about why a woman with two decades of guiding experience wasn't in any registry you cared to check.

Eleven times I'd crossed this col. Twelve, by tonight.

"Watch your left foot," I told Pemberton-Whitmore. "There's a lip under the snow. It lies about where the ground is."

"Everything up here lies," he said, and surprised me by placing his foot correctly.

* * *

We made the glacier tongue by early afternoon, and that's where the mountain decided to remind us both who was in charge.

It does that. Not out of malice — mountains don't have malice, whatever the romantic writers say — but out of indifference, which is worse. A mountain that hates you will at least be consistent. This one just shrugged a shoulder of cornice off the ridge above us while we were crossing the flat white middle section, and the sound came down the valley like a door slamming in a house the size of a country.

"Hold!" I shouted, though he was already frozen, because rich men freeze beautifully; they've been trained for it in boardrooms. The slide went wide of us, a lazy gray tongue of it, maybe eighty meters to our right, and stopped against the lateral moraine with a sigh that sounded almost disappointed.

Pemberton-Whitmore's breath came out in a long white flag. "That was close."

"That was Tuesday," I said. "Close would've been interesting."

"You enjoy this." He said it as an accusation, but there was something underneath it, something almost wistful. "You actually enjoy this."

I thought about lying to him. Clients prefer their guides either fearless or fatalistic; what they can't metabolize is a woman who takes them down a dangerous route because the safe route lets her think too much. But the air was thin and I was tired of carrying fictions — I carry enough of them; they're heavy, heavier than rope.

"I don't enjoy it," I said. "I need it. There's a difference. Enjoyment is optional. Need isn't."

He nodded slowly, and for a moment he looked less like money and more like a man. "My doctor says I need to get away from the office. He means a spa. I keep coming here instead." He gestured at the whole indifferent white world. "Why do you suppose that is?"

"Because a spa doesn't try to kill you, and some part of you has started to suspect that everything comfortable is lying to you."

He laughed — a real laugh, startled out of him — and then we roped up for the icefall and didn't talk for two hours, which is the best kind of conversation.

* * *

By the time we came down off the last of the ice and onto the goat track that led to the valley station, the light had gone long and gold, the kind of light that makes even wrecked landscapes look forgiven. Pemberton-Whitmore was transformed. They always are, on the last hour. The fear burns off like morning fog and what's left is a strange, clean happiness that they'll spend the rest of their lives trying to buy again and failing, because you can't buy it; you can only be carried into it by someone who knows the way.

"You'll guide me again," he said. It wasn't quite a question.

"If you want the dangerous route, I'm your woman. If you want the safe one, hire someone with a license."

"I don't want the safe one." He dug into his jacket and produced an envelope — actual paper, actual wax, the man traveled like it was 1890 — and pressed it into my hand. "For next season. Name your route. Anywhere. I'll sign whatever disclaimer you write."

I pocketed it without looking. Looking would mean planning, and planning meant a future, and futures were a category of thing I'd learned to handle carefully, like unexploded ordnance. Take the work that's in front of you. Don't take the work that's six months out. Six months out is where the past sets its traps.

The valley station at Serraille-Bas was nothing: a platform, a ticket window, a telegraph office the size of a confessional, and a café that served coffee the color and temperament of mud. The evening train south wouldn't come through for another hour. Pemberton-Whitmore settled onto a bench with the boneless gratitude of the recently survived, and I went to settle accounts.

The clerk at the telegraph office was a boy of maybe nineteen, all elbows and boredom, and when I gave him Pemberton-Whitmore's name he shuffled through his pigeonholes and came out with a telegram, and then — this is the part I keep replaying, the part I should have seen coming — he came out with a second one.

"For you," he said.

"I don't have a name here."

He looked at me over the counter with the flat patience of the very young. "It says *the Sterling girl*."

The world did a small, quiet trick. The café noise went distant. Five years of careful geography collapsed into the space between one pigeonhole and the next.

Nobody calls me that anymore. Nobody living, anyway. That was my father's name for me, back when names were simple, back when Sterling meant something other than *struck from the register*. And there was exactly one person alive who knew I'd been working this valley, because exactly one person alive made a career of knowing things she shouldn't.

I took the telegram. My hands were steady, which surprised me; I'd have bet on a tremor.

The boy went back to his boredom. I stood there under the flickering gas lamp and read it twice, because the first time I read it too fast, the way you flinch away from a stove.

**STERLING GIRL STOP HEARD YOU TAKE THE LONG WAY DOWN STOP SO DO I THESE DAYS STOP HAVE PROPOSITION RE KESTREL STOP SIX TIDES STOP YOU KNOW WHAT THAT MEANS STOP VOSS**

*Voss.*

Mara Voss. The name sat on the paper like a splinter worked deep under the skin — too small to see properly, too deep to dig out, and every time you forgot about it, something pressed on it.

Five years since I'd heard it spoken aloud. Five years since the inquiry, when she'd sat across the table from me in her regulation-perfect salvage master's coat and answered every question with the precision of a metronome while my father's body was still technically missing and my licenses were being fed into the shredder. She hadn't lied, Mara Voss. That was the unforgivable part. She'd told the truth, all of it, in that calm ledger-keeper's voice, and the truth had done what truth always does in front of a tribunal: it had cut exactly along the lines someone else had drawn.

They took my coastal license first. Then the mountain one, on a technicality so thin you could read a newspaper through it. Blacklisted from two countries inside a year. My father dead under the Kestrel's hull, the wreck written off as unstable, the salvage abandoned, and every registry from here to the equator agreeing, in official ink, that Gideon Sterling was a name that shouldn't appear on paperwork anymore.

And now Voss wanted to talk about the Kestrel. Six tides. As if tides were something you could count toward a thing like that, as if the wreck were a destination instead of a wound.

I read it a third time. Then I walked to the iron stove in the corner of the telegraph office, and I fed the telegram into it, and I watched the paper curl and blacken and let go of her name one letter at a time.

*VOSS* burned last. Of course it did. Names like hers don't go quietly.

"Gideon?" Pemberton-Whitmore called from the bench. "Is the train—"

"Hour yet," I said, and my voice came out level, which cost me something.

I bought two coffees from the café out of pure reflex — the ritual of it, the warmth of the cup — and sat down beside my client and watched the light die on the western peaks and told myself the lie I'd gotten very good at telling: *that's finished, that's buried, that's on the other side of five years and a hundred borders and it cannot reach you here.*

But the bartender — a broad, silent woman who'd been wiping the same glass since we arrived — set down a fresh bottle and said, without looking at me, in the tone of someone mentioning weather:

"Strangers were asking after you last week. Two of them. Well-dressed. Wanted to know if the Sterling girl still guided the high passes." She set the glass down. "I told them nobody by that name comes through here."

"Thank you," I said.

"Don't thank me." She finally looked at me, and her eyes were older than the rest of her. "Thank whoever taught you to take the long way down."

Outside, far down the valley, the evening train whistled once — a thin, rising note, the sound of somewhere else arriving whether you're ready for it or not.

I looked at my hands. Steady. Still steady.

Six tides, she'd written. Whoever Voss was now — and people change, God knows I had — she hadn't sent that telegram to reminisce. You don't burn money on telegrams to dead names unless you're coming to collect something.

And somewhere out past the edge of the lamplight, down where the valley dropped away into dark, the past had stopped walking.

It had started running.
