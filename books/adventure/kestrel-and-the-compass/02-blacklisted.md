## 2. Blacklisted

The telegram sat on the bar in front of me like something that had crawled out of the bilge and died there.

I hadn't opened it. I didn't need to. I'd read the name through the envelope — you learn to do that, in my line of work, the way a sailor learns to read weather off the water. One word, block capitals, and it was enough to sour the rum I'd been nursing for an hour: **VOSS**.

Mara Voss. A name I had hoped was dead. Not because she was dead — Mara Voss would outlive the ocean itself, probably negotiate favorable terms with the heat death of the universe — but because I had spent five years doing the work of killing her myself, one day at a time, and I resented being told I'd failed.

The bartender, a woman named Dree with forearms like mooring line and the patience of someone who'd heard every story this port had to offer twice, refilled my glass without asking. That was the arrangement. I paid for silence and rum in equal measure, and Dree, who understood commerce better than most governments, delivered both.

"You going to open that," she said, "or are we going to watch it decompose?"

"Decompose," I said. "It's traditional."

She shrugged and moved down the bar to a fisherman who was crying into his beer about a wife, or a boat, or possibly a wife-shaped boat. Port towns run on grief the way other places run on coal.

I turned the envelope over. The paper was cheap and thin, telegraph-office stock, the kind that disintegrates if you look at it wrong. Somewhere behind me, a gramophone scratched through a song about a girl from the islands, and the rain came sideways off the harbor, and I thought: five years. Five years since the Kestrel. Five years since I'd been a person whose name appeared on documents without asterisks.

You want to know how a mountain guide ends up blacklisted from two countries? It's a fair question. The short answer is salvage law and bad luck. The long answer takes a night and a bottle, and I happened to have both.

* * *

Five years ago, in another port, in another life, my father Aldous Sterling stood on the deck of a survey vessel called the *Meridian* and pointed at a chart with a pencil worn down to a stub, and he said the words that killed him:

"There. That's her. The Kestrel."

He wasn't a dramatic man. He was a careful man — a man who checked knots twice and tides three times, who kept a logbook so meticulous that surveyors' offices used to borrow it as a teaching example. He'd spent thirty years running cargo and crew across waters that ate lesser captains whole, and when he retired — when he *thought* he retired — he took up the gentle trade of locating wrecks for insurers, pointing at drowned ships from the safety of a deck while younger men got wet.

The Kestrel was his white whale, except he never wanted to harpoon it. He wanted to find it, log its position, hand the coordinates to the insurance consortium, and go home to his tea. That was the plan. I've had five years to examine that plan the way you examine a wreck, and I can tell you exactly where it broke apart.

The insurance consortium was a front. Not even a good one — a shell company called Halcyon Recovery Group, which sounds legitimate the way "Honest Abe's Used Anchors" sounds legitimate. They held papers claiming salvage rights to the Kestrel, papers that were, as it later emerged, forged, or stolen, or some creative combination thereof. What they wanted was not the wreck's cargo manifest, which was modest — timber, machine parts, a strongbox of coin long since claimed by rust and sea. What they wanted was the wreck's *provenance*. Because the Kestrel had gone down carrying more than her manifest admitted, and somewhere in her hull was a sealed compartment, and somewhere in that compartment was a ledger, and somewhere in that ledger were names.

But I'm getting ahead of myself, the way grief always does. At the time, all I knew was this: my father found the Kestrel, reported it to Halcyon per his contract, and Halcyon did what vultures do — they descended, fast and loud, with a salvage crew and a legal team and a timetable measured in tides.

And my father, careful man, meticulous man, looked at their operation and didn't like what he saw. Bad rigging. Overloaded lift bags. A dive supervisor who'd been drinking at breakfast. He said so. Loudly. In writing. And Halcyon's response was to point at his contract, which contained a clause — clause fourteen, I could recite it in my sleep for years afterward — stating that the locating party bore no liability for recovery operations but retained, quote, "a moral interest in the successful conclusion of the same."

A moral interest. That's what they called it when they let him stand on the deck and watch them butcher his find.

I was there because I was between guiding seasons and because he asked, and because I was thirty-one years old and still said yes to my father, which is either a virtue or a character flaw depending on how the story ends.

The storm came in early. Of course it did. Storms in that stretch of water don't announce themselves; they arrive like debt collectors, already inside the door. Halcyon's dive supervisor — a man named Corrick, whose name I have not spoken aloud since the inquiry and will not speak again here — decided to push through it, because the tide window was closing and tide windows, unlike men, cannot be replaced.

My father went into the water.

Not to dive — he was sixty-three and hadn't dived in a decade. He went in because a lift bag fouled on the wreck's superstructure and a young diver named Tam was caught under it, and my father was the kind of man who went in after people. No suit, no tank, just a knife and a rope tied around his waist and thirty years of knowing that particular wreck's bones better than anyone alive.

He cut Tam free. Tam lived. My father did not.

The official finding was drowning, secondary to entanglement. The unofficial finding — mine, arrived at over many sleepless nights — was negligence, secondary to greed, with contributing factors including clause fourteen and the fact that dead sixty-three-year-old locators don't sue.

Here is where the story becomes mine instead of his, and I wish it didn't, but chapters are chapters.

I filed a complaint. Then a formal grievance. Then, when those vanished into Halcyon's legal machinery like coins down a drain, I did something less procedural: I talked. To journalists, to maritime board investigators, to anyone who'd listen, about the forged rights papers, the drunk dive supervisor, the lift-bag math that didn't add up. I was, I'll admit, not subtle. Grief had burned the subtlety out of me like a fire burns the paint off a door — underneath, everything raw grain and splinters.

Halcyon's response was elegant. They didn't sue me. They didn't need to. They simply made calls, and the calls made calls, and within four months I discovered that my mountain guide's license — issued by the Alpine Registry, renewed eleven years running, spotless — had been suspended pending review of "unverified client conduct." Unverified client conduct. I still don't know what it means. I suspect nobody does; I suspect it means whatever the person reading it needs it to mean.

Then the maritime board declined to renew my salvage certification, citing my father's incident as evidence of "poor judgment in professional association." My father's incident. His death was my poor judgment. You have to admire the craftsmanship of a sentence like that. Somebody was paid very well to write it.

Two licenses. Gone. Not revoked for anything I'd done — revoked for what I'd *seen*, which is the modern way. The registries don't punish crime; they punish inconvenience. And once your name is struck, it doesn't come back. There's no appeals process, no tribunal, no dusty office where a tired clerk reviews your case and sighs and stamps it approved. There's just the list, and your name isn't on it anymore, and everyone who checks the list treats your absence as answer enough.

So I did what blacklisted people do. I worked unregistered. Mountain routes under other people's permits, salvage runs under flags of convenience, my name on nothing, my face in no directory. I learned to move constantly, because a person who stays put accumulates paperwork, and paperwork is where they get you. Never the same route twice. Never the same port longer than a season. Never look back at the Kestrel — that last rule I made myself, carved it somewhere below the ribs, because looking back is how you drown, and I had done quite enough drowning for one lifetime.

That's the long answer. The short answer, if you're buying me a drink: I asked too many questions about a dead man, and the living men who'd killed him owned the questions too.

* * *

Back in the present, the rain had settled into that steady harbor rhythm, and my glass was empty, and the telegram was still face-down on the bar like a card I refused to turn.

Dree drifted back my way, wiping glasses that didn't need wiping. She has this habit of hovering when she wants to say something, the way gulls hover when you've got chips.

"Those strangers," she said, casual as a knife going into a drawer. "They were asking after you again."

I didn't look up. "What strangers."

"Couple of weeks back. Well-dressed. Harbor-coat well-dressed, not tourist well-dressed. Asked if I knew 'the Sterling girl.'" She set a glass down with a click. "I told them I didn't know any Sterlings. Which was true, near enough. You pay cash and you drink alone and nobody's ever called you a girl in my hearing."

"What did they look like?"

"One was forgettable on purpose. The other had a lawyer's hands." She meant soft, uncalloused, the hands of someone who'd never hauled a line. "Left a card. I threw it out. Seemed the neighborly thing."

"And now?"

"Now there's a telegram with a name on it that made you stop drinking for a full minute, which I've never seen, and I've seen you drink through a hurricane warning." She leaned an elbow on the bar. "Gideon. I don't ask what you do. That's our arrangement. But I know what trouble looks like when it sends mail ahead of itself."

I picked up the envelope. Cheap paper, Voss's name, and beneath it a reference number — Halcyon Recovery Group's filing format. I recognized it because I'd spent a year of my life staring at their documents during the inquiry, learning their bureaucratic handwriting the way you learn an enemy's accent.

Voss, writing on Halcyon letterhead. Or Voss, using Halcyon's number to make sure I opened it. Either way, the message was the same: *we know where you are, and we know what you used to be.*

I should explain about Mara Voss, since apparently she refuses to stay buried. We came up together — rival guides, rival salvors, two women competing for the same contracts in the same thin-margin years. She was everything I wasn't: licensed, connected, immaculate. She played the registries like a fiddle and won. I played them straight and lost everything. For years that difference defined us — her inside the law, me outside it, each convinced the other was the fool. The last time we spoke was at my father's inquiry, where she testified for Halcyon. Read from a prepared statement. Called the operation "regrettably unfortunate but procedurally sound."

Procedurally sound. My father's body came home in a box with a flag on it because of procedurally sound.

So yes. When her name surfaced on that telegram, I felt something that wasn't quite anger and wasn't quite fear and was mostly, if I'm honest, exhaustion. Five years of running, and the past had finally gotten tired of chasing and decided to send correspondence instead.

I carried the envelope to the lamp at the bar's end. Dree watched. The fisherman had stopped crying and started listening, because port-town fishermen are constitutionally incapable of minding their own business.

There was a matchbook by the lamp — Dree's, cheap ones, the kind that light on the second try. I struck one, held it to the corner of the envelope, and watched the flame take it. Telegrams burn beautifully; the paper's treated to survive handling, so it goes slow and thorough, curling black at the edges like a leaf in autumn. I dropped it in the ashtray and watched Voss's name blister and vanish.

"Gideon," Dree said quietly.

"It's handled."

"That's not what I was going to say." She glanced toward the door, then back. "The strangers. They weren't the only ones asking. There was a young woman, maybe a week ago. Different sort. Work-worn hands, diver's build. Asked for you by name too." She paused. "Said she was family."

The ashtray smoke curled upward, carrying the last of the telegram with it.

Family. I have precisely one member of that category still breathing, and she hasn't spoken to me in five years — not since the funeral, where she stood on the lawful side of the church aisle in her registry-issued mourning coat and told me, gently, terribly, that pursuing Halcyon would only destroy what was left of us. Wren chose the system that ate our father. I chose exile. We'd been choosing opposite exits from the same burning building ever since.

If Wren was asking ports after me, then either the world had ended or it was about to.

I put money on the bar — too much, deliberately, the way you do when you might not be back — and pulled my coat on against the rain.

"Gideon," Dree said again. "Whoever finds you next — you want it to be the ones with the lawyer's hands, or the ones with the diver's hands?"

Outside, the harbor lights smeared across wet cobblestones, and somewhere beyond the breakwater the sea kept its own ledger, patient and complete.

"Neither," I said, and stepped into the rain.

Which is, of course, when I heard footsteps behind me, and a voice I knew said my name — the full name, the one on no registry anywhere — and I turned around to find Mara Voss standing in the doorway with the rain streaming off her coat and an expression like a signed contract.

"I sent a telegram," she said.

"I read it," I said. "Briefly."
