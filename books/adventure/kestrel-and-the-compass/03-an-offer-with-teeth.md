## 3. An Offer With Teeth

I'd been in Saltmere Harbor for nine days, which was eight days longer than my rule allowed, and I told myself it was the engine part.

The engine part was real. A fuel injector for a boat I technically didn't own — Pell's name was on the registration, one of four names we rotated through like bad weather — and the only machinist who could cut the fitting to spec worked out of a shed that smelled of diesel and cat, and he worked at his own pace, which was geological. So I waited. And waiting, as any honest runner will tell you, is just drowning in slow motion with better scenery.

Saltmere was a good place to drown. It had the two things every port needs and no port admits to needing: a chandlery that didn't ask questions and a bar that didn't remember faces. The latter was called the Drowned Rat, which was either honesty or ambition, and its bartender was a woman named Corvo who had forearms like mooring line and a policy of serving anyone who could pay and forgetting anyone who couldn't. I liked her enormously. I also knew she'd sell me out for the right price, because everyone would, and I respected her more for not pretending otherwise.

On the ninth evening I was on my third beer doing my second crossword when the door opened and the room changed temperature.

You can feel it before you see it. The way conversation dips half a tone, the way the man playing darts suddenly develops an urgent interest in his own shoes. I didn't look up from seven across — *four letters, "to make amends"* — because looking up is how you tell the room who matters. But I already knew. Some arrivals you hear. This one I felt, like a pressure change before a squall.

Mara Voss sat down across from me without being invited, which took a certain kind of courage or a certain kind of stupidity, and Mara Voss had never once in her life been stupid.

She looked good. That was the infuriating part. Five years since the Kestrel hearing, five years since she'd stood up in front of the maritime board with her regulation haircut and her regulation voice and testified — accurately, damningly, *legally* — about the shortcuts Sterling had taken, and she'd walked out of that room with her license intact and her reputation polished while mine went into the shredder. She'd aged the way good rope ages: tighter, grayer, stronger where it counts.

"Seven across," she said, nodding at my paper. "Atone."

"I know."

"You were staring at it like it owed you money."

"It does." I set down the pencil. "Whatever this is, Voss, the answer's no. You haven't asked anything yet, but the answer's no. Consider it a courtesy. Saves us both the small talk."

"Nine days in Saltmere." She signaled Corvo with two fingers — a gesture so effortlessly entitled that Corvo, who served nobody twice without payment in advance, actually poured. "That's a long time for someone whose whole religion is movement. Engine trouble?"

"Personal matter."

"The injector housing on a thirty-year-old Kestrel-class tender cracks along the seam. There are exactly three machinists on this coast who can true it, and none of them are here." She sipped her drink. Whiskey, neat, expensive enough that I revised my estimate of her expense account upward. "So either you're lying to me, or you're lying to yourself, and honestly, Gideon, both options bore me."

Seven across stared up at me. *To make amends.* Four letters. I wrote ATONE in the boxes just to have something to do with my hands that wasn't reaching across the table.

"What do you want, Mara?"

"Direct. Good. I remember that about you." She set her glass down and produced a folder from inside her coat — actual paper, actual string tie, the kind of document that exists specifically so it can be burned. "Six tides from now, the Halcyon Consolidated salvage fleet puts to sea from Cape Merrow. Nine vessels, two dredgers, a dive support ship, and enough lawyers to constitute their own weather system. Their destination is a wreck site at forty-one degrees north, sixty-three west. Depth of ninety fathoms. You know the site."

My beer went warm in my hand. "I know a lot of sites."

"Don't." Her voice didn't rise. It did something worse — it got quiet, and precise, the way it used to get right before she dismantled somebody in a deposition. "Don't make me say the name in a public house. We both know which wreck. We both know what's on it. And we both know that under maritime custom — which is the only law that operates past the twelve-mile line, whatever the registries pretend — first vessel on site with a filed intent holds priority claim. Halcyon files in eleven days. If anyone else's intent is logged first, even a paper boat, even a ghost, they have to litigate. And Halcyon *hates* litigating. It's the one thing they're bad at."

"And you want my paper boat to file first." I laughed, and it came out ugly. "Voss. My name is struck from every registry that matters in two hemispheres. I can't file intent. I can't file a *complaint*. As far as the maritime authorities are concerned, I'm a rumor with a boat."

"That's why you're perfect."

"That's why I'm *unusable*."

"No." She leaned forward, and for the first time I saw something under the polish — not softness, Voss didn't do softness, but urgency, banked and controlled, like fire in a sealed stove. "Unusable is what you think you are because you've spent five years believing the blacklist is a wall. It isn't a wall, Gideon. It's a door that only locks from your side. A claim doesn't need your name. It needs a vessel, a course, and a timestamp. I can supply all three under my flag. What I can't supply is a crew that can actually *get there* through water that Halcyon's scouts will be watching, in a window that closes in six tides. Triple rates. Triple. Plus hazard pay, plus a bonus structure we can negotiate once—"

"No."

"—and I haven't even told you what's—"

"No." I said it flat, the way you drive a piton. No flex, no argument, nothing to hang a counteroffer on. "You want me to run back to the wreck that killed my father so that you can win a pissing contest with Halcyon, and you think triple rates is the number that unlocks me. Let me save us the next hour. There is no number. There is no rate. There is no version of this conversation where I point my bow at those coordinates. Burn your folder. Drink your whiskey. Find another blacklisted guide to exploit, because apparently we're a renewable resource."

Corvo, thirty feet away polishing glasses that were already clean, found something fascinating about the far wall. Port bars, I thought. Every conversation is a performance and everybody knows their cue to look elsewhere.

Voss didn't burn the folder. She didn't raise her voice. She did the thing I remembered hating most about her, which was simply *waiting*, unhurried, letting silence do the work that arguments couldn't. The darts player threw a bullseye by accident and nobody congratulated him.

"You testified against me," I said finally, because the silence needed filling and I'd rather fill it with knives than let her fill it with strategy. "Five years ago. In front of the board. Everything you said was true, which somehow made it worse. My father was dead three weeks. I hadn't even collected his effects. And you stood there in your pressed shirt and you read the log discrepancies into the record like you were reading a grocery list, and the board ate it, and I lost everything, and you got promoted." I turned the beer glass a quarter turn. "So understand that 'no' is the polite version. The impolite version involves the harbor."

"I know what I did."

"Do you? Because I've had five years to imagine your reasons, and I've settled on three candidates. One: you believed it. Two: you were ordered. Three: it was convenient. And I genuinely cannot decide which one I despise most."

Something moved behind her eyes — fast, quickly caged. "The truth is worse than all three," she said. "But you're not going to believe it tonight, so I'll spend it differently." She untied the string on the folder. "Before you throw me into the harbor, you should know who's already on site."

"There's nobody on site. The fleet sails in six tides."

"The fleet sails in six tides." She drew a photograph from the folder and laid it face-down on the table between us, her hand flat on top of it. "Halcyon doesn't send lawyers first. Lawyers are for after. They send an advance team — divers, survey, site assessment. Small, fast, quiet. They went down ten days ago to fix coordinates and plant marker buoys so the fleet arrives to a tidy job instead of a search. Standard practice. Nobody talks about it because officially it doesn't happen."

"Then it has nothing to do with me."

"The advance team carries a radio schedule. Check-ins every twelve hours, encrypted, routed through a Halcyon relay. Forty-one hours ago the check-ins stopped." Her fingers tightened on the photograph. "No distress call. No mayday. Just silence. Halcyon's response has been to accelerate the fleet departure by two tides and classify the advance team's status as, and I'm quoting their internal memo, 'non-material to operational timeline.' Which is corporate for: we sent people down there and they stopped answering, and rather than admit it, we're going to pretend they were never there."

The bar noise seemed to recede, the way it does when your body decides some information deserves a quieter room. "People go dark on deep jobs," I said. "Equipment fails. Radios flood. It's tragic and it's common and it still has nothing to do with me."

"True." Voss turned the photograph over.

It was a personnel sheet — Halcyon letterhead, headshots in a grid, names printed beneath in the bland sans-serif of corporations that process human beings like cargo manifests. Six faces. Five of them strangers: hard-eyed contract divers, the kind of men and women who go wherever the money is cold enough to need them.

The sixth face was in the bottom row, third position. Younger than I remembered. Harder, too — the softness burned off her, five years of choosing the lawful path written in the set of her jaw. But I'd have known her at a thousand yards in fog. I'd have known her upside down. I'd known that face since before I knew my own, back when we shared a kitchen table and a father and the particular closeness of girls who only have each other.

**WREN STERLING. Dive Specialist II.**

The bar came back all at once, too loud, too bright. Corvo was still polishing. The darts player was losing now, on purpose or otherwise. And Mara Voss watched me across the table with the expression of a woman who has just placed her last card and is waiting to see if the table folds.

"She kept the name," I said. My voice sounded like someone else's. Gravel and rust.

"People keep stranger things." Voss slid the photograph an inch toward me. "Wren Sterling signed on with Halcyon fourteen months ago. Clean record, excellent references, certified to eighty fathoms. She volunteered for the advance assignment. Volunteered, Gideon. For the Kestrel site. Do you understand what I'm telling you? Your sister — who walked out of your father's funeral and straight into the arms of the same regulatory machine that erased you, who hasn't spoken to you in five years, who as far as I can tell chose Halcyon *specifically* — put herself on a boat to that wreck. And now she's silent, and the company she trusted has decided she's paperwork."

I looked at the face until it blurred. Five years of anger arranged itself in my chest, all of it load-bearing, all of it structural, and I felt it start — not to crumble, nothing so clean — but to *shift*, the way a hull shifts when the water comes in somewhere you didn't seal.

"Why show me this?" I asked. "If you wanted a crew, you could have led with the money and never mentioned her. This is leverage, Voss. You're spending your best leverage in a dockside bar on the first night. Either you're desperate, or—"

"Or I'm done being the person who wins by using people." She said it quietly, and it landed harder than any shout. "I told you the truth about what I did five years ago is worse than belief or orders or convenience. Here it is: I testified because I was *afraid*. Halcyon's founder held my financing. One word from him and I'd have joined you on the blacklist, and I knew it, and when they handed me the statement I read it aloud because the alternative was ruin, and I told myself it was integrity because the lies happened to be true." She finished her whiskey in one motion. "I have spent five years being exactly what the system rewards. And then I found out what the compass points to, and I stopped sleeping, and now I'm sitting in the Drowned Rat begging a blacklisted guide to help me commit what will absolutely be a crime, and I find that I've never been more comfortable in my life."

"The compass." I'd heard the rumors — everyone had. A brass instrument recovered from the Kestrel's chartroom during the original salvage, logged into evidence, disputed, sealed. Pointing wrong. Pointing at nothing anyone could chart. "What does it point to?"

"Not tonight." She stood, buttoned her coat, and left the photograph on the table between my crossword and my beer. "Tonight you have a decision. The fleet sails in six tides. Wren has been silent for forty-one hours. The tide tables favor a departure on the morning ebb, which gives you roughly—" she checked her watch, an old mechanical thing, no GPS, very her, "—eleven hours to assemble a crew insane enough to sail with you. I'll be at berth nine on the north quay. If you're not there by dawn, I go alone, and I lose, and probably so does she, and I'll live with it the way I've lived with everything else. Badly."

She put money on the table — too much, deliberately, a flourish I might have admired in a kinder mood — and walked to the door. Then she stopped, half-turned, and delivered the last blow with the precision of someone who'd saved it for exactly that moment:

"For what it's worth, Gideon — the reason I came to you first, before the money, before the plan, is that your father once told me that if I ever found myself standing in front of a locked door, I should find the Sterling girl. He said you'd always known which side of the lock mattered." She shrugged. "I thought he was being sentimental. Turns out he was giving directions."

The door closed behind her. The bar exhaled. Somewhere the darts player hit the wall.

I sat there for a long time with the photograph in both hands, my thumb covering the printed name because I didn't need to read it, I'd have known her anywhere, I'd have known her drowned. Eleven hours. Six tides. Ninety fathoms of black water and a wreck I'd sworn on my father's grave never to look at again, and my sister — my furious, lawful, unforgiven sister — down there in the dark with a company that had already filed her under *non-material*.

Seven across grinned up at me from the page. *To make amends.*

"Corvo," I said, draining the beer and standing. "I need a phone line, a tide table, and whatever Okafor's drinking these days."

She didn't ask questions. That was why I tipped her well.

* * *

Berth nine, north quay, 0440. Fog coming off the water in ropes. And Mara Voss, standing on the deck of a chartered survey boat with her coat collar up, watching the gangway like a woman watching a door she wasn't sure would open.

I came down it alone, bag over my shoulder, no crew yet, no promises, nothing settled except the thing that had settled in me somewhere between the seventh beer and the fourth hour of staring at a photograph: that I was going back to the Kestrel. That I'd been going back for five years, every route I'd ever taken bending away from it like iron filings around a magnet, and that the only way out had always been through.

Voss searched my face in the gray light, looking for surrender, or fury, or terms.

"Triple rates," I said. "Hazard pay. Full veto on route and dive ops. And Voss — if we get there and this was a trick, if Wren's fine and this was all bait to get me on that wreck, I will put you ashore on the first rock I find and wish you luck."

"Understood."

"And if she's not fine—" My voice caught, and I let it, because I was done pretending things didn't cost. "If she's not fine, then God help Halcyon, because I won't be negotiating."

Voss nodded slowly, and for one unguarded moment she looked less like a rival and more like a woman carrying something heavy a very long way. "Welcome aboard, Sterling," she said. "Now — who do we wake up first?"
