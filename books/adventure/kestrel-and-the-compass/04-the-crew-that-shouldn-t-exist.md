## 4. The Crew That Shouldn't Exist

### Gideon

You don't recruit a crew. You apologize to one.

That was the thought running through my head as I climbed the rusted stairwell of the Meridian dry dock at four in the morning, carrying a bottle of Okafor's preferred brand of forgiveness — a twelve-year single malt I'd bought with money I didn't have, because debts between blacklisted people are never settled in currency.

Okafor lived in the dry dock because, as far as the maritime registries of four nations were concerned, he didn't live anywhere at all. They'd struck his engineer's ticket eight years back for a boiler explosion that the inquiry, in its infinite wisdom, blamed on his maintenance logs rather than the shipping company's decision to run a ferry twenty thousand hours past overhaul. The logs had been honest. The company hadn't. Okafor had learned the lesson every one of us learns eventually: honesty is a thing you do to yourself, like a hobby, because nobody's paying for it anymore.

I found him in the belly of a half-dismantled trawler, elbow-deep in an engine block, wearing a headlamp that made him look like a minor deity of rust and grievance.

"Gideon Sterling," he said without turning around. "I'd say it's been too long, but the last time I saw you, you were stealing my torque wrench, so my feelings are complicated."

"I returned that torque wrench."

"You returned a torque wrench. It was not the same one. I know my wrenches like you know your mountains, and that one had a stranger's soul."

I set the bottle on a workbench. He looked at it. Then at me. Then back at the engine, because Okafor has never once in his life let a person see him react in real time.

"Voss," he said. It wasn't a question. Word travels fast among the erased — we're a small nation with no borders and excellent intelligence services.

"Voss," I confirmed. "The Kestrel. Six tides. Triple rates."

He finally shut off the headlamp, and the dark came down between us like a curtain between acts. "You told me you'd rather die in a snowfield than take money from that woman."

"I've had five years to reconsider my death preferences."

"And the wreck itself? The one that took your father? You told me you'd never go back. You said it the way people say things they mean."

I had. I remembered saying it. We'd been on his boat, the night after the inquiry published its findings, and I'd been drunk enough to be honest and sober enough to remember it. "The wreck's not the point anymore. There's a girl on the advance team."

He was quiet for a long moment. Somewhere above us, the dry dock groaned as the tide came in, the whole structure breathing like something enormous and old.

"A sister," he said. "You never mentioned a sister."

"There wasn't one, for a long time. There was a girl at my father's funeral who stood at the back and left before the casket went down, and I hated her for five years, and it turns out she's my father's daughter too, and Voss put her on a boat to the Kestrel knowing exactly what that would do to me." I heard my own voice and didn't love it. Too much truth before dawn. "I'm not asking you to come for the money."

"Good. The money's an insult. Triple rates for a job that killed a better man than any of us." He picked up the bottle, weighed it, and set it down unopened, which from Okafor was a sacrament. "I'm coming because if Halcyon plants their flag on that wreck, they'll have won something they don't even understand they're fighting for. And I have spent eight years being told I don't exist by people who shouldn't exist either, if the law were honest, and I am done being polite about it."

He picked up his tool roll and slung it over his shoulder. "Your boat's still in the north basin. I've been keeping her honest. She leaks a little from the stern, out of sentiment."

"She always did."

"Like her captain. Come on. We've got a tide to catch and you've got two more apologies to make, and I want to watch both."

### Pell

Pell had a system, and the system was this: never be findable, always be reachable.

It had taken Gideon three days to find him, which by Pell's standards meant she'd gotten sloppy or he'd gotten sentimental, and he suspected it was the latter, because he'd let his forwarding code sit unchanged in the harbor office at Cael Port for a full season, on the theory that sooner or later someone from the old crew would come looking, and he'd rather it be sooner.

He was drinking coffee on the seawall when she walked up, and he made her stand there for a full minute while he finished it, because a navigator who jumps when summoned is a navigator who gets summoned.

"Gideon."

"Pell."

"You look terrible."

"You look employed. What happened?"

He smiled despite himself. That was the trouble with Gideon — she led with the knife and then handed you the bandage before you noticed you were bleeding. "Chartering. Legitimate work, if you can believe it. Ferrying survey teams up the coast. The paperwork is immaculate. I wrote it myself."

"Immaculate paperwork. From the man who forged a quarantine flag out of a bedsheet and a paint kit in the middle of the Halcyon embargo."

"That bedsheet saved six lives and cost me my ticket, and I'd do it again, which is precisely why nobody with a registry stamp will let me within a hundred meters of their manifests." He set down his cup. "So. Voss found you."

"Okafor says word travels fast."

"Okafor says a lot of things. Voss bought drinks for half the pilots' association last week and asked very specific questions about your routing habits. I know because I was one of the pilots she bought drinks for." He stood, brushed off his coat. "The Kestrel."

"You don't have to—"

"Stop." He held up a hand. "I've rehearsed this. You're going to offer me money, and I'm going to pretend to consider it, and we're both going to waste the morning on a fiction. Here's the truth: I have spent six years drawing charts for people who will never know my name, because the one time I used my name on something that mattered, the name got taken away. I want it back. Not the license — the name. There's a difference, and you of all people know there's a difference." He looked out at the harbor, at the survey boat waiting for him with its clean, lawful, forgettable work. "The Kestrel job. The real one, five years ago. My father ran cargo out of that port for thirty years before Halcyon's founder bought his route out from under him for a tenth of its worth, and when he complained, he got audited into the ground. I was a boy. I thought that was just how the sea worked."

"And now?"

"Now I know it's how theft works, wearing a sea costume." He turned back to her. "I'm in. But I'm navigating, not advising. If you want my charts, you take my heading. All of it."

Gideon almost smiled. "You always were a tyrant with a parallel rule."

"It's the only way a ship stays a ship. When do we sail?"

"Morning tide. Sefu's the last piece."

Pell's face did something complicated. "Sefu. How is the old shark?"

"Alive, as far as I know. He's running dive charters out of Saltmere."

"He'll say no."

"He always says no."

"He always says no and then he's the first one on the boat." Pell picked up his sea bag, which had been packed, Gideon noticed, since before she arrived. "Morning tide. Don't be late. I hate starting a voyage by waiting for its captain."

### Sefu

Sefu saw them coming from two hundred meters out, because Sefu always saw everything coming — it was the whole architecture of his survival. He was mending a dive net on the pier at Saltmere, hands moving on their own, when he spotted Gideon Sterling walking the boards with Pell and Okafor flanking her like a verdict.

He kept mending. You never let them see you stop.

"Captain," he said when she was close enough to hear him without raising his voice. "You're a long way from your mountains."

"You're a long way from any water deep enough for what you used to do."

"I do shallow water now. Shallow water is honest. You can see the bottom, and the bottom can see you." He tied off the net and finally looked up. Okafor gave him a nod, Pell gave him a nod, and Gideon gave him nothing, which was correct, because what stood between him and Gideon Sterling was not the kind of thing you nodded across.

Five years ago, on the Kestrel job, Sefu had been the diver who came up first. He'd been on the wreck when the storm line moved in. He'd been the one who reported the conditions safe for one more descent — his assessment, his voice on the radio, and her father had gone down on that assessment and not come up. The inquiry had cleared him. The inquiry clears everyone it doesn't want to pay. But Gideon had never cleared him, and he had never asked her to, because he understood that some ledgers stay open.

"Voss," he said.

"Voss," she said. "The Kestrel. Six tides. There's a diver on the advance team I need to reach."

"Wren." He said the name gently. He was the only one of them who could. "I've heard the chatter. Halcyon's been hiring fast, loose crews for the survey. The kind of crews that don't file incident reports." He stood, slow, his knees announcing their opinions. "You're going to ask me to dive on that wreck again. The one where your father died. On my assessment."

"I'm going to ask you to help me get my sister off it. The wreck can rot."

"And after?"

"After, we'll see."

He looked at the three of them — the engineer the registries erased, the navigator the registries erased, the captain who'd erased herself — and felt the old pull, the one that had nothing to do with loyalty and everything to do with arithmetic. Four people the sea had already taken its fees from. Four names struck from every book that mattered.

"You know I took Halcyon money once," he said. "After the inquiry. Two seasons of survey work, because I couldn't get a berth anywhere else and I had a daughter to feed. I never sold anyone anything. But I took their coin, and I know how they think, and I know how their fleet runs." He rolled up the net. "If I come, that's what I bring. Not an apology. Intelligence."

"I'm not asking for an apology, Sefu."

"No," he said. "You're asking for something harder. You're asking me to go back down there." He shouldered his gear. "My daughter's grown. She runs her own boat now, clean papers, her mother's name. She doesn't need me to be careful anymore. Which means I get to be useful instead." He started up the pier, then stopped. "The boat's the old one? The Kestrel's shadow?"

"She's refitted. Okafor's kept her honest."

"Then she leaks from the stern, out of sentiment." He walked on. "Morning tide. I'll bring my own tanks. I don't trust rental regulators, and I trust this job less."

* * *

### Gideon

The boat — the *Long Way*, because I'd named her at three in the morning five years ago and never found the courage to rename her — sat in the north basin with her running lights off and her hatches dogged, looking like exactly what she was: a vessel that existed in no registry, answered to no port authority, and had been maintained by a man who loved her more than he loved most people.

We loaded through the small hours. Okafor took the engine apart and put it back together just to introduce himself to it again. Pell laid out charts I wasn't allowed to look at until he was done — his terms, honored. Sefu racked his tanks in the dive locker with the economy of a man who had done it ten thousand times and expected to do it ten thousand more.

At some point before dawn, the four of us ended up in the galley, drinking Okafor's whiskey out of mismatched cups, and nobody made a speech, which is how I knew it was going to hold.

"To the crew that shouldn't exist," Pell said, raising his cup.

"To the fleet that shouldn't win," Okafor said.

"To one more descent," Sefu said, and looked at me when he said it, and I held the look, because we'd earned that much from each other if nothing else.

"To Wren," I said, and we drank.

The tide turned at 05:40. Pell had it to the minute, because Pell always had it to the minute, and at 05:40 exactly the *Long Way* slid out of the north basin with no manifest, no destination filed, and no name on any book that any court would recognize.

Voss was waiting at the harbor mouth on her own boat, sleek and fast and flying no colors either, and as we fell into formation behind her I watched her silhouette at the wheel and thought: *I have made a deal with a woman I don't trust, to save a sister I don't know, on a wreck I swore never to see again, with a crew the world insists is imaginary.*

It was, all things considered, the most honest work I'd done in five years.

Sefu came up beside me at the rail and nodded at the horizon, where the sky was going the color of old brass.

"Captain," he said quietly. "That line of cloud off the east. You see it?"

I saw it. Low, hard-edged, sitting on the water like something waiting.

"Weather?" I asked.

"Maybe," Sefu said. "Or maybe it's just the sea, clearing its throat. Six tides, you said?"

"Six."

"Then let's hope the sea counts slower than Voss does."
