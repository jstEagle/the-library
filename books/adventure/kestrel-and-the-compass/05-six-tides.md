## 5. Six Tides

The *Magpie* handles like a drunk with a grudge, which is exactly what you want in a boat that's never been on any registry. Okafor rebuilt her from a decommissioned survey hull and stripped every transponder that could sing our name to a port authority. Pell swears she sails better than she deserves. Sefu just calls her "the bird" and feeds her engine oil like it's communion wine.

Me, I spent the first day out of harbor learning where Mara Voss liked to stand.

She favored the rail on the starboard side, fore of the wheelhouse, where the spray came over in long silver sheets and she could pretend she wasn't watching me watch her. She'd brought aboard two sea chests, a locked document case, and an air of command so practiced it had calluses. Five years ago I'd have called it arrogance. Now I recognized it for what it was: armor, same as mine, fitted by a different smith.

We were thirty hours into open water before she finally laid out the timetable, and she did it the way she did everything — with a chart, a grease pencil, and the assumption that everyone in the room was already halfway to agreeing with her.

* * *

The mess of the *Magpie* was built for four people and currently held six, which meant Voss's elbows were in my coffee and her chart was in my lap whether I wanted it or not. The others crowded around: Okafor with his arms folded, Sefu perched on the cooler, Pell standing in the doorway because he refused to sit at the same table as Voss until someone made him.

"Six tides," Voss said, tapping the grease pencil against the Kestrel's coordinates like she was knocking on a door. "That's when the Halcyon fleet clears its home berth. Twelve vessels. Two dredgers, four dive tenders, a claim barge with a magistrate's warrant already signed and waiting — they'll file the moment their anchor bites bottom."

"And if we get there first?" Sefu asked. He knew the answer. He wanted to hear her say it.

"Maritime custom," Voss said. "Older than any court that's ever embarrassed itself on this coast. First vessel on station holds salvage priority until relieved by lawful authority. Halcyon wrote half those customs themselves back when they were hungry. They can't argue them now without arguing away their own founding claims." She smiled, thin as a knife laid flat. "They're counting on us not knowing that. Or not being there."

Pell snorted from the doorway. "Custom. You want to hang a fortune on *custom*."

"I want to hang it on anything the courts can't touch," Voss said, "which is the only kind of hanging left to people like us." Her eyes came around to me. They were gray-green, patient, the eyes of a woman who had once out-negotiated a harbor master while both their ships were on fire. "Six tides, Sterling. The fleet makes eleven knots loaded. We make nine clean, ten if Okafor stops being gentle with the bird."

"The bird is not a metaphor," Okafor said. "She is a lady of advanced years and I will not have her spoken to like a tug."

"Ten knots," Voss repeated, ignoring him, "and we arrive with hours to spare. Eleven, and we arrive with a day. That's the whole arithmetic of this job. Everything else is weather."

I looked down at the chart. The Kestrel's wreck sat under a neat grease-pencil cross, forty fathoms down off the Teeth, in water I had sworn on my father's grave never to enter again. The route to it ran through three current systems, one fog bank with a reputation, and a stretch of reef that had eaten better boats than ours for breakfast.

"You've done this math before," I said.

"I do all my math before," Voss said. "It's why I'm still rich."

"You're not rich. You mortgaged something to put this crew together. I saw the paperwork you tried to hide in the second sea chest."

For the first time since she'd come aboard, something moved behind her face — quick, quickly buried. Pell shifted in the doorway. Sefu went very still, the way he does when he smells blood in the water but isn't sure whose.

"That," Voss said evenly, "is none of your business."

"It became my business when you put my sister on a dive team without telling me."

"Your sister signed her own contract. She's a grown woman with her own licenses and her own reasons, and unlike some people in this room, she didn't need five years and a photograph to decide whose side of this she's on." Voss rolled the chart closed with a snap. "You want to be angry at someone, Sterling, be angry at me. I can take it. But don't you dare spend this voyage being angry at her for going where you wouldn't."

Nobody breathed. The engine room hummed through the deck plates like a held note.

I should have thrown her overboard then. I thought about it — seriously, briefly, with Okafor already calculating the drag on the hull. Instead I picked up my coffee, which had gone cold, and drank it anyway, because my father raised me to waste nothing, including bad moments.

"Command structure," I said. "If we're doing this, we're doing it properly. This is my boat. My crew. You bought passage and a plan, Voss, not the helm. When we disagree, I decide. When we agree, I still decide, faster."

A muscle worked in her jaw. Five years ago I'd have read it as fury. Now I saw it was the effort of swallowing something whole.

"Agreed," she said. "Provisionally."

"There's no provisional on a boat."

"There's no boat without my money," she shot back, and then, before I could answer, she lifted both hands — surrender, or the shape of it. "Fine. Your boat. Your decisions. My timetable. Six tides, Gideon. Don't make me regret teaching you everything you know about racing a fleet."

"You didn't teach me anything," I said. "You just showed me what losing looks like. I took notes."

Sefu laughed — one short bark, quickly strangled. Okafor unfolded his arms, which from him was applause. And Voss looked at me across the cold coffee and the rolled chart, and for one unguarded second there was something in her face that wasn't rivalry at all. It might have been relief. It might have been grief wearing relief's coat.

Then it was gone, and she was Mara Voss again, and we had six tides.

* * *

The first two tides were almost peaceful, which should have warned me.

Open water has a way of sanding people down to their honest grain. Okafor lived in the engine room and emerged twice a day covered in grease and opinions. Pell navigated by dead reckoning and insulted the sun whenever it disagreed with him. Sefu ran dive drills with Wren's absence sitting on him like ballast — he'd trained her, years ago, back when we were all still licensed and legal, and he hadn't said ten words about it since we sailed.

And Voss stood at her rail and watched the horizon the way other people watch a door.

I found her there on the evening of the second tide, the light going copper on the water, and stood beside her because it was either that or go below and think too much, and thinking too much is how the past catches up with you.

"She's good," Voss said, without preamble. "Your sister. Best mixed-gas diver I've seen who isn't fifty years old. Calm on the line. Doesn't waste air."

"You've watched her work."

"I hired her to work. Watching comes free." A pause. Spray ticked against the rail. "She talks about you, sometimes. Not much. But when she does, it's not the story you'd tell."

"There is no story," I said. "There's a funeral and a choice and five years of silence. That's not a story, that's an accounting error."

Voss turned her head and looked at me, and whatever she saw made her decide not to push — which was new for her, and therefore suspicious. "The fleet departs in four tides now," she said instead. "After that, it's a straight race. No more margin for weather, no more margin for mistakes."

"We don't make mistakes. We make unscheduled learning opportunities."

"That's the Sterling I remember. The one who cost me the Corvus contract."

"You cost yourself the Corvus contract. You got greedy on the third leg."

"I got *ambitious*—"

"There it is," I said. "Same thing, different word."

And that was when Pell's voice came down from the wheelhouse, flat and careful, the way he speaks when he doesn't want to startle anyone but needs everyone moving yesterday.

"Gideon. Come look at this. Bring the glasses."

* * *

The cutter sat on the edge of the world, where the sea goes from blue to the color of a bruise deciding.

She was hull-down, barely a smudge against the last light — but she was the wrong smudge. Fishing boats wallow. Freighters plod. This shape was long, low, and *fast*, holding a course parallel to ours at a distance so precise it couldn't be accident. Nobody holds station that well by luck. You hold station that well because you're paid to.

"How long?" I asked.

Pell handed me the glasses without taking his eyes off the horizon. "Ninety minutes. Maybe less. She's been working up from astern — I caught her on the second sweep and told myself it was a trawler. It's not a trawler."

Okafor had come up without being called, wiping his hands on a rag that had given up long ago. He squinted at the smudge and made a sound low in his chest, the sound of a man diagnosing a terminal patient.

"Halcyon scout," he said. "Class-forty cutter, twin screws, maybe twenty-two knots. They run them ahead of the dredgers to fix positions and harass competition. Harassment's cheaper than litigation. Their words, not mine — I read it in a deposition once."

"Can we outrun her?"

"At nine knots? Against twenty-two?" Okafor shook his head slowly. "Gideon, you can't outrun a dog by being a slower dog. You have to stop being meat."

Behind us, boots on the deck plates. Voss arrived at the rail with her binoculars already up, and I watched her face do the arithmetic before she lowered them.

"They're not closing," she said. "They're pacing. Which means they're not here to take us — they're here to *report* us. Our heading, our speed, our composition. Somewhere east of us, twelve ships are going to adjust their departure to meet us at the wreck instead of racing us to it." Her jaw tightened. "They don't need to catch us. They just need to know where we are."

"Then we stop being knowable," I said.

"Fog bank's forty miles north," Pell said, tapping the chart he'd spread on the wheelhouse coaming. "The Serpent's Breath. It sits over cold water all season. We detour through it, we lose two hours minimum, and the currents in there are liars."

"Two hours against letting them fix our position for the next four days," I said. "I'll take the fog."

"And the cutter?" Sefu asked. "She sees us turn north, she knows why."

"She knows why," I agreed. "But knowing and following are different animals. Okafor — how fast can you give me a decoy?"

His grin came slow and terrible. "Define decoy."

"A light pattern. Something that says *we're still here, still on course, nothing to see* while we're actually forty miles north playing submarine in the soup."

He was already moving. "Give me an hour and the masthead lamp and permission to commit crimes against electrical theory."

"Granted, granted, and always," I said, and turned the *Magpie* north.

* * *

We ran dark after that. Running lights off, deck lamps hooded, the only glow aboard the green wash of instrument faces and Okafor's soldering iron spitting in the wheelhouse like a small angry animal. Behind us, true to form, the cutter held her station on our old heading, watching a set of lights that Okafor had taught to lie — a rhythm copied from a coastal freighter's running pattern, timed off a cam he'd machined from scrap, blinking our innocence out across the empty water.

"She thinks we're the *Meridian Star*," he said, not looking up from his work. "Or she thinks we're something with the same table manners. Either way, by the time she gets close enough to check the paint, we'll be fog."

The fog, when we hit it, was everything the charts promised and worse. The Serpent's Breath didn't roll in — it *condensed*, rising off the cold water in walls, until the world shrank to the length of the boat and the compass card and Pell's voice calling depths and drift corrections in a monotone that never wavered, never hurried, never once admitted that we were threading a needle blindfolded at eight knots.

I stood beside him for four hours of it. I don't know that I helped. But there are times when a helmsman needs to feel another pair of eyes on the same darkness, and this was one.

"Current's pushing us southeast," Pell murmured. "Half a knot more than the tables say. The tables are cowards."

"Noted."

"My grandmother sailed these waters. She said the fog bank was where the sea kept its secrets." A pause. "She also said it ate her brother's boat, so her testimony may be conflicted."

"Keep her between the secrets, then."

He adjusted the wheel a degree, two, and the *Magpie* leaned obediently into the dark. Somewhere behind us, miles south and falling behind, a class-forty cutter was staring at a lamp that lied beautifully, and somewhere east of that, twelve ships were making plans based on a fiction Okafor had built from spare parts and spite.

We lost the cutter at 0300. We lost the fog at dawn, coming out the far side of the Serpent's Breath into hard clean sunlight and open water, two hours poorer and infinitely richer in nobody-knows-where.

Voss met me at the rail with two cups of coffee, one of which she handed me without asking, which was either peace or strategy, and with her it was usually both.

"Clever," she admitted. "The light pattern. Very Okafor."

"Don't sound surprised."

"I'm not surprised. I'm *reassessing*." She sipped her coffee, eyes on the southern horizon where the cutter had been. "But understand what this buys us, Gideon. We shook her. We didn't blind them. A scout that loses contact reports lost contact, and fleets respond to silence the way bodies respond to blood loss — they send everything."

"So we have time."

"We have *less* time than the clock says. Four tides to their departure, and now they'll leave early, heavy, and angry." She looked at me sideways. "Which raises a question I'd rather ask now than at three in the morning when it matters."

"Ask."

"That cutter found us on open water, on our exact heading, ninety minutes after we cleared the last shipping lane." Her voice dropped, not for drama but for weight. "This boat doesn't broadcast. This boat doesn't file. Somebody told them where to look, Gideon — or somebody aboard is telling them still."

The coffee went cold in my hands, and not from the temperature.

I looked aft, down the length of the deck: Okafor in his engine room, Sefu checking dive gear, Pell at his charts, Voss at my shoulder with her careful gray-green eyes. Five people, one of whom had sent my sister into the deep without telling me, and any of whom — any single one — might have sold our wake to Halcyon.

Four tides, I thought. And now, apparently, a traitor.

"Find me a leak," I said quietly, "before I find one myself."
