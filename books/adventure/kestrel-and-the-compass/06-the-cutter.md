## 6. The Cutter

I have been navigating boats since I was nine years old, which means I have spent thirty-one years being blamed for the weather. It's the navigator's burden: you can chart a course around every reef, every shoal, every bureaucratic nightmare between here and the horizon, and if the wind turns ugly, somehow that's on you too. I've made peace with it. What I haven't made peace with is the particular species of dread that comes from looking at a chart and realizing the route you drew is wrong — not because you made an error, but because someone else is reading over your shoulder.

That was the shape of my evening, three days out from port, when the cutter appeared.

* * *

Let me back up, because Gideon will want me to be precise about this later, and precision is the one thing I own outright.

The *Magpie* — Okafor named her, after the bird that steals shiny things, because he says we are all thieves of one kind or another and honesty about it keeps the hull light — was running south-southeast along the shelf edge at a comfortable eight knots. Comfortable being relative. We had Voss aboard, which meant nothing aboard was comfortable; she occupied the galley like a customs inspection, asking questions she already knew the answers to and writing things down in a leather notebook that I would have paid real money to burn.

Gideon stood at the wheel. She always stands at the wheel, even when she doesn't need to, even when the autopilot could hold a straight line better than any human hand. I think steering is how she prays. I've never asked. You don't ask Gideon Sterling questions about her interior life unless you enjoy being looked at the way she looks at icebergs.

I was below with the charts, doing what navigators do, which is arithmetic. The Kestrel sat at coordinates that had been burned into all of us five years ago whether we wanted them there or not. Six tides to beat Halcyon's fleet out of its home port. Three days down, three to go, give or take the weather, which the barometer said was thinking about giving us something to remember.

Sefu came down first. He has a way of arriving without arriving — no footsteps, just suddenly present, like a change in pressure. He didn't say anything. He just pointed up, toward the aft hatch, and his face had the expression of a man who has found something in his soup.

I went up.

The cutter sat on the southern horizon like a splinter under the skin. Small, fast, white hull catching the last of the daylight, running a parallel course maybe four miles off. Not closing. Not falling behind. Just *there*, the way a shark is there, pacing a swimmer to see what kind of animal it's dealing with before committing to anything.

"Been there since the last light change," Sefu said. "Maybe longer. I only caught her because she turned."

"Turned?"

"When we turned. Two points to starboard, twenty minutes ago. She matched it." He shrugged, a big slow shrug that contained an entire philosophy of bad news. "Could be coincidence. Fishing boats wander."

"Fishing boats don't run white hulls with clean lines," I said. "And they don't pace a salvage boat for two hours without putting out nets."

Okafor came up the ladder with a pair of binoculars he'd clearly been holding for some time, which told me he'd spotted her before either of them and had been deciding how much to worry. That's Okafor. He worries in private so the rest of us can panic in public, division of labor being the foundation of any good crew.

"Engine signature's wrong for fishing," he said, passing me the glasses. "Too high-pitched. She's running turbines. That's a courier-class hull, or something built off one."

I put the binoculars up. The light was going amber and long, and through the glass the cutter resolved into exactly what Sefu and Okafor feared: lean, low, fast, with an antenna array that no fisherman ever bought. There was a name on her bow but the distance ate it.

"Halcyon," I said, because somebody had to.

Nobody argued. Voss emerged from the galley with her notebook closed, which I noted was itself remarkable — I hadn't seen her close it once since we sailed. She looked at the horizon for a long moment with the expression of a woman checking whether a bill had come due early.

"They're not attacking," she said. "They're mapping. They want our heading. Our speed. Where we stop, where we don't. By the time the fleet weighs anchor they'll have our entire route drawn in ink."

"Then we give them a boring route," Gideon said from the wheel. "We alter course, lose them in traffic, come back around."

"And lose a tide," Voss said. "Maybe more. We don't have a tide to spend on hide-and-seek."

"We don't have a boat to spend on pride, either," Gideon said. "Pell."

Everyone looked at me. This is the part of the job nobody puts in the recruitment brochures: the moment when six pairs of eyes decide that the solution to their problem lives somewhere in your head, and you have approximately ninety seconds to find out whether they're right.

I looked at the chart. Then I looked west, where the sun was going down into a smear of cloud, and then further west, to where the sea met a coastline I knew better than my own handwriting.

"There's fog," I said.

Voss raised an eyebrow. "There's fog most places, eventually."

"Not eventually. Tonight. Off Cape Merrow the cold current comes up against the warm shelf water and the whole coast goes blind — usually by midnight, sometimes earlier. It burns off mid-morning. If we can reach the fog bank before full dark and thread the channel inside it—" I tapped the chart, a narrow corridor between the Merrow shoals and the coastal rocks, the kind of passage that appears on charts as a dotted line and a warning symbol and, in older charts, a small drawing of a drowning man "—the cutter can't follow. Turbine boats don't do blind water. They'd have to stand off and wait, and if they wait outside, they learn nothing except that we went in."

"And if we hit a rock?" Sefu asked.

"We won't hit a rock."

"You sound very sure."

"I sound sure because doubt is contagious and I'm the only one aboard licensed to carry it." I rolled the chart flat and weighted the corners. "It's a night transit in fog through a channel I've run twice. Both times in daylight. One of those times I was nineteen and the other time I was fleeing a harbor authority, so my notes may be colored by adrenaline. But the channel is real, the depths are real, and I know the tide window. We go now, we catch the last of the flood, and the water under us is deeper than the bottom of my reputation."

Gideon studied me across the wheelhouse. She has a way of looking at people that makes you feel audited, and I mean that as a compliment — it's the look of someone who has learned that trust is a thing you verify rather than assume. Five years of never taking the same route twice will teach a person that.

"How long does the transit cost us?" she asked.

"Two hours against the open-water line. Maybe two and a half if the fog is thick enough to slow turns."

"Two hours." She said it the way other people say *surgery*. Then she nodded once. "Take us in, Pell."

* * *

Here is what threading a fog bank at night actually looks like, for those of you who imagine it involves drama and shouting. Mostly it involves radar, a stopwatch, and the profound loneliness of being the person whose word stands between everyone else and the rocks.

By midnight the fog had swallowed us whole. Not mist — fog, the genuine article, a wet grey wall you could lean against. The bow light barely reached the stem. The world shrank to the width of the *Magpie* and the sound of her engine, and everything beyond that was imagination, which is why you pay navigators: to keep imagination on a short leash.

Okafor rigged the decoy while I worked the channel. His idea, and a good one, because Okafor's ideas usually are once you scrape the salt off them. He strung a second set of lights on a small float raft off the stern — the kind of cheap battery rig he keeps in a crate labeled SPARE PARTS, DO NOT ASK — and set it drifting on a slow fuse of separation. From outside the fog, a radar or thermal eye would see two contacts: the raft showing navigation lights on what looked like a continuing southeast heading, and a confused scatter where we'd gone quiet into the murk. A probe vessel has to choose which contact matters. Choose wrong, and you chase a box of batteries across forty miles of empty ocean.

"I feel like we're abandoning a crewmate," Sefu said, watching the raft's lights dwindle astern.

"That raft has better manners than most crewmates I've sailed with," I said. "It'll die quietly and never complain about the food."

Gideon took the wheel herself for the transit. Of course she did. I called depths and headings; she made the helm answer. And I will say this for her, since I rarely say complimentary things within her hearing: the woman steers a boat through blind water the way other people walk familiar hallways. Every order I gave was executed inside a second, no hesitation, no second-guessing, which is rarer among skippers than you'd believe. Most of them want to argue with the navigator. Arguing with the chart is a hobby for people who plan to drown.

The channel itself was exactly as my memory had drawn it and twice as frightening. The shoal on the north side showed on the depth sounder as a sudden climb from forty fathoms to nine, and I called the turn south with my voice pitched carefully neutral, because a navigator who sounds scared produces a helmsman who steers scared, and scared hands overcorrect. Nine fathoms. Eight. The rocks off the starboard beam were close enough that I swear I could smell them, wet stone and kelp, though that may have been my imagination finally slipping its leash.

Then the sounder climbed again — the inner bar, the last gate — and dropped away into deep clean water, and we were through, and the fog closed behind us like a door.

I allowed myself exactly one breath of relief. Navigators ration relief. It spoils quickly.

* * *

The cutter did not follow. We heard her turbines pass to the east sometime after two in the morning, a thin high whine through the fog, running the line where we should have been. Whether she found Okafor's raft I couldn't say, but I like to think she did. Somewhere out there a Halcyon officer is writing a report about a suspicious contact that turned out to be a crate of batteries, and I hope it costs him a promotion.

We lost two hours and eleven minutes against the open-water schedule. I wrote the number down because Gideon would ask, and she did ask, at dawn, when the fog thinned to gauze and then gave out entirely and the sea came back gold and enormous and indifferent.

"Two hours, eleven minutes," I told her. "We make some of it back if we push eight and a half knots, but the old girl'll grumble."

"She can grumble." Gideon stood at the rail, looking back along our wake, and I watched her do the thing she never does — I watched her look backward — and I understood it wasn't the wake she was studying. It was the math. Her eyes were moving the way mine move over a chart, and when they stopped, they stopped on a point that had nothing to do with water.

"Pell," she said. "How did they find us?"

"The scout was positioned on the shipping lane. Could be luck."

"It wasn't luck. She turned when we turned. She knew our heading before we made it." Gideon turned to face me, and the morning light did her no favors; it showed every year of the five she'd spent running. "Our route. Our timing. Our departure. Somebody fed it to them."

"Voss planned the timetable," I said carefully. "Voss knows the route."

"Voss wants the wreck more than anyone alive. She's not selling her own race." Gideon's jaw worked. "Which leaves the rest of us."

"Sefu took Halcyon money once—"

"He told me. Years ago, desperation, done with it. I believe him." She said it flatly, and I believed her believing him, which is a strange sentence but an accurate one. "But belief isn't proof, and proof is what I need before I start throwing people overboard."

"Charming workplace culture," I said. "No wonder retention is so high."

Something almost like a smile crossed her face and died young. "Whoever it is, they've been talking since before we sailed. Which means they know the coordinates, the tide count, all of it. Which means the fleet isn't racing us, Pell. They're *hunting* us, and they've had the map the whole time."

I looked at the chart table, at my neat lines and careful figures, the route I'd drawn with thirty-one years of skill poured into every pencil stroke. And for the first time in my career, the numbers felt like they were being read by someone else.

Below us, faint through the hull, the radio room crackled to life. Sefu's voice came up the ladder, and it was the voice of a man carrying news he wished he could leave on the dock.

"Gideon. You'll want to hear this. It's Wren."

* * *

That's the thing about first blood in a race: you think the wound is yours until you learn whose it really is.
