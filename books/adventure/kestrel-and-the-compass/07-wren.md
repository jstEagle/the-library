## 7. Wren

The radio woke me at the wrong hour, which is the only hour radios ever choose.

I'd been sleeping in the chart room again — not because my bunk was bad, but because the chart room has a window, and I've spent five years learning that a person who can see the horizon sleeps better than a person who can't. Okafor says this is superstition. Okafor also talks to his engines by name, so I don't put much weight on his psychological assessments.

The burst came through at 0340, crackling and half-drowned, the way transmissions from cheap relay buoys always are. Pell had it on tape before I finished buttoning my shirt.

"Play it again," I said.

He played it again. Static, then a voice too compressed to be anything but machine-relayed, then static. But underneath the noise, in the gaps where the squelch breathed, there was a callsign. *Kestrel-Advance.* Wren's team. And beneath the callsign, three words that no advance dive team ever transmits unless everything has already gone wrong:

*Off-schedule. Off-contact.*

"That's it?" Sefu asked from the doorway. He slept in his boots; we all did now. "That's the whole message?"

"That's the whole message."

"No coordinates. No status. No 'we're fine, tell my sister to stop being dramatic.'"

"No," I said.

Pell ran the bearing twice. The signal originated from a relay buoy forty miles east of our position, which meant it had been rebroadcast from somewhere further out — somewhere near the Kestrel's coordinates, give or take the error margin of a buoy nobody maintained anymore. The timestamp said the original transmission had gone out six hours ago. Six hours of silence since.

Six hours is nothing. Six hours is a diver changing tanks, a radio operator eating dinner, a team going dark on purpose because they spotted Halcyon's scouts and didn't want to be found. Six hours is also, if you're trapped behind a jammed bulkhead with your air gauge bleeding down, most of the difference between a story and an obituary.

I knew all of this. I recited it to myself like a litany while the crew stood around the chart table watching me not fall apart, and I want it noted for the record that I did not fall apart. I did something worse. I got calm. The kind of calm that Okafor once described, after the Kestrel job, as "the quiet right before Gideon does something that gets us all blacklisted worse."

"Options," I said.

"We alter course for the wreck, direct," Pell said. "Fastest line. Also the most obvious line, which means if Halcyon's scout is still out there, we're handing them our heading gift-wrapped."

"We hold course and send a query burst," Voss said from the back of the room. She'd appeared the way she always did — silently, at the exact moment her opinion became least welcome. "Ask the advance team to confirm status. Standard protocol."

"They went off-contact, Voss. That's what off-contact *means*. Nobody's answering queries."

"Then we've lost nothing by asking."

"We've lost six hours by asking," I said. "Every hour we spend being careful is an hour Wren spends—"

I stopped. The whole room heard the sentence I didn't finish, and the whole room had the good grace to pretend they hadn't.

Voss studied me with those pale, patient eyes she probably practiced in a mirror. "You have personal history with the advance lead."

"I have personal history with my sister."

That landed in the chart room like a dropped wrench. Sefu looked at the floor. Okafor looked at me. Pell, bless him, looked at the radio, because Pell understands that some machines are easier to face than people.

"You didn't mention you had family on the advance team," Voss said.

"You didn't mention you'd hired one."

"I hired a professional with impeccable references and a reputation for doing exactly what she's contracted to do." A pause, delicate as a knife laid on silk. "The fact that she's also a Sterling was disclosed in the file you chose not to read."

She was right, which I hated. I hadn't read the file. I'd skimmed the manifest, checked the insurance riders, and skipped the personnel pages because reading names makes them real and I'd already agreed to enough real things for one lifetime. Somewhere in a folder on Voss's desk was a photograph of Wren in a dry suit, and I had chosen, deliberately, cowardly, not to look.

* * *

Here's what I know about my sister, and how little of it I have any right to know.

Wren is four years younger than me and taller, which she considers her single greatest achievement. She has our father's hands — broad, scarred, steady — and our mother's stubbornness, which is to say a stubbornness that looks like reasonableness right up until it becomes a wall. When Dad died, the registries held their hearings, and I stood up in that gray room and told the truth about what Halcyon's survey data had shown and what our skipper had been ordered to ignore, and the registries responded by striking my name from two countries' rolls and calling it due process. Wren stood up in the same room and told the same truth more carefully, with lawyers, in language the panel could digest without choking, and when it was over she still had her license and I still had mine revoked, and something between us broke along exactly that line.

She chose the law-abiding path. I chose exile. And for five years each of us had treated the other's choice as an indictment.

I sent her nothing at the funeral except myself, standing at the back in a coat I couldn't afford, leaving before the dirt went in because staying felt like admitting I belonged there. She sent me one letter, a year later. I never answered it. I told myself it was because answering would mean explaining, and explaining would mean defending, and defending would mean admitting there was something to defend against.

Five years of silence, and now she was lying somewhere under forty fathoms of cold water, or she wasn't, and either way the last thing I'd ever given her was a turned back at a graveside.

"Gideon." Okafor's hand on my shoulder. "Wherever you just went, come back. We need the version of you that steers boats."

"The version of me that steers boats is deciding whether to run for the wreck or run for the wreck faster."

"There's a third option," Pell said quietly. "We wait for dawn. We get a second transmission window off the relay buoy. If they're dark because they're hiding, a second window tells us—"

"If they're dark because they're *drowning*, a second window tells us where to recover the bodies."

Nobody argued. That was its own kind of answer.

* * *

We altered course at 0415. Direct line, full steam, consequences filed under later.

By 0600 the fog had burned off into a hard blue morning, the kind of beautiful weather that felt personally insulting, and I stood at the wheel watching the compass card settle onto our new heading and thought about my father's hands on this same arc of brass — no. Not this same compass. That compass was down there, mounted where he'd left it, in a hull that had killed him, and I had spent five years building my entire life around never going back to look at it.

Never taking the same route twice. Never looking back. Those weren't rules, whatever I'd told myself. They were flinches. And now the sea had reached up and taken the one person I'd been flinching away from hardest, and set her down inside the wreck I'd sworn off, and informed me that my rules were over.

Funny how the universe negotiates. It doesn't ask. It just presents terms.

Voss came up around noon and stood beside me at the rail without speaking for a long time, which for Voss is practically a hug.

"When we get there," she said finally, "if she's alive, she'll need a decompression schedule and a warm bunk and someone who isn't panicking. If she's not alive—" I started to turn on her and she held up a hand. "Let me finish, because this is the part you'll want to argue with. If she's not alive, you will want to go down after her anyway, past safe depth, past sane depth, and I am telling you now that I will order the crew to physically restrain you, because I did not hire you to die in that hull, and neither, I suspect, did she."

"You don't know anything about what she'd want."

"I know she took this contract knowing whose boat she'd be advancing for. She read the crew list, Gideon. She read *your* name and signed anyway." Voss looked out at the water. "Whatever you two broke, she was trying to fix it before you were."

I didn't answer. There wasn't an answer that wasn't either a lie or a wound, and I was saving both for later.

* * *

At 1900, Pell caught a second burst off the relay buoy. One word this time, or what might have been one word, shredded by interference: possibly *air*, possibly *aware*, possibly nothing but static shaped like hope.

Okafor spent twenty minutes with the tape and the spectrograph and came up grim. "Could be 'air low.' Could be a harmonic artifact. I can't certify it either way."

"Then we assume air low," I said.

"That's a big assumption to steer a boat by."

"It's the only assumption that keeps me useful. Steer by it."

Sefu surfaced from the engine room wiping his hands and said what everyone was thinking: "If Halcyon's fleet left port on schedule, they're three tides behind us. If they left early — if that scout bought them a head start — they could be closer. We might beat them there and find them already anchored over her."

"Then we beat them twice," I said.

"And if we can't?"

I looked at him. Sefu is the gentlest man I've ever sailed with and the only one aboard who's never raised his voice, so when he asks a hard question, you answer it honestly or you don't deserve the wheel.

"Then I go down alone, ahead of them, and I bring her up myself, and if the fleet wants to argue about salvage rights they can argue with the empty water where we used to be." I turned back to the compass. "This stopped being about the wreck somewhere around three-forty this morning. Voss can keep her timetable. I'm keeping my sister."

Behind me, nobody moved for a moment. Then Okafor said, quietly, to the engine room hatch, "You heard her. Full pressure on number two. She's carried us this far; tonight she carries us faster."

And Pell, at the radio, without looking up: "Bearing one-nine-five. Direct line. No more clever routes."

No more clever routes. Five years of zigzagging across two oceans, of never repeating a passage, of treating every straight line as a trap — and here I was ordering the straightest line of my life, straight at the thing I'd built the zigzags to avoid.

It should have felt like surrender. It felt like the first honest heading I'd steered since the funeral.

The Kestrel lay a tide and a half ahead, under forty fathoms, holding my father's ghost and maybe my sister's breath. Behind us, somewhere over the curve of the world, a salvage fleet was burning fuel to get there first.

I opened the throttle, and the *Marrow*, bless her rusted heart, leaned into the swell like she'd been waiting five years for me to stop running.
