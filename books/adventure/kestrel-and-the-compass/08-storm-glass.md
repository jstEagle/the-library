## 8. Storm Glass

I've been diving since I was fourteen, and in all that time I've learned exactly one universal truth: the sea does not care about your schedule.

The squall line proved it at dusk on the third day out, a bruise-colored wall of cloud stacked up along the western horizon like something the sky had been holding its breath to say. Pell saw it first — she always sees weather first, has some arrangement with the atmosphere I've never been able to negotiate for myself — and by the time she called it down to the deck, the barometer had already dropped enough to make my old knee ache in sympathy.

"Line squall," she said, coming down the companionway with her chart rolled under one arm like a baton she intended to conduct us with. "Thirty knots, maybe forty in the gusts. It'll be on top of us inside two hours."

Gideon looked up from the engine log. "Options."

"Two. We run north and shelter behind Cape Merrow until it passes. We lose six hours, maybe eight with the sea state after." Pell laid the chart flat and put one finger on a stretch of water that made me want to look away on principle. "Or we run the Serpent's Teeth tonight, ahead of it, and come out the far side having lost nothing but our nerve."

Nobody said anything for a moment. The *Kestrel* — no, that's not right, we never called her that, that name belonged to a grave — the boat hummed beneath us, Okafor's engines running sweet as a hymn, and I watched Gideon's face do the arithmetic that wasn't arithmetic at all.

"The Teeth," Voss said from the aft rail, where she'd been standing for three days like a woman waiting for someone to ask her opinion so she could pretend she hadn't wanted to give it. "You're seriously considering the Teeth. At night. In front of a squall."

"I'm considering losing a tide," Gideon said. "We don't have a tide to lose."

"We have less than you think," Voss said, and there was something in her voice then, a flatness, that made me look at her twice. Mara Voss had spent five days being carefully, professionally calm, the way rich people are calm about other people's risks. This was different. This was a woman doing sums she didn't like the answer to.

"How much less?" Gideon asked.

Voss held her gaze. "I'll tell you at the wreck."

I didn't like that. Neither did Gideon, judging by the way her jaw set, but she let it go, which told me more than any argument would have. When Gideon Sterling lets something go, it isn't forgiveness. It's triage.

She turned to the rest of us. "Okafor?"

Okafor wiped his hands on a rag that had stopped being white sometime during the previous administration. "Hull will take thirty knots if we take them head-on or quartering. Not abeam. Abeam and we find out what the plating remembers about the last owner." He paused. "Also the number two fuel line is weeping. I can bind it, but it wants replacing, and it wants replacing soon."

"Soon meaning?"

"Soon meaning before anyone asks this boat for anything heroic."

Gideon almost smiled. Almost. "Sefu. You know the Teeth better than anyone aboard."

That was true, and I hated that it was true. I'd run the channel four times, once legally with a survey contract and three times in circumstances I prefer to describe as "archaeological." The Teeth are a reef field that earned its name honestly — limestone spires rising out of forty fathoms like the graveyard of something enormous, with a winding channel between them that's deep enough for a boat our size only if you steer it like you're threading a needle wearing mittens. By day, with light and a steady swell, it's a challenge. At night, ahead of a storm front, with the wind pushing you sideways into rock that has been sharpening itself for ten thousand years?

It's a coin toss where heads means you live.

"They're saying the fleet left early," I said. "The scout ship bought them time. If we shelter, we arrive second."

"If we run the Teeth and lose," Pell said quietly, "we arrive nowhere, permanently."

Everyone looked at Gideon.

She stood there in the lamplight with the barometer falling around her like a verdict, and I thought about the first time I ever saw her, five years ago, before everything. She'd been younger then, and lighter, and she'd laughed at one of my jokes — an event so rare that Okafor still brings it up at anniversaries. The woman standing at the chart table now was carved out of what came after. But underneath the carving, the same person was still in there, and I could see her deciding.

Not whether the risk was worth it. Whether *we* were worth it. That's the thing people get wrong about Gideon Sterling. They think she's reckless. She's the opposite. She counts every life on this boat like coins she's already spent once and can't afford to lose again.

"Run it," she said. "Pell, you're on the helm through the channel. Sefu, you're my eyes forward — bow watch, big light, call every spire. Okafor, keep us alive mechanically. Voss—" a beat, and something dry entered her voice, "—stay out of the way and feel free to pray, if that's something you do."

"It isn't," Voss said. "But I'll make an exception."

* * *

The squall caught us twenty minutes before the channel mouth, which meant it missed us by nothing at all.

Rain came sideways, hard enough to sting, and the wind built from nothing to a shove in the space of ten minutes, the way squalls do — no negotiation, just arrival. I clipped onto the jackline at the bow with the big searchlight in my hands and the world reduced to a circle of white maybe fifteen meters across, rain hammering the lens, spray coming over the foredeck in cold slaps.

Behind me, muffled through the wheelhouse glass, I could hear Pell calling numbers to herself. She does that. Talks to the boat like it's a horse she's gentling. *Easy now, easy, two points to starboard, there's my girl.* I've watched her navigate through fog, through fire, once through a customs blockade with a forged manifest so beautiful the inspector framed it — I'm fairly sure he framed it — and I have never once seen her panic. Tonight, in the dark, with the Teeth ahead, she sounded exactly the same. That either meant everything was fine or that Pell's calm runs deeper than sense. With Pell, historically, both.

"Sefu." Gideon's voice in my earpiece, stripped down to essentials. "Talk to me."

"Mouth of the channel bearing green-two off the bow. Swell's building — three meters, maybe more, running with the wind. We'll be surfing." I swept the light across the water and found what I was looking for: the first spire, black and streaming, foam tearing off its crown. "First Tooth dead ahead. Channel's where it should be."

"Where it should be," Gideon repeated. "Comforting."

"You want comfort or navigation? Pick one."

There was a pause, and then, faintly, I heard her laugh. One short breath of it, gone as fast as it came, but real. In five years I'd heard her laugh maybe six times, and I counted every one the way a miser counts coins.

Then the channel took us, and there was no more talking.

I won't pretend I remember it clearly, because nobody remembers a night like that clearly — memory doesn't work at that speed. What I have instead is a series of frames. The light finding limestone walls sliding past close enough to touch. The boat climbing a swell and hanging at the top, weightless, obscene, before slamming down the back side hard enough to make my teeth click. Pell's voice, steady as a metronome, calling *starboard, starboard, hard over.* The second Tooth appearing out of the rain like a fist. Okafor's engines answering every demand without complaint, that man's machinery loving him back the way most people's families don't.

And once — once — the light found nothing. Open black water where the chart said reef, and for one full second I thought the swell had shifted us into a pocket I didn't know, and I felt the specific cold that comes not from rain but from knowing you're about to die.

"Bearing!" I shouted. "Give me the bearing!"

"Green-five!" Pell called back. "Trust the compass!"

I swung the light and there it was, the gap, narrow as a doorway, and we went through it with rock on both sides close enough that I could see the coral, pale and reaching, like hands.

After that it was ten minutes of the worst kind of flying, and then the channel opened, and the sea beyond was black chaos but honest black chaos, no rocks in it, just weather, and weather we could survive.

I stood at the bow with the rain hammering me and shook, quietly, the way you do afterward, and behind me the boat groaned once, deep, a sound I didn't like at all.

* * *

Okafor found the crack at midnight.

We'd cleared the squall's trailing edge and were running easy swells under a sky rinsed clean of everything but stars, and Okafor went below with a lamp to check his bilges the way he checks them every night, religiously, a man keeping his side of a bargain with a machine. Ten minutes later his voice came up the ladder, and it was very calm, which from Okafor is the equivalent of another man screaming.

"Gideon. You'll want to see this."

The cracked plate was amidships, low, on the port side — the side that had taken the quartering seas through the channel. A split in the plating maybe half a meter long, weeping seawater in a thin bright thread that Okafor had already stopped up with a wooden wedge and a wad of oakum, because of course he had; the man sleeps with oakum the way other men sleep with pillows.

"She held," Okafor said, running his fingers along the fracture. "But she's telling us she's done being brave. I can weld a patch in the morning, proper job, but she needs a slipway within the week. Real repair. No arguments."

"A week puts us at the wreck with two days' margin," Pell said, doing the math aloud because Pell always does the math aloud, it's how she thinks.

"Then we have two days' margin," Gideon said. "Patch it, Okafor. You did well."

"She did well," Okafor said, patting the hull like a horse. "I just listened."

He was kneeling there with the lamp between his knees, and in its light I saw his hands properly for the first time since the channel — knuckles split, palms raw where he'd gripped the throttle through the worst of it, blood dried black in the creases. He hadn't said a word. He never says a word. Okafor treats pain the way he treats rust: something you notice, address, and decline to discuss.

"Your hands," I said.

"What about them?"

"They're bleeding."

"They're hands," he said. "They've been worse." And he smiled at me, that wide unhurried smile, and went to find his welding kit.

* * *

I couldn't sleep after. That's common, after a night like that — the body stands down and the mind refuses to follow, replaying the frames, the black water where the reef should have been, the sound the hull made. So I went up on deck with tea I didn't drink and found Sefu — no, wait. I am Sefu. Let me start again, because this part matters and I want to tell it straight.

I found Gideon on deck. She was at the rail, looking south, toward where the wreck waited, and when I came alongside her she didn't turn, which from Gideon is a form of welcome. She saves turning for people she's decided about.

"You ran that channel like you'd dreamed it," she said.

"I've run it four times."

"Three times illegally."

"Once legally," I said. "A man must keep standards."

The wind had softened to a breeze. Somewhere below, Okafor's welder spat blue light through a porthole, steady as a lighthouse. I watched it for a while, and the thing I'd been carrying since the scout ship appeared on the horizon sat in my chest getting heavier, and finally I did the thing I'd sworn I wouldn't do.

"Gideon. There's something you need to hear from me before you hear it from anyone else."

She turned then. Of course she did.

"Three years ago," I said, "after they pulled my ticket — after the registry business, you remember — I was broke. Properly broke. Owed money to people whose collection methods involve boats and darkness. And Halcyon came to me. A fixer named Corrigan. They wanted survey data on the eastern approaches. Charts, currents, patrol schedules. Nothing about the Kestrel, nothing about your father — I swear that on whatever you want sworn on. Just general data. And they paid, and I paid my debts, and I told myself it was information, not betrayal. Information wants to be free, right?" I laughed, and it came out wrong. "That's what I told myself. For three years."

Gideon said nothing. The welder spat below. I kept going, because stopping would have been worse.

"And then that scout ship found us on a route only six people knew. And I thought — here it comes. Here's the bill for three years ago. Because that's how it works, isn't it? The sea keeps accounts. So either I tell you now, like a man, or I spend the rest of this voyage wondering when you find out, and I've done enough wondering."

The silence went on long enough that I started composing apologies, arranging them in order of desperation.

"That's why you've been watching Voss," Gideon said at last. "On deck. Watching everyone."

"I've been watching everyone my whole life. It's a professional habit. But yes. Lately more than usual."

"And the data you sold them. Could it have put that scout ship on our route?"

I made myself actually think about it instead of just wanting to say no. "No. What I sold was three years old and a thousand miles east. If Halcyon's on us, it's fresher than that. Someone's selling now, or something of ours is talking that we don't know about." I hesitated. "There's one more thing. Okafor's boat — this boat — she's carried a lot of electronics through a lot of years. Radios, transponders, gear he's installed and half-removed. If I were hiding a relay, I'd hide it in a history like hers."

Gideon looked at me for a long moment. Rain-wind moved her hair. Below us, the welder went quiet, and in the new silence I heard the hull ticking as it cooled, the boat talking in its sleep.

"Thank you," she said finally. "For telling me first."

"That's it? No fury? No dramatic accusations?"

"Sefu." Something crossed her face that on another woman might have been a smile. "You sold charts to stay alive. You didn't sell us. I know the difference. I've spent five years learning the difference." She turned back to the rail, to the dark water running south. "Besides. If someone aboard is selling, they'll sell again. And next time, I'd rather be the one who knows what's for sale."

Below, a hatch opened, and Okafor's voice floated up, tired and satisfied: "Patch holds. Somebody make me tea before I remember how much my hands hurt."

And then Pell's voice, from the wheelhouse, sharp enough to cut through everything:

"Gideon. Get down here. There's a signal on the band — burst transmission, short, addressed by name." A pause, and I heard her swallow. "It's for Wren's team frequency. It's a mayday."

The tea went cold on the rail. Nobody drank it. South of us, under the rinsed stars, something had gone wrong two tides early, and the race stopped being a race.
