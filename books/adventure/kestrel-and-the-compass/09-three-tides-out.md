## 9. Three Tides Out

I have been called many things in my career — ruthless, brilliant, a shark in a good coat, once, memorably, "a legal instrument with a pulse" — but nobody has ever accused me of being an honest woman. So when I finally decided to tell Gideon Sterling the truth about the compass, I did what any sensible person does before confessing: I waited until we were three tides out from the wreck, past the point of no return, with a crew of four exiles and one rival who'd happily throw me overboard if I gave her cause.

Timing is everything. My father taught me that. He also taught me to read contracts upside down across a desk, which is how he made his money and how he lost his soul, but that's another story.

The morning of the third tide was flat and gray, the sea like poured pewter, and Gideon was at the bow doing her ritual inspection of rigging she'd already inspected twice. She moved like a woman who trusted nothing that stood still — including herself. I brought two cups of coffee up from the galley, because I've learned that confessions go down easier with caffeine, and set hers on the rail beside her.

She didn't look at it. "If this is a pep talk, save it."

"It's a disclosure."

"That's worse."

I leaned against the rail beside her and watched the water slide by. Somewhere behind us, presumably, the Halcyon fleet was still chasing our phantom distress call toward a wreck site Pell had invented out of whole cloth — a fictional freighter at coordinates that would take their lead dredger a full day to disprove. Deception instead of violence. Gideon's idea. It had cost us our reserve fuel and bought us hours, and I was beginning to understand that this was simply how she operated: spend everything, trust nothing, keep moving.

"You've been wondering," I said, "why a woman like me hires a blacklisted crew for a job like this."

"I stopped wondering around the time you told me you'd already sent my sister down without asking." Her voice was level. Level the way ice is level. "Now I just want to know how deep the lie goes."

"All the way down," I said. "So let's start at the bottom."

* * *

Here is what the world knows about Halcyon Salvage: founded forty years ago by Edmund Rask, built on grit and innovation, savior of a hundred wrecks, employer of thousands, generous donor to maritime museums and judges alike. Here is what the world does not know, because I have spent eleven years making sure it stayed unknown until I could use it properly:

Edmund Rask did not find most of his wrecks. He stole them.

In the early days, salvage ran on crews — small, hungry, half-legal outfits that charted waters no corporation would touch. They found the ships. They marked them, logged them, filed their claims through whatever registry would have them. And then men like Rask arrived with better lawyers and worse morals, and the claims evaporated. A signature disputed here. A filing deadline missed there — often because the original crew's paperwork had been quietly delayed in someone's office. A blacklist manufactured out of thin air when a captain refused to sell. The registries weren't broken; they were owned. And the men who owned them kept ledgers.

Not metaphorical ledgers. Actual ones. Rask was old-world about his crimes — he wrote them down. Every defrauded crew, every forged denial, every bribe, every name. Dozens of outfits over twenty years. He kept the ledger in a vault aboard his own flagship, because he was arrogant enough to believe no one would ever dare touch it, and paranoid enough to want it close.

That flagship was the *Kestrel*.

* * *

Gideon had gone very still beside me. Not the stillness of surprise — the stillness of a woman holding something fragile between her teeth so it doesn't shatter.

"The *Kestrel* went down with your father," I said. "Rask's flagship, lost in the storm five years ago. Official story: cargo of bullion and company papers, tragic loss, salvage rights contested ever since. Unofficial story—" I took a breath, because this was the part I'd rehearsed in front of a mirror like a coward, "—your father wasn't just aboard. He was the reason the vault existed at all."

"Don't," she said quietly.

"He found Rask's frauds first, Gideon. Twenty-two years ago. Your father was a surveyor before he was a guide — he mapped sea floors for the early crews, and he saw the pattern in the denied claims. He confronted Rask. Rask couldn't kill him — too many people knew they'd met — so he did something cleverer. He hired him. Made him the keeper of the evidence. Told him it was insurance, that Rask needed a record to protect himself from his own partners. And your father spent two decades believing he was guarding a leash on a corrupt man when really he was guarding the corrupt man's trophy wall. Every name in that vault is a crew Rask destroyed. Including, eventually, the Sterlings."

"You're lying." But her voice cracked on the second word, and we both heard it.

"I'm many things," I said. "A liar when it's profitable, yes. But look at what lying would get me here. You'd throw me to the fish. No — hear me out, all of it, and then decide."

* * *

The truth, as much as I own any piece of it: three years ago, a recovery team working under Halcyon contract pulled a brass compass from the *Kestrel*'s chart room. Routine salvage, catalogued as a curio. It sat in a warehouse in Corvane for eighteen months until I bought it at auction, because I collect things connected to Rask — a hobby, I told people, though hobbies don't usually cost a year's fees.

The compass doesn't point north.

I discovered that the first night I owned it. It points south-southeast, steady as scripture, regardless of where you stand or how you turn it. I thought it was broken. Then I thought it was a bearing. Then I spent fourteen months and a small fortune learning what it actually was: a pointer. Rask had commissioned it as a joke for himself — a compass that pointed not to magnetic north but to his vault, keyed to the iron mass of the ledger case itself. His private north. The thing he trusted more than any human being.

It points into the sealed compartment off the *Kestrel*'s old cargo deck. The compartment your father helped design, Gideon. The one only he could open.

"And now he's dead," I said, "and the courts say the wreck belongs to whoever files first, and Halcyon has six dredgers and a legal department the size of a small navy coming to file. If they reach it, they'll strip the wreck for the bullion story, and the vault will end up in Rask's successor's hands — Aldous Rask, the son, who is not arrogant like his father but careful, which is worse. He'll burn the ledger and every name in it stays dead. Forty crews. Forty families who lost everything to a signature."

The wind had come up while I talked. Gideon hadn't touched her coffee. She was staring south-southeast, at nothing, at everything.

"Why tell me now?" she said finally. "Why not at the dock? Why not before I burned your telegram?"

"Because at the dock you'd have called it a trick, and you'd have been right to. Because I needed you committed to the race before you knew what the prize was — because if I'd told you the truth, you'd have refused. Not out of fear. Out of grief. Am I wrong?"

She didn't answer, which was answer enough.

"And because," I added, and here was the part I hadn't rehearsed, the part that came out rougher than intended, "because I owe a debt to one of those forty names, and I am not a woman who leaves debts unpaid. The *Meridian*. Crew of nine. Blacklisted in '09 on a forged safety violation. My father drafted the forgery."

Silence. Long silence. The boat creaked beneath us, patient as a confessor.

"Your father," Gideon said at last, "helped destroy my family, and you've been carrying his sin around like luggage, and you thought the way to pay it off was to hire me to rob a grave."

"When you put it that way—"

"It's the only way to put it."

* * *

She turned then and looked at me straight on, and I want to record, for posterity, what it feels like to be looked at by Gideon Sterling when she stops running: it feels like an audit. Like every entry in your books suddenly open to inspection.

"If the vault exists," she said, "and if it says what you claim — why not go to the courts yourself? You have standing. You have lawyers. You have a reputation I despise but which opens doors."

"Because the courts are where Rask won the first time," I said. "Because a claim filed through the registry gets assigned to a judge who owes his seat to Halcyon money, and the ledger becomes evidence, and evidence gets lost, and forty names stay dead forever. I've spent my career inside the law, Gideon. I know exactly how it works. It works for whoever paid last."

"So justice outside the law."

"So justice outside the law." I smiled, and it felt strange on my face, like wearing someone else's coat. "Welcome to my side of the water."

"Don't." The word came out sharp. "Don't welcome me anywhere. You sent my sister down ahead of us without telling me. You let me believe this was a treasure run. Everything you've said today might be true and it changes nothing about what you are."

"No," I agreed. "It doesn't. I'm the woman who used your sister as leverage and your grief as fuel. I've never pretended otherwise. But ask yourself one question, Gideon, and then decide whether to believe me."

"What question?"

"Your father spent twenty years keeping Rask's secrets. Why?" I held up a hand before she could answer. "Everyone assumes it was fear, or money, or some hold Rask had over him. But I've read the man's filings. I've read his survey notes from thirty years ago — meticulous, principled, the kind of work a man signs proudly. That is not the profile of a coward. So either your father was a collaborator, or he was playing a longer game than anyone gave him credit for. Either way — the answer is in that vault. Along with his name. Along with yours."

* * *

She walked away from me then, aft along the deck, and I let her go, because you can't rush a woman through the demolition of everything she believed about her own history. I drank both coffees. Mine had gone cold. Hers had gone cold too, and somehow drinking it felt like the most honest thing I'd done all day.

Okafor found me at the rail an hour later. He didn't ask what I'd told her — news travels through a small boat like current through wire — but he stood beside me and said, "If it's true, my brother's name is in that book. *Corvane Star*, 2011."

"Possibly."

"And if it's a lie?"

"Then she throws me overboard, and honestly, Okafor, some mornings that seems like the restful option."

He almost smiled. "Pell's got the cutter back on our wake. Far off. They've given up the phantom site."

Of course they had. Aldous Rask was careful, and careful men stop chasing ghosts when the real prize sits unguarded. We had perhaps three tides. Maybe less.

Below us, three fathoms down and fifty miles ahead, a drowned ship held a dead man's secrets and a living woman's inheritance, and I had finally handed over the map. Whether Gideon believed it — whether she'd use it or burn it the way she'd burned my telegram — was no longer mine to decide.

That was the moment Pell came up the companionway two steps at a time, radio handset in hand, face gone the color of the morning.

"Gideon needs to hear this," he said. "It's Wren's channel. Someone's transmitting her emergency code."
