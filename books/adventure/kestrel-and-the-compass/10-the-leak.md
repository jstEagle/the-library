## 10. The Leak

I want it on the record — my record, the one I keep in a greasy notebook in the engine room because paper doesn't crash and grease preserves things — that I found the signal before Pell did.

Pell will tell you otherwise. Pell tells everyone otherwise. That's what navigators are: people who have turned being right into a personality. But at 0340 on the fourth night out, when I went down to check the coolant lines because the port engine had developed a wheeze like an asthmatic accordion, my radio sweep caught something that shouldn't have been there. A carrier wave. Thin as fishing line, going out, not coming in.

Three seconds of burst transmission, encrypted, then gone.

I stood there with a wrench in one hand and my heart doing something unscheduled, and I did what any sensible engineer does when the boat starts talking behind her back.

I told Pell.

* * *

By dawn we had the whole crew crammed into the wheelhouse, which is designed for four people and a chart table, not five adults and one woman who treats personal space as a suggestion from a weaker government. Voss stood by the window with her arms crossed like she was posing for a portrait titled *Suspicious*. Sefu sat on the deck plates, back against the bulkhead, arms around his knees. Wren hovered near Gideon, close enough to touch, not quite daring to. And Gideon herself stood at the center of it all with the particular stillness she gets when she's deciding whether a problem is weather or a weapon.

"Say it again," Gideon said to me. "Slowly."

"Burst transmission. Three seconds. Directional, low power, aimed astern and down — someone who knew what they were looking for would catch it. Someone casual wouldn't." I wiped my hands on a rag that had given up being clean sometime last year. "It came from inside this boat."

"When?"

"0340."

"And how often has it happened before?"

That was the question, wasn't it. I'd only caught it once because I'd only been listening once. But Pell — of course Pell — had been keeping a passive log of the radio spectrum since the second night, because Pell keeps logs of everything including, I'm fairly sure, her own dreams.

"Four times," Pell said. She laid her logbook open on the chart table with the reverence other people reserve for scripture. "Night two, 0217. Night three, 0244 and 0511. Last night, 0340. Same profile every time. Short bursts, encrypted, directional." She looked up. "The intervals aren't random. They're spaced to avoid pattern detection by anyone running a standard sweep."

"Which means whoever's doing it knows how standard sweeps work," Gideon said.

"Which means it's one of us," said Sefu, quietly, from the floor. "Or it isn't, and I don't want to think about the third option."

Everyone looked at Voss.

Voss, to her credit, didn't flinch. She unfolded her arms and spread her hands. "Before we do the thing where we all stare at the outsider like she's got the plague ship's bell tattooed on her forehead — I've been on deck or in the cabin every night. Ask your sister. She sleeps in the bunk across from me and snores like a dying compressor."

"I do not snore," Wren said.

"You absolutely snore," Gideon and I said together, which was the first time in five days the two of them had agreed on anything, and I saw both of them register that fact with visible discomfort.

"This isn't about alibis," Gideon said, and the temperature in the wheelhouse dropped ten degrees. "Alibis can be arranged. This is about the cutter on night three. It found us in open water with no reason to be there. Someone told Halcyon where we were." She turned slowly, taking us all in, and I've known Gideon Sterling for eleven years and I have seen her afraid, furious, grieving, and drunk, but I had never seen her look at me the way she looked at all of us just then — like a woman counting her fingers after a card game and coming up one short. "So we're going to find it. Today. Before the next tide. Okafor, tear this boat apart if you have to. Pell, keep sweeping. Sefu—"

Sefu stood up. He's a big man and he stands up slow, like a drawbridge. "You want me to say it," he said. "So I'll say it. Four years ago I took money from Halcyon. A retainer. For information about coastal currents, they said. I gave them current tables. Nothing else. I never touched their ships, never ran their cargo, but I cashed their checks for eight months because my daughter needed surgery and the registries had my name on a list next to the words *unfit for hire*." His voice didn't shake. Sefu's voice never shakes; it just gets heavier, like ballast shifting. "I told you all this in the storm channel because I thought it might matter. If it's me you're looking for, you've already found me. If it's not — then somebody on this boat has a secret worse than mine, and I'd start with the one who profits most."

He looked at Voss when he said it. So did everyone else.

Voss laughed. Actually laughed, short and sharp, no humor in it whatsoever. "Profit? From selling your position to Halcyon? Sterling, use your head. If I wanted Halcyon to catch you, I wouldn't need a radio. I'd have booked you onto a route they already owned. I sent you out here because I need you to get there *first*. Every hour this boat loses is an hour my whole purpose rots. Why would I sabotage myself?"

"Because maybe your purpose is a lie," Gideon said. "Because maybe the compass story is bait and you're delivering this boat to Halcyon like a wrapped present. Because I have known you fifteen years, Mara, and every single one of them ended with you holding the winning hand and me holding the bill."

For a moment I thought Voss was going to swing at her. I honestly did. Her jaw did a thing, and her hands closed, and the whole wheelhouse held its breath like a flooded compartment.

Then she let it go. Let her hands open, let her shoulders drop half an inch. "Search my cabin," she said. "Search everything. If you find a transmitter on me, throw me overboard. I'll even help you tie the weight."

Gideon stared at her a long moment. "Don't tempt me," she said, and walked out to start searching.

* * *

We spent six hours tearing the *Marrow* apart, which is what we call the boat, officially the *Marrow of the Sea*, unofficially the Marrow, named by Pell after a word she read somewhere and refused to explain. I'll tell you what tearing apart a working boat means, because civilians think it sounds simple. It means pulling every panel off every bulkhead. It means emptying lockers that haven't been opened since the previous owner, a man named Dujon who sold her to me drunk and cried while he did it. It means finding things you forgot you owned — three kilos of dried fish nobody would claim, a child's shoe, forty-one cigarette lighters, a photograph of a dog — and cataloguing them while your stomach slowly ties itself into a knot shaped like the question *what if it's me?*

Because here's the thing nobody says out loud during a leak hunt: everyone checks their own gear first. Everyone lies awake replaying their own nights. Did I transmit anything? Did I touch anything? Is there some piece of my past wired into this boat that I've forgotten?

I checked my own gear first. Twice. My tools, my spare parts, my diagnostic rig, my personal kit. Clean. All of it clean. And still, somewhere under my sternum, a small cold voice kept saying: *you built this boat's nervous system, Okafor. If there's a wire in these walls that shouldn't exist, some part of it passed through your hands.*

I told the voice to shut up. Voices under the sternum don't shut up. They wait.

It was late afternoon when Pell found it. Not by searching — by listening. She'd rigged a direction-finding loop on a swivel mount in the wheelhouse and she'd been rotating it through the day like a woman churning butter, patient and mechanical, waiting for the next burst. At 1712 it came. Two seconds this time. Shorter. Hurried, almost.

Pell swung the loop. Called the bearing. Called it again. Then she called me on the intercom, and her voice had a strange flat quality I'd learned to recognize over the years as Pell being either very calm or very much the opposite.

"Okafor. The bearing resolves to a point four meters aft of the engine room bulkhead. Inside the void space behind the freshwater tank."

I knew exactly where that was. Of course I did. I knew every cubic meter of that boat the way you know the rooms of a house you grew up in.

And standing there with the intercom crackling, I remembered.

* * *

Here's the confession, and I'll give it straight because Sefu set the standard this morning and a man should meet the bar his friends raise.

Six years ago — before the Kestrel, before the blacklist, back when Gideon and I were still licensed and the world still returned our calls — I built a relay. A hidden one. A little autonomous burst transmitter with its own power cell, tucked into the void space behind the freshwater tank, wired to wake on a timer and sleep between transmissions. I built it because in those days I was doing a little quiet work on the side, moving data for brokers who paid in cash and asked no questions, and the relay let me pass messages without anything showing on the boat's official radio log. It was clever. It was elegant. It was, I told myself at the time, harmless — a tool, not a betrayal.

When the Kestrel job went down and everything burned — Gideon's father, our licenses, the whole bright stupid architecture of our legal lives — I stripped the boat of everything questionable. Or I meant to. I pulled the smuggler's locker, the false manifest plates, the second ledger. But the relay was behind the freshwater tank, and the freshwater tank weighs four hundred kilos full, and I told myself I'd get to it next haul-out.

Next haul-out never came. There were five years of running, and hiding, and grief, and the relay sat in its dark space behind the water tank like a seed in frozen ground, its timer ticking, its power cell — military surplus, rated for a decade — patiently waiting.

And when I rebuilt the *Marrow*'s systems for this run, I reactivated the old network architecture. Standard procedure. My procedure. And the relay, faithful and forgotten, woke up with everything else and started doing the only thing it knew how to do.

Transmitting. On schedule. Encrypted. To a dead frequency that hadn't had a listener in six years.

Nobody was selling us to Halcyon.

I was. By accident. By omission. By the oldest sin in engineering, which is assuming the past stays where you left it.

* * *

I carried the relay into the wheelhouse at 1900 and set it on the chart table, and I watched five faces look at it and then look at me, and I told them the truth in front of God and the barometer.

The silence afterward lasted long enough to age whiskey.

"So the leak was you," Gideon finally said. Her voice wasn't angry. That was the worst part. Anger I could have handled. Her voice was tired, the way it gets when the world confirms something she already suspected about how things go.

"The leak was a corpse," I said. "A dead circuit I built six years ago and forgot to bury properly. It hasn't talked to anyone. The frequency it transmits on has been silent since before the Kestrel. Nobody heard us. Nobody followed us because of it." I swallowed. "But the cutter —"

"— was coincidence," Pell said. She'd run the numbers twice, she told us, cross-referencing the cutter's approach vector against our actual position at each transmission time. The scout had found us on a heading projection, dumb luck and good seamanship, nothing more. "The timing doesn't match. The cutter was hunting a corridor, not a coordinate."

"So we lost a day," Sefu said, "to a ghost."

"We lost a day to ourselves," Gideon said. She picked up the relay, turned it over in her hands. Little thing. Smaller than a coffee tin. Six years of paranoia and near-mutiny in a package you could post with one stamp. "Every one of us walked onto this boat carrying something buried. Sefu's Halcyon money. Voss's real reasons. My father. Wren's five years of silence. And now this." She set the relay down gently, which somehow made it worse than throwing it would have. "Halcyon doesn't beat us, Okafor. We beat us. They just have to arrive in time to collect."

I couldn't argue with that. I've spent thirty years around machinery and I know how failures work. Machines rarely fail all at once. They fail by accumulation — the seal you didn't replace, the weld you didn't inspect, the small corrosion you decided you could live with. Boats sink by inches, by decisions deferred. Crews too, apparently.

"I'll strip the void space completely," I said. "Tank out, every centimeter inspected. Tonight. We lose the water pressure for a few hours, that's all."

"Do it," Gideon said. Then, quieter, in front of everyone, which I did not expect and will not forget: "Okafor. You could have sat on it. You found the signal before Pell did and you could have gone down there alone and quietly killed it and none of us would ever have known. Why didn't you?"

I thought about lying. Briefly. It would have been easy — I'm good with my hands and better with my face. But Sefu had stood up that morning and put his worst thing on the deck plates, and a crew that hides its corrosion is just a wreck on a delay.

"Because a boat is a system," I said. "And a system fails wherever you hide the fault. Better it fails here, in front of everyone, where we can fix it."

Voss made a sound that might have been a laugh or might have been a cough. "Poetry from the engine room. The registries really did lose something when they struck you all."

Nobody laughed. But something in the wheelhouse loosened anyway — a fraction of a degree, a held breath half-released. We weren't clean. We were never going to be clean. Five people with five buried things, sailing toward a wreck full of everybody else's buried things. But at least we'd stopped pretending the danger was only behind us.

* * *

I worked through the night. Tank out, void space open, every wire traced and logged. Found the relay's twin — a receiver, paired, also mine, also forgotten — and killed them both. Stripped the space to bare steel. Rebuilt it by 0400 with nothing in it but hull and honest rivets.

At 0430, Gideon came down with two cups of coffee, handed me one, and leaned against the bulkhead watching me wipe down tools that were already clean.

"One tide lost," she said. "Four remain."

"Four remain," I agreed.

She drank her coffee and looked at the open void space, bare and true, and said something I've been turning over since. "My father used to say the sea doesn't take honest men. It takes men who lie to themselves about the weather." She pushed off the bulkhead. "Get some sleep, Okafor. Tomorrow we make up the tide."

She climbed the ladder and left me alone with the engines, and I sat there in the smell of oil and salt, drinking coffee that had gone lukewarm, thinking about seeds in frozen ground and how everything you bury keeps a timer.

Then Pell's voice came down the intercom, tight and fast, and the tiredness fell right out of my bones.

"Gideon to the wheelhouse. Radio burst from the advance team's emergency band." A pause, and I heard her swallow. "It's Wren's code. The team's gone dark. And the burst originated from the Kestrel's coordinates — two tides ahead of schedule."

The coffee cup slipped out of my hand. It didn't break. Nothing on this boat ever breaks cleanly.

Above me, boots hit the ladder rungs, fast.
