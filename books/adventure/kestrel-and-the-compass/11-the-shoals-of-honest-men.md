## 11. The Shoals of Honest Men

I want it on the record — my record, the one that doesn't exist because I was struck from every registry that matters — that I told them about the shoals of honest men before we entered them.

Nobody calls them that officially. Officially they're the Corvane Approaches, patrolled by the Coastal Authority's Third District, a stretch of grey water where the paperwork has to be perfect and the paperwork is never perfect, which is how the Authority likes it. The imperfection is the product. You pay for the privilege of their looking away, and if you can't pay, you pay more. The men who run the checkpoint cutters are called honest the way a toll bridge is called free — you just have to find where the toll hides.

I'm Pell. Navigator, forger of manifests, keeper of the boat's paper soul. Five years ago the registries erased me over an arithmetic error that wasn't mine, and I learned something in the years since that I'll share with you now, free of charge: the law is a language, and like any language, it belongs to whoever speaks it loudest with the best accent. I have an excellent accent. It's the only thing they never managed to take from me.

* * *

We made the approaches at dawn on the fourth day out, running low on everything except secrets, of which we had a surplus.

"Third District checkpoint sits at the narrows," I said, spreading my charts on the chart table while the others crowded in the way people crowd when they don't want to admit they're crowding. "Two cutters, rotation every six hours, and a spotter plane when the weather allows, which today it will, because the weather hates us personally."

"Options," Gideon said. She'd been saying single words lately, the way a man conserves ammunition says nothing at all.

"Option one: we go around. Adds nine hours, puts us into the fleet's path, and we lose the tide." I let that sit. "Option two: we go through."

"Through means papers," Okafor said. He was wrapping his burned hands again, though he pretended he wasn't. "Our papers are ash. Literally. I watched Pell burn them in a barrel off Tarrow Point two years ago."

"I remember. It was a lovely ceremony." I pulled the folder from under the chart drawer's false bottom. "Which is why I've spent the last three nights writing new ones."

Voss leaned over my shoulder. She does that — leans, as though proximity were a form of authority. "The *Meridian Rose*, coastal freighter, registered out of—"

"Port Calloway. Cargo: machine parts, salt beef, and general stores. Master one A. Pell, which is close enough to true to survive scrutiny, because the first rule of forgery is to change as little as possible. Every lie needs a spine of truth or it collapses under its own weight." I tapped the manifest. "Crew of five, all listed, none of them under names anyone's hunting. Gideon becomes 'G. Stern,' deckhand. Voss, you're 'M. Vance,' supercargo."

"Supercargo," Voss repeated, tasting it. "I've spent eleven years being the person who inspects other people's cargo."

"Now you are the cargo's chaperone. Growth comes to us all."

Sefu laughed — a short, surprised sound, like he'd forgotten the machinery still worked. Even Gideon's mouth did something at the corner that in better light might have been a smile. But then she looked at the manifest again, longer, and I saw the thing I'd been watching her carry since Wren's radio went dark: not fear exactly. Fear would have been simpler. This was fear that had put on work clothes and reported for duty.

"Pell," she said quietly, away from the others, while Okafor went forward and Voss went to be inspected by her own reflection in the radar screen. "How good are these?"

"They're flawless."

"That's what worries me. Flawless costs money. Whose money?"

"The kind that doesn't ask questions afterward." I closed the folder. "There's a man at the narrows checkpoint. Inspector Dree. He took my bribe four years ago on a different boat with different lies, and he shook my hand after, and his handshake told me everything: he's not corrupt, he's *tired*. There's a difference. A corrupt man sells the law because he despises it. A tired man sells it because he's watched it up close for twenty years and knows exactly what it's worth."

"And what's it worth?"

"To Dree? About four hundred marks and a bottle of the good brandy. To everyone else—" I shrugged. "That depends on what we're carrying. And right now we're carrying a sister, a grudge, and a plan nobody's told me yet."

Gideon held my eyes for a moment. Then she said, "You'll hear it when it's ready," and walked away, which was both an answer and a promise, and I've navigated by less.

* * *

The narrows at midmorning looked innocent the way a customs office looks innocent: flat water, grey sky, two white cutters sitting fat and patient in the channel like spiders who'd read about patience in a book.

"Radio them our particulars," Gideon ordered. "Friendly. Bored. Bored is important. Nobody boards a bored freighter."

I keyed the set and gave the world the *Meridian Rose*: tonnage, port of origin, cargo, destination. My bored voice is my second-best forgery. The reply came back crisp and unhurried: *Meridian Rose, hold your station, inspection party inbound.*

"Inbound," Sefu muttered, coming down from the wheelhouse. "They never say 'we're just going to wave.' It's always a party."

"Because a party implies gifts," Voss said, appearing with the brandy already in a canvas sack, because of course she'd anticipated this; the woman anticipates weather systems. "Inspector Dree still stationed here?"

"Dree transferred to the northern patrol eighteen months ago," I said. "His replacement is a woman named Hask. Younger. Hungrier. I know nothing else, and 'nothing' is the most dangerous cargo on this boat."

The cutter came alongside with the practiced grace of a vessel that has done ten thousand boardings and enjoyed perhaps nine of them. Three officers came up our ladder, and the one in front was indeed young, indeed hungry, with the particular sharpness of someone who has recently discovered that the law is a ladder and she intends to climb every rung including the ones that haven't been installed yet.

"Master Pell." She read my name off the manifest without asking for it, which meant she'd read the manifest before boarding, which meant either diligence or a tip-off, and I've built a career on knowing which. Her eyes went around the deck — Okafor with his bandaged hands, Sefu at the rail doing an excellent impression of a man who has never been interested in anything, Gideon coiling rope with the mechanical calm of a person whose heart rate I could not see but could estimate. "Machine parts and salt beef to Calloway."

"General stores as well," I said pleasantly. "It's all itemized."

"Mm." She handed the manifest to the officer behind her, a soft-bodied man with the eyes of someone who reads documents the way other men read novels — slowly, and for pleasure. "We'll need below-decks access."

"Of course." I smiled the smile of a man with nothing below decks except machine parts, salt beef, and a sealed compartment containing a woman named Mara Voss, who was at that moment, I later learned, sitting on our one crate of actual contraband reading a shipping catalogue as though it were scripture.

Here is what happened next, and I tell it slowly because it happened slowly, the way disasters do — politely, with intervals.

Hask's reader went through the hold for forty minutes. He found the machine parts. He found the salt beef. He found the general stores, which included, thanks to my thoroughness, an invoice for rope from a chandler in Calloway whose letterhead I had reproduced so faithfully that I briefly considered framing it. He found nothing, because there was nothing to find, because I am very good, and I stood on deck in the cold watching him do it and felt the specific pride of an artist watching a critic circle a masterpiece and find no handle for his disapproval.

Then Hask came back up and said, "Your engine room, Master Pell. Routine."

And that's where it nearly ended, because Okafor's engine room contained a jury-rigged fuel reserve, a compressor with serial numbers ground off, and a workbench on which lay, in plain view, a half-disassembled radio relay that no legitimate coastal freighter has any earthly reason to own.

"Routine," I agreed, and my voice did not shake, because I had trained it not to, but inside I was composing my memoirs at speed and the title was *Everything Was Fine Until the Engine Room*.

Okafor stepped into the companionway ahead of them. I watched him go and thought: he'll stall. He's good at stalling. But Hask followed him down, and the reader followed Hask, and for ninety seconds the only sounds were the sea and the gulls and my own pulse conducting its private percussion section.

Then Okafor's voice floated up through the deck plates, warm and aggrieved and utterly convincing: "—no, ma'am, you want to mind the third step, she's slick, and whatever you do don't touch the blue valve, she bites—"

A pause. Hask's voice, muffled: "This relay. Purpose?"

"Condensation control," Okafor said, without missing a beat, and I could have kissed him. "Salt air plays hell with the wiring. You open the panels on these old girls, you find relays everywhere. Half of them I don't even remember installing."

Half of them he didn't even remember installing. Which was, I reflected, technically true, and also the entire problem with this crew laid bare in one sentence: we were all walking archives of things we'd rather forget.

Hask came back up. Her face was unreadable, which at her age and hunger is itself a skill, and possibly a warning. She looked at me. She looked at the manifest. She looked past me at Gideon, who was still coiling rope, and something passed across the young inspector's face — recognition? Suspicion? The memory of a photograph on a wall somewhere?

"Your deckhand," Hask said. "She's familiar."

"She has one of those faces," I said. "Very common. Practically public domain."

For three full seconds Hask said nothing, and the sea said nothing, and the gulls declined to comment. Then she closed the manifest folder and handed it back.

"Papers are in order," she said. "You're cleared through the narrows, Meridian Rose." And then, quieter, almost to herself, in the tone of a woman filing something away in a drawer she'll open later: "Safe passage."

They went down the ladder. The cutter pulled away. Nobody on my deck moved until it was a hundred meters off, and then everybody exhaled at once, a sound like a small storm.

"What did she see?" Gideon asked me, low.

"Nothing. Or everything. With that breed, it's the same thing." I watched the cutter shrink. "She let us go. Either because the papers were perfect, or because she wants something, or because some part of her — the part that joined the Authority before it taught her better — decided we weren't the criminals she signed up to catch."

"You believe that?"

"I believe," I said, "that the line between honest men and the other kind runs straight through every one of us, and mostly we're standing on it, shifting our weight."

* * *

We cleared the far buoy by midafternoon, and Voss finally emerged from below, brushed biscuit crumbs off the contraband crate, and delivered her report on the shipping catalogue ("outdated, but the typefaces are nostalgic"). That evening, in the galley, with the boat humming south and the fleet somewhere behind us and one tide gone forever, Gideon spread a chart on the table and began, at last, to show us what she'd been building in that locked room behind her eyes.

Not the wreck. Not the race. Something else.

"If we beat the fleet," she said, pencil moving, "we win a claim the courts will take years to honor, if they ever do. If we lose, Halcyon owns it outright. Either way, the ledger vault stays buried under lawyers." She drew a line that wasn't a route. It was more like a fault line. "So I'm done thinking about beating them. I'm thinking about making sure nobody wins."

Okafor looked up from his hands. Sefu stopped pretending to fix the coffee. Voss went very still, which for Voss is the equivalent of applause.

"There it is," I said, and got the look, and deserved it, because I'd known — I'd carried the plan's shape in my bones since the narrows, since Hask, since every forged page I'd ever signed. Justice outside the law isn't a loophole. It's a decision. Somebody has to pick up the pen and write the thing that isn't in the regulations, and sign it, and mean it.

Gideon looked around the table at the four blacklisted, erased, unregistered people she'd collected like driftwood, and said:

"Pell. How many copies of a document can you make that all look original?"

I smiled, and reached for my pens.

"As many as the truth requires," I said. "But you should know — whatever we're about to do, once it's on paper, it exists. Paper has a way of surviving things. People. Fleets. Even us."

Outside, the last light was going off the water, and somewhere south of us, one tide away, a strobe was blinking on a drowned ship, and my captain was drawing lines on a chart that no registry would ever recognize.

Good, I thought. Let them keep their maps.

We were about to draw a better one.
