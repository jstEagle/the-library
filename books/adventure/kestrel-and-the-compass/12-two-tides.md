## 12. Two Tides

The horizon lied to us at dawn.

I'd been standing the morning watch alone, which was how I liked it — just me, the rail, and the particular gray silence of open water before the sun commits to anything. Pell had the course plotted to the half-mile, Okafor had the engine humming the way he hums when he thinks nobody's listening, and for about forty minutes I let myself believe we were winning.

Then I saw the lights.

Not lights, exactly. A smudge. A suggestion of smoke against the paling east, where smoke had no business being, because there was no land out that way and no honest ship burns coal or diesel where nobody can smell it. I put the glasses on it and held my breath the way you do when you're asking the sea a question you're afraid to hear answered.

Three hulls. Low, wide, industrial. The lead one sat in the water like a filing cabinet that had learned to swim.

The Halcyon fleet. A full day ahead of schedule.

"You're staring at it like it'll apologize," Voss said behind me. She'd come up quiet, coffee in hand, hair still flat from sleep. She looked out at the smudge and I watched her do the same math I'd just done, watched her arrive at the same ugly sum.

"They were supposed to be a day behind us," I said.

"They were." She sipped her coffee. "The scout ship. When Pell shook it, we bought ourselves two hours and a fog bank. I think we also bought them a shortcut. They stopped pretending to probe and started running."

"Running toward what?"

"Us." She said it the way you'd say *rain*. Not cruel. Just weather.

I kept the glasses up. The dredger in the lead — I'd read Halcyon's fleet registry back when reading it was still legal for me, back before my name came off the lists — was the *Corvus Meridian*. Twelve hundred tons, a crane arm like a gallows, and a dredge system that could strip a wreck site down to bare sand in a week. It didn't dive. It *excavated*. There was a difference, and the difference was everything my father had died over.

"Options," Voss said, and it wasn't a question. It was an invitation.

I turned. She was watching me with that expression she'd perfected over twenty years of salvage law — the one that said *I have already thought of something and I'm curious whether you're brave enough to say it first.*

"Say it," I told her.

"We scuttle the *Corvus*."

The words sat there between us like something dead on a deck plate.

"Night approach," she went on, level, professional, the way she'd argue a motion in court. "They're heavy and slow. We're light and we know these waters now. We come alongside in the dark, Okafor puts a charge on her sea chest or her rudder stock, and the *Corvus* spends the next two days being towed to a repair yard instead of anchoring over our wreck. Nobody has to die. Halcyon carries insurance. It's a delay, not a massacre."

"It's a bomb," I said. "Put on a ship with forty sleeping men."

"It's a *rudder*, Gideon. It's a hole below the waterline in an empty compartment. I've read the *Corvus*'s class drawings. I know where she's soft."

"You've read her drawings." I laughed, and it came out with an edge I didn't bother to file down. "Of course you have. You've read everything. That's your whole tragedy, Mara — you know exactly how much damage a thing can take, and you've never once asked how much a *person* can."

Her jaw tightened. Good. I wanted her angry. Angrier than me, if I could manage it, because I was already angrier than I'd been in five years and one of us had to keep a clear head.

"Forty men," I said. "Dredge hands, welders, a cook, probably a kid or two on their first contract. You put a charge on that hull at night and you're gambling every one of their lives on your reading of a drawing. And if you're wrong — if one of them drowns because Mara Voss wanted a two-day lead — then we're Halcyon. Do you understand that? We become the thing with the fleet and the lawyers and the body count it calls *operational loss*."

"I'm proposing we win."

"I'm proposing we still recognize ourselves on Thursday."

She set her coffee down on the rail mount, slowly, with the deliberate calm of a woman choosing not to throw it. "You'd rather lose clean?"

"I'd rather *not become them*," I said. "That's not the same as losing. There's a third thing. There's usually a third thing. It's just harder to find and it never pays as well."

For a long moment she looked at me, and something moved behind her eyes — not agreement, not yet, but the grudging recalculation of a woman who'd spent her whole career inside the law and had only recently, painfully, discovered the law was a rented room. She wanted her one act outside it to be clean. I could see her wanting it. She just hadn't figured out yet that clean and violent don't share a bed.

"Then give me the third thing," she said. "You've got until they're hull-down on us. Which, at their speed, is tomorrow night."

* * *

I called a council in the wheelhouse, which is a grand word for six people crammed into a space built for two, arguing over a chart table that smelled like diesel and Pell's terrible pipe tobacco.

Okafor heard Voss's proposal secondhand — I gave it to them straight, all of it, the charge and the rudder stock and the forty sleeping men — and his face did something complicated. Sefu, who'd taken Halcyon money once out of desperation and had confessed it in a storm with bleeding hands and expected to be thrown overboard, went very still. Wren stood by the door with her arms crossed, still salt-crusted and hollow-eyed from the wreck dive, watching me like she was relearning her sister in real time.

"No," Okafor said finally, and his voice had gravel in it. "No bombs on sleeping ships. I've rigged charges my whole life and I'll tell you the truth nobody in this business says out loud: you never know. You never *know*. Water finds a way. Compartments that are supposed to flood slow flood fast. I won't carry it. I'd rather lose."

"Thank you," Sefu said quietly. It wasn't clear who he was thanking. Maybe all of us. Maybe the sea.

"So we don't fight them," Pell said, tapping ash into an empty cup. "We don't outrun them — they're faster on paper, and we've burned our reserve. We don't sink them. What's left?"

"Liars," I said.

Everyone looked at me.

"Halcyon is a company," I said. "Companies are machines, and machines do exactly what they're built to do. The fleet isn't sailing toward the *Kestrel* — it's sailing toward a claim. Coordinates. A fix. Somebody up in that wheelhouse has a position, and every hull in that formation is pointed at it. So we don't move the fleet." I put my finger down on the chart, well south of where we were, in a stretch of water where a freighter had gone down in the fifties and been salvaged to a shell decades ago. "We move the position."

Voss's eyes narrowed. She was ahead of me — she usually was, and I was learning to stop resenting it — but she wanted to hear me say it.

"Distress call," she said.

"Distress call," I agreed. "A salvage vessel in trouble, or better — a salvage vessel that's *found something*. A mayday that turns into a claim broadcast. *Unidentified wreck, substantial cargo, requesting salvage assistance, position follows.* Halcyon can't ignore it. Their whole legal strategy depends on being first on site at *a* wreck. If they believe there's a second wreck — a richer one, an easier one, one that's *already been located* — they have to split. Or chase. Either way, they burn hours, and hours are the only currency we've got left."

"They'll verify," Pell said. "They'll send the scout ahead, at minimum."

"Then we make the lie worth verifying. Okafor — can you build me a wreck?"

Okafor's slow grin was one of the few things in this world I still trusted completely. "Define *wreck*."

"Something that reads right on sonar. Something with a hull signature big enough to be interesting. And something that justifies a cargo claim — debris field, scattered metal, the works. We've got the *Kestrel*'s old survey data aboard, don't we? Voss?"

Voss was already moving, already pulling the waterproof case with the survey charts. "The *Andromeda*. Freight and ore carrier, went down in '54, salvaged in '71. Her keel's still down there, plus a scatter of hull plate and machinery across four hundred meters. She reads like a graveyard, because she is one." She looked up. "But Gideon — a claim broadcast means a position, and a position means when they get there and find nothing but a stripped hulk, they'll know they were played, and they'll know exactly who played them, and they'll come back angry."

"They'll come back angry anyway," Wren said from the door. Everyone turned. She hadn't spoken in the councils yet — she'd been a passenger, a rescue, a debt being repaid — and her voice came out rougher than I remembered it. "You think they were going to shake our hands? They sent a scout after us. They're a day early. They already know we're coming. The only question is whether they arrive while we still have air in the tanks." She pushed off the doorframe. "Lie to them. Lie big. I'll work the radio — I did two years of signals before I went legit, and I still sound like every other bored watch-stander in this ocean."

I stared at my sister. Five years of silence, one shared bulkhead, and now she was volunteering to be the voice of our fraud.

"You sure?" was all I managed.

"I'm sure I want to beat them," she said. "The rest we can argue about on the way north."

* * *

We spent the day building a ghost.

Okafor stripped the decoy light rig he'd built for the fog run and rebuilt it into something nastier: a cluster of strobes and work-lamps on a buoyed frame, weighted to sit just below the surface over the *Andromeda*'s bones, timed to flicker in the pattern of a salvage crew's night operations. From two miles off, in the dark, it would look exactly like what it was pretending to be — divers down, deck lights on, a crew working a claim they didn't want observed.

Pell and Wren took the radio. The plan was a two-act performance. Act one, a mayday from a fictional vessel — Pell supplied the name, the *Marguerite H*, and a registry number that belonged to a trawler scrapped in 1968, because Pell's paperwork was flawless even when it was fictional. Act two, forty minutes later, a follow-up transmission in clear: *situation resolved, no assistance required, we have located substantial wreck material, salvage claim being filed, position follows.* And then the coordinates of the *Andromeda*, and then silence — because nothing sells a lie like a man who stops talking the moment he's said too much.

Wren rehearsed it six times. On the fourth, she paused and looked up from the mic. "The *Marguerite H*. Where'd you get the name?"

Pell didn't look up from his charts. "My first boat. Before the registry took it. She burned at anchor in Suva with all my papers aboard and half my savings." He tapped his pen twice. "If her name's going to be useful for anything, I'd like it to be this."

Nobody said anything for a moment. There was a whole history sitting in that wheelhouse — four blacklisted sailors and one lawyer who'd defected, each of us carrying a name the registries had struck — and I understood, suddenly, that we weren't just lying to Halcyon. We were spending our erased names like currency, and for the first time it felt like spending rather than losing.

* * *

They came hull-down at dusk, exactly as Voss had promised, a smudge of smoke and steel on our southern horizon, and the whole crew gathered at the rail to watch them the way you watch a storm front — silently, with your hands busy.

Wren made the mayday call at full dark.

I stood in the wheelhouse and listened to my sister lie with a steadiness that scared me a little. *Mayday, mayday, mayday, this is salvage vessel Marguerite H, we have a diver injured, requesting — * a pause, a burst of static she manufactured by keying the mic against her sleeve — *stand by.* Then nothing. Then, forty minutes later, the follow-up: flat, bored, slightly rushed, the voice of a watch-stander who's already thinking about his bunk. The claim. The position. The click of the channel going dead.

Then we waited.

The fleet's formation changed at 0300. We saw it on Pell's bearing board before we saw it with our eyes — the lead smudge splitting, the *Corvus Meridian* holding course while two of her consorts peeled away south, toward the *Andromeda*, toward Okafor's flickering ghost lights and a freighter's stripped keel and a claim that didn't exist. The scout ship went with them, running ahead at speed to verify.

"Two hulls south," Pell reported, and his voice had the particular satisfaction of a man watching his paperwork perform. "The big one's still coming for us."

"Of course she is," Voss said. She'd appeared in the wheelhouse doorway, and there was no triumph in her face — only the arithmetic. "The *Corvus* is the claim ship. She carries the lawyers and the filing gear. She doesn't split off for a maybe. She goes where the *Kestrel* is." She looked at me. "We bought six hours. Maybe eight. The scout will be on that ghost wreck by mid-morning, and by noon they'll know."

"Then we dive at first light," I said, "and we're done before they're done being angry."

Nobody cheered. There's no cheering at that hour, when the enemy is a smudge on the horizon and your whole plan is a lie with a fuse on it. There was just the engine note, and the water, and the six of us in a wheelhouse built for two, all of us looking south at the lights we'd sent to die in our place.

Wren stood beside me at the chart table. She didn't say anything. She didn't have to. Five years ago she'd have told me lying was wrong, that there was a legal channel, that the system worked if you worked it honestly. Five years and one flooded cargo deck had taught her what I'd learned the hard way: the system was the flood, and sometimes the only honest thing left was a well-built lie.

"Eight hours," she said quietly.

"Seven and a half," I corrected, and turned the boat toward the *Kestrel*.
