## 13. One Tide

The gambit worked. That was the problem with gambits — nobody ever told you what they'd cost until the bill came due.

We'd left the Halcyon fleet chasing a phantom wreck forty miles east of anything real, Pell's forged distress signal so beautiful it had nearly made me weep. A dredger and two support ships peeling off at full steam toward coordinates that held nothing but cold water and their own greed. Voss had watched them go through the long glass with something like religious awe, murmuring, "I have spent twenty years filing paperwork that could never do that."

But the phantom wreck had eaten our reserve fuel to make the run look genuine — you can't fake a distress turn without burning the distance to sell it — and now the *Magpie's Daughter* was running on fumes and stubbornness, her engines muttering like an old man asked to carry furniture up stairs one more time.

"Reserve's dry," Okafor called up from the engine room, his voice arriving through the deck grating already resigned. "Main tanks give us arrival plus maybe ninety minutes of station-keeping. After that we drift."

"After that we drift," I repeated, because repeating bad news out loud is a superstition I've never managed to shake. If you say it in your own voice first, it can't ambush you.

Pell stood at the chart table with dividers in hand, measuring our future in small brass steps. She didn't look up. Pell never looked up when the numbers were ugly; she said looking at people while delivering arithmetic was how navigators learned to flinch.

"One tide," she said. "If we arrive on this heading, if the current holds where the almanac swears it will, if Okafor's engines don't develop opinions — we get one tide of margin. Not one tide of slack. Margin. The difference between those two things is everything, and I want it noted for the record that I hate everything."

"Noted," I said.

Voss leaned against the chart table's far edge, arms crossed, watching me measure the same water I'd been measuring since dawn. Five years ago I would have thrown her off my boat for standing that close to my charts. Three days ago I nearly did throw her overboard over a radio relay. We'd achieved some kind of equilibrium since, the way two knives achieve equilibrium in a drawer — not peace, exactly, but a mutual agreement about edges.

"You've been quiet since the fleet turned," she said.

"I'm always quiet."

"You're *brooding*. There's a difference. Brooding is quiet with intent."

I folded the chart along its worn crease. "The fleet bought our lie because Halcyon's people are greedy and their scout captain is stupid. That buys us hours, not safety. The moment their divers splash on empty water and find nothing, someone with a brain is going to re-plot our course, and they'll come here at flank speed." I tapped the Kestrel's coordinates, marked in my father's hand from five years ago, ink gone brown as old blood. "So we work fast, and we work scared, and we pray the ocean stays complicated enough to cover us."

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then we were never really here."

Sefu appeared at the companionway with coffee that smelled like it had been brewed twice and regretted once. He handed mugs around without ceremony — Sefu's whole manner was ceremony-free, which after the storm channel and his confession about the Halcyon money felt less like personality and more like penance he hadn't finished serving. He'd told us the truth in the dark with bleeding hands and a cracked hull plate groaning beneath us, and none of us had known what to do with it except keep working. Some confessions you forgive immediately. His we'd all agreed, silently, to forgive slowly, which is crueler but more honest.

"Visibility report from the last buoy data," he said. "Surface chop moderate. Below thirty meters, expect green murk and current shear at the thermocline. Classic Kestrel conditions, if the stories are true."

"The stories are true," I said. "I wrote half of them."

Nobody laughed. It wasn't a joke, or it was, or it was both — the way everything about that wreck was both a grave and a job site, depending on who you asked and whether you asked them before or after their father died there.

* * *

We made the coordinates at 04:40, in that gray hour when the sea forgets what color it's supposed to be and the sky hasn't decided either. Okafor brought us in on the main tanks' last dignity, and when he cut the engines the silence was enormous — the particular silence of a boat that has arrived somewhere it can still leave from, but only just.

The Kestrel sat below us in fifty-one fathoms.

You don't see a wreck at that depth. You feel it. Or I did — a pressure behind the eyes, a wrongness in the way the swell moved over that patch of water, rolling differently than it did a hundred meters off. The sea remembers weight even after the weight is gone. Somewhere under all that green-black water lay the hull my father had loved more than any registry ever loved him, broken across a shelf of rock, holding whatever it held.

I stood at the rail and did what I have done for five years: I did not look down.

"Gideon." Voss's voice, careful. "The dive plan."

"Wren's advance team went down four days ago with surface-supplied gear and a tender on station. They went dark two tides early. Standard protocol says if a team goes silent, the tender holds position and runs a search pattern at first light." I turned the words over like stones, checking each one for cracks. "There's no tender. Which means either they surfaced and ran, or—"

"Or the tender was never theirs to begin with," Voss finished. "Halcyon contract labor. Cheap crews, no loyalty, and when the money stops—"

"They run." My jaw ached. I realized I'd been clenching it since midnight. "Wren knew the risks. She took the job anyway, because Wren takes every job, because being careful is a thing that happens to other people."

"You're angry at her."

"I'm angry at everyone. It's efficient."

Pell joined us at the rail, dividers still hooked in her belt like a sidearm. She looked at the water the way she looked at fog banks — as a puzzle that would yield if you hated it correctly. "Current's setting northeast at three-quarters of a knot. Slack tide in—" she checked her watch against the sky, an old habit, "—ninety minutes. After slack, the flood starts building. By full flood you'll have current through that wreck site strong enough to sweep an unprepared diver off the structure and into open water."

"Meaning?"

"Meaning whoever's down there — if anyone's down there — has a window that closes with the tide. Same window we have. Everything on this boat runs out at the same time. Fuel, margin, tide, luck." She shrugged. "I'd call it elegant if it weren't trying to kill us."

Okafor came up wiping his hands on a rag that had given up being clean sometime last year. "Dive station's rigged. Sefu's kitting. Surface line's good for sixty meters with comms. Gideon—" He stopped. He looked at me the way crew look at a captain when they already know the answer and are asking anyway, out of respect for the ritual of asking.

"I'm diving," I said.

"You swore you'd never go down on that wreck again."

"I swore a lot of things five years ago. Most of them were grief wearing a uniform." I picked up my gloves. "My sister is somewhere under that water, and the tide doesn't care about my rules, and neither do I anymore."

* * *

It was Sefu who heard it first.

He was at the sonar, running a bottom profile out of habit — mapping the wreck's silhouette so the dive line wouldn't foul on structure — when he went very still. Sefu going still is louder than most men shouting. I crossed the wheelhouse and looked at the screen over his shoulder.

The Kestrel's shape bloomed in green phosphor, broken and familiar, a whale skeleton of a ship lying on her starboard side across the shelf. And near her flooded cargo deck, at the seam where the hull had split, a light pulsed.

Small. Rhythmic. Wrong.

"A strobe," Sefu said quietly. "Emergency pattern. Three flashes, pause, three flashes. That's a trapped-diver signal, Gideon. That's not a marker buoy. That's a person."

The wheelhouse got very small around me. I heard my own breathing, and beneath it the memory of a funeral five years ago where I'd stood on one side of a grave and my half-sister had stood on the other, and we had not spoken, because she believed in licenses and law and the slow machinery of justice, and I believed in nothing that required paperwork. Five years of silence built on that foundation. Five years of her choosing the system that erased our father's name, and me choosing exile, and both of us calling it principle when it was really just pride with better tailoring.

And now she was under the water our father drowned in, blinking three-and-three into the dark, alive, alive, *alive*, and the tide was turning against her.

"How much air does a trapped diver have?" Voss asked. Her voice had lost every ounce of negotiation polish. This was the woman underneath the contracts, and I was surprised to find I didn't hate her.

"Depends on depth, exertion, panic," Sefu said. "If she's got a bailout bottle and discipline, she's been rationing. Four days is past any reasonable margin. But the strobe's still going, which means she's conscious, which means she's thinking, which means—" he met my eyes, "—she's fighting. Sterling women fight."

"Get me suited," I said. "Now."

They moved. Okafor to the winch, Pell to the tide tables, Voss to the radio to keep one ear on the fleet frequencies in case our phantom-wreck lie collapsed early. Sefu helped me into the harness with hands that shook slightly, and I pretended not to notice, because pretending not to notice is sometimes the kindest thing a captain can do.

At the dive platform, the water waited, black-green and patient. Fifty-one fathoms down, my father's wreck. Fifty-one fathoms down, my sister. I had spent five years taking different routes so I'd never have to stand on this exact spot of ocean, and here I stood, and the ocean hadn't moved an inch, and neither had my grief. It had just been waiting, patient as tide, for me to come back.

"Gideon." Pell's voice in my earpiece, steady as arithmetic. "Slack tide in seventy minutes. Flood builds after that. Whatever you do down there, do it inside the window. I'll call your time every ten minutes. Don't make me call it fast."

"Copy."

"And Gideon?" A pause. Pell, who never wasted words, spent three of them anyway. "Come back up."

I spat over the regulator mouthpiece, checked my gauges one last time, and stepped off the platform into the cold.

The water closed over me like a door.

Above, faint and fading, the boat's hull passed overhead — then just light, then just green, then just the long descent into the dark where a compass once pointed north and a man once worked and a light blinked three-and-three, patient, desperate, *alive*.

I kicked downward, into the wreck that killed my father, to bring my sister home.
