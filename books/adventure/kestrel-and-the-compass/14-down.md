## 14. Down

There are rules, and then there are the rules I keep, and the difference between them is the difference between a suggestion and a spine.

Rule one: never take the same route twice. Rule two: never look back at the Kestrel. Rule three — the one I carved into myself so deep I sometimes forget it wasn't born there — never go down. Not to that wreck. Not to any wreck with her name on it, and every wreck has her name on it if you squint hard enough in the dark.

I stood on the dive platform with the regulator in my hand and the sea doing what the sea does, which is nothing, which is everything, which is waiting. Below me, forty fathoms down, a strobe blinked its small stupid heartbeat against the black. Wren's strobe. Emergency pattern, three-and-one, the rhythm we'd both been taught before either of us could spell *salvage*. Somebody had taught us that together. It took me a moment, hanging there over the water, to remember who.

Our father. Of course it was our father. Everything comes back to him eventually; that's the trouble with drowning men, they leave ripples.

"You don't have to do this," Sefu said behind me. He was holding my spare tank like a man holding a bomb he hoped wouldn't go off. "We can rig the lift bags, send Pell down—"

"Pell dives like a filing cabinet."

"Then Voss. She's certified."

"Voss would negotiate with the bulkhead." I checked my gauges. Checked them again. The third time is not superstition; the third time is the number of times it takes for your hands to stop lying to you. "It has to be me. You know why."

He did know why. Everybody on that boat knew why, which was the worst part. Five years of telling them nothing and they'd read the whole ledger off my face anyway.

The tide had maybe ninety minutes of slack left in it. After that, the current through the Kestrel's broken stern would turn the interior corridors into a washing machine full of rust and silt and, if I was slow, me. Somewhere below all that, behind a jammed cargo deck bulkhead, my sister was breathing a tank down to its last hundred bars and blinking a light at a sky she couldn't reach.

I spat in my mask, rinsed it, and put five years of good sense on the deck next to my fins.

"Gideon." Voss's voice, from the wheelhouse door. For once she wasn't performing. For once she sounded like a person who had buried people. "If you see something down there you're not ready to see—"

"I'll see it anyway," I said. "That's what seeing is."

* * *

Descent is the closest thing I know to prayer, if prayer were cold and tasted of rubber and involved equalizing your ears every few meters so your skull doesn't implode. I went down the mooring line hand over hand, watching the blue go grey, the grey go green-black, the world above shrink to a wobbling coin of light. My light cone swung ahead of me and found nothing, then silt, then nothing again, and I thought: *this is fine, this is just water, water is only water* — and then the hull came out of the dark.

She was bigger than I remembered. That's the lie memory tells, or maybe the truth — that grief shrinks things to fit inside a person, and reality keeps its original dimensions. The Kestrel lay on her port side at forty-two fathoms, a two-hundred-foot coastal freighter folded around a reef like a drunk around a lamppost, her starboard plates peeled back where she'd hit, her superstructure furred with five years of soft white coral that made her look almost gentle. Almost asleep.

My father had died in there. Somewhere in there. Deck crew said the flooding took the engine room in under four minutes, and my father had gone aft to check the seals because that was who he was, a man who checked the seals, a man who believed that if you did everything right the sea would keep its side of the bargain. The sea does not keep bargains. The sea takes deposits.

I hung in the water ten feet off the hull and my body tried to mutiny. Heart slamming, breath going fast and shallow — the one thing you cannot afford at depth, burning air like panic burns air — and I did the thing Okafor taught me years ago, the trick for when the animal brain takes the wheel. I named five things I could see. Hull plate. Coral fan. A fish, some kind of grouper, regarding me with the flat contempt of everything that lives down here permanently. My own hands, steady now, thank God, thank whatever. And a strobe light, far aft, blinking through a torn section of plating like a pulse through skin.

Three-and-one. Three-and-one. Still alive, then. Still spending air.

I swam toward the wound in the ship's side, and as I crossed the threshold — through the torn plate, into the throat of the wreck — I broke rule two without meaning to. I looked back. Just once, at the coin of daylight above, already going thin.

Then I looked forward instead, and kept swimming.

* * *

Inside, the Kestrel was a cathedral built by a catastrophe. Corridors that had been corridors were now chimneys; the cargo deck stretched away in both directions, a canyon of collapsed pallets and shifted containers, and my light kept finding details it had no business finding. A coffee mug fused to a bulkhead by coral. A boot. I didn't let myself decide whose boot. There are limits even to honesty.

The emergency strobe pulsed somewhere forward and low, past the second bulkhead, in the flooded section where the advance team's radio log said Wren had gone to survey the sealed compartment. Her partner had come back up two days ago with a bent tank valve and a story about shifting wreckage. Wren hadn't come back at all. Halcyon's fleet had written her off as a cost of business — I'd heard the phrase used, *cost of business*, from a man eating a sandwich — and Voss had put it on our chart table like a knife: *one tide, Gideon.*

I found the bulkhead by following the strobe's glow around a corner of collapsed decking. The passage beyond was blocked — not sealed, worse than sealed, *jammed*, a cargo container half the size of a bus wedged diagonally across the frame, its corner driven into the deck plating by whatever violence had folded the ship. The gap above it was maybe eighteen inches wide, choked with cable and conduit, and through that gap, faint as a rumor, the strobe blinked.

And under the strobe, in the beam of her own dying torch, my sister's face.

Wren Sterling — Wren Calloway now, she'd taken their mother's name after the funeral, a fact I had spent five years converting into ammunition — was sitting in eighteen inches of water in a pocket of trapped air, her back against the far side of the jam, her dive gear stripped to the vest harness because the space was too tight to wear it, her rebreather hose running back to the main tank propped beside her like a guest that had overstayed. She looked terrible. She looked gray-lipped and salt-crusted and alive, and when my light hit her face she flinched, then squinted, then did something I was not prepared for.

She laughed. A short, cracked, disbelieving laugh, the kind you hear from people pulled out of rivers.

I couldn't answer. My hands were busy being terrified, gripping the edge of the container, so I gave her the first honest signal I could think of, the oldest one in our family's private vocabulary: fist to chest, then fingers spread. *Still here.*

Her face did something complicated. Then she raised one finger — *wait* — and held up her gauge console to the gap so I could read it in my light.

Forty bars. Forty bars of air between my sister and the arithmetic of drowning. At her breathing rate, resting, calm, maybe twenty-five minutes. At her breathing rate if she saw me start to cry, considerably less.

So I didn't cry. I got to work.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about salvage: it is mostly plumbing. Romance is for the surface. Down here it's levers and fulcrums and lift bags and the patient physics of heavy things, and the heavy thing in question was a steel container wedged across a bulkhead frame at an angle that meant it wasn't stuck — it was *load-bearing*, holding up a section of collapsed overhead that would come down on anything underneath it if you moved it wrong. Move it wrong and I'd bury Wren under thirty tons of ship. Move it right and we'd have a gap big enough to pass a diver through.

Move it wrong and I'd be digging my sister out of a grave I dug. So: right.

I signaled the plan through the gap in the crude sign language divers use when words drown — *you, stay back, cover your head; me, bag, lift, slow; when I tap three times, move.* Wren watched, nodded, and then, instead of retreating, held up one finger again and pointed at the base of the jam.

I squeezed closer, put my face to the gap, and followed her finger. Wedged under the container's lower corner, keeping the whole assembly from sliding further into the frame, was a length of pipe — a jack stanchion, actually, a proper hydraulic ram, the kind of tool my father kept aboard because he believed in doing everything right. Someone had jacked the container up deliberately. Someone had gotten it most of the way clear and then run out of something — air, time, strength.

Wren tapped her own chest. *Me. I did this.* Then she pointed at the ram's control, visible through the gap, its hose trailing back toward her, and made a fist that opened slowly. *It slipped. I've been holding pressure by hand for six hours.*

Six hours. Holding a hydraulic ram closed with her bare hands in the dark, alone, breathing down her reserve, blinking a strobe at a sky that had decided she was a rounding error. I thought about every angry speech I'd composed for her over five years, every version of *you chose them over me*, and I want to report that those speeches dissolved in that moment, washed away by admiration. They didn't. They just suddenly seemed very small, the way a harbor seems small when you've seen open water.

I gave her the signal for *wait*, backed out of the gap, and went to fetch the lift bags from my rig.

* * *

The work took forty minutes and cost me most of my own reserve, and I will not pretend I remember it clearly, because the truth is that terror transmuted into purpose looks, from the inside, exactly like purpose. You inflate the bags into the gap above the container, you wedge them, you bleed the ram down a centimeter at a time while the bags take the load, and you listen — with your hands, with your teeth, with the animal part of you — for the groan of metal deciding whether to hold or fail. Twice the overhead shifted and rained rust down on us like orange snow. Twice Wren and I froze, faces eight inches apart through the gap, sharing the same held breath.

And somewhere in there — I couldn't tell you when, somewhere between the second bag and the third — we started talking.

Not with voices. With hands. But hands are honest in a way mouths aren't; you can't decorate a hand signal with grievance. She signed: *You came.* I signed back: *Obviously.* She signed: *You hate wrecks.* I signed: *I hate this wreck.* She signed: *Same thing.* And then, before I could argue, she signed the thing that undid me, the simple brutal sentence I'd been dodging since the funeral:

*I never chose them over you. I chose to believe the law worked. That's different. I was wrong.*

Fist to chest. Fingers spread. *Still here,* I told her, which in our family's language has always meant more than it says. It means *still yours.* It means *the ripples reached me too.* It means *Dad would have hated how long this took us.*

She laughed again underwater — a burst of bubbles, silver, rising past my faceplate like a handful of coins thrown at heaven — and I understood that we were going to make it, and the understanding made me careless, and carelessness at depth is how widows get made, so I put the feeling in a box and bled the ram another centimeter.

The gap opened to thirty inches. Then three feet. Enough.

I tapped the frame three times. Wren moved — fast, efficient, threading herself through the gap with the economy of someone who'd spent six hours planning her own escape — and then her hand shot back through and grabbed my wrist, and for a moment we just stayed like that, two sisters hanging in a drowned ship, connected by grip rather than rope, her pulse hammering against my glove fast enough to count.

Then she pointed, urgently, past my shoulder, deeper into the cargo hold, toward the sealed compartment at the heart of the wreck — the compartment the compass pointed to, the reason for everything, the vault.

And behind her visor, in the glow of my own light reflected back at me, I saw her expression change from relief to alarm, and she grabbed my arm and turned me fully around, and my light swept across the compartment's outer hatch —

— and found it standing open. Forced. Freshly forced, the pry marks bright metal in a world of rust, and from the darkness beyond it came a sound no wreck should make at this depth: the rhythmic churn of another diver's fins, moving away, heading for the breach in the hull, heading up.

Somebody from the fleet was already down here. Somebody had beaten us to the vault by hours, and was climbing toward the surface right now with whatever our father had died protecting tucked in their kit bag.

Above us, muffled through forty fathoms of ocean, I heard the engines of the Halcyon dredger begin to anchor directly overhead.
