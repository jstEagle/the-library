## 15. What the Compass Points To

The bulkhead gave way the way most stubborn things do — all at once, after pretending for hours that it never would.

One moment I was braced against the jammed plate with my shoulder screaming and my air gauge lying to me about how much time I had left, and the next there was a groan of metal like a whale with opinions, and the gap widened by half a meter, and Gideon's hand shot through it in the dark and found mine.

I want to say something dignified happened then. It didn't. I grabbed her forearm with both hands and made a sound into my regulator that no one will ever convince me was not a sob, and she held on, and through five years of silence and a funeral neither of us had been able to look at each other across, my sister's grip said everything her mouth wouldn't: *I've got you. I'm sorry. I'm here.*

We worked the rest of the jam together. Hand signals mostly — hers were guide-signals, crisp and economical; mine were dive-school standard with a few flourishes she clearly thought were ridiculous — and once, when the plate shifted and pinned my glove against the frame, a shared rope and a shared curse that needed no language at all. She pulled the plate free with a leverage bar from her kit, and I slid through the gap into the corridor beyond, and just like that, Wren Sterling was no longer trapped in the Kestrel.

I'd had a speech prepared, actually. Five years of speeches, compressed down during two tides of terror into about forty seconds' worth of material: *you could have called, you could have written, you chose the sea over your own family.* All of it true. None of it mattered even slightly, kneeling on a drowned cargo deck with my lungs burning and my sister checking my gauge with the fussy competence of someone who'd been terrified for hours and would die before admitting it.

She tapped my wrist. *Low?* I shook my head — enough air, enough time, barely. She pointed deeper into the wreck, toward the hold, and raised an eyebrow in a way I recognized from childhood, from kitchen tables, from the exact expression our father used to wear when he knew something we didn't.

*Come on,* the eyebrow said. *There's something you need to see.*

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about diving a wreck you grew up around: the dead don't haunt it. The living do. Every compartment of the Kestrel wore my father's handwriting — grease-pencil labels on pipe runs, his initials stamped into a repair bracket, the particular way he'd always rigged lines with a double hitch because single hitches offended him personally. He'd been dead five years and the ship still sounded like him. I kept expecting to round a corner and find him swearing at a corroded valve, sleeves rolled, telling me to hold the light steady and stop narrating.

Gideon moved ahead of me like she'd rehearsed this route in dreams, which, knowing her, she had. She didn't touch anything. That was the thing I noticed — every other diver I'd ever met on a salvage job touched everything, claimed everything, ran their hands along bulkheads like they were petting the ship into ownership. Gideon swam through her father's grave with her hands tucked close to her body, as if the wreck were a church and she'd finally decided to respect it.

Then she stopped at the aft hold, at a junction where three corridors met, and pointed.

The compass was mounted on a steel plate bolted to the bulkhead. Not carried. Mounted — set into the metal like a fixture, like it had been installed rather than placed, with a bracket our father had machined himself. I recognized the machining. Everyone who ever worked with Aldous Sterling recognized his machining; he finished everything to a tolerance the job didn't require, out of what he called principle and what Gideon and I called showing off.

It was a ship's bearing compass, brass-cased, old-fashioned, the kind with a card that floats in fluid. And it was wrong. The needle wasn't pointing north. It was pointing — steadily, unbothered by five years underwater, by storms, by the slow collapse of the ship around it — into the aft bulkhead. Into solid steel.

I looked at Gideon. She looked at the compass. And I watched five years fall off her face in real time.

"She told the truth," Gideon said. Her voice came through the comm thin and strange. "Voss. The compass doesn't point north."

"Where does it point?"

Gideon swam closer, close enough that her lamp threw the compass's shadow long across the bulkhead, and the shadow fell across a seam in the steel I might have taken for a weld if I hadn't grown up listening to Aldous Sterling explain welds. This wasn't a weld. It was too straight, too deliberate, and there — barely visible under a film of silt — was a recessed handle, the kind you'd find on a strongroom door.

"It points *through*," Gideon said.

* * *

My father was, by every registry and every court and every man who ever signed a paper about him, a licensed salvage operator of modest means who died in an accident. That was the official version. I'd built my whole life on the official version — the licenses, the permits, the clean paperwork, the belief that if you colored inside the lines, the lines would protect you. I chose the law when Gideon chose exile, and I'd spent five years telling myself one of us had to be right, and it was going to be me, obviously, because being right was the only inheritance I had left.

Standing in front of that hidden door, I understood that my father had never trusted the lines at all. He'd just colored inside them while building something outside them, quietly, for years, for us.

The handle turned easier than it should have. Okafor's maintenance, probably — or my father's paranoia, which amounted to the same thing. The door swung inward on counterweighted hinges, smooth as the day it was hung, and our lamps swept into a compartment maybe three meters square, dry. Actually dry — sealed well enough that five years of ocean hadn't gotten in. My father had built a vault inside a sinking ship and it had outlasted him.

No gold. No coin, no bullion, none of the cargo manifests Halcyon's lawyers had been reciting in courtrooms for half a decade. Shelves. Filing cabinets, marine-grade, latched. A workbench with a ledger open under a glass weight, pen still resting in its groove like he'd stepped out to check a valve and meant to come back.

Gideon went to the ledger first. I went to the cabinets, because I'm the one who reads documents for a living, and I unlatched the nearest drawer and lifted out a folder, and my hands started shaking before I'd read the second page.

Salvage contracts. Dozens of them. Originals, signed, witnessed — crews whose names I knew from the blacklist rolls, crews erased from every registry on the coast. And attached to each contract, correspondence. Halcyon letterhead. Terms renegotiated mid-job. Rights assigned, reassigned, reassigned again until the original crew's share evaporated into clauses so dense they needed a lawyer to translate and a lawyer to fight, and Halcyon employed both. Dates. Signatures. One memo — I'll remember it until I die — in which a Halcyon vice-president suggested, in cheerful corporate prose, that certain crews be "encouraged toward regulatory difficulties," followed by a list of names, and the third name on the list was Sterling, A., and beside it, in different ink, a note: *in progress.*

They hadn't blacklisted my father by accident. They'd done it on purpose, on schedule, and then they'd waited for him to die trying to prove otherwise.

"Gideon." My voice didn't work right on the first try. "Gideon, come look at this."

She was already looking. She'd gone very still in the way she goes still — total, geological, the stillness of someone deciding whether to break something — and when she finally spoke, her voice was flat and quiet and more frightening than shouting.

"He knew. He knew what they were doing, and he collected everything, and he hid it where only—" She stopped. Swallowed. Started again. "Where only family would find it. Where only someone who knew the compass was wrong would think to look."

"Not gold," I said.

"No." She touched the open ledger page, gently, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal. "Better than gold. Worse than gold. This is forty crews' worth of proof. This is a case Halcyon can't buy, bury, or drown." She laughed, one short ugly note. "He didn't leave us a fortune, Wren. He left us a *duty*."

And there it was. The inheritance redefined while I stood inside it. I'd imagined this moment a hundred ways — treasure, redemption, the thing that would make the last five years retroactively worthwhile. I'd never imagined obligation. But that's what evidence is. You can spend gold. You can't spend truth without spending yourself alongside it. Whoever carried these papers out of this wreck carried forty families' grievances, and the fleet above us wanted the vault, and the courts wanted nothing to do with any of it, and somewhere in the middle of that arithmetic my father had bet everything on his daughters figuring it out.

"We can't take it up," I said. "Not with the fleet overhead. Anything we surface, they surface."

"I know."

"And we can't leave it."

"I know that too."

Above us — muffled through five fathoms and a hull, but unmistakable — came a sound I'd been dreading since the radio went dark: engines. Heavy ones. Anchoring chain paying out with a rattle I felt in my teeth. Then light, slicing down through the water column in long pale blades, sweeping across the corridor outside the vault like searchlights interrogating the seafloor.

The Halcyon fleet had arrived.

Gideon looked up at the light falling through the broken deck above, then back at the vault, then at me. And my sister — the woman who'd spent five years refusing to take the same route twice, who burned telegrams and trusted no one and loved nothing she couldn't outrun — reached out and gripped my shoulder, hard, and said:

"They're not getting this. Whatever it costs. Whatever it takes. Nobody owns what Dad died protecting — not them, not the courts, not anyone." A pause, and then, quieter, almost a smile in her voice: "You okay to work?"

I looked at the ledger. At the names. At the memo with *Sterling, A. — in progress* written beside my father's life like a line item.

I'd spent five years being the sister who did things legally. It occurred to me, standing in the dry heart of a drowned ship with the enemy's lights crawling across the ceiling, that legality had never once protected anyone on that list.

"Get me a waterproof bag," I said. "And tell Voss she'd better be as good a liar as she claims."

The searchlight swept past the doorway again, closer this time. And beneath it, faint but rising, came the sound of divers entering the water.
