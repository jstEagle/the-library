## 16. The Fleet Arrives

I have negotiated with pirates, ministers, and one archbishop who kept a revolver in his mitre box, and I will tell you what I learned in thirty years of maritime law and its adjacent crimes: the loudest man in the room is always the one who has already lost. So when Halcyon's lead dredger dropped anchor directly above us — a floating cathedral of cranes and floodlights named, with the industry's gift for irony, the *Providence* — and its salvage master came down his ladder with a folder thick enough to stop a bullet, I put on my best coat and became the loudest woman on the water.

"Voss," he said, reading me off a manifest like I was cargo. "Mara Voss. Salvage broker. Three disciplinary actions, two suspensions, one commendation they never explained."

"And you're the man they send when they want something done *legally*," I said, shaking his hand before he could decide whether to offer it. "Which tells me Halcyon is worried. They only bring lawyers to funerals."

His name was Corliss. He had the complexion of a man who spent his life under fluorescent light pretending it was the sun, and the eyes of someone who had signed enough documents to know exactly what they were for. He held up the folder. "Claim of prior discovery. Filed this morning, countersigned by the coastal authority, notarized in triplicate. The Kestrel site is Halcyon property as of six hours ago. You're anchored in a restricted zone, Ms. Voss. I'm authorized to offer you and your vessel safe passage out — plus compensation for your trouble — if you're gone by the morning tide."

"And if we're not?"

"Then I'm authorized to do this." He produced a second document, thinner, and I confess my stomach did something unprofessional when I read it. An interdiction order. It named our vessel, our crew — all five names, including one that made my jaw tighten — and authorized "removal of persons and assets" from the site by whatever means the salvage master deemed necessary. It was legal the way a guillotine is legal. Every letter in its proper place, and a blade at the bottom.

"Six hours," I said, tapping the paper. "You filed this morning. The wreck's been on the charts for five years and nobody at Halcyon could find it with both hands and a flashlight. Then suddenly, this week, you have coordinates, notaries, and a dredger the size of a parish. Somebody's been feeding you information, Corliss. I'd ask who, but I suspect you don't ask questions either. That's why they gave you the folder instead of the truth."

Something flickered behind the fluorescent eyes. Good. There was a man in there, and men can be moved.

"I don't need to know what you're after," he said, and his voice dropped half a register, the register men use when they want to be overheard saying something they shouldn't. "I've done eleven of these. Deep claims, contested claims. Do you know what happens after the lawyers leave? We take everything. The brass fittings, the hull plate, the crew effects. We take the *logbooks*, Ms. Voss. Halcyon doesn't salvage wrecks. It erases them. Whatever's down there — whoever's down there — by next month there won't be a record it existed."

"And you're telling me this because?"

"Because you're the loudest person I've ever met and I've been listening to you for four minutes." He closed the folder. "You have until the morning tide. I'd use it."

He climbed back up his ladder, and I stood on our deck with the wind coming off the *Providence*'s floodlights like heat off a furnace, and I thought: *there it is.* The moment I'd been sailing toward since I first put a compass in Gideon Sterling's hands and watched her look at it like it was a loaded gun.

I had spent my entire career inside the law. I want to be precise about that, because Gideon never believed it and I don't blame her — she'd seen the law from underneath, the way a drowning man sees a boat, and from down there every hull looks like it's running you over. But I was inside it. I wrote the claims. I filed the papers. I sat in hearing rooms with wood paneling and watched blacklisted crews — men like Aldous Sterling, women like half the people on this deck — lose their names to a process so clean it never left fingerprints. I told myself the machinery was sound and the operators were rotten, and that I was an operator, and that was different.

It is not different. I learned that five years ago, reading a fraud case that crossed my desk with forty names attached, and I did nothing, because doing something would have cost me my license, and my license was the only thing I had left after my husband's debts ate everything else. I have been a coward with excellent penmanship for thirty years.

The morning tide was in eleven hours. It was time to stop being a coward with excellent penmanship.

* * *

I found the crew in the hold, and I want the record — my record, the one I keep in my own head, since no registry will ever hold it again — to reflect what I saw there. Okafor with her sleeves rolled and her hands inside the guts of the old winch, rebuilding something that had no business working. Sefu checking dive tanks with the tenderness of a man handling sleeping children. Pell at the chart table with a pencil, drawing lines that would have made a cartographer weep and a coast guard officer reach for his handcuffs. And Gideon Sterling standing over a schematic of the Kestrel with her sister's dive slate in one hand, tracing the flooded cargo deck the way you'd trace a scar on someone you loved.

"Corliss gave us until the morning tide," I announced.

"Corliss," Gideon said, not looking up, "gave *you* until the morning tide. He gave us a removal order with our names on it."

"Semantics."

"Semantics is what you call it when the paper has someone else's name on the bad part." She looked up then. Five years of running had carved her face into something lean and weatherproof, but the eyes were still her father's — the same gray that looks like a decision not yet made. "Talk fast, Voss. What's the play?"

"The play is I'm very loud and you're very quiet. I go up on deck and negotiate. I stall. I dispute the claim, I demand arbitration, I invoke three treaties and one papal bull I've been saving for a special occasion. Corliss will hate every minute of it, because Corliss, whatever else he is, actually reads what he signs, and the claim he filed has a defect in paragraph four that I could drive a dredger through. He knows it. He'll want to settle before I make it public. Every hour I keep him arguing is an hour you're down there."

"Down there doing what, exactly?" Sefu asked. He's a large man with a gentle voice, and he asks questions like that the way a priest asks them — already knowing the answer, giving you the chance to say it out loud.

"Sealing the vault compartment," I said. "And rigging the demolition charge."

The hold went quiet the way a courtroom goes quiet, which is to say: loudly, with everyone's breathing.

"You want to sink it," Okafor said. "The ledger. The proof. Forty crews' worth of restitution, and your plan is to put it at the bottom of a trench."

"I want Halcyon to never touch it," I said. "Those are different things. The courts had the evidence once — it's how the blacklist happened, did you know that? The original fraud case. The evidence went into a filing system, and the filing system went into a building, and the building burned in a fire the insurance company called an act of God and I called a Tuesday. Halcyon doesn't need to win in court. It only needs the evidence to stop existing. If we sink it, it stops existing. If we keep it, we spend the rest of our lives as a floating archive that every lawyer, pirate, and salvage fleet in the hemisphere is shooting at."

"Then we give it to the courts," Pell said.

"The courts are where it went to die last time."

"Then to a journalist," Pell said, and I looked at her — quiet, precise Pell, who navigates by mathematics and distrust — and she looked back at me with an expression I recognized, because I'd been wearing it in mirrors for five years. The expression of a person who has just realized the system they trusted is a machine for grinding up people exactly like them.

"Photographs," I said. "Copies. Enough to reopen the cases, distributed sideways, past the courts, past the registries, straight into the public's teeth. But that happens after. Right now, in the next ten hours, the original goes somewhere no fleet can ever file a claim on it. That's the play. That's the whole play. I'm the distraction, you're the demolition, and the Kestrel keeps my father's—" 

I stopped. Wrong father. I watched Gideon notice, and watched her decide — generously, or maybe just tiredly — to let it go.

"Your father's work," I corrected, "stays his."

Gideon looked at the schematic for a long moment. I have watched her make a hundred decisions in the weeks since she took this job, and she makes them the way she dives: fast, deep, and without looking back. This one took longer. Because this one wasn't about the wreck. It was about whether the last physical thing her father ever built would still exist tomorrow.

"The charge goes beside the compass," she said finally. "Not on the hull. If it goes down, it goes down whole. His workspace, his vault, his compass. All of it together." She rolled the schematic closed. "And Voss — when you're up there being loud? Be loud about *me*. Corliss already thinks you're the boss. Let him. If they board us, every second they spend looking at you is a second Sefu and Wren aren't underwater."

"I've been the boss for thirty years," I said. "It's the one thing I'm overqualified for."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about being the distraction: it is the most honest work I have ever done. For once, my job was to be exactly what I appeared to be — a loud, difficult, impeccably credentialed woman standing between a machine and its meal — and I threw myself into it with the relief of a nun finally allowed to swear.

I went aboard the *Providence* at dusk, under a flag of professional courtesy, and I gave Corliss the performance of his life. I disputed paragraph four. I cited the Hague Convention, the Coastal Salvage Accord of '09, and a ruling from a tribunal that no longer existed but whose precedent, I argued, was eternal, like bad weather. I demanded to see the notarization originals. I found a clerical error in the exhibit list — they'd misspelled *Kestrel*, which is the kind of thing that means nothing and wins appeals — and I made Corliss initial each correction individually, in blue ink, because black ink was "procedurally insufficient," a phrase I invented on the spot and delivered with such conviction that he went and found blue ink.

And all the while, through the *Providence*'s windows, I watched the floodlights on the water, and I counted the dive boats. Two went down at nine o'clock. Two more at ten. Corliss's own divers, doing his legal, documented, notarized work — surveying the wreck, tagging the hull, photographing everything Halcyon would later erase. And somewhere beneath them, below the lights, below the survey lines, Sefu and Wren Sterling were moving through the dark with sealant and detonator cord, doing the illegal thing so quietly that the legal thing never noticed it was being buried.

At eleven, Corliss poured me a whiskey I didn't want and asked the question I'd been waiting for.

"Your diver," he said. "The one who went down with the advance team last week. Wren Sterling. She's on your crew manifest now, but she wasn't on the advance team's contract. Whose diver is she?"

"Mine," I said.

"She was Halcyon's. Contracted through a subsidiary. Her team went dark two tides ago — missed two check-ins. Standard procedure is we list her as a casualty of an unauthorized dive and move on." He turned his glass. "I didn't file it. I want you to know that. I've been sitting on the paperwork for two days telling myself it's a clerical backlog." He looked at me, and the fluorescent light finally went out of his eyes, and what was underneath was tired. "Is she alive, Ms. Voss?"

I thought about a flooded cargo deck and a jammed bulkhead and two sisters passing a single length of rope between them in the dark, hand signals for words, and the first honest sentence Gideon had signed to her sister in five years — I know, because Wren told me, and then immediately pretended she hadn't — which was: *Dad would be furious at both of us. Good.*

"She's alive," I said. "And if your fleet's divers come within fifty meters of the cargo deck, she won't be for long. The compartment they're working near is unstable. One wrong anchor chain, one dropped tool, and the whole section goes." It was even mostly true. "Call them up, Corliss. Call them up and I'll tell you where the ledger vault is — the real one, the one your founder's been hunting since before you were hired. I'll give you the coordinates myself. In writing."

He stared at me. "You'd sell out the evidence? You? You've spent three hours telling me the claim is fraudulent."

"The claim *is* fraudulent. The vault is real. And I am a broker, Corliss — I sell things. Tonight I'm selling you the one thing Halcyon wants more than the ledger: certainty. You get the vault's location, documented, notarized, in your folder with your blue-inked corrections. You file it. You own it on paper. And in exchange, you pull your divers and you let my crew leave on the morning tide, because the vault compartment is rigged to flood — old wreck, old mechanisms — and if anyone but my diver opens it, the contents are pulp." I leaned across the table. "You'll own the vault the way you own the wreck, Corliss. On paper. And the paper will say you own a sealed box at the bottom of a trench that no court will ever order opened, because opening it destroys it, and no insurance company on earth will underwrite the attempt. You'll have won. Completely. Legally. Forever. Isn't that what Halcyon pays you for?"

I watched him do the arithmetic. I watched him arrive at the sum, and I watched him understand — the way I had understood, five years and a burned building ago — that winning on paper and winning in the world are different currencies, and his employers had been paying him in the worthless one.

He picked up the radio. "All dive teams, surface. Surface now. Survey's concluded." Then, to me, quietly: "The morning tide. And Voss? If I find out you've lied to me—"

"You'll do nothing," I said, "because you'll have checked the vault's location against the wreck's structural survey and discovered I told you the truth. The compartment exists. The flooding mechanism exists. I've simply neglected to mention that my engineer has been very busy tonight." I stood, and shook his hand, and gave him my best broker's smile. "You're a good man, Corliss. That's the tragedy of you. You keep filing papers for people who burn buildings."

* * *

I came down the ladder at half past midnight and the deck was dark, running lights doused, and for one bad moment I thought the fleet had taken us while I was upstairs being magnificent. Then Okafor's hand closed on my shoulder from the shadow of the winch housing, and I nearly had a heart attack, which I mention only so the record shows I remained dignified throughout.

"Report," Gideon said, materializing beside me like the ghost of the wreck we were about to finish.

"Corliss has recalled his divers. He believes he owns the vault. The morning tide is ours — he'll hold the *Providence*'s boats until then, he gave me his word."

"His word."

"He reads what he signs. It's the only kind of word he has." I looked past her, at the hatch to the hold, at the faint chemical smell that had crept up on deck — detonator cord and sealant, the perfume of my one act outside the law. "Is it done? Is the charge set?"

Gideon was quiet for a moment. When she answered, her voice had something in it I hadn't heard before — not fear, exactly. Reverence's difficult cousin.

"The charge is set. Sefu wired the primaries, Wren ran the backup line, Okafor built the trigger." She looked at me. "But the receiver's dead. Salt water got into the housing during the sealant work — Okafor thinks the compartment's humidity cooked it. The charge won't fire from up here."

My stomach dropped the way it does when a deal goes wrong at the signature. "Then we go down and re-wire it. There's time before—"

"There's no time to re-wire and seal both," Okafor said from the dark, and I heard what thirty years of machinery had put in her voice: the sound of a woman who has calculated something she hates. "We can arm it manually. Someone goes down, sets the trigger by hand, on a delay. It's a twenty-minute fuse once it's armed. But the compartment's already taking water, the tide's turning, and the receiver housing is jammed in a bracket behind the vault seal. Someone has to stand in that room, in the dark, next to my father's compass, and hold the trigger housing still while the mechanism seats." Gideon's voice didn't waver. That was the terrible part. "It can't be Wren. She's been down twice today and she's got a cracked rib from the bulkhead. It can't be Sefu — he's too big for the gap behind the seal. It's a small space, Voss. A small space, a jammed bracket, and twenty minutes of fuse."

"And you've been down there twice already today," I said. "You're running on fumes and you know it."

"I know the space," she said. "I know the bracket. And it's my father's compass, Voss. It goes down on my hands or it doesn't go down at all."

She was already pulling on her fins.

Above us, the *Providence*'s floodlights swept the black water one last time before Corliss dimmed them for the night — a long white blade across the swells, there and gone. Below us, six fathoms down, in a drowned room where a dead man had once mounted a compass that pointed at the truth, a demolition charge waited for a hand to arm it.

Gideon Sterling stepped off the ladder and into the water without looking back.

I stood at the rail and did the one thing I'm best at, the thing I'd spent thirty years perfecting and one night finally putting to honest use: I watched, and I waited, and I kept time.

Twenty minutes, once the fuse was lit.

The tide was already turning.
