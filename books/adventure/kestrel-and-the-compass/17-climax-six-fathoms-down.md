## 17. Climax: Six Fathoms Down

The charge was supposed to be simple. That's what Okafor said, and Okafor had never once in eleven years been wrong about a charge — which is why the silence that followed his thumb on the detonator felt less like an accident and more like a personal insult from the universe.

We were all on deck when it failed to happen. The Kestrel sat below us at six fathoms, dark and patient, and above her the Halcyon dredger *Meridian* swung at anchor like a fat man deciding whether to sit down. Her floodlights turned the water into a sheet of hammered tin. I could see the first of their divers' bubbles breaking the surface in little silver blisters.

"Relay's dead," Okafor said, for the third time, as if repetition might shame the electronics into obedience. "Salt water got into the receiver housing. It's not firing. It's not going to fire."

"So we go down and light it ourselves," Sefu said.

"We," I said.

He looked at me. Wren looked at me. Even Voss, up on the wheelhouse roof with a megaphone she was using to conduct a negotiation so loud and so fraudulent that two of Halcyon's officers were visibly reconsidering their careers, paused mid-sentence to look at me.

"I've been down twice tonight," I said. "My air's marginal, my hands are shaking, and I have already spent more time inside that wreck than any sane person spends in a lifetime."

"And yet," Pell said quietly, from the chart table, "you're already reaching for your fins."

I looked down. She was right. My hands had made the decision somewhere between Okafor's second report and Sefu's suggestion, and they hadn't bothered consulting me. Five years of rules — never the same route twice, never go back, never look at the wreck — and every one of them had been broken or bent since the moment Voss put that photograph on the bar. At some point you stop being a person who has rules and become a person who has a direction, and the direction is down.

"Wren holds the deck with Sefu," I said. "Pell runs the boat. Okafor gives me the manual arming sequence until I can recite it in my sleep, because there is a real chance I'll be doing exactly that. And Voss—"

"Voss," said Voss, descending from the wheelhouse roof with the megaphone still crackling, "is about to tell three separate lies to a salvage master who deserves every one of them. Go. I'll keep them looking up."

She caught my arm as I passed. Up close her face was older than it had been six tides ago, worn smooth in places like sea glass.

"Sterling. Whatever you find down there—" She stopped. Started again. "Your father built that vault to outlast him. Don't let it outlast everything else too."

It wasn't comfort. It was better. It was instructions.

* * *

The water took me the way it always did, cold closing over my head like a door, and then I was falling through floodlit green toward the shape I had spent five years refusing to look at.

Third dive. Third time through the cargo hatch, past the collapsed spar, along the corridor where the emergency strobe had blinked its wrong, desperate rhythm for two days before we got Wren out. The strobe was still there, still blinking, nobody left alive to answer it. I swam past it without stopping, because if I stopped I would think about how close two days had come to being forever, and thinking was not on the arming checklist.

Okafor's voice came through the hardline, thin and tinny in my earpiece. "Charge housing is on the frame beside the vault compartment. You'll see the indicator window. If it shows amber, the primer's live and you just seat the contact. If it shows black—"

"It shows black," I said.

A pause. "Then you bridge the contacts manually. Red wire to the terminal marked with the notch. Hold it. Count five. Get clear. Gideon — count five, not four, not six. Four doesn't set the primer. Six sets it while you're still holding it."

"Noted."

The corridor opened into the engine room, and the engine room opened into the workspace, and there it was.

My father's drowned workshop.

I had seen it twice tonight already and it still stopped me like a hand on the chest. The bench bolted to the bulkhead, tools hanging in their painted outlines, rusted now into brown ghosts of themselves. The vice. The coffee tin he'd kept his pencils in, though God knew what he'd ever written down here. And mounted above the bench, bolted where no compass had any business being bolted, the compass itself — brass case gone green, needle swinging slow and certain toward the sealed compartment in the far wall, pointing at the ledger vault the way it had pointed for five years, faithful to a man who'd trusted it to be.

Halcyon divers had been through here. I could see their marks — fresh scrape trails in the silt, a pry bar abandoned against the vault door, their lights' scorch-prints on the walls where they'd swept the room and found nothing but an old compass and a wall that wouldn't open without whatever key their lawyers hadn't thought to bring. They'd be back. Bubbles were already threading down the corridor behind me, two of them, maybe three, coming to do properly what they'd done hastily before.

I got to the charge housing. Amber would have been a mercy. Black, then. I stripped the cover, and there they were: the contacts, small and stupid and waiting, and the red wire coiled beside them like something asleep.

Behind me, faint through the water, a diver's light swept the corridor mouth.

"Gideon." Okafor's voice. "Status."

"Bridging now."

I took the red wire in my glove. My hands were steady. That surprised me more than anything else that had happened in six tides — after the storm channel, after the leak, after hauling Wren out from behind a jammed bulkhead with her air screaming low, after all of it, my hands chose this moment to be perfectly calm, as if they'd finally understood the assignment.

And because my hands were busy and my mind had nothing left to do, my mind went where it wanted, which was here, to this room, five years ago and also right now.

I had spent five years believing that coming back here would be losing. That was the whole architecture of the rules — never return, never look, because looking meant admitting the wreck had won, that it had taken my father and my licenses and my name and gotten away clean. Every route I'd never repeated, every port I'd never slept in twice, was a wall I built around this one room at the bottom of the sea. Keep moving, and the room stays locked. Stop, and it opens.

But here was the thing the room actually contained: a bench. A vice. A coffee tin of pencils. A compass bolted above a man's workspace, pointing at proof that he had been right — that Halcyon had stolen from forty crews, that his name belonged in registries, that the law that erased him was the thing that needed burying, not him. He hadn't died running. He'd died holding a door shut. And I had spent five years mistaking my exile for his failure, my blacklist for his defeat, when really the only person who'd ever lost anything in this room was me, voluntarily, one unrepeatable route at a time.

Letting go, I thought, watching the needle swing, is not the same as losing. Nobody had told me that. Possibly nobody knew. Possibly you only get to learn it six fathoms down with a detonator wire in your glove and Halcyon's divers thirty seconds behind you.

The light swept closer. A diver rounded the corridor bend, saw me, froze — a big silhouette in commercial gear, haloed in his own lamp, one hand going to the pry bar clipped at his hip.

I bridged the contacts.

Red wire to the notched terminal. The primer clicked, a sound I felt in my teeth more than heard, and the indicator window bloomed amber, then green.

Five, I counted. One. Two. The diver was moving now, pushing off the bulkhead, and behind him another light, and above us both, faint as weather, the dredger's floodlights trembled as Voss's negotiation entered what I could only assume was its theatrical final act. Three. The diver was ten meters out and closing, and I let go of the wire — green held, green held — and kicked for the corridor. Four. He grabbed for my ankle and got fin instead, and Sefu's voice crackled in my ear saying something I didn't catch, and it didn't matter, because five.

Green.

I swam.

* * *

The Kestrel gave me ninety seconds of grace, which was generous of her.

I was through the cargo hatch and rising past the collapsed spar when the first shockwave came up through the water — not a bang but a shove, the whole sea pressing its palms flat against me, and then the sound arrived, rolling and enormous, and the wreck below me exhaled. Silt bloomed outward in a great grey flower. Through it I saw, dimly, the structure folding inward, decks settling onto decks, the vault compartment and the compass and the bench and the coffee tin of pencils going down together into the trench that had waited five years for them, taking every claim Halcyon could ever file with them.

Above me, the water lit and shook. Somewhere overhead the *Meridian*'s divers were scrambling for their lines, and somewhere above that Voss was shouting into her megaphone with what I deeply hoped was triumphant perjury, and somewhere above that Wren was on my deck, watching the sea boil, watching for me.

I broke the surface into chaos and light and my sister's arms, and the last physical trace of my father went silent beneath us, and I will not pretend I didn't weep, because I did, ugly and loud, into her shoulder, while the crew stood around us in the spray and the *Meridian*'s searchlights wheeled overhead, finding nothing left to want.

Wren held on. She didn't say it's okay, because it wasn't, and she didn't say you did the right thing, because I hadn't asked. She just held on, and after a while I noticed she was crying too, and that we were, between the two of us, making enough salt water to sink the boat.

Then Pell's voice, dry as chart paper, cut across the deck:

"For the record — the *Meridian* is weighing anchor. But her launch is still in the water, and it's coming straight for us, and it is not slowing down."
