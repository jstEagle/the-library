## 20. Names Restored

The story broke on a Tuesday, which felt right. Tuesdays are the day the world does its accounting — rents come due, ships come due, and apparently, so do forty years of stolen salvage rights.

I was in a harbor town whose name I'll keep to myself, drinking coffee instead of anything stronger, because the last time I'd sat in a port bar waiting for news, the news had been Voss, and I'd decided to ration my bad habits going forward. Pell came out of the chandlery holding a newspaper like it was something dead he'd been asked to carry.

"You're page three," she said.

"Below the fold?"

"Above the fold. There's a photograph of the *Kestrel*."

"There's a photograph of everything," I said. "Half of them are wrong."

This one wasn't. It was an old survey shot, grainy, taken years before the wreck went down — my father's boat in her prime, and if you knew where to look, my father himself at the rail, one hand raised against the sun as though even then he was telling the camera to mind its own business. The headline ran: **HALCYON FOUNDER DEFRAUDED BLACKLISTED CREWS, LEDGER SHOWS**. Below that, in smaller type, the journalist's name — the one Voss had handed the copied pages to rather than any court — and below *that*, a list beginning to form. Names being reinstated. Registries reopening files that had been sealed with the same bureaucratic efficiency Halcyon had used to seal them in the first place.

My name was fourth on the list.

Pell set the paper down on the table between us and looked at me the way she looks at a chart when the soundings don't match the coastline. "Well?"

"Well what?"

"They're giving you back your license, Gideon. Both of them. Mountain guide, deep-water salvage. Full reinstatement, back pay considerations, a formal apology from the registry board." She said *formal apology* the way most people say *used bandage*.

"I read fast," I said.

"So?"

So. Five years I'd carried those two blacklists like ballast I couldn't jettison. Five years of taking the long way around every checkpoint, every harbor master's office, every man with a ledger and a stamp. Five years of telling myself the name they'd struck was the real one, and the woman walking around without it was just the shape left behind.

And now here was the board, hat in hand, offering to glue the name back on like it was a decal that had peeled off in the wash.

"They got it wrong," I said.

Pell waited. She's good at that. It's unnerving.

"Not the fraud part. That they got right down to the rivets. But the rest of it — the idea that they gave me something five years ago and can give it back now." I turned my coffee cup a quarter turn, for no reason except that my hands wanted work. "They didn't take my license, Pell. They took my *permission*. And I spent five years believing I needed their permission to be worth anything on a mountain or a sea. Turns out I crossed eleven passes and six tides and a reef channel at night without a single stamp in a single book. The blacklist was never the thing keeping me out. It was the thing I kept *in*."

"That's very quotable," Pell said. "The journalist would love it."

"The journalist can quote my elbow."

* * *

Okafor found me at the boat an hour later. He'd heard too — everyone had heard; the story had gone through the harbor like bilge water through a cracked seam — and he came up the gangway carrying two bottles of the local beer and wearing the expression of a man who has rehearsed a speech and intends to deliver it regardless of weather.

"Gideon Sterling," he said, formal as a christening.

"Don't."

"Reinstated guide. Reinstated salvor. A woman of standing and record." He handed me a bottle. "They're saying you could take government contracts. Survey work. Rescue coordination. They're saying the coastal authority wants to *consult* you."

"The coastal authority tried to arrest us three weeks ago."

"And now they want your opinion." He clinked his bottle against mine. "That's how empires apologize. They don't say sorry. They say *join the committee*."

I drank. The beer was mediocre and cold and exactly right. "What did you tell them? When they came asking about you?"

Okafor's grin faded into something more careful. "I told them I'd think about it." He turned the bottle in his big engineer's hands. "Five years ago I'd have signed anything they put in front of me. A stamp, a number, a berth on a sanctioned vessel — I'd have crawled over glass for it. Now?" He shrugged. "Now I know what their paperwork is worth. It's worth whatever they decide it's worth on any given Tuesday. My hands are worth more."

"Your hands are worth a great deal," I agreed. "Particularly when they're not bleeding."

"One time. One time I run a reef channel at night—"

"Two times. Don't make me find the logbook."

He laughed, and it rolled out over the water, and somewhere down the dock Sefu answered it with a whistle — the crew's old signal, *all present, all accounted for*, the one we'd used back before any of us had names worth striking. That was the thing about the weeks after the *Kestrel* fell silent. We were still together. Nobody had scattered. After a job ends, crews scatter like spilled coins — that's the natural law of it — but ours had stayed moored in the same harbor, doing small refit work, eating together, finding reasons to need each other that had nothing to do with money or tides.

I'd noticed it the way you notice a sound stopping. For five years I'd moved because stopping let the past catch up. And then the past had caught up anyway — dragged itself up from six fathoms in a diver's strobe light and a sister's rope-burned hands — and I'd stopped, and the world hadn't ended. The past had looked at me, and I'd looked at it, and we'd both decided we were tired.

* * *

Wren came aboard on Thursday.

She didn't knock — there's no knocking on a boat — but she paused at the gangway the way she used to pause at our father's study door, back when we were girls and the study was where the maps lived and you entered only if invited or brave. She was carrying a folder. Of course she was. Wren had spent five years on the lawful side of the water, and lawful people carry folders the way the rest of us carry knives.

"I saw the list," she said.

"Everyone saw the list."

"Your name's on it."

"Fourth. Okafor says I should frame it."

She came down into the cabin and sat across from me at the chart table, and for a moment neither of us spoke, because some silences are walls and some are doors and you have to wait to find out which one you've built. Hers, this time, was a door. I could tell because she kept glancing at it.

"I filed the paperwork," she said finally. "For you. Before the story broke. Three weeks ago — right after we got back. I pulled your case files, I wrote the petitions, I got two of Dad's old colleagues to testify about the original salvage claim." She slid the folder across the table. "You'd already been reinstated by the time the journalist printed anything. The story just made it official faster."

I looked at the folder. Petitions in her handwriting — Wren's handwriting hadn't changed since she was twelve, all sharp ascenders and no wasted ink. Dates going back weeks. Weeks during which I'd been mending hull plates and avoiding port offices, assuming the restoration was somebody else's charity, when it had been hers all along. Quietly. Without telling me. Without asking whether I'd want it.

"Why?" I asked. Not angry. Genuinely asking, which is rarer.

"Because you'd never do it yourself." She met my eyes. "Because you'd have stood on principle until you drowned in it. Because Dad spent his whole life believing the registries were fixable, and somebody had to finish his paperwork, and it was never going to be you." A breath. "And because I owed him. And you. In that order, depending on the day."

I opened the folder. Certificates. Stamps. My name — *Gideon Sterling* — restored to two registries, in ink, under seals, witnessed and countersigned. It should have meant more than it did. Or maybe it meant exactly what it did, which was this: proof that the door had always been unlocked, and the only person who'd ever locked it was me.

"Thank you," I said, and it came out rough, and I didn't try to sand it smooth.

"You're welcome." Then, because she's our father's daughter too and couldn't leave well enough alone: "Are you going to use it?"

I closed the folder. I thought about the committee invitations, the contracts, the coastal authority wanting my opinion, the whole vast machinery of legitimacy grinding into motion around my name like a fleet getting underway. I thought about how good it would feel, for about a month, to hand a harbor master a document and watch his face change. I thought about how long that feeling lasted. About a month.

"No," I said.

Wren nodded slowly. "I figured. But I had to ask."

"You figured?"

"Gideon. You took the dangerous route over a pass you'd crossed eleven times because the safe route bored you. You sank a fortune rather than let anyone own it. You think you're going to file float plans and renew stamps and sit on committees?" She almost smiled. "You'd die inside a year. They'd find you at a licensing seminar, perfectly preserved, with an expression of pure horror."

"That's fair."

"But—" and here she did the thing I should have expected and somehow didn't. She squared the folder on the table, the way she squared everything, and said, "Then I want to join the crew."

I stared at her.

"Not the registries' version of you. This version. The one with the boat and the rules nobody wrote down and the crew that shouldn't exist." Her voice stayed level, but her hands — her hands gave her away, the way they always had. "I spent five years on the right side of the law watching it fail people. Dad. You. Forty crews whose names are only coming back because a thief's own ledger convicted him. I'm done filing petitions after the damage is done. I want to be on the water when someone's drowning. Your boat goes where the drowning is. That's the whole business plan, isn't it?"

"It's barely a business plan at all."

"Exactly. I'm in."

I looked at my sister — at the sharp ascenders and the steady voice and the rope burns healing on her palms from a jammed bulkhead six fathoms down — and I thought about our father's logbook, which I'd been reading in slow installments like a man rationing the last of a good whiskey. *Take care of each other,* the final entry said. Not coordinates. Not a blessing in fancy words. Just that. He'd written it knowing one daughter would choose the law and one would flee it, and hoping, I think, that eventually they'd end up on the same boat.

"Conditions," I said.

Wren braced. Good. She remembered how this worked.

"One: you dive second. I go down first, always, until I say otherwise. Two: you don't carry folders. If it matters, you say it out loud. Three—" I pushed the folder back across the table to her. "Three: you keep these. Licenses, certificates, all of it. Somebody on this crew should stay legal enough to get us through the shoals of honest men. Pell's paperwork is flawless but forged, and forged only stretches so far."

She took the folder. Held it against her chest like a shield she'd finally been issued. "Accepted," she said. "All three."

"Then welcome aboard." I stood and put out my hand, formal as a christening, and she shook it, and neither of us cried, which for the Sterling family counts as restraint bordering on heroism.

From the deck above came Okafor's voice, not even pretending he hadn't been listening through the hatch: "*Does this mean we're a five-person crew now? Because I'm redoing the berthing assignments either way.*"

"*Redo them!*" Wren called back, and laughed, and the laugh went out over the harbor the way Okafor's had, and Sefu whistled the old signal again, and Pell — who had appeared at the hatch with the timing of a woman who misses nothing — simply said, "We'll need a bigger galley," which is navigator for *welcome home*.

* * *

That evening I climbed to the headland above the harbor, alone, the way I still sometimes do — not running anymore, just walking, which turns out to be a different thing entirely even when your legs make the same motions. From up there the boat looked small and right, riding her anchor light, five souls aboard where four had been that morning.

Below the town, in the harbor office, a lamp still burned. Through the window I could see the registrar at his desk, working late on the reinstatements, stamping names back into existence one by one. Somewhere in that stack was a certificate with my name on it, ready for collection, valid wherever the law ran.

I didn't go down for it.

Not out of spite — spite was ballast I'd finally jettisoned along with everything else. I just no longer needed the paper to know what I was. I was the woman who'd crossed eleven passes and six tides and one flooded cargo deck. I was the daughter of a man who'd hidden a compass in a wreck rather than let thieves profit from it. I was the sister of a woman who'd finished his paperwork and then thrown the folder's authority away to stand on a deck with the rest of us. The registries could keep their ink. I'd take the water.

Behind me, down the slope, a boy came running from the harbor office with a telegram flapping in his hand, calling my name — my full name, the restored one, shouted up the hillside like a summons. I turned. The lamp in the registrar's window had gone out. The telegram boy kept climbing.

Whatever it was, it had found me anyway. Things always did. The difference, now, was that I stopped walking and waited for it.

"Message for Gideon Sterling," the boy panted, holding it out. "It's marked urgent. From a young guide, up north — says she's stranded, says she's blacklisted, says—" he squinted at the wire, frowning at the last line, "—says she heard there's a crew that pulls people out of the water, and asks, please, which way is north?"

I took the telegram. I smiled.

I knew exactly which way.
