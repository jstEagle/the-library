## 21. The Route Nobody Maps

The boat we sailed north had no name on her stern.

Not because we'd scraped it off — though Pell had offered, with the particular relish of a man who'd spent thirty years filing paperwork and had recently discovered arson as a hobby. We'd simply never gotten around to painting one on. Okafor said a boat without a name couldn't be summoned. Sefu said that was superstition. Okafor said so was half of navigation, and Sefu didn't have an answer for that, because it was true.

So she went north unnamed, unregistered, uncontracted, and — for the first time in my adult life — with me at her wheel not because I was running from something but because I'd decided where I wanted to be.

It's remarkable how different those two things feel from the inside. Running has a texture to it. A taste, almost — copper and cold water, the flavor of five years of never taking the same route twice, never sleeping in the same bed a full week, never once looking back at the Kestrel even when I could feel her behind me like a hand between my shoulder blades. I'd told myself for years that this was freedom. It wasn't. Freedom doesn't flinch. What I had was just exile with better scenery.

This — the gray water off the northern coast, Wren asleep in the deck chair she'd claimed within a day of coming aboard, Okafor singing something tuneless in the engine room, Pell arguing with a chart he'd drawn himself and therefore could not blame — this had a different weight entirely. Heavier, oddly. Chosen things weigh more than escaped ones. But they sit differently on you. They don't dig in.

"You're smiling," Wren said, without opening her eyes.

"I'm steering."

"You're smiling *and* steering. One of those is new." She cracked one eye at me. My sister has our father's face rearranged by our mother's stubbornness — same jaw, same sea-gray eyes, but set at angles that suggest she'll argue with you about them. "The last time I saw you smile at a helm you were sixteen and you'd just stolen a skiff."

"I didn't steal it. I borrowed it without asking."

"It was the harbormaster's skiff."

"Which is why it was borrowing and not theft. Theft implies I intended to keep it."

She laughed — a short, surprised sound, like she hadn't authorized it — and pulled her jacket tighter, and went back to sleep in the sun, and I steered north, and nobody asked me for anything for another four hours, which may be the longest such stretch of my life.

* * *

We'd refitted in a yard south of the straits, a place run by a woman named Ilsa who owed Okafor a favor dating back to a war neither of them would describe to me in detail. Three weeks of work. New hull plate where the reef channel had bitten us — Okafor ran his thumb over the weld every morning like a man checking a scar. New fuel lines. A proper radio, which Pell insisted on testing twice daily by contacting people who mostly wished he wouldn't. And below decks, in what used to be the cargo hold, Sefu built out bunks and a galley and a small dry room lined with shelving, though none of us had discussed what the shelving was for.

I knew what it was for. So did he. The ledger pages Voss had photographed were already circulating — forty crews' worth of fraud, forty names Halcyon had buried under blacklists and legal fog, now sitting in the inbox of a maritime journalist who apparently had the patience of a saint and the discretion of a locked drawer. The story would break when it broke. In the meantime, word traveled the way it always does among people the registries erased: slowly, by radio, by rumor, by the occasional stranger sidling up to a bar and saying *you're the Sterling girl, aren't you* in a tone that five years ago would have sent me out the back door.

Now it made me stop and listen.

That was the other thing that felt different. For five years, being recognized had been a threat. Now it was starting to feel like an address.

* * *

On the fourth day north, past the last buoy anyone bothered to maintain, Pell came up to the wheelhouse holding two mugs and a look I'd learned to distrust.

"You should see this," he said, and handed me a paper instead of a mug.

It was a message slip. Radio traffic, logged and transcribed in his small, exact hand. Not addressed to us — nothing was ever addressed to us; we didn't exist, officially, which remained our best feature. But frequencies carry, and someone had been calling a mayday variant on a band the coastal authorities had stopped monitoring because no registered vessel was supposed to be there.

*"Vessel disabled, diver overboard, position approximate, requesting any station, repeat any station—"*

Then coordinates. Then silence.

"How old?"

"Fourteen hours. Signal strength suggests they're close. Close-ish." Pell is incapable of optimism without an asterisk. "Could also be a ghost. Could also be bait."

"For whom? Nobody knows we exist."

"Nobody official," Pell said. "But you've been having conversations in port bars again. Word gets around that there's a boat that answers."

I looked at the coordinates. Gray water, cold water, the kind of stretch where a disabled engine isn't an inconvenience, it's a countdown. And *diver overboard* — in water that temperature, fourteen hours ago, meant either they'd recovered him or they hadn't, and if they hadn't, there was no reason left to hurry, and if they had, there might still be a crew out there drifting with no engine and no registry and no one else coming.

Five years ago I'd have weighed this against the risk and the route and the fact that I never went back the way I came, and I'd have found a reason. That was the whole architecture of my life: reasons shaped like prudence, load-bearing walls made of fear.

"Gideon," Pell said carefully. "You're doing the thing where your face goes quiet."

"I'm deciding."

"You've decided. You did it before I finished talking. I just like to watch the process for professional reasons."

"Alter course," I said. "Two points east. And get Sefu warming the recovery gear."

Pell took back his paper, paused at the door. "You know," he said, "the old you would've needed a drink first."

"The old me needed a lot of things." I turned the wheel. "Turns out most of them were excuses."

* * *

We found them at dusk.

A thirty-foot workboat, dead in the water, riding low and listing slightly — engine flooded, they said later, saltwater in the intake, a rookie mistake by a skipper who'd clearly learned to dive before he'd learned to boat. Four aboard. The diver was back on deck, wrapped in every blanket they owned, hypothermic but breathing, which put him ahead of where he'd been six hours earlier when his line fouled and his buddy went in after him on a breath-hold that shouldn't have worked and did.

They were blacklisted. All four. A survey crew that had taken a contract from a company that turned out to be a Halcyon shell, and when the job went sideways and they complained about the safety violations, their names had gone into the same machine that had eaten mine. No license. No port rights. No rescue services, because rescue services are for people who exist.

Their skipper was a young woman named Ada Reyes, twenty-three maybe, with frostbite on two fingers and the particular hollow-eyed ferocity of someone who'd been awake for forty hours expecting to die. When our boat came alongside and Sefu threw the line across, she stared at me like I was a hallucination.

"We called every frequency," she said. "For two days."

"I know," I said. "Nobody monitors that band anymore."

"Then how—"

"Somebody's always listening," I said. "That's sort of the point of us."

We got their engine drained and restarted — Okafor worked miracles with their fuel system while lecturing the youngest crew member about intake maintenance in a tone that was somehow both scolding and deeply kind. We gave them food, charts, and a copy of the tide tables for waters nobody official mapped anymore. And when they cast off, Ada Reyes gripped my forearm hard enough to leave marks.

"They said once your name's struck, you're done," she said. "That's what they tell you. That's what I believed."

"Yeah," I said. "They tell you a lot of things."

"What changed? For you, I mean. How do you—" She gestured at the boat, at Sefu and Pell and Wren leaning on the rail, at all of it. "How do you keep going after they take everything?"

I thought about giving her the easy answer. *You just do.* The kind of thing you say to make the conversation end.

What I said instead was: "You find out they didn't actually take everything. They took the paperwork. Turns out I was never the paperwork."

She nodded like she understood, or wanted to, and then they were gone into the dark, running lights low, heading for a cove Pell had marked on their chart with a note in his precise hand: *safe anchorage, unfriendly harbor master, bring cash.*

Wren appeared beside me as the workboat's lights faded. She didn't say anything for a while. My sister has inherited exactly one trait from each parent and the trait she got from Dad was knowing when to shut up.

"You could have gone around them," she said finally.

"Could have."

"A year ago you would have."

"A year ago I was busy," I said. "Running takes up all your time. You'd be amazed how much room opens up when you stop."

She bumped her shoulder against mine — the Nystrom family embrace, full ceremony — and we stood there watching the dark water, and somewhere below us and behind us and ahead of us were a hundred more boats like Ada Reyes's, full of people whose names had been fed into a machine built by men who never got salt spray on their boots. The journalist's story was coming. Voss had told me that much before we parted, standing on the dock in a silence that held more respect than either of us knew how to say out loud. *When it breaks,* she'd said, *there are going to be a lot of people suddenly remembering they're angry.* I'd believed her. Mara Voss spent her whole career inside the law and found it rotten from the inside; when she predicts a storm, you check your moorings.

And when it broke, people would need somewhere to go. Boats that answered. Names that listened.

Sefu's shelving, waiting below decks.

* * *

That night, unable to sleep — a habit, not a problem; I've stopped pretending otherwise — I went down to the small cabin I'd claimed and opened the locker where I kept the one thing I'd carried through five countries and two blacklists and a war with my own conscience.

My father's logbook.

I'd had it since the funeral. Since the funeral I'd walked away from, technically — Wren had mailed it to me afterward, along with a note that said only *He'd want you to have this, and I hate that.* Five years I'd carried it. Five years I'd never read past the middle, because the middle was where the Kestrel job began, and everything after the middle was a story I already knew the ending of.

But tonight the boat was quiet, and steady, and moving toward something instead of away, and I suppose grief keeps its own calendar, because I opened the book to the final entry and started reading.

His handwriting slanted right, always had, letters leaning forward like a man in a hurry to reach the next word. The ink had feathered where damp had gotten at it. The date was three days before the Kestrel dove.

*Gideon will hate this job,* it began. *She's right to. I told Voss's people the survey parameters are wrong and they smiled at me the way companies smile when they've already decided. But the money clears Gideon's license appeal and Wren's tuition both, and a man can hold his nose for one season.*

*I've been thinking about what I leave them. Not money — there won't be money, whatever the contract says, contracts from these people are weather forecasts. I mean the real inheritance. So, girls, since neither of you will read this until I'm done needing to say it:*

*Gideon — you navigate by refusing. You always have. Even as a child you'd take the long way around a room rather than pass through the middle of it. I used to worry it was fear. It isn't. It's discernment. You know which doors aren't yours, and someday you'll find the one that is, and when you do, you'll walk straight through the middle of everything to reach it. Don't mistake the walking for running. You've never run from anything in your life. You've been circling. There's a difference, and you'll know it when you feel it.*

*Wren — you trust the map. Good. Maps are honest in ways people aren't. But remember your old man drew maps too, and I drew them wrong on purpose sometimes, to keep certain places safe from certain people. Some things are true and still shouldn't be written down. You'll learn which. You already know more than you think.*

*The compass stays with the Kestrel until it's needed. It doesn't point north. It points at what matters. That's not poetry — ask Okafor, he helped me build it. If you're reading this and I'm not there, it means the job went wrong, and I want you both to know the only thing I regret is the timing. Everything else — the routes, the risks, the two of you — I'd choose it all again, slower, with more sunscreen.*

*Go north in summer. Go anywhere together. That's the whole blessing. That's the whole map.*

*— Dad*

I sat in the lamplight for a long time with the book open on my knees, and the boat rose and fell beneath me, patient, unhurried, going nowhere in particular at my choosing.

*Circling.* Five years of thinking I was fleeing, and my father, three days before he died, had already seen through it — had known me well enough to name the thing I couldn't name myself. I hadn't been running from the wreck. I'd been orbiting it. Every route I never repeated, every horizon I put between us, every rule I carved out of grief — all of it was just a very long, very elaborate way of staying close to the thing I couldn't face.

And now I'd faced it. Gone down into it three times. Buried it with my own hands. Walked out of the water into my sister's arms.

The compass was gone, sunk with everything else in the trench. But he'd told me what it pointed at, and it turned out I'd been carrying the mechanism all along. It pointed at what mattered. Wren, asleep one deck up. A crew of people the registries erased, sailing anyway. A young skipper with frostbitten fingers who now knew somebody was listening. Forty names waiting for their story to break.

Above me, footsteps crossed the deck — Wren, checking the lines out of habit she'd learned from him, from me, from all of it. I closed the logbook gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping child, and went up to join her.

North in summer. Together.

Dad, I thought, watching my sister coil a line in the dark with quick, sure hands — you should see the route we're mapping. Nobody's ever drawn it before.

It doesn't point north.

It points wherever someone needs pulling out of the water.

And that, I decided, feeling the boat lean into the swell and turn her bow toward the open dark — that's north enough.
