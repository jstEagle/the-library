## 22. Kestrel and the Compass

A year is a long time to go without a destination, and I'd recommend it to anyone.

Not immediately. You have to earn the aimlessness first — you have to spend a few years running *from* something so that when you finally run *toward* nothing in particular, it feels like freedom instead of flight. There's a difference. Flight has a taste, like copper and swallowed words. Freedom tastes like Pell's terrible coffee at four in the morning while Okafor argues with the engine about a noise only he can hear, and Wren sleeps curled on the deck locker with her boots still on because she says she likes being ready, and Sefu stands his watch humming something off-key that might be a hymn or might be a complaint about the weather.

We don't file a course. That's the whole trick of us now. The boat — refitted, renamed, though we never did agree on what to rename her, so she's still just *the boat*, which suits her — goes where she's needed, and how anyone knows where she's needed is the part I'm still not entirely comfortable explaining, because it involves no paperwork whatsoever, and after everything, I've developed a deep spiritual attachment to the absence of paperwork.

Word travels the way word always has among people the registries forgot. A diver stranded past her air window because the charter captain panicked and filed a false report rather than admit he'd miscalculated the tide. A navigator blacklisted for a clerical error that was actually his employer's fraud, pinned on him because he had no lawyer and the company did. A salvage crew left unpaid in a foreign port because their flag state decided they'd stopped existing. They find us. Or someone who knows someone finds us, and a message arrives by radio burst or folded note or — once, memorably — a fishing trawler that pulled alongside just to hand over a sealed tin of tea with a scrap of paper inside, then chugged away without a word, as if delivering mail to ghosts were a perfectly ordinary Tuesday.

We charge nothing. Voss offered, once, to set up a fund — she's good at funds, at structures, at making money behave — and I told her that the moment we took a salary for this, we'd be an organization, and organizations have registries, and registries are how you end up erased. She looked at me for a long moment and then laughed, actually laughed, and said, "You know, five years ago I'd have called that naive."

"And now?"

"Now I call it the only business model that can't be bought." She'd flown out to meet us at anchor off some island whose name changes depending on which empire last drew the map. We drank coffee on deck — she brought real coffee, because Mara Voss does nothing halfway, including gifts — and we talked about the forty crews whose names were slowly crawling back onto the rolls, case by case, ledger page by photographed ledger page. Some of them wrote to us. Not all of them thanked us. One old man sent a letter that said only: *You should have saved the gold too.* I keep it in the chart drawer. It's a fair criticism. I reread it sometimes to remind myself that doing the right thing doesn't mean everyone claps.

Voss stayed two days. On the second evening, standing at the rail watching the sun do its usual extravagant performance over the water, she said, "I still think about what's down there."

"I know."

"The trench is nine hundred meters. Nobody's touching it."

"I know that too."

She nodded, and didn't say anything else, and that's why I respect her. We parted the way we always part — no handshake, no forgiveness, just two people who each know exactly what the other one is worth, which is a rarer thing than friendship and lasts longer.

* * *

The message came in on a grey morning in the northern latitudes, where the water is the color of unpolished steel and the light comes sideways all day like it's trying to sneak up on you.

Pell took it at the receiver, transcribed it in her small precise hand, and carried it to me without expression, which is how I knew it was bad. Pell has three expressions: neutral, smug, and the one she wore now.

"Read it," she said.

*STRANDED. GUIDE, AGE 24, BLACKLISTED LAST MONTH. TOOK A CLIENT PARTY UP THE SERAC ROUTE IN BAD WEATHER BECAUSE THE SAFE ROUTE WAS CLOSED BY THE COMPANY THAT FIRED ME. TWO CLIENTS DOWN IN THE ICEFALL. AUTHORITIES SAY IF I COME DOWN I'M ARRESTED FOR UNLICENSED GUIDING. THEY'RE RIGHT. I DON'T HAVE A LICENSE. YOU'RE THE ONLY NAME ANYONE GAVE ME. PLEASE.*

It wasn't signed with a name. It was signed with a set of coordinates and a frequency, which is its own kind of signature — the signature of someone who learned fast that names are liabilities.

I read it twice. The paper was cheap and the handwriting was desperate, letters crowding each other like people pushing through a door.

"Serac Route," Okafor said, reading over my shoulder. He said it the way you'd say *landmine field.* "That icefall eats a climber every season even with permits."

"She went up without permits," Wren said quietly. "In bad weather. Because the safe route was closed." She looked at me. "Because the company closed it."

"Because the company closed it," I confirmed. This is how it works now, everywhere, in every registry and every licensing office and every gleaming corporate office with its framed certificates: the companies close the safe routes to cut costs, fire the guides who complain, blacklist them for the crime of needing work, and then act astonished — *astonished* — when somebody desperate enough takes the dangerous way up. The mountain gets blamed. The dead get blamed. The guide gets blamed twice, once officially and once in every bar from here to the equator.

I know how it works because I invented half of it. Not the cruelty — nobody invents that, it predates us all — but the shape of it, the specific architecture of a life dismantled by paperwork. Five years ago I was the name struck from the list. Two years ago I stood in my father's drowned workspace six fathoms down and finally understood that the list was never the thing holding me. But there's understanding and there's understanding, and reading that message, I felt the old familiar pull behind my sternum — the one that used to mean *run* — except it pointed the wrong direction now. It pointed toward the coordinates.

"How far?" I asked Pell.

"Two days at cruising speed. Day and a half if we push, but we'd burn fuel we—"

"Push anyway. We'll figure out the fuel."

"Figuring out the fuel is my job," Okafor said, already moving toward the engine room, "and it's insulting that you keep saying it like it's yours."

Sefu was at the helm before anyone asked him. Wren checked the dive gear and the climbing kit both, because she's the kind of person who prepares for the mission and the mission after the mission fails. And I stood at the chart table with a stranger's desperate coordinates in my hand and felt something I hadn't felt since I was young enough to be stupid about it: certainty. Not the grim, clenched certainty of a woman racing a fleet. The other kind. The loose, warm, terrifying kind that feels exactly like happiness and is much harder to defend against.

"We're going," I said, unnecessarily, because the boat was already turning.

Nobody applauded. That's the other thing about us now — we've gotten past the stage where decisions require drama. Pell plotted. Okafor coaxed. Sefu steered. Wren made food that was technically edible. And somewhere south of the ice fields, a twenty-four-year-old girl with no license and two clients down in the ice sat by a radio waiting to find out whether the world contained anyone who would come for her without asking to see papers first.

* * *

We found her on the third day, because of course the weather added a day, because the weather is a bureaucrat too and demands its forms be filled in triplicate.

Her name was Isolde Kwan, and she was sitting in the mouth of a sea cave below the glacier terminus with two frostbitten clients wrapped in every layer she owned, having lowered them down the icefall herself over thirty-one hours on fraying rope, and when our dinghy grounded on the shingle she didn't cry or thank God or any of the things people do in stories. She just stood up, swayed, and said, "I couldn't take the coast road. They said there'd be a checkpoint."

"There isn't one anymore," I told her. "There hasn't been one for a year. But I understand why you didn't risk it."

"You do?"

"Girl, I once sailed four hundred miles around a harbor because I thought the harbor police had my description. They didn't. They'd lost my file entirely. I gave the blacklist more power than it had, because fear is generous that way — it inflates your enemies for free."

She stared at me. Up close she was all elbows and salt-cracked lips and the particular hollow-eyed steadiness of someone who has been awake too long and will stay awake as long as required, which is to say she looked exactly like I must have looked at twenty-four, before the wreck, before the lists, back when I was merely reckless instead of thorough.

"Are you Gideon Sterling?" she asked. "The messages said — they said Sterling's crew. They said you don't ask questions."

"We ask plenty of questions," Wren said, hauling one of the frozen clients gently aboard. "We just don't ask the ones with answers that matter."

It took two days to get the clients to a doctor who owed Sefu a favor and asked no questions either — the economy of favors among the unregistered is robust, I'll say that; it may be the last functioning economy on earth. Isolde stayed on the boat while they recovered, sleeping eighteen hours at a stretch and eating everything not nailed down, and on the third night she found me at the chart table doing the accounts, which these days consist of Pell writing numbers in a column and me nodding at them.

"They're going to reinstate licenses," she said. "Since the story broke. The fraud cases. My name could be cleared. Someone said there's a process now, an appeal, you just have to—"

"File paperwork," I said.

"Well. Yes."

I put down the pencil. I thought about how to say this, because it matters, because she was me, and because nobody said it to me when I needed it — I had to sail to the bottom of the ocean and blow up my father's grave to learn it, and I wouldn't wish that curriculum on anyone.

"Get your name cleared," I said. "Do the appeal. Take the license if they give it back. It's yours — it was always yours, and letting them keep it is letting them win twice."

She blinked. "But you — they say you refused yours. The famous Sterling refusal."

"They say a lot of things about me. Half of them are even true." I tapped the chart table. "Here's the part they leave out. I didn't refuse the license because licenses are evil. I refused it because I spent five years believing the license was the thing that made me real — that until some clerk stamped my name back into existence, I was a ghost. And then I learned the stamp was never the point. I was real the whole time. So was my crew. So are you. The license is a tool, Isolde, same as a rope or a radio. Useful when you need it. Fatal if you think it's holding you up."

She was quiet for a while. Outside, the northern water did its steel-grey breathing against the hull.

"What do you do," she finally asked, "if you don't belong anywhere? If there's no registry with your name in it? Where do you go?"

And I'll be honest — I almost gave her the speech. The one about freedom and chosen movement and the road nobody maps. But she was twenty-four and hollow-eyed and she didn't need philosophy, she needed a heading. So I did what my father would have done, and what took me thirty years and one drowned mountain to learn how to do.

I turned the chart around so she could see it, and I pointed.

* * *

After we dropped her at the port where the doctor worked — with a radio frequency, a list of names, and instructions that amounted to *when you're ready, pass it on* — we stood off the coast for the night, riding easy, no destination filed.

Wren came into the chart room carrying two cups of Pell's terrible coffee, which is how I know she loves me, because nobody drinks that coffee voluntarily. She set mine down next to the compass.

Our compass. My father's compass, salvaged from the vault compartment in the last minutes before the charge, because some things you bury and some things you carry, and I've never been able to tell you the rule for which is which. It sits on the chart table now, brass worn smooth, needle swinging lazy in its housing. It doesn't point north. It never did — that was the whole secret of it, the joke my father hid inside his own drowning, the punchline delivered posthumously to a daughter who took five years to open the logbook. It points wherever it points. Toward trouble, mostly. Toward people in the water. Toward whatever needs pulling out.

Wren followed my eyes to it. "Still broken?" she asked, and smiled, because we've decided together that it isn't broken, that *broken* was always the wrong word, that maybe the needle was the only honest instrument on any of our boats all along.

"Still pointing," I said.

She leaned against the table beside me, shoulder to shoulder, and for a while neither of us said anything, because we're sisters and we've earned our silences the hard way, through jammed bulkheads and shared rope and five wasted years, and silences like that don't need filling.

Then the receiver crackled. Pell's voice came through the bulkhead, dry as ever: "Message coming in. Unregistered source. Sounds young." A pause. "Sounds scared."

Wren looked at me. I looked at the compass, and the needle swung — not north, never north — settling toward some bearing only it understood, some coordinate of trouble or drowning or desperation that existed right now, tonight, somewhere out in the dark water, attached to a name that had probably just been struck from a list.

I picked up the microphone.

"Go ahead," I said. "We're listening. And we're already moving."

Because here's the thing I finally know, the thing I'd have paid five years not to learn any other way but am oddly glad I did: north is not a direction. North is wherever someone needs pulling out of the water. And that, I've decided, is north enough.
