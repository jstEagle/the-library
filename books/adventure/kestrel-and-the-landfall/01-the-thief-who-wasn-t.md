## 1. The Thief Who Wasn't

The thief made three mistakes before the sun cleared the eastern rim, and Wendell Winterbourne counted every one of them the way other people counted money.

The first mistake was coming to Saltmere at all. The town sat on the drowned flats like a bone caught in a gull's throat—too dry for fishers, too poor for merchants—and nobody with honest business arrived before dawn. The second was wearing boots with new soles. She'd seen them from her window above the chandlery: pale leather, unscuffed, catching moonlight like a confession. Nobody in Saltmere had new soles. The third mistake was the one that would end him: he took the long way around the salt pans instead of cutting straight across the crust.

He didn't know the flats. That was the whole of it. He didn't know that the ground out there wore two faces—one hard as kiln brick when the tide was out and the wind had baked it, and one soft as porridge where the brine seeped up beneath a skin of white. A stranger saw flat land and walked in straight lines. Wendell read the flats the way she'd once read tide-charts and convoy routes, and she read this one now from the ridge above the pans, watching his lantern bob toward a stretch of ground that would swallow him to the hip by the time he understood what was happening.

She should have let the flats do her work for her. It would have been cleaner. But twelve years of being nobody had taught her patience, and patience said: *watch first.*

So she watched, and the thief did something no thief should do.

He stopped. Set down his lantern carefully, shielding the flame with his body. And looked back at the ridge—at her—with the particular stillness of a man who knows he's been made.

Then he raised one hand, palm out, and waited.

Wendell stood a long moment with the wind pulling at her coat, feeling something old and cold turn over in her chest. Not fear. Recognition. This wasn't a man running from what he'd stolen. This was a man delivering what he'd carried.

* * *

She came down off the ridge by the goat track, giving the soft ground its due, and found him waiting at the edge of the pans with his hands visible and his lantern turned low. Up close he was younger than she'd guessed—mid-twenties, maybe, with the sunburned neck of a deckhand and the restless eyes of someone who'd been told to expect trouble and had rehearsed his lines too many times.

"You're not the town constable," he said.

"No."

"Good. Because I'd hate to have walked four hundred miles to be arrested for stealing my own boots." He glanced down at the offending soles and winced. "Vane said you'd spot me inside an hour. I told her two. She owes me a bottle."

The name landed in Wendell's stomach like a dropped anchor. She kept her face still. Twelve years of practice kept her face still.

"Don't know any Vane," she said.

"No," the young man agreed pleasantly. "You wouldn't. You're Wendell Marsh, ranger, licensed out of the Saltmere district, six years' service, no family, no debts, no history." He recited it like a manifest. "That's what the license says. What Captain Vane says is that you used to be the best navigator the salvage fleets ever poisoned themselves with, and that you've been hiding out here in the dust playing lawman for a decade because the last time you read a tide-chart, people died."

The wind moved across the flats, hissing through the salt grass. Somewhere behind them, in the dark, the first birds were waking over the shallows.

"You have until I count to five," Wendell said quietly, "to start walking back the way you came."

"I have until the tide turns," he said, "which is near enough the same thing, if you think about it." He reached into his coat—slowly, showing her every movement—and drew out a packet wrapped in oilcloth, tied with tarred twine. He set it on a flat stone between them rather than handing it over. Smart. His hands stayed empty; hers stayed free.

"Captain Ilsa Vane, of the *Magpie's Debt*, sends her compliments," he said. "And a contract."

"I don't take contracts."

"This one you'll want to burn rather than sign. But you'll want to read it first. Everyone does." He stepped back, giving her room, and there was something almost gentle in the way he did it—the gentleness of a man who has delivered bad news before and learned how much space grief needs. "It's the *Kestrel*, Ranger. The wreck's been found. And the fleet's already moving."

* * *

She didn't touch the packet for a full minute. She made herself stand there and look at it, because looking was safe and touching was not, and in those sixty seconds the flats did what the flats always did—they went on being indifferent, white and cracked and glittering under the strengthening light, as though the world contained nothing worth trembling over.

Twelve years ago she had been somebody else. Somebody with a different name and a different face and a navigator's chair aboard the finest hull in the salvage fleets, and that somebody had done a thing on a night of black water and burning pitch that she had spent every day since refusing to name even in the privacy of her own skull. The *Kestrel* had gone down with forty-one souls. The sea had kept the truth. Wendell had built a life on top of it the way the flats build skin over brine—thin, white, and load-bearing only if nobody steps wrong.

Now some fool had found the wreck.

She untied the twine. Inside the oilcloth lay a single sheet of heavy paper, written in a hand she remembered too well—sharp-angled, impatient, beautiful the way a knife is beautiful.

*Winterbourne—or whatever you call yourself these days—*

*I know you won't come for money. So don't pretend. Come because the rival fleet is going to raise her, and when they do, they'll find what's in her hold, and what's in her hold has your name written all over it. Literally. I've seen the manifest fragment. Has your name, your old rank, and a date.*

*I'm offering you a head start. My ship, my crew, my silence. We race them to the site, we take what matters, and we decide together what the world learns about the night the Kestrel sank. Or you stay here, keep playing constable, and learn what's in your own past from a salvage auction notice.*

*I always said you were the fastest thing on water. Prove it.*

*—I.V.*

*P.S. Six tides. After that, the *Corbie* sails without us, and everything in that hold belongs to whoever gets there first.*

Wendell read it twice. Then she folded it along its creases with great care, the way you fold something you intend to keep or destroy but cannot yet decide which, and looked up at the messenger.

"She sent you alone? Across the flats? With this?"

"She said if anyone could find me in the dark, you could. She said—" the young man hesitated, and for the first time he looked genuinely uncomfortable, "—she said you'd want to know it was Sefa's crew that found the wreck. The girl who sweeps your deck. Her cousin's on the *Corbie*, and the *Corbie*'s signed with Harrow's fleet."

The name Sefa went through Wendell like cold water through a crack. Her deckhand. Nineteen years old, quick-handed, laugh-first, the closest thing Wendell had let herself love since the night the *Kestrel* went down. And now tied by blood to the very fleet racing toward the grave Wendell had helped dig.

"The tide turns within the hour," the messenger said into the silence. He nodded at the flats, where the far edge of the white was already darkening, the brine rising. "Captain said you'd understand what that means better than anyone alive."

Wendell understood. The tide turned whether you were ready or not. It had turned twelve years ago, and she had run from it ever since, and now it had followed her across four hundred miles of dust to the one place she'd thought it couldn't reach.

She looked east, where the sky was going the color of a bruise healing, and thought of Sefa asleep in the bunkhouse, dreaming ordinary dreams. Thought of the crew who trusted her. Thought of a sheet of paper with her name on it, lying in a dead ship's hold, waiting to be pulled up into the light.

"Tell Vane," Wendell heard herself say, "I'll be at the harbor by the second tide."

The messenger grinned, saluted sloppily, and started back across the flats—along the hard ground, the safe path, the path she'd have shown him anyway.

Wendell stood alone as the sun came up over Saltmere, holding a dead woman's handwriting in her fist, and began—without quite deciding to—to count tides.
