## 2. Six Tides

**Wendell**

The thief's message sat on the chart table all night, and by dawn it had not grown any smaller or any kinder.

*One run. The Kestrel. Six tides to make the window. Half shares, and I keep my mouth shut about who you used to be.*

Vane's hand was as ugly as ever—letters like broken masts. Wendell read it a fourth time while the *Marrow's Grace* rocked at her moorings in Saltglass Harbor, and outside her cabin door she could hear the crew waking: Sefa banging the kettle, old Tammo coughing up the night, Brannic arguing with the gulls again. Twelve years of small sounds. Twelve years of being nobody in particular, a desert ranger who happened to own a flat-bottomed salvage skiff and a crew that trusted her.

She folded the letter into quarters, then eighths, until it was a hard little knot of paper she could have swallowed.

The problem was not that Vane knew. The problem was that Vane had always known—had known since the day Wendell walked out of the fleet's registry with salt-scoured lungs and a new name stitched into her collar—and had never once used it. Not when Vane lost the Meridian contract to her. Not when prices crashed and the *Grace* took work Vane's ships wouldn't touch. Twelve years of silence, bought for nothing, and now priced at exactly one run to a wreck Wendell had spent twelve years pretending she'd never heard of.

She went out onto the deck because staying in the cabin felt like drowning slowly, and out here you could at least drown honestly.

Saltglass Harbor at first light was brown water and rusted cranes, the drowned flats beyond it steaming where the night tide had gone out and left the world looking skinned. And there, past the harbor mouth, riding at anchor in the deep channel like a knife left standing in a table: the *Corvid's Debt*, black-hulled, three masts, Vane's flagship. Behind her, two more hulls were warping in from the offing, and behind those, on the horizon haze, the gray suggestion of more.

Not one ship coming for the Kestrel. A fleet.

"Captain." Sefa stood at the rail beside her, sixteen years old and all elbows, hair tied back with wire because ribbon cost money. The kid had been watching the same ships. "That's Vane's. That's the *Debt*. Captain, why is the *Debt* anchored in our harbor?"

"Passing through," Wendell said.

"Vane doesn't pass through anything. Vane arrives." Sefa squinted at her, and there it was—the look Wendell dreaded more than any storm, the look of a young person who had not yet learned that captains lie. "We're taking a job for her, aren't we. That's what the thief was about."

Wendell weighed it. She could feel the lie assembling itself in her throat, smooth and practiced, the way it had assembled itself twelve years ago on another deck in front of other trusting faces: *the manifest's routine, the cargo's nothing, trust me.* She'd been very good at it once. It had gotten thirty-one people killed.

"The job's a wreck run," she said, and every word of it was true, which is how the best lies are built. "Deep flats, past the Scalds. Six-tide window before the weather turns."

"And the cargo?"

"Salvage. Papers, mostly." Also true. Also a knife. "Old registry documents. Worth something to collectors."

Sefa looked at the *Corvid's Debt* a moment longer, then nodded, satisfied, and went forward to help Tammo with the lines, and Wendell stood at the rail feeling the knot of paper in her pocket burn like a coal, and understood—with a clarity that arrived the way bad news always does, all at once and far too late—that she had just done it. Just done the exact thing. The smooth voice, the partial truth, the crew nodding along on credit they hadn't agreed to spend.

Twelve years ago she'd told the *Kestrel*'s owners the channel markers were sound. They had believed her because she was the best navigator the fleet had ever certified, and belief in a navigator is a kind of cargo, and she had sunk it with them.

She went below and did not throw the letter in the stove. That was the thing she couldn't explain, even to herself. Every honest instinct said burn it, refuse the run, let Vane keep her secret and her silence both. But under the dread, under the guilt, something older and hungrier had lifted its head. Curiosity, maybe. Or the hope—foolish, unforgivable—that after twelve years the wreck might hold something besides proof of what she'd done. Some version of events where she'd been less guilty. Some current that ran a different way.

Six tides. She could survive six tides of lying. She'd survived twelve years of it.

She told herself that, and hated how easily it came.

* * *

**Sefa**

Sefa had stolen things before—apples, mostly, and once a brass compass worth more than the whole dockside market—but never information, and it turned out information was easier. You just listened.

By midmorning the whole crew had gathered in the fo'c'sle to argue the contract, and Sefa sat on the coil of rope by the door with a mug of tea going cold, doing what Sefa did best: saying nothing and missing nothing.

"It's Vane," Brannic said, pacing the three steps the compartment allowed. He was the biggest man aboard and the most nervous, which everyone pretended not to notice. "Nobody works for Vane twice. Ask anyone in the fleet."

"We're not in the fleet," Tammo said. He was mending a net without looking at it, hands moving on their own. "That's rather the point of us."

"She's paying half shares. *Half.*" Brannic stopped pacing. "For a papers run? Tammo, paper don't pay half shares. Paper pays a finder's fee and a thank-you. Either Vane's gone generous, or whatever's down on that wreck is worth killing for, and I don't love either answer."

"Could be charts," Tammo said. "Old charts go high."

"Charts don't bring a *fleet*, either." Brannic jabbed a thumb toward the porthole, where the black hull of the *Corvid's Debt* sat visible across the water. "You seen how many sails came in overnight? Four. Four heavy hulls, and that's just what's inside the harbor. There's more standing offshore—I counted mastheads at dawn. That's not a salvage op. That's an armada."

"So we ask her," said Petrel, the youngest full hand after Sefa, arms crossed, chin up. Petrel worshipped the captain the way Sefa secretly did and would have died before admitting it. "Whatever it is, she'll tell us straight. She always tells us straight."

And there it was, the sentence that made Sefa's stomach hurt. Because Sefa had been on deck at first light. Sefa had watched the captain's face while she lied—or thought she'd watched it, and this was the part that kept turning over like a fish on a hook: maybe she hadn't lied. Maybe Sefa had imagined it. Maybe wanting to catch a lie was its own kind of dishonesty.

But the captain had said *papers, mostly*, and her voice had done the thing it did when she was managing them, the half-step-too-calm thing, the voice she used on frightened passengers in weather. Sefa knew that voice. Everyone aboard knew that voice. Nobody ever said so, because the captain was kind with it, and kindness buys a lot of silence.

In the end the vote wasn't close. Half shares, six tides' work, and the chance to beat Vane's whole fleet to a prize—it carried four to one, with Tammo abstaining on grounds of superstition and Brannic voting yes while announcing loudly that he expected to regret it.

When the captain came down to hear the result, Sefa watched her face the way you watch a tide gauge: for the reading underneath the surface. The captain smiled, thanked them, said they'd sail on the evening tide. And then, for half a heartbeat, looking at the chart of the deep flats spread on the table—at a spot her finger found without hunting, dead center of the Scalds, like a compass needle finding north—her face did something Sefa had no word for. Not fear. More like recognition. Like someone walking into their old house after the family that bought it changed the locks.

"You know those waters," Sefa said. It came out before Sefa could stop it. "The Scalds. You didn't even check the chart."

"I've run those flats," the captain said, "in another life."

"What was the boat?"

The pause lasted one second too long. "A long time ago, Sefa."

She rolled the chart up and went topside, and Sefa sat with the cold tea and the shape of the lie, and thought: *papers, mostly*. Thought: *another life*. Thought about the black hull across the water, and the four-to-one vote, and how the whole crew had just signed on to a voyage whose true cargo was something the captain couldn't say out loud.

Tammo caught Sefa's eye across the compartment. The old man tied off his knot, slow, and gave the smallest shake of his head—not *don't ask*, Sefa realized. *Don't yet.*

* * *

**Wendell**

They warped out on the evening tide with the sun going down red behind the harbor cranes, and the *Corvid's Debt* raised anchor within the hour and followed.

Wendell stood at the helm through the first dark miles, running the channel by memory and starlight, and behind her the crew worked the deck singing one of Tammo's hauling songs, easy and unafraid, and every note of it landed on her like ballast.

At midnight she took out the letter one last time and read the line she'd skipped every previous reading, the line written small along the bottom margin, almost an afterthought:

*Bring your crew if you must. But come alone to the wreck, Wendell. We both know some cargoes shouldn't be witnessed.*

Ahead, far out on the black water, the leading lights of Vane's fleet burned in a ragged line, all of them headed for the same patch of drowned sea, and Wendell put the paper away and turned the wheel toward the Scalds and told herself, one more time, that she could carry it six tides longer.

Behind her, unheard, Sefa stood in the shadow of the deckhouse, having seen everything.
