## 3. What the Water Keeps

The Kestrel's grave announced itself the way graves do: by what wouldn't grow there.

Wendell felt it through her boots before she saw anything—a wrongness in the flats, a deadness where the salt crust gave way to silt that had never dried in twelve years. The tide charts said they had six hours. The sky said less. It had come up bruise-colored over the eastern rim of the world, and the wind carried that flat, mineral smell she'd spent half her life learning to fear.

"Storm water," Sefa said beside her, squinting at the horizon. The kid had a knack for reading weather the way other people read faces—too well, too young. "That's not a squall line. That's a wall."

"It's a long way off."

"It's coming *here*."

Wendell didn't answer. She was looking at the wreck.

The Kestrel lay on her side in a shallow basin the flood tides had scoured and refilled for a decade, her hull furred with barnacle and weed, her masts snapped to stumps like broken fingers. From this distance she looked almost peaceful. Almost natural. Wendell knew better. She knew every plank of that hull, because she had been the one who put it there.

*Twelve years.* She'd told herself the number so often it had gone smooth as river stone. Twelve years since she'd stood on the Kestrel's quarterdeck with a chart in her hands and a lie in her mouth, and navigated thirty-one people into a channel that did not exist.

The salvage crew spread out across the flats behind her—Vane's people, hired muscle with pry bars and winch sleds, and her own four, who had come because Wendell had asked them to and never asked why. Ropes went out. Lanterns were lit against the darkening day. And Captain Ilsa Vane came down the shingle with her coat snapping, wearing the smile of a woman who had already won something.

"You look like you've seen a ghost," Vane said.

"I'm looking at one."

"Mm." Vane stopped beside her, close enough that only Wendell could hear. "Funny thing about ghosts, Winterbourne. They keep better than people do. Paper especially." She tapped her own chest, where a waterproof document case hung on a strap. "Manifest's still sealed in the captain's strongbox, if the charts are right. Fleet's offered me more than this whole run is worth for it. Intact."

Wendell's stomach dropped through the mud beneath her feet. "You said cargo."

"I said what I had to say to get you here." Vane's smile didn't waver. "You're the only navigator alive who knows these channels, and you're the only one who knows why that matters. The fleet wants their traitor back, Wend. Or her head. I haven't decided which I'm selling."

The first thunder rolled in from the east, distant as a door closing.

* * *

They worked fast after that, because there was nothing else to do. Wendell ran lines herself, hauling sledges across the silt, and tried not to think about how the Kestrel's rail felt under her hands when she finally climbed aboard—familiar, damning, like shaking hands with someone she'd buried.

Sefa was below, in the old hold, cutting away rot with a boarding hatchet while the storm light flickered down through the shattered deck above. Wendell could hear the kid humming. That was the worst part. That Sefa still hummed. That Sefa trusted her enough to hum in the dark.

"Wend!" Sefa's voice cracked up through the decking, sharp with excitement. "There's a strongbox under the ballast stones! It's—hold on—it's got a name plate!"

Wendell went down the ladder three rungs at a time.

The box sat in the circle of Sefa's lantern, crusted green, its brass plate wiped clean by the kid's sleeve. Wendell read the engraving upside down and felt the world tilt.

*Kestrel — Manifest & Log — By order of Harrow Voss, Master.*

"That's the fleet founder's mark," Sefa said, eyes shining. "This is it, isn't it? This is what we came for. What's in it? Why does Vane want it so bad?"

"Sefa—"

But the kid had already found the seam, already wedged the hatchet blade in, already leaned weight into it the way Wendell had taught her—*commit or don't touch it*—and the lock gave with a crack that sounded, in the flooded hold, like a bone breaking.

Papers spilled into Sefa's lap. Wax seals, water-stained but legible. A manifest. A logbook. And clipped to the front page, a deposition in a hand Wendell recognized because she'd once signed it.

Sefa read. Wendell watched the kid's face do something she had never seen it do—not confusion, not fear, but the slow, terrible arithmetic of a person adding up years of small kindnesses and finding a different sum at the bottom.

"'...the navigator Winterbourne,'" Sefa read aloud, voice thinning, "'did knowingly alter the tidal charts and steer the Kestrel into the drowned channel at Marrow Reach, causing the loss of the vessel and thirty lives—'"

"Stop."

"—'thirty-one souls, of whom—'" Sefa looked up. "Wend. *Wend*, what is this? This isn't—this has your name. This has your—"

Above them, the storm arrived.

It didn't build. It simply landed—the whole sky dropping onto the flats at once, rain like gravel, wind screaming through the broken masts. Wendell heard Vane shouting somewhere topside, heard the winch crews scatter, and then heard the sound underneath all the others: the sea, coming back.

Flood tide. Hours early, driven ahead of the storm front, pouring into the basin faster than any chart had ever promised her.

"The box," Wendell said. "Leave it. We go now."

"But the manifest—"

"*Now.*"

She grabbed Sefa's wrist and hauled for the ladder, and the water met them halfway—waist-deep and rising, cold enough to stop breath. They came up onto the tilting deck into chaos: lanterns dying, lines parting, Vane's crew abandoning the wreck for the high ground with the selfish efficiency of professionals. Vane herself stood at the rail, document case strapped tight, watching Wendell with an expression that wasn't triumph anymore. It was almost pity.

"Tide's got the basin!" Vane shouted over the wind. "Your charts were wrong, navigator! How's it feel?"

Wendell didn't answer. There was no time, and the truth was too large to shout anyway. She counted heads—three of hers, four of Vane's—and came up one short.

"Sefa?" She turned. "Where's Sefa?"

A cry from the leeward rail. Sefa had gone back for the manifest. Of course she had. Wendell had taught her that too—*you don't leave proof behind*—and now the kid was clinging to the rail as a wave broke clean over the deck, and the wave took the hatchet line, took her footing, took her.

Sefa went over the side into black water churning with silt and ten years of drowned history, one hand locked around a waterlogged sheaf of papers, and the current took her toward the open channel like it had been waiting twelve years for exactly this.

Wendell dove.

The water hit her like a wall of fists. She'd grown up in these seas; she'd forgotten how much they hated her. She swam by feel, by the old navigation that lived in her spine deeper than any betrayal, and found Sefa's collar on the third stroke as the kid went under a second time. The papers streamed away between their fingers, ink blooming into the dark like wounds, and Wendell let them go—let the manifest, the log, the whole written record of what she'd done dissolve into the sea that had kept it all along.

*What the water keeps,* she thought, kicking for the surface with Sefa's arm locked in hers, *the water keeps.*

They came up gasping. The wreck slid past them, sinking back into its grave, and beyond it Vane's launch was fighting toward shore, and beyond that the harbor lights of Landfall were coming on one by one through the rain—warm, ordinary, unknowing.

Wendell got Sefa onto the shingle and pressed the water out of her until the kid coughed, alive, furious, staring at her with eyes that held no trust left in them at all.

"You," Sefa rasped. "It was *you*. All this time it was you, and I—" A sob tore loose. "I defended you. To Vane. To everyone."

"I know."

"They'll hang you."

"Probably," Wendell said, and stood, and turned toward the harbor council hall where the storm had driven everyone in town, where Vane would be telling her story even now, selling peace to the fleet with Wendell's name as the coin. "So I'm going to get there first."

Sefa stared at her through the rain. "Why?"

"Because they're going to hear it from me," Wendell said, "or not at all."

She started walking. Behind her, the sea closed over the Kestrel without a sound, keeping what was left.
