## 4. Landfall

The storm broke at dawn, and the harbor came back to us in pieces.

I stood at the *Kestrel's* rail—our *Kestrel*, the little one, the one Wendell had bought with twelve years of honest salvage and salt-scoured hands—and watched the water settle into that glassy, exhausted calm that follows a sea's worst temper. The flats beyond the breakwater were littered with the storm's leavings: kelp in black ropes, a split crate bobbing like a drowned bird, and far out, the mast of the rival fleet's lead ship, *Mercy of Crows*, listing at an angle that told me her captain had lost the argument with the tide.

Vane's ship had fared no better. She lay at anchor in the outer roads, patched and humbled, her crew working in silence that carried across the water like an apology nobody wanted to make.

Wendell wasn't on deck. She hadn't been on deck since we'd hauled Sefa—since we'd hauled *me*—out of the flood channel, coughing brine, my fingers too frozen to grip the line she'd thrown. She'd carried me up the ladder one-armed, and she hadn't let go until I was wrapped in canvas by the stove, and even then her hand had stayed on my shoulder a moment too long, trembling in a way I'd never seen her tremble.

She'd said one thing, hoarse as a gull's cry: "I have to tell them everything."

And I'd said, "Tell *me* first."

She had. All of it. The original *Kestrel*, twelve years ago. The manifest she'd altered, the channel markers she'd moved, the fleet's trust she'd sold for a price I still couldn't say out loud without my throat closing. The wreck we'd raced to wasn't just a wreck. It was her crime, sunk and waiting, and she'd known it the whole way across the flats while the rest of us thought we were chasing salvage rights.

She'd told me first. That mattered. I didn't know yet how much it would matter, but I held onto it like a lifeline.

* * *

The harbor council convened at noon in the Saltstone Hall, a room I'd only ever seen from the outside—a long chamber of driftwood beams and whale-oil lamps, where the five council members sat behind a table scarred by a century of decisions. The whole town seemed to have squeezed in. Fishermen still dripping from the docks. The salvage fleet's representatives in their oilskin finery. Vane herself, standing at the back with her arms crossed and her face arranged into something like patience.

Wendell stood in the center of the floor in her ranger's coat, the one with the silver tide-marks on the cuffs, and she didn't look at Vane. She didn't look at the council. She looked at us—her crew, crammed against the far wall, Sefa's hand white-knuckled around mine—and then she turned and faced the table.

"My name is Wendell Winterbourne," she said, and the room went so quiet I could hear the lamp flames. "Twelve years ago, I was the salvage fleet's chief navigator. And twelve years ago, I sank the original *Kestrel*."

The sound that went through the hall wasn't a gasp. It was older than that—a collective intake, like the sea drawing back before a wave.

She told it plainly. No performance, no self-flagellation dressed up as drama. The fleet had been running a salvage op on a wreck in contested waters, and the cargo—charts, a ledger of salvage claims, proof of a fleet captain's fraud—would have ruined a man named Aldous Crane, who had bought his way into the fleet's good graces and half the council's, too. Wendell had been paid to lose the *Kestrel* on the flats. To move the channel markers. To navigate her crew into the storm path and walk away before she went down. Three crew had died. She'd told herself it was necessary. She'd told herself a lot of things.

"I took the money," she said. "I took the name change. I took twelve years of being no one, and I told myself that was penance. It wasn't. It was hiding." Her voice didn't break, but it went thin as wire. "Captain Vane found the wreck's manifest three days ago. It names me. She offered me a choice: surrender the salvage rights and my silence, or she'd hand me to the fleet and buy peace with my arrest."

Vane stepped forward. "The offer stands—"

"It doesn't," Wendell said, and something in her voice made even Vane stop. "I'm not trading with you. I'm not trading with anyone." She turned back to the council. "I forfeit the salvage claim on the wreck. I forfeit my ranger's license. I'm not asking for pardon. I'm asking for a hearing—a real one, with the fleet's records and the survivors' families present. Whatever they decide, I'll stand for it."

Old Councilor Marsh, who'd been a boy when the *Kestrel* went down, leaned forward. "And your crew? They sailed under your name. The law says complicity—"

"The law says a lot of things," Wendell said. "My crew knew nothing. Punish me, not them."

And then Sefa moved.

I'll remember it the rest of my life. She pushed off the wall, this gangly kid of sixteen with storm-wrecked hair and a rope burn across her palm, and she walked to the center of the floor and stood beside Wendell like she'd been standing there all along.

"I knew," Sefa said. "Not the details. But I knew she was hiding something, and I sailed anyway. And last night, when the flood tide had me, she's the one who came in after me. So if you're weighing complicity, weigh that too." Her voice cracked, and she didn't care. "You don't get to have her courage without her crime. They're the same person."

The hall murmured. Vane's face did something complicated—defeat, maybe, or the strange relief of a gambler finally out of chips.

Councilor Marsh looked at Wendell for a long moment. "The hearing will convene in seven days. The fleet's advocates will come. The families will be heard." He tapped the table once. "Until then, Wendell Winterbourne, you are remanded to the harbor—not under guard. Under witness of the town. Go home."

* * *

We walked out of the Saltstone Hall into late afternoon light, and the whole crew was waiting on the steps—Brannic with his arms crossed, old Tull with his cap in his hands, little Osa perched on the rail like she'd never left it. Nobody spoke. The harbor lay gold and still around us, and somewhere a bell buoy clanged its slow, patient count.

Wendell stopped at the bottom of the steps. She looked older, suddenly—twelve years older, all at once, like the weight she'd been carrying had finally been set down and left its shape in her.

"You heard all that," she said. "You don't have to—there are other ships. Other harbors. I'll write you references, good ones, I'll—"

Brannic snorted. "References. From a woman who just gave up her license to tell the truth in front of the entire harbor." He shook his head, slow. "Captain. You sank a ship. You also sailed into a flood tide for a deckhand the law says you owed nothing to. I've served under honest men who wouldn't have done that."

"We're not staying because you're innocent," Tull said quietly, turning his cap in his hands. "We're staying because you stopped lying. That's worth more than a license."

Osa slid off the rail and took Wendell's hand like it was the most natural thing in the world.

Sefa looked at me, and I looked at the *Kestrel* riding at her mooring, and I thought about the name she carried—the name Wendell had chosen, or maybe the name that had chosen her, the name of the ship she'd sunk and the ship she'd become.

"Well, Captain?" I said. "Tide turns at sundown. We going to sit here all day?"

Wendell's breath came out of her like she'd been holding it for twelve years. She laughed—a rough, cracked, real laugh—and wiped her face with the back of her hand, and for the first time since I'd known her, she didn't look over her shoulder at the horizon like something might be coming for her.

"Landfall," she said. "First time in twelve years I've actually made it."

* * *

We sailed at slack water the next morning—not out to the flats, not to any wreck, just out past the breakwater and back, because Sefa wanted to feel the deck under her feet again and Wendell said the sea owed her a gentler conversation than their last one.

The hearing would come in seven days. The fleet's advocates were already sighting sail on the horizon, black hulls against the morning glare. There would be hard days ahead—worse than storms, maybe, because storms don't ask you to account for what you've done.

But as we came back through the harbor mouth with the sun full on the water, Wendell stood at the wheel with her crew around her and her name in her own mouth at last, and the town on the shore came out onto the docks to watch us in—not to point, not yet, just to see.

And high on the Saltstone Hall's tower, the harbor bell began to ring.

Not the alarm. The welcome.

Landfall, at last—and whatever came with the tide, we would meet it with the truth, and with each other.
