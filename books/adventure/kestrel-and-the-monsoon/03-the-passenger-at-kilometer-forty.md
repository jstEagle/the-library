## 3. The Passenger at Kilometer Forty

The river at kilometer forty smelled of iron and rotting melons. Sefa had been standing at the rail of the *Kestrel* for an hour, watching the banks slide by — mud, cane, a shrine to some river-god whose name the flood had eaten — and she was still not used to the way the water moved here. Not like the mountain streams of her childhood, which ran with the urgency of things escaping. This was a slow, muscular water, patient as a creditor. It carried everything and gave back nothing.

She had been aboard two days. Long enough to learn that the captain took their coffee black and unsweetened, that they slept four hours at a stretch like a cat, and that they lied the way other people breathed.

She had counted. Three times since Kilometer Twelve, when the patrol skiff had come alongside with its green-painted gunwales and its bored officer, Ivo Crane had opened the hold and shown the man a manifest that Sefa knew — because she had helped carry the crates it described — did not match what lay inside. The officer had read it, stamped it, wished the captain good water. And Ivo had stood there with that particular stillness they had, hands loose at their sides, face arranged into the mild blankness of a person with nothing to declare, and had not so much as blinked.

*Salted fish for the canneries at Vosse,* the manifest said. The crates held, among other things, a sealed lead-lined chest that Sefa would have recognized through three feet of oak, and a set of brass instruments wrapped in oilcloth, and — she was nearly certain — folios. She had seen the corner of one when the captain had gone into the hold at night and thought themself alone.

She had not been alone. She had been sitting in the dark behind the spare fuel drum with her knees drawn up, listening to the small sounds of a person deciding something. She had heard the pry-bar. She had heard the wax crack — a sound like a knuckle, like a promise breaking — and then a long, long silence, the kind of silence that has a person inside it.

Sefa knew that silence. She had made it herself, once, at fourteen, in her father's reading room in Vessia, the night before the men with the inventory lists came. Her father had put a letter in her hands and said, *If anything happens to me, this goes to the Custodian. Do you understand? The Custodian, and no one else.* She had understood the way children understand — completely, and not at all.

Now she stood at the rail watching the banks go by, and the coin in her pocket pressed against her hip like a small hot coal.

* * *

The girl came out of the reeds at Kilometer Forty.

Sefa saw her before the crew did — a smudge of movement in the green, then a figure standing on the mud shelf with a bundle on her shoulder and one arm raised, waving with the desperate, disciplined rhythm of someone who had learned that waving was a skill you could fail at. Not frantic. Measured. *Here. Here. Here.* Like a signaler.

Ivo saw her too. Sefa watched the captain's face do something complicated and quick — a flicker of assessment, a flicker of refusal, and then, arriving last, the thing that actually decided it: recognition. Not of the girl. Of the *gesture*. Ivo Crane had been that figure on a mud bank, once. You could see it land in them like a stone dropped down a well, taking a long time to hit bottom.

The *Kestrel* was a shallow-draught riverboat with a keel like a knife and an engine that Ivo coaxed rather than commanded, and she came in to the mud shelf the way a heron comes down — all economy, no splash. The girl waded out to meet them, water to her knees, and caught the rope they threw with both hands, and Sefa, watching from the rail, noted that she caught it *correctly*, wrapped and locked, the way you're taught when the rope is the difference between going home and going under.

She came up the ladder and stood dripping on the deck, and Sefa saw her whole self at once: early twenties, maybe, dark-haired, river-thin, with the particular posture of someone carrying something valuable in her bundle and something dangerous in her chest pocket. She had a transit pass held in her teeth while she climbed. When she took it out and offered it, her hand was steady.

Ivo read it under the awning light. The pass was good work — Sefa could see that from four feet away, and she had seen a great many passes in her life, good and otherwise. Correct stock. Correct stamps. The seal of the Interior Navigation Office reproduced with a care that suggested either a very talented forger or a very patient one.

But the date was wrong. Not obviously wrong — wrong the way a forged painting is wrong, in the grain of it. The Navigation Office had changed its date format eight months ago, and the girl's pass was in the old format, and whoever had made it was working from a template that was already a season dead.

Ivo saw it too. Sefa watched the captain's eyes flick to the date, flick to the girl's face, and then — and this was the part that interested her — flick *away*. Deliberately. The way you look away from a thing you've decided not to see.

"Pass is in order," Ivo said, and handed it back. "Where to?"

"Upstream. As far as you go."

"That's a long way."

"I know." The girl's voice was low and even. "I can pay."

She reached into her coat, and Sefa's whole body went tight, because she already knew — she knew with the sick, certain knowledge of someone watching a disaster they cannot speak about — what was going to come out of that coat.

The girl counted coins into Ivo's palm. Four of them. Old coins, heavy, struck in silver that had gone the color of smoke, each one stamped with a design Sefa had last seen on her grandmother's worktable: a kestrel with folded wings, and around it, in letters small enough to be missed by anyone not looking for them, the words of a language that officially did not exist.

*Vessian crowns. Minted before the Erasure. Coins that a girl of twenty-two should not have, could not have, unless someone had given them to her and made her promise.*

Ivo's hand closed over them. Sefa watched the captain's face and saw nothing — nothing at all, which was itself a performance of the highest order, because Ivo Crane had looked at that coin the way you look at a ghost. One heartbeat of pure animal recognition, and then the blankness came down like a shutter.

"Passage is eight," Ivo said. "You've overpaid."

"Keep it."

"I don't take—"

"Keep it." The girl said it quietly, and there was something under the quiet, some bedrock, and Ivo heard it and stopped arguing. "For the mud shelf. You didn't have to stop."

"River rule," Ivo said. "You stop for anyone waving from the water."

"That's not a rule."

"It is on my boat." The captain turned away, already moving, all business. "Bunk's aft, behind the engine room. It's warm there and it smells like oil. Meals at dawn and dusk. You don't come forward of the cargo hold without asking."

"I understand."

"And—" Ivo paused, half-turned. The awning light caught the coin still sitting in their open palm, and for a moment they just looked at it, and Sefa saw the captain's thumb move across the kestrel stamp, once, the way you touch a scar to check it's still there. "What's your name?"

"Mirin."

It was a lie. Sefa knew it was a lie the way she knew the pass was forged — not from evidence but from grain, from the small hesitation before the word, the way the girl's mouth had to go looking for it. *Mirin* was a name from nowhere, a coat you put on over the name you actually carried.

Ivo knew it too. Sefa would have bet the boat on it. And the captain looked at the girl for a long second, and then said, "Welcome aboard, Mirin," in a voice that made it clear that both of them knew, and that neither of them was going to say so, and that this — this mutual, silent, unremarked agreement to let a lie stand — was the oldest courtesy on the river.

* * *

Sefa found her that evening, aft, behind the engine room.

It wasn't an accident. She had spent the afternoon deciding whether to make it one, and had finally concluded that on a boat this small, with a captain who missed nothing, nothing was an accident. Better to choose her ground.

The girl was sitting on an overturned crate, her bundle in her lap, her hands on it the way you rest your hands on a sleeping animal. The engine room breathed heat through the bulkhead behind her, and the light from the passageway came in at a slant, and she looked up when Sefa's shadow crossed her, and her whole body went still — not afraid, exactly. *Ready.* There was a difference, and Sefa knew it intimately, because she had lived in that difference for six years.

"You're the other passenger," the girl said.

"I am."

"They said your name was Sefa."

"It is."

"That one's true, then." The girl's eyes were quick and dark and did not blink enough. "Mine isn't."

"No," Sefa said. "It isn't."

The girl's hands tightened on the bundle. For a moment Sefa thought she'd overplayed it, that the girl would bolt or worse — there was a knife, she'd seen the outline of it under the coat, everyone on this river carried a knife — but then something in the girl's face shifted, some weight finding a new balance, and she said, "Who are you? What are you to the captain?"

"A passenger. Like you."

"Passengers don't watch the hold at night."

The words dropped between them like a stone into still water, and Sefa felt the ripples go out and come back. So the girl had seen her too. Of course she had. Two watchers on a small boat, each certain of their own invisibility, each catalogued by the other. She almost laughed.

"You were in the hold," Sefa said. "The first night. Behind the fuel drum."

"I was in the hold," the girl agreed, "the first night. Behind the fuel drum." She said it without apology, without defiance — a simple exchange of facts between professionals. "You heard me open something."

"I heard you open something."

"And you've been watching the captain lie to patrol officers for two days, and you haven't reported it, which means you're not with the patrols."

"Neither are you."

"Neither am I," the girl said, "with anyone." And then, after a silence in which the engine ticked and the river muttered against the hull: "You know what the captain's carrying."

It was not a question. Sefa looked at her — at the steady dark eyes, the bundle held like a sleeping animal, the forged pass folded away in a coat that also held a knife — and understood that she was looking at a mirror with a few years' difference in the glass. A keeper of false papers. A carrier of true things. A person who had been given something by someone who was probably dead now, and who had been walking upriver ever since, looking for a door.

"I know what they're carrying," Sefa said carefully, "the way you know what's in a wrapped parcel. By the shape of it."

"The shape of it is a nation," the girl said.

The engine room ticked. Somewhere forward, Ivo's boots crossed the deck, unhurried, and both of them went silent until the boots had passed — a shared, instinctive discipline, the reflex of people who had grown up listening for footsteps, and Sefa saw the girl register the synchrony of it, their two silences falling into step, and something in her expression loosened by a single degree.

"My grandmother minted those coins," Sefa said.

The girl went very still.

"The kestrel with folded wings. The lettering around the rim. That was her die-work — she cut the stamps herself, in a workshop in the old mint quarter, the last year. She made three hundred and kept them in a flour tin, because she said a country isn't its army or its borders, it's the small heavy things people can hold in a closed hand and remember." Sefa heard her own voice and was surprised by it — low, and rough at the edges. She had not said her grandmother's work aloud to anyone in six years. "So when I saw you pay with four of them, I knew two things. I knew where they came from. And I knew that whoever gave them to you trusted you with something that couldn't be replaced."

The girl looked at her for a long time. The heat from the engine room moved the air faintly, a warm breath against their faces.

"They were my mother's," she said at last. "She carried them sewn into a coat lining through two checkpoints and a winter. She gave me four and kept the rest, and then she—" The girl's jaw moved. "Then she didn't keep them anymore. Or anything else."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Be careful." The girl's voice had gone hard and quick. "You and the captain, you're both lying your way up this river like it's a game, like the papers are the trick and the trick is enough. It isn't enough. There are men who buy rivers. My mother told me — she said the worst ones don't come with soldiers, they come with contracts, and by the time you understand what they're buying, you've already signed. Whatever the captain pulled out of that sandbar, whatever's in the hold — if it's what I think it is, then it's not cargo. It's not salvage. It's a *person's whole life*, a country's whole life, packed in a box by someone who died getting it ready. And it is not the captain's. And it is not yours."

"No," Sefa said quietly. "It isn't."

The girl blinked. Sefa watched her recalibrate — she had come armed for a fight, for the possessive argument, for the *finders-keepers* of river law, and Sefa had simply agreed with her, and the agreement had taken the ground out from under the fight.

"It's mine," Sefa said. "Or it was. Or it was my father's, and he gave it to a man named Crane to carry, and the man named Crane died, and now his child is running it up a river in a boat full of lies, and I have spent six years looking for it." She held the girl's eyes. "So you can stop watching me, *Mirin*. I'm not a rival. I'm the reason it exists."

* * *

Later — much later, past midnight, when the river had gone silver-black and the banks were only a sound — Sefa stood at the stern rail and watched the wake unspool behind them, and Ivo came to stand beside her.

They did this sometimes. It was the closest thing to conversation the captain allowed: two people at a rail in the dark, looking the same direction, saying nothing or almost nothing. Sefa had learned to read the silences. This one had a question in it.

"Your friend behind the engine room," Ivo said at last, not looking at her.

"What about her?"

"That coin she paid with."

"What about it?"

A long pause. The wheel ticked under the autopilot's lazy corrections. Out in the dark, something large turned over in the water and sank again.

"I've seen that stamp before," Ivo said. "On the chest. In the hold." The captain's voice was flat and careful, each word set down like a foot on river ice. "The seal of Vessia. The kestrel with folded wings. And now it turns up in the pocket of a girl with a forged pass who pays in a dead nation's silver and lies about her name, on the same boat, the same week, heading the same direction." Ivo finally turned, and in the low light of the binnacle their eyes were very steady and very tired. "I don't believe in coincidences that well-made. So I'm going to ask you once, and you can lie to me if you want — you're good at it, I've watched you — but know that I'll know."

The river ran. The wake unspooled. Sefa thought of her father's hands on the letter, of the flour tin of coins, of the girl asleep behind the engine room with a knife and a dead mother's silver, of the chest in the hold with its lead lining and its wax and its letter addressed to a name Ivo Crane had grown up hating.

"The chest," Sefa said, "was your father's. But it was never his. And the girl knows what's in it. And so do I."

Ivo Crane looked at her for a long, long moment. Then the captain reached into a coat pocket and set something on the rail between them: one of the girl's four coins, the kestrel catching the binnacle light, wings folded.

"Then tomorrow," Ivo said quietly, "the three of us are going to have a conversation. And this time," and here something moved through the captain's voice that Sefa had not heard in two days aboard — not anger, not fear, but a kind of grim, exhausted relief, like a man setting down a load he'd forgotten he was carrying — "this time, nobody lies."

Behind them, from the direction of the engine room, a floorboard creaked. The girl had been listening at the bulkhead after all. She stepped out into the binnacle light with her bundle in her arms and her forged pass in her pocket, and she said, "No. Tonight."
