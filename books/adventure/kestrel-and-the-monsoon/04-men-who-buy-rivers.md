## 4. Men Who Buy Rivers

The lock at Harrow Bend announced itself an hour before Ivo saw it, the way locks always did: a smell of creosote and wet rope on the wind, then the long gray line of the toll-house chimney standing up out of the willows like a finger raised in warning. Ivo had shot this stretch of the Serein maybe two hundred times in twenty years, and every time the chimney came into view, something in their chest tightened and then let go — the small ritual dread of a person who lived by not being noticed.

Today it did not let go.

They told themselves it was the chest. It sat below the floorboards of the hold, under a false layer of bilge planking Ivo had laid down themselves, under three sacks of river gravel for weight and a coil of fouled anchor chain nobody would want to steal. A lead-lined box stamped with a seal from a country that no longer existed, riding in the belly of a boat that officially belonged to a shipping concern registered in a jurisdiction Ivo had chosen precisely because its paperwork was fiction. There was no reason on earth for anyone at Harrow Bend to look twice.

But the chimney kept standing there. And the river kept carrying them toward it.

"Sefa." They said it without turning from the wheel. "We'll be through the lock inside the hour. When we tie up, you stay aboard."

From the stern came the sound of the girl doing something with her hands — she was always doing something with her hands, mending net, re-wrapping the handle of her one bag with cord, as if preparing to run at any moment, which Ivo supposed she was. "Why?"

"Because the lockmaster's clerks count heads, and heads mean transit fees, and fees mean questions."

"You lied to me about the cargo manifest at Kilometer Forty," Sefa said. Not accusing. Observing, the way she observed everything, with that flat dark patience that made Ivo feel like a specimen pinned under glass. "You can lie to them too."

"I lie better when I'm alone."

"No," she said. "You don't. You lie worse when you're frightened, and you've been frightened since the sandbar."

Ivo didn't answer, because there was nothing true to say that wasn't also dangerous. The kestrel-boat slid on through the morning haze, past fisherfolk mending set-nets, past a drowned orchard whose dead trees stood in the shallows like a congregation waiting for a sermon that had been postponed for a century. The current here ran slow and brown-green, fat with the low season, and the boat moved through it the way Ivo liked best: quietly, unremarkably, one more hull among hundreds.

Invisibility was a craft, like any other. You learned it young or you didn't learn it at all. Ivo had learned it at fourteen, the year the name Crane became something to be sanded off every document, every hull registration, every tavern conversation. *Crane* — the smuggler's name, the father who'd vanished up some mountain road with boxes nobody asked about, leaving behind a child and a reputation like an oil slick. Ivo had spent two decades becoming nobody in particular: a competent captain, a fair hand at salvage, a face that lockmasters forgot before the gates closed. It was work. It was the only work Ivo had ever done well.

And now there was a lead-lined chest in the hold with their father's seal on it, humming like a struck bell that only certain people could hear.

* * *

Harrow Bend itself was nothing — a kink in the river where the Serein slowed to fill a stone-banked basin, twin gates of black timber, a toll-house of yellow brick going soft at the corners, a quay where three barges waited their turn behind a grain carrier from downriver. The air smelled of tar and cut hay and the particular sourness of lock-water. Gulls argued over something on the upstream gate.

Ivo brought the kestrel-boat in against the current, gentle as setting down a sleeping child, and made fast at the outer bollard to wait their turn. The lockmaster's clerk — a boy with ink-stained fingers and a ledger chained to his desk — came down the quay with his fee-book open, and Ivo paid him in small coin and answered his small questions with the smoothness of long practice. Cargo? General freight, some salvage. Origin? Downriver trading stations. Destination? Upriver, the foothill markets. All of it true enough to survive a glance and false enough to survive a search.

The boy wrote it all down without looking up. Ivo felt the familiar loosening in the shoulders. Through the gates, upriver, gone. Another day of being no one.

That was when the man stepped off the grain carrier's gangway.

He was dressed wrong for the river — that was the first thing. A long coat of good gray wool, city-cut, buttoned to the throat despite the mild morning; polished boots that had never known mud; a hat with a brim narrow enough to be fashionable and wide enough to shade a careful face. He was perhaps fifty, silver at the temples, with the kind of pleasant, unlined features that seemed designed to be forgotten. He crossed the quay at an unhurried walk, and Ivo watched him come the way they'd watch a floating log in a channel — assessing drift, mass, and what it would do to the hull if it connected.

"Captain." The man stopped a polite distance off. His voice was soft, cultured, pitched to carry no farther than the space between them. "Might I impose on a few minutes of your time?"

"Freight rates are posted on the board," Ivo said. "I don't do passengers."

"I'm not selling passage. I'm buying consultation." He produced a card from an inner pocket with two fingers, the way a magician produces a coin, and held it out. Heavy stock. Cream-colored. Embossed at the center with a device Ivo knew without wanting to: a meridian line crossing a globe, quartered like a pie being divided.

**ALDOUS VANE. ACQUISITIONS. THE MERIDIAN COMPACT.**

Below it, in smaller type: *Estates, Archives, Antiquities, Rights.*

"We've heard good things about your salvage work, Captain," Vane said. "Particularly your eye for unusual recoveries."

"There's nothing unusual on my boat."

"I'm sure there isn't." He smiled. It was a good smile, warm at the edges, practiced at the center. "May I speak plainly? I represent collectors. Serious ones. Institutions, families, foundations — people who care deeply about the material record of places that have... changed hands, historically. Places whose records have become, shall we say, orphaned. Our work is preservation, Captain. Custody. We ensure that the pasts of small nations don't simply rot in attics or drown in rivers. We give them a home."

"A home," Ivo repeated.

"A vault," Vane agreed pleasantly. "Climate-controlled. Catalogued. Accessible to accredited scholars, upon application." He let that last phrase sit in the air between them like a hook in clear water, gleaming and patient. "Now. To business. The Compact is prepared to offer triple freight rates — triple, Captain, plus a finder's premium — for salvage consulting services. One consultation. An hour of your time, perhaps less."

"I don't know anything worth consulting about."

"I think you may." Vane's eyes — pale, quick, and utterly without warmth beneath all that courtesy — moved once, briefly, toward the kestrel-boat's waterline. Toward the hold. Then back. "Registry number 4471-Vessia. Lead-lined transport chest, custodial seal, contents catalogued at time of sealing as folios, instruments, correspondence. Lost with the river traffic out of Vessia during the evacuation year. The Compact has been looking for it for a very long time."

The world went very quiet and very loud at once. Somewhere behind Ivo, a gull screamed. The lock gates groaned as the grain carrier began its lift, water churning white in the chamber. And Ivo stood on their own deck with their hands loose at their sides and their heartbeat slamming against the base of the throat, and did what twenty years of practice had trained them to do.

Nothing. Absolutely nothing. No flicker, no flinch, no glance at the hold. Just a slow blink and a shrug that came from somewhere deep and rehearsed.

"Number means nothing to me," Ivo said. "I pull drowned trees and lost anchors out of the shallows. If someone lost a fancy box, it wasn't me found it."

"Mm." Vane studied them for a moment, head tilted, like a man listening to an engine for a specific rattle. "You know, Captain, most people, when I mention a registry number like that, go pale. Or they laugh too loudly. Or they look at their own boat." He smiled again, gentler now, almost kind. "You went still. That's rarer. That's actually rather impressive."

"Stillness comes free on a slow morning."

"It does." Vane reached into his coat again, and Ivo's hand drifted — casually, they hoped — toward the boathook leaning against the rail. But the man only drew out a folded paper, cream to match the card, and laid it on the capstan head between them. Unfolded, it showed figures. Generous figures. Absurd figures, for a river captain. Triple freight, plus premium, plus a retainer that would have bought the kestrel-boat outright and a new one besides. "The offer stands for as long as you find it useful. No pressure. No urgency. The Compact is patient — it's rather our defining virtue. Rivers change course, Captain. Sandbars shift. Things surface." He tapped the paper once with one polished fingertip. "Things always surface."

"I'll leave it for the gulls."

"Of course." Vane stepped back, unhurried, and settled his hat more firmly on his head. "One more thing, purely professional courtesy. The man who sealed that chest — the last custodian — he had children, I believe. The Compact's records are imperfect on the point. Families scatter, names change." Those pale eyes rested on Ivo's face with the lightest possible touch, a fingertip laid on a bruise. "It would be a kindness, in my experience, if any such descendants understood that the Compact bears them no ill will whatsoever. We're not thieves, Captain. We're librarians. And librarians only ever want one thing."

"The book," Ivo said.

"The book," Vane agreed. "Good morning, Captain. Safe passage upriver. The water's low above the Bend — mind the middle channel, it's lying about its depth this season."

He walked away along the quay, unhurried, boots ringing softly on the stones, and paused once to exchange a word with the lockmaster's clerk, who laughed at something and touched his cap. Ivo watched him go and understood, with a cold settling certainty, that the clerk would remember the laughing stranger fondly, and that the fee-ledger entry for a kestrel-boat named after a small falcon was already copied into somebody else's book somewhere.

* * *

The lock took them up forty minutes later. Ivo worked the gates and the paddles with hands that knew the motions so well they could have done it blind, while the rest of them stood somewhere outside their body watching a gray-coated man's back diminish down the quay.

Sefa appeared at the hold hatch as the upper gate swung wide. She'd heard. Of course she'd heard — sound carried across lock-water like rumor carried through a village. She didn't say anything at first. She just looked at Ivo with those flat dark eyes, and then at the deck boards under which everything they were both running from lay hidden, and then back.

"He knew the registry number," she said.

"Yes."

"That's a catalogue number. Catalogues live in archives." Her voice stayed level, but her hands had gone still on the hatch coaming, and Ivo had learned in the past days that Sefa's stillness was worse than other people's shouting. "Which means somewhere there's a record of that chest — sealed, listed, valued. Which means whoever sealed it reported it. Reported it to someone who kept books."

"Our fathers kept books," Ivo said quietly. "That was rather the whole trouble with them."

The current took the boat through the gate and out into the wider river above the Bend, and Ivo set the wheel over for the middle channel — the one Vane had warned them about, which meant it was exactly where Ivo intended to be, because a man like that did not give navigation advice out of kindness. He gave it to be quoted back. To be remembered. *He helped me,* Ivo would think, next time doubt crept in. *Maybe he's decent.* The offer on the capstan had been left behind, but Ivo had taken it anyway, without meaning to — had read every figure twice, had done the arithmetic in their head before hating themselves for it. That was how men like Vane worked. They didn't buy chests. They bought the small traitorous arithmetic inside people.

Triple freight. A clean name. A life where the Chimney at Harrow Bend was just a chimney.

"Captain." Sefa's voice pulled them back. She was crouched at the rail now, watching the banks slide past — willow, hayfield, a church spire doing its slow vanishing act behind the trees. "That man. He'll make another offer. And then another. And each one will be easier to hear than the last. My mother used to say the Compact doesn't chase people. It waits for them to arrive."

"Your mother sounds like she'd have made a fine captain."

"She'd have made a fine prisoner, which is nearly the same thing." Sefa turned, and for the first time since Kilometer Forty, Ivo saw something in her face that wasn't wariness or grief. It was fear, plain and shared, offered across the deck like a rope thrown to a drowning man. "So. What do we do, Captain? Because I've been running since I was twelve years old, and I know how that ends. It ends tired, in a place with no name, holding nothing."

Ivo looked upriver. The Serein ran on ahead, low and brown and full of secrets, bending away north toward hills they could just see, blue-gray, at the edge of the haze. Somewhere up there was a pass through mountains that opened three weeks a year. Somewhere up there was whatever their father had been carrying besides boxes.

For twenty years, invisibility had been the whole of the plan. Be no one. Go nowhere memorable. Keep the name sanded off. And it had worked, right up until a sandbar that appeared on no chart had handed them a bell that wouldn't stop ringing.

"We do what the river does," Ivo said finally. "When it can't go around a thing, it goes through."

"And if the thing is a mountain?"

"Then we learn mountain."

Sefa almost smiled. Almost. It was gone before it landed, replaced by that flat watchfulness, but Ivo had seen it, and seeing it did something strange to the tightness behind their ribs.

They ran on through the afternoon, past the last of the toll-locks, into water no ledger had ever bothered to chart properly. Behind them, Harrow Bend shrank to a smudge of chimney smoke. Ahead, the hills grew.

And somewhere back downriver — Ivo could feel it the way you feel weather coming in your knee, the way you feel a following sea before it rounds the point — a patient man in a gray coat was writing their description into a book, and the book was already traveling faster than any boat could run.

Invisibility, Ivo thought, steering into the long gold light of evening, was a craft like any other.

And theirs had just been sunk.
