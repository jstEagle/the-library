## 6. Night Run Through the Reeds

Ivo heard the launches before they saw them, which was the only reason either of them was still alive to hear anything at all.

It was the silence that gave them away. The Serein at night had its own grammar — the slap of water against hull, the complaint of mooring lines, frogs in the shallows keeping their ancient ledgers — and into that grammar the Compact's boats inserted a wrongness Ivo felt in their teeth before they understood it with their ears. A wake where there should have been none. A pressure in the dark, like something swimming against the current without disturbing it.

They were moored in the willow shade below Kilometer Sixty-Three, a bend Ivo had used a dozen times over the years because the overhanging trees broke the silhouette of the wheelhouse and the current here ran slow and deep enough to hold a boat quiet. Sefa was below, sleeping in the hold among the crates, or pretending to. Ivo was on the roof with a tin cup of cold tea, doing the arithmetic of the river the way they did every night — how far to the next fuel, how long the ice on the lock fees would hold, how many days until the season turned and turned them with it.

Then the arithmetic changed. Two wakes, parallel, coming up the back-channel behind the long island where no freight boat had any business being. Running without lights. Running quiet.

Ivo set the cup down without a sound and went down the ladder on the balls of their feet.

"Sefa." Not a whisper exactly — a shape of a word, breath given edges. "Wake up. Don't strike a light."

She came up out of the hold the way she'd come aboard at Kilometer Forty: all at once, already awake, already deciding. In the dark her eyes found Ivo's and held there, asking nothing, which Ivo was beginning to understand was her whole character in a single gesture. A girl who'd grown up with false papers in the house learned not to waste questions on the obvious.

"Two launches," Ivo breathed. "Back-channel, behind the island. Quiet engines. They're sweeping."

"Sweeping for us."

"Sweeping for anything that isn't theirs." Ivo was already moving, pulling the canvas off the wheel, checking the bilge by feel. "They'll run the channel to the bend, then split. One goes up the main stem, one comes down the slough on the far side of the reeds. We're in the pocket."

"The pocket."

"The pocket." Ivo almost smiled, and the smile tasted strange in their mouth, like a coin found in old clothing. "There's a way through. There's always a way through. That's the whole religion of this river."

* * *

The religion had a liturgy, and Ivo had been saying it alone for twenty years.

They cast off without engine, letting the current take the kestrel-boat's stern first, walking her backward out of the willow shade on the river's own slow hand. Ivo stood at the wheel they weren't using, reading the dark the way other people read print. The reeds along the slough made a sound like rain that wasn't falling — millions of dry leaves whispering to each other — and beneath that whisper, faint as a lie told well, the Compact launches were still working their way up the back-channel, patient as bailiffs.

"There." Sefa was at the stern rail, pointing with her chin. "The light."

Ivo had seen it already. A green eye, low, sweeping the water beyond the island's tail — a search hooded down to a slit, the kind of light meant to find rather than to blind. Beyond it, briefly, the black shoulder of the nearer launch, low and long, a knife laid flat on the water.

"They'll have the channel mouth in four minutes," Ivo said. "Maybe five. Then they'll see this bend is empty and start wondering where the empty is."

"So we go through the reeds."

"So we go through the reeds." Ivo said it the way a person admits to something. "There's a cut. My father found it. Or made it. He never said which, and I never asked, because asking questions about my father's river was how you got silence for an answer." They could feel Sefa's attention on their back like a hand between the shoulder blades. "It runs behind the reed islands for two kilometers. Six feet wide in the wet season. In this water — low water, the lowest October in nine years — it'll be four. Maybe three."

"And your boat?"

"Is thirty feet of oak with a six-foot beam." Ivo took the wheel anyway, out of habit, out of need to hold something. "So we're going to find out what she's made of. And so are we."

Sefa didn't argue. That was the thing about her that unsettled Ivo most, more than the Vessian coin, more than the folios she'd read in the hold with her lips moving like a woman at prayer: she didn't argue. She assessed, and then she committed, and the committing had a finality to it that made Ivo's own hesitations look like what they were — the flinching of a person who had spent a lifetime keeping every door within kicking distance.

"Tell me what to do," she said.

* * *

What Ivo told her to do was to take the pole.

They had a barge pole aboard, sixteen feet of ash, meant for shoving off from locks. Ivo showed her the stern, showed her the reeds, showed her the dark water between the dark water — the difference visible only as a texture, a smoothness where the wind couldn't reach, a blackness that was blacker because it was deeper.

"You'll feel her answer through the pole before you see anything," Ivo said. "Mud talks. Sand hisses. If the pole goes down soft and comes up soft, we're fine. If it comes up with weed, we're over the channel edge. If it comes up with a sound like a knuckle cracking — that's clay, and we're about to be a monument."

"And if we go aground?"

"Then we go aground quietly, and I get out and pull, and we hate the river together." Ivo looked at her. In the starlight her face was all planes and patience. "I've run this cut twice. Once at dusk. Once at night, and the night was kinder, because at night you can't see how little water there is."

Sefa took the pole the way she took everything, with both hands and no complaint, and Ivo opened the throttle to the smallest whisper the old engine would give — a sound like a man clearing his throat in another room — and turned the kestrel-boat's bow toward the reeds.

The reeds closed over them.

It was like being swallowed by a field. The stalks stood eight feet high, dry and rattling, taller than the wheelhouse roof, and the boat slid into them the way a needle slides into cloth. Ivo ran her at a walking pace, no more, steering by the feel in the wheel and the sound of Sefa's pole — the soft *shk* of ash in mud, the soft *shk*, a heartbeat made of wood and water. The channel, when it existed, existed as a rumor. Twice the reeds scraped the hull with a sound like whispering crowds. Once the pole came up with weed, and Sefa said nothing, and Ivo corrected two degrees to starboard, and the weed did not come again.

Behind them, faint through eight feet of reed, they heard the Compact launch pass the bend. Its quiet engine made a sound like a hive dreaming. The searchlight's glow bled green through the reed stalks, moving, moving — a slow green tide washing over the boat from astern — and Ivo stood at the wheel with their heart going like a bilge pump and did not breathe, did not blink, while the light slid across the canvas, across the wheelhouse, across Sefa's upturned face, and passed on.

The launch's wake arrived a minute later, a small shudder through the reeds, and then the hive-dream sound faded up the main stem, and they were alone with the frogs and the dry whisper of ten thousand acres of reed, and Ivo discovered that exhilaration and terror, at sufficient concentration, are the same substance.

Sefa started to laugh. It was a small sound, strangled, helpless, and it was the first time Ivo had heard her do it, and it did something to the air in the wheelhouse that Ivo didn't have a name for.

"You're laughing."

"You're grinning," she said. "I can hear it."

"I am not."

"You are. It sounds like a door creaking."

Ivo found that they were, in fact, grinning, and that the grin had been on their face since the light had passed, and that it would not come off. "Hold your course, archivist."

"Hold your *shk*," Sefa said, and laughed again, and the pole went down into the mud, and the mud answered soft, and they went on.

* * *

The river, of course, kept accounts.

They were two kilometers into the cut, deep in the reed maze where the channels braided and forgot themselves, when the pole came up with a sound like a knuckle cracking.

Clay.

Sefa didn't say anything. She just looked at Ivo, and Ivo was already reversing, already swinging the wheel, but the kestrel-boat had a six-foot beam and the cut had three feet of water and the boat's stern, swinging wide, kissed the reed bank with a softness that would have meant nothing anywhere else on the river and here meant everything — because the bank wasn't bank, it was the lip of the old levee, and the levee had a stump in it, and the stump was exactly the height of a hull's turn of the bilge.

The sound was small. A tearing, a giving, like heavy cloth giving way under a patient hand. Then water where water should not have been, and the particular wrongness of a boat's motion when she has begun to drink.

"Forward," Ivo said. The word came out flat and cold, all the exhilaration gone out of it, replaced by the older and colder thing, the thing that had kept them alive through twenty years of low water and high debts. "Forward, hard, sixty meters — there's a cattle ford past the dead willow, gravel bottom, we can put her nose on it."

Sefa drove the pole like a woman who had been poling boats all her life, which Ivo would later learn was nearly true. The engine whispered its throat-clearing whisper. The kestrel-boat dragged herself through the last of the reeds with water climbing in her bilge, and Ivo ran the pump and steered and listened to the sound of their boat dying by inches, and the dead willow came up out of the dark like a hand raised in oath, and beyond it the gravel shoal gleamed faint under the stars.

They put her nose on the gravel with a grinding gentleness, and Ivo went over the side into water to their knees, cold as a held breath, with a lantern hooded to a coin of light, and found the wound.

It was not large. That was the mercy. A gash in the strake aft of the turn of the bilge, a foot long, laid open where the stump had caught her — planking sprung, a seam opened like a split lip. In deep water it would have drowned her in an hour. On the gravel, with the pump keeping pace, she would hold until morning.

Ivo knelt in the cold current with their hand on the wound and did not say anything for a long time.

Sefa stood in the stern, pole still in her hands, water dripping off the blade. "How bad?"

"She'll live." Ivo's voice came out strange to them, scraped. "She needs a shipwright. A real one. Caulk, a new strake section, the seam re-laid. That's not a riverbank job."

"Where?"

Ivo looked upriver, downriver. The reeds went on forever in both directions, whispering their dry gossip. Somewhere behind them the Compact's launches were sweeping the channels with their dreaming engines, patient as interest accruing. Somewhere ahead, at Harrow Bend and every lock between here and the hills, the Compact was buying the river lock by lock, name by name, the way it bought everything — quietly, courteously, completely.

"Ossory Quay," Ivo said finally. "Haul-out yard, forty kilometers up. Old man Ferro. He owes my father a favor." They heard themselves say it and flinched. Everything now led back to the father — the cut, the quay, the chest, the debt. The man had been dead nine years and was still steering the boat.

"Then we go to Ossory Quay."

"We go to Ossory Quay." Ivo climbed back aboard, wringing the river out of their sleeves, and stood a moment in the wheelhouse looking at the dark. "Sefa. I need you to understand something. That cut — the reed run — that was the last of it. The last place on this river where nobody could find us. I've kept it like a coin in a boot for twenty years. Never marked it. Never told a soul." They turned to look at her, and the grin from the reed run was long gone, and what was underneath it was older and barer. "We just spent it. And they're still back there, and they're patient, and they have money. There is no downriver left to hide in. There's no *anywhere* left to hide in. From here on, we're not running away from them."

Sefa set the pole down and looked at Ivo with those unasking eyes. "Then what are we doing?"

Ivo looked upriver, into the black where the hills would be, where the Karst would be, where the chest in the hold was pulling them like a tide.

"We're going to Ossory Quay," they said. "And then we're going to find out what my father was carrying over that mountain — because whatever it is, it's the only map we've got left."

Sefa was quiet a moment. Then she said, "I already read some of it. The folios. Before you caught me." She didn't apologize. She never apologized; Ivo was beginning to suspect she'd been raised in a house where apology was a currency too dear to spend. "There are charts, Ivo. Hand-drawn. Of the Karst pass."

The frogs kept their ledger. The reeds whispered. Somewhere far behind them, a quiet engine dreamed its way up the main stem, hunting an empty bend.

Ivo put their hand on the wheel, on the boat, on the whole small wounded world of it, and said, "Then tomorrow we start for the hills."
