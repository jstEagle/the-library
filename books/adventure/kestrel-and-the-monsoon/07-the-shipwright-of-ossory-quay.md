## 7. The Shipwright of Ossory Quay

The gash in the kestrel-boat's hull was the length of a forearm and honest about it — a torn lip of planking curled back like something flayed, reeds still wedged in the wound from the night run through the shallows. Ossory Quay smelled of tar and old rope and river-mud drying in the sun, and when the travel-lift swung the boat out of the water, Sefa stood on the quay with her arms crossed and watched the river drip off the hull in long bright threads, as if the Serein were reluctant to let them go.

She had been aboard four days now. Long enough to learn that Ivo Crane slept in shifts even at anchor, one ear always cocked toward the channel; long enough to know the boat's small economies — the kettle set on the same burner-ring scar, the chart rolled and rubber-banded with a precision that bordered on ritual. Long enough to have read three more folios by lamplight while Ivo pretended to sleep above her, and to understand that pretending was a language they both spoke fluently.

"Yard'll want to know what happened," Ivo said, watching the slings take the weight.

"Then we tell them reed-wreck. Night running, low water, bad luck." She shrugged. "It's even true."

"It's true the way a coin is true." But Ivo didn't argue it, and she noted that too: a captain who lied well knew when a lie would hold its own weight.

Ossory Quay was a working yard, not a pretty one — a straggle of sheds along a mudflat reach where the river widened and went slow, half a dozen hulls hauled out on cradles, sawdust drifting like weather. The man who came down the ramp to meet them was broad as a bollard, sixty-odd, forearms scarred white with old caulking burns, wearing an apron gone stiff with years of pitch. He looked at the boat first and the people second, which Sefa decided immediately was the correct order of things.

"Kestrel," he said, reading the transom. Then he looked up, and his eyes went past Ivo and settled somewhere in the middle distance, doing arithmetic. "Crane's girl or Crane's boy? Doesn't matter. You've got the way of standing."

"I'm neither," Ivo said carefully. "I'm Crane."

"Mm." The shipwright — Tam Voss, the sign over his shed said, though everyone on the quay called him Old Tam — walked the length of the hull without touching it, the way a doctor reads a body. At the gash he crouched, pressed the torn planking flat with one thumb, and made a sound low in his chest that might have been sympathy or contempt. "Reed-wreck."

"Night running," Ivo said. "Low water."

"Low water." Tam straightened up and looked at Ivo for a long moment, and whatever passed between them was conducted entirely in silence, the way old debts are acknowledged. "Bring her round the side shed. I've got a bay free till Thursday. After that I don't want her sitting in my yard looking like what she is."

"And what is she?" Sefa asked.

Tam's gaze flicked to her — took in the foreign cut of her coat, the coin she wore on a cord at her throat because she'd never learned anywhere safe to keep valuables except against her own skin — and then away again, quick as a man touching a hot stove. "Trouble," he said. "Come inside. There's tea, if you don't mind it stewed."

* * *

The shed smelled of oakum and linseed and forty years of other boats. Tam poured tea into tin mugs without asking how anyone took it, which told Sefa he'd been pouring tea for desperate people a long time; desperate people drink what they're given. He set a stub of pencil behind his ear, dragged a tarpaulin-covered crate across from the wall, and sat them down on it like children called before a schoolmaster.

"Your father," he said to Ivo, "carried boxes over the Karst every summer for eleven years. Did you know that?"

Sefa watched Ivo's face do something complicated and quick — a shutter going halfway down and jamming there. "He ran freight," Ivo said. "Timber, salt, machine parts. Ordinary freight."

"He ran ordinary freight downriver so nobody'd ask about the extraordinary freight upriver." Tam tapped the pencil against his knee. "Every July, after high water, he'd come up this reach with his hold full of crates stenciled as agricultural equipment. Agricultural equipment doesn't need dry straw packed around it. Agricultural equipment doesn't get carried by mule-train over a glacier pass. And agricultural equipment doesn't make a grown shipwright swear an oath on his mother's grave not to talk about it."

"You swore an oath," Sefa said quietly, "and you're talking about it."

"I swore I wouldn't talk while the old man lived, or while his cargo was in danger of being *found*." Tam drank his stewed tea and grimaced at it with something like affection. "His cargo's already found, isn't it? River gave it up. So the oath's dead, and dead oaths are just stories." He leaned forward, elbows on knees. "You want to know what I know, or you want to keep your story tidy?"

Ivo said nothing for the space of three breaths. Sefa had learned to read those silences. This one wasn't refusal. It was grief arriving late to a door it should have knocked on twenty years ago.

"Tell us," Ivo said finally.

* * *

What Tam Voss told them, in a voice like gravel poured slowly, took the rest of the afternoon and rearranged everything Sefa thought she understood about the chest in the hold.

The elder Crane — Maren Crane, though Tam only ever called him *the captain*, as if there were no other — had come to Ossory Quay first in the spring of a year Sefa could date precisely, because Tam dated everything by floods. The year the millrace drowned. A lean man with river-gray eyes and hands that were wrong for a boatman, too fine-knuckled, a scholar's hands pretending. He'd asked Tam not for repairs but for a favor shaped like a repair: build me two crates, false-bottomed, that can pass a customs glance at ten paces and carry paper without crushing it.

"I built them," Tam said. "And then I asked him what was in them, because I was young and stupid and curious, and he told me the truth, which is the last thing I expected. He said: these are books from a country that's being sold. Not conquered — sold. He said there were men who bought nations the way you'd buy a warehouse lot, and when they finished buying, the nation stopped having ever existed. Maps redrawn. Names struck. Graves re-labeled. He said somebody had to be the country's pockets until it could afford itself again."

Sefa's hand had gone to the coin at her throat without her deciding anything. Her grandmother had minted it — no, that wasn't right either; her grandmother had *saved* it, from the last mint run in Vessia before the Compact's appraisers arrived with their ledgers and their scales and their polite, annihilating questions. Her father had carried folios out of the archive in a fishmonger's cart. Her mother had died carrying nothing at all, which Sefa had always privately considered the greater heroism. She had spent her whole life believing the archive's survival was her family's work alone — her father's cart, her own forged passes, the coin warm against her sternum like a second heart. It had not once occurred to her that the work had *hands*. That strangers had carried it. That a riverboat captain with a scholar's hands had hauled it over a glacier every summer for eleven years, in crates marked for agriculture, and told no one, and asked no credit, and raised a child who grew up ashamed of him.

She looked at Ivo across the shed. Ivo was staring at the floorboards as if they were water and something was drowning in them.

"Eleven summers," Tam said. "He'd load at my slip after dark. My wife used to leave bread out for the mule-men — Vessian mule-men, some of them, men who'd come down off the Karst generations ago and still sang the old counting-songs when they loaded. Dalla's family, some of them. You'll meet Dalla eventually, if you're fool enough to go up there." He paused. "You're fool enough. I can see it."

"The route-notes," Sefa said. "In the chest. Charts of the pass."

"Charts he drew himself, year by year, because the pass moves. Ice moves. What was a tunnel the summer I was thirty was a lake the summer I was thirty-five." Tam turned the pencil over in his fingers. "He kept asking me, near the end — last time I saw him, must be fifteen years back now — whether I remembered the name of the porter who'd guided the first crossing. Old man, half-blind, dead since. Because the notes weren't complete without the names. He said an archive without its people is just a pile of paper. He said—" and here Tam's voice did something rough, "—he said, *a custodian who keeps the books and loses the readers has failed twice.*"

The shed was very quiet. Outside, a gull argued with another gull about something neither would win. Sawdust drifted through the light from the doorway in slow golden diagonals.

"I sold his notes," Ivo said. It came out flat, pre-confessed, a thing said to the floor rather than to the room. "When I was seventeen. To a broker in Harrow Bend. For enough to buy my first boat."

Sefa had known this — had pieced it together from the shape of Ivo's silences, the way Ivo flinched from the word *route*, the registry number Aldous Vane had recited at the toll-lock like a man reading a receipt. Knowing it secondhand and hearing it spoken were different animals. Hearing it, she felt the whole architecture of the last week click into place: the Compact hadn't found the chest. The Compact had been *given the map to it* by the custodian's own child, decades ago, for the price of a hull and an escape.

She didn't say any of that. She was her father's daughter; she knew what it cost to confess, and she knew confession was not a debt she was entitled to collect.

Instead she said, "The broker. Harrow Bend. Does he still keep an office?"

Tam looked between them — the captain's child and the archivist's daughter, sitting on a tarpaulin crate in his shed, already conspiring — and something in his weathered face softened and hardened at once. "Berrin Lask. Third floor over the chandlery. Keeps a green lamp in the window like he's proud of it." He stood, joints cracking like kindling. "Now. About your hull."

* * *

The work took two days, and Sefa spent both of them learning the yard.

She had never been useful with her hands in this particular way — her craft was paper, forgery, the patient violence done to documents — but a boatyard runs on the same principle as an archive: everything is held together by layers, and you repair from the inside out. She steamed planks. She mixed pitch under Tam's grudging instruction and got the ratio wrong twice and right on the third try, and Tam said nothing, which she had learned was his highest praise. She watched him fit the new oak into the gash, draw the fastenings tight, caulk the seam with a mallet and iron until the line of it disappeared into the hull as if the river had never bitten.

And all the while, the boat became a beacon. She felt it happening and could do nothing about it. A kestrel-boat hauled out in a small yard draws eyes the way a wound draws flies: the lock-keeper's boy came by twice with transparent excuses; a man in a good coat who claimed to be a surveyor measured the slipway with unnecessary thoroughness; a woman selling eels from a basket lingered at the gate and asked Sefa, in a friendly way, where they'd come from and where they meant to go, and Sefa gave her a smile and a lie and watched the woman walk away too quickly, basket swinging, already composing whatever report she'd been paid to compose.

"They're pricing us," Tam said on the second evening, nodding at the empty gate. "That's how it starts. Somebody comes to count what you're worth. Then somebody comes to buy it. Then somebody comes to take it, and by then the paperwork says it was always theirs." He spat expertly into the sawdust. "Your father hated that part worst. Not the danger. The *accounting*."

On the second evening, too, Sefa paid.

She'd been turning it over since the first morning: the coin at her throat, the last coin of the last mint, her grandmother's thumbprint worn into the reverse from fifty years of holding. It was the most valuable thing she owned and the least spendable, because spending it meant admitting the past was currency, which was exactly the Compact's theology — that everything, including memory, had a price and a buyer. She had sworn, in the ruins years, never to spend it.

But Tam Voss had broken a dead man's oath for them. And the yard was leaking their position by the hour, and Thursday was coming, and the road ahead ran over a glacier she couldn't see and Ivo wouldn't speak of.

She took the cord from around her neck and laid the coin on Tam's workbench while he was cleaning his mallets.

He looked at it a long time. Longer than a man looks at money. He picked it up, turned it to the light, and read the reverse the way he'd read the transom of the boat — and Sefa saw his lips move slightly, sounding out the old letters, and realized with a jolt that he could *read them*. Half-remembered, rusted, but real. A shipwright on a mudflat reach, three hundred miles from a drowned country, carrying its alphabet in his head like ballast.

"Dalla's mother engraved those dies," he said. "Did you know that? Before the mint closed. Whole families of the high pasture worked the coining in the last year — women mostly, because the men were already fighting, and then not fighting anymore." He set the coin down gently, as if it might bruise. "I can't take this."

"You owe my— you owed the captain a favor. Favors should cost something or they rot." She heard her own voice go formal, archival, and didn't stop it. "Take it and give it back to the mountain. Dalla's family, if you ever see them. Or melt it into a fitting for someone else's boat. I don't care. But I won't carry a price tag on my neck into the Karst, and I won't owe you nothing, because owing nothing is how the Compact likes its people."

Tam was quiet. Then he laughed — short, surprised, a bark of genuine delight — and pocketed the coin. "The captain talked like that," he said. "*Owing nothing is how they like us.* Word for word, near enough." He shook his head. "All right. The favor's paid, and here's what it buys: Thursday morning, dawn, before the yard opens. I'll put you through the lock myself and log you as going downriver. Then you turn north at the second bend where the willows screen you, and nobody on this reach will say different, because everybody on this reach owes the captain something and knows what happens to men who forget." He fixed Ivo with a look. "Both of you. Listen now, because I'll only say the route once."

* * *

He drew it on the bench in spilled sawdust, sweeping with the flat of his hand: the river north, the foothills, the trail-town at the end of the water — Last Light, he called it, and the name went through Sefa like cold water, because her father had said it once, drunk and grieving, the night before he died: *get the books to Last Light, and Last Light will get them over.* She had thought it a metaphor. It was a town. It had always been a town.

Beyond it, the high pasture, and beyond that the pass — the glacier, the calving season, the window that opened and shut like a mouth. Tam's finger traced it all in sawdust, and Sefa memorized it the way she memorized everything, which was completely and forever.

"One more thing," Tam said, when the map was swept away and the tea was cold and the light through the shed door had gone amber and low. He hesitated, which he plainly never did. "The summer I was nineteen, I asked the captain why he did it. Eleven crossings, mule-fees, bribes, frostbite, a dead porter one year — why? He wasn't Vessian. No blood in it. He could've run timber and retired rich." Tam rubbed his jaw. "And he said — I've kept this fifteen years like a stone in my boot — he said, *because my child will inherit my name either way, and I'd rather it be worth carrying than easy to hide.*"

The shed went silent.

Sefa looked at Ivo. Ivo was standing very still by the half-repaired hull, one hand flat against the new oak, and the look on their face was not gratitude or grief or anger but all three fused into something that had no name yet — the expression of a person discovering that the parent they'd spent a lifetime outrunning had, all along, been building them a road.

Outside, the tide of evening sounds filled the yard: halyards ticking, a dog somewhere, the river going about its endless indifferent business. And under it, faint, from the direction of the main stem, a sound Sefa had learned to hate with her whole nervous system — the soft, patient thrum of an engine idling where no honest engine idles, out in the darkening channel, holding its position.

Someone had come to count what they were worth.

Thursday was two days off. Sefa touched the bare place at her throat where the coin had lived, and found, to her surprise, that it weighed less than she feared and more than she'd expected — and that the road ahead, drawn in sawdust and memory, had already begun pulling at her like a current.
