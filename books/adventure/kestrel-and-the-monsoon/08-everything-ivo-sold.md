## 8. Everything Ivo Sold

The tavern at Ossory Quay was called the Drowned Compass, and it had earned the name honestly: a brass compass rose set into the floorboards by the door had been worn to illegibility by two centuries of boots, so that every direction it pointed was the one its owner had last been walking. Ivo had drunk there since before they were old enough to drink there legally, back when the quay smelled of tar and herring instead of diesel and tourist money, and in all those years they had learned one thing about the place that mattered: the corner table beneath the stern lantern of some wrecked coaster was the only seat where no one could stand behind you.

They took Sefa there on the third night of the boat's repairs, after the shipwright's boy had come down to say the new planking would need another day to cure and another day to pay for. The rain had settled in for the evening, a fine grey drizzle that turned the lamplight along the quay into smears, and the harbor water lay flat as poured pewter, holding the lights upside down like something drowned.

Sefa sat across from her with her hands wrapped around a clay mug of mulled wine she hadn't touched. She had spent the day below decks with the folios again — Ivo had stopped pretending not to notice, and she had stopped pretending not to know that Ivo noticed — and whatever she'd read had left a stillness in her that Ivo recognized from funerals. Not grief exactly. Grief's quieter cousin, the one that comes later, when the crying is done and what remains is arithmetic.

"You've been avoiding this," Sefa said. It wasn't an accusation. That was somehow worse.

"I've been supervising repairs."

"You've been walking past the boat four times an hour and finding reasons to argue with the shipwright about caulking." She turned the mug a quarter turn on the scarred wood. "You do that. When there's something you don't want to say, you get very busy with your hands."

Ivo looked at the compass rose by the door, at the boots crossing it without knowing, and felt the thing in their chest shift position the way ballast shifts in a following sea — slowly, and then all at once, and then you're listing and there's nothing to be done but admit it.

"There's a story," Ivo said, "that goes with the chest."

"I assumed there were several."

"One that matters." They signaled the keeper for a second mug, though they had no intention of drinking it; it was simply something to put between their hands and the truth. "And if we're going to keep doing what we're doing — running upriver, spending your grandmother's coin, betting both our necks on my father's route-notes — then you deserve to hear it from me before you hear it from anyone else. Because someone else will tell you. Someone always does. And they'll tell it worse than it was, or better, and either way you'll never look at me the same again, and I'd rather you did that looking now, at a table I chose, than later, at a moment I didn't."

Sefa set the mug down. She folded her hands. She said nothing at all, which was the most frightening response available to her.

* * *

Ivo had been seventeen the year the river took everything sideways.

Their father had been dead eleven months by then — drowned, officially, though the body had never come up out of the Serein, and Ivo had long ago stopped believing in official versions of anything connected to that man. The boat was gone too, seized against debts nobody could produce the paperwork for, and Ivo had been living aboard a borrowed barge with a widow named Maren who fed them out of pity and worked them out of necessity, and every night they lay awake in the fo'c'sle doing the same arithmetic every orphaned river-rat does: what can I sell, how fast, and who won't ask questions.

What they had was a oilskin packet their father had kept locked in the cabin desk, which Ivo had pried open with a chisel the week after the funeral because grief makes burglars of us all. Inside were route-notes. Hand-drawn charts of the upper Serein and the country beyond it — the Karst foothills, the braided shallows where the river died, and, drawn in a fine patient hand that Ivo had hated for being so calm, a pass through the glacier. Elevations. Seasonal windows. A branch marked in pencil with a notation in a language Ivo couldn't read and hadn't tried to. Their father had never explained the notes. He had only once, when Ivo was small, caught them leafing through the packet and closed it gently and said: *not yet.* Two words, and then eleven years of *not yet*, until death made the question moot and the packet made it profitable.

A broker worked out of a rented office above the chandlery at Harrow Bend — a soft-spoken man named Quill whose real trade was buying paper from people in trouble. Maps, letters, manifests, deeds. He paid fairly, which everyone said like it was a virtue, and he asked nothing, which should have been the warning. Ivo brought him the packet on a Tuesday morning in the rain, hating themselves before they even crossed his threshold, and he counted out bills onto the desk with the unhurried precision of a man shelling peas, and he said, "Your father had an interesting eye," and that was all. No questions. No follow-up. Ivo walked out with enough money for a down payment on a half-rotted kestrel-class hull, and they told themselves for years afterward that the transaction had ended there, that paper sold is paper gone, that a broker buys to collect the way anyone collects.

It took Ivo another decade to learn what Quill actually was: a procurement agent. A finder. The Meridian Compact didn't send its own people into river towns to buy maps from teenagers; it let the river towns grow their own Quills, the way a floodplain grows reeds, and harvested them at leisure. The route-notes went up the chain. Somewhere in a filing room in a city Ivo had never seen, a clerk had opened an oilskin packet containing a hand-drawn chart of a glacier pass that appeared on no commercial map, and had entered it into a ledger under a registry number, and the Compact had learned — from Ivo Crane's own hands, for the price of one rotten hull — that Vessia's archive had a road out of the mountains, and that the road had a season, and that seasons could be waited for.

"Say the rest," Sefa said quietly. She had not moved. Her eyes had not left Ivo's face since the second sentence.

"The rest is that I told myself a story for twenty years." Ivo's voice came out lower than intended, roughened against the tavern noise. "That I'd sold old papers. That Dad was dead and the notes were just ink and ink doesn't bleed. I built a whole life on top of that story — the false names, the moving every season, the way I never once ran the upper river past Kilometer Sixty even though it's the best water on the Serein, because I didn't want to look at the country those charts described. You think I run from the Compact because they're dangerous? Everyone dangerous is dangerous. I run because every lockmaster who looks twice at me might be looking at a girl who sold her father's life's work for a boat, and I can't —" The sentence snagged. Ivo picked at the seam of the table with a thumbnail. "I can't be seen carrying it. So I made myself invisible, and invisibility is just shame with good navigation."

The lantern above the corner table hissed. Out on the quay, a winch groaned somewhere in the dark, hauling something heavy and unseen.

"When Vane said the registry number," Sefa said. "At Harrow Bend. He knew the number because—"

"Because the packet had one. Quill assigned it. Which means it's been in their system twenty years, catalogued, cross-referenced, probably annotated by three different clerks. Everything I've spent my adult life keeping off paper, they've had on paper since before I could legally drink." Ivo laughed, and it came out wrong, a dry cracked sound with nothing in it. "He offered me triple freight rates while standing on the exact spot where I sold them the map to find him. If that isn't the river telling a joke, I don't know what is."

"And the chest." Sefa's voice stayed level, but Ivo saw her knuckles whiten around the mug. "The chest your father sealed. The archive he died carrying. Do you understand what you're telling me? Your father trusted you with the inheritance — the *real* one, the one under the floorboards — and you'd already sold the only proof of where it lived."

"Yes."

"He carried boxes over the Karst every summer. The shipwright said so. Every summer, alone, for years, for a nation that no longer existed on any map, and his own child—"

"Said yes to a broker in the rain." Ivo met her eyes and did not look away, though every instinct screamed to. This was the part that mattered; this was the part that had to be borne upright. "I'm not going to soften it. I was hungry and angry and seventeen, and I hated him for dying with secrets instead of leaving me anything I could hold, and selling his charts felt like — I don't even have the word. Justice, maybe. Punishing him for the *not yet*. And then I bought the boat with the money and named her Kestrel because it was the only honest thing I owned, and I've been paying for her ever since at an interest rate I didn't know I'd agreed to."

Sefa stood.

For one lurching moment Ivo thought: *there it is,* the walk-out, the door, the compass rose crossed by boots that wouldn't come back. Sefa pulled her coat from the chair back, and the lantern light moved across her face, and there was no fury in it — that was the terrible part. Fury would have been survivable. What was in her face was the arithmetic again, the quiet counting of costs, and Ivo understood that she was weighing whether the archive's survival needed them, the way one weighs whether a leaking hull needs a particular patch.

She sat back down.

"Don't mistake this for forgiveness," she said. "I need you to hear that clearly, because you seem like someone who would take a person staying as absolution, and I am not giving you that. My grandmother minted the coins I paid the shipwright with. My father wrote letters to a man named Crane for fifteen years, trusting him with the only unburned copy of a country, and that man's child sold the road to the people who erased us. There is no version of tonight where I forgive that. Maybe in ten years. Maybe never. Forgiveness isn't mine to hand out anyway — it belongs to the dead, and the dead haven't voted."

"Then why stay?"

"Because." She turned the mug again, a full slow circle, watching the wine catch the light. "Because you could have lied. You've lied beautifully since the day I met you — cargo manifests, transit papers, your entire biography. You could have told me the Compact found the pass some other way, or that it didn't matter how they found it, or forty other smooth things, and I'd likely have believed you, because I wanted to believe you. Instead you sat me in the one corner of this room where nobody can stand behind you and handed me the worst fact about yourself like it was cargo that had to be declared." She looked up. "My father used to say you learn more about a custodian from what they confess than from what they protect. Anyone can guard a treasure. It takes a different animal entirely to guard a truth against itself."

"That's generous."

"It's practical." A ghost of something crossed her mouth — not quite a smile, but related to one, the way a headland is related to the coast. "We have a boat with fresh caulking, a chest under the floorboards, a mountain range between us and anywhere safe, and a syndicate that has known about that pass for twenty years longer than I have. I cannot afford to hate you efficiently, Captain. Hatred takes time, and the snows are early this year." She pushed the untouched mulled wine toward Ivo. "Drink that. You've gone grey."

Ivo drank. It scalded going down and settled warm and wrong in the hollow where the confession had been, filling the shape of it imperfectly, the way any replacement fills the shape of a thing given away.

* * *

They walked back along the quay in the drizzle, side by side, not touching, the lamps smearing their reflections on the black water. At the head of the slipway, where the kestrel-boat sat propped in her cradle with her belly open to the shipwright's lamps, Sefa stopped.

"The branch tunnel," she said. "On your father's chart. The pencil notation you couldn't read."

"What about it?"

"I can read it." She said it simply, the way one reports weather. "It's Vessian. I've been working through the folios all day. Tomorrow, when the caulking's cured, I'll show you what it says." She started down the slipway, then paused, rain beading in her hair, and added over her shoulder: "It's addressed to whoever carries the chest. Both of you, I think. Him and you. Whether you sold his maps or not."

She went aboard. Ivo stood a while longer in the rain, looking at the boat — at the name they'd bought with the worst money of their life, at the hull that had carried them out of every trouble except the one inside them — and somewhere upriver, days away and gaining, the Compact was already buying mules.

Ivo went aboard too, and slept, for the first time in twenty years, without checking the anchor light twice.
