## 9. The Broker's Ledger

Sefa had learned to pick locks in a house where the doors were always locked.

Not because her family had anything worth stealing — an archivist's salary in an exiled nation's diaspora bought bread, lamp oil, and paper, in that order — but because her father believed that a people who could not open their own doors would one day find themselves locked out of everything. He had taught her with a hairpin and a length of clockspring, kneeling in the hallway of a rented room in a city that had never heard of Vessia, saying *feel for the pins the way you feel for the current under ice, child. Everything locked has a shape. Everything with a shape can be known.*

She thought of him now, crouched in the alley behind the broker's office on Cordwainer Lane, her breath making small ghosts in the pre-dawn cold, her fingers reading the shape of a lock that had never once considered it might be known.

The lock was good. Brass-cased, three-lever, salt-air corrosion in the keyway that told her it hadn't been oiled in a year, which told her the broker was either careless or arrogant, and from everything Ivo had said about Ferren Quill — *a man who sells other people's roads and sleeps like a baby* — she guessed arrogant. Arrogance was a lever like any other. She seated the pick, felt for the first pin the way her father had taught her, and gave it the patience she gave a river.

Behind her, in the black mouth of the alley, Ivo kept watch. She could hear them not breathing — the particular held stillness of a person who had spent a lifetime being unnoticed and had made a craft of it. Every few minutes Ivo's voice came low as water under a hull.

*Cart's coming. Cart's gone. Cat on the wall, not a cat, a cat. Two men at the corner, drunk, moving off.*

Sefa worked. The first lever gave. The second resisted, then surrendered with a click she felt in her teeth rather than heard. The third was stiff with rust, and for a long moment the whole mechanism hung suspended in her fingertips like a boat on the edge of a sandbar, and then it turned, and the door to Ferren Quill's brokerage of stolen geographies swung inward on hinges that needed oil worse than the lock had.

*Everything locked has a shape,* her father said in her memory, in his patient, tired voice, the voice of a man who had spent thirty years cataloguing a country that no longer existed and had died before anyone thanked him for it.

*Then I'll learn its shape,* Sefa told him silently, and went inside.

* * *

The office smelled of dust and sealing wax and, beneath both, the faint sourness of old money — the smell of a room where transactions happened that no one was meant to remember. She had brought no lamp. Ivo had argued against it, and against her coming at all, and against the entire enterprise in the particular grumbling register that Sefa had learned over the past weeks to translate: *I am afraid, and fear in me sounds like objection.*

But Ivo had come anyway. That was the thing about Ivo Crane that Sefa was only beginning, warily, to understand. They would argue a plan to pieces and then execute it flawlessly, as if the arguing were a kind of ritual, a toll paid before passage. A lockmaster of the self.

She let her eyes adjust. Moonlight came through a high window in a thin grey wedge, enough to read the room's grammar: a desk with a ledger-stand, a wall of pigeonhole shelves, a map case, a safe whose door stood half open — Quill was arrogant, then, or someone had been here before her — and, on the wall behind the desk, a framed chart of the Serein River from the delta to the foothills, the kind of chart Ivo could read the way Sefa read her grandmother's letters.

The route-notes. That was what had brought her here. Somewhere in this room was the paper trail of the notes a sixteen-year-old Ivo Crane had sold to buy their first boat — the notes that had taught the Meridian Compact that a glacier pass existed at all, that a drowned nation's archive had a road. Sefa had spent three days in the public record office tracing the sale: broker's commission stamps, a transfer docket, the particular watermark of Compact paper that she had been taught to recognize before she could write her own name in Vessian script. The trail ended at this door. Everything ended at doors. Her father had taught her that too.

She went to the desk first. The ledger-stand held a book of current commissions — freight rates, toll-bribes, the small venal commerce of a river town — and she skimmed it with a professional's speed. Her father had kept ledgers too. She knew how a ledger breathed, where it kept its secrets: not in the entries, but in the gaps between them, the pages that had been cut out, the numbers that were too round.

The third drawer was locked, but its lock was a cousin of the front door's, and it gave her less trouble than the alley had.

Inside: a slim black book, bound in something that was not quite leather, stamped on the cover with a device she knew from her nightmares and from the wax seals on the Compact's correspondence — a meridian line crossing a globe, and beneath the globe, small enough that you had to look, a scale bar, as though the world were a thing to be measured for purchase. Which, she thought, was exactly what it was.

*Acquisitions Register. Meridian Compact, Southern Directorate. Confidential — Custody of the Broker of Record.*

Her hands were steady. She noticed that with a strange detachment, the way you notice the river going calm before it goes over the falls. She had expected her hands to shake. Grief shook. This was something else, something colder and more level, and it frightened her more than shaking would have.

She carried the book to the wedge of moonlight and opened it.

* * *

The first pages were old. The ink had gone brown, the hand cramped and careful, and the entries read like a shipping manifest written by a man who had slowly stopped sleeping.

*Lot 14. The Marchlands of Osten. Contents: boundary stones (9), parish registers (2 chests), a bell. Condition: fair. Vendor: municipal council, distressed. Price: —*

*Lot 15. The Salt Coast. Contents: lighthouse chart, fisher-guild rolls, songs (transcribed, incomplete). Condition: songs incomplete. Note: the old woman who held the remainder declined. Note appended: the old woman who held the remainder has since been relocated. Acquisition complete.*

*Lot 22. The Kingdom of Vell. Contents: everything. Condition: excellent. The king sold it himself.*

Sefa read standing up, because she was afraid that if she sat she would never stand again. Each entry was a country. Not a war, not a conquest, not a burning — those left ruins, and ruins could be mourned, and mourning could become memory, and memory could become a story told to grandchildren by firelight. The Compact had understood something that armies never had. You did not need to burn a nation. You needed only to buy its past, warehouse it, seal it, and wait for the last person who remembered to die of old age in a rented room. Then the country was not conquered. It was simply *no longer available.* A gap in the atlas. A silence where a language had been.

*Lot 31. The Valley of Dalla's people* — no, she was ahead of herself; the valley's name was there, but she did not yet know it was Dalla's, would not know for weeks, and the name in the ledger was rendered only as *Lot 31, High Pasture Valley, contents: place-names (extensive), one engraver's workshop.* She read it and felt nothing yet except the cold. The cold was enough.

She turned pages. The entries grew more recent, the hand more confident, the prices larger. Whole watersheds. Mountain ranges. A desert people's star-charts, acquired through a middleman who had never been told what he was selling. An island nation bought in three installments. Each entry had the same closing formula, stamped or written: *Acquisition complete. Records consolidated. Public memory: declining. Estimated full dissolution: —* and then a date, sometimes a decade out, sometimes less. Dissolution. As if the country were a sugar cube. As if the remembering were a process with a half-life, and all the Compact had to do was wait out the decay.

Sefa's father had died believing he had failed. She understood that now with a clarity that felt like a blade being turned. He had spent his life copying, sealing, hiding, carrying — and he had died thinking the archive was a lifeboat with a leak, that he was bailing against the inevitable. He had never known that the Compact kept ledgers. That his work was visible in them at all. That somewhere a man with a broker's neat hand had written *Vessian archive, whereabouts unknown* and been irritated by it.

She found the entry on the fourth page from the current one.

*Lot 87. VESSIA.*

Her own country, in a stranger's alphabet, in a stranger's book, and she read the entry three times before she let herself understand it.

*Lot 87. Vessia. Contents: royal registers (partial), parish rolls, the Exile Archive (location long sought). Condition: scattered. Note: the archive was entrusted to a courier network at the dissolution; the network has been traced to a single family of river-freighters operating under an assumed name on the Serein. Route documentation acquired from within the family, year —* and here was the date, and Sefa did the arithmetic and felt the floor of her chest give way, because it was the year Ivo had told her about in the tavern at Ossory Quay, the year of the confession, the year a sixteen-year-old had sold their father's route-notes for a boat.

*— acquired from within the family. The pass over the Karst is thereby confirmed viable for a summer expedition. The Exile Archive remains unlocated; presumed with the custodian's heirs. Status: PENDING FINAL DELIVERY.*

*Pending final delivery.*

Not destroyed. Not lost. Not even hated — hatred would have been almost a comfort, hatred would have meant she mattered. *Pending final delivery.* The phrase of a warehouse. The phrase of a man checking his inventory before a shipment. Her father's life's work, her grandmother's coin, the language in her mouth, the names of every family that had ever loved anyone under a Vessian sky — all of it reduced to a lot number and a status line, waiting in a queue behind a bell and some fisher-guild rolls, waiting to be *delivered* to a vault where no one would ever read it again, and the waiting had already outlasted her father, and it would outlast her too if the Compact had its way, and the entry would be closed by some clerk who had never heard a Vessian boat-song, with the neat final stamp: *Acquisition complete.*

The cold in her went all the way through and came out the other side as something with edges.

She had come here for grief, she realized. She had told herself it was about the paper trail, the route-notes, the practical business of knowing what the Compact knew — but underneath, she had come to grieve, to stand in the room where her country had been sold and feel the proper feelings, the way you visit a grave. She had expected to leave heavier.

Instead she left armed.

Because the ledger was not only a wound. It was a map. The Compact had written down, in its own hand, in its own book, everything it knew and everything it lacked — *the archive remains unlocated; presumed with the custodian's heirs* — and a thing written down could be read, and a thing read could be anticipated, and a thing anticipated could be outrun. Her father had taught her that too, though he had taught it with rivers and locks and the habits of careful men: *the one who knows the other's chart holds the truer course.*

She copied the Vessia entry into her notebook, and then, because she was her father's daughter and thoroughness was a form of love, she copied the entries around it — the lots before and after, the pattern of the Compact's seasons, the note that the Southern Directorate's next survey season began with the thaw. She copied until her fingers ached. And then, at the bottom of the last page she read, she found the entry that stopped her copying and started her heart.

It was recent. The ink was still black. The hand was not Quill's — this hand was elegant, unhurried, the hand of a man who signed things rather than wrote them.

*Note to file, re Lot 87. The heirs have been identified and are under observation. Negotiation recommended before force; the elder Crane's reputation for stubbornness appears hereditary. If negotiation fails, the mountain will do what the ledger cannot. The pass closes with the snows, and nothing that closes with the snows is ever found again. — A.V.*

*A.V.*

Sefa stood in the wedge of moonlight with the elegant handwriting swimming in front of her, and the cold fury in her chest acquired a name, and the name had a face — a courteous face, a patient face, a face that offered triple freight rates and called it salvage consulting. *The mountain will do what the ledger cannot.* He was not chasing them to take the chest. He was chasing them to the mountain, and the mountain was his instrument, and the snows were his accomplice, and he could wait at the trailhead with his bought mules and his bought beds and his bought silences and simply let the season do the work.

Unless they crossed before the snows. Unless they found the pass in the window the glacier allowed. Unless her father's charts — the ones in the chest, the hand-drawn pass charts she had not yet finished deciphering — held what she half-believed they held.

She put the black book back in its drawer. She closed the drawer with the same care she had opened it. On the desk, on a sheet of Quill's own letterhead, in a hand disguised as a clerk's, she left a single line in Vessian script, the old blessing her grandmother had said over departing boats: *May your cargo be lighter than your memory.* Let the broker puzzle over it. Let him be afraid. Fear was a lever like any other.

Then she went to the door, and paused, and looked back at the framed chart of the Serein on the wall — the river where her father's friend had run his boat, where his children had grown up strangers to their own name, where a sandbar that appeared on no chart had given up a sealed chest to the one person alive who could carry it. The river knew something about the Cranes. Perhaps the river had known about the Compact all along, too, and had simply been waiting, the way rivers wait, for someone to ask.

Sefa slipped out into the alley, into the cold, into Ivo's held-breath stillness, and the door closed behind her without a sound.

* * *

Ivo read her notebook by the grey first light in the lee of a warehouse wall, and Sefa watched the fury move through them like weather — not the hot flash she had expected but a slow, tectonic darkening, the face of a person watching their own worst act being itemized in a stranger's ledger. *Route documentation acquired from within the family.* Ivo's jaw worked once. Their hands, wrapped around the notebook, were white at the knuckle.

"They wrote it down," Ivo said at last, very quietly. "Sixteen years old, and I've been in their book ever since. Lot eighty-seven, appendix, *the heir who sold the road.*"

"You were a child who needed a boat."

"I was a child who needed a boat," Ivo agreed, "and I sold a nation's only road to get one." They closed the notebook and handed it back with a care that was almost ceremonial. "Vane's note. *The mountain will do what the ledger cannot.* You understand what that means."

"It means we cross before the snows or we don't cross at all."

"It means he's already at the trailhead in his mind. It means every guide in Last Light will have his coin before we do." Ivo looked up the street, toward the river, toward the foothills just visible as a bruise on the horizon. "It means the running is over, Sefa. You can't outrun a man who owns the map. From here we go *through* him or not at all."

Sefa put the notebook inside her coat, against her heart, where her grandmother's coin used to ride. The fury was still there — it would always be there now, she understood that; it was not a weather but a climate — but beneath it lay something steadier, something her father would have recognized: the archivist's cold, the keeper's patience, the certainty that a thing written down could be fought with other things written down, and that a nation pending final delivery was not yet delivered.

"We'll need the charts," she said. "All of them. And we'll need to move tonight."

Ivo looked at her — really looked, the way you read water before committing the hull — and whatever they saw in her face made them nod once, slow, like a lock accepting a key.

"Tonight, then," they said. And then, further up Cordwainer Lane, where the morning was still too dark for strangers: "Because someone's been standing at the corner watching this alley for the last ten minutes, and he's wearing a red scarf, and I think we're about to be offered something we can't afford to refuse."

Sefa turned. At the mouth of the lane, patient as a toll, a figure in a red scarf waited with the unhurried confidence of a man who had already bought everything around him — the street, the silence, the morning — and had only one small signature left to collect.

Hers.
