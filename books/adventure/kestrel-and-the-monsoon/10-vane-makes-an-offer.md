## 10. Vane Makes an Offer

The fuel dock at Harrow Bend smelled of diesel and old rope and the particular sweetness of creosoted pilings baking in the afternoon sun. Ivo had been pumping fuel there since they were nineteen years old, back when the attendant was a one-eyed man named Brasic who kept a parrot that swore in three languages, and the routine of it was so worn into their body that their hands moved through it without instruction: hose couplings, gauge checks, the slow gurgle of the tank taking what it was given.

Which was why the voice behind them landed like a hook set in the jaw.

"Captain Crane."

Nobody called them Captain. Nobody called them Crane. On the Serein they were Ivo, or nothing, or — among the lock-keepers and reed-cutters who'd known them twenty years — *that quiet one with the kestrel boat*. The name on their papers was a fiction two layers deep, a name bought from a dead man's estate in a province nobody audited. So when the stranger said *Crane*, Ivo's hands went still on the hose and the river seemed to go quiet all around them, the way water does before something surfaces.

They turned.

The man from the toll-lock stood at the head of the gangplank with his hands folded loosely in front of him, as if he were waiting for a portrait to be finished. He wore a coat the color of wet slate, cut close, no insignia, no badge, nothing that would photograph badly. His face was the kind that aged well because it had never worked hard: fine lines at the eyes, silver coming in at the temples like frost finding the edges of a window. He looked, Ivo thought, like a man who had never once been rained on unexpectedly.

"You have me at a disadvantage," Ivo said, and made their voice do what it always did — flat, unhurried, a man with nowhere particular to be.

"Aldous Vane." He didn't offer a hand. That was its own message; men who offered hands wanted something small. "We spoke briefly yesterday. At the lock."

"We spoke about freight rates."

"We did." Vane smiled, and the smile was warm the way a banked fire is warm — you could put your hand near it and feel nothing but comfort right up until the moment it wasn't. "I'm afraid my colleagues at the Compact are somewhat more direct than I am. They wanted me to make you an offer of employment. Salvage consulting, we call it. I told them you'd decline. I told them you'd decline rudely, in fact, and that we should try a different door."

Ivo shut off the pump. The tank was full enough. Their heart was not behaving like a full tank; it was behaving like a river in flood season, pushing against everything.

"I don't consult," they said. "I haul cargo. Grain, timber, occasionally a coffin if the family pays the handling fee. If you've got freight—"

"Registry number VS-114-Kestrel." Vane said it gently, almost tenderly, the way a doctor says the name of a disease he has seen many times and no longer finds frightening. "Lead-lined. Double-hasped. Stamped with the seal of a nation that, according to every atlas printed in the last forty years, does not exist. Recovered from a sandbar at approximately kilometer sixty-two of the lower Serein, on a date I could tell you to the hour, because the river survey buoy at that bend reported an unregistered grounding."

The diesel smell suddenly seemed very loud. Ivo became aware of the dock beneath their boots, of the kestrel boat riding low and innocent at her moorings, of Sefa somewhere below decks with the folios spread out like a surgeon's instruments. They thought of the chest's weight in their hands on the sandbar, the way the river had given it up like a debt being called in.

"You've been watching the water," Ivo said.

"We watch everything the water carries." Vane took a step closer, and though he made no threatening motion of any kind, the space between them changed character entirely. It became a room. It became an office. Somewhere behind Ivo's eyes, a door they hadn't known was open swung quietly shut. "Captain — may I call you Captain? — I want to be honest with you, because I suspect honesty is a currency you rarely see spent."

"People say that right before they lie."

Vane laughed. It was a real laugh, brief and unforced, and somehow that was worse. "Yes. All right. Then let me lie to you first, so you know what it looks like." He gestured at the boat. "I could tell you the Meridian Compact is a philanthropic society dedicated to preserving endangered cultural heritage. That is what our letterhead says. I could tell you we acquire archives to protect them from neglect, war, damp, and the indifference of governments too poor to care. Some of that is even true. We do preserve things. Beautifully. Climate-controlled vaults in four countries, conservation staff the envy of every national library on the continent." He paused. "What our letterhead does not say is that preservation and possession are, in practice, the same act. A thing in our vaults is preserved precisely to the degree that no one else can ever reach it. That is the whole of our business, Captain. We buy pasts. Small ones. Nations that have run out of the money or the guns or simply the luck required to keep theirs. And Vessia—" his voice softened, and Ivo hated how much it sounded like grief, how well he wore it, "—Vessia is the finest acquisition remaining on the market."

"The market," Ivo repeated.

"Everything is a market. Grief especially. Grief holds its value remarkably well." Vane turned, unhurried, and looked out over the water the way a man looks at inventory. "You found the chest. That makes you either its owner or its obstacle, and which one you are depends entirely on a conversation I am hoping to have with you today. Not the conversation my colleagues wanted me to have. The real one."

* * *

Ivo had spent twenty years learning how conversations ended. Most of them ended the same way: with Ivo saying less than the other person expected, giving away less than the other person needed, and walking away while the other person was still deciding whether to be offended. Invisibility was not a talent. It was a discipline, practiced daily, like knots or breathing.

So they did what the discipline demanded. They leaned against the piling, arms crossed, and waited.

Vane appreciated the silence. That was clear. He settled onto a bollard with the ease of a man who had sat on worse things in worse places, and when he spoke again it was quieter, almost conversational, a man leaning across a tavern table rather than standing in a market.

"Your father," he said, "was a smuggler."

The words went into Ivo like a splinter going in clean — barely felt at first, all the pain arriving later. Their face did nothing. Twenty years of practice.

"That's the official record," Vane continued. "Smuggling, tax evasion, false registry on three vessels, and a drowning under circumstances the coastal authority declined to investigate. A bad man who died badly, and whose child sensibly changed their name and disappeared upriver." He tilted his head. "It's a good record. Thorough. Convincing. Do you know who wrote it?"

"The coastal authority."

"The Meridian Compact, Captain. Forty-one years ago. Your father came to us first, you see — or rather, we came to him. He had access to materials we wanted. He declined to sell. So we made sure that when he died, he died as a criminal, and that anyone who came asking after him afterward would find only a smuggler's grave and a smuggler's shame, and would conclude there was nothing worth digging for." Vane's eyes came back to Ivo's face and rested there with terrible gentleness. "We erased him. Professionally. It's what we do. And then we waited thirty years for the archive to surface, because it always surfaces. Rivers are patient, but so are we."

The dock swayed slightly under Ivo's feet, or seemed to. They thought of the letter in the hold, addressed to *the Custodian*. They thought of their father's hands on the wheel, teaching them to read current off the surface of moving water, and how those same hands had supposedly run contraband. They thought of the shame they had carried since they were fifteen years old, the shame that had a name and a shape and a weight, and they understood, with a lurch like a deck dropping away in a swell, that the shame might have been manufactured. Fitted to them like a hull fitted to a frame. Built by someone else, for someone else's purposes.

"Why tell me this?" Their voice came out lower than they intended.

"Because I want you to understand that I am not your enemy." Vane stood, brushing nothing at all from his immaculate coat. "My colleagues are your enemies, or will be, if this goes the usual way. There are men in this syndicate who would take that chest off your boat tonight and leave the river to decide what happened to you. I am not those men. I am the reason those men haven't come yet. I told them I could bring you in willingly, and buying your cooperation cleanly is cheaper than the alternative, so they've granted me time. But time, Captain, is the one commodity I cannot buy more of indefinitely."

"So make your offer."

"I intend to." Vane reached inside his coat and produced, of all things, a slim leather folio, unsealed, plain. He held it out. "But first, a question. What is your name? Your real one. The one you were born with, before you bought the dead man's papers."

Ivo said nothing.

"Quite." Vane set the folio down on the bollard between them, open. Inside were documents — Ivo recognized the format instantly, the heavy stock, the embossed seals, the watermarks that caught light only at certain angles. Coastal authority records. Court registries. Shipping manifests going back decades. And threaded through all of it, in red ink that had been struck through with the particular double-line used for expungement, a name.

Their name. The name they hadn't spoken aloud in twenty years. The name that had drowned with their father in everyone's memory but their own.

"Expungement is not forgery," Vane said quietly. "Forgery gives you a new name and leaves the old one buried where anyone with a shovel can find it. Expungement removes. Every registry, every court docket, every manifest, every customs ledger in eleven jurisdictions. The Crane record ceases to exist. Not sealed — sealed records can be unsealed. Removed. As if the family had never been entered into any book anywhere." He tapped the red-struck name with one finger. "And in its place, a clean history. A captain with no debts, no flags, no questions. Free to moor wherever you like. Free to sign your own name on your own boat, in daylight, in front of witnesses, for the first time in twenty years."

Ivo did not look at the folio. Looking at it would cost something, and they knew the price of looking, the way they knew the price of showing a channel where the chart showed none.

"And in exchange," they said, "you get the chest."

"In exchange, the Compact gets delivery. One chest, one registry number, delivered intact to a location of our specifying, signed for by you as salvor-in-possession transferring title voluntarily. No coercion. No theft. A legal acquisition, documented properly, which matters enormously to us — stolen property can be reclaimed by heirs and courts for generations, but purchased property is ours forever." Vane's smile returned, softer now, almost apologetic. "That is the entire transaction. You hand us a box. We hand you back your name. You go back to your river, richer and cleaner than you have been since you were a child, and you never think about Vessia again. Frankly, Captain, between us — you never thought about Vessia before last week. Why start now?"

There it was. The hook, set so gently it felt like kindness.

Because that was the unbearable truth underneath the offer, the truth Vane had built his whole approach around: Ivo *hadn't* thought about Vessia. Hadn't wanted to. Had spent a lifetime treating the drowned nation as ballast to be jettisoned, a name to be outrun, a father's silence to be inherited along with his boat. The chest had arrived like a debt collector, yes — but Vane was offering something no debt collector could: cancellation of the entire account. Not just the Compact's claims. Their own. Permission to stop running, signed and stamped and legally binding.

And some tired, flooded-backwater part of them wanted it so badly their teeth ached.

They thought of waking at anchor with no need to check the horizon. Of writing their name on a harbor form and watching the clerk's face stay bored. Of the twenty years of small flinches — every checkpoint, every toll-lock, every stranger who asked one question too many — simply ending, like weather.

"You've done this before," Ivo said. "Bought people back their names."

"Eleven times." Vane didn't flinch from it. "Nine accepted. Two declined. I remember all eleven. The nine live comfortably. The two—" he paused, and for a moment something moved behind his face that was not quite regret and not quite envy, "—the two are dead now, or might as well be. Running is not a life, Captain. It's a postponement. I am offering to end the postponement."

* * *

Below decks, faintly, Ivo heard a sound. A drawer sliding shut. Sefa, moving through the hold, careful as she always was, keeping herself invisible the way Ivo had taught the river taught them both. A girl with forged papers and old coins and a nation in her mouth that she still spoke when she thought no one was listening.

Two people on one small boat, each carrying a false name, each running from the same erasure — except she was running *toward* something, and Ivo had only ever been running *away*, and had gotten so good at it they'd mistaken the fleeing for a life.

"Give me a day," Ivo said.

Something flickered across Vane's expression — satisfaction, mostly, banked quickly, but underneath it something else, something almost like disappointment, as if part of him had hoped for a refusal and was sorry to be denied the pleasure of respecting one.

"A day," he agreed. "The folio stays with you. Read it carefully. Note the seals — they're genuine; we don't forge what we can simply purchase. When you're ready, send word to the toll-lock. Any method. We'll complete the transfer within the week." He straightened his coat, a small precise gesture, and stepped back toward the head of the gangplank, and just like that the office dissolved and they were standing on a fuel dock again, in ordinary sunlight, with diesel on their hands.

He stopped at the top of the plank and turned half around.

"For what it's worth, Captain — and I say this as a professional courtesy, not a threat — the chest is not the only thing in play anymore. My colleagues have begun interviewing the crew of every vessel that passed kilometer sixty-two in the relevant week. Pilots, lock-keepers, reed-cutters. People talk to us. They always talk to us eventually." His eyes went, briefly, deliberately, to the boat's cabin hatch. "Whatever arrangements you've made aboard, whatever company you're keeping — consider how long you can protect it. Protection is expensive. Names are cheap. Mine lasts forever."

Then he walked away up the quay, unhurried, coat the color of wet slate, and the crowd of stevedores parted around him without knowing why.

Ivo stood a long time at the rail. The folio lay on the bollard where he'd left it, open, the red-struck name catching the light. Below, faintly, the cabin door opened and closed again — Sefa, coming up, or hiding deeper, Ivo couldn't tell.

They picked up the folio. It weighed almost nothing. That was the horror of it; it weighed almost nothing at all.

Down in the hold, the chest sat where it always sat, lead-lined and patient, holding a dead custodian's letter and a living girl's inheritance and a nation that refused, against all professional effort, to stay drowned. Ivo looked at it through the open hatch for a long moment.

Then they carried the folio below, laid it on the chart table beside the route-notes, and went to find Sefa — because there was a day on the clock now, and a name on the table that they hadn't spoken in twenty years, and a decision that was going to require, for the first time in their adult life, telling someone the truth.
