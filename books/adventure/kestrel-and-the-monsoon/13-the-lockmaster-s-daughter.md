## 13. The Lockmaster's Daughter

The lock at Tern sat in a fold of the river like a held breath. Ivo saw it from two kilometers out — the long stone throat, the twin gates, the signal mast flying the yellow pennant that meant *inspection in progress* — and felt the old animal knowledge wake along the spine, the same knowledge that had once read sandbars in dark water. Trouble ahead. The kind that wore a uniform.

"Lock's flying yellow," Sefa said, from the bow, where she'd spent the morning learning the river's grammar one sign at a time. She had a way of naming things plainly, as if naming them made them manageable. Ivo had begun to find it steadying rather than irritating, which was its own kind of trouble.

"I see it."

"What does yellow mean?"

"It means someone paid for a look inside every hull that passes." Ivo brought the kestrel-boat's head around, angling toward the willow shade on the far bank, letting the current do the work of making them look idle. "Compact money buys lockmasters the way it buys everything else. Not all of them. Enough."

"And the ones it doesn't buy?"

Ivo didn't answer right away. Below the lock, the river ran wide and slow through hay meadows going gold at the edges, and a heron stood in the shallows with the patience of something that had never once been in a hurry. It was the kind of country where a man could imagine the world was simple. Ivo had spent twenty years not imagining that.

"There was a lockmaster at Tern named Corrag," they said at last. "When I was a boy. He had a daughter who used to sit on the gate beam and fish with a bent pin, and my father used to bring her river-candy wrapped in wax paper, and she'd trade him gossip about every boat that came through. That was thirty years ago. Corrag will be dead. The daughter will be someone's wife in some town I couldn't find on a chart. It doesn't matter."

"You remembered her name before you remembered mine," Sefa said.

Ivo had no answer for that either, so they let the boat drift and pretended to study the current.

* * *

They moored in the willows through the afternoon and watched the lock work. Three barges went up: a grain barge flying the Meridian colors — of course, the Compact moved grain the way it moved everything, as ballast for its real cargo, which was paper — and two timber rafts, and a fat little packet boat with tourists on its roof deck photographing the gates. Each hull was boarded at the downstream apron by two men in grey coats who moved with the unhurried confidence of people who had never once been told no. They carried a document tube and a hand lens and, on the grain barge, they were down in the hold for the better part of an hour. When they came up, the barge's master was pale, and one of his deckhands was walking off the boat with a sack over his shoulder and his wages in his fist, and the Compact men were laughing about something.

"They're not looking for a chest," Sefa said quietly. She'd come to stand at the rail beside Ivo without either of them deciding it. "They're looking for *lies*. Anything undeclared. That's how they work — you read the ledger in the broker's office. They don't need to find the thing. They need a reason to take everything."

"They'll have the kestrel-boat's registry," Ivo said. "Bought it off the Harrow Bend toll-rolls the same day Vane made his offer. They'll have my description, Sefa's description, the gash in the hull from the reed run — patched, but patched fresh. They'll have the name I signed at Ossory Quay, and they'll know it's a name I haven't used in twenty years, which tells them everything about how scared I am." Ivo turned the wheel spoke under their hand, a habit with nothing to steer. "A lock inspection isn't a search. It's a confession booth. You either lie to their faces with papers, or you don't go through."

"Then we don't go through." Sefa said it simply, the way she said most true things. "We take the boat apart and carry it overland."

"Sixty kilometers of towpath and two ranges of hills, with a boat on our backs, ahead of snows, with Vane buying every mule between here and Last Light." Ivo almost smiled. "You've been reading my route-notes."

"I've been reading your *father's* route-notes. There's a difference. His are better." She looked at the lock a long moment, and Ivo watched her do the thing she did — the archivist's thing, the thing that had broken into a broker's office and come out holding a ledger of swallowed nations. She looked at a system until she found the seam in it. Then her eyes came back around, slow, and settled on Ivo with an expression Ivo couldn't read. "The yellow pennant means inspection. What does the blue pennant mean?"

Ivo followed her gaze to the signal mast. Below the yellow, half-hidden in its shadow, hung a second pennant, small, faded, blue as a vein. Ivo had seen it and dismissed it — harbor flags, tide flags, a dozen blue pennants meant a dozen harmless things on a dozen stretches of river.

"Old signal," Ivo heard themself say, and the voice didn't sound entirely like theirs. "My father used to say — " and there it stopped, because the rest of the sentence was arriving from thirty years away, from a deck at dusk, from a man's hand pointing at a lock mast. *Blue at Tern means the house is open. Blue means you can knock.*

Ivo had spent a lifetime keeping their father in a locked room in the back of their skull — the route-notes sold, the shame, the silence — and now the room was open and everything in it was walking out and pointing at things.

"Stay with the boat," Ivo said, and was over the rail and into the shallows before the sentence finished.

* * *

The lockhouse at Tern was stone, low, older than the lock itself, with smoke rising from its chimney and a garden gone wild with beans and marigolds, and a girl of perhaps nineteen splitting kindling in the yard with an axe too big for her, each stroke landing with the economy of someone who had done ten thousand of them. Ivo came up the path with their hands visible and their heart going like a bilge pump, feeling like a man knocking on a door out of a dream, and the girl looked up, and Ivo saw Corrag in her face — the same heavy brow, the same unimpressed eyes — and something in the chest went loose and strange.

"You'll be wanting something," the girl said. Not unfriendly. Just certain.

"I'm looking for the lockmaster."

"You've found the lockmaster. My father's hips went last winter. The Compact's examiner signs whatever I tell him to sign, because I tell him where the snags are and he tells the barges, and nobody drowns, and everybody's happy." She rested the axe against the block. "You're not a barge master."

"No."

"You came up the willow bank and you moored where the hay cart can't see you, and you've been watching my lock since noon, and you flinched when the yellow went up." She wiped her hands on her apron. "So. You'll be wanting the blue pennant. And I'll be wanting to know who told you it still meant anything, because I fly it for my father's memory and for maybe four boats a year, and none of them flinch at the yellow, because none of them have reason to."

Ivo stood in the marigolds with thirty years of silence in their throat. There were a hundred ways to say it. Every one of them was a lie or a key.

"My name is Crane," they said. The real one. First time in twenty years, and it came out rusty. "My father ran this river. He'd have come through your lock in the famine year — the year the blight came up from the lowlands and the villages above Tern were eating bark. He ran medicine upstream. Against the freight. At night. He'd have — "

" — tied up below the apron and sent a boy up the path with wax paper," the girl finished. Her voice had changed. Not warmer, exactly. Deeper, like water finding its old channel. "River-candy. My mother kept the papers. She said if the man who ran the medicine ever came back, we were to fly the blue pennant and open the house, and that was twenty-six years ago, and I have flown it my whole life for a ghost." She studied Ivo with Corrag's unimpressed eyes, and then, underneath, with something that was not unimpressed at all. "You're his?"

"I'm theirs." Ivo let the old evasion stand and then, because the girl had flown a pennant for a ghost her whole life, took it down. "I'm their child. And I'm in trouble that has a name and a ledger, and there's a boat in the willows that can't go through your lock in the open."

The girl — she said her name was Maren Corrag's-daughter, because her mother had refused the examiner's registry name and put the old patronymic on the birth papers, and the Compact had never bothered to fix it, because what was one woman's name to men who erased nations — Maren looked past Ivo at the lock, at the yellow pennant, at the grey coats packing up their document tube for the evening.

"Tomorrow morning," she said, "the grain barge from the Compact comes back down. It's a big hull and it rides light and my father always said the Compact's grain barges draw like lies — shallow and full of nothing. There's room in its lockage for you and your boat and the boat's whole history." She picked the axe back up. "Be at the downstream apron at first light. And Captain Crane — "

"Ivo."

"Ivo. The medicine your father ran — it was quinine and fever-powder and it came from a woman in the hill villages who never let him pay. He used to argue with her about it every summer. I know because my mother wrote it all down." Maren's mouth did something that was almost a smile. "Your father thought he was paying his debts. He never understood that he was *making* them. The good kind. The kind that wait."

* * *

First light came up the valley the color of skimmed milk, and the kestrel-boat slid out of the willows with the mist still on the water, Sefa at the stern line and Ivo at the wheel, both of them silent in the way of people about to do something that cannot be undone. The Compact grain barge — the same one, its master still pale, its hold still smelling of nothing much — was already in the lower chamber, gates closing behind it, water beginning to fall, and Maren stood on the gate beam above it with a lantern in one hand and the other hand flat against the stone, and she looked down at Ivo's boat coming in low and slow along the wall, and she did not look away, and she did not hurry, and Ivo understood that this was what it looked like when a person kept a promise for twenty-six years without ever being asked twice.

"Along the wall," Maren called down. "Tight as you can. Fenders on the barge side. If the examiner looks down, he sees a barge in a lock, and that's all he sees, because that's all he's ever been paid to see."

The kestrel-boat slid into the gap between the grain barge's flank and the lock wall — a gap narrower than a coffin, a hand's width off the barnacled stone, the barge's shadow falling over the deck like a lid closing. Ivo brought the boat in on breath and fingertips, the way they'd once brought her through the reed channels with Compact engines sweeping the dark, and felt Sefa at the stern doing the same arithmetic with the line, and for a moment — the gates shutting above them, the water beginning to rise, the whole world reduced to stone and rope and the creak of timber — Ivo was not hiding. Ivo was being *carried*. Held inside someone else's ordinary morning, inside a lockage, inside a promise, inside a network their father had built one night-run at a time and never once mentioned, and the strange grief of it rose up like lock water, lifting everything.

"He never told me," Ivo said. It came out before they could stop it. "Any of it. The villages, the medicine, the — " a gesture that took in the lock, the pennant, the girl on the gate beam, the whole invisible architecture of debts waiting patiently in the hills. "Twenty years I thought the only thing he built was a smuggling route, and the only thing I inherited was the shame of it."

Sefa coiled the stern line without looking up. "You thought he was a smuggler because you sold his notes to a man who called him one. Brokers don't write histories, Ivo. They write *inventories*." She looked at the barge wall sliding past an arm's length away, close enough to touch. "Your father's inventory was never the cargo. It was this. Every lock, every village, every person who'd fly a pennant for a ghost. He was building a river that could keep a secret. You're not walking through enemy country. You're walking through *his* country. He just never sent you the map."

"He sent the chest."

"He sent the chest," Sefa agreed, and something in her voice — the archivist's voice, the voice that had read the custodian's letter aloud in the hold with her hands shaking — said *and the map was in it all along, and you're only now learning to read.*

The water rose. The upper gates swung. Above them, Maren raised the lantern once — a slow arc, a signal older than either of them — and the grain barge groaned out into the morning and the kestrel-boat came out of its shadow into open water, unexamined, unregistered, unseen, and the examiner on the apron never looked up from his tea.

Maren came down the path as they tied off above the lock, and she pressed something into Ivo's hand: a folded paper, old, soft as cloth, written in a woman's hand. "My mother's list," she said. "Every stop your father made, famine year. Every door that opens. There are eleven locks between here and the foothills, and I can vouch for four, and the paper vouches for the rest." She stepped back, and Corrag's heavy brow came down, and the unimpressed eyes went bright for just a moment. "Fly nothing. Knock at everything. And Captain — when you get where you're going, send word down the river. I've flown a blue pennant my whole life. It would be good to take it down for a reason."

Ivo held the paper the way they'd once held the chest — like something that weighed more than its weight.

They cast off into the morning current, and Sefa stood at the bow reading the list over Ivo's shoulder, and when she reached the fifth name on it she went very still.

"Ivo," she said. "I know this name. It's on the register — the family register from the branch chamber cache. This village, this family." She looked up, and her eyes were the eyes of someone watching two maps suddenly agree. "Your father's list and the archive's register are the same list. He wasn't running medicine *and* the archive. It was always one errand." She turned the paper toward the current, toward the hills, toward the eleven locks and the foothills beyond. "And Ivo — the last name on it. It's not a village. It's a name I've seen before, on the ledger, in the broker's office, in Vane's own hand."

She read it aloud.

It was the name Vane had offered to expunge.
