## 14. What the Villages Kept

The hill-stream came down out of the Karst foothills the way a shy child comes into a room — sideways, apologetic, all at once — and where it met the Serein the water turned a color Sefa had no name for in any language she owned. Green over brown. Two grammars braided together.

Ivo brought the boat in against the current with that unhurried precision she had been studying for days now, the way a person studies a language they refuse to admit they're learning. A touch of reverse. A quarter-turn of the wheel. The hull slid into the lee of a gravel spit where three fishing skiffs were drawn up nose-to-tail like sleeping animals, and the kestrel-boat settled against the bank with a sound like a long exhale.

"Confluence," Ivo said, as if that were an explanation. Maybe it was. "Hill people bring their wool down here to trade. There'll be a market by morning, if the weather holds."

It was late afternoon. The light came low and gold across the water, and on the far bank a village climbed the hillside in terraces — stone walls, slate roofs, woodsmoke standing up straight in the still air. Sefa counted the chimneys out of habit. Counting was what she did instead of hoping.

They went ashore for water and, if the gods were generous, eggs. Ivo carried the empty jerry cans; Sefa carried the small purse of honest coin they had left, and against her ribs, wrapped in oilcloth inside her coat, the folio she had begun to think of as *the letter*, though it was more than a letter now. She had read it four times. Each time, the last line landed in the same place, just under the sternum, like a swallowed stone: *Keep it moving, or it becomes a relic, and relics can be bought.*

*Keep it moving.* Her father had spent thirty years keeping paper still — flat, catalogued, dusted, safe. And the man who had carried the chest north had understood something her father had perhaps only suspected: that an archive in a vault is a corpse with good posture. It was the moving that kept it alive.

The path up from the spit was packed earth and sheep droppings, terraced on both sides with beans and late cabbages. A dog announced them with the bored professionalism of a dog who had announced a hundred travelers and been wrong every time. And then, as they came level with the first stone wall, Sefa heard it.

Singing.

A woman's voice, unhurried, coming from behind the wall — a harvest song, the kind sung while cutting, the kind that exists to keep hands in rhythm. Sefa stopped walking. Ivo took two more steps before noticing, and turned, and stood with the jerry cans swinging slightly, watching her face do whatever it was doing.

Because the song was in Vessian.

Not all of it. The verses were in the hill dialect, the same blunt syllables the porters used in Last Light, the same tongue that had carried her up the river for weeks now as an outsider. But the refrain — the line the woman returned to, over and over, the way a river returns to its channel — the refrain was her grandmother's language, worn smooth as a pebble, half its endings fallen away, one vowel shifted where a hill mouth couldn't make the sound. But it was Vessian. It was *her*. It was the sound her grandmother had made over the stove, over the ledger, over Sefa's own head as a lullaby, in a country that no longer appeared on maps, sung by a stranger behind a bean wall in a village whose name Sefa had never heard.

*Zhalu, zhalu, vodha i zheren —*

*Gold, gold, water and the millstone.*

She knew the millstone verse. Her grandmother had sung it with different words, city words, but the line was the line. The line had crossed a mountain range and a border and a hundred years and an erasure, and it was still here, still turning, still keeping time for a woman's hands in a bean field.

"Sefa." Ivo's voice, quiet. "You've gone white."

"Don't stop her," Sefa said, and didn't recognize her own voice. "Please. Don't let her stop."

The song went on. The woman — grey-haired, sleeves rolled, a sickle hooked over the wall beside her — sang through the refrain twice more, and Sefa stood on the path with her hand pressed flat against her own sternum as if holding a door shut, and wept. Not gracefully. Not the single dignified tear of a memorial service. She wept the way you weep when something you had privately begun to mourn as dead walks up behind you and puts its hand on your shoulder.

Ivo set down the jerry cans and did not touch her. That was the right thing. Ivo had a gift, Sefa was learning, for the right kind of not-touching.

"She's singing the millstone song," Sefa managed, when she could. "Do you understand what — my grandmother sang that. In Vessia. Before. This woman can't have — she's never seen a map of Vessia. She probably couldn't point to it. She doesn't know what she's keeping."

"Maybe that's how it survives," Ivo said. "Things nobody knows are worth stealing."

* * *

The woman's name was Maret, and she was delighted to have listeners, because a good refrain wants an audience the way a river wants a bank. She fed them eggs boiled in their shells and flatbread off a stone, and talked with her hands as much as her mouth, and when Sefa — carefully, the way you test ice — sang the refrain back to her with the city vowels, the old vowels, the ones with the soft back-of-the-throat *h* that hill mouths had worn away, Maret's face did something extraordinary. It went still, and then it lit, and she grabbed Sefa's wrist across the table.

"That's how my gran's gran said it," Maret said. "The old way. My mother said I sang it wrong. Said the words used to have more bones in them."

"More bones," Sefa repeated. Her throat ached. "Yes. That's — yes."

They sat in Maret's kitchen while the light went amber and then copper, and Sefa wrote in her notebook — the notebook that was becoming, page by page, a second archive, an unofficial one, an archive of the unarchived — everything the village had kept without knowing it kept anything.

The boat-songs first. Maret's husband, Toma, was a fisherman with forearms like mooring rope, and he sang two of them for Sefa with the shy embarrassment of a man performing in his own kitchen. One was a hauling song, a rhythm-song for pulling nets, and its counting words were Vessian — *yin, dva, trei* worn down to *yin, va, treh* — numbers outliving the language that counted with them, because numbers are the last thing to die in a mouth. The other was a launching song, sung when a new skiff first touched water, and it was almost entirely Vessian, so old that Toma thought it was just "the old hill words," the way people think a fossil is just a funny-shaped rock.

Sefa translated it for Ivo in a low voice, line by line, and watched Ivo's hands go still around their cup.

*Little boat, little boat, you carry more than wood.*
*You carry the names of everyone who ever slid you down the shingle.*
*Go out heavy, come home light.*
*The river remembers what the bank forgets.*

"The river remembers what the bank forgets," Sefa said aloud. "That's — Ivo, that's the whole thing. That's what my father spent his life doing and never once said in a sentence that clean."

"Vessians were boat people before they were paper people," Ivo said. "Maybe the paper was just the river going another way."

She looked at them then — really looked, the way she had been learning to, past the careful flatness Ivo wore like a tarpaulin — and saw something she hadn't seen before. Not guilt. Something older and stranger. Recognition. Ivo Crane, who had spent twenty years signing false names on boat papers, was sitting in a stranger's kitchen hearing a song that said *the river remembers*, and the song was looking at them.

Then there were the rites. Maret walked them out at dusk to the village's harvest ground, a flat shelf above the confluence where the whole settlement would gather in three days for the cutting-in. There was a stone there — a squat, lichen-eared stone that the villagers called, in hill dialect, *the grandmother*, and around which the first sheaf was always laid. And the words the reeve would speak over it, Maret recited for Sefa, haltingly, the way you recite something learned by ear from a grandmother who learned it by ear from hers:

*Bread of the drowned field, rise.*
*What the water took, the water returns.*
*Count us among the kept.*

"Count us among the kept," Sefa whispered. Her eyes were burning again and she let them. "That's a Vessian harvest formula. It's in the liturgical folios — the ones in the chest. It's the closing line of the autumn rite. It's *supposed* to have been said last in Vessia, in the capital, by the last custodian, the year before—" She stopped. The year before everything. "It was supposed to have ended. And instead it walked."

"Things walk," Maret said simply, as if reporting the weather. "Songs walk. Names walk. My gran said the old country walked uphill when it drowned. That's why we say the words up here. Higher ground."

Sefa wrote that down too, her hand shaking. *The old country walked uphill when it drowned.* A child's cosmology, a grandmother's comfort — and more accurate, in its way, than any of her father's careful accession registers. The nation had not died. It had climbed.

And the names. That was the last thing, and in some ways the one that undid her most completely, because names were her trade and she had thought she understood what it meant for a name to be lost. The stream itself — the hill-stream, the shy green water they had moored beside — was called by the villagers the *Vessara*. Sefa had assumed it was a coincidence, some hill syllable-cluster. But no: the oldest families said the name came down from "the river-folk," the people who had come over the mountain in the hard years, generations back, and settled the upper valley, and married in, and been absorbed so thoroughly that only the water's name and a handful of song-words and a stone called grandmother remained. The *Vessara*. A river named for a drowned country, flowing into the Serein, and every map of the region — she had checked, she had checked three atlases on Ivo's chart shelf — called it something else. Something administrative. Something bought.

The maps had one name. The water had another. And the water had been here longer.

* * *

She sat up long past the fire dying, in the corner of Maret's kitchen they had been given, writing by candle stub. Ivo appeared at some point — Sefa never heard them move, a boat-habit, she supposed, moving quiet as a hull through fog — and sat down across from her without asking, and for a while just watched the candle.

"You should sleep," Ivo said.

"I can't. If I sleep I'll lose the exact sounds. The hill vowels are already — they're already drifting in my memory toward the city ones. In a week I'll remember this song wrong. I need it down now, while it's still true."

"That's not how memory works."

"It's exactly how memory works. That's the entire problem. That's why the Compact wins without firing a shot. They don't have to burn anything. They just have to wait. Wait for the vowels to drift. Wait for the refrain to lose its bones. Wait for *Vessara* to become an administrative name on a map nobody reads closely. Erasure isn't an act, Ivo. It's a *decay rate*. And the only thing that beats a decay rate is—" she tapped the notebook, "—this. Copying. Redundancy. My father understood that. A thing exists as many places as it's been copied. One copy is a relic. Two copies is a backup. A hundred copies is a *fact*."

Ivo was quiet a long moment. The candle guttered and steadied.

"When I was a kid," they said, and stopped, and Sefa waited, because she had learned that Ivo's sentences were like the river in low water — you didn't push them, you let them find their depth. "My father used to hum. Working on the boat. I thought it was just — a sound he made. A habit. I never asked what it was." Their jaw worked. "It was one of these. I'd bet the boat on it. One of these songs. And I heard it my whole childhood and I never once asked, because asking would have meant admitting what we were, and what we were was the thing I was trying to stop being."

"You were a child."

"I was a child who sold his route-notes," Ivo said, flat, the old wound opened on purpose, the way they sometimes did it now — not to be forgiven, Sefa understood, but to keep the scar from smoothing over into forgetfulness. "And the Compact learned the pass existed because I wanted a hull of my own. And meanwhile my father was humming Vessian harvest songs over my head the whole time, and I thought it was nothing. It was the opposite of nothing. It was the archive. It was the *whole archive*, in the air, free, and I let it decay right out of me because I wasn't listening." They looked up. "You're listening. That's the difference. That's maybe the only difference there's ever been between a nation that survives and one that doesn't. Somebody listening, and writing it down before the candle goes out."

Sefa didn't know what to say to that, so she did the only honest thing. She turned the notebook around and pushed it across the table, and showed Ivo the page where she had written out the launching song in Vessian script, with the hill pronunciation underneath, with the city pronunciation underneath *that*, three layers deep, like sediment. And beneath the song, in smaller letters, she had written: *Heard at the confluence of the Serein and the Vessara, from the mouth of Toma, fisherman. He does not know what he kept. Now he does.*

Ivo read it twice. When they looked up, their eyes were wet, and they didn't hide it, and that — more than the confession in the tavern, more than the torn contract, more than any of it — was the moment Sefa stopped thinking of them as the man carrying her father's chest and started thinking of them as the other keeper.

"Copy me the numbers song," Ivo said. "The hauling one. *Yin, va, treh.* I want to — I want to have it. In case."

"In case of what?"

"In case I'm the last one who knows it," Ivo said, "and I finally have to be worth something."

* * *

They stayed two days. It was Ivo who insisted — Ivo, who had spent the whole river up to now measuring every stop in hours, in sightlines, in distance-from-pursuit — and Sefa understood why without it being said. The Compact was behind them, buying lockmasters; the pass was ahead of them, closed until calving; and here, in the green seam between two waters, was the one place on the journey where the chest could rest and the keepers could learn what they were keeping *for*.

On the second evening, the village's children came down to the spit to stare at the kestrel-boat — children always did, and Ivo always let them aboard one at a time under supervision, an old captain's indulgence — and Sefa sat on the gravel with six of them and taught them the millstone refrain, the city vowels, the bones in the words. They sang it wrong, gloriously wrong, the way children sing everything wrong, and then one small girl with a cowlick got it right, all of it, the soft *h* and the long *a*, and sang it back to Sefa alone, standing at attention as if reporting for duty.

"Again," Sefa said, and her voice cracked on it. "Once more. I'm writing it down."

That night she added a page to the notebook, and then, on impulse, she took the letter from its oilcloth — the custodian's letter, her father's hand, the stone under her sternum — and read the last line again by candlelight. *Keep it moving, or it becomes a relic, and relics can be bought.*

She had thought it meant the chest. Carry it north, over the Karst, ahead of the snows. But sitting in Maret's kitchen with a notebook full of songs that had already walked — walked for a hundred years, uphill, on their own, without any custodian's permission — she understood the sentence was bigger than the chest. The chest was one copy. The letter was one copy. The song in Maret's mouth was a copy. The stone called grandmother was a copy. The river's true name was a copy. The Compact could buy a chest. It could not buy a refrain that lived in forty hill throats who didn't know what they were guarding.

Her father had built a vault and called it an archive. The villages had built a chorus and never called it anything at all. And the chorus, she was beginning to suspect, was the stronger vessel.

She was still writing when Ivo came in from the night air, moving quiet, and stood in the doorway with a strange stillness she had learned to read as *news*.

"Boat light," Ivo said, low. "Upstream. Running dark, no running lights, hugging the far bank." A pause. "That's not how fishermen come home."

Sefa closed the notebook over her pencil and felt the two days fold themselves up and put away, the way a good archive folds — not lost, just shelved. The songs were down. The vowels were down. Whatever was coming up the river in the dark could not take the refrain out of Maret's mouth, or the numbers out of Toma's, or the name out of the water.

"How long do we have?" she asked.

"Depends whose boat it is," Ivo said, and began, without hurry, without a wasted motion, to get the kestrel-boat ready to run.
