## 15. The Informer

The boat-light came up the far channel the way a rumor comes into a town — slowly, then all at once, and by then everyone already knew.

Ivo killed the galley lamp first, then the binnacle light, cupping a hand around the wick until the flame drank itself out. Sefa was already at the stern line, coiling it wet and silent, her breath fogging in the cold coming off the water. Neither of them spoke. There was nothing left to say that the river hadn't already said. They had two days of songs in a notebook, a chest under the false floor, and maybe ten minutes.

"Backwater past the willow bar," Ivo murmured, more shape than sound. "There's an old log raft sunk there. Enough of it shows to look like wreckage. We lie against it and we're just another dead thing in the reeds."

They drifted, engine unlit, pole in hand, the kestrel-boat sliding on its own momentum like a held breath. The willow bar came up black on black, and Ivo found the sunken raft by feel — a knuckle of old timber brushing the hull, gentle as a hand on the shoulder — and let the boat settle into its shadow. Then they lay flat among the deck cargo and waited.

The other boat came on. It was a workboat, broad-beamed, diesel-hearted, and it ran without lights because whoever steered it had learned the same lessons Ivo had learned, in some other school with the same tuition. It passed within forty meters. Close enough to hear the engine idle down as its pilot swept the near bank with a hooded lantern, close enough to smell the fuel. The beam crossed the willows above them, crossed the reeds below them, and never quite crossed the kestrel-boat, which lay so low in the water it might have been a trick of the current.

Ivo watched the lantern swing and thought, not for the first time, that being invisible was a skill like any other, and that they had spent twenty years getting good at it, and that it had never once made them safe — only delayed.

The workboat idled a long moment at the mouth of the hill-stream where they'd moored two nights running. Where the fires had been. Where Maret had sung. Then it turned, unhurried, and went on upriver toward Tern Lock, and Ivo understood that it hadn't come for them tonight. It had come to *look*. To confirm. Somebody had told the Compact where to start looking, and somebody else wanted to see if the telling was true.

Which meant the telling was recent. Which meant the teller was close.

* * *

His name was Pellas, though everyone on this stretch called him Old Pell, and he had shared their fire three nights ago with a bottle of plum brandy and a laugh like gravel poured slow. He was a pilot of the small waters — the braids and cutoffs between Tern Lock and the confluence, water too shallow for freight, too tricky for strangers — and he had spent an evening drawing channels in the ash with a stick, showing Ivo where the river had moved since any chart was printed. He had liked Ivo. That was the thing Ivo kept turning over afterward, the way you turn over a coin to check for a flaw. He had been *liked*, openly, by a man who fed them stewed pike and told stories about his late wife and asked nothing.

And now a workboat had come sniffing along the bank where they'd slept.

Ivo didn't believe in coincidence on a river. Currents didn't do coincidence. Water found every low place eventually, but it always came from somewhere.

So at first light, while Sefa slept folded into the tarpaulin like a comma, Ivo took the skiff and paddled the half-mile to Pellas's landing — a leaning shed, two punts, nets hung like laundry — and arrived without announcing themselves, which on the river was simply manners. Pellas was awake. Pellas was dressed. Pellas was standing at his window looking downriver with a stillness that did not fit a man whose nets needed mending.

Ivo grounded the skiff and climbed the bank and said, conversationally, "Morning, Pell."

"Morning." And then, before Ivo could ask anything at all: "You should move your boat."

"I know. I'm moving it." A pause, weighted. "Funny thing, though. Someone came looking last night. Ran the channel dark, no lights, right past the willow bar. Swept the bank where we camped." Ivo kept their voice level, almost lazy, the way you keep a knife level. "That's not how fishermen come home either."

Pellas's hands, big and cracked and honest-looking, tightened on the windowsill. For a moment neither of them moved, and the river ran on behind the shed saying nothing, the way rivers do, carrying everything.

"You've known me thirty years," Pellas said finally. His voice had gravel in it and something else now, something finer and worse. "I taught you the cutoffs when you were nobody's captain, just a kid with a stolen dinghy."

"You did. Best instruction I ever got."

"Then you know I wouldn't—"

"I know what I saw, Pell. And I know what I know about men, which is that they don't sell people they hate. Hating's too much work. They sell people they *like*, because liking doesn't cost anything and debt does." Ivo stepped closer, boots quiet on the frozen mud. "Who holds the paper?"

There it was. Not a confession — Pellas was too proud and too frightened for that — but the flinch, the small collapse of the shoulders, the way a man looks at the ground when the ground has become the only safe direction. His grandson. His daughter's boy, nine years old, lungs like wet paper since the fever spring before last, and the Compact had bought the clinic at Harrow Bend the way they bought everything, quietly and completely, and now the medicine came monthly and the bill came monthly and the bill had grown teeth.

"They don't even want money anymore," Pellas said, and his voice broke on the plainness of it. "They want *names*. That's the currency now. You pay in names. One name buys one month." He looked at Ivo with red-rimmed eyes. "One month. That's what my Tam's breath costs. One month."

Ivo stood very still. Below the shed the river slid past, indifferent and gorgeous, doing what it always did, taking what it was given.

They could have done many things. That was the terrible freedom of the moment, and Ivo felt all of it at once, fanned out like a hand of cards: they could break the old man's jaw, burn his shed, put him off his own landing, take the punts, leave a mark that would teach the whole stretch of river what happened to informers. Every hard mile of their life had prepared them for exactly that arithmetic. An eye, a tooth, a lesson. It would even work, probably, for a season. Fear traveled fast on small waters.

Instead Ivo sat down on the woodpile beside him, which was the last thing an avenging ghost would do, and said, "Tell me about the medicine."

* * *

It took an hour, and most of it was Pellas talking because once the wall broke it broke all the way, and Ivo mostly listened, which was its own kind of violence, listening to a man dismantle himself. The clinic at Harrow Bend. The ledger with the family names in one column and the months remaining in another. The courier who came up on the second Tuesday, a polite young man with soft hands who always asked, gently, whether Old Pell had heard anything on the water lately, anything at all, no pressure, sir, we understand families are complicated.

"He never says what he wants," Pellas said. "That's how I knew it was them. Honest thieves ask straight out."

"And Tuesday's tomorrow."

Pellas nodded. His face had gone the color of the mud.

Ivo looked downriver, toward Harrow Bend, and did the arithmetic they were always doing, the arithmetic that had replaced prayer. The Compact knew they were on this stretch. Knew it within a few miles. The workboat last night had confirmed the general area but not the boat — otherwise it would have come back with friends. Which meant the confirmation was due tomorrow, second Tuesday, courier, ledger, one month of a child's breathing purchased with a set of coordinates.

"Here's what happens tomorrow," Ivo said. "You meet your courier. You give him what he asks for. You tell him the kestrel-boat went through Tern Lock four days ago, headed downriver, running light, and that you heard she took water in the reeds below Kilometer Forty and likely broke up. Tell it slow. Tell it bored. Men believe bored."

"That's a lie."

"It's a chart correction," Ivo said. "The river moves. So does the truth." They looked at the old man until he looked back. "But hear the rest, Pell, because this is the part that isn't negotiable. This is the last name you sell. Not because I'll hurt you — I'm not going to hurt you, and I want you to understand that clearly, because it would be easier for both of us if I threatened you and we could go back to pretending the world runs on threats. I'm not going to hurt you because hurting you gets me nothing. But here's what I *will* do. If the Compact comes up this stretch again with guns instead of ledgers, I will make sure every soul on this water knows exactly which landing the coordinates came from. Not me. Them. The lockmasters, the netters, the ferry wives, Dalla's people up the trail — all of them will know that Tam's medicine is paid for with neighbors' blood. And there is no clinic price, no monthly bill, no debt anywhere in the world worth what this river does to a man it has decided to be ashamed of. You'll have your son alive and yourself dead in every way that matters, and you'll live a long time, and it will be awful."

Pellas's hands shook. "You'd do that?"

"I'd hate every minute of it," Ivo said honestly. "And I'd do it. Because mercy that can't be enforced isn't mercy, it's surrender, and I stopped surrendering somewhere back downriver." They stood. "You taught me these cutoffs, Pell. You taught me that the river always keeps a channel open, even in the worst shoals, but you have to respect the marks. Consider this a mark."

The old man sat on his woodpile for a long time after Ivo pushed off in the skiff. From the water, Ivo looked back once and saw him still sitting, small against his leaning shed, a man holding a debt in one hand and a warning in the other and discovering, perhaps, that they weighed nearly the same.

* * *

Sefa was awake when Ivo got back, and had the kettle on the spirit stove, and said nothing for a while, which was her way of asking questions.

"We were given away," Ivo said. "Old Pell. The pilot. Fire, brandy, channel charts, all of it real — and underneath it, a sick grandson and a Compact ledger. He sold us for one month of medicine. Tomorrow he sells them a lie instead, because I asked him to and because I made sure the truth would cost him more."

Sefa poured the tea and handed a tin cup across and studied Ivo's face the way she studied folios, patiently, looking for the marginalia. "You're not angry."

"Oh, I'm angry." Ivo wrapped both hands around the cup. "Just not at him. At the machine that makes a good man's love for his grandson into a weapon against his neighbors. That's the whole Compact in one transaction. They don't buy rivers. They buy the *reasons* people protect each other, and then they wait for everyone to sell everyone, and what's left is a nation of account holders." A swallow of tea, bitter and too hot. "Vane never once pointed a gun at anyone. Remember that. He offers. People sign."

"And you let Pell go."

"With a warning and a job. He'll swear the boat broke up below Forty. By tomorrow evening the Compact's map of this river will have us sinking in the reeds, and we'll be thirty miles past Tern Lock." Ivo said it lightly, but Sefa heard what was underneath, because she always did.

"What?" she asked. "Say the underneath."

Ivo set the cup down and looked at the water for a while. "When I caught him at the window, ready to sell us — for one second, one, I understood him completely. Not forgave. *Understood.* I knew exactly what it was to hold something you love in one hand and someone else's survival in the other and have to choose, and to choose fast, and to spend the rest of your life building a story around whichever one you picked." Their voice stayed steady, which somehow made it worse. "I sold my father's route-notes when I was sixteen for a boat hull. I told myself a story about it for twenty years. And this morning I sat on a woodpile and promised an old man ruin if he ever did what I already did, and he believed me, and the promise was even true, and none of that makes it clean. Mercy with witnesses, Sefa. That's what this is. I used to be able to be decent in private, when decency cost nothing and nobody was keeping score. Now every kindness is a move on a board Vane is watching, and every flaw of mine is a door someone can walk through, and I can't decide if that's the worst thing that's happened to us or the first honest thing."

Sefa refilled the cup without being asked. "Both," she said. "That's usually what it is."

They got the kestrel-boat underway by mid-morning, running the braided shallows above Tern Lock with the sun high enough to read the water — Ivo reading the surface like type, every ripple a word, every smooth tongue of green a sentence — and by dusk they had slipped the lock road entirely and tied up in a hill-stream mouth that appeared on no chart Ivo owned, deep in country where the banks grew steep and the villages kept their faces turned toward the Karst.

That night, at the fire, Sefa brought out the notebook again and read the boat-songs back to Maret's tune, and Ivo listened, and for an hour the fear was just weather passing over something sturdier than fear.

Then, near midnight, a knock came at the rail — a fisherman in a punt, a woman actually, gray-braided, offering smoked trout for kerosene, and she stayed to talk, and she said a strange thing before she paddled off into the dark. She said the high passes had closed early this year. She said her cousin up-valley had sent word down the water, the old way, song to song, village to village: *the snows are coming a moon ahead of schedule, and the mountain is keeping its own calendar now.*

Ivo banked the fire and stood a long time in the dark, listening to the river run down behind them and imagining, ahead, the white silence waiting above the tree line.

Behind them, the lie was working. Ahead, the window was closing. And somewhere between the two, the Compact's couriers were still riding the second-Tuesday boats, paying one month at a time for the names of everyone who had ever been kind.

"Pack for cold," Ivo told Sefa, softly. "We're done letting the river decide our schedule."
