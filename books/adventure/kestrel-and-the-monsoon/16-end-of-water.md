## 16. End of Water

The river began to die three days before they noticed.

It died the way old men die in river towns, Sefa thought — slowly at first, so quietly you could convince yourself it was only resting. The current that had carried them north for weeks, that had argued with the wheel and bullied the rudder and sung under the hull in its gravel voice, began to falter. The water spread. That was the first sign, though she hadn't known to read it: the Serein losing its conviction, pouring itself out sideways into a hundred channels too shallow for shame.

By the fourth morning Ivo was standing at the wheel with a look Sefa had learned to dread — not fear exactly, but the particular stillness of a person listening to something they hoped they were mishearing.

"Braids," Ivo said.

"Is that bad?"

"It's the river giving up." Ivo's hands moved on the wheel, small corrections, the kind that meant they were thinking rather than steering. "Up here the Serein comes down off the Karst in a hundred threads instead of one. In spring, snowmelt ties them back together and you can push a boat through. In autumn—" they nodded at the water ahead, where a gravel bar broke the surface like the spine of something surfacing, "—it unravels. Every year it unravels, and every year it forgets how it was woven."

Sefa watched the channels split around the bar, rejoin, split again. It looked less like a river than like a rumor of one, a story told in too many versions to trust.

"How far can we go?"

Ivo was quiet for a while. The engine muttered beneath them, working harder for less water, the propeller chewing at shallows with a sound like teeth on stone. Gulls stood in the middle of what had been, a month ago, deep channel, ankle-deep and unbothered.

"Past the last confluence," Ivo said finally. "Maybe to the old ferry landing at Tern's end. After that—" They didn't finish it. They didn't need to. Sefa had watched them read water for weeks now, and she knew that when Ivo Crane declined to finish a sentence about a river, the sentence had no kind ending.

The foothills had been growing all week, blue at first, then grey, then close enough that she could see the snow on them — not the white of winter but the dirty, permanent white of glaciers, light that had fallen as snow a hundred years ago and never been allowed to leave. The Karst. She had grown up hearing the word the way other children heard the names of monsters, or gods. *Over the Karst*, her mother used to say, meaning *impossibly far*, meaning *past the edge of what can be carried*. Her father had crossed it every summer of her childhood, carrying boxes she was never allowed to open, and had come back each time a little lighter, as if the mountains took a payment out of him that the river never gave back.

Now the mountains stood in front of her, and behind her, down the whole length of the dying river, the Compact was buying lockmasters.

* * *

They grounded at noon, gently, the way you ground when you've been expecting it. The kestrel-boat shuddered, slid, and settled into a sandbar with a sigh, and Ivo killed the engine, and the silence that followed was the loudest thing Sefa had heard since the night the Compact's launches had swept the reeds behind them.

"End of water," Ivo said. They said it the way other people said the names of the dead.

They waded the last stretch, Ivo carrying the chest on their back with a pack-frame rigged from spare line and a hatch cover, Sefa carrying the folios wrapped in oilcloth against her chest like a child. The water never came above her knees, but it was cold in the way that water off a glacier is cold, and it made her gasp, and Ivo, wading ahead, did not turn around, which was its own kind of courtesy. She had learned that about them. Ivo Crane's kindness was almost entirely a matter of not turning around.

The old ferry landing at Tern's end was a rotted apron of pilings and a shed with half a roof, and standing on the apron, coiling rope with the economy of someone who had done it ten thousand times, was a young woman Sefa recognized from the lock at Tern.

"You," Ivo said.

"Me." The lockmaster's daughter — Sefa remembered now, the girl who had hidden their boat inside her father's own barge lockage, who had stood on the lock wall afterward and not waved. She was perhaps twenty, broad-shouldered, with her hair bound back in a red kerchief and her father's heavy forearms. "Word came upriver faster than you did. Braided water, end of season. I figured you'd ground here or drown here, and you don't look like drowners."

"You walked?"

"Three days. My father's barge is laid up for the winter haul, and someone had to come see." She dropped the coil of rope into a barrel, precise as a signature, and looked at the kestrel-boat sitting in the shallows with her hull scarred from the reed-run, her paint gone grey, her name — *Kestrel* — faded to a ghost on the transom. "She's taken damage."

"She's taken a life," Ivo said.

The girl — her name was Maret, Sefa learned, Maret Osten — looked at the boat for a long time. Sefa watched her look, and understood that she was watching something being appraised in a currency that had nothing to do with money.

"I'll buy her," Maret said.

Ivo went very still. "She's not for sale."

"Everything's for sale. That's the whole lesson of this river, Captain Crane. The Compact taught it to every lockmaster between here and Harrow Bend." Maret's voice was flat, unoffended, stating weather. "The difference is what you sell her *for*. Vane's people would give you a clean name and a numbered account and a boat registered to a dead man in a country that doesn't exist. I'll give you what she's worth in the only coin that matters up here." She nodded at the boat. "Her name. I'll keep her name. I'll keep her hull sound, and her engine turning, and I'll run her every season she'll float, and when people ask whose boat that is, I'll say *that's the Kestrel, and her captain is Ivo Crane*, and I'll say it out loud, in front of whoever's asking, for as long as I have a voice. Can Vane's money do that?"

The wind came down off the foothills and moved the reeds, and the boat rocked against the sandbar, and Sefa saw Ivo's hands open and close at their sides — the hands that had steered her through the reed shallows in the dark with Compact engines hunting them, the hands that had touched the wheel that first night like a man touching a locked door from the inside.

"You don't know what name you're offering to keep," Ivo said quietly.

"I know it's a name that ran medicine upstream in the famine year," Maret said. "My father told me when I was nine. He told me the man who did it never let anyone thank him, so the thanks had to go somewhere, and it went into waiting." She looked at Ivo straight on. "I've been waiting three days. Name your price."

* * *

They hauled the boat that afternoon, all three of them and a borrowed mule, winching her up the slipway behind the ferry shed onto cradles Maret had somehow already arranged — she had come prepared to buy, Sefa realized; she had come with rope and cradles and a bill of sale half-written in her saddlebag, which meant she had come with certainty, which meant somewhere behind her a father had raised a daughter who did not know how to doubt.

Sefa worked the lines and watched Ivo.

She had watched Ivo for weeks now — at the wheel, at the chart table, in the hold with the chest open between them like a wound — and she had catalogued their habits the way she'd been trained to catalogue manuscripts: the way they touched the wheel twice before leaving it, palm and then fingers, like a goodbye said in two parts; the way they never sat with their back to the river; the way they said *she* about the boat and *it* about everything else, as if the boat were the only thing they'd ever permitted themselves to love out loud.

Now Ivo stood in the shallows beside the cradled hull, and did not touch her.

"You could come with us," Sefa said. It came out before she had decided to say it. "Over the pass. We'll need—"

"No."

"Ivo—"

"You've read the folios, Sefa. You've read what my father wrote about the crossing." Ivo's voice was level, and that was how Sefa knew how much it was costing. "Three weeks of calving window. Early snows. Porters who won't take the high route. And a man with a red parka buying every silence between here and the ice. What I'm going to do is stand on this mudflat and sell the only home I've ever had to a stranger, and then walk toward the mountains because the alternative is walking back, and there is no back. There hasn't been a back since Harrow Bend."

"You could have said yes to him," Sefa said. "The records. The clean name."

"Yes." Ivo looked at the boat. "And then my name would be on the paper that erases yours. I've been hiding behind this hull for twenty years, Sefa. Do you know what I learned? A hiding place is just a home you're ashamed of." They put their palm flat against the kestrel-boat's hull, finally, and left it there, and Sefa looked away, because some griefs are not meant to be witnessed at close range.

The bill of sale was written by lantern light in the ferry shed, on the back of a freight manifest, because that was what came to hand. One boat, kestrel-class, name *Kestrel*, hull sound, engine tired, sold to Maret Osten of Tern Lock for a sum of one promise, freely made and to be kept loudly. Maret wrote it out in a careful hand and read it aloud and made Ivo say the words *sold and delivered* and made Sefa witness it, and then she signed it, and then she held the pen out to Ivo, and Ivo looked at it for a long moment.

"I haven't signed my real name to anything in twenty years," Ivo said.

"Then it's time the river saw it again."

Ivo signed. Sefa watched the letters go down — *Ivo Crane*, plain as a fence post, no alias, no false registry, no dead man's name borrowed for the season — and something in the shed seemed to shift, the way a channel shifts when the river finally commits to one course.

* * *

They slept that night aboard the boat, on the cradles, because Maret said she'd have it no other way — one last night aboard, since the river had given them no other farewell. Sefa lay in the narrow bunk that had been hers since Kilometer Forty and listened to the water move against the hull, thin now, a hundred small voices instead of one deep one.

In the morning Ivo was at the wheel.

The boat wasn't going anywhere. The engine was cold, the river was a rumor, the wheel was bolted to a deck sitting on timber cradles forty meters from the waterline. But Ivo stood there with both hands on it, in the grey light before sunrise, and Sefa stood in the companionway and did not announce herself, because she had read enough of Ivo Crane by now to know that this was a letter being written that no one would ever send.

Ivo's hands moved. Small corrections. The kind that meant thinking. The wheel turned a degree to port, held, came back — reading a current that wasn't there, a channel that had braided away years ago, a river that existed now only in the muscle memory of a person who had spent a lifetime letting water do their remembering for them. Sefa understood, standing in the doorway with her breath held, that Ivo was not saying goodbye to a boat. They were saying goodbye to the person the boat had let them be — the captain with no name, no nation, no past, nothing to deliver and no one to be. The boat had been a hiding place, and it had been a home, and the terrible truth that Ivo was touching with both hands was that these had never been two things.

The sun came up over the Karst and lit the snowfields red, and Ivo's hands went still on the wheel.

Then, palm and then fingers — the two-part goodbye — they let go.

They stepped down off the wheelhouse without looking back, and their eyes were dry and their face was terrible and calm, and when they saw Sefa they said, in a voice with nothing left over in it:

"Get your pack. Dalla's mules leave for the high pasture at noon, and I want to be a mile gone before anyone who sold us a bed can describe us twice."

Sefa shouldered the folios. At the top of the slipway she stopped, once, and looked back — at the kestrel-boat in her cradles with the sunrise on her scarred hull, and at Maret Osten already kneeling at the transom with a brush and a small pot of paint, renewing the ghost of the name, letter by careful letter, *Kestrel*, in full view of the whole empty river, keeping a promise out loud where anyone could hear it.

Then the trail bent toward the mountains, and the water was behind them, and ahead there was only stone, and snow, and whatever her father had carried up it every summer, lighter each time, as if paying a debt the mountains never finished collecting.
