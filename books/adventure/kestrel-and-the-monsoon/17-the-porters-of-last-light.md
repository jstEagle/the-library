## 17. The Porters of Last Light

The town announced itself by smell before sight: woodsmoke and wet wool and the sour-sweet reek of mule dung steaming in the cold, all of it carried down on a wind that had crossed a hundred kilometers of stone and remembered nothing but how to be cold. Ivo smelled Last Light an hour before they saw it, standing at the prow of the hired cart while Sefa slept against the luggage, and the smell did something to them that no map ever had. It smelled like the far end of water. It smelled like the place where rivers gave up.

They came down out of the foothills as the light was doing what the town was named for — going long and amber and final across the valley floor, gilding the slate roofs, setting every window burning like a lamp lit for someone expected. The town sat where the last tributary of the Serein bent north and died in a fan of gravel, and beyond it the Karst Range stood up out of the earth like the hull of something enormous that had run aground in deep time and never been refloated. Even from this distance Ivo could see the glaciers hanging in its high valleys, pale and patient, the way debts hang over a family.

"Snow's already down to four thousand meters," the cart driver said, jerking his chin at the white line stitched across the mountainsides. "Early. Earliest I've seen it." He said it the way men say things they've said before, without looking at his passengers, and Ivo understood that he'd been told not to look at them, or paid enough not to remember them, or both. The Compact's money moved ahead of them like dye through water. You couldn't see it anymore, but you could taste it in everything.

Sefa woke when the cart stopped. She woke the way she always did — instantly, completely, one hand already inside her coat where the register rode flat against her ribs — and her eyes went to the mountains first, then to the town, then to Ivo, reading their face the way she read folios: quickly, thoroughly, missing nothing.

"Well," she said. "It's real."

"It was always real. That was the problem."

* * *

Last Light was built for endings. Everything about its architecture admitted it: the wide stable-yards sized for caravans disbanding rather than forming, the chandleries that sold rope and crampons and grief in equal measure, the taverns with names like The Far Bank and The Last Crossing. It was a town where people arrived with too much and left with less, where porters drank their wages in one night because tomorrow the mountain might take the rest. Ivo had passed through once, twenty years ago, in another life, with another name sewn into their collar, and had sworn never to come back. But oaths made at nineteen are written in water, and here they were.

They took rooms at a tavern called The Weight of It, which struck Ivo as either very honest or very cruel, and by the time the bags were up Sefa had already spread the route-notes across the bed in their oilcloth sleeves, weighting the corners with candlesticks, her finger tracing the father's hand-drawn contours.

"The pass is here." Her nail tapped a notch in the high ridge, west-facing, marked with a symbol Ivo had learned to dread: three short strokes beneath a triangle. "Your father wrote *opens with the calving* beside it. And Dalla—"

"Dalla hasn't agreed to anything yet."

"She will." Sefa said it without bravado, which was worse. She said it the way she said things she'd decided were true and would now simply wait for the world to catch up. "Her mother engraved the astrolabe, Ivo. That's not a coincidence. There aren't enough of those left in the world for that to be a coincidence."

Ivo looked at the chart instead of answering. Their father's hand was small and exact, every contour line drawn with the patience of a man who knew his drawings might be the only version of these mountains to survive him. *Opens with the calving.* Three weeks, if the glacier kept its old calendar. Less, if the early snows meant what the cart driver thought they meant. And between here and the pass lay the high pasture, the tree line, the ice — country Ivo had never walked, guided by charts drawn by a man they had spent thirty years trying not to become.

"We need porters," Ivo said. "Six, maybe eight. Mules if we can get them, though Vane's probably bought every animal within two days' walk."

"Then we hire the people the animals belong to."

"That's what I'm afraid of."

* * *

The hiring happened in the stable-yard of The Weight of It, because that was where hiring happened in Last Light, and Ivo learned within the first hour exactly how much the Compact's money had already done.

They started with names Sefa had gathered from the route-notes' margins — families the elder Crane had trusted, families who'd carried his boxes over the range summer after summer, back when carrying Vessian paper was a crime no one bothered to prosecute because officially there was no such thing as Vessian paper. Some of those names still lived in the town. Ivo found two of them by noon.

Maret Osten ran the rope-walk at the edge of town, a broad woman with forearms like mooring lines and a face that gave away nothing except, briefly, everything, when Ivo said their father's name. She set down the hank of cordage she was splicing, wiped her hands on her apron with great deliberation, and looked at Ivo for a long moment.

"You're the Crane child," she said. Not a question. "He talked about you. Once. Said you could read water like scripture and people like a closed book, and that you'd inherited the wrong talent first." She picked the cordage back up. "I don't go high anymore. My knees went, and my nerve went after them. Ask me for anything in this valley and it's yours. Ask me for the pass and you're asking a drowned woman to swim."

"And the others? The families in the notes?"

Maret's splicing hands paused again, just a beat. "Ask them yourself," she said. "But I'll tell you what I tell everyone who comes through here buying silence or selling it: this year, everybody's counting somebody else's money before they count their own."

It was the same at the farrier's, at the granary, at the house of the old schoolteacher whose grandfather had carried the archive's first boxes. Everyone remembered the Cranes. Everyone owed the Cranes something, or claimed to, or had a story about the quiet man with the river voice who'd moved their grandmothers' letters through the mountains in flour sacks. And everyone refused the high route with the same gentle, immovable courtesy, the way you refuse a dying relative's request — with love, and absolutely.

By late afternoon Ivo understood the shape of it. It wasn't fear of the Compact, though the Compact was everywhere in the town's hesitations, in the way conversations thinned when strangers approached, in the shuttered windows of the assay office where Vane's agents had registered themselves like a plague with paperwork. It was the season itself. The glacier pass opened during calving season — three weeks, perhaps less — and the snows had come early, and every guide in Last Light had spent their whole life learning the arithmetic of the mountain: that the difference between a window and a grave was measured in days, and that no wage was worth betting your children's inheritance on a glacier's punctuality.

"They're right," Sefa said that evening, in the room with the charts. She wasn't arguing; that was the strange part. She was agreeing, and it frightened Ivo more than refusal would have. "If the snows hold, waiting is correct. If the snows close early—"

"Then the pass closes with us on the wrong side of it, and Vane walks in behind us whenever he likes."

"He won't walk in. He'll buy in. He's already bought the town." She said it flatly, without accusation, and Ivo felt the truth of it like a hull taking water below the line. That afternoon, the innkeeper had quietly returned half their payment. The farrier had suddenly found no time until spring. A boy who'd been following them all day with open curiosity had been pulled indoors by his ear by a mother who wouldn't meet anyone's eyes. The town hadn't turned hostile. It had turned careful, which was worse, because carelessness you could forgive.

Downstairs, the common room filled with the evening crowd, and Ivo went down among them because sitting still felt like sinking. They bought a round for the porters' table — the men and women who lived on the margin between the valley and the ice, thick-necked, weather-cracked, hands like tree roots — and listened to the talk flow around them. Prices. Snowlines. A wedding in the spring, someone's brother lost to a crevasse two years back and mourned now the way you mourn weather, as fact. And underneath it all, threaded through every conversation like a dark current under bright water: the strangers at the assay office, the money moving through town like meltwater through gravel, finding every crack.

A woman at the end of the table was watching Ivo. Old, straight-backed, brown as a fence post, with a knitted cap pulled low and eyes the color of the ice they'd all grown up beneath. She didn't drink the wine Ivo had bought. She held it, warming her hands, and watched, and finally she stood, crossed the room, and sat down across from Ivo without asking, the way mountains arrive.

"You're looking for porters," she said. Her voice was smoke and gravel. "Everyone in this room knows it. Everyone in this room has already decided to say no, and everyone in this room is wondering why you haven't asked me yet, since I'm the only one left who goes above the pasture."

Ivo kept their voice level. "And do you?"

"I go where I decide to go." She turned the cup once. "You're the Crane child. The one who sold the route-notes."

The words landed like a boathook. Around them the table noise went on, but Ivo heard nothing else for a moment. Sefa had told no one. Which meant the broker's ledger had traveled faster than they had, or Maret had talked, or the town simply knew — towns like this knew things the way rivers knew channels, without being told.

"Yes," Ivo said. There was nowhere left to hide it, and hiding had gotten expensive. "That was me."

The old woman nodded slowly, as if confirming a measurement. "My mother engraved instruments," she said. "Astrolabes, mostly. For people crossing the ice who needed to know where they were when there was nothing left to name where they were." She reached into her coat and set something on the table between them, and the room seemed to tilt.

It was a rubbing — charcoal on cloth — of an engraving Ivo had slept beside for weeks. The star-chart from the base of the astrolabe, every line of it, reproduced in smudged black and grey with the tenderness of someone who had made rubbings of it many times, over many years, from memory or from a sister instrument or from the original itself.

"You know it," she said. Not a question either. Nothing she said was a question; questions were for people who could afford uncertainty.

"It's in my hold," Ivo said. "In a chest. Along with—" They stopped, recalibrated, and decided, in the space of one breath, to stop lying in this town if nowhere else. "Along with an archive that belongs to more people than I can count, and a letter addressed to my dead father, and a set of charts of the high pass drawn in his hand. And I need to get all of it over the Karst before the snows close, and every guide in Last Light has just told me to wait for spring."

"Spring is a lie the valley tells itself," the woman said. "The pass opens when the glacier calves. Three weeks, if the ice keeps its grandmother's calendar. Two, if the early snows mean what I think they mean." She pushed back from the table and stood, unfolding upward, taller than Ivo had guessed, straight as a serac. "My name is Dalla. My mother made your astrolabe, or its twin, and I have been waiting forty years for someone to carry it back up the mountain."

"Why?" Ivo asked, and hated how young their own voice sounded.

Dalla looked at them for a long moment, and something in her face — some grief so old it had become architecture — shifted just enough to let light through.

"Because the Compact bought my valley's name off the maps when I was a girl," she said, "and I have spent my whole life learning that the only thing they cannot buy is what people carry on their backs up impossible ground. Get your chest ready, Crane child. We leave when I say, not when you do." She paused at the edge of the firelight, and added, almost as an afterthought, in a tone that made Ivo's blood move:

"And pray the ice keeps its calendar. Because this year, I think it's keeping yours."

Behind her, at the bar, a man in a grey traveling coat who had not been there an hour ago set down his glass, unhurried, and slipped out into the dark toward the assay office.
