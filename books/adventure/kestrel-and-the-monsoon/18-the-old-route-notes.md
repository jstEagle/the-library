## 18. The Old Route-Notes

The lamp in the back room of the Last Light waystation burned tallow that smelled faintly of mutton, and Sefa had come to associate that smell with the deepest hours of her life — the hours when the world was reduced to a table, a light, and a dead man's handwriting.

She had been at the folios for four nights now. The chest held thirty-one of them, wrapped in waxed linen gone stiff as bark, and each one was a small fortress of a dead language's conventions: Vessian archival script, the formal hand used by custodians for records meant to outlive their writers. Her father had written his personal letters in it too, when he didn't want her mother reading over his shoulder, and so Sefa had learned it at eleven years old as a kind of espionage — a daughter decoding her own household. She had never imagined the skill would one day be the only tool she owned worth anything.

Ivo slept in the next room, or lay awake pretending to. She could hear them turning over on the narrow cot, the old springs complaining in a rhythm she'd begun to know the way she knew river sounds — creak, pause, creak — a current with its own grammar. They had not spoken much since the confession in the tavern. Something had shifted between them, not repaired but re-set, like a bone that would heal crooked if it healed at all. She stayed anyway. She was no longer entirely sure whether she stayed to guard the chest or to guard Ivo, and the not-knowing sat in her chest like a stone she'd decided to carry rather than put down.

Tonight she worked on folio nineteen.

It was different from the others. The first eighteen were inventories — meticulous lists of what the archive contained, itemized in the custodian's hand: *one register of births, eastern parishes; three maps, cadastral; correspondence of the Ministry of Waterways, sealed; songbooks, annotated, provenance uncertain.* A nation folded into paper. But folio nineteen opened flat to a page that made her breath catch, because it wasn't an inventory at all.

It was a map. Hand-drawn, ink faded to the brown of dried blood, on paper that had been folded and refolded so many times the creases had gone soft as cloth.

*The Karst,* she realized. The Karst Range, rendered in a draftsman's confident strokes — but not the Karst as any living map showed it. This was the range from above and below at once, the ridgelines drawn in firm black and then, beneath them, in a paler wash of blue-gray, the ice itself: the glacier that filled the high pass, drawn in cross-section like a river seen through its own banks.

Her father — no. Not her father. *Their* father. Crane's father. The man whose name she carried now in her mouth like a coin she hadn't decided to spend. He had drawn this. She could feel him in the line-work, the same economy she'd seen in the letters, the refusal to waste a stroke. And in the corner, in the cramped marginal hand she was coming to think of as his speaking voice, a note:

*The pass is not a place. It is a season. Whoever reads this: you do not cross the glacier. You wait until the glacier agrees.*

Sefa read it three times. Then she did what her father had taught her to do with any document that mattered: she stopped reading and started *interrogating*. What was the paper? Rag stock, Vessian mill watermark still faintly visible under the lamp — pre-exile, so drawn before the drowning, or from stock carried out before it. What was the ink? Iron gall, standard, but mixed with something blue — glacial flour, maybe, ground ice-rock, a cartographer's vanity or a signal: *this line is water.* Where had it been folded? Into eighths, pocket-sized, carried often. This was not an archive document. This was a working tool. This was a map someone had walked with.

She smoothed it flat again and began, carefully, to trace the route.

The high pasture came first — a long ascending sweep marked with contour lines and, beside them, numbers in the old Vesian counting system, which she had to work through twice before she understood they were *paces*. Distances measured in footsteps. Someone had counted their way up this mountain and trusted the count enough to write it down. Then the tree line, marked with a small symbol she recognized from other folios: a star with six points, the Vessian wayfinding star, the one carved into doorposts and boat prows and, if the stories were true, into the lintels of chapels where no chapel should have been.

Then the ice.

Here the drawing changed character. The upper glacier was rendered lovingly, almost tenderly — seracs drawn as jagged teeth, crevasses hatched in warning-blue, and along the safe line a dotted path threaded between hazards with the confidence of a road. Beside certain points, tiny annotations in the marginal hand:

*Crevasses bridge by midsummer. Do not trust bridges after first thaw-water.*
*The tongue calves on the old calendar — ninth week after solstice. The mountain keeps our year even if the maps do not.*
*Meltwater runs beneath, loud. If the water goes quiet, get off the ice. Quiet water under ice means the channel has moved, and moved channels mean new holes.*

Sefa sat back. Her eyes stung. She had expected, if she was honest, to find treasure-maps — the romantic nonsense the Compact presumably imagined, vaults and gold. Instead she found a father writing survival notes to whoever came after him, in the voice of a man who had crossed this ice many times and expected others to follow. *The mountain keeps our year even if the maps do not.* She pressed her palm flat against the page, as if she could feel warmth left in it, and hated herself a little for the sentimentality, and did it anyway.

It was on the third tracing of the route that she saw the branch.

It was easy to miss — deliberately so, she suspected. Below the main dotted line, where the glacier's cross-section deepened toward its heart, there was a cluster of hatch marks that she had taken for shading, texture, the draftsman filling empty space. But the spacing was wrong. Shading drifts; these marks repeated. She fetched the lamp closer, tilted the page against the light, and saw it: beneath the hatch pattern ran a second set of lines, drawn in that blue-tinted ink, forming a corridor. A tunnel. It branched off the main route at a point marked with the six-pointed star, descended beneath the ice — *beneath*, through the blue body of the glacier itself — and ran laterally for what the pace-counts suggested was nearly two kilometers before rising again to rejoin the surface beyond the worst of the icefall.

In the margin, in letters smaller than any others on the page, almost apologetic:

*The meltwater cut this chamber centuries ago and abandoned it. The ice closed over it and forgot. The Compact's surveyors will not find it because they map what the light shows them. We have always been better at mapping what the dark keeps.*

Below that, one more line, and Sefa's throat closed around it:

*If you are reading this because you are running — go under. The ice hides its children.*

She did not realize she was crying until a tear hit the page and she snatched it up, horrified, blotting the salt water away with her sleeve before it could touch the iron-gall lines. Some part of her brain, the archivist's part, catalogued the moment even as it happened: *conservation note — reader wept on folio nineteen, night four, damage negligible, cause human.*

*The ice hides its children.*

She thought of her grandmother minting coins in a kitchen that smelled of cardamom and fear, pressing a nation's face into metal small enough to sew into a hem. She thought of the villagers downriver singing boat-songs in half-remembered Vessian, words worn smooth as river stones, meaning half-lost but rhythm intact. She thought of the chapel graffiti Dalla had told them about, pilgrims' names scratched into stone centuries deep. Everywhere the nation had drowned, it had also *gone underground* — into songs, into coins, into names, into ice. And here was a man who had spent his whole life walking that hidden country, drawing its maps, leaving signs for anyone patient enough to read them.

And the Compact — she thought of the ledger, *Vessia: pending final delivery* — the Compact mapped what the light showed them. They bought registries and chart-houses and lockmasters. They bought surfaces. But they had never learned to read a fold-pattern, a watermark, a counting-system that survived only in lullabies. Their money was powerful precisely because it was shallow. It could buy everything except depth.

The inheritance was not a chest. It had never been a chest. The chest was just the mailbag, and the mail was this: a way through. Literally. A road under the ice that no buyer's map showed, kept alive in a dead man's hand, waiting — how many years? — for someone to need it.

Footsteps. The floorboard outside the room that always announced Ivo — heavier on the left side, she'd noticed, some old injury she'd never asked about. The door opened without a knock, which by now was its own kind of knock.

"You're still up." Ivo stood in the doorway in their coat, hair pressed flat on one side from the cot, holding two cups of the waystation's terrible chicory. "I could hear you turning pages. You turn pages like you're interrogating them."

"That's exactly what I'm doing." She took the cup. Her voice came out rougher than she intended, and she cleared her throat. "Sit down. I found something."

Ivo lowered themselves onto the bench across the table, and she watched their face as she spread folio nineteen flat between the cups, weighting the corners with a spoon and a brass button. She watched the recognition move through them — the quick professional scan of a river pilot reading water, finding the current, finding the hazard, finding the line through.

"That's the Karst," Ivo said quietly. "That's the high pass."

"Your father drew it. Pace-counts, calving seasons, crevasse bridges, all of it. He crossed it dozens of times — the shipwright was right about that, and this proves it." She turned the page toward the lamp, angled so the blue lines caught the light. "But look here. Under the main route. Under the icefall."

Ivo leaned in. She watched their lips move slightly, following the hatch marks, and then stop moving.

"That's not shading," they said.

"No. It's a tunnel. Meltwater-cut, abandoned, sealed over by the ice. It branches below the star marker and comes back up past the icefall — it bypasses the most dangerous stretch of the whole crossing." She tapped the marginal note. "He wrote instructions. Read this one."

Ivo read. She watched them read it twice, the way she had, and she watched something happen in their face that she had never seen there before — not on the river, not during the night run, not even at Tern Lock when the lockmaster's daughter had hidden them. Their jaw worked. When they finally spoke, their voice was low and uneven.

"*The ice hides its children.*" They looked up. "He wrote that for us. Before I was born, maybe. He wrote that for—" They stopped. Pressed the heel of their hand against one eye. "I sold his route-notes, Sefa. I sold everything I could carry, and I never once looked at what I was selling. And the whole time, this existed. A way through. He built a road under a glacier for people he'd never meet."

"He built it for everyone," Sefa said. "That's what a custodian does. That's what the letter said — remember? *Not a keeper of things; a keeper of access.*"

"I remember." Ivo laughed, a short broken sound. "I thought inheriting the chest meant inheriting his judgment of me. All these years I assumed if I ever found out what he really was, it would be a verdict." They traced the blue tunnel-line with one finger, careful, not quite touching the ink. "It's not a verdict. It's a map. He left me a map instead of a sentence."

"And not just you." Sefa pulled the folio gently back and began, with the practiced motions of her training, wrapping it in its waxed linen. "Think about what this means, Ivo. Vane's expedition will be using Compact surveys — bought charts, bought guides, the surface route. Every guide in Last Light refuses the high route this time of year, so Vane will either wait for calving season like everyone else or push early and die. Either way, he maps what the light shows him." She tied the linen cord. "We don't have to outrun him across the ice. We can go where his money can't follow. Under it."

For a long moment Ivo said nothing. The lamp guttered, and Sefa trimmed the wick without looking up from her work, and outside the wind worried at the shutters the way it does in towns built at the edge of weather.

"There's a problem," Ivo said at last.

"Name it."

"The window. Dalla says the pass opens during calving — three weeks, maybe less if the snows come early, and everyone says they're coming early. Your father's own notes say the tongue doesn't open until the ninth week after solstice." Ivo turned their cup slowly on the table. "Vane's already sending scouts up-valley. We heard the mule-buyers today. If he moves before the window—"

"Then we hold the route-notes and let him exhaust himself on the surface," Sefa said. "Or we find the entrance early and wait inside. Your father counted paces, Ivo. He marked the star. Men who draw maps like this don't leave the door unmarked." She heard the certainty in her own voice and recognized it — it was her father's certainty, the archivist's faith that a thing properly recorded cannot truly be lost. She had inherited that too, sideways, through a chest that was never hers. "Hope with teeth," she said aloud, not quite meaning to.

"What?"

"Nothing. An old saying. My father used to say grief is just hope with the teeth pulled." She smiled, tired. "This is the other kind."

Ivo looked at her across the lamplight for a long moment — and she saw something settle in their face, some final piece of the decision they'd been circling since the tavern. When they spoke, it was quiet and plain.

"All right. Tomorrow we take the folios to Dalla. If anyone alive knows that star-marker on the ground, it's her family — her mother engraved the astrolabe, her people walked this route before your father did. And we start asking about snow conditions, quietly, the way you ask about water." They stood, took both empty cups, and paused at the door. "Sefa. Thank you. For reading him properly. I wouldn't have known how."

After the door closed, Sefa sat alone with the wrapped folios stacked in front of her, thirty-one small fortresses, and allowed herself — briefly, the way you allow yourself one breath before a dive — to imagine the crossing. Blue walls overhead. Meltwater roaring somewhere below, and above it, walking in the dark that kept them, the ghosts of everyone who had ever counted their paces up that mountain so that others could follow.

Then she blew out the lamp, and in the sudden dark she heard, distantly, through the shutter cracks, the sound of hooves on the frozen street below — many horses, arriving late, moving with the particular unhurried confidence of men who had already bought the town.

* * *

Morning confirmed it. From the waystation's upper window Sefa watched riders in city wool pick their way down Last Light's single street, and watched doors close ahead of them one by one, like a tide of silence going out. At the far end of the street, in front of the stable, a familiar figure in a gray coat dismounted and handed his reins to a boy without looking at the boy, looking instead — slowly, thoroughly — at the buildings, as though reading them.

Aldous Vane had arrived. And somewhere in the chest below her hands, wrapped in waxed linen, lay the only map in the world he couldn't buy.
