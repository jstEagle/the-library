## 19. Vane Buys the Town

The trouble with Last Light, Ivo thought on the third morning after their arrival, was that it was a town built for being left.

It sat where the river's memory ended and the mountain's began — a scatter of slate roofs and drying-timbers strung along the last navigable reach of the Serein, the kind of place cartographers drew as a smudge if they drew it at all. The name came from an old ferryman's joke: this is where you catch your last light before the dark of the high country. The town had made a modest industry of endings. It sold rope to climbers, grain to porters, beds to the grieving and the greedy alike, and it asked no questions, because questions were bad for trade. Ivo had moored here twice in twenty years of running water, and both times had felt the particular comfort of a place that did not want to know your name.

That comfort had lasted four days. On the fifth, Vane's expedition came up the valley road.

Ivo heard it before seeing it — not engines, which would have been easy to hate, but bells. Mule-bells, dozens of them, a bright idiot music rolling down from the pass road while the morning fog still lay in the streets like spilled milk. From the window of the room above the Ferryman's Rest — the best room, because the innkeeper, Marta Osei, had given it to them at half rate and refused to discuss why — Ivo watched the column come down into town: forty mules under pack, twelve hired men in matching oilskins, two sledges of expedition gear, and at the head of it all, riding a gray mule with the posture of a man attending his own investiture, Aldous Vane.

He looked exactly as he had at Harrow Bend. That was the obscene thing about him. The same soft hands, the same courteous half-smile, the same traveling coat that probably cost more than the kestrel-boat. He had chased them three hundred miles upriver and overland through the worst autumn in a decade, and he arrived looking like a man who had merely stepped out to post a letter.

Beside Ivo, Sefa stood very still at the window. "Forty mules," she said quietly. "There aren't forty mules in the whole valley."

"There are now," Ivo said. "He brought his own."

* * *

By noon, the buying had begun.

Ivo watched it happen from the tavern bench by the window, over a cup of tea gone cold, and understood within the first hour that they were watching something more dangerous than a search party. This was not a hunt. Hunts were loud, and hunts could be outrun. This was an acquisition.

Vane did not go door to door himself. He sent his men, and they were good at it — polite, patient, carrying leather folios instead of weapons. By mid-afternoon Ivo had counted six transactions through the tavern's fogged glass. The farrier sold his entire string of spare mules at triple market and shook the buyer's hand like a man who'd won something. The granary master leased his loft for storage. Two porters who had turned Ivo and Sefa away only yesterday — *the snows are early, girl, nobody crosses now* — signed on with the Compact at wages that made their eyes shine, and came out of the negotiation wearing new boots.

And then there were the silences.

That was the part Ivo hadn't anticipated, though they should have. A man named Corrag — one of Vane's lieutenants, a broad, gray-templed Basque with a notary's manner — went from house to house with a different kind of paper. Ivo watched him sit with old Yannec Brac, the ferry pilot who had shared their fire on the second night and told stories about the drowned villages upstream until midnight. They talked for twenty minutes. When Corrag left, Yannec stood in his doorway a long time, looking at the coin purse in his hand the way a man looks at a fish he has decided not to eat but cannot bring himself to throw back.

The next morning, when Ivo passed him on the quay, Yannec looked at the water.

* * *

"You should understand what he's actually doing," Sefa said that evening. She had spent the day doing what she did best — listening. She'd bought bread, mended nets with the fisherwomen, sat with children on the church steps. Invisibility through usefulness; Ivo recognized the technique because it was their own. "It's not bribery. Bribery is crude. What he's buying is *permission to forget you*."

"Town's forgetting me fine on its own," Ivo said. "Always has."

"No." She set down her cup. "Towns forget strangers. This is different. Yesterday, Marta gave us the good room. Today she changed our linens without speaking to either of us. Yesterday, the boy Toma followed me around asking about the boat. Today his mother called him inside when I passed. Nobody has threatened anyone. Nobody has been rude. That's the horror of it, Ivo. He hasn't bought their hatred. He's bought their *neutrality*, and neutrality is all a hunter needs. You can hide from enemies. You can't hide from people who've agreed not to see you."

Ivo thought of the sandbar that appeared on no chart, and how the river had known things about them before they knew them themselves. Towns were just slow rivers, maybe. Currents of obligation, running every which way, and Vane had simply dammed them all at once.

"How much is a silence worth?" Ivo asked.

"In this valley? Less than you'd hope." Sefa's mouth twisted. "His son's medical debt bought Yannec's silence, I heard. Forty crowns. My grandmother minted coins worth more than that, and her nation fit in a chest."

* * *

On the sixth day, Vane came to find them personally.

Ivo saw him crossing the square in the blue hour before dusk, alone, unarmed, coat collar up against the wind, and made the deliberate choice not to run. Running in a small town was a confession. Instead they stayed on the tavern bench, back to the wall, tea going cold again, and let him approach.

"Captain Crane." Vane stopped at a respectful distance, hands visible, folded. "Miss Sefa. May I?"

"There's a chair," Ivo said.

He took it as if it were an honor. Up close he smelled of wool and cold air and nothing else — a man so controlled he didn't even permit himself a scent. "I wanted to say, before anything else, that I admire the crossing you made. The reed channels below Harrow Bend — my pilots still argue about whether they're navigable at autumn draft. You ran them dark, at night, with a gashed hull. That's seamanship."

"It was fleeing."

"Fleeing done well is indistinguishable from mastery." He smiled faintly. "I'm not here to threaten you. I never threaten. I'm here because I believe in being honest about arithmetic. My expedition is fully provisioned. I have forty mules, twelve men, glacier guides out of the eastern valleys, and a route survey purchased from a broker in Harrow Bend some years ago — you may be familiar with its provenance." His eyes didn't flicker, but the words landed like a hand on an old scar. "You have a chest, a letter, and a young woman's conviction. Winter is eleven days early. The pass will not open for three weeks, if it opens at all. When it does, my party will be first through, rested and fed, and yours will be whatever is left after you've begged porters no one will lend you. That is the arithmetic. I don't need to take anything from you. Time is taking it for me."

"And yet you crossed a country to tell me this in person."

"Because I wanted to ask you something no courier could ask properly." Vane leaned forward slightly, and for a moment the courtesy dropped away and Ivo saw the thing underneath, which was not cruelty exactly but a terrible, patient appetite. "Your father carried those boxes over the Karst every summer for fifteen years. Every summer, Captain. Do you know what that means? It means the archive was *meant* to move. It means custody is a journey, not a possession. I am offering to complete your father's work — to give the archive a permanent home, climate-controlled, catalogued, insured, visited by scholars. Everything your father wanted, except one detail." He spread his soft hands. "Ownership. And ownership, Captain, is the only thing that keeps memory alive. Unowned things rot. Ask any museum."

"Ask any village," Sefa said quietly, "and you'll get a different answer."

Vane inclined his head to her, genuinely courteous. "Miss Sefa. Your father indexed nine thousand folios in exile. I have read his catalogue. It's beautiful work. He would have understood what I'm offering better than anyone." 

Something moved behind Sefa's face — grief, fury, and beneath them both a horrible flicker of temptation, quickly mastered — and Ivo understood that Vane had chosen his words with a scalpel. He rose, buttoned his coat.

"The town has been kind to you," he said. "I'd ask you to notice, over the next few days, how long kindness lasts once it has a price. Good evening."

He left coins on the table for the tea. Neither of them touched them.

* * *

The days that followed taught Ivo what attention cost.

It was not menace. It was worse than menace — it was geometry. Everywhere Ivo walked, space opened. The fishmonger, who had joked with them about the season's poor catch, suddenly had business at the back of his stall. Children who had trailed them through the lanes were called indoors by mothers who wouldn't meet their eyes. At the Ferryman's Rest, Marta served their meals promptly, perfectly, and with a careful blankness that hurt worse than any eviction notice, because it was the blankness of a woman obeying a rule she hated.

Only once did the mask slip. Ivo came down late at night for water and found Marta standing in the dark kitchen, and before she could arrange her face she said, low and fast, "High pasture. Dalla Ferreol. Above the tree line, past the shrine — ask for her at the summer hut, but *ask for nothing*, do you understand? Ask for nothing." Then she filled the pitcher, handed it over, and became an innkeeper again, and neither of them ever spoke of it.

Ivo stood in the stairwell holding the pitcher a long time, understanding that they had just been paid — not in coin, in risk. Somewhere in this town being hollowed out around them, someone had decided the Compact couldn't buy everything. It was the first warmth in days, and it frightened them more than Vane's arithmetic, because warmth was a debt too, and debts got collected.

* * *

On the ninth day, the porters' answer came, and it was no.

Not formally. Formally, the trail-head guild simply posted its winter rates — figures so absurd they were a refusal wearing a suit — and then closed the hiring book "pending weather." Ivo stood in front of the guild board reading the notice twice, feeling the trap close exactly as Vane had promised. No porter would sign. No guide would take the high route even if paid, because the snows were early and the pass was shut, and every honest road out of Last Light now belonged, in practice, to the man with forty mules.

Sefa found them at the board. She was pale, and she wasn't looking at the notice; she was looking at the hills above the town, where the first real snow of the year had dusted the high shoulders white overnight.

"Marta talked to Dalla's granddaughter at the wash-line," she said. "Dalla will hear us out. But Ivo — the high pasture is forbidden ground. Her own people won't go up there. If she says no—"

"If she says no, we wait three weeks in a town that's forgotten how to see us, and race Vane's rested column through a pass that may never open." Ivo looked at the white shoulders of the Karst, at the light already dying early off the peaks, and felt the old river instinct stir — the one that said *when the channel closes, find the channel no one charts*. "Then we'd better make sure she doesn't say no."

They climbed toward the summer huts as the last light went off the valley — the town's own light, the light it was named for — and behind them Last Light settled into its evening, every window warm, every window, Ivo noted, carefully unwatching.

Above the tree line, in the blue cold, a single lamp burned in a stone hut, and an old woman was waiting, though no one had told her to expect them.

"I know why you've come," Dalla Ferreol said, before either of them could speak. Her eyes went past their faces, past the packs, and fixed on the brass shape wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of Sefa's rucksack, as if she could see through canvas and decades both. "And before you say a word — show me the astrolabe."
