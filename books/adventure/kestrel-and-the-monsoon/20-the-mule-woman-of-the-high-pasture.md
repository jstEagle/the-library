## 20. The Mule-Woman of the High Pasture

The rain came sideways off the Karst foothills, and Last Light smelled of wet wool, dung smoke, and the particular sourness of a town that had already begun to sell itself. Sefa felt it in the way the innkeeper's eyes slid past her now, in the mule-traders who quoted prices and then withdrew them with a small apologetic shrug that meant *someone has paid me not to deal with you*. Vane's money moved through a place like dye through water. You couldn't see it anymore, but everything was stained.

She sat in the taproom of the Wayfarer's Rest with the chest between her boots and her hands wrapped around a clay mug of barley tea she wasn't drinking, watching Ivo fail for the fourth time that day.

The porter across the table was a broad man named Hesk, with forearms like mooring posts and a face that had once been friendly. He'd shared bread with them two nights ago. Now he turned his hat in his hands and looked at the floorboards.

"I'm sorry," he said. "Truly. But there are men at the trailhead asking after anyone who hires out above the tree line. They pay a season's wage for a name."

"They're paying you right now to say no," Ivo said. Not cruelly. Just accurately.

Hesk's jaw worked. "Aye."

"Then take it," Sefa said, and pushed three coppers across the table anyway, because she would not be the kind of person who let a man betray her for free. "For your trouble."

Hesk looked at the coins like they were something dead, took them, and left without meeting their eyes again.

Ivo leaned back against the wall and rubbed their face with both hands. In the lamplight they looked older than the river had ever made them look — hollowed out, the way driftwood is hollowed, by weather and by what the weather takes away. "That's four," they said. "Four guides. Every one of them knows the high route exists. Every one of them says the same thing: the pass doesn't open until calving season, three weeks yet, and the snows are coming early this year. The low route is Compact country end to end."

"So we wait three weeks."

"We don't have three weeks." Ivo said it quietly, which was worse than shouting. "Vane bought every bed in this town except ours. That's not hospitality, that's a net. He's not trying to catch us on the mountain. He's trying to make sure we can't get on it at all. And when the snows close in properly, he won't even need to chase us. He'll just wait for us to starve politely in an inn he owns."

Sefa looked down at the chest. It sat between her feet the way it had sat through everything since the sandbar — patient, lead-lined, heavier than its weight. Her father had spent eleven years of his life assembling what was inside it, cataloguing folios by hand in a basement in a city that no longer appeared on maps, and then he had handed it to a riverboat smuggler and died believing it would reach someone. She had spent the whole journey telling herself the same thing Ivo told themselves: *keep moving, keep it hidden, deliver it whole.* But delivery required a road, and the road was being purchased out from under them coin by coin.

"There has to be someone," she said. "There's always someone the money hasn't reached."

"Everyone can be reached. That's the whole point of the Compact. That's their entire philosophy. They don't conquer places, they *acquire* them." Ivo turned the mug a slow quarter-turn on the table. "My father used to say—"

They stopped. Sefa waited. It still cost Ivo something, every time, to speak of him without flinching.

"My father used to say you can't buy a person who doesn't consider themselves for sale. I used to think that was sentiment. Now I think it was instructions."

* * *

They found Dalla the next morning, or perhaps Dalla found them; afterward Sefa could never quite decide which, and eventually concluded that the distinction didn't matter, because Dalla was not a woman who permitted things to happen to her.

She was in the livestock market at the muddy bottom of the town, where the porters gathered before dawn with their animals, and she was easy to pick out from the hired men because she was old and alone and utterly unbothered. Seventy, maybe more. Small as a folded tent, wrapped in a coat that had been patched so many times the original cloth existed only as a theory. She stood beside a single gray mule with a face like a disapproving aunt, and she watched Ivo negotiate with a fifth guide, watch that negotiation die, and then she laughed.

It was not a kind laugh. It was the laugh of someone who has watched the same bad performance many times and still finds one detail of it fresh.

"You," she said, when Ivo turned around. Her voice was gravel over ice. "You're the ones everyone's been paid not to see. The boat people."

"And you've been paid too?" Ivo asked.

"I've been offered." She said it the way another woman might say *I've been insulted*. "Meridian men. Three days ago. Very polite. Very clean boots. They wanted to know if I'd guide for them over the high pasture when the pass opens." Her eyes were pale gray, the color of glacier melt, and they went from Ivo to Sefa to the chest slung under Sefa's oilcloth and back again. "I told them no old woman crosses ice for money. Which is true. I cross ice for other reasons."

Sefa's heart did something complicated. "What reasons?"

Dalla ignored her. She was looking at Ivo with an expression of professional assessment, the way a lockmaster looks at a hull. "You want the high route. Everyone wants the high route. No one will take you because the snows are early and the calving is late and any guide worth their rope knows the middle weeks are death. So you'll find some fool, or you'll go alone, and you'll die in a whiteout at four thousand meters, and the Meridian men will collect your bones in spring along with whatever's in that box." She said it all flatly, a weather report. Then: "Show me what's in the box."

"No," Ivo said.

"Dalla," Sefa started—

"The box or nothing. I don't carry strangers' secrets up my mother's mountain for free, and I don't carry them blind either. Show me."

"It's not your mother's mountain," Ivo said.

Something moved in the old woman's face then — not anger, something older and stranger, the look of a door opening onto a room no one had entered in decades. "Isn't it," she said, and it wasn't a question, and somehow it ended the argument before it began.

They took her up to the room because there was nowhere else private, and Sefa unstrapped the chest with hands that weren't quite steady, and lifted out the astrolabe.

She had handled it a dozen times by now. Brass gone green-black at the joints, rings nested within rings, the rete cut into a lacework of stars and pointer-arms worn smooth by a century of thumbs. On the mater, engraved around the rim in a script Sefa could read but had never heard spoken aloud, ran a line of Vessian: *the sky does not emigrate.*

Dalla took it the way you take a newborn. Both hands. Absolutely still.

The silence in the room went on so long that Sefa could hear the rain changing its mind against the shutters.

"Turn it over," Dalla said at last. Her voice had changed. All the gravel had fallen out of it and left something bare underneath.

Sefa turned it over. On the base, hidden until you knew to look, was a maker's mark: a small bird, wings spread, worked into the brass with a graver's finest tool — and beneath the bird, a date, and beneath the date, a name half worn away.

"*Mira*," Dalla said. "Mira of the High Pasture. My mother."

The rain filled the pause.

"She made eleven of them," Dalla went on, and now she was talking less to them than to the object, turning it in the light, finding with her thumb the wear-marks of other hands. "Eleven astrolabes, the winter the Compact first came buying. She sold her loom, her goats, her wedding silver, and she commissioned these from a metalworker in the valley who owed her a debt, and she gave them to eleven families and told them: *when they take the maps, when they take the names off the registers, when your children grow up thinking they come from nowhere — hold this to the sky, and it will tell you where you are from.* Because a map can be bought." She looked up, and her pale eyes fixed on Sefa with a force that made the younger woman's breath catch. "But the sky was here before the Compact and it will be here after. The sky cannot be acquired."

"That line," Sefa whispered. "On the rim. *The sky does not emigrate.*"

"Mira's saying. She put it on every piece she ever made." Dalla set the astrolabe down on the table with exaggerated gentleness, as if setting down something sleeping. "Where did you get this?"

So Sefa told her. Not all of it — not the sandbar, not yet, that was Ivo's to tell or withhold — but enough. A nation erased. An archive carried out in secret. A custodian named Crane who ferried folios upriver for years under cargo manifests, and a daughter of the archivist who had spent her life learning to forge papers so she could one day follow the papers home. She spoke of her father's basement and the wax seals and the register of families, and while she spoke, Dalla listened the way stone listens, motionless, and twice her hand went to the astrolabe and rested there.

When Sefa finished, Dalla was quiet a long moment. Then she said, to Ivo rather than to Sefa:

"Your father. The custodian. Did he ever come over the range?"

Ivo went very still. "Once a summer," they said finally. "When I was young. He told my mother he was running salt."

"He was running your chest's cousins," Dalla said. "Boxes. Sealed, lead-lined, heavy as sin. I carried two of them myself, the summer I was twenty-five, up through the frozen chapel and down the eastern side. He paid in medicine that year — famine year, the valley below Tern Lock remembers it still." She shrugged inside her patchwork coat. "I never knew what was in them. I never asked. That was the arrangement. We carried, and we didn't ask, and that was how small things survived large times."

Sefa watched Ivo absorb this — watched them stand there holding the knowledge that their father's secret routes had been walked by other feet, witnessed by other eyes, kept by other silences. Twenty years of shame and hiding, and all that time the father had never been alone in it. There had been a web. Faint, frayed, mostly dead now — but a web.

"You knew him," Ivo said. It came out almost accusatory.

"I knew a careful man with a river accent who overpaid porters and thanked them in their own language," Dalla said. "I knew him forty years ago. If I'd known his child would come needing the high pasture, I'd have—" She stopped herself, waved the sentence away. "Doesn't matter. Here's what matters."

She laid one knotted finger on the astrolabe.

"My mother made this so that people who lost their country could still find their way. Eleven families. Some of those lines have died out. Some are scattered east of the range, in villages that don't know what they are. And one line," and here her voice did something Sefa hadn't heard it do before, something almost soft, "is standing in front of me."

"I'm not—"

"Don't." Dalla's tone cracked like a whip, and the mule in the yard below shifted at the sound. "Don't tell me what you're not. I've watched the Meridian men work this town for a week. They buy names, they buy silences, they buy the space around a thing until the thing itself forgets it exists. That's how nations die — not with soldiers, with *receipts*." She picked the astrolabe up and pressed it back into Sefa's hands, closing the girl's fingers around the cold brass. "And you walk in here carrying my mother's work and a chest full of everything they're trying to erase, and you ask me if you're one of mine. Child. You are the most *one-of-mine* thing to come through Last Light in forty years."

Sefa hadn't realized she was crying until the brass blurred. She wiped her face angrily with her sleeve. "We need a guide. The pass opens in three weeks, the snows are early, every guide in town has been bought—"

"Every guide in town is a man under sixty who thinks a mountain is a job," Dalla said. "I am not a guide. I am the last of the high-pasture women. My grandmother crossed in a whiteout with a baby on her back. My mother crossed eleven times. I have crossed more times than I will admit to you, and I know the glacier the way your captain knows the Serein — including the weeks it sleeps and the week it wakes." She looked at Ivo. "Calving season. Your charts say the tongue opens then?"

Ivo blinked. "How did you—"

"Because whoever drew those charts learned them from us. From the pasture women. My mother traded route-knowledge for safe passage more than once, and she always said the river-man wrote everything down." Something flickered across her face — grief, maybe, for the dead custodian, arriving forty years late. "Yes. The pass opens at calving. Three weeks, if the snows hold off. Earlier, if they don't. And yes, the middle weeks are deadly, and yes, I'm seventy-one years old, and yes, I will take you."

"Why?" Ivo asked. Flat. Blunt. The question of a person who has been lied to about kindness too many times to accept it at face value.

Dalla smiled, and it transformed her face into something ancient and amused, a face carved on a chapel lintel.

"Because they offered me money," she said, "and my mother taught me that anything the Compact offers to buy, you must therefore give away free. That's the only arithmetic they can't do." She jerked her chin at the astrolabe in Sefa's hands. "Besides. Mira made that for a family that needed finding. Forty years I've wondered which family got number seven. Now I know, and now I'm going to walk them home over my mother's mountain, and when the Meridian men ask why, I'll tell them exactly what I told them before: no old woman crosses ice for money."

She was already moving toward the door, gathering her coat, whistling sharply for the gray mule.

"Be at the north trailhead tomorrow, an hour before light," she said over her shoulder. "Bring the chest, bring the charts, bring food for eight days and rope for six. And captain—" she paused in the doorway, and the rain stood behind her like a curtain, "—bring your humility. The mountain will take it from you anyway. Better to arrive having packed it yourself."

Then she was gone, and the taproom noise rushed back in around the space she'd left, and Sefa stood holding the astrolabe against her sternum with both hands, feeling it cool slowly toward the temperature of the room, feeling something else warm slowly toward the temperature of hope.

Ivo exhaled, long and slow, and sat down heavily.

"A pasture woman," they murmured. "He never mentioned— no. Of course he never mentioned." They looked up, and for the first time since Harrow Bend there was something in their face besides dread. "Three weeks. If the snows hold."

Sefa turned the astrolabe over one more time. The little engraved bird. Wings spread. Flying, though it was made of metal and had never moved.

Outside, far down the valley toward the trailhead, she thought she could hear mules — many mules, organized mules, the sound of an expedition provisioning. Vane was buying the mountain's doorstep while they slept.

Three weeks, she thought. Let the snows hold.

Let them hold just long enough.
