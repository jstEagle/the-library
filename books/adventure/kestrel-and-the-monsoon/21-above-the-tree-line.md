## 21. Above the Tree Line

The first thing Ivo learned about the mountain was that it did not care how good a captain they were.

That lesson arrived on the morning of the second day, delivered without malice, the way the river delivered its own lessons — suddenly, and at the exact moment confidence became expensive. They had been climbing since before dawn out of the high pasture where Dalla kept her mules, following a track that was less a path than an argument between goats and gravity, and Ivo had begun, somewhere in the second hour, to feel competent again. The pack sat right on their shoulders. Their breath found its rhythm. The ground rose steadily but honestly beneath their boots, stone after stone, and stone was something you could read. Stone held still.

Then the track crossed the first snowfield.

It was only thirty paces wide, a white tongue lying in the shade of a rib of rock, and Dalla crossed it without breaking stride, her mules stepping onto the crust as if it were a floor she had swept that morning. Sefa followed, arms out slightly for balance, face set in the careful blankness of someone refusing to admit fear. Ivo went last, and halfway across, the mountain reached up and took their ankles.

There was no warning. No sound. One step planted firm; the next slid forward under them like a deck plate gone slick with fish oil, and Ivo's whole weight swung sideways, arms windmilling, the chest-straps of their pack wrenching against shoulders already raw from three days of carrying everything they owned and several things they didn't. They caught themselves on an ice axe they were only beginning to learn to hold, blade biting crust, and hung there over the slope with their heart going like a bilge pump in flood, looking down forty feet of hardening snow at a runnel of rocks that would have caught them the way a weir catches driftwood.

Dalla did not even turn around.

"Snow is not ice," she said, to the mule ahead of her, or possibly to the mountain. "Ice holds what it takes. Snow gives you back — if you ask politely."

Ivo lay against the axe a moment longer, breathing, feeling the cold come up through their gloves into the bones of their hands. On the river, a slip like that would have been nothing. On the river, their body knew things their mind had never been taught: how a current leaned into a bend, how a hull spoke through the wheel before anything went visibly wrong, how the water two hundred yards downstream was already written in the boil and seam of the water under the bow. Twenty years of reading the Serein had sunk so deep into them that they had stopped thinking of it as a skill. It was simply what their hands were.

Their hands knew nothing here. That was the truth of it, and the truth did not get easier on the third day, or the fourth. It got worse, because the higher they climbed, the more the mountain began to look like water — and the more it looked like water, the more confidently wrong Ivo's instincts became.

* * *

They made camp that night in a hollow below the tree line's last ragged krummholz, the pines there twisted knee-high by wind, growing sideways like river grass bent by current. Dalla built the fire small and shielded, behind a wall of stone she assembled with the unhurried economy of a woman who had done ten thousand small necessary things in weather that wanted her dead. The mules stood hipshot in a row, dozing, their breath steaming. Sefa was asleep almost before her head found the pack-roll, exhausted in the way of someone whose body had finally accepted that the climb was real.

Ivo sat up past their turn at watch, which was a captain's habit and a bad one, watching the sky.

The storm had been chasing them since the pasture. They had all seen it — a gray wall building along the western ranges each afternoon, towering, dissolving, rebuilding itself taller, like a lock gate holding back a season. Dalla read it every evening with a glance and a grunt. "Tomorrow it will be behind us," she said the first night. "Day after, beside us." She was never wrong, and she never explained, and this began to gnaw at Ivo more than the storm itself did. On the river, weather was something you outran or endured, and either way it came from somewhere you could name. Here the storm seemed to have intentions. It seemed to be following them specifically, patient as a creditor.

On the third day it drew level.

They were crossing a broad shoulder of scree and old snow below a ridge the charts called the Shepherd's Comb when the light changed. Not darkened — changed, thinned, the way light goes greenish in deep water. Ivo felt it before they understood it, a pressure behind the ears, and looked up to see the western sky gone the color of wet slate, the storm front standing over the next valley like a wave that had decided not to break yet out of professional courtesy.

"Move," Dalla said.

They moved. But the mountain, unlike the river, did not reward speed. The scree shifted underfoot with every stride, each rock announcing its neighbors' intentions too late, and Ivo — who could have threaded a flooded channel at night by feel alone — found themselves stumbling like a landsman on a heaving deck, twice falling to one knee, once going down hard enough to skin a palm through a torn glove. Sefa hauled them up the second time without a word, gripping their forearm, and the look she gave them was not unkind, which somehow made it worse. Pity from a fellow passenger was bearable. Pity from someone who trusted you to steer was not.

They sheltered in a fold of the ridge while the storm's edge passed over — not the heart of it, just a shoulder of wind and stinging spindrift, half an hour of white noise and zero visibility — and when it cleared, Dalla made them sit, and made tea, and then said the thing she had evidently been waiting three days to say.

"You keep walking like the ground is trying to drown you."

Ivo stared at her.

"I've carried freight over this range thirty-one summers," Dalla said. "Rivermen, caravan men, soldiers, priests. Every one of you walks the same way at first. You walk like the mountain is a river that has stopped moving, and you're waiting for it to start again." She handed Ivo the tin cup. Her hands were enormous, cracked leather around the knuckles, steady as cleats. "The mountain isn't stopped. It moves all the time. Just slower than you're used to. Slower than your eyes believe."

"Then how do you read it?" Ivo asked. It was not a proud question. Three days had burned most of the pride off.

Dalla considered them over the steam of her own cup. Whatever she saw — and Ivo suspected she saw a great deal, the way old pilots see the whole channel in one glance — she apparently decided was worth teaching.

"The same way you read your river," she said. "You just never had words for it because the words are different." She set down the cup, took up a stick, and drew in the ash at the fire's edge. "You told me on the pasture that you can feel a shoal before you see it. Through the wheel. Through the hull."

"Yes."

"Why?"

Ivo thought about it honestly, which took longer than expected. "Because the water tells me what's underneath it. A shoal slows the current over it. The surface piles and smooths. The boat feels the change in the push before my eyes find the boil."

"So," Dalla said, and tapped the stick against the ash-drawing, where she had scratched a long slope with wavy lines running down it. "Snow is a river. It flows. Slowly — inches in a season, sometimes feet in a day — but it flows, downhill, always, the way your water does. And where it flows fastest, and where it drags, and where it pools — all of that is written on the surface, if you know what smooth means and what rough means." She tapped the top of the slope. "Here, near the crest, the wind strips the snow away. Hard, bare, blue-white, glittering. Wind-carved. Safe-ish. Like gravel bar." The stick moved downslope. "Middle of the pitch, the snow lies deepest and softest. Look for it to go matte. Dead. No sparkle. That's powder sitting on nothing. That's your sandbar that wasn't there." Ivo flinched; she noticed, and did not comment, which was its own kind of courtesy. "And here—" the stick reached the bottom of the drawn slope, "—where the snow comes out in tongues and lobes, bulging, cracked like drying mud, that's where it's moving fast enough to tear itself. Glacier ice. Never trust it. Like a weir. Pretty from upstream."

Ivo looked from the drawing to the actual slope above them, and felt something rearrange in their chest — the specific vertigo of a familiar sense waking up in an unfamiliar body. Because now that she had said it, they could see it. The pitch above camp wore its history openly: the wind-scoured crest glittering hard and honest, the mid-slope lying flat and featureless and matte as poured milk, the lower runnels bulging and crazed with tension cracks fanning out like the seams of an old hull. It was a chart. The whole mountainside was a chart, drawn in a language they had spoken for twenty years without knowing the mountain spoke it too.

"Read the snow like water," Dalla said, gathering her stick. "That's all any of it is. Water that learned patience."

* * *

The humility took longer than the lesson.

Knowing how to read a slope and trusting yourself to read it were separated by exactly the distance a drowning man trusts a rope he tied himself, and for the next two days Ivo walked that distance over and over. In the mornings, fresh and rested, they would pick their line down a snowfield with genuine skill — testing the crust with the axe spike, cutting steps on the steep pitches, choosing the wind-carved spine over the matte hollow every time, and feeling, briefly, like a captain again. By afternoon, tired, cold, altitude pressing on them like a hand on the stern, the old doubt would come flooding back. They would second-guess a line Dalla had already approved. They would stop in the middle of a safe stretch because some texture of the surface reminded them of a boil, and stand there sweating inside their parka while Sefa waited patiently ten steps ahead, until Dalla said, mildly, "You are standing on gravel bar. Walk."

The worst hour came late on the fourth afternoon, on a traverse beneath a hanging glacier — a tongue of ancient ice plastered to the flank of the peak above, blue-green in its depths, dripping meltwater in long silver threads that froze again a few feet down the rock. The route-notes — Ivo's father's route-notes, the ones Sefa had deciphered in the trail-town — marked this section with a single line in cramped pencil: *Keep close under the wall. The ice above sheds at midday. Do not linger.* Which meant they had to cross directly beneath several tons of frozen history during precisely the hours when it liked to let go.

Dalla went first with the lead mule, quick and quiet. Sefa followed. Ivo came last, and partway across, the mountain gave its demonstration.

It was not dramatic. There was no roar. A serac the size of a wagon calved off the tongue above with a sound like a door closing in another room, and a few hundred pounds of ice came down the gully fifty yards behind them, hissing, scattering, arriving at the track as a drift of shards and slush that would have broken a leg and ruined a week. The mules flicked an ear. Dalla said nothing. Sefa turned and looked at Ivo with wide eyes and then kept walking, because stopping was the one thing you did not do under a shedding glacier.

But Ivo stopped. For four full seconds — an eternity, on that slope — they stood rooted, staring up at the blue tongue, doing the thing their body had done ten thousand times on the river when danger passed close: going still, taking the bearing, logging the hazard. And in those four seconds they understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water down the collar, that the instinct was useless here. On the Serein, marking a hazard was enough; the boat moved on and the hazard stayed where hazards lived, in the water, behind you. Here the hazard moved. The hazard was time. The hazard was the sun climbing toward midday, warming the ice, loosening the welds that had held since before Vessia drowned. Standing still to study it was not caution. It was volunteering.

"Sefa's right," Dalla said, when they caught up at the far end of the traverse. She hadn't looked back. She never needed to. "Under ice, you don't watch. You count your steps and you arrive."

"I froze," Ivo said. It cost something to say it aloud.

"You froze like a man listening for a bell that doesn't ring on this mountain." Dalla adjusted a lash on the lead mule's load, tugging the rope tight with two efficient pulls. "Your river will be there when you go home. It hasn't forgotten you. Let it rest." She paused, then added, quieter, in the tone she saved for things she actually meant: "Right now you are a passenger. Let the mountain carry you. All you have to do is not argue with it."

A passenger. The word went through Ivo like a splice through a line. They had not been a passenger anywhere since they were fifteen years old and took the wheel of their father's boat for the first time, and even then it had felt less like being given something than like taking it. Twenty years of steering — away from the name Crane, away from Vessia, away from every map that remembered either — and now an old woman with engraved-metal hands was telling them that survival meant letting go of the tiller, and meaning it as a kindness.

They walked on. Somewhere in the next mile, without deciding to, they stopped fighting it. They put their feet where the snow told them to put their feet. They counted steps under the ice. They let Dalla choose the campsites and Sefa carry the route-notes and the mountain do the steering, and discovered, to their considerable surprise, that nothing terrible happened — that the world continued to turn while they were not gripping it — and that the climbing got easier, not harder, the moment they admitted it wasn't theirs.

* * *

The fifth evening, the storm they had been outrunning for three days finally caught them properly, and Dalla did not try to outrun it back. She led them instead into a shallow cave beneath an overhang of gneiss, banked the mules hip-to-hip in the dry, and announced that they would stay until it passed, because "the mountain gives you weather the way your river gives you locks. You wait your turn. Nobody argues with a lock."

They waited. The storm arrived at dusk as a white curtain marching up the valley, ate the peaks, ate the ridgelines, ate the last gray light, and then spent the night throwing itself against the overhang like a customer locked out of a tavern. Inside, it was almost cozy — fire small, bodies close, tea endless, Sefa reading the route-notes aloud by lamplight in her clear archivist's voice, translating her father's colleague's cramped Vessian shorthand into plain speech while the wind tried to shred the world outside. Ivo listened the way they used to listen to other pilots talk shop in riverside bars: hungry, envious, aware of how much they didn't know.

It was near midnight, Ivo on second watch, when the storm paused — one of those sudden inexplicable lulls, the whole gale setting down its burden at once — and the clouds tore open overhead.

The stars came out all at once, ferocious with altitude, and Ivo stepped out of the cave mouth to look at them and forgot, entirely, to be afraid.

Because the sky was doing the thing. The thing the river did. The Milky Way arched overhead in a great slow bend, dense and luminous, and its current ran — there was no other word for it — ran from northeast to southwest, curving, dividing, braiding around dark rifts of dust the way the Serein braided around islands in the low-water season. Star-streams. Dark channels. A confluence low in the south where the glow thickened and pooled like water backing up behind a dam. Ivo stood in the windless cold with their breath smoking and their hands forgetting the cold, and read the sky the way they read the river at night, by feel, by shape, by the grammar of flow — and understood that Dalla was right, that it was all one thing, that the mountain and the river and the sky were the same water moving at three different speeds, and that their father had known this, had walked this pass every summer carrying boxes of a drowned country's memory, reading snow like water the whole way, and had never once thought to tell them.

Or perhaps had tried, Ivo thought, standing very still under the streaming stars. Perhaps had tried, and been talking to a teenager who was already saving up to buy a boat and leave.

Behind them, in the cave, Sefa slept with the astrolabe tucked against her ribs like a child sleeps with a doll. Dalla slept the way stone sleeps. The mules breathed. The storm gathered itself beyond the overhang for the next assault.

And far to the west, down the valley they had climbed out of, Ivo thought they could see — though it might have been imagination, though it might have been a village burning oil — a string of lights, small and orderly and wrong, moving up the trailhead road in single file, patient as a towline.

Vane was on the mountain.

Ivo went back inside, woke Dalla with two fingers pressed to her bedroll, and told her what they'd seen. The old woman listened, looked out at the lights for a long moment, and grunted — the same grunt she gave for weather, for scree, for everything the mountain intended to do whether anyone approved or not.

"They'll be slow," she said. "Rich men climb badly." Then she began saddling the mules in the dark.
