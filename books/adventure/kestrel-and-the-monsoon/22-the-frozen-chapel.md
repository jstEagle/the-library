## 22. The Frozen Chapel

The storm found them an hour after the tree line gave out, as if it had been waiting for the trees to step aside.

Sefa felt it first in her teeth — a pressure change, a hollowness, the way the air went thin and metallic the day her father died. She had learned on this climb to trust the body's older instruments. The astrolabe in her coat pocket was beautiful and precise, but it could not tell her what the ache behind her eyes was telling her now: *the mountain is about to close its hand.*

"Dalla," she called into the wind. "It's coming."

The old woman did not answer with words. She answered the way she answered everything on the ice — with a gesture, economical as a lockmaster's arm, a crook of two mittened fingers toward the western wall of the cirque where the snow lay in a long, unbroken drift against a shoulder of black rock. Then she turned and made a sound Sefa had never heard her make before, a low two-tone whistle, and the mules — the two that were left — swung their heads toward it as if it were a name.

They crossed the cirque leaning into wind that came off the icefield above like water off a weir. Ivo was ahead of her, and Sefa watched the captain's body argue with the ground the way it had argued for three days now — the river instincts reaching for a footing the mountain refused to give, the long balanced stride of a deck breaking down into the shorter, blunter steps Dalla had drilled into them. *Read the snow like water.* Ivo was learning. Sefa could see it in the way they no longer fought the slope but negotiated with it, the way they had begun to place their feet the way Dalla placed hers, heel-first, listening through the boot.

The drift against the rock face was not a drift. Dalla went into it shoulder-first, and snow that had looked like snow turned out to be a crust over a hollow, and the hollow was a doorway, and the doorway had been carved.

Sefa stopped so suddenly that the mule behind her breathed on her neck.

The lintel above the buried door was stone, and the stone was carved with stars.

Not the eight-pointed stars of the Karst valleys, not the compass roses the Compact's surveyors stamped on everything they claimed. These were the seven-rayed stars of Vessia — the ones her grandmother had stitched into a christening cloth, the ones her father had inked into the margins of his catalogues, the ones Sefa had not seen outside of the chest's folios in eleven years. They ran along the lintel in a procession, worn shallow by centuries of weather, and each one was different, and each one was deliberate, and the wind was screaming past her ears but she stood there and let it scream because she could not move.

"Inside," Dalla said, not unkindly, and put a hand between Sefa's shoulders.

The doorway opened into a low passage, and the passage opened into a room, and the room took Sefa's breath in a way the altitude had not managed in three days of climbing.

It was a chapel. She knew that before she knew anything else about it, the way you know water. A waystation chapel, the kind the old trade routes had carried over every pass in the world before the Compact began buying the passes — a shelter for pilgrims and porters, a place to wait out weather, a place to pray or not pray, a place simply to be *inside* while the mountain did what mountains do. It was perhaps eight paces long, barrel-vaulted in stone, with a hearth at the far end and a stone bench along one wall and niches cut into the rock above the bench, most of them empty, a few of them still holding the stubs of candles fused to their shelves by old wax and old cold. Half the ceiling was buried under the drift they had come through; the rest was black stone veined with quartz that caught the light of Dalla's lantern and held it, so that the vault seemed to glow faintly from within, like ice at dawn.

And the walls — the walls were covered in stars.

Sefa turned in a slow circle with the lantern held up and understood that the seven-rayed stars on the lintel were only the beginning, only the public face of the place. Inside, out of the weather, out of the reach of anyone who had never climbed this high, the carving gave way to something else. Scratched into the stone with knives, with nails, with the points of compasses, with the broken tips of ice-axes, in lines that ranged from deep and confident to faint as breath on glass, were hundreds of marks. Stars. Names. Dates. Little boats. Little mountains. A hand here and there that had drawn, with obvious care, the double-curve of the Serein's lower bends. Words in Vessian.

She read them the way a drowning woman reads air.

*Mirola Vess, 1409, the pass was open and my heart was not.*

*For Anja, who waited below.*

*I climbed to see if the sky was different. It is not. It is ours anyway.*

*Seven stars, one boat, all waters.*

*Here stood Toma, son of Toma, son of the drowned city, and the city stood in him.*

The dates went back and back. Fourteen hundreds. Thirteen hundreds. There was a star so deep in the stone that it had been polished smooth by later hands touching it, and beside it a date she had to crouch and hold the lantern to her nose to read: a year that predated the Compact by four hundred years, predated the fall of Vessia by more. People had been climbing to this room and leaving their names in it since before the nation had a name to lose.

"They didn't die," Sefa said. Her voice came out strange, too loud in the stone room, and she heard it crack and did not care. "Dalla. They didn't die. They *climbed*."

Dalla was already at the hearth, methodical as tide, laying the fire she had clearly known would be there to lay — dry split wood stacked in a stone niche, kept by some arrangement of hands that had continued across generations, a chain of strangers keeping wood dry for strangers. "Sit before your legs learn bad habits," she said. "Grief is heavy. Don't carry it standing."

Ivo came in last, shaking the storm off like a dog, and stood a moment in the doorway with the wind at their back, looking at the walls. Sefa watched them read the same words she had read, watched the lantern-light move across their face, watched something happen there that she had seen only once before — in the hold of the kestrel-boat, the night the letter was open between them and neither of them had spoken for a long time. The look of a person finding a room in a house they thought had burned down.

"The route-notes said a waystation at the cirque's west wall," Ivo said. "A shelter. That's all it said. A shelter."

"Your father knew what it was." Dalla struck flint to steel, and sparks bloomed and died and bloomed again. "He carried boxes over this pass every summer of his life. You think he didn't know every stone with a star on it between the river and the ice?"

"He never said."

"He never said a great many things," Dalla said, and the fire caught, and the room filled with light and the smell of resin, and the shadows of the carved stars leapt and settled like birds coming to roost. "That was the trade, in those years. Saying less. But he stopped here. Everyone stopped here. You can see where the porters slept." She nodded at the bench, at the hollow worn into it by centuries of tired bodies. "You can see where they waited out storms just like this one, and got bored, and got frightened, and got lonesome. And every one of them picked up a knife and said *I was here* to a wall that would outlive them."

Sefa set the lantern down and walked the walls the way she had once walked the stacks of her father's archive, slowly, cataloguing.

That was what her hands wanted to do — catalogue. It was the reflex of her whole life, the reflex her father had built into her before she could read: *when you find something, describe it; when you can't keep it, describe it better.* She counted. There were, by her rough count, more than three hundred separate inscriptions. She found dates clustered in patterns — dense years and empty years, and the empty years she could map without trying against the history she knew: the years of the Compact's first acquisitions, the years the roads closed, the years the maps were redrawn and the names scraped off them. And in every empty decade, one or two marks anyway. One or two people who had still climbed. One or two who had still needed to say *I was here* badly enough to do it in secret, in storm, in danger.

There was a gap in the record, she realized, of exactly eleven years.

Eleven years ago, her father had died, and the chest had gone into the river, and the route had gone quiet. She stood in front of the last inscription before the gap and read it three times.

*The archive sleeps in the water. The water remembers better than we do. — E. Sefa, for the Custodian, who carries what I cannot.*

Her own name. Her father's hand. He had stood in this room. He had stood in this room with the chest already gone from him, already given over to a riverboat man he trusted more than he trusted any vault, and he had climbed up here anyway — or stopped here on his way down, one last crossing — and he had written on the wall the truest thing he knew: that the water remembers better than we do. He had not known, then, how literally the river would take him at his word. He could not have known that a sandbar would rise where no sandbar was, that a kestrel-boat would ground on it, that his daughter would one day stand in a frozen chapel reading his handwriting on a wall while a storm sealed the pass behind her.

Or maybe he had known. Maybe that was what custodians were. People who wrote letters to a future they would not see, and trusted strangers to deliver them.

Sefa put her palm flat against the stone, over her father's words, and the stone was cold in a way that felt less like absence and more like patience.

* * *

The storm settled in for the night the way storms do at altitude — not raging so much as *remaining*, a white roar against the buried half of the chapel, the wind finding the one gap in the masonry and playing it like a reed. Dalla fed the fire with a miser's rhythm. The mules stood in the passage, steaming, chewing, content in the way of animals who have decided a place is safe. Ivo sat against the bench with the route-notes open on their knees, though Sefa noticed they had not turned a page in an hour. They were listening to the wind with their whole body, the way they listened to a river at night, reading the storm the way they read current — and she understood that somewhere in that listening was the captain's version of what she was doing, which was counting. Counting the hours the storm would hold them. Counting the hours it bought the men behind them.

She did not let herself think about that. Not yet. Tonight the wall was enough.

She had found, in her circuit of the room, a place where the stone was soft — a seam of paler rock, a flaw in the masonry that generations of carvers had clearly favored, because it was crowded with marks, layered over each other like the paint on a ferry's hull. She knelt in front of it with the lantern and looked for a long time at the empty space at the bottom, the space below all the accumulated centuries, the space that had been waiting.

"You're thinking about it," Ivo said. They had come to stand beside her without her hearing, which on the ice was either a compliment or a warning.

"Everyone here wrote their name."

"Everyone here had a knife."

"I have a knife." She did. Dalla had made her carry one from Last Light onward, a short blunt thing for cutting rope and cheese, and it had felt absurd until now.

Ivo crouched beside her. In the firelight their face was all planes and shadows, and for a moment they looked young, younger than Sefa had ever let herself see them — the age they must have been when they sold their father's route-notes, the age when a person first learns that a name can be a thing you put down and walk away from. "You understand what it means," Ivo said. It was not a question and not quite a warning. It was the voice they used at the wheel when the channel narrowed. "A name on a wall is a claim. The Compact reads claims. Vane would give a year of his life to stand in this room with a camera."

"Then we should be grateful he's a day behind and the storm is a day ahead."

"That's arithmetic, not gratitude."

"It's both." Sefa turned to face them. "Ivo. My whole life I have kept my name in drawers. Forged passes, false papers, my grandmother's coin sewn into a hem because a name that true was worth more hidden than spent. My father taught me that. *Hide it, keep it, carry it.* He was right. It kept me alive." She looked back at the wall, at the three hundred hands, at the eleven-year gap, at the space at the bottom. "But he wrote on this wall anyway. Knowing what the Compact was. Knowing what names cost. He wrote on the wall because some things are not kept by hiding them. Some things are kept by *saying them*, in places like this, where the saying is the keeping."

Ivo was quiet a long moment. The wind played its reed-note in the masonry. "Twenty years," they said at last, "I haven't signed my real name to anything. Not a manifest, not a toll-book, not a letter. You're about to do in thirty seconds what I haven't been able to do in twenty years, and you're going to do it in a room the Compact doesn't know exists, and you're right, and I still want to tell you to wait until the storm passes." A breath that was almost a laugh. "That's what running is. It's always *wait until the storm passes*."

"And then the next storm."

"And then the next storm." Ivo took out their own knife and held it a moment, turning it, and Sefa thought they were going to hand it to her. Instead they reached past her and, low on the wall, in the space below the last of the old marks, they cut two small letters. *I.C.* Shallow. Almost nothing. A mark a stranger would take for a mason's sign, a surveyor's code, anything but a name. But Sefa saw the hand that cut it — the same hand that had written the route-notes, the same disciplined, half-hidden script as the charts in the chest — and she saw that it shook slightly, and that Ivo let it shake, and did not stop.

Then they passed her the knife without a word.

Sefa knelt. The stone was cold through her trousers. She thought about what to write the way she had once thought about what to write in her father's memorial book, and found that the answer was the same, and had always been the same, and was not clever and did not need to be.

She cut her name. *Sefa.* Just that — her own name, the one her grandmother had minted into coin, the one her father had carved on this wall in another form eleven years ago, the one no registry had ever held because registries were things that happened to other people, to nations with maps. Below it she cut the seven-rayed star, and her hand knew its shape without instruction, seven rays from a common heart, the way her grandmother's had known it, the way the hands of three hundred strangers had known it, and the knife went into the soft stone easily, as if the stone had been saving the space.

It took less than a minute. When she sat back, the mark was so small, so shallow, so *little* — a scratch, a child's mischief, nothing beside the deep confident centuries above it — and she found she was crying, quietly, the tears freezing at her jaw, and that the smallness was the whole point. That was the defiance of it. Not a monument. Not a vault. A scratch in a hidden room that said *I was here, we were here, we are still here*, made by a hand that would be dust before the stone noticed. You could not buy that. You could not buy it because it was not for sale, and it was not for sale because it had never belonged to anyone — it belonged to the wall, and the wall belonged to the mountain, and the mountain had been keeping Vessia's calendar longer than any nation had kept its own.

Dalla came and stood behind them, looking at the two new marks, and said nothing for a while. Then: "My mother's star is up there somewhere. Third row, near the hearth. She climbed this pass the summer she was seventeen, with the engraving tools on her back, and she cut her name, and she told me it was the only signature she ever made that no one could take to a bank." The old woman's voice was as level as ever, but her mitten came to rest, very briefly, on the top of Sefa's head, the way you touch a thing you are proud of. "Now there are three Cranes and a Sefa on this wall. The mountain will make something of that. It always does."

Outside, the storm went on. But somewhere beneath the white roar, Sefa heard — or thought she heard, or her bones heard — a sound from higher up the icefield, from the glacier above the cirque. A deep report, muffled by distance and storm, like a door closing in a very large house. Then another. Then a silence with a strange texture to it, a listening silence, the whole night holding its breath.

Ivo's head came up. Sefa saw the river in them answer the mountain — heard them say, softly, in the voice they used for reading water: "It's talking in its sleep. That's not the wind."

Dalla looked at the fire, then at the buried doorway, then at the two new names on the wall, and her face did the thing it did when she was doing arithmetic of her own.

"Sleep while it's only talking," she said. "Come morning, we'll want to know what it's decided."
