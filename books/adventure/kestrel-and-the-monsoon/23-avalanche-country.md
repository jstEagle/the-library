## 23. Avalanche Country

The mountain spoke first in the language Ivo knew best: the sound of water deciding where to go.

They had been climbing since before dawn, a rope of six — Dalla out front with the lead mule, then Sefa, then the two hired porters from Last Light, then Ivo with the chest lashed to their back like a second spine, and young Pell at the rear with the spare loads. The slope above them was a white shoulder of corniced snow, four hundred feet of it, hanging over the trail the way a wave hangs over a reef just before it commits.

Ivo had been listening to it all morning without knowing they were listening. That was the thing about rivers — you learned to hear the water before you saw it move, the low gathering note under the surface noise, the change in pitch when a current found new purchase on the bottom. Snow, Dalla had told them three days ago above the tree line, was only water that had forgotten how to run. Your job was to remind yourself of that before it remembered on its own.

So when the pitch changed — when the morning's silence under their boots acquired a faint, subterranean groan, like a hull taking strain — Ivo's body understood before their mind did.

"Dalla," they said, and it came out as almost nothing, a breath with edges.

But the old woman had already stopped. She had one hand flat against the snow, palm down, the way another person might touch a sleeping child to check for fever, and her head was cocked. Her face did not look afraid. It looked like a face doing arithmetic.

"Off the line," she said. Not loud. There was no time for loud. "Downslope. Now. Cut loose nothing."

Then the shoulder let go.

It did not roar, not at first. That was the horror of it — the first two seconds were almost gentle, a soft slumping sound, powder lifting off the crown of the slope like breath on cold glass. Then the whole face of it came alive, and the gentleness became a freight-train bellow, and the world turned white and sideways.

Ivo threw themselves downslope as Dalla had said, toward the fall line rather than across it, arms over their face, the chest grinding against their spine. The porters scattered like spilled beads. Sefa went down hard and Ivo saw her tumbled twice by the leading edge of spume before the main body of it passed above them — passed *above*, because Dalla's call had put them below the fracture line, in the narrow band of ground the slide would scour but not bury.

The mule was not so lucky.

Ivo heard it — a single braying note of animal terror, cut off — and then the world was thunder and pressure and a dry suffocating weight of snow driving the breath out of their lungs, packing into collar and cuff and ear, pressing their cheek into ice. And then, as suddenly as it had begun, it ended, and there was only the ringing quiet after, and the taste of blood where they'd bitten their lip, and somewhere beneath all of it the slow settling tick of snow finding its final angle of repose.

"Count yourselves," Dalla's voice, hoarse but level, cutting through the white. "Sound off. Hands up if you can."

"Sefa." A gloved hand punched up through the surface twenty feet away, snow streaming off it.

"Pell."

"Bram."

"Hesh."

"Ivo." They dug themselves free, coughing, and stood up into a landscape that no longer existed. The trail was gone. The switchbacks were gone. Where the path had bent around the shoulder there was now a smooth, brutal fan of debris two hundred yards wide, blocks of old ice the size of cottages shoved along like driftwood, the whole mass steaming faintly in the cold where the friction had warmed it.

And at the toe of the fan, half-buried, one brown leg sticking up at an angle that left no room for hope.

* * *

They dug anyway. You dug anyway; that was the rule, and everyone on the mountain knew it even if no one said it. Bram and Hesh shoveled with their hands and their shovels and finally with a section of broken sled runner, and it took forty minutes to reach her, and she was gone long before they reached her — the lead mule, the strongest of their three, the one who had carried the heaviest share of the food because Dalla trusted her footing above anyone's.

Her name had been Marigold. Ivo learned this standing in the pit they'd dug, watching Hesh press his forehead against the animal's frozen muzzle, and learning it felt like being handed a bill.

"She was mine eleven years," Hesh said, not looking at anyone. "Bought her off my brother when he went down to the coast."

"We'll take turns carrying," Dalla said quietly. "Nothing else to be done. Get her pack off."

That was when the arithmetic started, and Ivo felt it begin the way you feel a fever start — a small heat behind the eyes that would not stop building.

Marigold's panniers held the bulk of their provisions: the barley and oil, the dried fish, most of the fuel tablets, the tent canvas. Between the impact and the melt-freeze already setting into the torn snow, perhaps half of it was recoverable. Half. Ivo stood in the pit doing the division while the others worked, and the numbers refused to come out kind.

Six people. Three days' climb to the pass mouth, if the father's charts told true and the calving window opened when it promised. Then the pass itself — the blue ice, the crevasse fields, the branch tunnel — call it four days more, five if weather closed. Eleven days of food at full rations became six at half rations, and half rations on a glacier was not dieting, it was borrowing strength from tomorrow at interest. And behind them—

Behind them was Vane.

* * *

They made camp that night in a hollow below the debris fan, backs to a rock wall, because Dalla said never camp under anything that could let go twice. The wind had come around to the north and it had teeth in it. They ate a third of what they wanted and Ivo sat outside the tent circle on the pretext of checking the ropes, and took out the Compact's map.

They had taken it off one of Vane's advance men in Harrow Bend, weeks ago now, in another life. It was a beautiful object — heavy paper, engraved contour lines, the Karst Range rendered in confident ink. And penciled across it, in a hand Ivo now knew as well as their own heartbeat, were Vane's annotations: distances, campsites, the names of every lockmaster bought between the river and the foothills. Vane planned the way he talked, courteously and completely.

Ivo laid the route-notes beside it — their father's charts, copied in Sefa's careful hand onto waterproofed paper so the originals stayed wrapped in oilcloth with the chest. Two maps of the same mountain. One drawn by a syndicate that erased nations, one drawn by a man who had spent his life carrying what those nations remembered.

The notes said the pass mouth lay beyond a black granite spur the locals called the Anvil, visible from miles off, unmistakable. From tonight's camp, Ivo estimated — and estimation on a mountain was a different craft than estimation on a river, and they were still learning the exchange rate — the Anvil was a day and a half away. Hard climbing. The snows were early this year; Dalla said it every evening now, looking north, saying it the way sailors say a storm is coming, not to inform anyone but to keep the fear moving through her own body instead of pooling.

A day and a half to the pass. And Vane?

Ivo thought back through the last three days. The cairn at the tree line that had been freshly rebuilt — rebuilt, not ancient, the stones still showing unweathered faces. The boot prints in Tuesday's snowfall, a party of eight or ten, roped intervals, professional spacing. The fire-scar at the last camp with coal still warm enough to smell when Bram kicked it apart. Each sign alone meant little. Together they drew a line, and the line ran up the same mountain behind them, and the line was straighter than theirs because Vane's party wasn't stopping to dig out mules or wait on a seventy-year-old porter's knees.

A day behind. Maybe less. Gaining maybe half a day per day, because grief and age and caution slowed their line and money sped his.

Ivo sat with that until the numbers stopped swimming and became simply true. Then they folded both maps away and went and looked at the sky, which was doing something worth looking at: the cloud deck to the north had begun to organize, stacking itself into the long smooth plates Dalla called *sheep-backs*, and underneath them the air had that glassy, held-breath quality Ivo recognized from the river before a flood crest came through. Pressure falling. Weather queuing up behind the pressure like barges behind a towboat.

Snow soon. Heavy snow. Snow that would erase tracks — theirs and Vane's alike — and also erase the trail, and fill the pass mouth, and possibly close the calving window before it opened.

Or open it early. Dalla had said calving was triggered by warmth and water and weight, and a big storm loading the icefields with fresh snow was weight, was water, eventually. The mountain kept its own calendar. Nobody, including the Meridian Compact, had ever successfully bribed it.

* * *

Sefa came out of the tent past midnight. Ivo heard her boots and didn't turn; there were only so many people the boots could belong to.

"You're not sleeping."

"Doing sums."

"Find any that work?"

"Not yet." Ivo shifted on the rock to make room. She sat, pulling her coat tight, and for a while neither of them said anything, and the wind filled the silence with its one long syllable.

"The food," Sefa said finally. "Half of it's under forty feet of snow-concrete. We can't dig it. So we go in short, or we go back, and going back means meeting Vane coming up. Those are the choices."

"That's the arithmetic, yes."

"There's a third thing."

Ivo turned. In the starlight her face was mostly shadow and breath-steam, but he could see the set of her jaw, the particular stubbornness that had gotten them both this far and would probably be the death of them.

"The register," she said. "The chamber cache. My father's letters — the letters *your* father cached. If the pass opens, we deliver them. That's the mission. But the chest—" she nodded back toward the tent, where it sat wrapped in oilcloth, heavier than lead, heavier than grief, "—the chest is the part Vane wants. The folios, the astrolabe, the seals. The part that makes us targets. What if some of it doesn't need to cross the mountain at all?"

Ivo was quiet for a long moment. "You're talking about caching it. Burying it here."

"I'm talking about the difference between what has to reach the other side and what only has to survive. We know how to hide things in this country now. Dalla knows caves. The chapel is a day ahead — stone walls, pilgrim traffic for centuries, nobody looks twice at old boxes in a place like that." Her voice dropped. "Vane can't buy what he can't find. And we can't outrun him carrying everything."

"It's your inheritance too," Ivo said. "More than mine. I won't decide that."

"I know." She looked north, toward the sheep-backed clouds. "I'm not deciding tonight either. I'm telling you the numbers have a third column. Sleep on it."

She went back inside. Ivo stayed on the rock a while longer, and the strange thing — the thing they would remember afterward, in the blue ice, in the years after — was that the fear had changed shape. This morning, under the avalanche, fear had been an animal thing, all lungs and legs. Now it had become what Dalla's face had been on the slope: arithmetic. Cold, divisible, solvable-or-not. A day behind. Half the food. Six mouths. One window. And somewhere ahead, a glacier that opened once a year like a lock gate, keeping a drowned nation's calendar.

On the river, Ivo had survived twenty years by never stopping, never signing, never owning anything the current couldn't carry away. The mountain was teaching the opposite lesson: you could not outrun a storm up here. You chose ground. You dug in. You made yourself into something the weather had to negotiate with.

Below the camp, far down the valley, a light moved. Small, steady, swinging in the patient arc of someone walking with a lantern — too low on the flank to be a village, too deliberate to be a lost shepherd. It vanished behind a ridge, and Ivo counted seconds out of habit, the way you count the interval between lightning and thunder, and got a distance, and the distance was about a day.

They went and woke Dalla.

The old woman listened with her eyes shut, which was how she listened to everything important. When Ivo finished — the light, the interval, the arithmetic — she was silent for the length of three breaths.

"Tomorrow we climb before first light," she said. "No fire. Cold food. And Ivo—" she opened her eyes, and they were bright and hard as river stones, "—when we reach the Anvil, look at the ice tongue before you look at anything else. Calves come early in a storm year. If the mountain keeps Vessian time, it may keep it faster than any of us planned."

"How fast?"

Dalla smiled, and there was no comfort in it, only respect for the adversary.

"Fast enough," she said, "that by tomorrow night, the question won't be whether Vane catches us. It'll be whether the pass opens before he does — and which of us is standing at the gate when it does."
