## 27. Vane Enters the Ice

The eyelet was a wound in the mountain, no wider than a coffin stood on end, and Ivo had found it the way they found everything now — by the father's hand. A notation in the route-notes, cramped and confident: *wind gap above the second serac band; look down the throat of the pass; if the ice is singing, wait.* The ice had been singing all morning, a low organ note that rose through the soles of Ivo's boots and settled somewhere behind the sternum, and so they had waited, wedged into the gap with their back against stone that had not been warm in ten thousand years, and watched the road below.

The pass was a corridor of blue. From the eyelet it looked almost gentle — a river of ice frozen mid-journey, its surface scalloped and ridged like the wake of some enormous drowned thing, its walls rising sheer on either side in cliffs of compressed glacier that held the light strangely, greenly, the way deep water holds it. Meltwater ran somewhere beneath, and its sound came up through the ice like a voice through a closed door. Ivo had spent thirty years learning to hear what a river meant under its surface. This was the same grammar, spoken in a colder tongue.

They had come up to the eyelet at first light with the field glasses Dalla had lent them, brass and heavy, the leather case worn soft as an old glove, and they had told themselves they were checking the route ahead. That was a captain's habit: read the water before you commit the hull. But the water ahead was clean — the ice-tongue route ran open and blue for a full kilometer before it bent behind a buttress of rock — and Ivo knew, the way you know a lie in your own mouth, that they had not climbed three hours above the camp to look at ice.

They had climbed to look behind.

Because the Compact was coming. There had been no doubt of it since Last Light, since the town's windows had begun to turn away like eyes, since every mule and every bed and every silence had been bought and paid for. The only questions had been *how close* and *how many*, and Ivo had spent two days not asking them, the way you don't press on a bruise, the way you don't check the bilge when you already hear the water.

Now they pressed on the bruise.

* * *

They came into view an hour after dawn, and Ivo's first thought — absurd, disloyal, unforgettable — was that they looked *competent*.

That was the thing about the Meridian Compact that no one warned you. You expected monsters. You expected the men who erased nations to arrive with whips and burn-marks, with the faces of the people who had come to Harrow Bend and bought lockmasters and made pilots informers. But what came down the pass below was a line of climbers strung on rope like beads on a wire, moving with the unhurried economy of professionals, each man placed exactly where his weight belonged, each step tested and committed, and the whole procession flowing over the broken ice with the ease of a boat running a familiar channel. They were good. They were very, very good. And they were here because a fifteen-year-old on Ossory Quay had sold a sheaf of route-notes for the price of a leaky hull.

Ivo counted them through the field glasses. Fourteen, then fifteen as a straggler closed up — porters in the middle, armed men at the ends, two figures near the head moving with the particular care of men carrying something fragile and expensive. Survey instruments, probably. Or a lawyer's documents, wrapped against the cold. Or a chest of their own, empty, waiting to be filled.

And at the head of the line, unmistakable at a kilometer and a half, a parka the color of a wound.

Red. Not the red of signal flags or rescue gear — nothing so honest — but a deep arterial crimson that had no business on a glacier and every intention of having it anyway. Aldous Vane did not dress to disappear. Aldous Vane dressed to be *seen*, because being seen was the point of him; because a man who erased countries wanted the countries to watch him do it. He moved at the head of his rope team with his ice axe carried like a walking cane, unhurried, and even at this distance Ivo could read the set of his shoulders: the posture of a man attending a viewing, not a fight. He had come to inspect the merchandise.

Ivo's hands were steady on the glasses. That surprised them. They had expected shaking, had expected the animal surge of *run*, the old reflex that had carried them off the Serein, up the river, over the foothills — the reflex that had a lifetime's practice and had never once, in all that time, actually saved anything. It only postponed. It postponed and postponed, and the debt grew interest, and here at the top of the world the interest had come due in red parkas and fifteen ropes.

The animal was quiet. What was left, in the stillness, was something colder and more useful.

They watched Vane's party negotiate the first serac band, the same band the father's notes had warned about — *do not trust the ice below the second wall after mid-morning; it weeps* — and Ivo found themselves reading the expedition the way they'd read a rival's boat: where it sat in the water, where it strained, what it would do in bad weather. The porters were locals; Ivo could see it in the way they moved, in the small economies of people who had carried loads since childhood, and something in their faces — even at this distance, something — suggested men who had not chosen this road. Vane's own people were the ones with the easy stride. The others were the ones who looked at the ice like it might look back.

Fifteen souls. One of them a man who had offered Ivo a clean name with a lawyer's smile, and meant it, and that was the worst of it — he had *meant* it. Vane was not cruel the way knives are cruel. He was cruel the way ledgers are cruel, and ledgers, Ivo knew better than anyone, do not hate you. They simply close.

* * *

Below and behind, at the camp under the frozen chapel, Sefa and Dalla were striking the tents. Ivo had left them to it — had insisted on climbing alone, though Dalla had looked at them with her old-woman directness and said, "You're going to look, not to scout," and Ivo had not lied to her, because lying to Dalla was like lying to weather. Sefa had wanted to come. Sefa had argued, briefly, with her jaw set the way her grandmother's coin was minted — small, hard, unmistakable — and then she had looked at Ivo's face and stopped arguing, because she had seen whatever was in it.

"Come back," she had said. Not *be careful*. Not *don't do anything stupid*. Just: *come back*. As if Ivo were a boat she'd lent out and wanted returned in one piece.

Ivo had said, "I always come back," and heard, as the words left, how much that sounded like a man who had spent twenty years never staying anywhere long enough to be missed. Sefa had heard it too. She had said nothing, which was worse.

Now, wedged in the eyelet with the glacier singing below, Ivo let themselves do the arithmetic they'd been avoiding. The Compact was a day behind, maybe less — the calving had opened the route for them as it had opened it for Ivo's party, the mountain impartial in its hospitality. Ahead, the pass ran two more days to the eastern descent, if the route-notes held, if the weather held, if the ice held. Behind, fifteen ropes and a red parka. The chest was on Sefa's sledge now, and the register — the register of every family the archive belonged to, the mailbag undelivered, the whole drowned nation's return addresses — was wrapped against Ivo's own chest, and neither of them had spoken aloud the thing they had both been carrying since the crevasse field: that there was no outrunning this. Not here. There was no downriver left. The mountain had no reed shallows, no back-channels, no dark running. You could not pole a glacier at night.

Which meant that somewhere in the next two days, the running would have to stop. Not because Ivo chose it — though they were beginning to suspect they had chosen it, somewhere back at the toll-lock of Harrow Bend, the moment they'd declined Vane's money too quickly, the moment invisibility ended and they'd felt, for the first time in their adult life, *located*. It would stop because the ice would make it stop. The pass narrowed. The route was the route. Two parties on one rope-line of blue ice, and only one of them needed to arrive with its cargo intact.

Ivo lowered the glasses and sat with that, back against the stone, breath smoking in the wind-gap's draft. The ice sang its long note. Somewhere under the pass, meltwater ran with the force of a river in flood, and Ivo listened to it the way they listened to everything, and heard what it was saying: *I am stronger than all of you. I am patient. I was here before your nations and I will be here after, and I do not care what you carry.*

Good, Ivo thought. Neither do I. Not anymore.

* * *

They were halfway down from the eyelet when they saw it — the thing that turned the cold arithmetic into something with teeth.

The Compact party had reached the bend behind the buttress, out of the eyelet's sightline, and Ivo had dropped below the wind gap to cut back toward camp when the glacier spoke. Not sang. *Spoke* — a crack like a ship's mast going, sharp and enormous, rolling up the pass and off the walls and back again, and Ivo froze on the slope with their hand flat on the ice and felt the mountain's whole attention turn. A serac, somewhere ahead on the route. A big one. The kind of collapse that reshapes the map you were navigating by.

When the echo died, Ivo climbed two body-lengths back up to the gap and put the glasses on the route again, and found the bend, and found — where the route had been — a new wall of fallen ice blocking the corridor like a lock gate slammed shut. And found Vane's expedition strung out before it, stopped, small, patient, already reorganizing. Even from here Ivo could read the choreography: porters unroping, scouts moving along the base of the fresh collapse, the red parka standing apart from all of it, motionless, looking — Ivo was sure of it, sure the way you're sure of a face across a crowded quay — *up*.

Not at the fallen ice. At the walls. At the high ground.

Vane knew they were being watched. Vane had perhaps always known. A man who bought silences in every town on the far side of a mountain range did not walk into a pass without knowing who had gone in ahead of him, and the red parka stood at the head of the stopped rope-line with its face turned up toward the high country, and even at two kilometers, even across all that blue distance, Ivo felt the look land. Felt it the way you feel a lockmaster's eyes on your hull papers. The way you feel the moment a stranger on a dark river knows your name.

*There you are,* the look said. *I always find the river's owner.*

Ivo did not wave. Ivo did not duck. They stood in the wind gap with the wind pulling at their hood and let themselves be seen — let the glasses glint, let the small dark figure on the ridge be a fact Vane could carry down to his lawyers — because some animal, older and wiser than the one that had spent twenty years running, understood that the hiding was over whether Ivo willed it or no, and that the only thing left to decide was what a person did *after* they were found.

They turned and went down to camp, fast and sure on the slope, and the ice sang beneath their boots the whole way, and they thought about the chest, and the register, and the branch tunnel that the Compact's maps did not show, and they thought: *he can see me now. Let him see me. Let him spend his fifteen ropes chasing a man who knows this ice better than his bought maps ever will.*

At camp, Sefa looked up from the sledge, read Ivo's face in one glance the way she read everything, and said, "How close?"

"A day," Ivo said. "Maybe less. And Sefa — he looked right at me."

She was quiet a moment. Then she pulled the route-notes from their oilcloth and spread the father's pass chart on the ice-slick stone between them, her finger finding the branch tunnel's thin inked line, and she said, "Then we stop being the ones who are found," and her finger tapped the chamber at the tunnel's end, twice, like a knock on a door.
