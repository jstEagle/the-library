## 28. The Choice at the Crevasse Field

The crevasse opened the way a river opens — without malice, without warning, simply deciding one morning to be wider than it had been.

Sefa heard it before she saw it: a groan underfoot, deep and structural, like a door in an enormous house being forced. Then the snow bridge ahead of them sagged, exhaled a breath of blue mist, and split.

She stopped so fast that Dalla, roped ten meters behind her, nearly walked into her back. Ahead, the crack ran across the glacier from wall to wall, a black seam in the white, and even as she watched it was working — the near lip crumbling in small wet calvings, snow blocks sloughing into the dark with soft, obscene sounds, like something eating.

"Two hours ago we could have walked over it," Dalla said. She spat to the side, a porter's habit, reading the ice the way Ivo read water. "The melt's come up early today. Sun on the upper seracs." She squinted eastward, where the light had turned the ice walls the color of old honey. "It'll widen all afternoon."

Sefa looked back along their line: herself, then Ivo, then Dalla, then the two young porters who had come up from Last Light before the town went quiet and bought. Six people, four mules lost to the avalanche two days past, and between them everything that remained of Vessia.

The chest rode on Ivo's back. The register rode against Sefa's chest, wrapped in oilcloth inside her coat, bound to her body with the same straps her father had once used for survey instruments. She had carried it that way since the branch chamber, since the night she'd sat by lamplight reading names by the hundred — *Aldemar, Vessin, Okra, Tern* — families listed not as property but as keepers, each entry ending in the same instruction in her father's careful hand: *deliver.*

"We go around," Ivo said. "There's a crossing point downstream where the route-notes mark a snow bridge. The old man trusted it."

"The old man trusted a lot of things," Dalla said mildly. "That's why he drew maps instead of walking them twice."

Ivo didn't answer. Sefa watched them study the crevasse with the particular stillness they brought to moving water — head cocked, eyes tracking, some private arithmetic running behind the face. She had learned to recognize it over these weeks: Ivo calculating odds the way other people breathed.

But this time the stillness went on too long. And when Ivo finally spoke, it wasn't about the crossing at all.

"Maybe we don't cross."

Sefa turned. "What?"

Ivo's eyes were still on the black seam. "Think about what's behind us. Vane's party is less than a day back — we saw their smoke last night. Think about what's ahead: three more days of ice, then the descent, then villages full of people who didn't ask for any of this. Every kilometer we carry this thing, we're drawing a line through the snow that anyone can follow." They shifted the chest's weight on their shoulders, and Sefa heard the faint slosh of folios settling inside the lead lining. "Or we end it here. Right here. The crevasse is two hundred meters deep if it's a centimeter. Lead-lined box, wax-sealed paper — it goes down, the ice closes over it by spring, and no one finds it. Not Vane. Not the Compact. Not anyone. It just becomes part of the mountain."

The words came out level, reasonable, the voice of a captain proposing a route. That was the worst part. Sefa had heard Ivo lie smoothly about cargo manifests and transit passes; she knew how reason could be laid over a thing like paint over rot.

"You're talking about drowning it," she said.

"I'm talking about keeping it from being *bought.*"

"Those aren't the same thing."

"Aren't they?" For the first time Ivo looked at her directly, and she saw something raw underneath the captain's calm — exhaustion, fear, and beneath both, the old shame that had surfaced in the tavern at Ossory Quay and never fully gone back down. "You read the ledger, Sefa. You saw how they list things. *Vessia — pending final delivery.* You know what happens if Vane gets this chest. It doesn't get read. It gets *registered.* Sealed under their own stamp, indexed, priced, and buried in a warehouse in Meridian City next to nine other nations nobody remembers. At least down there—" they nodded at the crevasse "—it stays *ours.* Unowned. That's not destruction. That's—"

"That's exactly what destruction looks like when a frightened person does the arithmetic," Sefa said.

The wind picked up, dragging spindrift off the far lip in long white banners. Dalla moved the porters back a rope-length, saying nothing, the way she'd said nothing through every argument since Last Light — the old woman's policy of letting weather pass.

"I carried my grandmother's coin for six years," Sefa said. Her voice came out lower than she intended, and she let it. "Six years, Ivo. Do you know what I did with them? Nothing. I never spent one. Because the moment I spent the last one, there would be no more Vessia anywhere in my pocket. So I starved a little in Kilometer Forty rather than spend a nation." She pulled the register's strap tighter across her chest, feeling the folios press against her ribs like a second heartbeat. "And now you want to drop the whole country down a hole because it's *heavier than you thought.*"

"It's not about weight—"

"It's entirely about weight! Yours!" She heard her own voice crack across the ice and didn't care. "You've been carrying guilt since Ossory Quay instead of carrying the archive, and now you want to set both down at once. You sold your father's notes when you were sixteen — fine. That was a crime, and it cost lives, maybe including his. But destroying the archive doesn't undo that. It just means the Compact wins *and* Vessia loses. Both things. You'd be doing their work for them and calling it mercy."

Ivo flinched as though she'd struck them. Behind them, one of the young porters coughed delicately into his muffler.

"Memory isn't mine to kill," Sefa said, quieter now. "That's the whole point of it, Ivo. My father understood that. Your father understood that. That's why he didn't burn anything either — did you ever think of that? He ran medicine through famine locks and smuggled boxes over glaciers for twenty years, and he never once destroyed a page. He knew what he was carrying belonged to people who weren't born yet. People like me. You don't get to decide that the future doesn't get its mail."

"*Mail,*" Ivo repeated. Something in their face shifted — not softening exactly, but recognition, the look of someone hearing their own argument turned around and finding it hollow. "You sound like him."

"Good. One of us should."

The silence stretched. Below them the crevasse breathed its cold breath, patient as debt. Sefa realized her hands were shaking — not from cold, or not only from cold — and pressed them flat against the register through the oilcloth.

Then Ivo laughed. It was a short, broken sound, half bitterness and half surrender, and it startled a pair of choughs off the ice wall above.

"Do you know what I was going to do," they said, "if you'd agreed? I was going to do it myself. Tell you the strap broke. Carry the blame alone, one more time, one more thing sold or sunk or lost. It would have been easy. It's always been easy for me to lose things." They looked down at the chest straps, knotted the way their father had taught them, and their voice dropped. "But you're right. It isn't mine. It never was. I'm just the boat it came in."

Sefa felt the anger drain out of her and leave something else behind — not forgiveness, not yet, but the ground forgiveness might someday stand on. "We deliver it," she said. "Every name. Door to door if we have to. Let the Compact try to buy a nation that's already been handed out."

"And if Vane catches us on the ice first?"

"Then he catches us carrying it," Sefa said. "Not hiding it. There's a difference, and he'll feel it."

Dalla chose that moment to spit again and jerk her thumb downstream. "Bridge is marked forty minutes back the way the dead man drew it. If we argue on the move, we'll be warm." She paused, and her old eyes went from Sefa to Ivo and back, and something almost like approval crossed the weathered face. "My mother used to say: a map burned is a village unnamed. Sounds like you two finally learned it."

They rigged the line and moved.

The detour took them along the crevasse's edge for nearly an hour, close enough that Sefa could see into the blue dark between the lips of ice — depthless, ancient, lit from within by that strange compressed glow, like looking down into frozen deep water. Once, crossing a snow bridge that Dalla tested with three careful blows of her staff, Sefa looked straight down through a thin place and saw, far below, the ghost-green gleam of trapped meltwater, and thought: *this is what the Compact would make of us. A pretty glow at the bottom of a hole.*

By late afternoon the crevasse narrowed, and the route-notes' crossing held — a broad old bridge, sagging but solid, which they crossed one at a time with Dalla counting steps in a language older than either nation the Compact had ever erased.

On the far side, Ivo stopped and looked back the way they'd come, toward the smoke of Vane's camp somewhere beyond the shoulder of the icefall. Then they unslung the chest, knelt, and retied every strap with double knots, checking each one the way they checked rigging before a night run.

"For what it's worth," Ivo said, not looking up, "I'm glad you said no. I needed someone to say it louder than I could think it."

Sefa put her hand briefly on the lead lid, feeling the cold of it, the weight of everything her father had trusted to strangers. "Then keep walking," she said. "Both of us. All the way to the doors."

They made another two kilometers before the light failed — and it was Ivo, climbing the last rise to scout a campsite, who suddenly went still in the way that meant danger, and dropped flat, and waved them down.

Sefa crawled up beside them.

Below, strung out along the glacier like beads on a gray thread, lanterns were moving. Roped figures, deliberate and confident, following the very route-notes Ivo's father had drawn — following *them.*

And at the head of the line, small even at this distance but unmistakable, a red parka burned like a coal in the blue dusk.
