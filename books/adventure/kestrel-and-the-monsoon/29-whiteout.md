## 29. Whiteout

The mountain took the sky away at midmorning, and it did it politely, the way a thief lifts a watch.

Ivo felt it first as a change in the light — not dimming but flattening, every shadow erased from the ice walls around them until the pass became a page with nothing written on it. Then the wind arrived from the east, warm for the altitude and wet, carrying the smell of distant rain that would fall here as snow. Dalla stopped mid-stride, held up one knotted fist, and said a word in her valley tongue that Ivo didn't know but understood anyway.

*Now.*

They had been in the blue ice for two days by then, following the father's route-notes through corridors of compressed glacier that arched overhead like the ribs of some drowned cathedral. The meltwater ran beneath them the whole way, a river under the road, and Ivo had found a strange comfort in its sound — the one voice on this mountain that spoke their language. Rivers they knew. Rivers could be read: the seam of a current, the V of debris pointing downstream, the way water lied about its depth. Ice was supposed to be different. Ice was stone that had learned to move.

But when the whiteout came down, Ivo discovered that ice, too, was just weather wearing a mask.

The first hour was merely bad. The second was worse than bad, because it taught them what *merely* meant. Snow came not falling but flying, horizontal, filling the space between one blink and the next with a grain so fine it seemed less like precipitation than like the air itself deciding to become solid. The blue walls vanished. The rope between them vanished. Sound flattened until Sefa's voice, calling from ten meters back, seemed to come from inside Ivo's own skull.

"Rope check!" Dalla bellowed, and the word went down the line like a struck bell: *rope check*, passed mouth to mouth, six lives tied into ninety meters of line with knots spaced like heartbeats.

Ivo stood third in the order — behind Dalla, behind her lead mule's ghost of a memory, ahead of Sefa, ahead of the two Compact porters who had come over to them on the tongue of the icefall and never gone back, ahead of young Pell who had been Vane's man for exactly as long as it took him to see what Vane's manhood cost. The rope ran from Ivo's harness to Sefa's waist, and when Ivo turned to check it, there was nothing to see. Just white. Just the line's faint tension, humming like a mooring line in a swell, and the knowledge that if Sefa went down a crevasse on the other end of that hum, Ivo would feel it before hearing it, and would have perhaps three seconds to drop and dig in and become, for the length of one catastrophe, an anchor.

Trust made physical. That was the phrase that kept surfacing in Ivo's mind, unbidden, unwelcome, true. On the river you trusted your boat, your charts, your own hands. Here the trust lived outside the body entirely. It lived in hemp and knots and the willingness to be stopped by another person's weight.

"Dalla!" Ivo shouted forward. "How do we read this?"

The old woman's answer came back through the roar, unhurried, as if she were discussing the price of oats. "You don't read it. You count it."

* * *

Counting-steps. That was Dalla's craft, and she began to teach it the way Ivo's father must once have taught the river — by making the student do it wrong first.

She called the pace herself for the first stretch, her voice a metronome against the wind: numbers in her own language, then again in trade-speech, each stride matched to the last, each turn announced before it came. *Forty steps along the left wall. Wall will sing where the melt cuts thin — listen for it. At the singing place, nine steps out onto the blue, then right, hard right, the crevasse breathes cold on your cheek.* And it did. Ivo felt it — a ribbon of colder air sliding across their face in all that churning warmth, precise as a door left open in a heated house. The crevasse breathing. The glacier inhaling through its wound.

"You're feeling for current," Dalla said, materializing out of the white beside Ivo for a moment, snow crusted in the seams of her face like mortar. "Same as your water. Wind is just a river that got above itself. It runs in channels. It pools in hollows. It goes around stone." She tapped Ivo's chest with one mittened hand. "You've been reading moving things since you were small enough to drown. This is only a slower flood."

Something loosened in Ivo's chest at that — not relief exactly, but recognition, the feeling of a lock accepting a key cut years ago for a different door. All their life they had believed the river and the mountain were opposites, that coming off the water meant entering a country where everything they knew was worthless. They had said as much to Sefa on the climb, bitterly, after the third day of stumbling: *On the river I'm worth something. Up here I'm cargo.* Dalla had heard, or heard of it, and now she was answering it in front of God and the glacier.

The route-notes helped. Ivo carried the relevant folio inside their coat, wrapped in oilcloth, and at each halt they read the father's annotations by feel more than sight — the paper stiff with cold, the ink lines raised enough under a thumb to trace. *Bend at the twin seracs. Beware the moulins after the narrows; in thaw they swallow. If the light dies, trust the water's voice: it always runs beneath the safe side.* Trust the water's voice. Their father had written instructions for this exact blindness decades before Ivo walked into it, and Ivo followed those instructions with the strange sensation of holding a dead man's hand in the dark.

* * *

They moved for hours that way, or for minutes, or for a year — time dissolved in the white like salt in meltwater. The rhythm became everything: Dalla's count, the rope's hum, the call-and-answer down the line that they performed every hundred paces because silence was the only thing more dangerous than the storm. *One. Two. Three.* Each voice answered in turn, and Ivo learned the voices the way you learn voices on a night run — by texture rather than name. Sefa's answer came quick and clipped, a woman rationing her breath. Pell's came late, always late, a half-beat of hesitation that Ivo had begun to dread without knowing why. The two porters — Brannic and Osei, though they'd only given the names that morning — answered in unison, trained into it by some other rope on some other mountain, and their unison was steadying, a double heartbeat in the line.

At the fourth halt — or the fourteenth — Sefa worked her way up the rope hand over hand until she stood pressed close, shouting into the shell of Ivo's hood.

"The register!" she yelled. "It's soaked through my coat — I can feel it freezing!"

"Let it freeze," Ivo shouted back. "Ice won't hurt paper. Thawing will. Keep it frozen till we're under roof."

She nodded, and then didn't leave. For a moment she stayed there, forehead nearly touching Ivo's shoulder, both of them leaning into the wind as if into a gale on deck, and Ivo understood that she wasn't staying for shelter. She was staying because the rope ran taut between them and she wanted, for ten seconds, to be connected to something by choice rather than necessity.

"Ivo." Her voice dropped below a shout, which meant Ivo had to bend closer, which meant her next words arrived almost gently. "If I go into a hole — you stop. You hear me? You don't be a hero. You drop, you hold, you shout for Dalla. You are not allowed to jump after me."

"And if it's you on this end?"

"I follow orders," she said. "I'm good at it. Ask anyone I've ever disobeyed."

Despite everything — the cold chewing through three layers, the fear sitting in the stomach like ballast — Ivo laughed. It came out as a bark, torn away instantly by the wind, but Sefa felt it through their coats, and she grinned, a flash of teeth rimed white, and went back down the rope to her station.

That was the arithmetic of the rope, Ivo thought. You gave your weight to the person behind you and borrowed the strength of the person ahead, and somewhere in that exchange the question of whether you trusted them stopped being a question. Your body answered it every time the line went tight.

* * *

The crisis came at the narrows, where the father's notes promised the moulins.

Dalla had been counting long silence — no numbers reaching Ivo, only the metronome pull of the rope — when the hum changed. Every sailor knows the difference between a line that is loaded and a line that is *suddenly* loaded, between weight and shock, and what came up the rope into Ivo's harness was shock: a single vicious jerk, a scream swallowed whole by the wind, and then the terrible specific silence of someone falling.

Ivo was already dropping. Body flat, boots braced against a ridge of blue ice, arms locked, the rope burning across the shoulder where they'd caught it — instinct doing what thought couldn't have done in time. The line snapped taut and held, and held, and the weight on the far end swung once, twice, pendulum-slow in the unseen dark, and Ivo lay with their cheek against the glacier and breathed the cold and did not move.

"Pell!" Sefa's voice, from behind, raw. "Pell's gone! He's on the rope — Ivo, he's *on the rope*—"

"I've got him!" Ivo roared back. "Dalla! Anchor team! Now!"

What happened next happened in pieces, each one small and enormous. Dalla's voice cutting through the storm with a string of commands. The two porters arriving out of the white like ghosts, dropping beside Ivo, their boots finding the same ridge, three bodies now braced against one falling man. Sefa paying out slack from behind with hands that shook and did not fail, feeding the rope millimeter by millimeter while Dalla called the depth by counting the seconds of a shouted echo — *one, two, three — shallow, he's shallow, forty feet, maybe less.*

"Talk to him!" Dalla commanded. "Falling men let go when they're alone! Talk!"

So Ivo talked. Lay flat on the ice with a man's life sawing at their shoulder and talked into the wind, into the hole they couldn't see, telling Pell about the river — about the Serein in flood season, about the sandbar that wasn't there, about a chest that had come up out of the water and ruined everything, and how ruining everything had turned out, so far, to be the best thing that ever happened to them. Nonsense. Beautiful nonsense, shouted in pieces, and after a long time a voice came up out of the dark, thin and cracking and alive: *"Don't — don't stop talking—"*

"I never stop talking," Ivo shouted. "Ask anyone I've ever lied to."

They hauled him out in stages, prusik loops and muscle and Dalla directing by feel, and when Pell finally came over the lip of the crevasse he was blue-lipped and sobbing and clutching the rope with both hands like a child with a mother's skirt, and the first thing he did was look at Ivo and say, "You talked about boats. The whole time. Boats."

"Comfort of the trade," Ivo said, and discovered their own voice was shaking.

They made camp ninety minutes later, because Dalla judged — by counting, by the slope of the wind, by arts Ivo couldn't begin to audit — that they had reached the lee of a serac field, and because Pell could not have taken ten more steps. The tent went up in the white chaos by pure drill, everyone knowing their task without seeing anyone else perform theirs, and then they were inside, six bodies in a space built for four, and the storm's roar changed pitch against the fabric and became, incredibly, almost domestic.

* * *

The stove hissed. Snow melted in the pot. Pell slept like the dead, wrapped in every spare layer, his rope burns salved and bandaged, and the rest of them sat in the orange stove-light and watched the walls flex and listened to the mountain try to get in.

Sefa sat across from Ivo with the register in her lap, frozen stiff as slate, turning it slowly as it warmed near the stove — too slowly, she was careful, she was painstaking, a page at a time held at arm's length like something newborn. Ivo watched her and thought about the rope. About the moment it had gone taut. About how, in the first half-second before thought arrived, there had been no question of letting go. No negotiation, no weighing. The body had simply refused, absolutely, to surrender what it held.

When had that happened? When had Sefa's weight become non-negotiable? Somewhere between Kilometer Forty and here, apparently, between a forged transit pass and a frozen register, between *who are you* and *hold on*. Trust hadn't been decided. It had accumulated, the way glacier ice accumulated — layer on invisible layer, pressure doing what time alone couldn't, until the whole mass moved as one thing and could hold a man over a void.

"You saved him," Sefa said quietly, not looking up from the register. "You know that. Dalla anchored, the boys pulled, but you were the first weight on the line. Half a second later and he'd have swung into the far wall."

"He'd have done the same."

"No," Sefa said. "He wouldn't have. He told me, while you were rigging the loops. He expected you to cut him loose." She looked up then. "He said everyone expects the Compact to cut people loose. He was surprised when the rope held."

Ivo had no answer for that, so they fed the stove instead, and watched the flame stand up straight in the still air, the only straight thing in the world.

Outside, the whiteout ground on. Inside, Dalla began, unprompted, in the tone of a woman who has decided the storm will last the night and the night might as well be used — "You want to know why I count," she said. "Why counting-steps. Why my mother put numbers into children instead of maps." She settled back against the pack-wall, her eyes on the stove, and the wind rose to meet her like an audience. "Because maps can be bought. I was a girl when they bought ours..."

And Ivo, exhausted, frostbitten, happier than they had any right to be on a mountain trying to kill them, leaned back against the cold fabric and listened.
