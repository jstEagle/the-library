## 30. Dalla's Story

The storm came down on them like a hand closing.

Sefa felt it first as a change in the light — the flat white of the glacier dimming to a bruised grey, then to nothing at all, the world beyond three arm-lengths erased. Dalla called a halt with two sharp whistles and turned them off the route without ceremony, angling toward a dark seam in the rock wall where the ice had pulled back from the mountain like a lip curling from a tooth.

"A cave," Ivo said, half question, half prayer.

"A waystation," Dalla corrected. "Older than your river, boat-captain. Move. The wind behind you is not a thing you want to meet at speed."

They crowded into it — Sefa, Ivo, the two remaining porters, and the mule they had left after the avalanche, a shaggy grey animal named Broom who smelled of wet wool and patience. The chamber went back farther than its mouth promised, a throat of stone that swallowed the storm's voice until it was only a pressure in the ears, a vast muffled breathing somewhere above and behind them. Someone had built a low wall across the entrance once, dry-stacked and long collapsed; someone else had left an iron ring bolted into the rock, rusted to lace. Sefa ran her fingers along the wall as they settled and found, near the back where the ice never reached, carvings: eight-pointed stars, dozens of them, some worn nearly smooth, some still sharp-edged enough to catch her thumbnail.

Vessian stars. Here, halfway up a mountain whose name she did not know because the Compact had never bothered to buy or erase it — it had simply never been worth naming.

She said nothing. She was learning that on this side of the tree line, feeling things out loud cost warmth.

Dalla moved through the cave with the economy of a woman who had done this a hundred times and expected to do it a hundred more. She got the little spirit stove going in a hollow of stone, shielded by her own body, and set snow melting in a dented pot. She fed Broom a nosebag of oats and made the porters check each other's hands and feet for white patches, turning their fingers over like a mother counting children home from a flood. Then, only then, did she sit down on her rolled sleeping pad, put her back against the carved wall, and close her eyes.

The stove hissed. The storm breathed. Sefa sat across from her, knees drawn up, the chest between herself and Ivo — always between them now, a third passenger none of them spoke about.

It was Ivo who broke the silence, which surprised her. "The astrolabe," they said. Their voice was rough from days of thin air. "You recognized it. On the trail you said your mother's mark was on it. You never finished."

Dalla opened one eye.

"No," she agreed. "I didn't." She looked at the stove flame for a while, and Sefa watched something happen in her face — not reluctance exactly, but the careful settling of a woman deciding how much weight a story could bear at this altitude. "But we may be here until tomorrow noon, and there is nothing to do in a storm but keep warm and keep honest. So."

She reached into her coat and brought out a strip of dried meat, tore off pieces, handed them around. When everyone had taken some, she began.

"My valley is called Serelka," she said. "You will not find it. That is the whole of what I have to tell you, so let me say it properly: you will not find it, because it is not there anymore. Not the valley — the valley is there, the river is there, the forty-one houses are there, or were when I was young enough to count them. But the *name* is gone. Bought. My mother used to say the Compact did not steal our valley. They bought it the way you buy a horse — paid the surveyors, paid the cartographers, paid the printers, and one spring the new maps came out and Serelka was printed as a blank slope with a tributary running through it, unnamed, unowned, unhistoried. A place where nothing had ever happened, belonging to no one, therefore available to anyone."

Sefa's chest tightened. She knew this. She had known it since she was small enough to sit under her father's archive table and listen to him dictate letters to custodians in a dozen shrinking countries. But knowing a thing as a fact and hearing it as a woman's childhood were different animals entirely.

"They wanted the grazing rights," Dalla went on. "And the water. And mostly they wanted the precedent — a valley with no name cannot object to anything. My grandfather went to the provincial office with deeds going back nine generations, written on birch bark, and the clerk was very kind and explained that deeds must reference a registered parcel, and the parcel did not exist, and so the deeds described nothing, legally speaking, and would he like a cup of tea? My grandfather walked home over the pass in November and was dead by Christmas, though my mother always said it was the tea that killed him, the humiliation of it, drinking tea with a man who was erasing you politely."

One of the porters, a broad quiet man named Toma, made a sound that might have been a laugh or might not. Dalla glanced at him and almost smiled.

"So," she said. "My mother. Yeva. You've seen her work, or part of it." She nodded toward the chest. "She was not a great artist. She would have told you so herself, cheerfully. She engraved astrolabes — small ones, brass, the size of a saucer, sometimes smaller. Not fine instruments. A navigator would laugh at them. But every plate carried the sky over Serelka as it stood on the night of the founding festival, the old calendar date, and around the rim she cut the names — the real names, the bought-out names — of forty valleys and rivers and passes in the high country. Forty plates, she made, over twenty years. One for each family that could pay in eggs or wool or nothing at all. She said an instrument tells you where you are. Hers told you where you were *from*, and that those were not the same question, and that the second answer was the one people kill for."

"She made forty," Sefa said quietly. "I have seen perhaps six, ever, in collections. All catalogued as anonymous folk work."

"Anonymous." Dalla tasted the word. "Yes. That is how they take the second thing, after the name. First they take the place, then they take the maker. My mother died with her plates scattered from here to the coast, sold or given away or buried in gardens, and every one of them unsigned, because signing them would have been painting a target on whichever house held it. She signed none of them except—" she stopped, and for the first time her composure shifted, weather moving behind stone, "—except the ones for her own children. There were three of us. Mine had a star on the reverse, small, under the pivot pin, where no assessor would ever look. I was eleven. I thought it was just decoration. It took me until I was thirty to understand it was a signature, and a message, and a will all at once. It said: *this child knows where she is from.*"

The stove hissed. Outside, the storm threw itself against the mountain in long slow gusts, and the cave drank the sound.

"The Compact men came twice," Dalla said. "The second time they offered my mother money for her tools and her pattern-book. Real money — more than the valley saw in a decade. She invited them to eat, fed them for two days, and on the third morning they found their mules loaded and their papers gone and every tool in the forge broken at the neck, snapped clean, useless, worthless. She'd done it in the night with a hammer, forty years of craft, gone in an hour. My father was furious. My mother said — I remember exactly, I was fourteen, I stood in the doorway — she said, *'They can buy the name off the map. Let them try to buy the sky.'*"

Ivo had gone very still. Sefa glanced sideways and saw the captain staring into the stove flame with an expression she had learned to read on the river: the look of a person recalculating everything downstream of a fact they had just hit. Ivo had spent a lifetime believing the Cranes' connection to Vessia was cargo — boxes ferried, fees collected, a smuggler's trade. Sefa could see the arithmetic running: *her mother made the instrument in my father's chest. Her mother knew my father. This was never smuggling.*

"And the plates?" Sefa asked. "After she died?"

"Buried, mostly. Given away. Two I know of went east with families who emigrated before the border closed. One —" Dalla's mouth did something complicated, "— one I sold myself, when my son's lung went bad and the doctors were in the low country and everything costs. I sold a plate of the night sky over a valley that officially does not exist, to a dealer who asked no questions, and I have regretted it in the ordinary human way, which is to say completely and forever. So do not look at me with pity, archivist's daughter. I am not a martyr of memory. I am a woman who has both kept it and spent it, and knows the price of both directions."

"I sold route-notes," Ivo said.

It came out flat, almost inaudible. Sefa had heard the confession once, in a tavern on Ossory Quay, and had not forgiven it and had stayed anyway, and neither of them had spoken of it since. Now Ivo said it again, to Dalla, unprompted, and Dalla looked at the captain for a long moment with those weathered eyes that missed nothing above the tree line or below it.

"Yes," she said. "Your father told me."

Sefa's head came up. "*What?*"

Dalla almost smiled again. "Every summer, fifteen years, the elder Crane crossed my pasture with Vessian boxes on his mules, and every summer he stopped at my fire and drank my terrible tea and told me too much, because he was a man who needed one person on each side of a range who knew the whole shape of his life. He told me he had a child who was ashamed of him — ashamed of the secrecy, ashamed of the poverty of it, the endless hauling of other people's heavy history for other people's gratitude and no money. He told me the child sold his notes to a broker in a river town for the price of a boat hull. He wasn't angry when he told it. He said —" and here Dalla's voice changed, softened into something borrowed, a dead man's cadence, "— he said, *'My kid bought a boat with my map. Well. A boat is also a way of carrying things. Maybe the child will carry something someday that makes up the difference.'*"

The silence in the cave was total. Even the storm seemed to lean in.

Sefa looked at Ivo and saw something she had never seen on that competent, guarded face: a person standing in the open with nowhere to hide, no current to run, no channel to slip down. Twenty years of running, and it turned out the father had known the whole time — had carried the wound openly, had spoken of it gently, to a stranger over bad tea, and had *waited*.

"He knew," Ivo said. It was barely a whisper. "All those years on the water, every lock, every false paper, every mile I put between us — he knew, and he never—"

"He never stopped packing your share," Dalla said simply. "There was always a box on the mule-line marked with a kestrel. He never said what was in it. I never asked. But it rode in the middle of the line, the safest position, every single crossing. Whatever he thought you might come back for, he kept it in the safest place he had."

Sefa had to turn away. She pressed her palm flat against the carved wall, against the eight-pointed stars worn smooth by centuries of other palms doing exactly this, and breathed until her eyes stopped burning. Grief, she was learning, did not arrive once. It arrived in installments, and this one was Ivo's, and she had no right to it and no way to refuse it either.

* * *

The storm held through the afternoon and into the blue dark. They ate, melted snow, checked each other's extremities again. Toma and the younger porter, Ilse, slept in shifts against the wall. Broom dozed standing, steam rising off her flanks.

In the deepest part of the night, when the stove had burned to coals and the cold pressed in like a tide, Dalla spoke again, quieter, as if continuing a conversation begun hours ago.

"You want to know why I'm taking you over the high route," she said. "When every guide in Last Light refused, when the snows are early and the fee is a fortune and I am too old for this by any sane measure."

"You recognized the astrolabe," Sefa said. "Your mother's star."

"That started it. That is not why." Dalla stirred the coals with a stick, and sparks rose and died. "When the Compact bought Serelka's name, do you know what happened to the people? Nothing violent. That is the genius of it. No soldiers came. The taxes simply got stranger. The school books arrived with the valley missing. The priest was reassigned. Young people left for towns where their home could be pronounced. Within a generation, half the valley agreed to forget, because forgetting was easier and the maps agreed with them, and agreement is a powerful comfort. My own brother —" the stick paused in the coals, "— my own brother says *the old name* with air quotes, Sefa. Air quotes, over his mother's grave. He thinks the past is a debt the living shouldn't pay. And maybe he's right. Maybe. But I have watched what happens to people who let the maps do their remembering. They become available. Available for anything. A person with no origin can be assigned one, and the Compact assigns them wholesale."

She looked up, and in the coal-light her face was all ridges and shadow, ancient as the carvings behind her.

"Your nation is not dead, girl. I have climbed through it for three days. It's in a chapel full of graffiti. It's in boat-songs in villages that don't know they're singing it. It's in a chest your father's friend carried for twenty years and a register of families waiting to be delivered. Nations don't die of conquest. They die of *delivery failure* — of archives lost, registers burned, names unwritten, mail undelivered. The Compact understands this perfectly; it's why they buy paper and not land. And you two are carrying the last undelivered mail of Vessia through a storm, and I am an old woman whose mother taught children where they were from, and I will tell you plainly: I am not guiding you. I am delivering."

Sefa found she could not speak. She reached out instead and touched the old woman's wrist, and Dalla let her, and that was the whole exchange, and it was enough.

Later, near dawn, wrapped in every layer they owned, Sefa drifted at the edge of sleep and heard Ivo's voice, low, from across the coals.

"Dalla. The box with the kestrel mark. Did he ever say—"

"He said if his child ever came up this mountain, I was to give it over without a word and ask no questions." A pause, the coals shifting. "He also said the child would probably be angry about it. He said, *'Anger means the inheritance landed. Indifference is what I was afraid of.'*"

Across the dying fire, Sefa heard Ivo exhale — long, shaking, the sound of a rope paying out after years at full strain.

She slept, finally, and dreamed of a valley with forty-one houses and a name, and woke to a silence so complete it rang.

Dalla was already at the cave mouth, squinting into the clearing air. Beyond her, the storm had swept the sky down to hard clean blue, and the glacier below lay scoured and bright, and far to the east — impossibly, beautifully — the ice-tongue of the pass caught the early sun and flashed like a struck bell.

"Storm's spent," Dalla said, shouldering her pack. "And look there. The tongue's shining clear. Calving season's opening early this year — the mountain keeps its own calendar, whatever the maps say." She grinned at them, wolfish, alive. "Eat fast, children. The pass is open, and I promise you we are not the only ones who noticed."
