## 31. The Calving Begins

The mountain had been talking all night.

Ivo lay in the shallow scoop of scree above the branch tunnel's mouth, listening to it the way they had once listened to a river through the hull of the kestrel-boat — not with their ears alone but with their spine, their teeth, the small bones behind the knees that knew when water was lying about its depth. The glacier spoke in the same grammar. A groan from deep under the blue ice, long and tectonic, like a lock gate taking strain. Then silence. Then a crack like a whip-crack of frozen rope, sharp and high and close. Then nothing again, which was worse than anything, because a river that went quiet was a river gathering itself.

Dalla had said this would come. Three nights ago, hunched over a spirit stove in the snow cave while the storm screamed itself hoarse outside, she had counted on her fingers — knuckles gone broad and square from sixty years of rope and cargo — and told them: *Peak calving. The glacier sheds what it can't hold. It does it on its own schedule, not yours, not Vane's, not anyone's. You want to cross the tongue, you cross it in the window after the big falls settle. Before that, you're walking under a hammer that hasn't decided where to land.*

Sefa had asked how you know when the window opens.

*You don't,* Dalla said. *You listen until your body knows before your head does. My mother could hear it a day out. I can hear it half a day. You two will learn to hear it never, so you'll do what I say.*

Ivo had smiled at that in the dark, or something like a smile, and now, lying under a sky gone the pale grey of old pewter, they were not smiling. Because the mountain had stopped talking in sentences and started talking in paragraphs, and every paragraph ended the same way: with ice falling somewhere below them, out of sight, down in the pass where the Compact expedition had entered yesterday afternoon.

They crawled to the ridge eyelet.

It wasn't much — a gap between two slabs of gneiss no wider than a coffin, worn smooth by meltwater in some earlier, warmer century — but from it the whole upper pass unrolled like a chart someone had drawn in white and blue. The glacier tongue ran down the throat of the pass for better than two kilometers, flanked by walls of ice so steep they held the light differently than rock did, giving back a green-blue glow even under cloud, as if the ice were lit from inside by something drowned. Seracs stood along the upper icefall like a ruined city — towers and leaning walls and arches of compressed snow, twenty meters tall, forty, some leaning at angles no architect would sign. And threaded along the base of those towers, strung out on a single rope like beads on a wire, was the Compact party.

Ivo counted them twice to be sure. Eleven figures. Nine porters roped in pairs, moving with the careful, mincing steps of people who understood exactly what hung over them, and ahead of them two men in matching red parkas — Vane's expedition leads, hired professionals, the kind who charged by the day and billed for discretion. Vane himself was not visible. Either he was back at Last Light nursing the leg the crevasse had nearly taken from him, or he was somewhere in that line wearing a colour Ivo couldn't pick out at this distance. Ivo found themselves hoping, with a bitterness that surprised them, that he was there. That he was watching his own purchase walk him into the ice.

The wind came up-valley, carrying the smell of snow and stone and nothing else, and beneath it — faint, rhythmic, almost tender — the sound of the porters' voices. Not words. Just the call-and-response of roped climbers, one voice rising, another answering, a human metronome keeping eleven bodies moving as one animal. Ivo knew that sound from rivers. It was the sound of a crew running a rapid, everyone calling the water before it called them.

Then the mountain raised its voice.

It began as a sound Ivo felt rather than heard — a deep subsonic shudder through the scree, through the rock, through the bones of their hands where they gripped the eyelet's lip. Dalla, three meters away with her binoculars made of brass and bone pressed to her eyes, went rigid.

"*Down-tongue left,*" she said, very quietly, the way she'd say *rope's about to part.* "*Big one. Left-hand serac wall. They're directly under it.*"

Ivo put their own eyes to the gap.

The left-hand wall of the icefall was letting go. Not all at once — that was the horror of it, the patience of it. A crack opened along the base of the tallest tower, a black seam zigzagging upward like lightning frozen mid-strike, and then the tower leaned. Slowly. Gracefully, if grace could be said to exist in something that weighed as much as a village. Snow puffed from its shoulders in little white sighs. And eleven tiny figures on the glacier floor below kept moving, because from down there it would have looked like nothing yet — just a shadow growing, just a sound arriving late across the ice.

"They can't hear it," Ivo breathed. "They can't see it from underneath. The angle—"

"No," Dalla said. "They can't."

The tower fell.

Even at this distance the sound arrived like a blow — a concussion Ivo felt in their chest, a white detonation of powder snow that swallowed the lower third of the icefall, and when it cleared, the route the Compact party had been threading simply wasn't there anymore. A new wall of shattered blocks stood where the path had run. And the rope-line — Ivo searched, heart slamming, searched the churned white chaos with an urgency that had nothing to do with whose side anyone was on —

There. Four figures, up-glacier of the fall, scrambling back toward the entrance of the pass. Safe. Seven more, down-glacier, cut off — pinned against the right-hand wall in a pocket of ice the size of a threshing floor, walled off from retreat by the fresh debris, walled off from advance by the icefall still standing above them, still groaning, still holding towers that had watched their brother fall and were considering following.

And the mountain, having spoken once, remembered it liked speaking.

A second crack, higher up. A third. Somewhere deep in the glacier's gut, meltwater that had been roaring all week changed its note — dropped half an octave, thickened, the way a river changes when a slide comes down upstream and the current starts looking for a new way through. Ivo had spent thirty years learning what water sounded like when it was about to do something irreversible. This was that sound. Transposed into ice.

"Sefa," Ivo called, without turning. "Get the flare kit. Now."

They heard her move — quick, no questions, which was itself a measure of how far they'd come — and then she was beside them at the eyelet, cheeks raw with windburn, breath steaming, and she looked down at the trapped figures and Ivo watched her understand it all in the space of one long exhale.

"That's them," she said. "That's Vane's people."

"Some of them." Ivo's voice came out flat and strange, as if it belonged to someone else. "Nine porters. Locals, most likely. Hired at Last Light or bought out of debt the way Vane buys everything." They had learned that on the tongue of the glacier four days ago, hauling two drowning men out of a meltwater channel — conscripted men, sons of valleys whose names had been sold off maps, boys who carried Vane's loads because the alternative was a debt collector and a winter with no work. One of them had wept on the stable ice and thanked Sefa in a language that was half Vessian, and Sefa had held his face in her gloved hands and said *you're allowed to go home now* and he had said *there isn't a home to go to, that's why I'm here,* and Ivo had turned away so neither of them would see their face.

"And the other two?"

"Vane's leads. Professionals. They knew the risk when they signed."

Sefa looked at them. Wind pulled a strand of hair across her face and she didn't brush it away. "And you're telling me that like it matters. Like it makes a difference to what we do."

"It doesn't," Ivo said. "That's the problem. I keep trying to make it matter. I keep trying to find a version of this where some of them count less than others, because if they all count—" Their jaw tightened. Below, in the pocket of trapped ice, one of the porters had climbed onto a block and was waving both arms at the walls around him, at the sky, at anyone, the universal gesture of a man who has realized that no one is coming. "If they all count, then everything I've wanted since Harrow Bend is a lie. I wanted them buried, Sefa. When we watched them string into the pass from the ridge, some part of me — the part that remembers my father's name being dragged through the mud, the part that read that ledger with nations listed like cargoes — that part wanted the ice to take them and close over and never give anything back."

"I know," Sefa said quietly. "Mine too."

"And now they're down there and the ice is going to take them, and it turns out wanting a thing and watching it happen are different animals entirely."

Below them, the glacier answered the argument for itself. Another serac — smaller, on the right side this time — calved with a report like artillery, and the trapped party flattened against the wall as shrapnel ice whistled across their pocket. One figure didn't get flat fast enough. Ivo saw him stagger, saw the rope snap taut as his partner caught him, saw the two of them stand swaying for a moment like dancers deciding whether the music had stopped.

"The pocket won't hold," Dalla said. She hadn't moved from her own vantage, and her voice had the terrible calm of a woman who had watched mountains kill people before and expected to again. "Look at the meltwater. It's backing up against the debris dam. Within the hour that floor becomes a lake. Within two, the dam goes and takes the pocket with it. Or the icefall finishes calving first. Either way, seven people die down there today unless somebody tells them where to walk."

"Tells them?" Ivo turned. "Tells them what? There's nowhere—"

"There's the branch tunnel mouth." Dalla's finger, brown and steady, traced a line down the right-hand wall of the pass, past the icefall, to a place where the glacier's edge curled away from the valley wall and left a shadowed slot — the same slot Ivo's father's charts had marked with a symbol like a bird's foot, the same slot they themselves had used two days ago to bypass the worst of the icefall. "It runs under the ice, along the bedrock, comes out above the debris field. It's tight. It's dark. It's got a chamber halfway where six people can stand while the mountain does its shouting. Your father's charts show it clear as scripture. Those people down there are standing a hundred meters from the door and they don't have the map."

The wind gusted. Powder snow streamed off the serac tops in long banners, eastward, toward the villages on the far side of the range where Sefa had already begun delivering names to doors. Ivo looked at the flare pistol lying in the bottom of the kit bag — a heavy, ugly, wonderful thing they'd traded for in Last Light along with the mule-hire, thinking of it then only as a signal for help if the mountain broke *them.*

One flare. Red, arcing high, unmistakable against the grey sky. It wouldn't tell the trapped party everything. But it would tell them *someone sees you, someone is trying, look up, look for the next sign.* And then Ivo could use the mirror from the survival kit to flash the tunnel mouth's location in the sun — except the sun was behind cloud, might not break, might—

Or silence. Silence was also a choice, and it required nothing, cost nothing, risked nothing. Silence meant the Meridian Compact lost nine porters and two leads and possibly Vane himself, lost its expedition, lost its claim on the archive not to defeat but to geology. Silence meant the ledger that listed nations like cargoes would list this acquisition as *lost in transit.* Silence meant Ivo Crane finally, finally stopped running and let the mountain fight for them.

Silence meant seven men who had done nothing but carry loads for a monster would die in a pocket of melting ice, and Ivo would live the rest of their life listening to glaciers and hearing them laugh.

"You don't have to decide alone," Sefa said.

"Yes I do." Ivo's throat was tight. "That's what a captain is. It's the one thing my father ever taught me that I kept. He used to say — when the river splits around a bar and both channels drown, the pilot doesn't get to not choose. Choosing badly is forgivable. Not choosing drowns everyone." They laughed, short and broken. "He said it about water. He never said it about people. I don't think he ever thought I'd need it about people."

Sefa put her hand over theirs on the cold stone. Her glove was wet; her grip was strong. "My father locked the archive in a chest and gave it to a smuggler rather than let the Compact buy it. Everyone in our families has spent their whole lives choosing the lesser drowning." Her eyes were bright and furious and certain. "Choose the one you can live inside."

Ivo looked down at the pocket one last time. The man on the block had stopped waving. He was just standing now, small and dark against all that white, waiting for the mountain to finish its sentence.

The choice didn't feel like a decision. It felt like a hand moving before the mind finished arguing — the same hand that had thrown lines to drowning strangers in flood seasons, the same hand that had once, shamefully, sold route-notes to a broker, the same hand that had torn Vane's contract into pieces small enough for the river to eat. It took the flare pistol out of the bag. It checked the cartridge with fingers that shook only a little. It rose above the shelter of the rocks, into the open wind, exposed on the ridgeline where any glass in the pass below could find it.

"What are you doing?" Dalla snapped. "Stay under cover—"

"Someone has to be visible," Ivo said, "or the signal means nothing. A flare from nowhere is weather. A flare from a person is a promise."

They aimed high and wide of the icefall, up into the dead grey air above the trapped pocket, and fired.

The flare went up with a sound like a struck match the size of a church, trailing smoke, climbing, climbing, and then it burst — a bloom of burning red against the pewter sky, huge, obscene, beautiful, raining slow crimson sparks onto the roof of the world. For three full seconds it painted the whole pass red: the seracs, the debris field, the trapped pocket, the upturned faces of seven people who had believed, in the way people believe at the end, that no one was left.

Then Ivo snatched up the signal mirror, and the sun — capricious, merciful, breaking through the cloud at that exact moment as if the mountain itself had opinions — caught it, and Ivo began to flash. Long-short-long. Toward the shadowed slot at the glacier's edge. Again. Again. *There. There. There is a door and here it is and you are not going to die today.*

Below, the pocket erupted into motion. Figures pointing. The rope-line gathering. Someone — one of the leads, the professional, the man who'd known the risk when he signed — actually raised an arm toward the ridgeline, toward the flash of light, in something that from this distance looked unbearably like a salute.

And then the mountain, insulted, spoke again — the loudest yet, a rolling thunder from the deep heart of the icefall, and Dalla's head came up like a hawk's.

"That wasn't a fall," she said. "That was the whole icefall shifting. The window's closing, Crane. Whatever they're going to do, they do it in the next ten minutes — and so do we. Get your people moving. We're crossing the tongue ahead of that dam going out, and we're doing it *now.*"

Ivo flashed the mirror one last time — *hurry, hurry, hurry* — shoved it into the bag, and turned to Sefa, whose face was lit red still, ghost-red, by the falling sparks of the flare they had both chosen.

"Run," Ivo said. "We just told the mountain we're not on its side anymore. Let's hope it was listening to the glacier instead."

Behind them, with a sound like the world clearing its throat, the debris dam at the foot of the pocket began to fail — and the meltwater, patient all morning, started looking for its way through.
