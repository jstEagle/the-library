## 32. Rescue on the Tongue

The flare Ivo fired still hung in Sefa's memory like a red wound in the white morning, and now the morning itself was screaming.

She had heard ice calve before — from the valley, from a distance, where the sound arrived softened and strange, like thunder that had forgotten its own name. But this was different. This was the glacier speaking at conversational volume, if the glacier were a god with a grudge. The icefall below them was coming apart in pieces the size of chapels, and the Compact expedition was in the middle of it, small as spilled ink on a page that was being torn out.

"They're in the runout," Dalla shouted, her voice barely clearing the roar. The old woman stood braced against the wind with her mule-stick planted in the ice like a pilgrim's staff. "The tongue! Look — two of them, off the rope, down on the blue!"

Sefa looked. Below, where the glacier's tongue flattened and spread before the long climb to the eastern saddle, two figures crawled on the ice. One was moving. One was not. Around them, serac towers leaned and groaned, and the meltwater that had been a murmur underfoot all morning had become a drumroll, as if the whole mountain had decided to keep time for a hanging.

"We can't," said one of the Compact porters who had come up with them in the strange truce of the last day — no, Sefa corrected herself, those were their own porters, Dalla's people. It was Ivo who said it, or started to say it, and then stopped, because Sefa was already uncoiling rope.

"You fired the flare," she said. It was not an accusation. It was arithmetic, the same arithmetic the mountain had been teaching them since Last Light: every choice cost, every cost was chosen. "You don't get to fire the flare and then stand here."

"I know what I don't get to do." Ivo's face was gray with cold and something older than cold. "I'm saying the route down. The tongue's rotten. One more calving and—"

"Then we go fast." Sefa tested the anchor Dalla had set — a buried ice-axe, deadman style, the kind of thing she'd learned in three days and would keep for the rest of her life. "Dalla, hold our line from here. If the ice moves, pull. Don't be gentle."

Dalla spat, which Sefa had learned was the old woman's form of prayer, and took the rope around her back. "Go," she said. "And if the mountain asks who sent you, tell it the astrolabe's daughter."

* * *

Descending onto the tongue was like stepping into the throat of something that had not yet decided whether to swallow.

The blue ice was older than any nation. It had a color Sefa had no word for in Vessian or in the trade-tongue — a blue that seemed lit from underneath, from some memory of sky the ice had swallowed centuries ago and never digested. Meltwater ran in channels beside their boots, quick and glassy, and every few minutes the whole surface shivered and a sound rolled through it like a word being repeated in a language almost understandable.

The moving porter saw them and stopped moving. He had a Compact-issue ice axe, Compact-issue rope, a Compact-issue parka with the syndicate's gray-and-gold compass rose stitched at the shoulder — and under all of it, when he turned his face up, a face Sefa knew.

Not his face specifically. His kind of face. A valley face, a river face, the brown weathered look of people who lived where the maps got thin. He had the cheekbones of the hill country above Tern Lock, or somewhere like it. Somewhere like every place the Compact had ever bought.

"You're Crane's people," he said. His voice was hoarse. "The flare — that was you?"

"Who else is stupid enough to be out here?" Ivo dropped to a knee beside the second figure, the one who wasn't moving, and did something quick and professional with fingers at the throat, then at the wrist. "He's breathing. Pulse is thin. Get him up — we rope him between us. What happened to your party?"

"Scattered." The porter laughed, and the laugh had a crack in it. "Vane pushed us onto the tongue before dawn to make the saddle before the calving peaked. Said the window was closing and the fee for losing the window came out of all our pay. Then the first serac went, and the rope team on the left—" He stopped. Swallowed. "Three men. Just gone. The ice took them like they were nothing, like they were—"

"Like they were cargo," Sefa said.

He looked at her then, really looked, and something in his face shifted — recognition of a different kind. "You're the archivist's daughter," he said. "They told us about you. Said you were a fanatic. Said you'd burn the archive rather than let civilized hands have it." He shook his head slowly. "They say that about everyone from home, you know. Every valley they've got a ledger line for. Fanatics. Savages. People with too much past and not enough sense to sell it."

"Help me lift him," Sefa said, because her throat was doing something she didn't want it to do, and lifting was easier than speaking.

* * *

The second porter was younger, barely more than a boy, and his leg was broken below the knee — a clean break, the bone not through the skin, which Dalla would later call the mountain's one courtesy of the day. He had gone unconscious when a serac's pressure wave had thrown him, and he woke as they splinted his leg with an ice axe shaft and Dalla's spare webbing, and the first thing he did, before he even opened his eyes properly, was start apologizing.

"I'm sorry," he said, to the ice, to the sky, to Sefa's hands on his ankle. "I'm sorry, I'm sorry, I didn't want to come up here, I told them—"

"You're safe," Sefa said. "You're on stable ice. Breathe."

He opened his eyes. He was maybe nineteen. He had frost in his lashes and a Vessian star tattooed — no. Sefa leaned closer. Not a Vessian star. A star *like* the Vessian star, the way a cousin resembles you at the wrong angle. The eight-pointed kind, but with the points barbed, the way they drew it in the valley of the Upper Serein, two hundred kilometers from anything her father had ever called Vessia.

"Where are you from?" she asked him.

He blinked at her. "Marnholt," he said. "The Marnholt valley. Below the—"

"Below the Grey Saddle," Sefa finished. "I know it. My father's charts have it. The old charts call it something else."

The boy stared at her. "The old charts call everything something else," he said, and there was no bitterness in it, only a tiredness so deep it sounded like wisdom. "My grandmother used to say the old name for our valley at night, like a lullaby. She said if she stopped saying it, it would die. My mother said she was a foolish old woman talking to the dark." His eyes went past Sefa, up the slope, to where the Compact's remaining climbers were regrouping near the icefall's edge, tiny and gray against the white. "I took this work because Marnholt has no work. The Compact bought the sawmill, then closed it, then offered us porter wages to carry their loads into mountains my grandfather hunted in. I carried their boxes." His voice dropped. "I carried boxes with other people's names on them. Papers. Deeds. Things that made it so my valley had never been my valley. I knew what they were. I carried them anyway."

Sefa sat back on her heels in the blue light and felt something rearrange itself in her chest — not forgiveness, not yet, maybe not ever, but a kind of terrible kinship, the way you recognize your own hands on another person's body.

The Compact did not run on believers. She had imagined them, all this way — Vane and his kind, the men who bought rivers and erased valleys — as a machine of the convinced, an engine of people who had *chosen*. But a machine like that needed hands, and hands came from the erased places, because the erased places were the only places left with nothing to lose and everything to eat. Vane's empire ran on the grandchildren of the people it had unmade, carrying the paperwork of their own unmaking up the mountains their grandmothers had sung to.

"You could have refused," she said, and heard how cruel it was the moment it left her mouth.

"Yes," the boy said simply. "And my sister would have gone instead. She's smaller. She'd have died on the icefall today, not me." He looked at his splinted leg almost with relief. "This is the first honest work I've done for them. Lying on the ice."

* * *

They hauled the boy up the slope by relay, Sefa on the rope with the elder porter, Dalla calling the count, Ivo anchoring and watching the icefall with the fixed attention Sefa had learned to read as the captain's version of prayer — not asking, just attending. Twice the glacier groaned and shifted, and twice they froze, four bodies and one stretcher lashed to the mountain's skin, waiting to see if the mountain would take them. It didn't. It took, instead, another serac on the far side, a tower the size of a granary folding into itself with a sound like a door slamming in a house the size of the sky, and the spray of it reached them as powder, fine as flour, settling on the boy's face so that he looked, for a moment, like something baked.

At the top, on the stable ice above the tongue, the arithmetic resumed. The Compact party had lost three dead, two injured, and — Sefa watched them counting, watching her — their nerve. Vane was not among the figures on the ice. She searched the gray cluster of them and did not find the red parka, and the absence of it frightened her more than its presence would have.

"Where's Vane?" she asked the elder porter, quietly, while Dalla re-rigged the stretcher.

He didn't answer at once. He was looking at his hands. They were a workman's hands, scarred across the knuckles, and one finger was splinted where the rope had burned it.

"Separated," he said finally. "When the first ice went. He was ahead, at the branch chamber — your branch chamber, the one in the old charts. He had the maps your father made. Bought them, years ago, from a broker." His eyes came up, and there was no apology in them, only the flat honesty of a man done lying on behalf of other men. "He told us if he got the chest, every family in the Compact's ledgers got a bonus. He said it like it was charity." A pause. "He's alone in there. In the blue ice. With your father's maps and no one to carry him out if the mountain changes its mind."

Sefa looked down the slope, back toward the pass, toward the branch chamber where Ivo had walked that morning with the strange light of reconciliation on their face, and felt the day tilt beneath her — the rescue finished, and the next thing already beginning.

The elder porter followed her gaze. Then he said the thing that made her understand the truce was no longer unspoken:

"Captain Crane went down that way an hour ago. Alone." He flexed his burned hand. "If you're going after them — and you are, I can see it, it's written on you the way the old names are written under the new ones — then take our rope. Take our medical kit. Take whatever we've got, because we're not climbing back into that ice for him." He looked at the boy on the stretcher, at his own barbed-star tattoo showing where his sleeve had torn. "But you should know. Some of us would have, if we could."

Sefa took the rope. It was Compact rope, gray and gold-flecked, and it weighed nothing and everything.

Behind her, Dalla was already re-pitching the camp, and the boy with the Marnholt star was watching the icefall with his grandmother's patience, and the glacier went on calving, on and on, like a nation counting itself aloud.
