## 33. Vane at Gunpoint of the Glacier

The branch chamber smelled of old paper and cold stone, and for the first time since the sandbar, Ivo Crane felt something close to peace.

They had been alone in it for an hour — Sefa reading names aloud from the register by lamplight, her voice low and steady as a woman reciting a rosary, Dalla dozing against a mule-pack with her hands folded over her walking stick, the two rescued Compact porters curled together near the entrance like men who had not yet decided whether they were prisoners or guests. The chamber was small, a pocket the glacier had hollowed and then forgotten, its walls ribbed with blue ice so dense it drank the lamplight and gave back nothing. The father's cache sat open between them: oilcloth bundles of letters gone soft at the folds, photographs silvered nearly to ghosts, and the register itself — every family, every name, every village on both sides of the range that had ever carried a Vessian word in its mouth.

"Kestrel," Sefa read, and looked up. "There's a Kestrel family. From the eastern valley. Fishers." She said it carefully, watching Ivo's face.

"My mother's people claimed the name came from a bird that flew against the current," Ivo said. "I always thought she was making it up."

"She wasn't." Sefa ran her thumb down the column. "It's here. Three generations before you."

Ivo wanted to say something to that — something large, something worthy of the moment — but what came out was only, "Read me the rest of the line," and Sefa did, and the names went into them like warmth going into cold hands, slowly, painfully, all the way through.

That was when the light changed.

Not the lamp. The entrance. A shadow crossed the pale glow of the ice-tongue beyond it, deliberate, unhurried, and Ivo's hand was on the chest lid before their mind caught up with their body — twenty years of running distilled into a single reflex, shoulders dropping, weight shifting onto the balls of the feet, eyes finding the darkest corner of the room out of pure animal habit.

Aldous Vane stepped into the chamber as if he had been invited.

He was alone. His red parka was torn along one sleeve and crusted with ice where he'd fallen — Ivo had watched him fall, had gone down the rope after him, had hauled him gasping onto stable ice while the astrolabe dropped away beneath them both into the blue dark — and his left glove was missing, the bare hand reddened and stiff. But the pistol in his right hand was dry, and level, and he held it the way he did everything: correctly, without drama, as though it were a document he had been asked to review.

"Gentlefolk," he said. His voice carried oddly in the ice, flattened of all its usual drawing-room polish, thinner than Ivo remembered it from Harrow Bend, from the fuel dock, from every courteous ambush along a thousand kilometers of river. The mountain had gotten into it. "Please don't rise. This will be brief."

Dalla woke without moving. That was the trick of her, Ivo was learning — she could go from sleep to total attention without any visible seam, her stick already gripped, her eyes already measuring the distance between the man and each of them. The two porters near the entrance did not move at all. They looked at Vane the way employees look at an employer who has just become something else.

"You're separated from your team," Ivo said. Their voice came out steadier than they felt. "Your rope team is two hours behind you, maybe three. You came up alone."

"I came ahead." Vane let his gaze travel the chamber — the lamp, the open cache, the register in Sefa's lap, the chest under Ivo's hand. He catalogued it all in about four seconds, and Ivo watched him do it and understood, finally, that this was the whole man: not cruel, not even greedy in any way that would interest a priest, but a creature whose entire soul was filing. Everything in the world was either filed or unfiled, acquired or pending, and the sight of an unfiled thing caused him genuine distress. "My porters are slow because my porters are frightened. Yours, I notice, have changed sides. That's a problem I'll address later."

"They were never yours," Sefa said. "They're from the Erze valley. Your syndicate bought their valley's timber rights in '09 and called it development. They know exactly what you are."

"I'm sure they think they do." Vane took another step forward, boots crunching on ancient grit frozen into the floor. The pistol stayed level, aimed at no one in particular, which was somehow worse than being aimed at someone. "Captain Crane. Miss — or is it Mrs.? No matter. Sefa, daughter of Archivist Demel, late of the Vessian Exile Archive, currently of nowhere, which is the entire difficulty." He smiled, and it was almost warm. "I want to make you an offer. A final one. And I want you to understand, before you refuse it out of hand, that it is genuinely final. Not because I threaten you — although I am holding a gun, and we should both acknowledge that — but because the window is closing. The calving season ends when the snows come, and the snows are coming early. Whatever happens in this chamber in the next ten minutes will be the last negotiation either of us conducts on this subject. After that, the mountain files everything itself."

Ivo's hand was still on the chest. Under their palm, through the lead lining, they could feel the faint cold of the folios inside, the weight of everything their father had carried and never delivered. *You can't buy what already belongs to everyone.* They had rehearsed the sentence on the glacier, in the whiteout, roped to strangers, and now it sat ready behind their teeth like a drawn bolt.

But Vane kept talking, and Ivo had learned enough about him across rivers and passes to know that the talking was never padding. It was the product.

"Here is the offer," Vane said. "You give me the chest. The contents are transferred to Meridian Compact custody under Compact seal, registered, conserved, indexed — I want you to hear those words, Captain, because your father's materials have been sitting in a lead box rotting for thirty years, and mine will sit in climate-controlled vaults for three hundred. You receive full consideration. And—" he raised the bare hand, the frostbitten one, as if taking an oath— "your record burns tonight. All of it. The broker's ledger entry from when you were seventeen. The route-notes sale. Every debt, every alias, every border you crossed under a name that wasn't Crane. Expunged. Not hidden — expunged. There is a difference, and you of all people know it. Hidden records are a leash. Expunged records are freedom. You could moor a boat anywhere in the world and sign your own name to the papers."

The chamber was very quiet. Somewhere far below them, muffled through a hundred meters of ice, the glacier groaned — a long, tectonic complaint, like a ship working at anchor in weather.

Ivo found, to their surprise, that they were not tempted.

They had expected to be. On the river, in the dark watches of the night runs, they had turned the offer over the way a tongue turns a broken tooth. *A clean name.* Twenty years of false papers, of answering to *Crane* only when lying seemed safe, of flinching at registry offices and lockmasters and anyone with a stamp. The offer was not money. Money Ivo had refused before, easily. This was the thing underneath all the running — permission to stop. Vane had understood that from the beginning. It was why he was dangerous, why he had been worth fearing more than any gunman: the man sold people their own futures, and he priced them accurately.

And yet, standing here in their father's chamber, with Sefa's lamp burning and the register open on her knees and the name *Kestrel* written in a dead archivist's careful hand three generations deep — standing here, Ivo felt the offer slide off them like water off waxed canvas.

"No," they said.

Vane's expression didn't change. "You haven't heard the terms."

"I heard them at the fuel dock. I heard them at Harrow Bend. You've been reading me the same contract for two thousand kilometers, Mr. Vane, and every version has the same clause buried in the fine print." Ivo stood. Slowly, hands visible, the way you stand up in a boat when the water's rough — balanced, unhurried, giving the man with the pistol nothing sudden to react to. "The archive goes under your seal. My name goes on the transfer as heir-consent. That's the price. That's always been the price. You don't want the papers, you want my signature. You want the last Crane to certify the erasure of Vessia so the Compact can point at it forever after and say, *even they agreed.*"

"A signature is a formality."

"It's the whole transaction." Ivo took one step, and stopped, and made themselves look at the pistol instead of away from it. "Everything else — the vaults, the conservation, the clean record — that's wrapping. What you're buying is consent. You've bought land and timber and valleys and the names off maps, and every one of those purchases needed somebody on the other side to take the money, because a theft nobody agrees to is just a war, and wars leave witnesses. You don't want witnesses, Mr. Vane. You want paperwork."

Something moved behind Vane's eyes. Not anger. Recalculation.

"You're romanticizing theft," he said quietly. "Your father stole these documents from the Compact's legitimate claim. We purchased the Vessian portfolio in good faith from the provisional government-in-exile's creditors—"

"The government-in-exile was four clerks and a filing cabinet in a rented room in Ossory," Sefa said, rising beside Ivo, the register still cradled in her arms like a child. "And the creditors were your own shell companies. My father wrote it all down. Dates, names, account numbers. It's in the folios. Would you like me to read it to you? I've had a week to memorize it."

For a moment — just a moment — Aldous Vane looked tired. The mountain had taken the polish off him layer by layer, the way it takes the paint off anything left out in the weather, and what was underneath was not a monster. It was a middle-aged man with frostbite on one hand, holding a gun in a hole in a glacier, trying to close a deal that had been slipping through his fingers since a sandbar appeared on no chart.

"The register," he said. His voice dropped, and Ivo realized with a strange lurch that this was the real voice, the one under all the others, and it sounded almost pleading. "Give me the register, at least. The chest can stay. The letters, the photographs — sentiment, keep it all. But the register is the asset. Names mapped to descendants, claims mapped to bloodlines. Without it, the rest of the archive is... literature. With it, it's title. You're carrying a deed, Captain. Deeds get enforced. By whoever holds them."

"And if I give it to you," Ivo said, "you enforce it. Family by family. Village by village. You find everyone whose grandmother spoke Vessian and you buy their past for the price of a winter's coal, and the ones who won't sell—" they thought of the informer on the river, the pilot with his son's medical debt, and their throat tightened— "the ones who won't sell, you wait out. You're patient. It's the only thing you've ever been."

"Yes," Vane said simply. "That is what I do. And I will keep doing it whether you give me the register or not. The only question your refusal answers is whether you do it the hard way or the easy way. Refusal doesn't save them, Captain. It only makes you feel clean while they're bought anyway."

"That's true," Ivo said.

Sefa turned to look at them. So did Dalla, motionless by her pack, and the two porters by the entrance, and the lamplight held them all in one small ring of gold against all that drinking blue.

"But here's what you haven't costed out," Ivo went on, and their voice found something it hadn't had before — not courage exactly; more like ballast. "You're right that the register is a deed. You're wrong about who holds it. It's not mine to sell because it was never my father's to keep. He was a custodian. Custody isn't ownership — it's a job. And the job ends when the mail gets delivered." They nodded at Sefa. "She started delivering it yesterday. In the villages on the western slope. Do you know what happens to a deed when every person named in it already knows what it says? When they've read their own names, and their neighbors' names, and copied them into their own books, and taught them to their children on the walk home?" Ivo shook their head slowly. "You can't buy what already belongs to everyone. You can't even sue them for it. There's no counterparty left. There's just — everyone."

Vane was silent for a long moment. The pistol drifted, very slightly, downward — not lowered, but no longer quite aimed, the way a pen drifts when the writer loses the thread of the sentence.

"That's not how property works," he said at last, and there was something almost wounded in it, the bafflement of a man shown a machine that ran on no fuel he recognized.

"No," Ivo agreed. "It's how memory works. Different system. Older. Terrible bookkeeping — nothing's indexed, nothing's sealed, copies everywhere, provenance impossible to establish. You'd hate it." They paused. "Although I notice your porters learned it fast enough. Two days off your rope team and they're asking Sefa to write their grandmothers' names in the front of the register. In pencil. So it can be corrected. She told them that's how archives work — everything in pencil, eventually."

"Sefa." Vane said the name carefully, testing it. "Daughter of Demel. Your father breached contract—"

"My father honored a better one," Sefa said. Her voice didn't rise at all, and somehow that was louder than shouting. "He promised a nation it wouldn't be forgotten. You were one of the parties trying to make him break it. He died keeping it. Read that however you like in your ledger."

Below them, the glacier groaned again — longer this time, deeper, a sound like a door being tested in its frame. Grit sifted from the ceiling of the chamber. The lamp flame leaned and recovered.

Vane glanced upward. Ivo watched him do the arithmetic every person on this glacier was doing now, all day, in every silence: *how many days left in the window; how many days to the far tree line; how early will the snows come.* Then he looked back at the chest, and the register, and the two people standing between him and both, and Ivo saw him arrive at the number and not like it.

"You understand," Vane said, "that I can simply take it. I have a firearm. You have a lamp."

"You could," Ivo said. "And then you'd have to carry it out yourself, past your own porters, who would help you load it and lose pieces of it the whole way down and swear afterward that the ice ate them. And then you'd have to explain to your principals why the delivery is one chest of rotted letters and no register, while every village on two slopes is copying names into schoolbooks faster than your lawyers can draft acquisition papers." Ivo tilted their head. "Or — and I say this with respect, because you've chased us further and smarter than anyone I've ever run from — you could sit down, and warm your hand, and let Sefa show you the photograph of the archive staff taken the summer before the drowning. There's a young clerk in the back row with your jaw. Did you know that? We found him two nights ago. Second cousin, once removed, on your mother's side. Vessian spelling of the surname. It's in the register, Mr. Vane. Page forty-one. You're in it too."

The silence that followed was the longest of Ivo's life.

Vane's face did not collapse. Nothing so generous. But something behind it shifted — a load-bearing wall developing its first crack — and the pistol, finally, deliberately, lowered until it pointed at the floor. He did not holster it. He was not a man who surrendered tools. But he pointed it at the ground as though filing it, temporarily, under *pending.*

"That," he said quietly, "is a fabrication."

"It's in pencil," Sefa said. "So it can be corrected. Bring your own records. Bring proof. That's how archives work — we welcome correction. It's the one thing we have that the Compact never will." She turned the register toward him, open, the lamplight falling across columns of names in her father's hand, and held it out — not handing it over, never that, just letting him see. "Page forty-one."

And Aldous Vane, syndicate officer, buyer of nations, the most patient man Ivo had ever feared, stood in the blue heart of a dying glacier with a gun he was no longer pointing and looked at the page.

He looked at it for a very long time.

When he finally raised his eyes, the glacier groaned again — louder, closer, a cracking report somewhere above them followed by a long rumbling exhalation, and grit rained down through the lamplight, and Dalla was on her feet with her stick braced, and the two porters had scrambled clear of the entrance — and in that shudder of the whole world, Vane said, in a voice stripped down to bare wire:

"How many others?"

And above them, the ice began to answer.
