## 34. The Ice Gives Way

Sefa heard the glacier speak before she understood what it was saying.

They had been in the branch chamber perhaps an hour — long enough for Dalla to get a small spirit stove burning in the lee of the ice wall, long enough for the rescued Compact porters to huddle at the far side with the wary silence of men who had not yet decided whether they were prisoners or guests. The chamber itself was a pocket the mountain had carved for its own reasons: a vaulted hollow of blue ice above, a floor of old snow compacted to stone, and at its center, spread out on a groundsheet like a merchant's wares, everything her father's friend Crane had carried up here and left for a future no one had promised him.

Letters bundled in oilcloth. Photographs in paper sleeves gone soft as cloth at the corners. The register itself, open now, its columns of names running down the page like water finding its level. Sefa had been reading aloud — not to anyone in particular, but because the names wanted saying, because a name unspoken was only ink and a name spoken was a person — when the sound came.

Not a crack. Not the pistol-crack of stressed ice that she had learned to flinch from over the past days. This was deeper, slower, a groan that seemed to come up through the soles of her boots and into her teeth, the sound of something vast changing its mind.

"Out," Dalla said. She did not shout it. That was the terrible thing about the old woman; even now, even with the floor of the world beginning to move, her voice stayed at the pitch of a woman telling you supper was ready. "Off the chamber floor. All of you. Now."

Sefa grabbed for the register. Ivo was already moving — she saw it in the periphery of her fear, the captain's body understanding before the mind did, one hand closing on the chest's rope handle, the other hauling at the sleeve of the nearest porter. The stove hissed and died under Dalla's boot. And Vane —

Vane was standing at the chamber's far edge, where the floor thinned toward the black seam of a crevasse that had been hidden under drifted snow, invisible, patient. He had come into the chamber alone, separated from his team in the whiteout two days past, limping on the leg the mountain had already punished once, and he had stood there with his pistol held low and his lawyer's calm intact while Ivo answered him with the register open between them like a shield made of paper. *You can't buy what already belongs to everyone.* Sefa would carry those words of Ivo's for the rest of her life, and she would carry too the look on Vane's face — not rage, not defeat, but the flicker of a man recalculating, always recalculating, even as the arithmetic stopped making sense.

Now the arithmetic stopped entirely.

The floor went with a sound like a door being torn off its hinges. Not all of it — a slab the size of a threshing floor, the section where Vane stood, calving away from the chamber's heart and dropping into the dark beneath with a smoothness that was worse than violence, as if the ice had simply opened its hand and let him go. His pistol clattered somewhere below. His voice did not follow it down; there was a half-second where Sefa thought he was simply gone, erased the way he erased nations, quietly and without witnesses —

— and then the rope.

He had roped himself. Of course he had; whatever else Aldous Vane was, he was a man who read fine print, and the fine print of glaciers said *tie in.* The rope snapped taut from the lip of the new crevasse and ran back across the chamber floor to the anchor he'd set — she hadn't even seen him set it, sometime during the standoff, while they were all watching his face and his gun — and now it hummed with his weight, singing a single high note, and the anchor was a buried ice screw whose placement Sefa could not vouch for, and the whole arrangement depended on physics having mercy on a man who had never once extended any.

Nobody moved. That was the truth she would have to live with: for three full seconds, nobody moved. The porters looked at their boots. Dalla looked at the rope with an expression Sefa couldn't read. And Sefa herself stood with the register crushed against her chest, thinking — she would confess this later, on the far side, in the valley, to anyone who asked — thinking *let him fall, let the mountain keep its ledger balanced.*

Ivo moved.

Later, trying to reconstruct it, Sefa could never get the sequence right. There was the chest, set down — not dropped, *set down*, gently, the way you moor a boat, because some habits go deeper than emergency. There was Ivo's knife out and cutting a length of rope from the spare coil, eyes never leaving the humming line. There was the moment Ivo reached the crevasse lip and looked down into it, and Sefa looked too, and what she saw was blue — not the friendly blue of the arch overhead but the blue of deep water, of places light gives up on, going down and down until the blue became a rumor of itself.

"Ivo." Her own voice, cracked and strange. "Ivo, don't."

"He's roped," Ivo said, as if that settled it, as if that were a reason rather than the whole problem. "If his anchor holds, I can reach him. If it doesn't—" They were feeding rope through their harness, tying in, checking knots with fingers that shook only slightly, and Sefa realized with a lurch that the shaking wasn't fear. It was fury. Ivo was furious, furiously calm, doing the thing they most despised because it was the thing that needed doing. "Hold my line. Both of you. Wrap it around your backs, sit into it, and do not stand up."

Dalla moved first. Whatever she thought of Vane — and Sefa had heard her opinion of map-buyers expressed once, in three words, two of which Sefa had had to ask about — she wrapped the rope around her back and sat into the snow like a woman settling onto a bench she'd sat on for sixty years. One of the porters joined her, then the second, shame-faced, wordless. Sefa took her place last, the rope biting across her shoulders, and felt the weight of another human life arrive in her body like ballast.

"Ivo," she said again, and didn't know what came after.

Ivo looked at her. In the blue light of the chamber, with the register still pressed against her ribs and the rope humming its note, Ivo Crane smiled — small, crooked, the smile of someone who has finally caught up with themselves after twenty years of running. "Somebody has to be the kind of person Father pretended I was," they said. "Might as well start today."

And they went over the edge backward, feet against the ice face, paying out line, descending into the blue.

* * *

What Sefa knew of the next minutes came mostly through the rope.

It talked to her. That was the uncanny thing she had never anticipated — that a rope under load is a language, that every movement at the far end arrives as vibration in your spine. She felt Ivo's controlled descents, boot-spikes chipping purchase, the rhythm of a climber who had never climbed but who understood, bone-deep, how weight moves through a system, how current runs, how you read force the way you read water. She felt the moment Ivo reached Vane — a jolt, a change in the load, the note of the rope shifting key. She heard Vane's voice rise up out of the crevasse, thin with cold and distance, and she was close enough to the lip to catch the words, and what she caught undid her a little:

"You're a fool," Vane was saying. "You understand that. I offered you everything. A clean slate. Your name off every list. And you're going to die in a hole in the ice for me."

"Not for you," Ivo's voice floated back, level, breathless. "For the ledger. Somebody has to show it a different column."

Then the work began, and there was no more talking, because hauling is a whole-body argument and it leaves room for nothing else. Sefa threw her whole weight backward into the rope, Dalla beside her, the porters beside Dalla, four bodies leaning against the pull while Ivo below rigged Vane — she learned afterward that Ivo had clipped him to their own harness, double-secured, trusting nothing, not even the man's own frozen hands — and they hauled, and the rope burned, and the snow under Sefa's boots packed and squeaked, and above them the chamber's remaining ceiling groaned its slow disapproval, as if the mountain resented the rescue and might yet veto it.

Vane came over the lip the way drowned things come ashore: gracelessly, entirely, a dead weight that suddenly remembered it was alive. He collapsed on the solid ice ten feet from the edge, coughing, his red parka shredded to gray at the shoulders, his face the color of the old snow. The porters dragged him clear without being asked. Whatever they thought of him — and Sefa had seen enough of conscripted men to know it was not loyalty — he was their employer, and more than that he was a body, and bodies got pulled clear. That was the law the mountain enforced and no syndicate could amend.

Which left Ivo still below.

"Haul," Ivo called up, and the note of the rope changed, and Sefa hauled, and the rope came hand over hand, and then it came wrong — a sudden slackness, a give, the sickening lightness of a line that has lost its argument with gravity. She heard Ivo cry out, short and sharp, not a scream but the sound of something letting go.

"The astrolabe," Dalla said.

Sefa turned. The old woman was looking down into the crevasse, her weathered face unreadable, and Sefa followed her gaze and understood. Ivo had carried the astrolabe since the sandbar — had carried it through locks and whiteouts and avalanches, had slept with it inside their coat like a second heartbeat, her grandmother's engraving, Dalla's mother's engraving, the instrument that had bought them their guide and their way through the pass. It must have been clipped to Ivo's belt, or slung at their hip, and in the final haul — in the moment the rope went slack and Ivo lunged for the lip — it had torn free.

It fell the way small heavy things fall, without ceremony. Sefa watched it turn once in the blue light, brass catching a last coin of illumination, and then the blue took it, and the blue kept it, and there was no sound at all, which was somehow the worst part. An object that had crossed a nation and a century and a family's whole broken history, and it ended in silence, swallowed by the same indifferent depth that had almost taken Vane.

A hand appeared over the lip. Then a shoulder, an arm, the torn shoulder of a coat. Ivo came up over the edge on their own arms, rolled onto solid ice, and lay there breathing in great ragged pulls, staring at the ceiling of blue above them as if it were the sky.

"The astrolabe," Sefa said. Not a question. She already knew.

"Gone." Ivo said it to the ceiling. Their voice was scraped raw. "Down where the river goes."

"It was my mother's," Dalla said. Everyone stilled. The old woman knelt slowly, joints cracking, and put one brown hand flat on Ivo's sternum, feeling for damage or heartbeat or both. "My mother engraved it so children would know where they were from. You know what she'd say, if she could see where it is now?" She waited. Ivo shook their head, a tiny motion against the ice. "She'd say an astrolabe's job is to find things. Let it find the bottom of the mountain. We know where we're from. It's written down." She nodded at the register, still clutched against Sefa's chest, dented now with the shape of her grip. "It's written in *people*. Brass was only ever the backup copy."

Ivo laughed. It came out broken, half sob, but it was laughter, and Sefa found herself laughing too, kneeling in the melt-slush with rope burns across her shoulders, and then the porters were laughing, exhausted hysterical laughter, and even Vane made a sound that in a better man might have been a laugh, lying wrecked and dripping on the ice with his empire reduced to a wet red parka and a lost pistol.

The chamber chose that moment to answer. A long crack ran across the remaining floor with a sound like a whip, and Dalla was on her feet calling them all toward the tunnel mouth, and they went — Ivo hauled upright by Sefa's arm, the chest shouldered by a porter who took it without being told, the register passed hand to hand like a child over a fence — and behind them, with a sigh that sounded almost satisfied, the branch chamber let go of its floor entirely and dropped away into the blue dark, taking the standoff, the pistol, the offer, and the last of the Compact's claim with it.

* * *

They camped that night in a wind-carved hollow below the icefall, and Sefa could not sleep.

The glacier spoke all night — that was the thing no one told you, that calving season was not an event but a conversation, booms and groans rolling up and down the valley like a parliament in session. Somewhere below them, the Compact's main party was regrouping; two of the porters had gone down at dusk under a flag of truce Ivo had insisted on, carrying word to Vane's team that their employer lived, that the archive was not for sale, and that any man who wanted passage down the eastern side under truce would be given it. Whether the message would hold, whether Vane's expedition would honor a truce its leader had never authorized — none of that was settled. What was settled sat around the small stove: Dalla mending rope, the porters sleeping the sleep of the genuinely spent, and Ivo sitting at the hollow's edge with their back to the group, looking east.

Sefa went and sat beside them. Below, the glacier's tongue gleamed under a rising half-moon, fissured and blue and alive, and beyond it the eastern slopes fell away into darkness where, if the charts were true, the villages waited — the villages with the names in the register, the families who did not know yet what was coming down the mountain toward them.

"You went down the rope," she said. It wasn't what she'd meant to say first. "After everything he offered you. After the contract, after Harrow Bend, after all of it. You went down the rope for him."

Ivo was quiet a long time. When they spoke, their voice had found its river-bottom again, low and even.

"When I was sixteen I sold my father's route-notes," they said. "You know that. I've said it out loud exactly twice in my life, and both times were to you. Do you know why I did it? Not for the boat. Well — for the boat. But underneath that, because I wanted the name Crane to mean *nothing*. I wanted to trade the whole inheritance away, every box, every route, every summer he walked the Karst, and buy myself a clean hull with no history in it. And the Compact took the notes and started buying, and I got my boat, and for twenty years I told myself the trade was done, closed, paid." They turned the empty space at their hip where the astrolabe had hung, an unconscious gesture, a phantom limb. "Going down that rope was the first time I ever gave anything back. And the joke of it — the ugly, perfect joke — is that the man I gave it to is the one who taught me the price of selling."

Sefa watched the moonlight move on the ice. "Your father would have gone down the rope," she said. "I knew him only in letters, in other people's memories. But everyone tells the same story. He carried medicine upstream in a famine year. He carried boxes over a mountain for fifteen summers and told no one. He would have gone down the rope for Aldous Vane, and hated every foot of it, and gone anyway."

"That's what frightens me," Ivo said. "That it's becoming true. That I'm becoming—"

"Yours," Sefa finished. "You're becoming yours. His was just the first draft."

Below them, the glacier boomed — a great detonation somewhere in the icefall, followed by the long rumble of a serac surrendering, the sound rolling up the valley and washing over their hollow like a tide. Tomorrow they would race the window down the eastern side, the snows already days early, the truce holding by threads. Tomorrow the register would begin its real journey, door to door, name to name. Tonight the mountain spoke its own language, and Sefa found, listening, that she could almost translate it: not threat, exactly. Calendar. The landscape keeping Vessia's time, the way it had kept it all along, opening and closing its pass on a schedule older than any syndicate's ledger.

Ivo touched her shoulder once, briefly, and went to check the ropes.

Sefa stayed at the hollow's edge a while longer, the register warm against her knees, and watched the moon climb over the icefall — and it was then, in the long reverberating quiet after the last boom, that she heard it: voices, faint but unmistakable, coming up the glacier tongue from the south. Many voices. Moving fast.
