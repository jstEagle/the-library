## 35. Descent Before the Snows

The mountain gave them three days, and it counted them out loud.

Ivo heard it first as a change in the glacier's voice — the deep, patient groaning of the pass giving way to something quicker, sharper, the cracks of ice letting go like knuckles popping. By the second morning the calving had become constant, seracs shedding their shoulders in white thunder, and Dalla stood at the head of the line with her ear tilted toward the icefall the way Ivo had spent a lifetime tilting an ear toward water.

"Listen," she said. "It's hurrying. We hurry too."

They went down.

Not the way they'd come up. The eastern side of the Karst fell away in long shelves of blue ice and grey scree, the route-notes' inked line threading between them like a river channel drawn by someone who loved it. Ivo walked with the charts open against their chest inside an oilcloth sleeve, checking each cairn against the father's hand — *cairn of red stone, keep it on your left shoulder; where the ice splits, take the right fork though the left looks easier; do not trust the shelf below the second serac after noon* — and found, every time, that the dead man had been right. It should have felt like being led. Instead it felt like being read. Like walking through a letter addressed to them personally, written thirty years before they knew they'd need it.

Behind them came the others.

That was the strangeness of it, the thing Ivo kept turning over like a coin whose weight was wrong. The Compact's porters — six of them now, minus two lost to the crevasse field and one to a twisted ankle on the moraine — carried their loads alongside instead of behind. No one had negotiated this. No one had spoken it aloud. It had simply happened on the tongue of the glacier, in the hours after Sefa hauled those two men out of the blue dark, when Vane's expedition stopped being a column and became a crowd. The porters' ropes were coiled now rather than strung. They walked where they liked. And somewhere in the last day, without ceremony, their loads had changed: less of Vane's survey gear, more of the shared food, the shared fuel, the shared weight of the chest itself, which passed from shoulder to shoulder down the line like something being handed through a village rather than guarded by an army.

One of them — the broad-faced man Sefa had pulled from the ice, whose name was Teodor and who spoke river-Vessian with a hill accent — had taken to walking beside Ivo without asking why. He pointed at the route-notes once, at the small marginal sketch of a chapel with stars cut into its lintel.

"My grandmother talked about that place," he said. "She called it the post office." He didn't explain, and Ivo didn't ask. Some inheritances you recognize on sight.

Vane came last.

He limped. The leg the crevasse had nearly taken was bound tight beneath his expedition trousers, and he moved with the careful, economical steps of a man who had re-priced pain and found it acceptable. His own men watched him the way cargo watches a hold door — not hostile, not loyal, simply aware that the arrangement could change. He had no rope anymore. He had refused Ivo's twice, and then accepted the third offer without acknowledgment, which Ivo decided was the most honest thing the man had done in the whole of the Karst.

"You're slowing us down," Ivo told him on the second morning, not unkindly.

"I'm paying for my slowness," Vane said. "In attention. You'll find I'm very good at spending what I have left." He adjusted his stride around a rib of rock, breath smoking. "Captain Crane. May I say something that will sound like flattery and isn't?"

"You'll say it whether I permit it or not."

"I've acquired eleven archives," Vane said. "Eleven nations, if we're generous with the word — and we were always generous with the word, that was part of the craft. Do you know what they all had in common? Someone at the center who believed the past was a thing that could be *held*. A king's library. A church vault. A family that thought ownership was protection." He looked up at the ice walls leaning over the route, ancient and indifferent. "You're the first custodian I've met who understood that the only safe place for memory is everywhere. Scattered. Unbuyable." He smiled, thin and genuine, which was worse than the other kind. "If I were twenty years younger and less principled about my own syndicate, I'd be trying to hire you."

"You tried to buy me."

"I try to buy everyone. It's how I say hello."

* * *

They camped that night on a shelf of moraine above a meltwater lake gone green as bottle glass, and the mountain counted its days at them all night long — the crack and boom of distant calving rolling across the ice like artillery from a war nobody had declared. Ivo took first watch because sleep had become a country they visited briefly and without luggage.

Sefa came and sat beside them at midnight, wrapped in the blanket she'd refused to surrender even at the coldest camps, and for a while neither spoke. Below the shelf, the Compact's fire burned small and disciplined. Above, the stars did their slow wheeling, and Ivo found themself navigating by habit — locating north, locating the pass behind them, locating the route ahead — before remembering there was no one left to hide the boat from.

"The register," Sefa said finally. She had it in her coat, as she always did now, wrapped in oilcloth against her body like a second heartbeat. "Teodor asked me today what was in it. Names, I told him. Family names, village names, what each household gave to the archive and what the archive owed back." She turned the bundle over in her hands. "He said his grandmother's name would be in it. He said it like a man asking whether a well still has water."

"It does."

"I know." She was quiet a moment. "Ivo. When we get down — when we're past the snowline and the pass is closed and none of this can be followed — what happens to me? To it?" She tapped the register. "We've been running so long I've forgotten how to arrive anywhere."

Ivo looked east, where the ice gave way by degrees to black rock, then to the faint suggestion of forest far below, invisible but certain, the way a river is certain beneath fog. "My father carried these boxes over this range every summer for fifteen years," they said. "And I sold the map of how he did it for enough to buy a rotten hull with an engine. So I don't know that I'm the person to ask about arriving." They paused. "But I think maybe arriving looks like what you did on the ice. Pulling people out. Even theirs."

Sefa snorted softly. "Even theirs."

"*Especially* theirs. That's the whole trick, isn't it? That's what the chest was. Not treasure. Mail." Ivo nodded toward the sleeping shapes below, the porters curled together against the cold, Vane apart from all of them, awake — Ivo could tell by the absence of snoring — staring at the same stars. "Undelivered mail. Thirty years late. And here we are with half the delivery crew made up of the syndicate's own hands."

"They'll go back to him," Sefa said. "When we reach the valley. They'll take his wages and forget our names."

"Some will." Ivo fed a stick to the fire and watched it catch. "But they'll know where they're from. That's not nothing. That's the entire something, actually. Ask your father. Ask mine."

Sefa looked at them sideways. "Did you just make a joke about your father?"

"He and I are negotiating terms," Ivo said. "It's going better since he died."

She laughed — a real laugh, brief and startled out of her, swallowed quickly by the cold — and Ivo realized it might be the first time either of them had laughed above the tree line. The mountain boomed distantly, approving or objecting, impossible to say.

* * *

On the third morning the ice ended.

It went quickly, the way rivers end — not with a wall but with a thinning, the glacier splitting into braided tongues that unraveled into dirty snowfields, then into scree stitched with the first stubborn grasses, then into a valley so sudden and green that Ivo's eyes watered adjusting to it. The air thickened. Somewhere below, water ran in a voice Ivo recognized: a river, young and shallow and loud, falling off the mountain's knee in white steps.

Dalla stopped at the edge of the last snowfield and took off her hat, and the wind took her grey hair and made a flag of it. She looked down the valley for a long time.

"There," she said, pointing to a thread of smoke rising from a fold in the green. "Kestrel Hollow. My mother's mother wintered there." She put the hat back on and turned to face them all — Ivo, Sefa, the porters, the limping man in the expensive parka — with the authority of someone who had carried loads over this range longer than any of them had been alive. "Down there, the mountain stops being in charge. Remember what it taught you anyway."

They descended into the trees.

It was Teodor who broke the truce's silence, an hour later, when the path widened enough for two abreast. He fell in beside Ivo and walked a while before speaking, choosing words the way a man chooses footing on loose rock.

"When we reach the trailhead," he said, "there will be a telegraph office. And Mr. Vane will send a wire, and by the time the snows close the pass, there will be men in the valley with offers. Good ones. Real ones." He glanced at Ivo. "You know this."

"I know this."

"And you're not afraid."

Ivo considered it honestly. "I'm afraid the way I was afraid of rapids when I was young. It doesn't go away. You just learn that fear is information, not instruction." They shifted the chest on their shoulder — it had come back to them somewhere on the descent, passed hand to hand down the line until it arrived, inevitably, at the captain. "What about you?"

Teodor was quiet for several steps. "My grandfather was conscripted into a war for a country that erased his village afterward. He used to say: they can take the name off the map, but they cannot stop the river remembering which way it runs." He smiled, broad-faced and tired. "I carried Mr. Vane's boxes for four years, Captain. I never once asked what was in them. Then I pulled myself out of a crevasse onto your lady's rope, and she asked me my grandmother's name." He shook his head slowly. "Four years. Nobody ever asked."

Ahead of them, the trees opened, and the valley received them.

It was evening when they reached the trailhead — a scatter of timber houses, a mule paddock, a shrine with offerings weathered past reading, and the telegraph office Teodor had promised, its window lit yellow against the blue dusk. Word ran ahead of them the way word does in small places; by the time they reached the well at the village center, people had come out of their houses and stood watching the procession come down the street: two strangers, an old porter, six hired men with Compact canvas on their packs, and a limping gentleman in a red parka whom everyone seemed to recognize on sight, because of course they did — Vane had bought this valley's wool prices two seasons ago, and everyone remembered the man who paid fair and owned everything.

Vane stopped at the well. He looked at the villagers looking at him, and something moved behind his eyes that Ivo couldn't price — regret, perhaps, or inventory.

"Gentlemen," he said to his porters, and his voice carried the lawyer's calm intact, barely scuffed. "Your wages, plus hazard pay, plus passage home. The wire goes out tonight; the sledges meet us at the eastern depot in ten days." He paused. "Or," he said, and let the word sit in the cold air like a coin on a table, "you do whatever you like. The Compact holds no one. It only buys."

No one moved toward him. No one moved away. Teodor set down his load, straightened his back, and looked at the lit window of the telegraph office, and then at Sefa, and then at the houses where grandmothers were surely already telling children about the post office under the ice.

Ivo set the chest down on the well's stone lip, in full view of the whole street, and began unbuckling the straps.

Vane watched. "You're doing it here? In front of everyone?"

"That was always the point," Ivo said. "You just never had a category for it."

From the nearest house, a door opened, and an old woman came out into the dusk carrying a lamp, and behind her a boy, and behind the boy more doors opening, lamps kindling one by one down the street like a river answering a lock-gate — and the old woman looked at the chest, and then at Sefa, and said in hill-accented Vessian, clear enough for the whole street:

"We've been waiting a long time for the post."
