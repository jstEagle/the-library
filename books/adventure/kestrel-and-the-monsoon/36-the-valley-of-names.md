## 36. The Valley of Names

The eastern slopes announced themselves in the smell of woodsmoke and cut hay, and Sefa, who had spent eleven days learning to walk downhill without falling, stopped on the shoulder of the trail and breathed it in like a woman surfacing from deep water.

Below them the valley opened in a long green fan — terraced fields stitched to the mountainside with drystone walls, a river running silver through the middle of it, small and quick after the glacier's roar, and villages strung along its banks like beads on a cord. Smoke rose from chimneys in straight columns. Someone was beating a carpet over a fence rail, three fields away, and the sound carried up to them clean as a bell.

"That's Ostmere," Dalla said, pointing with her chin. She had come down with them — none of them had believed she would, but she had, mule-less now, walking with a stick she cut herself on the second morning and refused to explain. "And past it, Vell's Hollow, and past that the river villages all the way down to the lowlands. My mother sold astrolabes to half these houses. They won't remember why they hang the star-charms over their doors. But they'll know you."

"They won't know me," Sefa said.

"They'll know your name," Dalla said, "once you say it right."

Ivo said nothing. Ivo had been saying very little since the ice, since the rope, since hauling Aldous Vane out of the blue dark like a man pulling his own drowning self up hand over hand. They stood beside her with the chest strapped to their back — the chest that had started everything, its lead lining dented, its seal broken, its contents already half-gone, distributed page by page into Sefa's coat pockets, into oilcloth packets, into the careful keeping of strangers on the far side of the pass. What remained inside it was paper. Only paper. It weighed what paper weighs, which is nothing, which is everything.

"Come on, then," Ivo said at last, and started down.

* * *

The first house in Ostmere was a low stone farmhouse with geese in the yard and a star-charm nailed above the door — five points, worked in tin gone green with weather, exactly the mark stamped on the chest's lid, exactly the mark carved into the frozen chapel's lintel a thousand meters above the snowline. Sefa stood looking at it for so long that a dog came out to inspect her, then a child, then a broad-shouldered woman wiping flour off her hands onto an apron.

"You're off the mountain," the woman said. Not a question. Word traveled fast in valleys; the porters' network was older than any road.

"We are," Sefa said. Her Vessian came out before she could stop it, the old formal register her father had drilled into her, *we have crossed, we bring greetings from the high places.* The woman's face did something complicated — a flicker, a stillness, like a lamp turned down and then relit.

"My grandmother talked like that," she said slowly. "When she didn't want the tax man understanding."

"I know." Sefa's throat was tight. "That's why we've come."

She unslung the register.

It was not a grand thing. Her father — no. *Their* father, the elder Crane's hand was all through it too, corrections and additions in a different ink, two custodians' work braided together across decades — had bound it in oiled canvas against water, and the pages were dense, cramped, loving: family names, lineages, the villages where each family had settled after the erasure, the names they had kept and the names they had been forced to trade away, cross-referenced with the new names the border clerks had assigned. *Vessin → Vestal. Vessarion → Vernon. Ashkeli → Ashley.* Whole genealogies compressed into clerk's shorthand, waiting, all these years, for someone to read them aloud.

Sefa had thought, crossing the ice, that delivering it would feel like emptying out. It did not. It felt like planting.

The woman's name was Marta Vestal, and when Sefa found her line in the register — third column, fourth entry, *Vessin of the Ostmere terraces, millers by trade, seven children carried east* — Marta sat down on her own doorstep without meaning to, apron and all, and read the entry four times.

"Millers," she said. "We're not millers. We're—"

"There's a millstone leaning against your barn," Ivo said quietly. Sefa hadn't even noticed it. "Half-buried. Been there longer than the barn, I'd guess."

Marta looked at the millstone. Then at the register. Then at her own hands, floured to the wrist.

"Come in," she said. "Both of you. Come in and eat, and tell me everything, and don't leave anything out because my grandmother used to say—" she stopped, pressed her knuckles to her mouth. "My grandmother used to say the names would come home one day. We thought she meant the dead."

* * *

They stayed three days in Ostmere, and it went like this:

In the mornings Sefa walked the village with the register open on her forearm like a server carrying a dish, going door to door, reading entries, asking questions. *Do you keep a midwinter feast with honey-bread shaped like boats? Do you sing a song about a gray bird on the water? Does anyone in your house remember a word that sounds like* vessa *and means* home *?* In the afternoons the answers came back to her, and she wrote them into the margins in her own hand — the archive growing again, but outward now instead of upward, roots instead of a tower.

The gray bird song was everywhere. That undid her more than anything. Half the valley sang some version of it — a kestrel, a hawk, a bird on the water — with words worn smooth into nonsense syllables, *shera-lai, shera-lai*, and when Sefa sang the true verses back to them, the old grammar her father had preserved in folio forty-one, the villagers went silent as if someone had opened a door onto a lit room they'd lived beside all their lives without knowing.

An old man named Tam wept into his beer in the taproom and made her sing it twice more. "My mother hummed that," he kept saying. "My mother hummed that and I never knew what she was humming *about*."

"It wasn't about anything," Sefa told him. "It was about being from somewhere. That's what it's about."

In the evenings Ivo sat by fires and said little, but people brought them things to look at — boat-models carved in a style Ivo recognized from river towns a thousand kilometers west, knot-work on harness that matched patterns in the chest's folios, a christening custom where the father whispered the child's *true* name in its ear before the public one, "just between us and the water." Ivo held those objects the way they'd once held the wheel of the kestrel-boat, and Sefa watched them understand, night by night, that their father's route-notes had not been a smuggler's ledger at all. Every summer crossing, every box carried over the Karst, every favor called in at Tern Lock and Ossory Quay — it had been a delivery route. The longest-running postal service in exile. And Ivo had spent twenty years ashamed of a man who had simply never stopped working.

On the third evening Marta's eldest daughter asked Ivo directly: "Were you the one who brought it over?"

"The mountain brought it over," Ivo said. "We just tried not to drop it."

But later, banked firelight, Sefa heard them talking to Dalla by the door. "He never told me," Ivo said. "Twenty years I ran that river hating him for a smuggler, and he never once said *come with me, I'll show you what it's for.*"

"He couldn't," Dalla said. "A secret shared is a secret halved, and a nation's memory doesn't survive halving. He was waiting for you to be ready."

"I sold his notes, Dalla."

"And the mountain ate the buyers," Dalla said, "and here you are, carrying the rest. Settle it however you like, but settle it. Guilt is just grief wearing a workman's coat. Put it down and pick up the grief; grief you can carry properly."

* * *

Vell's Hollow took four days, because word had outrun them.

They arrived to find people waiting — not crowds, nothing so organized, but knots of families outside houses, a woman standing at the crossroads with a lantern though it was barely dusk, an ancient pair of sisters who had walked in from a side-valley on sticks because their grandmother had been born a Vessarion and *someone had to be there when the book came.*

Sefa learned quickly that the register could not simply be handed over whole. That was the temptation — give each family a copy, done, archive delivered, task complete. But her father's marginalia warned against it, in his careful hand: *A name given to one house is a treasure. A name given to every house is a target. Let them find each other; do not deliver them pre-assembled.* So she copied. She sat at kitchen tables under lamplight and copied out single lineages onto single sheets, sealed them in wax — she'd bought sealing wax in Last Light, in another life — and gave each family only their own thread, plus the knowledge that other threads existed, plus, when they asked, the name of the next valley over where a related line had settled.

Let them find each other. Let the network rebuild itself the way it had always rebuilt itself: sideways, household to household, grandmother to granddaughter, in songs and charms and whispered true-names, too diffuse to buy, too alive to burn.

Some deliveries were hard. One man in Vell's Hollow took his sheet, read it, and burned it in his stove while they watched. "My grandfather chose to forget," he said. "Who am I to un-choose for him?" Sefa bowed to that, and left him the wax seal as a keepsake, and cried about it later behind the barn, and Ivo found her there and said nothing, just stood with her in the cold until the crying was done, which was its own kind of fluency.

Other deliveries were impossible to describe. The sisters from the side-valley — ninety and ninety-three — held their sheet between them and began, unprompted, to recite the names of their dead in the old cadence, call and response, the way it had been done before there were books, and Sefa realized she was hearing the archive's original format, oral, breathing, distributed among the living all along. The register hadn't created the memory. It had only written down what the valley had never stopped doing.

*You can't buy what already belongs to everyone,* Ivo had told Vane in the blue ice. Standing in that lamplit kitchen, Sefa finally understood it from the inside. The Compact collected nations by acquiring their records — singular, seizable, ownable. But a nation dispersed into ten thousand kitchens, into nonsense-syllable songs and green tin stars and whispered names, has no single deed of ownership. You cannot serve papers on a lullaby.

* * *

On the sixth day a boy of maybe ten found them at the well and tugged Sefa's sleeve.

"You're the name-woman," he said. "Grandda says you can find out what our name was."

"What does your grandda say your name is now?"

"Brant. But he says it's a *tax* name. He says real names got traded for grain in the famine year and nobody wrote down the trade." The boy's chin was set in a way that reminded her absurdly of Ivo. "Is it in the book?"

Sefa knelt. "What's your grandda's first name?"

"Edek."

Her finger moved down the register's columns, page after page, the way she'd once run fingers down tide-tables, and there — *Edekin, son of Brann of the Vessin line, ferry-folk, last recorded at the Ostmere landing, famine year.* Ferry-folk. Her skin prickled.

"Ferry-folk," she said aloud. "Your family ran boats. On this river."

"Grandda's got an old boat!" the boy shouted, delighted, and tore off down the lane, and Sefa stood at the well with the register open and understood that she was not delivering the past at all. She was introducing people to themselves. The archive was dissolving — that was the only word for it, watching it happen day by day — dissolving into the people it described, becoming blood and habit and a boy sprinting toward a rotting boat with his whole heart ahead of him.

That evening Edek Brant himself came to their lodgings, slow, hat in hands, and asked if it was true, and Sefa showed him the line, and the old man read it with one finger under each word like a child, lips moving.

"Ferry-folk," he said. "I always wanted the water. Everyone said, why does Edek stare at the river. Sixty years they said it." He looked up, eyes wet and furious and shining. "Sixty years. It was in me the whole time and nobody had the *paper*."

"Would you take passengers?" Ivo asked gently. "Come spring, when the melt comes down. If the old boat floats."

Edek Brant gripped Ivo's arm hard enough to bruise. "Ferry-folk take passengers," he said. "It's what the name *means*."

* * *

They worked down the valley through the turn of the month, village by village, and the chest grew lighter and lighter until one gray afternoon in the last hamlet, where the river widened and slowed and forgot it had ever been ice, Sefa lifted out the final sheet — a single page, the last lineage, a family named Crane.

She had known it was there. She had known since the branch chamber beneath the glacier, since the photographs and the letters and the complete register of every family the archive belonged to — including, entered in her father's hand in the newest ink of all, dated the year Ivo was nine: *Crane, custodian-line, one child. The child does not know. May the child forgive.*

She carried the page to Ivo, who was sitting on the chest itself, which was now empty enough to lift with one hand, looking out at the wide brown water with the expression of a person listening to a current they could no longer feel.

"This one's yours," Sefa said. "Last delivery."

Ivo took the page and read it for a long time. Their thumb moved over the ink — over *custodian-line*, over *may the child forgive* — the way Edek's finger had moved, the way everyone's did, as if the words needed touching to be believed.

"He wrote me down," Ivo said finally. "In the book. Before I ever did anything. Before I sold the notes, before I ran, before—" They stopped. Folded the page along its crease, unfolded it, folded it again. "He wrote me down as if I'd come home."

"You did."

"No." Ivo shook their head, and there was something in their face Sefa had never seen there, not on the river, not on the ice, not in the whiteout roped body to body — something unbuckled, unarmored, young. "Home isn't a place. You taught me that. Home is—" they gestured at the valley, at the smoke rising straight from a hundred chimneys, at the sound, faint from the far bank, of someone singing *shera-lai* to a child. "Home is this. People knowing your name and what it's *for*."

"So sign it," Sefa said.

Ivo looked at her.

"The register has blank leaves at the back. Your father left them. I think he left them for exactly this." She held out the pen. "Custodian-line, continued. One line. Your hand, not his."

Ivo took the pen. Held it a long moment over the page. Then wrote — slowly, in the careful hand of someone signing something that matters, the same hand that had repainted KESTREL on the transom in full view of the whole quay — *Ivo Crane, ferry-folk, present and accounted for.*

They closed the register. There was nothing left to deliver. The chest, emptied, sat on the ground between them like a shed shell, and Sefa found she felt no sadness about it at all. A mailbag, Ivo had called it once, in the ice. Mailbags aren't meant to be kept. They're meant to arrive.

"What now?" Ivo asked. "River's down that way. Snows are coming down the other. Winter will close everything for months."

Sefa looked down the valley, where the water ran easy and unhurried toward the lowlands, toward the Serein, toward a certain sandbar that appeared on no chart — except, now, on theirs. She thought of the schoolboat they'd spoken of in the whiteout, half-delirious, holding hands on a rope: songs and river-craft, anyone aboard, the archive scattered beyond any buyer's reach. She thought of Edek Brant's rotting ferry-boat waiting for spring. She thought of a boy running down a lane with his whole heart ahead of him.

"Now," she said, "we go and see whether ferry-folk's boat floats."

Downriver, faint on the wind, someone was singing the gray bird song — and this time, Sefa realized, singing the true words.
