## 37. What Vane Takes Home

The trailhead at the foot of the eastern slopes was called, on the maps that bothered to name it at all, Stonefold — a scatter of timber sheds and a mule-pen and a shrine to some river-god whose name had been worn off the stone by weather or by policy. The snows had come down behind them in the night with a sound like a held breath released, sealing the pass, and now the whole company stood blinking in the flat grey light of the lower world, smelling woodsmoke and dung and pine instead of ice.

Ivo Crane had imagined this moment a hundred times during the crossing, usually at three in the morning when the wind screamed and sleep was a rumor. In most of those imaginings, Aldous Vane was dead. In a few of them, Ivo was. In none of them did Vane stand at the edge of the mule-pen, leaning on a walking stick cut from green birch because his own had been lost in the crevasse, looking like a man who had come to the end of a long audit and found the numbers, if not favorable, at least legible.

His left leg was bound in a splint of pack-frame strapping. His red parka — that flag of ownership he'd worn up the whole mountain like a man planting a claim — was gone, traded two days ago to one of his own porters for a plain brown coat, though whether as penance or pragmatism Ivo couldn't say. He looked smaller without it. He looked, for the first time, like an actual person rather than an institution wearing a person's face.

"You're staring," Vane said pleasantly, without turning his head. "I've found people do that, after. They want to see what defeat looks like on me. I'll save you the trouble. It looks like arithmetic."

"I'm not staring," Ivo said. "I'm deciding whether to shake your hand."

"Ah." Vane turned then, and smiled, and the smile was the same courteous instrument it had been at Harrow Bend, at the fuel dock, at every station of this long pursuit — a smile calibrated to suggest that whatever happened next would be reasonable, and reasonable men could always find terms. "I'd advise against it. My hands are cold, and you'd only be doing it for the story, and the story will be better without."

Sefa stood ten paces off with the register wrapped in oilcloth against her chest, the way she had carried it since the chamber, since the crevasse, since the whiteout when four lives hung on one rope and Dalla's counting-steps were the only map in the world. She wasn't looking at Vane. She was looking at the tree line, where the first village smoke rose through the pines, and her face had the expression of a woman listening to a language she'd thought extinct coming back to her one word at a time. Ivo let her have it. Some victories weren't meant to be shared with the loser.

Dalla was seeing to the mules, which is to say she was standing near them while they saw to themselves, and the Compact porters — the conscripted ones, the neighbors, the men from erased valleys who had hauled Vane's ambition up a glacier on their backs — were stacking their loads in a neat square in the snow, as if the mountain had taught them, in these last weeks, a new grammar of what belonged to whom.

* * *

They had come down out of the ice in a procession so strange that Ivo still half-believed they'd dreamed it. The calving season had spent itself in fury — seracs falling all along the tongue like a city demolishing itself — and when the last of the thunder rolled away east, the survivors had simply started walking. Vane's porters carried their own packs and, after a silence in which no treaty was signed and no words exchanged, half of Ivo's and Sefa's loads as well. Vane limped among them, supported on either side by men he had hired to carry his ledger and who now, without discussion, carried him. Nobody had put him under guard. Nobody needed to. A man with a shattered ankle on a mountainside is already in custody; the only question is who holds the key, and the answer, up there, had been the weather.

Ivo had watched him the whole descent, waiting for the lawyer's calm to crack, for the offer to come again — expunged records, a clean name, permission to stop running, all of it repriced now for a desperate market. But Vane had said almost nothing for six days. He had looked at the ice walls going past, and once, at the branch tunnel mouth as they passed below it, he had looked a long time, and Ivo had seen something move across his face that was not greed and not anger but something older and more tired, and Ivo had decided not to name it, because naming it might have made it sympathy.

Now, at Stonefold, with the pass sealed behind them and the world opening out below in terraced fields and woodsmoke, Vane found his voice again.

"I want you to know," he said, "that I intend to file no report recommending further expenditure on this acquisition."

"That's very generous of you."

"It's not generosity. It's bookkeeping." He tapped the ground with his birch stick, twice, like a man punctuating a clause. "The Compact does not pursue losses. It writes them down, learns what can be learned, and reallocates. You have cost the Meridian Compact — and I can tell you this because the figure will be buried in a quarterly statement no one reads — a sum that would have bought three small nations outright. Nations, Captain, with harbors. With railways. With things worth owning." He gestured vaguely upward, at the white shoulder of the Karst now vanishing into cloud. "What you've cost me is a chest of paper on top of a glacier, guarded by weather and by a woman who cries when she hears boat-songs. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

"That we're not worth the ink."

"That you are worth exactly what it costs to take you, and the price has gone up past the point of reason." He said it without rancor, the way a tide chart says the water will be high at noon. "That is your victory, such as it is. Not safety. Margin."

Ivo looked at him for a while. The mules shifted and blew. Somewhere down-valley a dog was barking at nothing, or at everything, or at the sheer audacity of a new day.

"And yet here you are," Ivo said, "at my trailhead, talking to me."

"Here I am." Vane smiled again, and this time there was something underneath it, something that moved. "Because there is one item outstanding."

* * *

He had them bring his document case down from the load-stack himself — insisted on it, waving off the porter, hobbling the four steps on his ruined leg rather than let anyone else touch it, and Ivo understood: it was theater, but it was also jurisdiction. Whatever ritual came next, Vane wanted witnesses to know he had performed it with his own hands.

From the case he took a single sheet of paper. Blank. Heavy stock, cream-colored, with a faint watermark that caught the light — Ivo recognized it with a lurch, having held a hundred folios just like it: archive paper. Vessian rag-paper, the kind the exiled archivists had carried over the range summer after summer in boxes marked as dye goods and church candles. The Compact had bought enough of it, somewhere along the years, to stock its own acquisitions.

"A folio page," Vane said. "Blank. From the same mill as the rest of your inheritance — I checked the registry before we ever met, Captain, I want you to know that. I knew what your father carried before you did. Possibly before he did." He held it out. "I am going to take this home with me."

Ivo didn't take it. "It's blank."

"It is entirely blank. That is its value." Vane turned it in the grey light, admiring the watermark the way another man might admire a horizon. "Do you know what the Compact actually collects? Not objects. Objects burn, sink, get stolen by sentimental archivists' daughters. We collect *finality*. A nation ends when its records exist in exactly one place, under one seal, subject to one signature. Then it can be bought, and having been bought, it can be said never to have existed except as our property — and property has no homeland, no grief, no songs." He tucked the page away inside his coat, over his heart, with a small ironic bow. "This page goes home blank. In fifty years, if anyone asks, it will be the sole surviving leaf of the Vessian state archive, acquired at considerable expense, contents regrettably lost in transit. And because everyone who could contradict that account will be old or dead or scattered across forty villages holding papers I cannot buy back—" he spread his hands, acknowledging the field of battle, conceding it, "—the story will hold. Mostly. For a while."

"That's not a receipt," Sefa said. She had come over without Ivo noticing; her voice was rough from the cold and from the week of weeping she would deny to her grave. "That's a lie you're planning to tell."

Vane regarded her with what looked, horribly, like respect. "All receipts are lies, madam. They are promises that a transaction concluded. Nothing concludes. That is the first thing they teach you and the last thing anyone believes." He settled his weight on the good leg. "But yes. It is also a lie. I am a great many things, but I have never been dishonest about being dishonest. Ask anyone I've ruined."

* * *

He should have hated him. Ivo kept waiting for the hate to arrive, the clean hot kind that makes the future simple. Twenty years of running had been built on the premise that somewhere behind them was a thing worth hating — the family name, the drowned nation, the debt-collector shape of inheritance. And now the embodiment of all of it stood in the mule-muck at Stonefold with a broken ankle and a blank page over his heart, and what Ivo felt was not hate but a terrible, exhausting recognition. Vane was what happened to a person who stopped believing places could belong to the people in them. Ivo had spent a lifetime one bad winter away from the same country.

"The Compact never stops buying," Vane said. It wasn't a threat; it was closer to a weather report, or a confession. "You've won a season, perhaps a decade. You've scattered your papers into villages where they'll be sung instead of shelved, which is — I'll grant you this freely — a storage solution we have never cracked. Memory in mouths. You can't foreclose on a mouth." He winced, shifted the splinted leg. "But the world consolidates, Captain. It always has. Small things get absorbed. Names get shortened. Songs lose verses. One day some great-grandchild of yours will need money quickly, or badly, or for love, and will sell a folder of old paper to a dealer who sells to a broker who sells to us, and the ledger will open again at the page marked Vessia, and someone very much like me — younger, better funded, less inclined to conversation — will come up a river and find you."

"Then I'll tell them what I'm telling you," Ivo said.

"Which is?"

Ivo looked down-valley, at the smoke rising through the pines, at the villages where Sefa had spent the last days walking door to door with the register, watching old women touch their own true names like water in a drought. Thirty-one families restored in the first village alone. Forty in the second. The archive dissolving into the people it described, past any buyer's reach, past any seal, past any single point of failure — including, Ivo noted with some satisfaction, including themselves.

"That the ledger will find them slower now," Ivo said. "Slower every year. Because every child in those villages who learns the boat-songs is a page you can't buy. Every harvest rite, every river-name, every grandmother who teaches a grandchild to count in Vessian — that's a margin against you, and it compounds. You measure everything in acquisition. Fine. Measure this: you're not buying a chest anymore. You'd be buying a language out of living throats, one throat at a time, forever. Your quarterly statements won't bear it. Your successors' successors won't bear it. The price only goes up, Vane, and it goes up faster than your kind can pay."

Vane was quiet for a moment. Wind came down off the Karst and moved the smoke sideways and stirred the blank page's corner where it showed at his collar.

"That," he said finally, "is the first argument anyone has ever made to me in my own currency." He bent, painfully, and retrieved his walking stick. "It won't hold forever. Nothing holds forever. But I confess I did not expect to leave this mountain having learned anything, and I find I resent it slightly less than I expected to."

He turned to go — two of his porters moving to flank him, unasked, the way you steady a man you have decided not to abandon — and then stopped, and looked back, and for a moment the courtesy dropped away entirely and what was underneath was just a tired man in a borrowed coat on a cold morning.

"For what it's worth," he said, "when the floor gave way — I saw you come down the rope. I was conscious the whole time. I have been trying since to construct a motive that fits my model of you, and I have failed, and I dislike failing more than I dislike losing." He adjusted his grip on the stick. "So I'll ask plainly, once, man to whatever you are: why?"

Ivo thought about it honestly, because the man had earned one honest answer even if he'd earned nothing else.

"Because if I'd let you fall," Ivo said, "you'd have been right about everything."

Something crossed Vane's face then — quick, involuntary, gone — and he nodded once, sharply, like a man closing a ledger line, and turned, and limped away down the valley road toward the lowlands and the railways and the quarterly statements, a blank page over his heart and his empire's margin narrowed by one glacier's worth of stubbornness.

* * *

Sefa came and stood beside Ivo in the mule-muck and they watched him shrink down the road until the pines took him.

"He'll write it all down tonight," she said. "Everything. Us, the route, Dalla, the tunnel. He can't help it. Men like him think a thing isn't real until it's filed."

"Let him file it. By the time anyone acts on his filing, the snows will have moved the pass and the villages will have moved the names." Ivo flexed their hands, feeling the rope-burns, the cold still living in the knuckles. "We bought time. That's all anyone ever buys."

Sefa was quiet a moment. Then: "The register has eleven families left. The last village is two days down-valley, past the ford." She didn't look at Ivo; she looked at the road, and her voice did something careful around the next words. "After that, there's nothing left to deliver. Just — people. Who know each other now."

Ivo heard it, what was underneath: the crossing was over, the hunt was over, the running was over, and neither of them had ever yet lived a life that didn't have a pursuit in it. Two keepers of false papers, suddenly holding true ones, with no one left to hide from.

"Well," Ivo said, and found the smile arriving on its own, unfamiliar and unguarded, "we'll want a boat."

Down-valley, past the ford, the Serein's headwaters glinted through the trees — the same river, they'd both realized on the glacier, the same river that had given up a lead-lined chest on a sandbar that wasn't there, the river that had known something about them all along. It ran east now, toward the lowlands, toward spring, toward a lockmaster's daughter who had promised to keep a name.

Sefa followed their gaze, and understood, and laughed — short, disbelieving, alive.

"You want to go home," she said.

"No," Ivo said, picking up their pack, turning down-valley, turning at last with the current instead of against it. "I want to see what the river knows next."
