## 38. First Snow

The sound came at dusk, and Sefa felt it in her teeth before she heard it with her ears.

They had made the eastern tree line by late afternoon, the last of them — Dalla's mules, the two Compact porters who had simply never left, Ivo limping on a knee that had taken the mountain's opinion of them all — and for an hour they had stood among the first black pines, catching their breath, watching the pass above them the way you watch a door you have just walked through and cannot yet believe is closed behind you.

Then the glacier spoke.

It was not one sound but many, braided together: a deep concussion somewhere high in the icefall, like a cannon fired under water; then a long grinding sigh, the whole white throat of the Karst settling its weight; then, fainter, almost tender, the hiss of snow beginning to fall in earnest. The season had been threatening for three days, hanging over them like a creditor. Now it arrived.

"First snow," Dalla said. She said it the way other people said *grace*.

Sefa stood apart from the fire they were building, her back against a pine trunk rough with lichen, and watched the mountain close itself.

She had imagined this moment, in the way you imagine things you dread and want at once: the pass sealing behind them, cutting off pursuit, cutting off retreat, cutting off everything except forward. What she had not imagined was how much it would look like a burial. And how much it would feel like one she could finally stop grieving.

The snow came down thick and silent between the gusts, filling the air above the tree line until the peaks dissolved into a gray-white nothing. Somewhere up there — she did the arithmetic without wanting to, the way Ivo did river-miles, automatically, relentlessly — somewhere up there was the frozen chapel with its carved stars and its centuries of pilgrim names, hers now among them, *Sefa, daughter of Edvin, who carried the mail*. Somewhere up there was the branch tunnel, and the chamber where her father's letters still lay wrapped in oilcloth where they had left half of them, because some things belonged to the ice until someone was ready. Somewhere up there, at the bottom of a crevasse no chart would ever show, was her grandmother's astrolabe.

She had cried about the astrolabe already, two nights ago in the snow cave, quietly, into her sleeve, while Dalla pretended to sleep. Her mother's hands had engraved that brass. Her mother's hands, and then Dalla's mother's hands before that — the same small chisels passed down a line of women who understood that a people could lose everything except the knowledge of where they were from, and even that only if someone kept making instruments to hold it. Now it lay in blue dark under forty meters of ice, and there was no expedition on earth that would find it, and that was —

That was the point. That was what she kept coming back to, turning it over like a coin worn smooth. That was not loss. That was *placement*.

"You'll catch your death standing there," Dalla called from the fire. "Or worse, you'll think too long and turn to stone. Happens to archivists."

Sefa smiled and didn't move. "A minute."

"Take ten. But then come eat. Grief keeps better on a full stomach. My mother said that too." A pause, and then, softer: "Everything worth knowing, my mother said. I'm going to spend my whole old age quoting her to people who don't listen."

* * *

Ivo found her there after the fire had burned down to a red heart and the others had rolled into their bedrolls. Her captain moved carefully these days — the knee, the rope-burned palms, and something else, something slower to heal than any wound: twenty years of running, set down all at once, the way you set down a load so heavy your arms keep remembering it for days.

Ivo didn't speak at first. Just stood beside her, shoulder almost touching hers, looking up at where the pass had been. The snow had erased it entirely. There was no pass. There was a white wall from ridge to ridge, seamless, as if the mountain had never been crossed by anything but weather.

"It's loud," Ivo said finally. "For a silence."

"I was just thinking it sounds like a door."

"A door or a lid?"

Sefa considered. Below them, down the long eastern slopes they would descend tomorrow, the valley villages waited — the ones on the register, four hundred and eleven families, each name written in her father's careful hand, each family not knowing yet that a stranger was walking toward them carrying their own name folded in oilcloth. Down there was the rest of the work. It would take months. It might take years. Some of those families had Vessian words left in their boat-songs and nothing else; some had grandmothers who still prayed in it and were ashamed; some, she suspected, would shut the door in her face, because erasure taught people to flinch from their own reflection.

But that was tomorrow's weather.

"A lid," she said at last. "But the good kind. Like a strongbox. You know how Papa used to say the archive wasn't a treasure? It was a mailbag. Undelivered." She turned to look at Ivo, whose face in the dying firelight was older and younger at once. "The mountain just took delivery of its share. Chapel, tunnel, astrolabe. All of it filed away where no buyer will ever get a receipt."

Ivo huffed — almost a laugh. "Vane took a blank page."

"And called it a receipt. Yes." She shook her head slowly. "You know what I keep thinking? He wasn't wrong to take it. That's the terrible part. A blank page is the only honest thing the Compact ever owned. Everything else they've got is other people's names filled in."

The wind moved through the pines above them, sifting snow off the branches in soft collapses. Sefa pulled her coat tighter and let herself remember the chapel — the way the light had come through the ice-crusted window in bars of blue, the way the graffiti climbed the walls in layers, centuries deep, pilgrims carving stars and names and one unforgettable line in a hand gone shaky with cold: *we climbed so they couldn't bury us flat.* She had added her name beneath it with the point of her knife, pressing hard enough to hurt her thumb, and had felt — for the first time since her father died — not grief, exactly. Continuity. The smallest possible act of defiance, performed in the most durable church her people had ever built.

Now the snow was sealing over that too. And she found she didn't mind. A grave and a vault are the same hole dug with different intentions.

"They'll try again," Ivo said. It wasn't despair. It was navigation — marking the hazard on the chart ahead. "Vane said it himself. They never stop buying."

"No. But look at what it costs them now." Sefa counted it off on her fingers, the way Dalla counted trail-steps in a whiteout. "Their maps show no pass. Their ledger says *pending final delivery*, and the final delivery just scattered itself across two hundred villages on both sides of a range they can't cross for eight months. Every family we reach makes the archive harder to buy, because you can't purchase what's already been given away. Vane understands acquisition. He does not understand *gift*." She paused. "None of them do. That's the whole disease."

Ivo was quiet a long moment. When they spoke again, their voice had the careful flatness Sefa had learned meant the words cost something.

"When I sold the route-notes," Ivo said, "I told myself it was paper. Old paper, dead man's scribbles, worth a boat. I didn't sell a nation. I told myself that every day for twenty years until I almost believed it." Their breath fogged and drifted. "Standing here, listening to it close — I keep thinking, this is what I should have done at sixteen. Burned the notes. Or better, given them away. It only took me a lifetime and a dead father and you to walk the road I sold."

Sefa reached over and took Ivo's rope-scarred hand. She did not forgive — she had told them that plainly, on a quay, in a tavern, twice since — but forgiveness and companionship had turned out to be different doors in the same corridor, and she had learned on this mountain which one she could open.

"You didn't give them away," she said. "We did. All of us. Dalla, the lockmaster's daughter, the villagers singing boat-songs in a language they thought was just theirs. The mountain did the last part." She squeezed the scarred fingers once and let go. "Your father built a network out of kindness and never told you, because he knew you'd have to find it yourself or it wouldn't hold your weight. It held."

Above them, the mountain boomed again — distant, final, tectonic — and a plume of powder lifted off the icefall and hung in the starless air like a held breath released.

* * *

Morning came white and windless, the world reduced to three colors: the black of the pines, the gray of the sky, the blinding white of everything else. The snow had fallen a meter in the night. The pass was not merely closed; it was *unwritten*.

Dalla stood at the head of the mule train, checking loads with the unhurried competence of a woman who had been doing this since before either of them was born. The two Compact porters — Tomas and the young one whose name Sefa still stumbled over — worked alongside without being asked, because on a mountain, Dalla had explained, there are no employers, only hands. Even Vane, somewhere down-valley, limping toward whatever reckoning awaited him with his own rescued men, had ceased, for the length of this crossing, to be anyone's buyer.

Sefa took out the register one last time before wrapping it for the descent. Four hundred and eleven names. She read the first three aloud under her breath, just to hear them, just to feel them in her mouth the way the villagers' boat-songs had felt — alive, unowned, impossible to invoice. Then she bound the folio in oilcloth, tied it with the cord her father had used, and stowed it against her spine, where it rode like a second heartbeat.

She looked up at the mountain one more time.

The peaks were gone, swallowed in cloud and falling snow. But she found she could see it anyway, clearly, the way you can see a room you grew up in with your eyes shut: the chapel with its stars, the tunnel with its cached letters, the crevasse holding the astrolabe like a pupil holds light. Buried, yes. Sealed, yes. But burial, she understood now, standing at the tree line with the register warm against her back, was only preservation performed by patient hands. The mountain had joined the work. It would keep what it had been given for as long as it took — centuries, if necessary — and it asked no fee, signed no contract, recognized no seal but its own.

"We can go," Dalla called. "Snow's firm enough by midmorning. We'll make the first village by dark if the mules agree, and they will, because they're smarter than all of us."

Sefa turned from the mountain. Ahead, the slope fell away through the pines into a valley threaded with woodsmoke — roofs, a river's glint, a bell tower with a crooked spire. Somewhere down there, a family was waking up not knowing that today, or next week, or next spring, a stranger would knock and say a name aloud, and the ground under their feet would change.

She adjusted the pack straps. Beside her, Ivo touched the old brass compass at their belt — the river instrument, the one thing they'd kept from the kestrel-boat — and looked down-valley with the expression of someone reading water they hadn't run before.

"Which house first?" Ivo asked.

Sefa opened the register to the first page, and read the name of the family who had waited longest.
