## 1. Three Years of Miles

I stopped counting birthdays the year my brother's ship went down. Birthdays are a landlubber's arithmetic — they measure how long you've been above water, which has never been the statistic that mattered to me. Instead I count air tanks. I count river miles. I count the days since the *Kestrel* went to the bottom of the Kestrel River with two hundred and eleven souls aboard, one of whom shared my surname and, until the navy sealed the file, my mother's hope that he'd come home.

Three years, one hundred and forty-two days. Not that anyone's counting.

The wreck sits in forty feet of green water off Halloway Bend, where the river widens and forgets to move. Officially, it is a restricted naval site. Unofficially, it is a tomb the navy would prefer everyone forget, which is why they patrol it the way a man pats his pockets for a wallet he's already spent. Officially, I am not supposed to dive it, photograph it, salvage it, or so much as sneeze in its direction. Officially, my brother died in a tragic accident and the matter is closed.

Officially, I have learned, is the navy's favorite word.

I went down at first light, before the mist burned off, because the mist is the only ally a salvage diver on the Kestrel has. My gear was old and honest: a tank I'd filled myself, a lamp with a cracked housing I'd taped twice, and no camera, because the camera was the one thing they'd actually caught me with, two years back, and the fine had eaten a season's earnings. A man can mourn without a camera. A man can mourn with a wrench, if he's honest about what he's really down there for.

The *Kestrel* lay on her starboard side in the silt, the way big ships die — undignified, half-buried, her hull furred with a decade of river moss. Fish have moved into the cargo decks. The river has been quietly unmaking her, plate by plate, rivet by rivet, patient as a lawyer. Somewhere in her, in a bunk I've never been able to reach because the corridor's collapsed, is a locker with my brother's initials scratched into the door with a knife. I know because I put them there, the summer we were fourteen, when he shipped out on her the first time and I told him he'd get lost in a ship that big.

He didn't get lost. The ship did.

I worked the outer hull, running my gloved hand along the plates the way you'd read braille, feeling for what the river hadn't yet claimed. That's the thing about wrecks — they talk, if you know the language. Stress fractures say one thing. Scorch marks say another. And a hole punched outward from the inside says something no accident report wants to hear.

I'd found nothing new in three years of diving her. I'd found everything, in the sense that I knew her hull the way I knew the lines of my own face, and she had never once told me why.

Then my hand caught on an edge that shouldn't have been there.

Silt shifts every season. The river had peeled back a skirt of mud along the keel line, and underneath it, half-buried, was a hull plate — not torn, not rusted through, but *cut*, sheared clean along a seam, the way you'd cut a plate if you were in a hurry to get inside a ship from the outside. And stamped near the edge, faint under a skin of corrosion, was a serial number.

I know hull serial numbers the way priests know scripture. I'd grown up in a shipyard town. And this one was wrong. Not damaged-wrong, not illegible-wrong. *Wrong* wrong — a prefix I didn't recognize, from a yard that didn't build river transports, or if it did, the navy had never said so.

Here's the arithmetic of a salvage diver: one plate, maybe forty pounds with the river's grip included, against a fine I can't afford, a patrol boat that runs the bend every ninety minutes, and a brother who can't testify. Every sensible figure in that equation says leave it.

I've never been accused of sensible figures.

I got my pry bar under the plate's lip and leaned into it. The river resisted, then gave with a groan I felt through my teeth, a sound like the wreck clearing her throat. Mud bloomed around me in a brown cloud, and I stood there blind in forty feet of green-black water, hugging a stolen piece of a dead ship, listening to my own breathing the way you listen to a clock in an empty house.

That's when I heard the engines.

Patrol engines have a particular voice — twin diesels, idling down, the sound of men in a boat who have all the time in the world and none of it yours. They were close. Closer than ninety minutes close. The mist had burned off early, and I was kneeling on a restricted wreck with contraband in my arms and a cloud of stirred silt rising above me like a signal flare made of dirt.

I did the only thing a man can do in that situation, which is the stupid thing done quickly. I jammed the plate under a fallen cargo strap where the shadow would swallow it, killed my lamp, and flattened myself against the hull, becoming one more piece of the *Kestrel*. The engines grew. A searchlight swept the water above me — a pale blade cutting down through the murk, close enough that I watched it pass an arm's length from my regulator's exhaust bubbles.

Bubbles. Right. A dead man doesn't exhale. I clamped my breathing to the slowest rhythm I could manage, sipping air, letting the exhaust scatter wide, and the light swept past and swept back and swept past again, and somewhere above me a voice said something I couldn't make out, and another voice laughed, and the engines idled, and idled, and idled.

I have spent forty minutes in a flooded mine with a bad air line. I have spent a night clinging to a capsize buoy in a squall. I have never spent anything like those six minutes under that searchlight, hugging the ship that killed my brother, listening to the navy decide whether to catch me.

They moved on. Eventually. The engines climbed, the light swung away toward the far bank, and the river closed over the sound like a book.

I waited until I couldn't hear them at all, then some more after that, because patience is the one virtue this river has ever taught me. Then I pulled the plate out from under the strap, and I did not put it back.

* * *

The surface was bright and indifferent. I came up behind the pilings of the old rail trestle, half a mile downstream of where I'd gone in, and hauled myself and the plate into my skiff, which is named *Second Thoughts* for reasons that were never funny and are less funny now.

The plate sat in the bilge, weeping river water, wearing its wrong serial number like a forged passport. I wrapped it in a tarp, mostly so I wouldn't have to look at it, and mostly so I would.

Here is what I told myself on the row back to my dock, and I tell it to you now so you can judge me fairly: I told myself it was evidence. That the sheared seam, the wrong prefix, the plate cut from the *inside-out* of a ship the navy calls an accident — that all of it was a thread, and if I pulled it, something would unravel. That somewhere at the end of the thread was a reason. A report. A name. An answer with my brother's death attached to it, the way a price tag is attached to a thing.

I have been telling myself versions of that for three years. I no longer believe answers come to men like me. I believe you go down and get them, and mostly you come up empty, and the going down is just grief wearing a wetsuit.

But I pocketed the plate anyway. That's the part worth noting. A man who has stopped believing in answers can still steal a piece of a question.

* * *

My dock is at the wrong end of Halloway Bend, which is the end with the rusted crane and the bait shop that's been *going out of business* since before the war. I keep my boat there because the navy's chart of the bend, the one they hand to patrol crews, shows the dock as derelict. There's a lesson in that, too: the surest way to hide a thing is to put it on paper somewhere it doesn't exist.

I was halfway through stowing my gear when I saw him.

An old man, walking the shore path with the gait of someone whose legs have filed a formal complaint, a canvas pack on his back and a fever shining out of his face like a lamp behind a curtain. He came down my dock without asking, which on my dock is either suicide or an introduction, and stood at the end of it swaying, looking at the river as if it owed him money.

"Rafael Thornbury," he said. It wasn't a question. Word gets around a river town faster than floodwater, and I suppose *the diver who keeps diving the wreck* qualifies as news in a place with nothing else going on.

"You've got the wrong dock," I said. "And the wrong decade."

"I've got the right one." He turned and looked at me, and up close the fever in his eyes had a focus to it that scared me more than the searchlight had. Feverish men usually look through you. This man looked *at* me, the way a surveyor looks at a benchmark. "You're the one who keeps going down to her. Three years. Everyone says you're grieving." He smiled, and it wasn't a kind smile, but it wasn't a cruel one either. It was the smile of a man holding a winning hand he's too tired to play. "I say you're *looking*. There's a difference."

"Is there."

"I've got something you've been looking for." He shrugged the pack off his shoulder, and his knees nearly went with it. "And you've got something I need. A boat. And a man fool enough to take it upstream."

"Upstream where?"

He sat down on the dock's edge with the care of a man lowering a load he can no longer lift, and pulled a oilcloth packet from inside his coat, and held it out to me with a hand that would not stop shaking.

"The source," he said. "Of the Kestrel. Where she's born." His eyes went past me, upriver, toward the highlands the navy's charts show as blank. "And where your brother's ship was *born* too, in a manner of speaking. Before she ever touched water."

I didn't take the packet. Not yet. A man who's stopped believing in answers learns to be slow about believing in them again — it's a muscle that atrophies.

The old man watched me not take it, and something in his face flickered, and he said, quietly, almost to the river: "It wasn't an accident, Thornbury. What happened to her. I know because I drew the maps they used to hide it."

The packet hung in the air between us, and the river ran past us toward the sea, carrying everything it knew, telling nothing.
