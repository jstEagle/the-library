## 2. The Prospector's Bargain

The old man arrived the way trouble always does at my dock — quietly, at the wrong hour, wearing the face of someone who had already made up his mind to be a problem.

It was past midnight, and I was on the deck of the *Magpie* doing the arithmetic I did instead of sleeping: eleven tanks, nine of them certified, two of them liars. Forty miles of river between me and the wreck. Three years of the navy telling me the water was closed, and three years of me pretending that a man who measures his life in miles can be told a river isn't his.

Then I heard the footsteps on the planks. Unsteady ones. The kind that belong to a man negotiating with his own legs.

He came around the piling with a walking stick that had started life as a rifle stock, and I knew the type before I knew the name. Prospector. The river breeds them the way it breeds mosquitoes — stubborn, bloodsucking, and impossible to fully exterminate. This one was seventy if he was a day, with a beard gone the color of river silt and eyes that burned too bright, the way eyes do when a fever is running the machinery.

"You're Thornbury," he said. Not a question. Which was the first wrong thing, because nobody who knew me came looking for me on purpose.

"Depends who's asking."

"Aldous Wren." He said it like I should have known it, and maybe I should have. Old-timers spoke the name in the tone you reserve for weather that killed somebody. "I need a boat."

"It's midnight."

"I know what time it is. I've been walking since Tuesday." He swayed, caught himself on the rail, and grinned at me with the manic good cheer of a man whose body had stopped taking his calls. "I need a boat, and I need it to go upstream, and I need you to do it whether I'm alive to point or not."

I should have told him to sleep it off on a bench and gone back to my tanks. That's the version of events I'd tell at a bar, anyway. What actually happened was that I looked at his hands — knuckles like river stones, fingers trembling with something worse than cold — and I heard myself say, "Upstream's closed. Navy checkpoint at Mile Twelve."

"Since when has a sign stopped you?"

"You don't know me."

"I know your brother's name," he said, and the fever in his voice dropped away for one clean second, and what was underneath it was grief, old and polished smooth, the same kind I carried. "Everyone on this river knows the Thornbury boy who went down with the *Corvane*."

The night got very quiet. Somewhere out on the water a heron complained, and I stood there with a stranger's words sitting in my chest like a swallowed hook.

"Come inside," I said.

* * *

My cabin on the *Magpie* is seven feet by nine and decorated in Early Diver: charts, spare regulators, a photograph of a boy in a navy uniform who never got old enough to be a man. Wren lowered himself onto my bunk like his strings had been cut. Up close the fever was obvious — sweat standing on his brow in a cold room, breath coming shallow and fast, the tremor in his hands no longer pretending to be anything but what it was.

"When did you last eat?" I asked.

"Thursday."

"When did you last see a doctor?"

He laughed, which cost him. "Doctors want to talk to you about dying. I'm busy."

I put water and the last of my bread in front of him. He ate like a man who'd forgotten food was optional, and while he ate I watched him, and I'll be honest about what I was thinking: I was thinking *con man*. The river sends them too. Old-timers with a story and a sob and a hand out, and I'd been taken twice before I turned twenty-five, so I'd earned my suspicion fair and square.

But then he stopped eating, and pulled an oilcloth packet from inside his coat with the care of a man handling a reliquary, and unwrapped it on my chart table, and I stopped being a skeptic and started being a thief in my own head, because what lay there was the most beautiful thing I'd ever seen that wasn't underwater.

A map. Hand-drawn, hand-inked, the work of years. The Kestrel River from the delta to its source, every mile annotated in a small, fierce script — depths, currents, gravel bars, deadfalls, and then, above Mile Twelve, above the checkpoint, above the dam that every official chart pretended was the end of the navigable world — *more river*. Dozens of miles more. Rapids that appeared on no survey I'd ever bought or stolen. A tributary system drawn in with the confidence of a man who'd walked every yard of it. And at the top, at the source, in the highlands where the navy's exclusion zone began and the charts went blank as a held breath: a small, precise symbol, and beside it, in letters pressed so hard into the paper they'd nearly torn through —

*THE WRECK CAME FROM HERE.*

"You drew this," I said. Stupid thing to say. It was like looking at a cathedral and asking if a carpenter had been involved.

"Fifty-one years," Wren said. "I prospected every mile of that river before the navy decided it belonged to them. I knew it before they knew it. I knew it better than the men who drew the charts they won't let anyone see." He tapped the source symbol with a trembling finger. "And I knew where the *Corvane* was going, and what she was carrying, and why she never should have been on that stretch of water at all."

My mouth had gone dry. "The *Corvane* went down in a storm. That's the finding. That's the only finding they've ever released."

"Yes," Wren said pleasantly. "And I'm the only man alive who knows it's a lie, which is why I've spent three years being followed, and why I'm dying in a diver's cabin at one in the morning instead of in my own bed." He looked up at me, and the fever-brightness in his eyes was terrible to see up close, because underneath it was fear. Real fear. The kind that doesn't come from dying. "They know I have this. They've known for months. That's why I walked. That's why I came to you."

"Why me?"

"Because you dive the wreck they forbade you to dive. Because you've been caught twice and came back a third time. Because a man who measures his life in river miles—" he'd seen my charts, I realized, seen the tally marks, the obsessive cartography of my own grief "—doesn't stop at a checkpoint because a sign told him to." He closed his eyes, and when he opened them again the fever had won another inch of ground. "I need you to carry this upstream. To the source. Before they find it, before they find *me*, before—" A cough took him, long and wet and awful. "Before I'm not here to explain what it means."

"I'm not a courier. I'm not a crusader. I'm a salvage man with a bad reputation and worse finances."

"Yes," he said. "Perfect."

* * *

He faded in and out after that. I got more water into him, got his coat off, got him flat on the bunk, and sat in the one chair my cabin owned and watched an old man fight a fever he'd clearly been losing for days. Somewhere around three in the morning he surfaced long enough to talk, and what he talked about was a daughter.

"Mara," he said. "Cartographer. Navy, once. Good one. Better than me, God help her." A pause. "Doesn't speak to me. Hasn't in nine years. I said things. She said things. The things I said were worse." His hand found the map on the table, or tried to. "If I don't make it, she gets the map. Not the navy. Not the river. *Her.* You understand me, diver? It goes upstream and it goes to her, and I don't much care in what order."

"Why not just send it to her now?"

"Because she'd bring it to them." His voice cracked on it, and I couldn't tell if it was the fever or the truth. "She's spent nine years trying to prove her father was a liar. Let her prove it with the map in her hands, on the river, where she can see what I saw. Not in some office, where they'll file it and lose it and her with it." He was shivering now, hard. "You have a family, Thornbury?"

"I had a brother."

"Then you know. The dead don't need maps. It's the living who get lost."

He was out again before I could answer, and I sat there in the dark with a stranger's inheritance on my table and my brother's photograph watching me from the wall, and I did the thing I do instead of praying, which is arithmetic. Eleven tanks, nine good. Forty miles to the checkpoint. A hundred and forty more above it, if the map was honest, through water no chart admitted existed, with a navy cutter somewhere behind me and an old man dying on my bunk.

Nobody had ever left me anything before. Not a thing, not a debt, not a reason. My parents left early and messily. The navy took my brother and gave me a form letter. I had built a whole life on the principle that you inherit nothing on this river — you take it, tank by tank, mile by mile, and you don't owe anybody, because owing is how they get you.

And here was a dying stranger handing me the one thing I'd wanted for three years — the *why* of the wreck, the *why* of my brother — wrapped in an obligation, which is what wanting something always costs, if you're honest.

I hated him a little for that. I want that on the record. I hated him for making it a promise instead of a gift, because a gift I could have refused.

* * *

He died at dawn, the way people in stories do, except there was nothing storylike about it. It was ugly and small and it happened in my bunk with my blanket over him, and at the end he gripped my wrist with a strength that surprised us both and pulled me down close, and his breath was a furnace against my ear.

"Wasn't an accident," he whispered. "The wreck. Wasn't an accident. The gold went down on purpose. *She* went down on purpose. Ask the dam. Ask the dam who built it." His grip loosened. "Upstream, diver. Keep your promise. It's the only thing on this river worth more than gold."

Then he let go — of my wrist first, and then, a few minutes later, of everything else.

I sat with him while the light came up gray over the water. Then I wrapped the map back in its oilcloth, and I did not put it in a drawer, and I did not take it to the navy, and I did not take it to the constable, because every one of those options ended with the map in a file and the file in a furnace.

Instead I counted my tanks. Nine good. I loaded all nine.

Then I went topside to make coffee and found a woman standing at the foot of my gangplank, soaked to the knees, with a naval cartographer's bearing and a stranger's eyes, and she looked at my cabin door and then at me and said, in a voice like a drawn blade:

"Where is he?"
