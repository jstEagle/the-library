## 3. What the Plate Says

*Rafael*

The woman arrived on the morning ferry, which meant she'd already lied to somebody, because the morning ferry didn't run to my dock unless you paid the pilot extra and nobody paid extra for a dock with no town attached to it.

I watched her come up the ramp with a canvas bag slung crossways and a walk that said she'd spent years being told where to go and had recently stopped listening. Navy posture, gone slightly to seed. She stopped at the end of my pier, looked at the wreck barge, looked at me, and made a face like she'd ordered coffee and been handed a fish.

"You're Thornbury."

"Depends who's asking."

"Mara Wren. Aldous Wren is my father."

The name landed in my chest like a dropped wrench. I'd spent two days deciding what to do with a dead man's map, and here was the dead man's daughter, arriving with the timing of a bad joke. I kept my face doing nothing, which is the one skill my mother swore I mastered early.

"Was your father," I said. "He died Thursday. Fever. I buried him up the slope, under the birch."

Something moved behind her eyes — grief, or the effort of not showing it, which from close range looks identical. "I know. I read the telegram. I'm here for his things."

"He didn't have things. He had a coat with holes in it and opinions about river gravel."

"Then I'm here for the map."

I leaned on the rail. "What map?"

She looked at me the way a surveyor looks at a reading that doesn't match the terrain. "Mr. Thornbury. My father was a lot of things — a bad husband, a worse correspondent, a genuinely gifted drunk — but he was not a man who died without leaving instructions. He wrote me twice a year for twenty years, always the same letter: *when I find it, you'll know.* Then a telegram from a river doctor saying he'd died, and no letter at all. Which means the letter existed and went somewhere. And you're the somewhere."

I'll give her this: she was better at this than I was. I'd had the map in a waterproof tube under my bunk for two days and had already checked on it four times like it was a sleeping animal that might bite.

"I buried your father," I said. "That's the extent of our transaction. He was delirious. He talked about gold, and rivers, and a woman named Odette who I suspect was a mule. If he mentioned a map, it went wherever fevers go."

"You're lying."

"Almost certainly. But you can't prove it, and I can't afford to care."

She set her bag down on my pier — an act of ownership, a flag planted — and pulled out a tin cup and a thermos, as if she intended to wait out the season. "Then I'll stay until you're a better liar."

* * *

*Mara*

He was lying, and he was bad at it, which was almost worse than being good at it. A good liar you can work with; you know the shape of the lie and you dig around it. Rafael Thornbury lied like a man who'd never had anything worth lying about until recently and hadn't practiced.

I stayed three days. He let me sleep in the wheelhouse of his wreck barge, which smelled of diesel, mildew, and a grief so old it had stopped being personal and become architectural. He fed me river trout and silence in equal portions. On the second day I inventoried the barge while he dove, on the principle that a man who won't talk will still leave his things where he thinks no one's looking.

That's how I found the hull plate.

It was under a tarp behind the compressor, maybe two feet of riveted steel, salt-scoured, with a stamping near one edge that the river had half-erased. I didn't need the whole stamping. I needed four characters and a dash, because I had spent eleven years as a naval cartographer and three more as a disgraced one, and there are certain serial blocks you memorize the way you memorize your own name. This one I knew. Every cartographer in the fleet knew it, the way you know a scar.

I sat down on the deck plates with the thing across my knees and did the arithmetic twice, because the first time it came out impossible.

The *Vantage* — hull number matching this plate — had been struck from the registry thirty-one years ago. Scrapped. I had personally, as a junior officer with too much initiative and not enough sense, once requested her construction records for a hydrographic survey and been told the file was sealed, which was odd, because scrapped ships don't have sealed files. Scrapped ships have invoices and photographs of men with cutting torches.

This ship had a hull plate sitting on a salvage barge in the upper Kestrel, pulled — by the river stains on it — from a wreck that the navy had spent three years telling everyone didn't exist.

I was still holding it when Thornbury came up the ladder, dripping, saw my face, and stopped like he'd hit glass.

"Put it down."

"Where did you get this?"

"Put it *down*, Wren."

"Answer the question and I will." I stood, and the plate was heavier than steel by then; it was carrying the weight of every sealed file in the fleet. "This is the *Vantage*. The *Vantage* was scrapped before I was born. So either you're diving ghost ships, or the navy has been lying about a hull for thirty-one years, and I have to tell you, based on my professional experience, they lie about hulls *constantly*."

He climbed the rest of the ladder slowly, water sheeting off him, and I watched him make a decision. It went through his face like weather. Then he sat on the rail across from me, and something in him unlocked — not trust, nothing so clean, but the exhaustion of a man who has been alone with a thing too long.

"Three years ago," he said, "a transport went down in the middle Kestrel. Forty-one aboard. The navy called it a grounding. They've had a patrol on that stretch ever since, and they've threatened me with everything up to and including treason for diving it. My brother was aboard." His voice did something small and controlled on the word *brother*. "I pulled that plate off her hull two weeks ago. It's the only thing I've ever taken that I didn't have a right to, and it's the only thing that's ever felt true."

"And the serial."

"You recognize it."

"I *memorized* it. Thornbury, the *Vantage* was supposedly broken up in drydock before the Kestrel transport was even *built*. This plate can't be on that wreck. Unless the wreck isn't the ship they said it was. Unless—" I stopped, because the next thought arrived all at once, cold and complete. "Unless they needed a ship to sink, and they used one that was already dead on paper."

He looked at the plate for a long moment. "Aldous said it wasn't an accident. Dying, feverish, half out of his mind — he grabbed my wrist and said *it wasn't an accident, boy, they scuttled her on purpose, I saw the charges from the bank.*"

"And you didn't tell me that for three days."

"I was deciding whether you were navy."

"I *was* navy." I set the plate down, carefully, the way you set down something that might still detonate. "Eleven years. Cartography branch. I was cashiered for falsifying a chart — except I didn't falsify it, I *corrected* it, and the correction happened to make a reservoir look like what it actually was, which was a mistake somebody senior had already signed his name to. So now I draw harbor plans for a shipping firm in the capital and hate myself on a schedule." I looked at him. "My father prospected the Kestrel for thirty years. He wrote me twice a year. And in thirty years he never once told me he was watching the navy sink ships."

"Maybe the letters were the map."

"What?"

"Nothing." But he'd gone strange and still, the way men do when they're deciding something they'll regret, and I recognized the look because I'd been wearing it for three days myself.

* * *

*Rafael*

Here's the thing about Mara Wren: she was the most suspicious person I'd ever met, and I trusted her anyway, which worried me more than the navy did.

I got the tube out from under my bunk that evening and unrolled the map on the chart table, weighting the corners with the hull plate, which felt appropriate — the dead man's paper held down by the dead ship's skin. Mara leaned over it and went quiet in a way that had weight to it.

"That's his hand," she said finally. Her fingertip hovered over the ink without touching. "He drew like this when I was small. He'd put the river on butcher paper and I'd trace it and he'd say, *careful, girl, the river remembers who's rude to it.*" She traced it now, an inch above the paper. "This is the Kestrel above the falls. Nobody charts above the falls. There's nothing up there but the highlands and a decommissioned dam."

"There's a source," I said. "He was very insistent about the source."

She bent closer, and I watched her do what she did — the cartographer's read, the way she took in symbols, bearings, the little surveyor's marks along the margin that I'd taken for decoration. Her finger stopped on a notation in the upper corner, a cluster of figures and a word.

She read it aloud, and her voice came out flat, which is how I'd learned to recognize when she was frightened. "These are depth soundings, Rafael. These are *mine* soundings. He surveyed something up there. Something with an official grid reference." She looked up at me. "My father was the navy's contract surveyor for the upper basin. I found his commission letter when I was nineteen and never understood it. He told me it was for a road project."

"And now?"

"Now I think he drew this map as a confession." She straightened, and in the lamplight she looked, for the first time since the ferry, like her father — the same stubborn set to the jaw, the same eyes that had watched a river for thirty years and finally seen what was in it. "The plate says the wreck is a ghost. The map says the ghost has an address. And my father spent his whole life walking toward it and never had the nerve to knock."

I rolled the map closed. Outside, the river did its endless work in the dark, and somewhere downstream a navy patrol was doing its own kind of endless work, and I thought about Aldous Wren's hand on my wrist, the grip of a man spending his last coin.

"He made me promise to carry it upstream," I said.

"Then you have a problem," Mara said, "because upstream is past a checkpoint, past a patrol cutter, and into the one stretch of river the navy would sink us in rather than let us reach." She held out her hand — not for the map, I noticed, but to shake. "Fortunately for you, I still have my old credentials, and I've been lying to the navy since before you grew a conscience. When do we leave?"

I shook her hand. It was the first bargain I'd made since the dock, and like the first one, I suspected I'd only understand the price later.

"Ferro's boat," I said. "Dawn, if the fog holds."

"Dawn," she said, and started rolling the map the rest of the way, and that was that — two people with nothing in common but a dead man, pointing the same direction upriver.
