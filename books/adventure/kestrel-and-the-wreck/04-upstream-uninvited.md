## 4. Upstream, Uninvited

There are three ways to get past a navy checkpoint on the Kestrel, and I'd designed two of them before my disgrace, back when I still believed the service was a body worth belonging to. The first is paperwork. The second is a blind spot in the patrol rotation, which I also designed, which tells you something about how the navy spends its money. The third is nerve.

We used all three, and I'm still not proud of it.

Rafael handled the boat. I handled the lie. It's a division of labor that suited us both, because Rafael lies the way a stone floats — with enormous effort and visible disbelief — while I could tell a checkpoint officer the river flowed backward and he'd nod and ask me to initial the log.

"Name and purpose?" The lieutenant at the Mile 40 boom had a jaw built for chewing regulations and eyes that never quite finished reading you.

"Mara Wren. Cartographic survey, civilian contract." I slid my credentials across, the ones I'd been told to surrender in a ceremony that involved a lot of brass and one sentence about falsified soundings. They'd never actually collected them. Bureaucracies are generous with their own ghosts.

He looked at the card. He looked at me. Behind him, the boom's chain hung slack over brown water, and beyond it the river ran north into country no chart in the naval archive admitted to.

"Survey for whom?"

"Hydrographic remediation." I smiled the smile I'd been taught at the academy, the one that says *I am bored and competent and no threat to anyone's morning*. "Mile 40 to the headwaters. The old charts are off by as much as two kilometers in the gorge reaches. Somebody's going to run a hull aground one of these years and it'll be a paperwork nightmare."

He almost laughed. That was the trick of it — give a navy man a paperwork nightmare and he'll wave you through just to make it someone else's.

"Two kilometers," he said, stamping something. "That's sloppy."

"That's the Kestrel," I said, and it was the truest sentence I'd spoken all week, which is its own kind of confession.

The boom lifted. Rafael didn't exhale until we were a full bend downstream, and when he did, it sounded like a man setting down something heavy.

"You enjoyed that," he said.

"I enjoyed being believed. It's been a while." I kept my eyes on the chart table, on my father's map, because if I looked at Rafael I'd have to decide what I felt about him yet, and I wasn't ready. Three days I'd known him. Three days since I'd stood in his dock shed and watched him peel a tarp off a hull plate with a serial number that should not have existed, and watched my whole understanding of my father's death rearrange itself around that number like iron filings around a magnet.

*The Kestrel wasn't an accident.* Aldous Wren's last words, according to the man who'd held his hand. I'd spent eleven years not speaking to my father, and now I was spending my days decoding his hand-drawn river, learning his shorthand, tracing the loops of his *W*s — and hating how much of him I recognized in my own.

* * *

Ferro found us at the Mile 62 landing, or perhaps we found him; with Ferro it was always ambiguous who was doing the finding. He came down the bank carrying a coil of line over one shoulder like a stole and a crate of engine parts under the other arm, and he looked at Rafael the way a dog looks at a man who once pulled it out of freezing water.

"You're going upriver," Ferro said. Not a question. Ferro didn't ask questions he could read off a person's face, and Rafael's face had been reading *upriver* since the day I met him.

"We are," Rafael said.

"You'll want a pilot."

"The charts—"

"The charts are wrong." Ferro dropped his cargo on the deck of Rafael's boat — the *Merganser*, a salvage scow with the grace of a filing cabinet — and started stowing the line in the locker where it belonged, uninvited, unhesitating, the way men do when they've already decided and are merely letting you catch up. "I ran this river before the navy closed the upper reaches. There's a gorge at Mile 90 that isn't on any chart. There's water above the dam that comes out of the ground, not the mountains. And there's a cutter on the radio right now asking about a scow headed north with two people aboard, one of them a woman whose face was in the disciplinary gazette two years ago."

I went cold. "You've been listening to patrol traffic."

"I've been *fishing*," Ferro said, with dignity. "Fishing requires a radio."

Rafael and I looked at each other. It was the first time we'd done it as allies rather than adversaries, and I'll admit the alliance felt like a rope bridge — load-bearing, but I wouldn't want to test it in a storm.

"Can you get us past a cutter?" Rafael asked.

Ferro grinned. He had a face built for grinning and ruined for everything else. "I can get us past anything except my own conscience, and I drowned that years ago."

* * *

The gorge appeared at Mile 90 exactly as Ferro promised and exactly as my father's map showed and exactly as no naval chart admitted. The river narrowed from a hundred meters to forty, and the walls came up gray and dripping on both sides, and the water — I am a cartographer, water is my scripture — the water moved wrong. Too fast for the gradient. Too smooth for the speed. It slid like something poured.

"Unlogged rapids," I said, marking my father's notation. He'd drawn them as a series of short, vicious strokes, like a signature written in anger, and beside them he'd written a word in his survey shorthand: *unnatural*.

My father had been a careful man. Careful men don't write *unnatural* about a river unless the river has done something to deserve it.

"Watch the third channel," Ferro called from the wheel. "It looks open. It isn't. The navy dredged it shut in '09 and let the silt seal it — you'd run thirty meters into a dead end and then the current would fold you against the wall like laundry."

"How do you know that?" I asked.

Ferro was quiet for a moment. The engine talked for him, holding us against the current while the walls walked past.

"Because I was the pilot they used," he said finally. "Navy contract, one season. We moved equipment up this gorge at night, no lights, no logs. I asked what the equipment was for and they paid me enough to stop asking." He looked back at me, and the grin was gone, and underneath it was something older and tireder. "I've been running this river for twenty years trying to outrun that season. Your father's map is the first thing that's ever pointed the other direction."

I didn't say anything. There was a strange arithmetic happening in my chest — my father, who I'd written off as a drunk and a ghost and a man who chose rivers over his daughter, had been paddling the same current as this stranger, twenty years earlier, and neither of them had ever said a word to me about it. Secrets, apparently, were the family business. I'd just never been given a share.

* * *

The cutter came at dusk.

Ferro heard it first — not the engine, which was muffled, but the wake pattern, a long slow swell that didn't match the wind. He killed our running lights and eased the *Merganser* into a fold of the bank where the overhang of alder made a black seam against black water, and we sat there in the dark with the engine off and listened to a hundred and ten feet of naval steel slide past us close enough to count rivets.

It didn't stop. It didn't turn. It slowed, just slightly, at the mouth of the gorge, and a searchlight swept the water in a lazy arc, and then it continued north, unhurried, the way a cat continues past a mouse hole it intends to come back to.

"It's not chasing us," Rafael said quietly.

"No," Ferro agreed. "It's *herding*. They want to know where we're going before they decide what we're worth."

I sat on the chart table with my father's map in my lap and felt the shape of it — the trap of it. We were being allowed to lead them somewhere. Every mile upstream was a mile of evidence accumulating against us, and the only thing keeping the cutter at arm's length was their curiosity, and curiosity is a budget item; it runs out.

"They know who I am," I said. "They know what I was. Which means someone upstairs signed off on letting a disgraced cartographer walk into the one river basin the navy has spent decades falsifying." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "We're not trespassing. We're being *invited*. There's a difference, and the difference is that invited guests get buried in the garden."

Rafael took the map from my hands — carefully, the way he handled everything, as if the world were made of hull plates waiting to be catalogued — and spread it under the red lamp. His finger traced the river north, past the gorge, past the dam my father had marked with three exclamation points and the single word *built wrong*, to the source itself. A small circle. Inside the circle, in letters pressed so hard the pen had torn the paper: *ASK THE MINE*.

"Whatever's up there," Rafael said, "your father wanted it found. That's why he gave me the map instead of burning it. A dying man doesn't hand off a secret he wants kept."

"A dying man hands off a secret he wants *witnessed*," I corrected. "There's a difference, and I'm starting to think it's the only difference that matters."

Ferro restarted the engine. The alders let us go, and the river took us north into the dark, and the cutter's wake was somewhere behind us, patient as arithmetic.

I should have been afraid. I was, a little. But underneath the fear was something I hadn't felt since the academy took my commission: the specific vertigo of being *believed* — by a man who owed my partner his life, by a dead father who'd trusted a stranger with his life's work, by a river that was finally, after eleven years of falsified soundings, running in my direction.

We were three people with nothing in common but secrets, running a stolen truth up a river that had been redacted.

By morning, I knew, the navy would stop being curious.

By morning, I suspected, we'd find out what it cost when they weren't.
