## 5. The Drowned Village

The maps lied about the village the way old men lie about their youth — confidently, and with something like affection.

We'd been poling up the drowned arm of the Kestrel since dawn, past a shoreline that kept changing its mind about where it ended. Dead trees stood in thirty feet of water, gray as candle smoke, their crowns still holding a few stubborn leaves that should have fallen years ago. Ferro ran the boat with his hat pulled low and his jaw set at an angle I'd learned to read as *I know something you don't, and I'm deciding whether to tell you.*

Mara sat in the bow with her father's map spread across her knees, one hand pinning it flat against the wind.

"There," she said, for the fourth time. "Harrow's Bend. It should be right there."

I looked where she was pointing. There was nothing there but water and the ghosts of fence posts, standing in a row like men waiting for a ferry that never came.

"Your map says Harrow's Bend has a church steeple," I said.

"My map says a lot of things." She didn't look up. "My map also says this river doesn't exist above the falls, so forgive me if I've stopped treating it as gospel."

Ferro cut the engine and let us drift. The silence out here had weight to it — not the peaceful kind, but the kind you get in a room where someone has just stopped talking. Water lapped against the hull. Somewhere under us, I'd have sworn I heard a bell buoy knocking, except there were no buoys. There was no anything. Just the reservoir, holding its breath over whatever it had been told to forget.

"Dam's maybe two miles up," Ferro said finally. "Built before the war. My father hauled cement for it." He spat over the side, which seemed to be his way of honoring the dead. "They flooded the valley to make it. Paid the villagers to leave."

"And did they?"

"Some." He shrugged. "Some are probably still down there with their furniture, for all anyone says otherwise. Nobody talks about Harrow's Bend. You'll notice the maps don't either."

But they did, I thought. That was the thing. Mara's chart showed the village plain as day, streets and all, sitting high and dry above the waterline. Somebody had drawn it after the flooding and simply declined to update it. Which meant somebody wanted people looking at the wrong version of this place — or no version at all.

I'd spent three years learning what it looked like when the navy decided a piece of river didn't exist. I knew the shape of that silence from the outside. What I hadn't understood until now was how much work silence takes. How many hands it needs. How much paper.

"We dive here," I said.

Mara looked up at me then, and I braced for the argument. Instead she just folded her father's map along its creases, careful as a woman folding a flag.

"I thought you might say that."

* * *

The water was cold enough to make my teeth ache through the regulator, and green-black as bottle glass. I went down the anchor line into a world that had once been somebody's Tuesday.

A streetlamp first — iron post, glass gone, rising out of the silt at a drunk angle. Then the roof of a house, shingles furred with algae, a chimney still standing proud like the last man at a party. A wagon. A wellhead with its crank still attached. And everywhere, everywhere, the small honest debris of lives: a boot. A doll with porcelain face turned up toward a sun it would never see again. A church bell lying on its side in the mud, mouth open, mid-silence.

I'd dived wrecks my whole adult life. Ships go down in violence, usually — there's drama in them, story, a reason. This was different. This was a place that had been murdered politely, by committee, with paperwork. The water hadn't rushed in. It had just arrived, day after day, patient as bureaucracy, until the village traded air for depth and everyone upstream agreed to call it progress.

My light swept across a storefront window and I caught my own reflection ghosting in the glass, superimposed over shelves that had been stocked forty years ago and never sold down. For one stupid second I saw my brother's face instead of mine. Eli had that way of looking at water — like it owed him money. I shook it off. Grief does that, dives with you sometimes, uninvited, breathing your air.

Then the light caught something that wasn't a house at all.

It lay in the deepest part of the basin, near the dam face, settled into the silt with its back broken — a hull, long and low, riveted steel, maybe two hundred feet of it. Naval lines. Transport lines. I knew those lines the way you know a face in a crowd, because I'd spent three years staring at its twin rotting in the shallows downstream, forbidden to photograph it, forbidden to salvage it, forbidden even to ask good questions about it.

The *Meridian*. Or her sister. Same class, same era, same everything — except this one hadn't wrecked. Wrecks have wounds. This hull was whole, top to bottom, unmarked by reef or rock or fire. Her seacocks were open. All of them. Opened deliberately, from inside, by hands that then climbed neatly into boats and went away.

She hadn't sunk. She'd been put to bed.

I swam her length slowly, heart hammering against my ribs hard enough to fog my vision. Cargo hold one: empty, silted, ordinary. Cargo hold two: crates. Dozens of them, stacked in military precision, strapped and sealed, tarred canvas over stenciled markings I couldn't read through the film of fifty years. They weren't scattered. They weren't salvaged. They'd been left — which meant whoever scuttled this ship never intended for anyone to come looking, and never imagined anyone would be stupid enough to dive a drowned village that officially wasn't there.

Well. Stupidity is a family tradition. I'd just never known how far back it went.

I worked a seal loose with my knife — just one, just enough — and got my fingers inside a crate. Not gold. Not coin. Paper. Reams of it, wax-wrapped, and ledgers bound in oilcloth, and beneath the ledgers, ingots wrapped in burlap, heavy as sin. I took one ledger, one ingot sample, and a rubbing of the stencil off the crate side, and I left the rest exactly as I found it, because a man who empties a grave gets accused of robbery, and a man who documents one gets believed.

Mostly.

When I surfaced, Ferro hauled me over the gunwale and Mara was already reaching for the slate. I wrote fast, hand shaking — half cold, half something else.

*Sister ship. Scuttled. Holds full. Sealed cargo — paper and gold. Someone sank her on purpose and walked away.*

Mara read it twice. Her face did something complicated.

"That's not possible," she said, in the voice of a cartographer who has just watched her life's work develop a crack down the middle. "Transports don't get scuttled with full holds. The paperwork alone—"

"The paperwork," I said, spitting out my regulator taste, "is probably in hold three."

Ferro hadn't said anything. He was standing at the wheel, looking upriver at the dam, and he had the expression of a man doing arithmetic he hates.

"What?" I asked.

He was quiet a moment longer. Then he pointed at the dam — at the great curved concrete spine of it holding back the whole sky-colored weight of the reservoir, at the date stone set into its crest, worn but legible even from the water.

"You see that name?" he said. "Builder's mark. Every kid who grew up on this river knows it. It's on the plaque at the falls, too. Ishikawa Construction. Old man Ishikawa built this dam, and the locks below, and half the piers from here to the sea."

I felt it before I understood it — a slow tilting, like the deck going out from under me while I stood perfectly still.

"Ferro," I said carefully, "my grandfather built this dam."

The words came out of my mouth sounding like someone else's. Because of course he had. Of course he had. Grandpa Aldous— no. Not Aldous. Grandfather *Thornbury*, the engineer, the legend, the man whose tools I'd inherited along with his name and his silence and his stories about rivers, the man whose photograph hung in the navy yard lobby like a saint's icon. The man who taught me to read water. Who taught me that every river keeps its accounts.

He'd built the dam that drowned Harrow's Bend. And the transport — the wreck the navy had spent three years keeping me away from, the wreck that held my brother — had been built, sailed, and sunk in the shadow of his concrete.

Mara was watching me with an expression I couldn't read, and didn't try to.

"Rafael," Ferro said quietly. "There's more. That date stone? Your grandfather's firm poured this dam the same year the *Meridian*'s sister went into service. Same contracts, same office, same navy liaison." He tapped the wheel with one finger. "This whole valley — the flood, the ship, the crates, the silence — it's all one job. One signature."

One signature. I thought of my grandfather's handwriting on the engineering certificates framed in my mother's hallway. Strong, square letters. Honest-looking letters. Letters that had signed away a village and sealed a ship and — God only knew yet — maybe written the orders that put my brother on a transport that was never supposed to come home.

The water below us held a town. The water behind us held a ship full of evidence. And the name holding both of them together was mine.

I looked at my hands. Diver's hands, scarred, steady. My grandfather's hands, give or take sixty years.

"Well," I said, and my voice came out flatter than I felt, which was saying something. "Now we know why the navy guards the river."

Mara unfolded her father's map again, and laid it flat, and put her finger on the source — the mine, the headwaters, the beginning of everything.

"Then we'd better find out what else your family signed," she said.

Upstream, around the dam's shoulder, a navy cutter's wake glinted white against the dark water — coming on slow, patient as the flood that had swallowed Harrow's Bend, like it had all the time in the world and all the river besides.
