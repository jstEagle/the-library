## 6. Ferro's Price

I have been paid for my life exactly once, and I never asked for the currency.

Three years ago, Rafael Thornbury pulled me out of a capsized skiff in the Kestrel's spring flood, when the water was the color of rust and moving like it had somewhere important to be. He dove under twice. The second time he came up with my collar in his fist and my lungs full of river, and he didn't even have the decency to look heroic about it. He just said, "Breathe," like it was a suggestion, and then he went back for my gear, because Rafael is the kind of man who thinks a man's charts matter as much as his lungs.

So when he asked me to run the upper Kestrel — past the checkpoint at Gray Narrows, past whatever the navy was doing with its cutters this season — I didn't ask questions. You don't ask a man to itemize a debt. You just notice, one day, that he's holding the note.

What I didn't expect was the note getting called in by somebody else.

* * *

It happened on the third morning above Gray Narrows, in a stretch of river the maps called the Stillwater but which was about as still as a kettle left on a hot plate. Mist sat on the water in long gray ropes. Mara was in the bow with her father's map spread across her knees, tracing the river's line with one finger like she could feel a pulse in it. Rafael was below, checking the air tanks for the fourth time that hour, which is what Rafael does instead of praying.

I was at the wheel when I heard the engine.

Not ours. Ours had a knock in it, a little stutter on the left bank of cylinders that I'd been promising to fix since before the flood that almost killed me. This engine was smooth, deep, and coming up fast from downstream, which meant it wasn't a fisherman. Fishermen on the Kestrel run the current, not against it, and they don't run it at six in the morning in fog.

"Company," I said.

Mara looked up. Rafael came up the companionway with a wrench still in his hand, which told me everything about how he planned to greet our visitors.

The cutter came out of the mist like a rumor. Gray hull, navy pennant, a deck gun that nobody had bothered to disguise as anything friendlier. It swung alongside us with the insolent ease of a ship that has never once worried about a sandbar, and a voice came over the loudhailer, polished and pleasant as a hotel lobby.

"Motor vessel, heave to. Naval inspection. Comply and this will take five minutes."

"Five minutes," I muttered, and cut the engine. "They always say five minutes. It's never five minutes."

Rafael put the wrench down. Slowly. That was the only concession he made.

* * *

They came aboard two of them. The first was a lieutenant, young, with a jaw like a protractor and the particular stiffness of a man who has rehearsed this. The second was older, a commander by his cuff, gray at the temples, with eyes that did something I've only seen a few times in forty years on the water: they looked at me and saw a price tag.

"Gentlemen. Ma'am." The commander's voice was mild. He introduced himself as Commander Voss, and I believed him, because a man lying about his name doesn't usually pick one that boring. "Routine survey of river traffic. There's been some concern about unlicensed salvage in the upper reaches."

"Concern," Rafael said. "Is that the technical term?"

Voss smiled like Rafael was a child who'd said something clever at dinner. "Captain Ferro, isn't it? Ferro." He said the name the way you'd turn over a coin to check its weight. "You've got a record. A good one, mostly. One incident, some years back. A capsizing. A rescue." He let that sit. "The navy keeps files on everyone who works this river. It's a habit of ours. We keep other things, too."

And then he did the thing I'll be honest with you, I did not see coming, though I should have, because I've been bought before, just never with something I actually wanted.

He took an envelope from inside his coat. Not money. Paper. A photograph, and a letter, and a form with a seal on it that I recognized from a life I'd tried to sink.

"Amnesty," Voss said. "Full pardon for the incident at Marlow Bend. The one that cost you your license, your boat, and — I understand — rather more than that."

I want to tell you I laughed in his face. I want to tell you my hand didn't shake. But a man who lies in his own head is worse than a man who lies to a commander, so here is the truth: my hands shook, and I didn't laugh, because I was looking at the photograph.

A girl on a dock. Sixteen, maybe seventeen now. Dark hair, my mother's jaw, and a scowl she got from me, God help her. Lucia. My daughter, who I hadn't seen in four years, who wrote me a letter every Christmas for two years and then stopped, the way people stop when the answer never comes. The photograph was recent. Somebody had been taking pictures of my daughter, and that should have made me furious, but all I could think was: she's taller. She's so much taller.

"The pardon is real," Voss said quietly. "The charges at Marlow Bend were inflated and we both know it. You were running medical supplies through a quarantine zone because the quarantine was a fiction and people were dying. You did the right thing and the navy punished you for it. This corrects that." He held the envelope out. "All I need is the map. The Wren survey. Hand it over, take your pardon, and go home. Go to her. She's in Port Calloway. She knows you're being offered this. She's the one who asked us to find you."

That last part was the knife. Not the pardon, not the envelope. The idea that Lucia had asked. That somewhere in Port Calloway my daughter had sat across from a navy officer and said, *find my father, tell him there's a way back.*

I looked at the photograph until my eyes burned, and then I looked at Rafael.

He wasn't watching the envelope. He was watching me. And here is the thing about Rafael Thornbury that people get wrong, thinking he's cold: he's not cold. He's just a man who has already grieved everything, so nothing surprises him anymore, including betrayal. He looked at me the way you look at weather coming in. Resigned. Already calculating how much canvas to take in.

Mara was watching me too, and she wasn't resigned at all. Her hand had drifted toward the chart case where her father's map lived, and her eyes said, *try it.*

* * *

Here's what nobody in that cabin knew, because I've never told anyone, and I'm telling it now only because the story needs it: I know what it is to be handed your life back by an institution that took it in the first place. I know how sweet that envelope smells. It smells like nothing. It smells like a government office. And the thing it's buying — the daughter, the dock, the scowl that's mine — that part is real, and that is what makes it the most dangerous cargo I've ever been offered.

I took the envelope.

I want that on the record, since we're being honest. I took it. I held it. I read the pardon twice, and my name spelled correctly on it, which no government document has ever managed before, and I thought: four years. Four years of running cargo for men who paid in cash and contempt, sleeping in the wheelhouse because a bunk felt like something I hadn't earned.

"I'll need to see the map," Voss said, "to confirm it's authentic."

"Course," I said. "It's in the chart case. Mara, you want to —"

She didn't move. Rafael stepped between me and the case, easy as breathing, and said, "Ferro. You don't owe me anything. I want you to know that. Whatever you decide."

And that — that was worse than an accusation. Because it was true, and he meant it, and a man can't even enjoy his betrayal when the betrayed man insists on forgiving it in advance.

I laughed. I couldn't help it. Voss's protractor jaw tightened.

"Captain," Voss said, "this offer expires when I leave this boat."

"I know," I said. "That's the thing about you navy men. You always put a clock on grace. Grace doesn't work that way. Ask any drowning man." I folded the pardon closed. "Here's my counteroffer, Commander. You take your envelope, you take your cutter, and you go back downstream and tell whoever sent you that the map is a fake. That Wren was senile. That the old man drew treasure maps for gin money and there's nothing up this river but rocks."

"And why," Voss said, "would I do that?"

"Because if you don't," I said, and here I put my hand on the wheel, on the wheel I've held through a flood and a quarantine and four years of nowhere to be, "I'm going to run this boat straight at your pretty gray hull, and we're both going to find out what's in these holds, and it won't be a map. It'll be two hundred gallons of fuel and a very bad day for both our careers."

Silence. Mara had gone very still. Rafael was looking at me with an expression I'd never seen on him before — something that might have been hope, which on Rafael's face looks a lot like confusion.

"You'd sink us both," Voss said. "Over a map."

"I've drowned once already, Commander. It's overrated." I fired the engine. The knock in the left bank stuttered like a heartbeat. "Now get off my boat."

* * *

They went. Voss paused at the rail and said, quietly, only to me: "The offer stands. Port Calloway. She'll wait a month. People like us don't get longer than that." And then he was gone over the side, and the cutter fell away astern into the mist, and I stood at the wheel and shook for a full minute where nobody could see.

But here's the part I'm not proud of, and the part that cost me.

I didn't tell them about Lucia. Not the whole of it. When Rafael asked what was in the envelope, I said, "An old pardon. Nothing." I lied to the man whose life I was carrying around like a coin in my pocket. Because the pardon was real, and the photograph was real, and if I told them the truth — that the navy could reach into my chest and find the one soft thing left in it — then every hour after that, they'd be wondering. Watching me. And worse, I'd be watching myself, wondering which way I'd break if Voss came back with a better envelope.

So I lied, and I turned us upriver, and I did something else besides.

The Stillwater ends at a fork the charts don't show — a false channel, wide and easy, that dead-ends in a logjam two miles in. Every pilot who's worked this river knows it. It's the kind of knowledge you keep in your boot, because it's the difference between a shortcut and a grave. Voss's cutter draws too much water for the true channel at this season. He'd have to take the fork. He'd have to trust the charts.

So while Rafael slept and Mara pored over her father's lines, I altered our course just enough — a degree, no more, the smallest sin a helmsman can commit — so that the wake we left pointed down the false channel. So that when the cutter picked up our trail in an hour, it would follow the lie instead of the truth.

It would buy us a day. Maybe two.

It would also mean that when this was over — if it was ever over — I couldn't take the true channel back down. Not with the navy waiting on it, not with Voss's parting promise hanging over me like weather. The route I was burning was the route home. To her. I was trading the only map to my daughter for a dead man's map to a drowned mine, and I did it without telling anyone, and I did it because Rafael pulled me out of the river and never once, in three years, asked me what I'd do to pay him back.

That's the thing about debts. You think you're carrying them. Then one day you look down and realize they're carrying you.

* * *

We made the narrows below the old dam by dusk, and the river went quiet in that way water does when it's about to show you something. Mara came aft and stood beside me at the wheel. She didn't say anything for a long time. She's her father's daughter; she knows when a man is doing arithmetic he doesn't want company for.

"The envelope," she said finally. "It wasn't nothing."

"No," I said. "It wasn't."

She nodded, and looked at the water, and said, "My father lied to me for twenty years. About who he worked for, about what he drew. I spent four years not speaking to him because of it." A pause. "I'd give anything for one more of his lies. Just to hear his voice."

I didn't answer. There wasn't an answer that wouldn't have cracked me open at the wheel, and a helmsman in the narrows doesn't get to crack.

But that night, anchored in the shadow of the dam — my grandfather's dam, Rafael said, though he said it like a man spitting — I took out the photograph and looked at my daughter's scowl for a long time. Then I took the pardon and fed it to the stove, page by page, because a man can't steer two courses at once, and I had already chosen mine.

The fire ate it green. And upstream, past the dam, past the drowned village, past whatever the navy had killed a ship to bury — the river waited, and it sounded, if you asked me, like it was already laughing.
