## 7. The Source

The river above the dam is a liar.

Every chart I ever drew — every chart anyone ever drew — shows the Kestrel beginning as a broad, confident stream in the highlands, fed by snowmelt and good intentions. What we found instead was a thread of water no wider than my arm, threading through scree like it was trying to escape the mountains entirely, and above it, a hole in the world.

"That's the source," Ferro said, and spat, which is his version of a benediction.

The hole was a mine adit, timbered at the mouth with beams gone silver-grey, and from it poured the river — not a trickle but a cold, steady flood, as though the mountain had been holding the water in its mouth for sixty years and had finally grown tired.

Rafael stood beside me with his hands in his pockets, the way he does when he's trying not to feel something. He'd been quiet since the dam. Since the sister ship. Since Ferro said the words *your grandfather built this* and the air went strange and heavy around us, like the inside of a bell.

"You knew," I said. Not an accusation. Just checking my instruments.

"I knew there was a mine on my grandfather's old survey notes," Rafael said. "I didn't know the river came out of it. Rivers are supposed to come from rain and snow. Not from—" he gestured at the adit, at the dark water, "—from whatever that is."

"Ledgers," I said. "If my father's map is honest."

"My father's map," Ferro said, "got three men killed before it got to you. So let's hope it's honest."

We made camp in the trees above the adit, out of sight of the water, because water reflects and reflections carry. The navy cutter had lost us two days ago — Ferro had seen to that at a cost he hadn't let either of us name — but lost isn't gone, and cutters are patient the way institutions are patient. I took first watch mostly because I didn't want to sleep. Sleep lately had been full of my father's voice, and my father's voice had been full of coordinates.

* * *

Here is what I know about Aldous Wren, as of this morning: he was a better liar than I ever gave him credit for, and I gave him credit for a great deal.

I grew up thinking my father was a failure. That was the family story, the one my mother told with a particular tightness around her mouth. Aldous Wren, naval cartographer, drummed out after some scandal no one would name, who took to the highlands with a mule and a gold pan and came home twice a year smelling of river and regret. I learned to read charts at his knee, and I learned to hate the navy at his dinner table, and when I joined the navy anyway — because I was nineteen and stupid and wanted to be better at his own job than he'd been — he didn't speak to me for a year.

I thought he was ashamed of me.

I had it exactly backwards, and I have been carrying that backwards thing up a mountain for six days.

The mine adit was barred with a rusted gate that surrendered to Rafael's crowbar with a shriek that probably carried to the valley. Beyond it, a gallery sloped down into standing water, black and flat as poured ink. Rafael went first, of course. He's a salvage diver; he treats all flooded spaces as personal correspondence. I went second because I refused to let him have the discovery, and because — though I would have denied it at gunpoint — I didn't want to be alone with whatever we found.

The water was thigh-deep, then chest-deep, then we swam, our lamps making weak yellow coins on the ceiling. The gallery opened into a chamber, and the chamber had been engineered, which is the word that made my stomach drop. Natural caves are chaotic. This was squared. This had rails, corroded into orange lace, running along the floor into the deep. This had crates.

Dozens of them. Sealed, tarred, stacked in the flooded dark with a tidiness that spoke of time and care and a payroll. Rafael pried one open on the rail bed, and the lamplight fell in, and we both looked at the dull yellow weight of it for a long time without saying anything, because there is a particular silence that gold produces in people, and we were both too well-read to fall into it out loud.

"Smuggled," Rafael said finally. "Under naval escort. The transport wasn't carrying supplies."

"The transport was carrying the *story*," I said. "The gold went overland, through the mine, down the river. The transport was the receipt. You sink the receipt, you sink the ledger, and the gold becomes a rumor."

"And the crew?"

I didn't answer. I had spent three years of my career being quietly ashamed of the navy's map room — the way certain rivers bent wrong on official charts, the way certain inlets simply stopped existing. I had told myself it was bureaucratic laziness. Cartography is full of lazy ghosts. It had never once occurred to me that the ghosts were *employed*.

* * *

It was on the second crate — or rather, in the oilskin packet lashed to its lid — that I found my father's handwriting.

I knew it before I knew it. That's the awful thing about a parent's hand; it bypasses the eyes entirely and lands somewhere behind the sternum. Aldous Wren's letters were narrow and slanted, the hand of a man who drew lines for a living and resented every one of them. The packet held survey notes. Final survey notes, dated the spring before the transport went down — dated, I realized with a cold that had nothing to do with the mine water, the spring before he left the navy. Before he became the failure my mother pitied and I resented.

I read them by lamplight in four inches of water, standing on a rail bed at the bottom of a mountain, and my father narrated his own damnation in a tidy cartographer's cursive.

He had been assigned. That was the first thing. Not recruited, not tempted — *assigned*, by a commander whose name had been redacted with a straightedge and a steady hand. His task was to produce a survey of the Kestrel's upper reaches that would justify the dam. The dam would flood the valley. The valley's village — Veyra, its name was Veyra, and I sat down in the water when I read that, because I had drawn that valley a dozen times and always left the village off, following the official base maps, following the *lazy ghosts* — the village would be erased from the record, and with it the only civilians who knew the mine existed and what came out of it.

The gold was naval gold. That was the second thing. Not smuggled *past* the navy. Smuggled *by* it — moved under escort, off the manifests, into private hands whose names my father had collected in a coded index he was too proud of his own craft not to keep. The transport, the *Kestrel's own transport*, the ship Rafael's brother died aboard, had been scheduled for scrapping for a decade before it sailed. It sailed as a prop. It was sunk as a period at the end of a sentence.

And my father — my father, who taught me that a map is a promise you make to strangers — had drawn the maps that made it all invisible. The dam survey. The rerouted river. The village that wasn't there. He had signed them. His initials were in the corner plates, the little cartographer's signature I had imitated as a girl in my schoolbooks, *A.W.*, like a blessing.

The last page was different. The hand had deteriorated, the lines slanting harder, the letters crowded. It was dated the night before the transport sailed.

*I have finished the charts they ordered and I have made one they did not. If anyone finds this, the true source of the Kestrel is the mine. The river is a pipe. I am going upstream to see it with my own eyes, and then I am going to spend the rest of my life being a failure where no one from the navy will think to look for a cartographer. I am sorry to my daughter, who will understand this too late, which is the only way anyone understands anything.*

I read it three times. The water rose and fell with my breathing. Somewhere behind me, Rafael was saying my name, gently, the way you'd speak to someone standing on a ledge, and I suppose in every way that matters I was.

"He knew," I said. "He knew the whole time. He didn't fail. He *hid*."

"Mara—"

"Thirty years. He let me think he was a coward. He let the *navy* think he was a coward, because a coward is the last thing they'd hunt." I laughed, and it came out wrong, wet and jagged. "I joined the service to spite him. I spent my whole career drawing his lies over again because they were in the base maps and I trusted the base maps."

Rafael crouched down beside me, lamp lowered, so the gold in the open crate went dark and stopped mattering. "He drew you the map," he said. "The one that got us here. He made *you* the last line of the chart."

"He made a stranger the last line of the chart," I said. "He couldn't even trust his own daughter with it."

"Could you have kept it?" Rafael asked. "Thirty years ago. If he'd handed it to you at the dinner table and said, this is why I left, this is why we're poor, this is why the navy will watch us — could you have sat on it?"

I wanted to say yes. I opened my mouth to say yes. What came out was: "I don't know. I was so angry at him. I'd have marched it straight to my commanding officer to prove I was the better Wren."

"Then he knew you, too," Rafael said quietly. "He was a cartographer. Reading the terrain is the whole job."

I sat in the black water of my father's last survey and let that land, and it hurt in the specific way of things that are true and arrive decades late — the way a letter does when the address was always wrong.

* * *

We surfaced into grey afternoon and Ferro's face, which was doing something I'd never seen it do, which was nothing. Ferro's face is usually a busy intersection. This was a closed road.

"You need to see the ridge," he said. "South. Above the tree line."

We climbed. The mine's tailings had cut a scar down the mountainside, and from the ridge you could see the whole lie of the land at once: the dam holding back its drowned village, the river running obedient and false through the valley, and — far downstream, a grey sliver on the water, moving with the unhurried confidence of something that has never once been lost —

— the cutter.

It had found the river again. Of course it had. Rivers only go one place, and we had gone to it.

"They're a day out," Ferro said. "Maybe less, if they've got a launch."

Rafael was looking at the cutter, but I was looking at the adit below us, at the black water breathing out of the mountain, and at the oilskin packet in my jacket, against my ribs, where my father's last honest chart lay over my heart like a hand.

"He left me the truth," I said, "and a thirty-year head start, and I still almost missed it. They're coming to make sure nobody else does."

Rafael turned from the cutter. I watched him do the arithmetic he always does — air tanks, river miles, odds — and I watched the number come out bad, and I watched him decide he didn't care.

"Then we get the ledger out before they get here," he said. "All of it."

Below us, the river came out of the mountain and ran toward the sea, carrying its secret the way it had carried it for sixty years — and for the first time, it wasn't carrying it alone.
