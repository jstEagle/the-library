## 8. Broken Compass

The mine announced itself before we saw it — a smell of wet iron and old timber riding down the current like a warning. The Kestrel narrowed there, pinched between walls of granite the color of an old bruise, and above us the headframe leaned out over the water on legs of rusted timber, a gallows for a mountain. Aldous Wren's map had called this point *the throat*. It was a good name. Everything upstream had to pass through it, and everything downstream came out changed.

We tied Ferro's boat under an overhang of rock where the cutter's searchlights couldn't reach without coming in low and stupid, and I climbed the last stretch with Mara one step behind me and Ferro limping one step behind her, and none of us said what we were all thinking: that we'd run out of river. There was nowhere left to go but into the water.

The flooded gallery opened below the headframe like a mouth. My dive light found the first crate forty feet down, wedged against a collapsed trestle, its stenciled markings still legible after all these years — naval serials, escort codes, a manifest number that matched the plate in my pack. The gold wasn't a rumor anymore. It was cargo, catalogued and sunk on purpose, and my grandfather's dam had drowned the road that led to it.

"We photograph everything," Mara said beside me, her voice thin through the regulator. "Every crate. Every serial. Then we get out."

I nodded, because arguing underwater is a good way to die, and we worked. Two hours of cold black water and careful hands. I'd done harder dives. I'd done harder dives alone, which is worse. But the whole time, some animal part of me kept counting the exits — the throat downstream, the scree slope above, the narrow game trail Ferro swore led over the ridge — and every count came back the same. We were in a bottle, and somewhere out in the dark the navy held the cork.

Ferro saw them first. He was on watch when the searchlight swept the gorge, and he didn't shout or scramble; he just put his hand on my shoulder the way he does, heavy as an anchor settling, and said, "Company."

* * *

Commander Elise Voss came up the trail alone.

That was the first thing wrong with the picture. Cutters carry boarding parties, and boarding parties come in numbers, and officers who want you dead don't walk toward you in the open with their hands visible. Voss walked toward us in the open with her hands visible, gray-haired and unhurried, wearing three years of the same patience that had kept me off that wreck in the shallows, and she stopped ten feet away as if there were a line drawn on the ground that we'd both agreed to respect.

"Rafael Thornbury," she said. "Mara Wren. And the pilot." She looked at Ferro a moment longer than she needed to. "You've made my career very difficult, and my retirement very simple. I appreciate neither."

"You could have sent a letter," I said.

"I did. Three years of letters. Cease and desist orders, exclusion notices, a hazard designation for a wreck that isn't hazardous." She folded her hands. "You never read them. You read hull plates instead."

Mara shifted beside me. I felt her weight move onto her back foot, the way she stands when she's deciding whether a map is worth redrawing or burning. "The manifest," she said. "That's why you're here. Not for us. For the paper."

"For the paper," Voss agreed. "And for the silence that goes with it." She turned back to me, and here is the thing I will not forgive myself for, the thing Mara saw before I did: she looked at me not like an officer looking at a criminal, but like a woman about to hand someone exactly what they've been begging for. "Mr. Thornbury. Do you know how your brother died?"

The question went through me like cold water through a torn suit. "A transport accident. Everyone knows—"

"Everyone knows what we wrote." She said it without shame, which was somehow worse than shame would have been. "Daniel Thornbury was not lost in an accident. He was aboard the *Kestrel Star* as a junior supply officer, he found discrepancies in the cargo ledger during the final crossing, and he filed a report. A formal one. Through channels." Her voice stayed level, almost gentle, the way people speak at funerals. "The report reached the wrong desk. The ship was scheduled for scuttling within the week. Your brother was aboard when it went down because they made sure he was aboard when it went down."

The gorge got very quiet. I could hear the river working at the rocks below, patient as ever, and I could hear my own breathing doing something ugly.

"He tried to report it," I said. "And they—"

"They erased him. Erasure is our oldest technology, Mr. Thornbury. Older than the wheel." Voss reached inside her coat and brought out a folder, sealed with the navy's wax-and-lead stamp, the kind of seal that outlives governments. She held it out. "His complete file. The report he wrote, in his own hand. The correspondence that followed. The order that followed that. Everything you have spent three years diving for, everything you've counted air tanks and river miles trying to find — it's in here. Signed release. No conditions except two."

"The map," Mara said flatly.

"And your silence. All three of yours." Voss's eyes didn't leave mine. "The file becomes yours. The wreck stays a hazard designation. The gold stays buried, the ledger stays lost, and Daniel Thornbury gets his truth delivered privately, to the only person who ever loved him enough to look for it. Is that so terrible? You'd know. Finally. After all these miles."

I want to tell you I laughed in her face. I want to tell you the answer came up from somewhere below thought, clean as a diver's reflex. But the truth is that my hand moved. Not far. An inch, maybe two, toward the folder — and an inch is a country wide when everyone watching knows what it means.

Because God help me, I considered it. In the space of about four seconds I lived a whole alternate life: me on my dock with Daniel's handwriting in my hands, reading his report, hearing his voice again through paper, the mystery solved and the war over and nobody else having to drown for it. Four seconds of the sweetest arithmetic I've ever done. Trade a stranger's promise for a brother's voice. Trade the truth for the knowing.

Then I heard Mara breathe in — that sharp little intake, half gasp, half verdict — and the four seconds ended.

"You're considering it," she said.

"Mara—"

"You're *considering it.*" Her voice cracked on the second word, and what came through the crack wasn't just anger. It was her father. Aldous Wren had spent thirty years carrying this same secret the way you carry a stone in your boot, and it had eaten him alive in a shack by the river, and his daughter was standing here watching the same trade get offered to another man and seeing the whole rotten inheritance pass hands again. "He promised you nothing, Rafael. My father owed you nothing. He gave you a map and a dying man's fear, and you took it, and now a woman in a uniform offers you the one thing you can't resist and your *hand moves*—"

"Your father helped build the cover-up!" It came out of me before I could dress it up, cruel and true, and I watched it land. "Voss said it herself — erasure is their technology. Who drew the charts that kept divers away? Whose survey notes are in those crates' route plans? He carried it thirty years and told no one, not even you. So don't stand there and tell me about promises, Mara. You don't know what I'd give to hear from my brother again. You had thirty years to ask your father questions and you spent them being estranged!"

The words hung there, and I knew instantly they were the worst ones available, because they were true, and because they were mine, and because Voss stood there listening to us tear each other open and smiled very slightly, the smile of a woman whose entire method is waiting for people to do this to themselves.

"That's enough," Ferro said quietly. He hadn't moved from the rock he'd settled on, bad leg stretched out, and he said it the way he says everything, like a man commenting on weather. "Both of you. Look at her hands."

I looked. Voss still held the folder out. And behind her, down the trail, in the dark between the searchlight sweeps — shapes. Six, maybe eight. Boarding party after all. The lone walk had been theater. The offer had been theater. The fracture between us hadn't been a risk to her plan; it had been the plan.

"The file is real," Voss said, unbothered. "So is the alternative. Give me the map and the photographs, and you walk to the boat under escort, and Mr. Thornbury reads his brother's words tonight. Refuse, and the gorge has a long history of accidents, and I have three years of paperwork already written about reckless salvagers." She glanced at Mara. "Your father understood, Ms. Wren. That's why he ran. That's why he died alone. Secrets keep people *safe*—"

"My father died free," Mara said, and her voice had gone quiet, which from her is louder than shouting. "He died scared and sick and alone, yes. But the last thing he did was give that map away. To a stranger. Because he finally couldn't carry it anymore." She looked at me then, really looked, and whatever she saw in my face must have been enough, because something in her shoulders came down half an inch. "Well, Rafael? What kind of inheritance are you taking tonight?"

And there it was. The compass broken into its three needles, all pointing different directions at once. Loyalty to the dead — to Daniel, whose voice sat in that folder like a bird in a box, one inch from my hand. Loyalty to the living — to Ferro with his burned escape routes and his daughter downriver, to Mara who had every right to hate me and hadn't left. And loyalty to the truth, which belonged to no one, which had cost Aldous Wren his life and my brother his life and would probably cost mine, and which could not be traded, not even for the one thing I wanted most in the world.

Three years I'd measured myself in miles. Miles of river. Miles down a cable to a dead ship. Miles between me and any answer. And I finally understood that Voss was offering me the last mile — the terminal distance, the one that ends at a private grief with the world safely unchanged — and that Daniel, whoever he'd been, the man who filed a formal report through channels believing the system would work, would have taken one look at that folder and told me to burn it.

My hand came back to my side.

"No," I said.

Voss studied me for a long moment, and I'll say this for her: she didn't seem surprised. Disappointed the way a cartographer is disappointed when a river refuses to run where the map says. "Then we do this the old way," she said, and stepped back, and the shapes behind her came forward into the light with their lamps and their rifles and their paperwork faces.

Behind us, at the boat, Ferro stood up. Bad leg and all. He looked at the cutter's searchlight sweeping the throat downstream, then at the flooded gallery above, and he grinned at me with all the warmth of a man about to do something unforgivably expensive.

"Rafael," he said. "How fast can you dive?"
