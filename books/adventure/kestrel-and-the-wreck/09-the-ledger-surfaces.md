## 9. The Ledger Surfaces

*Rafael*

"No," I said.

Commander Voss stood in the mine's flooded gallery entrance with my brother's file held out like a communion wafer, and I watched myself — some distant, calmer version of me — want it. That was the ugly part. Not that I considered it. That I considered it for three full seconds, and in those three seconds I could feel the weight of that folder, could feel Elias's handwriting under my thumb, could feel three years of counting air tanks finally adding up to something.

Three seconds. Then Mara's voice in my memory, cold as river water: *You'd trade her father's dying breath for a folder?*

"No," I said again, and this time it came from the place where I actually lived.

Voss's face didn't change. That was the worst thing about navy men — they'd rehearsed every answer except the honest one. "Rafael. Your brother died a smuggler in the official record. I'm offering you a brother who died a whistleblower. That's not a bribe. That's a correction."

"It's a receipt," I said. "You don't hand out corrections for free. You hand them out when the alternative is a ledger surfacing." I nodded at the black water behind him, the flooded gallery where the crates had been born and the manifest had been left to drown. "You want the map. You want the survey notes. You want Mara's father's confession to stay at the bottom of a mine shaft your own grandfather helped flood. And in exchange you'll give me a story about my brother with the ending rewritten."

Mara stood at my shoulder. I could feel her not looking at me, which was its own kind of verdict. She'd accused me of considering it an hour ago, and I'd hated her for it, and she'd been right, and we were both going to carry that.

"Then you'll die a smuggler too," Voss said. "Both of you. The river's long and the navy's patient."

"The river's longer," I said.

He almost smiled. "Get them out of here," he told his men, and turned back to the water, and that was the mistake. Men who rehearse every answer forget to watch the ones who've stopped answering.

Because Ferro had the boat.

* * *

*Mara*

I heard the engine before I understood it — that particular two-stroke stutter of Ferro's river boat, the sound I'd spent four days learning to love and three hours learning to fear — coming *up* the mine's flooded approach channel at a speed no sane pilot would attempt.

Ferro was not a sane pilot. Ferro was a man who'd spent the last day staring at a photograph of his daughter and saying nothing, which in Ferro's language was a scream.

"He's not—" Rafael started, and then we both understood at once, the way you understand a rope going taut.

The cutter sat broadside in the channel, its searchlights painting the gallery mouth white. Voss spun. His men spun. And Ferro brought his boat around the bend with the throttle wide open and no lights at all, a shadow moving at the speed of a debt being paid.

"Ferro!" Rafael was already running, which was pointless and which I loved him for. "FERRO!"

The impact sounded like the mountain clearing its throat.

The cutter's flank folded around the little boat's bow. Searchlights swung wild, caught rock, caught water, caught Ferro — standing at the wheel, both hands on it, not diving clear, not even flinching, the way a man stands when he has decided exactly what his life is for. Then the lights went out and the world went to noise, and Rafael grabbed my collar and hauled me off the gallery ledge into the black water, because the only way out was the way Ferro had bought us: down.

The gallery was flooded to within a meter of its roof. I'd spent two days mapping it by lamplight — my father's lamplight, forty years ago, when he'd surveyed this shaft for men who paid him to make the maps lie. I knew where the manifest office was. I knew the shelf. I'd found the survey notes in a rusted tin an hour before Voss found us, and I had them sealed in a bag against my chest, and now I had one more thing to carry up from the dark.

The manifest office was a steel door off the second gallery, and the water inside it was the color of forgetting. Rafael went through first. I followed his bubbles. The safe — my father had drawn it, of course he had, he'd drawn everything, that was his curse, he could never stop recording — the safe was exactly where his map said it would be, and it was open, because the navy had emptied it decades ago.

But they'd emptied it of gold. Ledgers are paper, and paper is worthless to men who count in bullion, and so the cargo manifest — the actual, signed, escorted, *navy-stamped* manifest of the *Kestrel's* last run — lay in the flooded drawer where some clerk had left it, thinking a drowned mine was a filing cabinet no one would ever open.

Rafael pulled it free and looked at me through the murk, and even down there, even in the dark, I could see him decide. Not to keep it. Not to trade it. To *deliver* it. Upstream, downstream, through a spillway, through hell — the promise he'd made a dying stranger, kept at last, at the only price left.

Then the gallery shuddered, and the roof began to come down, and we swam.

* * *

*Rafael*

The spillway was a concrete throat, and we went down it the way everything goes down a throat.

I'd like to tell you I was brave. What I actually was was concussed, because a piece of the cutter's rail — or Ferro's bow, God forgive me — had caught me across the temple during the ram, and the world had a bright ringing edge to it that never quite resolved into pain. Mara had my collar. I had the manifest in its oilskin wrap, and the wrap was tied to my wrist with the same knot I use for salvage, the knot my father taught me, the knot my grandfather's dam had failed to drown.

The spillway took us in a rush of white and dropped us into the tailrace below the dam, into the cold and the dark and the astonishing, stupid fact of being alive. Mara surfaced beside me, coughing, one hand still fisted in my jacket, the other pressed flat over the tin of survey notes against her chest like she was holding a wound closed.

"Ferro," she said.

I didn't answer. There was nothing to answer with. The cutter was settling in the channel above, and Ferro's boat was a scatter of planks in the spill foam, and the man who owed me his life had paid it back with the interest most men never find the courage to owe.

"He got out," Mara said. Not a question — an instruction. She said it the way you say a thing you intend to make true. "Ferro doesn't die in a channel, Rafael. Ferro swims like an otter and lies like a lawyer. He got out."

I chose, for the moment, to believe her, because the alternative was to stop swimming.

The navy was coming. I could hear it — engines above the dam, voices on the wind, the whole patient machine of them grinding down the river after us. We had maybe twenty minutes of dark water and a river's worth of current, and nothing else. No boat. No Ferro. No plan that would survive contact with daylight.

But I had the manifest. I had a dead man's map folded in my jacket, and a dead man's daughter swimming beside me, and somewhere downstream — I had to believe — a journalist named Okafor who owed me a favor and had the one thing the navy had never figured out how to sink: a printing press.

"Mara," I said. "The rapids. The ones on your father's map that aren't on any chart."

She was already turning downstream. "The navy doesn't know they're there."

"The navy's maps are wrong," I said, and for the first time in three years, saying it felt like a victory instead of a complaint. "His aren't."

We swam into the dark with the evidence between us and the current at our backs, and above the dam the searchlights swept the water where we weren't. Behind us the cutter groaned against the rocks. And somewhere in that wreck, I told myself, an otter-swimming liar was already climbing ashore — because I had promised Aldous Wren I'd carry his map upstream, and I had kept it, and I was not going to let the river take the only other person who'd helped me keep it.

The first white water was audible before it was visible. Mara looked back at me once, and in the light of the burning channel above, I saw that she was grinning.

"Hold the manifest," she said. "And pray my father drew the exit as well as he drew the crime."
