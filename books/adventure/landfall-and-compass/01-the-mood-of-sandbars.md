## 1. The Mood of Sandbars

Fog on the Vell is not weather. It's a negotiation.

I'd been haggling with it since before dawn, easing the *Marguerite* down the long bend above Tallow Reach, where the river doubles back on itself like a man pretending he hasn't forgotten where he left his keys. The fog sat thick as wet wool, and every sound arrived late and guilty — the slap of water against the hull, the creak of the paddlewheel behind me, Tull coughing in the cargo hold because the boy refused to admit he was catching a cold.

I stood at the wheel with one hand on the spokes and the other holding a cup of coffee that had gone bitter an hour ago, and I read the water.

That's the trade, if you're wondering. Any fool can steer a boat. Reading water is the work. The Vell talks to you if you listen — a smooth brown sheet means depth, a seam of ripples means a bar shifting, a long V of current pointing downstream means the channel's honest, and a boil of confused water means the river is lying to you and you'd best assume it knows something you don't. Forty years of silt come down from the high country settles in new patterns every season. Charts are opinions. The water is testimony.

There was a sandbar building off the left bank where none had been in spring. I could see it only as a faint smoothing of the surface, a place where the fog seemed to rest heavier, like a blanket laid over something sleeping. I swung the wheel two points to starboard and gave it a wide, respectful berth.

"Bar's moved," I called back to Tull without turning around.

"Bar always moves," he called back, which was true, and which was the whole problem with rivers, and possibly with everything else.

The *Marguerite* is a stern-wheeler of no particular distinction — a hundred and forty feet of riveted iron and old arguments, built before I was born and kept alive since by stubbornness and spare parts of increasingly dubious provenance. She carries four hundred tons when she's feeling well and five hundred when I'm lying to myself about her condition, which is most of the time. Her boiler is older than my deckhand. Her captain is older than her boiler's patience. We suit each other.

We were running light that morning — baled cotton down from Harrow Bend, crates of nails, a coffin somebody's family had ordered from the joiner at Millbrook because apparently even grief has supply chains now. Eleven miles to Casterly Wharf, where there'd be a dock agent waiting with contracts, and where I intended to do what I always did: sign nothing that tied me anywhere longer than the time it took to load.

People think a river pilot runs away for dramatic reasons. They've read too many novels. Nobody runs toward anything dramatic; drama finds you fast enough when you stand still. You run because staying has a price, and you've already paid it once, and once was educational.

The fog thinned for a moment near the church spire at Little Fenn — just enough to show the spire, gray on gray, like a pencil mark someone had regretted — then closed again. A bell buoy clanged somewhere off my port side, patient and idiotic, ringing for boats that weren't there.

"Captain." Tull came up beside me, wiping his nose on a sleeve that had given up on dignity months ago. "Agent Pell sent a runner up to Harrow Bend yesterday. Says he's got a contract for us. Big one."

"Pell always says big one."

"No, this one's —" He squinted at a scrap of paper. "Twelve-month exclusive. Grain run, Casterly to the mills at Osterlin and back, guaranteed tonnage, guaranteed berth. Retainer paid quarterly."

A year. Twelve months of the same sixty miles, the same docks, the same faces learning your name and using it like they owned a piece of it. Twelve months of waking up in the same berth knowing exactly where you were, which is the particular hell nobody warns you about — that safety, sustained long enough, starts to feel like being buried politely.

"What's the money?" I asked, because asking costs nothing.

He told me. It was good money. It was insultingly good money, the kind of number a man offers when he wants to own you rather than hire you, and the fact that some grain factor wanted the *Marguerite* that badly should have interested me more than it did.

"Tell him we're committed through the season," I said.

"We're not committed through the season."

"He doesn't know that."

Tull looked at me the way young people look at old people who are doing something obviously stupid but doing it with confidence. "You hate grain runs anyway," he said. "You say they're for men who've given up."

"I say a lot of things."

"You do."

I drank the dead coffee. Somewhere ahead, hidden in the fog, the bend was about to ask us a question, and I wanted my hands free for the answer.

* * *

Here is what the fog gives you, if you let it: silence with texture. The engine drops to half-turns so you can hear over it. The world shrinks to thirty yards of brown water and the sound of your own boat breathing. Some pilots hate it. I've known men who'd tie up and wait out a fog for two days rather than feel their way through, and I've never understood them, because fog is the only time the river and I are alone together, and I trust it more than I trust most harbors.

I brought her down to bare steerage way and listened.

Off the right bank: willows, which meant shallow water slopping against roots, which meant the channel had shifted left since last month. Good. Left suited me. Then, faintly, the sound I was listening for — or rather the absence of sound, a stretch where the current made no noise against the banks at all, which meant deep water, slow water, the throat of the channel swallowing easy. I eased the wheel over and let her find it.

You learn to hear lies too. A bar will sometimes make a soft hiss where the current drags across it, and a lazy pilot hears that hiss and thinks *shallow, go wide*, when actually the hiss marks the deep channel cutting past the bar's shoulder. The river punishes both kinds of stupidity equally: the kind that rushes and the kind that hesitates. It only rewards attention, and attention is exhausting, which is why attention pays.

Tull watched me work for a while, the way he does when he wants to ask something and is assembling the courage in installments.

"My mum says you used to have another name," he said finally.

"Everyone used to have another name."

"Not like that. She says the Winterbournes of—" He stopped himself, which was more tact than I expected from a nineteen-year-old with a head cold.

"Tull."

"Captain."

"The next person who brings up that name on my boat gets to swim alongside until the fog lifts. It's excellent exercise and it builds character."

"Yes, Captain." But he didn't move away, and after a moment he said, quieter, "It's just — Pell's contract. A year's berth. Steady pay. You could fix her properly. New decking, real boiler tubes. You could stop—"

"Stop what?"

He didn't answer, because there wasn't a version of the answer that didn't end with *stop running*, and Tull was many things but not cruel.

I put us through the bend instead. The fog opened just enough to show me the cutbank on the outside of the turn, raw earth sliding into the water in great torn slabs, the river eating its own bank the way it eats everything eventually, patiently, without malice. I gave the cutbank its room. The *Marguerite* leaned into the turn with a groan of timber and came out the other side straight and honest, and for maybe ten seconds the fog lifted entirely and I could see a mile of river unrolling ahead, silver-brown and empty, and I thought — the way I always think, the way it's practically liturgy now — *this. Just this. Keep moving and nothing can land on you.*

Then the fog closed, and the thought went with it, and I was just a man steering a boat through soup, avoiding a sandbar that hadn't existed in spring and wouldn't exist next week.

* * *

Casterly Wharf announced itself by smell before sight — coal smoke and tar and the fried-onion reek of the dockside stalls, all of it arriving through the fog a full mile before the pilings did. By the time the wharf emerged, ghost-gray, with its cranes standing like gallows birds, Agent Pell was already on the dock, and Pell never waits on a dock unless he's carrying paper.

Pell is a small round man with the eyes of a customs inspector and the soul of a bookmaker, and I say that with affection, mostly. He waved us in with both arms like a man directing an orchestra of one.

"Winterbourne! Beautiful arrival. Textbook. You know, half these captains would've tied up and waited out that fog."

"Half those captains still have hulls."

"And the other half have contracts!" He beamed, fell into step beside me as the lines went ashore, and produced the document from inside his coat with the flourish of a magician producing a dove. "Now. Before you make that face—"

"This is my face."

"This is the face you make before money. Look at the retainer. Look at the guaranteed tonnage. Winterbourne, this is the kind of contract men retire on. One river, one route, twelve months, and the factor at Osterlin is flexible on delivery dates. Flexible! Do you know how rarely that word appears in a grain contract?"

I took the paper. I read contracts the way I read water — looking for the lie under the smooth surface. Guaranteed tonnage, quarterly retainer, penalty clauses for delay, renewal option at the factor's discretion. And there, buried in the fourth page like a hook buried in bait: *vessel to remain berthed at Osterlin between runs.*

Berthed. Between runs. Which meant moored, which meant stationary, which meant three hundred and forty days a year with the *Marguerite* tied to the same pilings while I slept in the same room above the same chandlery and learned, slowly, the names of everyone on the street, and they learned mine, and one morning someone would use my name in a sentence I couldn't walk away from.

"It's generous," I said.

"It is!"

"I'll pass."

Pell's face did something complicated, like a map being refolded wrong. "Winterbourne. Forgive me, but — I've been brokering on this wharf for twenty-two years. I know what a man looks like when he's running from debt, and I know what a man looks like when he's running from something else. You're the second kind, and the second kind always thinks the road goes on forever." He tucked the contract away, unhurt, professional. "Roads don't, Captain. Rivers especially. Every river ends somewhere."

"Mine hasn't yet."

"No?" He glanced up at the wheelhouse, where Tull was pretending very hard to coil a line. "And what happens when it does?"

What happens is you find out what you were running from has been walking beside you the whole way. What happens is the thing you buried grows patient. What happens is a name comes down the river looking for you, wearing whatever disguise the season requires.

But I said none of that. I said, "Tell your factor thanks, and tell him the *Marguerite*'s committed elsewhere," which was a lie so comfortable it was nearly furniture, and Pell sighed the sigh of a man who has watched this exact scene before and knows how the third act goes, and went off to bother some captain with better sense.

* * *

We took on coal that afternoon. I like coaling days — honest work, black dust, everybody filthy and equal. Tull shoveling, the fireman singing something obscene about a lock-keeper's wife, me checking the flues because a cold spot in a flue is how boilers become bombs.

The fog burned off by mid-afternoon into a sky the color of pewter, low and heavy, the kind of sky that means the mountains upstream were getting rain whether we liked it or not. Old-timers call that sky *river weather* — the Vell rising somewhere far away and sending its intentions downstream ahead of itself, the way bad news travels.

Sera Quill was not on the dock that day. I want to be clear about that, because later, when people asked, I remembered checking. There was a coal surveyor's clerk expected on the evening packet, some official from the Survey Office coming upriver to measure seams nobody cared about, and Pell mentioned it while counting my freight receipts, and I mentioned that I didn't care, and neither statement was entirely true, though I didn't know the second part yet.

What I knew that evening was simpler. We cast off at dusk on the falling tide, running empty back upriver to Harrow Bend to load cotton, and the *Marguerite* went up the middle of the channel with her wheel turning steady and sure, and I stood at the rail below the wheelhouse and watched Casterly Wharf shrink behind us into the pewter dusk, and I felt the specific relief I always felt leaving a port — the loosening in the chest, the sense of the world opening again like a hand unclenching.

Tull found me there. He'd gotten worse at hiding the cold and better at not mentioning it.

"Pell says the factor's going to offer again in spring," he said. "Higher."

"He can offer me the moon on a chain. Berthed between runs."

"You could say yes someday. To something. A year isn't a grave, Captain."

"A year is a door that locks from the inside," I said, and immediately regretted saying it out loud, because it was too honest, and honesty on my boat was rationed like fresh water.

Tull looked at me for a long moment. Behind him the paddlewheel turned, throwing up its fan of white water, and the banks slid past dark and dripping, willows and cutbanks and the occasional lamp of a farmhouse burning small and stubborn in the wet.

"You read water like scripture," he said suddenly. "My mum says that. Everyone says that. But scripture's got an ending, Captain. Water doesn't. So what are you actually reading for?"

I didn't have an answer then. I'd like to tell you I was wise enough to know I didn't have one, but the truth is I thought I had one — *I'm reading for tomorrow's channel, boy, same as any pilot* — and I believed it, the way you believe a chart.

The storm came up that night from the southwest, earlier and harder than the barometer promised. Rain like gravel on the cabin roof, lightning turning the river white, and I stood my watch at the wheel with the world reduced to the compass card and the hiss of the rain and the deep mutter of the Vell rising under us, carrying down from the high country everything the mountains were ready to give up.

By morning the river would be a different animal. By morning the flood would have done its work along the drowned banks above Tallow Reach, prying loose the things people bury, the way floods do.

I didn't know that yet. I went below at midnight and slept the dreamless sleep of a man who believes the road goes on forever, and the river rose all night around my boat, patient as a creditor, and reached into the places I'd spent ten years making sure nothing could reach.

In the morning, Tull would haul something aboard out of the brown water, and it would have a seal on it I hadn't seen since I was nine years old, and the road — as Pell could have told me, as the river had been trying to tell me all along — would end exactly where it had always been planning to.
