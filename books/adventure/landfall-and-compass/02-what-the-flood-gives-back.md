## 2. What the Flood Gives Back

The storm spent itself against the Vell like a drunk against a locked door—all night, furious, and ultimately pointless. By dawn the river had gone flat and brown and satisfied with itself, the way it always does after taking something.

I was on deck before first light, because that's what captains do: they survey the damage and pretend they expected it. The *Marguerite* had ridden out the night at her moorings off Tallow Bend, pitching hard enough to shake the coffee out of my cup and the sleep out of my skull. One fender lost. A rail stave cracked. The canvas over the forward cargo had torn loose and I'd spent two hours in the rain re-tying it while lightning did its best to photograph me for posterity.

Now the sun was coming up the color of weak tea, and the river was giving back what it had borrowed.

That's the thing about floods nobody puts in the almanacs. They don't only take. They take from one place and hand it to another, downstream, without so much as a bill of lading. Fences. Livestock. An entire henhouse once, sailing past me at Casterly Wharf with a rooster standing on the ridgepole like an admiral reviewing his fleet. The Vell is a thief with a conscience that catches up to it every spring.

Tull came up from below rubbing his eyes, seventeen years old and built like a question mark, and stopped dead when he saw the water.

"Cap," he said. "You seeing this?"

"Depends what I'm supposed to be seeing."

"Out there." He pointed past the bow. "There's a whole orchard coming down."

There was. Apple trees, six or seven of them, roots first, drifting in a slow procession like mourners late to a funeral they'd rather miss. Behind them came a fence gate, half a roof, and a barrel that turned out—when Tull gaffed it—to contain somebody's entire winter supply of pickled cabbage. We kept the barrel. Waste not, and all that. The cabbage smelled like victory.

It was maybe an hour later, while I was coiling line and pretending the ache in my shoulders was someone else's problem, that Tull shouted again. This time his voice had a different shape to it. Not delight. Something closer to the sound you make when you find a snake in your boot.

"Cap! There's a—come look at this!"

I came and looked at this.

Floating ten yards off our starboard side, riding absurdly high for its size, was a chest. Not a crate, not a trunk—a chest, iron-banded, oak-planked, the kind of thing you'd expect to find guarding the foot of some dead lord's bed. It turned slowly in the current, catching the morning light along one edge, and it was coming straight for us with the patience of something that had already decided where it belonged.

"Don't," I said.

"I didn't say anything."

"You were about to say we should fetch it aboard."

"I was about to say it's going to hit us."

It wasn't. It was going to pass us by four feet, easy. But Tull was already moving, and there's no force on this earth—not flood, not famine, not the coalition navy—that moves faster than a teenage boy who has spotted something interesting. He had the gaff out and hooked it before I finished saying the word *leave*, which he later claimed he never heard, and which I have chosen to believe.

The chest came over the rail heavier than it had any right to be, hit the deck with a boom that rattled my teeth, and lay there dripping.

We stood around it. Me, Tull, and the pickled cabbage.

"Well," Tull said. "Open it."

"It's not ours."

"It floated to us."

"That's not how property works."

"Says the man who kept the cabbage."

The boy had me there. I looked at the chest, and the chest sat there being smug about it, water pooling under its bands, and I'll tell you honestly what I felt, because a man ought to be honest at least once per chapter: I felt dread. Plain and simple, cold behind the breastbone, the same feeling I get when a stranger at a dock says my family name in a voice that's too casual. Some part of me—the part that has kept me moving up and down this river for ten years, never mooring longer than a season, never signing anything longer than a contract—some part of me knew that chest had come looking for me specifically, and I wanted very much to push it back over the rail and let the river have it back.

But curiosity is a cheaper vice than fear, and I've always been a thrifty man in the wrong directions.

"Get the pry bar," I said.

* * *

The bands were swollen shut but the wood had given up arguing somewhere in the night. Tull worked the bar while I watched the treeline, because if a chest floats downriver out of a flood, other things float downriver out of a flood too, and some of them have hands.

Inside: water. An inch of it, brown and still, sitting atop everything like a lid of its own. And beneath the water—

Paper.

Dozens of documents, oilcloth-wrapped bundles of them, sodden but intact, packed with a care that even forty-eight hours of drowning hadn't ruined entirely. Whoever had sealed this chest had wanted what was inside to survive. Had planned for it to survive. You don't waterproof a chest full of paper unless you expect the world to try very hard to drown it.

And stamped into the lid, deep enough to survive the water, the rust, and whatever violence had pried it loose from wherever it had been hidden, was a seal.

Three crowns. Stacked. Interlocked at the bases so they made one shape out of three.

I knew that seal the way you know a face from a story you were told as a child and told never to repeat. Everyone on the Vell knows it, the way everyone knows not to say the name that goes with it. It was on the old coins that turn up in dredge nets sometimes, and the dredge men throw them back quick and wipe their hands. It was carved—badly, secretly—on a gravestone outside Marrow Ford that the parish keeps threatening to sand down. Three crowns, stacked.

Ostrava.

A country that hasn't existed since before Tull was born. A country that, officially, has never existed at all—you can look through every atlas printed in the last forty years and find nothing between the Vell headwaters and the Greymarch hills but coalition territory, surveyed, registered, and signed for. A nation dissolved by treaty, erased by ink, and buried so thoroughly that mentioning it aloud in certain company gets you a visit from men with clipboards and no sense of humor.

"Tull," I said quietly. "Go check the bilge."

"The bilge was fine an hour ago."

"Check it again. Take your time."

He took the hint, mostly. He lingered long enough to give the seal one more look, and I saw him file it away somewhere behind his eyes, and I made a note to have a conversation with him about the value of forgetting things. Later. Everything later.

I knelt and lifted out the first bundle.

The oilcloth peeled away wet, and inside were pages written in a hand so fine and formal it looked typeset. I won't pretend I read them all then and there—I didn't. My hands were shaking, which annoyed me, and I'm a man who deals with annoyance by slowing down. But some words announce themselves regardless, the way a broken rib announces itself no matter how calmly you breathe.

*...the lawful succession having been preserved against all dissolution...*

*...the registry complete and unbroken, held in trust...*

*...unto whomsoever of the blood shall present these tokens, the claim descends entire...*

And then, on the third page, in a different hand—heavier, hurried, a hand I would have known anywhere, because I'd seen it forged across a hundred debt notices sent to a house that no longer answered its door:

*J. Winterbourne.*

Not *Juniper*. Just the initial. Just enough.

My father's handwriting, naming my father's name—or close enough to it—in a document sealed under the crown of a drowned kingdom, floating down a river I'd spent ten years using to outrun him.

I sat back on my heels. The *Marguerite* rocked gently beneath me, indifferent. Somewhere upstream, whatever attic or cellar or grave that chest had been hiding in had given it up to the water, and the water had carried it past orchards and fences and drowned hens, down all those miles of current, and set it against my hull with the accuracy of a debt collector.

The past doesn't chase you. That's the mistake everybody makes. Chasing implies effort, implies the possibility of escape. The past just waits downstream, because it knows where you're going. Every river runs to the same place eventually.

"Cap?" Tull's voice from the hatchway. "Bilge's dry. Knew it would be."

"Mm."

"So what's in it?"

"Cabbage recipes," I said. "Very old ones."

He grinned, because he knew I was lying and knew I knew he knew, and that's the arrangement we have, Tull and I. He doesn't press, and I don't insult him by pressing harder. But as he turned back to his work, I caught him glancing at the open chest the way a man glances at a door he's decided not to ask about.

I wrapped the documents back in their oilcloth, all except the one with my father's hand on it. That one I held a moment longer. The ink had run at the edges where the water found it, so that the initial *J* had begun to blur, spreading outward like a stain, like something growing.

Here's what I told myself, standing there on my own deck with a dead nation's paperwork in my hands: it could be anyone. *J. Winterbourne.* Half the river towns are full of Winterbournes, or close enough—Winterby, Winterson, a whole clan of them up past Grey Locks spelling it Wynterbourn with a pretension that has survived three generations of mockery. The chest wasn't mine. It was coincidence, salvage, flotsam. I'd dry the pages, sell them to some collector with more money than sense, and be two locks upstream by week's end with the incident filed under *weather.*

That's what I told myself.

What I actually did was stand there until the sun cleared the hills, holding a single sheet of paper while the river went on doing what rivers do, which is deliver things whether you've ordered them or not.

* * *

By noon I'd made decisions, which for me constitutes a crisis.

Decision one: the chest goes below, under the spare canvas, behind the engine spares, where no customs man has ever bothered to look because no customs man has ever cared enough about my boat to look twice. Decision two: we make way for Casterly Wharf by evening tide, because Casterly has a tavern, and the tavern has a man named Odell Fenwick who used to be a clerk in the provincial registry before drink and opinions retired him, and if anyone on this stretch of the Vell can tell me what a dissolved country's inheritance papers look like—and what they're worth, and to whom—it's Odell.

Decision three, which I did not write down and barely admitted to myself: I needed to know if that initial meant what it looked like it meant. Because if it did—if my father, the quiet, failed, debt-ridden man whose greatest legacy to me was a surname I'd been dodging like a tollgate—if that man had been mixed up in the business of Ostrava, then the chest wasn't salvage.

It was a fuse. And I was standing on it, holding the match.

Tull cast us off without being told twice. As the *Marguerite* swung into the current and Tallow Bend fell away behind us, I took the wheel and put us mid-channel, where the water runs truest, and tried to feel like a man hauling ordinary cargo to an ordinary market.

The chest sat below my feet, silent as a held breath.

Halfway to Casterly, Tull appeared at my elbow with two cups of coffee and the expression of a boy assembling courage out of spare parts.

"Cap," he said. "That seal. The three crowns."

"What about it?"

"My gran used to sing a song about those. Lullaby sort of thing. About a king under the water." He blew on his coffee. "She said if you ever found something with that mark on it, you shouldn't read what's inside."

"Why not?"

"Because it reads you back." He shrugged, delivered this with the magnificent unselfconsciousness of the very young, and wandered off to bother the winch.

I laughed. I want that on the record—I laughed, and I steered south toward Casterly Wharf, and I told myself the boy's grandmother had been a superstitious woman with a head full of river nonsense.

But my hand stayed on the wheel, and the other hand, without asking my permission, kept touching the pocket where I'd put the page with my father's handwriting. Just checking it was there. The way you check a wound.

The past had found me. All that remained to learn was what it wanted—and who else was looking for it.

I got my answer to the second question sooner than I liked. But that was Casterly's business, and Casterly was still a day downstream, and for one more night the chest was only wood and water and a story I hadn't finished telling myself yet.
