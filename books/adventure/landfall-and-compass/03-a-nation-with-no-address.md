## 3. A Nation With No Address

The chest sat in my cabin like a guest who wouldn't take a hint.

I'd turned it over twice during the night, once by lamplight and once by the stubborn gray light that comes before dawn on the Vell, and both times it had looked back at me with the same triple-crowned seal pressed into its lead lining, patient as a debt collector. Water had gotten inside—of course it had, water gets everywhere on this river, it's the one tenant nobody evicts—but not enough. The documents in their oilskin wrapper were legible. That was the problem. Salvage is supposed to be boots, chain, a crate of someone else's bad whiskey. Salvage is supposed to be anonymous.

This had my name on it. *J. Winterbourne, lawful heir.* In ink. In triplicate.

Tull knocked at six, which meant he'd been up since five pretending he wasn't curious.

"Captain," he said through the door, "we're coming up on Marrow's Bend. Thought you'd want the wheel."

"I always want the wheel."

"Yes, sir. Also thought you might want breakfast."

"Did you make breakfast?"

"I made an attempt at breakfast."

"Then I want the wheel more." I latched the cabin, which was a gesture with no meaning—the Marguerite's locks were decorative, like her figurehead and her promises—and went forward into a morning the color of dishwater.

Marrow's Bend is where the Vell stops pretending to be gentle. The channel dog-legs around a bluff of red clay, and every spring the flood rearranges the sandbars like a card sharp who doesn't like your face. You read the water or the water reads you, and only one of you gets to keep the boat. I took us through on the back eddy, riding the seam between the current and the slack, close enough to the willow tangle on the point that Tull stopped breathing for a full minute. Then we were through, into the long straight reach below Casterly Wharf, and the river went back to being merely indifferent.

"You knew that bar was there," Tull said.

"The river told me."

"Rivers don't talk."

"Yours doesn't. Mine's been talking to me for ten years. Mostly it complains about you."

He grinned and went aft, and I stood at the wheel and did not think about the chest, which is to say I thought about nothing else. A man can outrun a sheriff, a creditor, a woman with a good memory. He cannot outrun his own name written down somewhere in a hand steadier than his own.

Casterly Wharf came up by noon: coal dust, fish scales, rope ends, and the particular smell of a town that has decided to stop trying. There's a tavern there called the Drowned Bell, which is either a joke about the bell of a sunken steamer mounted over the bar or a joke about everyone who drinks there, and I've never determined which. It's where cargo pilots go to learn things. It's also where they go to be seen learning them, which is a different trade entirely, and I've tried to stay out of both.

But a name needs asking about, and asking takes witnesses, so I tied off, told Tull to keep aboard and keep the cabin shut, and went into the Bell with the chest's oilskin wrapper folded flat under my coat like a guilty conscience.

* * *

The Drowned Bell at midday is mostly empty, which is how I like my taverns: fewer ears, cheaper lies. Old Marta ran the bar the way she ran everything, which was as if she owned it and resented it equally. She poured me something brown without my asking, because she remembered what I drank, and remembering is dangerous in a woman like Marta.

"Juniper Winterbourne," she said. "Still alive. The river's slipping."

"Give it time."

"You look thin."

"I look efficient."

She snorted and moved off to terrorize a pair of deckhands, and I took my drink to the corner table by the window, where a man could watch the wharf and be watched only by the rain starting to come down. I ordered stew I didn't want, because a man eating alone reads as a man waiting; a man with food reads as a man minding his business. On the Vell, appearance is nine-tenths of law.

The documents were dry now, spread across my knee under the table's edge. I'd gone through them twice more on the walk over, and the picture hadn't improved. There was a registry page, heavy paper, an embossed crown over three towers. There was a letter in a lawyer's cramped hand referring to "the dissolution" as though it were weather. And there was the instrument itself—or the copy of it—a treaty bearing signatures and a date forty years gone, dissolving something called the Kingdom of Ostrava and distributing its territory among four signatory powers, "in perpetuity, and to the extinguishing of all claims royal, hereditary, or popular."

Extinguishing. Like blowing out a lamp. Like the whole country had been a candle somebody got tired of.

Except here was a document saying the candle had been cupped in two hands and carried, secretly, by a man named Aldous Winterbourne, my father, and passed—to me, of all people, to the son who hadn't spoken to him in eleven years and hadn't needed to, because dead men finish their sentences eventually whether you listen or not.

I was still staring at the word *extinguishing* when the voice came from behind my left shoulder.

"You're reading that wrong."

I don't startle. Startling costs money on this river. But I'll admit my hand found the table edge and stayed there. The speaker was a big soft-bodied man in a coal merchant's coat, the kind of coat that's cut to hide a pistol and usually succeeds at hiding nothing else. He had a face like a dropped pie and eyes that weren't looking at me so much as at the paper under my arm.

"Reading what wrong?" I said. "I'm reading a menu."

"Marta's stew isn't on any menu worth reading." He pulled out the opposite chair uninvited and sat, which told me he was either very confident or very stupid, and the coat argued for confident. "That document. The dissolution instrument. Everyone reads it as a land division. It's not. Read the fourth clause sometime. It's an amnesty clause. It pardons the men who did the killing."

"I really couldn't say. I salvage. Boots, mostly."

"Boots," he agreed pleasantly. "With triple-crown seals on them."

The rain ticked against the window. Somewhere behind the bar Marta broke a glass, which meant she was listening, which meant half the room would know my business by nightfall regardless of what happened next. I made my face do what it does best, which is nothing.

"Friend," I said, "you've got the wrong pilot. I don't know anything about seals. I know about draft depths and fuel prices and which customs men can be bribed with tobacco and which need coin."

"And your name?"

"Is on my license. Ask the harbor office."

"Your other name."

"There's only the one. My mother was economical."

He smiled at that, a wide pie-faced smile, and leaned back. "Forty years ago," he said, conversational, almost cozy, "there was a country upriver. Past the Grey Locks, past the Judgment, all the way to the source. Ostrava. Triple crown, three towers, the works. You'll notice I'm speaking quietly."

"I notice you're speaking at all."

"There's a reason for that." He turned his glass a quarter-turn on the table, precise, a man who liked systems. "Ostrava had a coup. Bad business. The king's own chancellor opened the gates, or that's the story. There was a massacre at the palace—two hundred souls, give or take, though nobody counted properly because counting was also discouraged. Then the neighbors drew a map with no Ostrava on it, called it peace, and made mentioning the old name a crime in three of the four new territories. Sedition. You can lose a boat over it. You can lose more than a boat."

"Fascinating history lesson," I said. "Is there a version with a point?"

"The point, Captain, is that the country didn't fall. Countries don't fall. They get *closed*, like accounts, and somebody always keeps the books." His eyes flicked down, just once, toward my coat. "And somebody always inherits them."

I laughed, and it even sounded like a laugh. Ten years of practice. "You're the second man this month to mistake me for somebody interesting. The first wanted to sell me a dredge."

"Keep the papers dry," he said, standing, dropping a coin on the table for a drink he hadn't ordered. "And Captain—don't ask about Ostrava again. Not here, not downriver, not anywhere with walls. Asking is worse than knowing. Knowing you can bury. Asking, people hear."

He walked out into the rain without a coat collar turned up, which bothered me more than anything else he'd said. Men with secrets mind the weather. Men on assignment don't.

Marta came over with the pot and refilled me without being asked. Her voice dropped to a register I'd only heard her use twice before, both times about drownings.

"That man asked me yesterday if a Winterbourne boat ran this stretch."

"What did you tell him?"

"I told him every boat on the Vell is named after somebody's dead wife." She set the pot down hard. "Juniper. Whatever you fished out of that flood—and don't tell me it's boots—I'd float it back downriver and let the river have it back. Some things the flood gives up by mistake."

"Everything the flood gives up, it gave up on purpose. That's what a flood is."

"Then you're a fool with scripture," she said, and went back to her glasses, and I sat there feeling the truth of it settle into my boots like bilgewater.

* * *

I stayed another hour, which was an hour too long, but a man who bolts looks like a man with something to bolt from, and I was working very hard at looking like a man with nothing at all. I ate the stew. It was terrible. I paid. I tipped. I watched the window.

The rain kept on. Wharf traffic thinned. And then, near three o'clock, a man came in out of the wet whom I hadn't seen arrive—a small, neat, gray man, the sort of person rivers produce in bulk, forgettable as silt. He took a table with a view of mine, ordered tea, and did nothing whatsoever, competently, for twenty minutes.

I know that competence. I've practiced it myself in a dozen ports. So when he rose, passed my table, and paused—just a half-step, just long enough—I was already turning my shoulder to shield the oilskin, already composing the bored expression.

He didn't reach for the papers. He reached into his coat and produced a small brass-boxed camera, the new kind, the kind that needs no flash and no tripod and no permission, and he took a photograph of the documents on my knee at a distance of four feet, calm as a man photographing a sunset.

"Hey—" I was up, chair scraping, but he was already moving, unhurried, threading between tables with the ease of a man who had rehearsed the exits. I followed him into the rain and the street was empty in both directions, wharf-long, rain-hammered, and the gray man was nowhere in it. A man doesn't vanish on a straight street unless he knows the street better than it knows itself, and there are doors in Casterly Wharf that aren't doors, and I suddenly understood I was standing in a town where somebody had arranged, in advance, for a man to disappear.

I stood in the rain a while anyway, doing the arithmetic. One stranger who knew the fourth clause of a treaty I'd owned for two days. One photographer who knew exactly what to point at. One barkeep warned off my name before I ever walked in with it. That's not curiosity. Curiosity travels alone. This traveled in relays, like a message being passed upriver faster than any boat could carry it—which meant somebody downstream was already holding the end of the string, and pulling.

I went back into the Bell. Marta saw my face and reached for the bottle instead of the pot.

"Back door," she said. Not a question.

"How many ways out of this town?"

"For most people? Two." She slid the bottle across. "For you, three, if your boat draws less than four feet and you don't mind the old logging cut."

"When did I ever mind?"

"Juniper." She put her hand flat on the bar, an old woman's hand, river-roughened, and for a moment she wasn't the Drowned Bell's keeper, she was just somebody who'd known me since I was a deckhand stealing apples off her counter. "Whatever name you're running from—it found you. Names always do. The only question left is whether you meet it on your feet or on your knees."

"I've met everything on my feet so far."

"So far," she agreed, "the river's been shallow."

I finished the drink standing. Outside, the rain had thickened into something with intent, monsoon weather arriving early and angry, and down at the wharf the Marguerite sat low and loyal against the pilings with Tull's silhouette in the wheelhouse, keeping watch over a chest he didn't understand and a captain he maybe understood too well.

Somewhere behind me, in a room with no sign on the door, a photograph was drying. Somewhere downriver, a man I hadn't met yet was going to look at it and know precisely which boat, which bend, which pilot. The fuse didn't hiss. Fuses never do. They just sit there, quiet, patient, burning toward whatever you love most.

I bought a second bottle from Marta, tipped my hat, and went out into the rain to move my boat before anybody could ask me why.

By the time I reached the Marguerite, Tull was waiting at the gangway, rain streaming off his cap, wearing the expression of a boy with news he didn't want to deliver.

"Captain," he said. "There's a woman at the wharf office. Been asking after us since noon. Says she's a coal surveyor." He hesitated. "She's still there. She says she'll wait."

Of course she would. They always wait. That's what makes them dangerous.

"Did she give a name?" I asked, already knowing there'd be one, already knowing I'd remember it.

"Quill," Tull said. "Sera Quill."
