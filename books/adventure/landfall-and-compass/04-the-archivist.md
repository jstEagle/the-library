## 4. The Archivist

I have lied to a great many people in my life, but I had never before lied to a riverboat captain at four in the morning, and I will say this for the experience: it is easier than it looks. All you need is a clipboard, a plausible profession, and the willingness to stand in the rain looking bored. Boredom, I have found, is the most convincing costume a person can wear. Nobody interrogates a bored woman. They simply assume she has somewhere better to be and let her pass.

Casterly Wharf at that hour was all coal smoke and gull argument, the kind of place where the fog comes down the valley to die. The *Marguerite* sat low in the water at berth nine, her paddlewheel dripping, her paint the color of a decision someone regretted twenty years ago. I walked her length twice with my surveyor's clipboard held just so—tilted, official, faintly annoyed—and on the third pass I asked the deckhand if the captain was aboard.

"He's sleeping," said the boy, whose name I would later learn was Tull and who would later learn, to his cost, not to trust women with clipboards.

"Coal survey," I said. "Port authority. It can wait until he's up."

It could not wait, of course. But lies keep better when you give them room to breathe.

Here is what I actually was: an archivist. Third-generation, Guild-trained, expelled for asking questions with the wrong surnames attached to them. And here is what I actually wanted: ten minutes alone with the contents of a chest stamped with a triple crown that no longer existed on any map printed south of the Vell.

I want to be honest about how I justified it to myself, because honesty is the one luxury I've never been able to afford and so I hoard it privately. I told myself the documents belonged to history, and history belongs to everyone. This is the sort of thing archivists tell themselves right up until they're crouched in a stranger's cabin at dawn with a copying lens in their teeth, and the stranger walks in.

* * *

He didn't shout. That was the first surprise. I'd rehearsed shouting—I had a whole speech prepared about port authority jurisdiction and how he'd be hearing from the surveyor's office—but Juniper Winterbourne didn't shout. He stood in the doorway of his own cabin with his coat still wet from the wheelhouse watch, looked at me kneeling among his papers with a tracing stylus in my hand, and said:

"You're holding that wrong."

"I beg your pardon?"

"The stylus." He came into the cabin like a man entering a tavern he already disliked. "You're bearing down on it. You'll tear the vellum. Those pages are forty years old and they've been underwater; they'll forgive a lot, but they won't forgive you."

I looked down at the page under my hands—the page with the triple crown pressed into its seal, wax gone the color of old honey—and realized my hand was shaking. Not from fear. From proximity. Ten years I had chased the ghost of Ostrava through other people's footnotes, through redacted registries and burned parish rolls, and here it was, damp and real and close enough to smudge.

"You're not going to call the harbormaster," I said.

"No."

"You're not going to throw me off the boat."

"Probably not." He sat down across from me, unhurried, and poured himself coffee from a pot that had clearly been keeping him company since midnight. "You copied three pages before I caught you. Show me which ones."

"Why?"

"Because if you were working for Brannic or Vane or anyone else with a payroll, you'd have taken the chest and been gone by now. You didn't take the chest. You *copied*. Nobody pays for copies when they can pay for originals." He drank his coffee. "So either you're an idiot, or you want what's in these papers for reasons that don't fit in a purse. I've met both kinds. Idiots hold the stylus wrong too, but they don't apologize to the vellum first."

I hadn't apologized to the vellum. But I had, I realized, been breathing carefully around it, the way you do around something fragile and beloved, and apparently it showed.

"My name isn't on the surveyor's roll," I said.

"I know. There is no coal survey. Casterly doesn't export coal; it imports it, badly."

"My name is Sera Quill."

"I know that too. It was on your boarding paper. You didn't even change your name." Something moved at the corner of his mouth—not quite a smile, more like the memory of one. "For a spy, you're terrible. For whatever else you are, I'm listening."

And that—that was the moment I understood I was in genuine danger, and it wasn't because he might turn me over to the coalition. It was because he was reasonable. Men who shout, you can manage. Men who pour coffee and ask precise questions will get the truth out of you eventually, and I had spent ten years making sure nobody got the truth out of me, because the truth about my family was the kind of thing that got people expunged.

But there was a triple-crown seal six inches from my knee, and I was tired, and the rain on the cabin roof sounded like the archive stacks back home, all those years ago, before home stopped being a word that applied.

"Show me the rest of the papers," I said. "All of them. And then I'll tell you why my family has no grave."

* * *

He laid them out on the chart table with the care of a man handling something between scripture and ordnance. I had seen fragments before—a line quoted in a banned monograph, a seal described secondhand by a smuggler who charged me a month's wages for the description. Never the whole. Never the registry itself.

The dissolution treaty. The surrender terms. And beneath them, in a different hand, older and smaller: the royal registry of Ostrava, page after page of names, births, holdings, lines of descent—the administrative skeleton of a country that had been declared never to have existed.

"Do you know what erasure means, legally?" I asked him. My voice came out steadier than I expected. "Not metaphorically. Legally. When the coalition dissolved Ostrava under the Treaty of Nine Rivers, they didn't just annex the territory. They annulled the *people*. Citizenship revoked retroactively. Property reverted. Names struck from every civil roll in the coalition. My grandmother died in Casterly in '41, and the death certificate says she was born nowhere. No nation of origin. The clerk literally wrote *nowhere*, because writing Ostrava was sedition and he had children to feed."

Juniper said nothing. He was watching the registry pages, not me, and there was something in his face I couldn't read yet—something tighter than curiosity.

"The Quills served the Ostravan crown for six generations," I went on. "Keepers of the western archive. When the coup came, my grandfather got the registry out. Got it as far as the Vell. Then—" I made a gesture that was meant to be casual and wasn't. "Then the river did what rivers do. The chest went down somewhere in the flood country, and my grandfather went down with it, and for forty years everyone assumed the registry drowned. Including me. Until three days ago, when a salvage report crossed my desk about a deckhand hauling a sealed chest out of a sandbar near Mirefoot."

"And you traced it to me."

"I traced it to the *Marguerite*, which is famous in exactly one way: her captain refuses contracts, avoids ports, and cannot be found when he wants to be unfound. Which meant I couldn't come to you openly, because the moment I filed a claim on those documents through any official channel, the coalition would file a bigger one, and then the chest would go into an evidence vault in Corvane and I would go into a cell, and the names in that registry would stay dead another forty years." I finally looked up at him. "So yes. I lied to you. I used you. I'm not sorry about the lying, and I am only slightly sorry about the using, because you were using yourself just as hard—you've built an entire life out of not being findable, Captain, and I'd very much like to know what name you're hiding from, because it occurs to me that a man who won't stop moving might be running from the same treaty I am."

The silence went on long enough that I heard Tull on deck above us, swearing at a fouled line in the mild, conversational way of sailors.

"It's Winterbourne," Juniper said at last. "You already knew that."

"It's on your registration. Registration papers lie. Yours are too clean."

That earned me a longer look than I'd bargained for. He gathered the registry pages together, squared their edges with a precision that seemed almost compulsive, and set them aside from the treaty documents—and I noticed, filing it away the way archivists file everything, that he handled the two piles differently. The treaty he touched like evidence. The registry he touched like a wound.

"There's a name in there," he said. "In the registry. A signature on the last attestation page."

I turned to the attestation page. The keeper's certification, signed by the last Archivist of Ostrava. And below it, countersigned—the man who had witnessed the registry's removal and secret transport, whose signature I had spent a decade trying to find in coalition records and finding nothing, because the nothing had been purchased.

E. Winterbourne.

I looked up. He was watching me the way men watch weather coming in off open water.

"That's my father," he said.

* * *

I want to record, for the sake of whoever reads this someday, that neither of us trusted the other at that moment, and both of us needed to, and we arranged the difference like two swimmers agreeing to share a plank. That's what an alliance is, really. Not trust. Arithmetic.

We talked until the fog lifted off the wharf and the gulls changed shifts. He told me—sparsely, each fact costing him visibly—that his father had been a treaty clerk, a minor functionary, a man who signed things for a living. That he had grown up on the story that his father's work was dull and his father's death was ordinary. That he had left home at nineteen with a pilot's license and a grudge against every fixed point on the map, and had not stood still since, because standing still meant being found, and being found meant answering questions he didn't have answers to.

And now, it seemed, the answers had floated up out of a sandbar and knocked politely on his hull.

"Your father preserved the registry," I said. "He was there when they dissolved the country, and instead of burning the names like he was ordered to, he put them in a chest and sank them where only a flood could give them back. Do you understand what that makes him?"

"A traitor," Juniper said. "To somebody. Depends which side writes the book."

"To us," I said, and my voice cracked on it, which embarrassed me deeply. "To every family in that book. He committed treason against the coalition to keep forty thousand people real. Your father is the reason my grandmother's grave can someday say she was born *somewhere*."

He turned his coffee cup in a slow circle, not drinking. "Or he's the reason I'm going to be shot. Have you thought about that part? Because I have, since the night Tull hauled this thing aboard, and I keep arriving at the same arithmetic. There's a seal on that treaty with the power to dissolve nations, and there's a name in that registry that says the heir to a dead crown is currently piloting cargo upriver for less money than he's worth. If either of those facts reaches the wrong desk, I stop being a man and start being a problem, and problems get solved. Permanently." He looked at me. "You knew that when you boarded. You climbed onto a fuse and brought a clipboard."

"I climbed onto the only copy of my family's existence in the world," I said. "I'd have boarded a burning ship."

"Yes," he said. "That's what I figured. It's why I haven't thrown you off."

* * *

Here is the arrangement we made, and I set it down plainly because plainness was the currency of that whole strange morning: he would not surrender the chest to me, and I would not leave without access to it. So we split the difference the way scholars and smugglers always do—he kept possession, I kept study. I would catalog and copy everything, page by page, and in exchange I would do what I was actually trained for: read the registry properly, trace the inheritance clause, and find out precisely what the words *lawful heir* committed J. Winterbourne to. Because I had skimmed that clause at dawn with shaking hands, and I had seen enough of coalition legal drafting to know that inheritances written by victors are never gifts. They are traps with ribbons on them.

"You realize what we look like," Juniper said, as I packed my copying kit and the fog outside began to burn off into a gray, workaday morning. "A captain who won't stay anywhere, and an archivist who shouldn't be anywhere. Each of us is the other's alibi now. You board as my surveyor, nobody asks why I'm lingering at Casterly. I keep the chest, nobody asks why a coal surveyor needs a cabin."

"Wary alliance," I agreed.

"Two people using each other as shields."

"I was going to phrase it more kindly, but yes."

He almost smiled. It was, I would learn, the standard model: the smile arrived, considered the situation, and withdrew without committing. "There's a bunk aft," he said. "Tull will show you. And Quill—if anyone boards this boat asking about coal, you're the expert. Convince me you know something about coal."

"I grew up next to a gasworks. I know that coal is black, dirty, and full of buried fire."

"That'll do," he said. "That describes half the people on this river."

I went aft past Tull, who watched me with the frank suspicion of the young, and took possession of a bunk narrower than my conscience, and lay there listening to the *Marguerite* tick and groan as the tide turned.

I should have felt victorious. Forty years of hunting, and the registry was thirty feet from my pillow. But sleep wouldn't come, because my mind kept returning to the attestation page—to E. Winterbourne's signature sitting directly beneath the last Archivist's seal, two names joined in ink across the gap of a destroyed country—and to the son of that signature, who had looked at the proof of his father's courage and called it treason, and whose hands had shaken, very slightly, when he squared the pages.

He thinks he's the traitor's son, I realized. He's spent ten years running from the wrong crime.

Above me, boots crossed the deck. The wheelhouse door opened. And through the hull planking I heard Juniper Winterbourne tell Tull, in a voice pitched for a man who believed himself unheard, to raise steam—we'd be leaving Casterly on the morning tide, ahead of schedule, because a harbor clerk had come down the wharf at dawn asking berth-nine's master a great many polite questions, and polite questions, on this river, were how trouble introduced itself.

I closed my eyes and began, silently, to catalog everything I'd copied, and everything I hadn't yet.

The tide, I noted, was turning faster than the tide had any business turning.
