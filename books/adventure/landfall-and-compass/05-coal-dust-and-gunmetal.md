## 5. Coal Dust and Gunmetal

The whistle came at dawn, two long notes and a short one, which is how the Coalition says *we are the law and we know you can hear us*.

I was on the roof of the pilothouse with a cup of coffee going cold in my hand, watching the fog do its slow arithmetic over the water. The Marguerite was tied up at a fueling dock outside Marrow Bend, taking on coal through a chute that groaned like an old man standing up. Tull was below, supervising the load. Sera Quill was in my cabin with my father's documents spread across my bunk like a diagnosis.

And out on the gray water, a patrol boat was coming up the channel with its running lights still lit, which is either incompetence or theater. With the Coalition it is usually theater.

"Captain Winterbourne." The voice came through a speaking trumpet, flat and official. "Prepare to receive a customs inspection."

I set down the coffee. There is a particular way a morning goes bad, and I have learned to recognize the first symptom: it's when your hands stay steady while everything behind your sternum starts moving very fast.

* * *

Here is what every river pilot knows and no landsman believes: a boat is not a hiding place. A boat is a machine for moving things, and every cubic foot of her is spoken for—cargo hold, bunkroom, lazarette, the space under the wheelbox where I kept spare fuses and, until this week, nothing else worth mentioning. When someone comes aboard looking for something, they are not really searching your boat. They are searching your face. Boats don't lie well. People lie beautifully, if they've had practice, and I'd had a decade of practice, though most of it had been spent lying to myself, which turns out to be poor training for lying to professionals.

The chest was the problem. You cannot hide a chest the size of a coffin in a boat the size of the Marguerite, not from men who do this for a living. But you can change what a thing *is*. A chest full of seditious documents is contraband. A chest full of boiler scale and clinkers is garbage. And there is one place on any steamboat where garbage goes and nobody asks questions, because nobody wants to look at it.

The boiler flue.

I'll grant you it's not elegant. The flue runs from the firebox up through the deckhouse and out the stack, a passage of riveted iron about as hospitable as a cannon barrel. To get the chest into it, I had to drop the inspection plate above the firebox—a job that takes two hours and a curse vocabulary in three languages—and then lower the chest down on a block and tackle while Tull stood below with his arms up like a man catching a baby that hated him. The heat alone would have cooked anything living. Paper survives; paper is patient. That's the whole trouble with paper.

"You're putting our only valuable possession," Tull said, wiping soot off his chin, "inside the chimney."

"I'm putting it where a man would have to be paid very well to look."

"That's usually where you keep things you want found."

"Tull." I looked at him. He's nineteen and built like a capstan and he has never once in a year of working for me asked me a question I couldn't answer with weather. "Go count the coal."

He went and counted the coal. Good boy.

Sera came up as I was wrestling the inspection plate back into place. She had the sense, I'll say that for her, to look like exactly what she claimed to be—a coal surveyor—which meant she'd put on clothes that fit badly and smudged her own face with an economy that suggested practice. There was a moment, crouched there in the firebox light, when she looked at me and I looked at her, and neither of us said the obvious thing, which was: *we are both liars, and one of us is better at it.*

"Where do you want me?" she asked.

"Away from me. Surveyors don't chat with captains. Go fuss at the coal pile. Complain about the quality. Be tedious."

"I'm naturally tedious."

"Then today you're finally useful."

She almost smiled. It was gone before it landed anywhere, but I saw it leave the station.

* * *

They came aboard at seven minutes past six by my chronometer, four of them, in gray coats that the fog hadn't finished dampening. Three were muscle—you can always tell, they walk like the deck is going to argue with them—but the fourth was different, and I knew he was different before he said a word, because he came up the gangway last and let the others go first, the way a man does when he already knows what he'll find.

He was maybe fifty, lean in the way of rope rather than rail, with a face that had been arranged around patience. His coat fit him properly, which on a Coalition man is more alarming than a sidearm. He carried a leather folio under one arm and nothing else visible, and he stood on my main deck taking inventory of the Marguerite with his eyes the way a buyer does, or an undertaker.

"Captain Winterbourne." He didn't offer a hand. "Inspector Brannic, Coalition Customs Directorate, Third River District." He opened the folio without hurry. "Routine manifest verification. You're listed as carrying general cargo, Casterly Wharf to points south."

"That's right."

"No salvage?"

The word dropped into the conversation like a coin into a well. I watched it fall all the way down.

"Salvage rights on the Vell require a district permit," I said. "I don't have one, so I don't salvage. I haul grain, machinery, and other people's bad decisions. Mostly the third."

Brannic looked up from the folio. Not quickly. Slowly, the way a man turns a page he intends to remember.

"There was a storm two nights ago," he said. "Unusual flooding above Marrow Bend. Debris fields. Several properties along the cutbank lost their cellars to the river." He closed the folio. "Rivers give things back after floods, Captain. It's a documented phenomenon. The Directorate keeps a list of what the river gives back, because some of what it gives back belongs to the state."

"Most of what the river gives back," I said, "is somebody's fence posts and their grandmother's chairs. You want me to report grandmothers' chairs?"

"I want you to report anything bearing a national seal."

"And if I find a spoon with an old crest on it? Half the silver on this river has crests on it. Nations come and go. Spoons endure."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. It wasn't a smile. It was the place where a smile would be stored, if he kept such things.

"May I see your boat?"

"It's a free dock," I said, and stepped aside, and hated myself for how smoothly I did it.

* * *

I will tell you now what I told myself then, which is that the next hour was boring. It was not boring. Boring is what fear calls itself when it's trying to get you to relax.

Brannic inspected the way water gets into a hull—not fast, not dramatic, just everywhere. He went through the cargo hold and ran his thumb along the frames. He opened my icebox and looked at my coffee tin. He read the names scratched into the bulkhead by deckhands dead and gone, and I caught myself wondering whether he was reading them or memorizing them. In the pilothouse he stood at the wheel without touching it and looked down the river for a long time, and I understood that he was not admiring the view. He was learning how I saw it.

"Good boat," he said finally. "Old. Old boats have histories."

"Old boats have leaks. Histories are extra."

"You've worked the Vell ten years?"

"Give or take."

"Under this name?"

There it is. That's the question. Everything else had been scenery; this was the play. I let three seconds pass, because a guilty man answers too fast and an innocent man answers too slow, and somewhere between them lives the truth, or at least a good imitation of it.

"The name on my papers is the name I answer to," I said. "You're welcome to wire the registry at Casterly Wharf. They love hearing from the Directorate. It breaks up their week."

"I may do that." He turned from the wheel. "Your surveyor. Quill. How long has she been aboard?"

"She signed on at Casterly. Two days."

"For a coal run."

"Coal's finicky business. You want an expert."

"We checked. There's no Quill on the surveyors' rolls for this district."

The fog had burned off and the sun was doing its ordinary work on the water, and I stood there in all that honest daylight and lied with a captain's calm, which is to say I looked mildly inconvenienced, which is the hardest expression in the world to fake and the easiest to believe.

"Then she's using a working name, same as half the people on this river. Women sign on under names that don't follow them home. Ask your own agents—they've hired enough of them." I shrugged. "She surveys coal. Check her work. If she can't tell lignite from anthracite, throw her off at the next lock and I'll find another."

It was a good lie. It was a good lie because it was mostly true—she could tell lignite from anthracite, I'd tested her that morning over breakfast, and she'd done it with a contempt that convinced even me—and because it offered him something better than suspicion: a test he could win. Men like Brannic don't want to believe you. They want a procedure. Give them a procedure and they'll thank you for it.

He gave me a long look. Somewhere in the middle of it, Tull came up from below with coal dust on his hands and terror carefully folded away behind his ears, and Sera, God help her, was down at the coal pile being tedious at two dock workers who would remember her for years.

"Your stoker," Brannic said. "How long?"

"Year and change."

"He seems young."

"They all seem young. I seem young. It's the river air."

He almost smiled again, that storage-space smile, and then he didn't, and the not-smiling was worse.

* * *

They were aboard ninety minutes. In the last ten, Brannic did the thing I'd been dreading and pretending not to dread, which was to stand near the boiler. He put his palm flat against the casing the way a doctor palpates a belly, and he looked up at the stack, and he said, conversationally:

"Hot run?"

"Making steam for departure."

"Mm." His hand stayed where it was. "You know what I admire about steamboats, Captain? Every part of them has a purpose. Nothing decorative. No wasted space." He took his hand back. "Which means when something doesn't make sense, it's usually because someone made it stop making sense on purpose."

"Or because it's a hundred and forty years old and held together with prayer and rivets."

"Yes," he said. "That too."

He gathered his men with a tilt of his head, and at the gangway he stopped, and turned, and gave me the courtesy of looking me in the eye, which I have since decided is the most frightening thing he did all morning.

"Thank you for your cooperation, Captain Winterbourne. We'll be seeing each other. This river is smaller than it looks." He paused. "Everything upstream comes downstream eventually. That's not policy. That's just hydrology."

Then he was gone, and the patrol boat backed off the dock with that insolent ease big boats have, and the fog was fully burned away, and the morning was bright and clean and ruined.

* * *

We got underway within the hour, because staying at a dock after a customs inspection is like standing in a doorway after a fight—you're just giving the next punch a target. I took her down to the second bend and then into a slough behind a willow island where the current wouldn't advertise us, and I shut her down, and I went below and started pulling the inspection plate off the flue with my hands shaking so badly I skinned two knuckles and didn't notice until I saw the blood.

Tull helped. Neither of us talked. The chest came up black with soot and hot enough to bake bread on, and I set it on the main deck in the shade of the deckhouse, and Sera knelt beside it and pressed her palm to the lid like she was taking a pulse.

"He knew," she said. "Not about the chest. About *something*. He boarded you like a man checking a trapline."

"He boarded me like a man who checks traplines for a living."

"Juniper." She looked up. Soot had gotten onto her cheekbone somehow, a small dark crescent, and I wanted to wipe it off and wanted even more not to. "He said your name like he'd read it somewhere before this morning."

Everyone says that. My name is on manifests and dock ledgers from the head of navigation to the delta. But I heard it too—the small pause before *Winterbourne*, the way a man sounds out a word he learned from a page rather than a person—and I had filed it under *later*, which is where I file most things.

"So we run," Tull said. He'd gone pale under the coal dust. "South, past the locks, lose ourselves in the delta traffic. Nobody finds a boat that doesn't want finding. You taught me that."

I looked at the chest. Inside it was a nation that no longer existed and a name I'd spent ten years drowning, and the river had gone to the trouble of digging both up and handing them to me personally, which felt less like luck than like a bill coming due.

"Running's the plan," I said. "Running is always the plan."

But even as I said it I was thinking about Brannic's hand on the boiler casing, and the pause before my name, and the way he'd said *we'll be seeing each other* like a man writing an appointment in a book he expected me to keep. I have outrun creditors, deputies, a husband in Point Adair with a strong right arm and a long memory, and one memorable season of the entire Marrow Bend constabulary. I am good at leaving. It is the one inheritance I chose for myself.

What I had never had before was a witness. Someone who watched me evade and took notes. Brannic hadn't found the chest, but he'd found something better, from his point of view: he'd found the shape of the hole where it wasn't. A man like that doesn't need to catch you. He needs only to keep you in sight and wait for the river to do what rivers do, which is deliver everything, eventually, downstream.

Sera stayed on deck that night, keeping watch with the chest under a tarp beside her, and I stood my trick at the wheel with the running lights doused and the moon doing the navigation for me, and I thought about what a man buys when he lies well. He buys time. Time is the only currency the river honors, and I'd just spent a fat purse of it.

Toward midnight, Tull climbed up to the pilothouse and handed me a folded paper that a dockhand at the fueling wharf had given him before we cast off. "Fella said it was business," Tull said. "Wore a good coat. Said you'd know the name on it."

I opened it under the binnacle lamp. The handwriting was expensive, the kind of penmanship that has never once hurried. One line, and beneath it a signature, and the signature was a name I had refused to hear once already this month, across a tavern table, attached to a contract I'd turned down flat.

*The offer remains open, Captain. So does the alternative.*

Lord Aldous Vane.

I read it twice, and then I folded it small and put it in my breast pocket, over the heart, where a man keeps the things that are aiming at him.

"Straighten up ahead," I told Tull, and put the wheel over, and ran on into the dark.
