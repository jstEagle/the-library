## 7. Night Run Through the Narrows

There's a particular sound a river makes when it's lying to you. Most people never hear it. It's a softness in the water, a kind of practiced calm, like a card sharp smoothing his sleeve before the deal. The Vell was making that sound all evening, and I'd been pretending not to listen, because the alternative was admitting what I already knew: we were being followed, and the only way out was through water that had no business being run in daylight, let alone at night with a chest full of treason in my hold.

I'd first spotted the patrol boat an hour past dusk, or rather I'd spotted what patrol boats always show you first—their lights, or the absence of them where lights should be. A coal barge out of Grey Locks would be running three lanterns and a stern light, sloppy and bright. This thing was running dark except for a single green eye at the bow, winking in and out behind the bend at Marrow Point. Coal barges don't run dark. Coal bargemen are proud of their lights the way priests are proud of their bells.

"Juniper." Sera's voice came low from the wheelhouse door. She'd been below with the documents spread across my bunk, memorizing them like scripture, which I suppose they were, of a kind. "The light. I've been watching it for ten minutes. It hasn't moved."

"It's moved. It's just moving the way a knife moves—slow until it doesn't." I eased the Marguerite's throttle back a hair, listening to the engines drop their note, feeling the current take more of the work. "That's Brannic's boat. Or it's Vane's. At night, out here, the distinction is academic."

"What's downriver?"

I looked at her. She was lit by the binnacle lamp, and she had that archivist's expression, the one that said she already knew the answer and was testing whether I'd lie about it.

"The Narrows," I said.

She was quiet a moment. "The chart says—"

"The chart says *impassable, all traffic, by order of the Coalition Board of Rivers.* The chart's been saying that for thirty years, and for thirty years the Board has had exactly two reasons: one, the rock shelves shift every flood season and no survey survives a spring; and two, a man ran it in '41 with a hold full of untaxed brandy and made the Board look like fools. They don't forgive that sort of thing." I turned the wheel a degree, feeling the current lean on the rudder like a drunk leaning on a friend. "The chart is right, by the way. It is impassable. For anyone else."

"And for you?"

"For me it's merely suicidal." I managed half a smile. "There's a difference. It's mostly a difference of pride."

She didn't laugh. She'd stopped laughing somewhere around the moment she'd confessed her family's name was on a list of the erased, and I understood that, but I found I missed it more than I wanted to admit. There was a woman on my boat who looked at a chest of drowned kingdom's papers and saw her dead, and I was starting to look at her and see something I'd sworn off years ago, which was a reason to be somewhere.

The green eye winked again, closer. Maybe half a mile. On the Vell at night, with the current running two knots over flood stage, half a mile is the length of a decision.

"Tull," I called down the deck. "Get the cargo lashed double and everything loose below secured. Then come up here and pray, if you know how. If you don't, improvise."

The boy scrambled to it without questions, which was one of the things I liked about Tull and one of the things that worried me. A deckhand who doesn't ask questions has either great faith or a second employer, and I'd been burned by the second kind before. But that was a problem for a night with fewer rocks in it.

"Sera," I said. "There's a lamp in the chart locker. Red lens. I need you on the searchlight, but you don't sweep it. You hold it on one thing at a time, when I call for it, dead ahead, two seconds, then dark. Can you do that?"

"Two seconds."

"Any longer and they'll read our position off the glow. Any shorter and we'll both die ignorant." I checked the engine gauges by feel more than sight; I'd been running this boat so long I could read her temperature in the tremor of the deck plates. "The Marguerite's drawn three feet six since I had her bottom re-plated in Casterly. The Narrows runs four feet in the channels that exist. That leaves us six inches of grace, and the river gets to decide where the grace goes."

"And the channels that don't exist?"

"Those are the ones we'll be using."

* * *

The Narrows begin where the Vell forgets to be a river and remembers it's a mountain stream with delusions. The valley pinches down to barely twice the boat's beam, walls of black granite rising sheer on both sides, and the water—normally lazy and brown and full of silt—goes suddenly green and fast and articulate, the way water does when it's been squeezed. There are three channels through the worst of it, or there were, the last time anyone honest surveyed them. The flood two seasons back had almost certainly redrawn the map. The river doesn't consult the Board of Rivers. The river consults itself.

I brought us in at half throttle, which felt like walking into a dark room at a dead sprint. The walls came at us. That's the thing about the Narrows that charts can't convey: it isn't the rocks you can see, it's the acoustics. The echo off the granite comes back a half-second late and wrong, and your ears start insisting the channel is wider than your eyes say, and you have to choose, every second, which of your senses is lying. Experience teaches you it's usually the ears.

"Hard to port," I said, mostly to myself, and put the wheel over.

The Marguerite leaned. Somewhere below, the chest shifted against its lashings, and I heard Sera's breath catch—she'd gone down to sit with it, one hand on the wood like it was a sleeping animal that might startle. Maybe it was. That chest had already cost me a tavern's worth of quiet, a stranger's photograph, and the comfortable fiction that I was a man with no history. I was starting to suspect it intended to cost me considerably more.

"Light," I said.

Sera hit the searchlight. Two seconds of red glare: a shelf of rock directly ahead, black and wet and exactly the height of my waterline, and beyond it a gap that might have been a channel or might have been a very expensive opinion.

"Dark."

Darkness. I held the picture in my head, the way you hold a face in a crowd. The gap was real. The gap was maybe a boat's width and a half, and the water going through it was making a sound I did not like—a low, grinding mutter, like an old man reading his own eulogy.

Behind us, faint past the canyon walls, the green eye winked. Still coming. Brannic, or whoever he was, had apparently decided we'd gone mad enough to try the Narrows and sane enough to manage it, and was content to let the river do his killing for him. Smart. That was the thing about Brannic that unsettled me: he never spent more than he had to. He'd wait at the far end. He'd let the Vell soften us up.

"All right, old girl," I murmured to the wheel. "You've lied to customs men and outrun a revenue cutter and floated through two wars without a scratch. Don't develop principles now."

I took us through the gap at a dead crawl, engines at bare steerageway, the current shouldering the hull toward the shelf with the patience of something that has all night and no mercy. I heard it once—the kiss of granite along the starboard plates, a long soft scrape like a fingernail drawn down the world's spine—and I felt it come up through the wheel and into my teeth, and I stood there and did not flinch, because a pilot who flinches at the sound of his own hull dying is a pilot who deserves to die with it.

Then we were through, and the gap opened into a pool, black and deep and momentarily kind, and I let out a breath I felt I'd been holding since Marrow Point.

"Juniper." Sera's voice from below, strange. "There's water in the hold. A little. Coming in forward."

Of course there was. The scrape hadn't been a kiss; it had been a promise. I put Tull on the pump and told him to sing if it got worse, because Tull singing meant everything was fine, and I meant to hold him to that standard all night.

* * *

The middle section of the Narrows is where the river stops pretending to be navigable at all. The channels braid and un-braid, splitting around gravel bars that move with every flood, and the current runs so fast in the chutes that steering becomes a form of negotiation rather than command. You don't pilot through it. You propose terms and hope the river is in a mood to accept.

I'd run it once before, seven years ago, at dawn, with a hold full of machine parts and a hangover, and I'd come out the far end having aged approximately a decade. I'd sworn then, with great sincerity, never again. But that's the thing about oaths made to rivers: the river doesn't countersign them.

The green eye was gone now—swallowed by the canyon walls, or holding back, or circling to some downstream ambush I didn't have the charts to imagine. That should have been a relief. Instead it sat in my chest like a swallowed stone, because a hunter who stops showing you his light is a hunter who's stopped needing to.

"Light," I called, and Sera gave me two seconds of the chute ahead: white water, a standing wave the size of a barn, and past it—maybe—a channel threading between two boulders that the flood had left leaning together like conspirators.

"Dark." I turned the wheel and gave the engines everything.

The Marguerite hit the chute and the world went to noise. Spray came over the bow in sheets, cold as a slap, and the old girl bucked and complained and held—she held, God bless her riveted, patched, mortgaged hull—and we shot the standing wave with the deck awash and came down the far side with a slam that I felt in my spine's foundations. Then the two boulders, the conspirators, and I threaded the needle with six inches of grace on either side and the river's mutter rising around us like a congregation.

And in the middle of it—in the exact middle of the worst water on the Vell—I felt it. Not fear. Fear I knew; fear was an old shipmate. This was the other thing, the thing I'd been running from for ten years and had never once managed to name in daylight.

It was joy.

Pure, filthy, singing joy. My hands on the wheel, the boat alive under me, the river trying to kill us and failing, and some animal part of me roaring with delight. This was what I was *for*. Not ports, not contracts, not the warm gravity of a dockside week with a woman who looked at me like I was a puzzle worth solving. This. Motion. The narrows. The night. The running.

And that was when the exhilaration curdled, right there in my chest, mid-chute, because I understood the arithmetic of it at last. I could run the Narrows blind. I could outrun Brannic, outrun Vane, outrun every patrol boat and customs cutter and debt collector on this whole drowned river. I had been doing it for ten years and I was *good* at it, and that was precisely the problem. Because the chest in my hold wasn't cargo. It was a question, and the question was *whose man are you, Juniper Winterbourne*, and no boat ever built could outrun that. You can put thirty miles of white water between yourself and a patrol boat. You cannot put thirty miles between yourself and your own name. I knew that the way I knew the sound of my hull on rock: not from charts, but from contact.

The channel bent. I bent with it. But something in me had already run aground.

* * *

The last section of the Narrows is the cruelest, because it looks easy. The canyon opens, the water spreads, the current gentles, and a pilot's hands start believing the night is over. That's where the river keeps its last joke: a shelf of rock that runs out from the eastern bank just under the surface, invisible in any light, marked on no chart because the flood that put it there postdated every survey. The locals call it the Widow's Welcome. The locals, I noted, were not currently aboard.

"Light," I said, and got my two seconds, and saw nothing but kind, flat, lying water.

"Dark." And I heard it—the softness in the river, the card sharp's calm—and I knew.

"Hard to starboard. *Hard!*" I put the wheel over and gave her everything the reverse of everything, and the Marguerite leaned like a woman changing her mind, and the Widow's Welcome came out of the dark and took us anyway—glancing, along the port bow, a grinding, splintering, hull-deep crunch that threw Tull off his feet on the foredeck and put the pump song to an end.

We came off the shelf and into deep water and the boat settled wrong. I could feel it in the wheel, a list, a new reluctance in the rudder, the Marguerite limping the way she'd never limped in ten years of my care.

"Report," I called.

"Water's faster," Tull shouted back, which was Tull for *we're taking it badly.* "I'm pumping. Juniper, I'm pumping, but—"

"I know." I eased her down the last mile of the Narrows at a crawl, one hand on the wheel, one hand flat against the deckhouse as if I could hold the boat together by touch, the way you'd hold a bleeding man. Ahead, past the canyon mouth, the Vell spread wide and slow under a sky just beginning to consider dawn. And there, riding at anchor off the far bank with her running lights finally lit and unashamed, sat the patrol boat. Waiting. Patient as a lien.

Sera came up the wheelhouse stairs and stood beside me. She didn't say anything for a while. She just looked at the patrol boat, and then at me, and then at the water seeping gray through the deck seams around our boots.

"You could have gone around," she said quietly. "Waited for daylight. Taken the long way through Hollis Bend."

"Hollis Bend has a customs station."

"You could have surrendered the chest. Given it back to the river. It came out of the water. Water would take it back."

I looked down at the gray seep around my boots, and I thought about how easy that would be—how the whole nightmare would end right there, and I'd be a cargo pilot again, nameless, free, running brandy and machine parts and nothing that had a crown in it. The river would keep its secret. The river keeps everything, eventually.

"I could have," I said.

"But?"

"But it's not mine to throw away." I heard myself say it and was surprised, the way you're surprised by your own voice on a recording. "It's yours. It's your family's. It's a few thousand families'. I'm just the boat it came in on."

Sera looked at me for a long moment, and there was something in her face I didn't have a chart for.

"The pump's holding," she said. "Barely."

"Then we'll make for the shallows past the anchor light, run her aground on purpose somewhere Brannic's draft can't follow, and patch her at first light." I turned the wheel a few degrees, bringing the limping Marguerite around, and behind us the patrol boat's anchor light burned steady and green and patient. "And Sera—if Brannic's waiting for us at the end of the Narrows, he's not working alone anymore. Vane doesn't post one man to a job this size. Which means somewhere downriver, there's a second boat we haven't seen yet."

As if the river had been waiting for me to say it out loud, a light came on—far downstream, past the anchor boat, at the mouth of Hollis Bend. White, this time. Deliberate. Sweeping the water once, slow, the way a man sweeps a porch he owns.

Sera's hand found my sleeve.

"Juniper," she said. "What's down there?"

"Nothing," I said, and throttled up the wounded old girl, and took her into the light.
