## 9. Port of Small Mercies

**Juniper**

The Marguerite went onto the blocks at Port of Small Mercies on a Tuesday, which I only knew because the harbormaster told me, and I only believed him because he had no reason to lie about anything so dull. The name of the place was a joke older than the docks. Somebody had christened it with irony a century back—*small mercies*, as in the port gave you exactly enough to keep breathing and not one breath more—and the town had spent the intervening years living down to it.

She'd taken a log across the stern paddle on the run down from the Narrows. Not a bad wound, as wounds went. The wheel was cracked in three places, the port shaft bearing had gone to gravel, and the hull was weeping along a seam like a widow at a wedding she'd opposed. Old Marek, the shipwright, walked her length twice, sucked air through his teeth with the theatrical precision of a man pricing grief, and said, "Eight days. Maybe nine if the timber's honest."

"Nine," I said, and felt the number land in my chest like an anchor.

I had a rule. I'd had it for ten years, and it had kept me alive and unloved in roughly equal measure: never be anywhere longer than a repair takes, and never let a repair take longer than it must. A boat is a schedule. A schedule is a spine. You stand up straight by always leaning toward the next port.

Nine days. I stood at the rail of the grounded Marguerite and watched Sera Quill walk down the gangplank with a carpetbag and an expression of scholarly curiosity aimed at the town, and I thought: *nine days is plenty of time to ruin a perfectly good arrangement.*

* * *

**Sera**

Port of Small Mercies had three streets, two churches that disagreed about everything except the price of candles, and a dockside chandlery that smelled of tar and oranges in a combination I found inexplicably comforting. I bought paper, ink, a lamp with a cracked globe, and a hat I did not need but which made me feel like a woman on holiday rather than a woman in flight, and which Juniper looked at for exactly four seconds before saying, "That's a good hat," in the tone of a man who has been ambushed by his own sincerity.

We took a room above the chandlery—two rooms, I want that on the record, though the wall between them was more of a suggestion—and fell into the shape of a week the way water falls into a channel: without discussion, without resistance, as though the shape had been waiting for us.

Mornings, we worked. He was up before the gulls, and I learned quickly that Juniper Winterbourne could not be still, so I gave his restlessness a job. I sat on an upturned crate by the slipway with the Ostrava documents spread under oilcloth, transcribing, translating, cross-referencing the registry against the fragments of census I'd memorized in a former life, and he hauled timber, argued with Marek about caulking, and materialized at my elbow every hour or so with a question disguised as a complaint.

"Your registry says forty-one families granted riverine passage rights in the final year," he said on the third morning, squatting beside my crate, sawdust in his hair. "Forty-one. Who grants passage rights to forty-one families? That's not governance, that's a guest list."

"It's exactly a guest list," I said. "The last loyal households. The ones the crown meant to evacuate before the treaty closed the borders. It never happened."

He was quiet a moment. "Forty-one families," he said again, differently, and went back to the timber, and I watched him go and understood that I was watching a man read his own name in a ledger he'd spent a decade pretending not to have.

Afternoons, we stopped working, and that was the dangerous part.

* * *

**Juniper**

Here is what I learned about Sera Quill in a dockside week, catalogued in the manner of a man who catalogs things to avoid feeling them:

She took her tea bitter enough to strip varnish and claimed this was a scholarly discipline. She hummed when she transcribed—three notes, over and over, some fragment of a song she refused to name, and when I asked she said, "My mother's. Don't make it a thing." She was hopeless with dice and magnificent at cards, which told me everything about how she'd survived the years since her family was erased: not by luck, but by watching other people's hands.

She laughed twice a day, minimum, and both times looked faintly betrayed by it.

And she talked about Ostrava the way other people talk about the dead—not in the past tense, never in the past tense. "Ostrava *is* a nation with its borders temporarily misfiled." "Ostravan law *holds* that—" She conjugated a country that no longer existed, and I began to understand that for her the documents in that chest weren't evidence. They were a heartbeat.

On the fourth evening we sat on the dock with our feet over the water, sharing a bottle of plum brandy the harbormaster swore was medicinal, and she said, "Tell me about the name."

"What name?"

"The one you abandoned. You flinch when people use it. Winterbourne. You wear it like a coat that doesn't fit anymore but you can't take it off in public."

"That's very literary of you."

"I'm an archivist. We're trained to notice what's misfiled." She passed the bottle. "You don't have to tell me. I'm professionally familiar with sealed records."

I took a drink and looked at the water, which was doing its evening trick of turning everything on it to hammered copper, and I got as far as the edge of it. I could feel the whole story standing behind me like a passenger waiting to board: my father, the treaty, the signature, the reason a boy of seventeen had walked onto a cargo boat and never walked off. I could feel Sera beside me, warm as a lamp, patient as a ledger.

"Juniper," I said, instead of the story. "My mother's name. Everyone else called me by my father's. I picked the one she left me."

Sera didn't push. She just nodded, slowly, like a woman filing something in the correct drawer, and said, "That's a good name," and the terrible thing, the genuinely dangerous thing, was that when she said it, it sounded true. It sounded like the name fit after all.

I almost told her the rest. I got to the door of it. And then a night heron went over the water with a sound like a ripped sail, and I looked up, and the moment passed the way moments do when a man holds the door for his own cowardice.

"Tomorrow," I said. "I'll tell you tomorrow. Marek says the timber's honest, but he says a lot of things."

"Tomorrow," Sera agreed, and did not believe me, and had the grace not to say so.

* * *

**Sera**

He was going to tell me. I knew it the way I knew a document was forged—by the strain in the hand that produced it. Every evening that week he circled the story the way a boat circles a berth, checking the wind, finding a reason not to come in.

I let him circle. You cannot force a seal; you can only warm the wax.

The truth was that the week was undoing me in ways I hadn't budgeted for. I had planned for Juniper Winterbourne the way I planned for all sources: assess, approach, extract. He was a means of transport with a chest in his hold and a name in its papers. That was the inventory. Somewhere between the second morning—when he came up from the engine room black to the elbows, handed me a wrench without being asked because he'd noticed my transcribing table wobbled, and said nothing about it—and the fifth evening, when I caught him teaching the chandlery's daughter to tie a bowline by wrapping her small hands in his own and telling her *the rabbit comes out of the hole, around the tree, and back down*, the inventory had stopped balancing.

I am not a sentimental woman. I have spent eleven years being a woman who is not sentimental, because sentiment is heavy and I have carried enough. But there is a particular kind of ruin in watching a man be gentle in a language he clearly believes no one speaks, and I was ruined, thoroughly, by Thursday.

On Friday night the town had some excuse for a festival—the feast of a saint who was, per the two churches, either a fisherman or a heretic, depending—and someone strung lanterns along the dock and a fiddler who was too good for the town set up outside the Anchorlight, and Juniper appeared at my elbow in a shirt that had been ironed, which meant he had thought about it, which meant I was in trouble.

"Captain," I said. "You've made an effort."

"Marek ironed it. He said I was shaming the boat."

We walked the lantern-lit dock because neither of us suggested anything else and neither of us suggested leaving. The fiddler's music followed us. The river ran dark and patient under the planks. And somewhere in the middle of the walk, in the middle of a sentence about the relative merits of the two churches' candle prices, his hand found mine, or my hand found his—I will never certify which, and as an archivist that failure will haunt me—and neither of us made it a thing, in honor of the song, and we did not let go.

"Nine days," he said, near the end of the dock, where the lanterns ran out and the dark started.

"Eight, now. Marek's ahead of schedule."

"Right." He looked at the water a long time. "I keep a rule," he said. "Never be anywhere longer than the repair. It's kept me—"

"Alive?" I said. "Or just unloved?"

He turned and looked at me, and the lantern-light did something honest to his face, and he said, "Both. It's kept me both," and I felt the whole week tilt, the way a deck tilts, and I understood that whatever he was about to tell me, he had been carrying it since a night heron interrupted us, and that he was going to give it to me now, here, at the edge of the light.

"Juniper," I said, "you don't have to—"

"My father," he said, "signed the dissolution treaty."

The river kept running. The fiddler kept playing. Somewhere behind us a church bell argued with the other church's bell about the hour.

"He didn't just sign it," Juniper went on, and his voice had gone flat and careful, a man reading from a document he hates. "He *wrote* the surrender clauses. He sat across from Aldous Vane and he traded a kingdom for a promise, and the promise was that the families on the evacuation list would be spared, and then Vane burned the list, and my father came home and told me the river was the only honest thing left in the world, and I believed him, and I have been running down it ever since because every port I stop in, I can hear him explaining himself."

I did not say anything. I did the only correct archival thing, which was to hold still and preserve the record.

"And the worst of it," he said, "the part I can't—" He stopped. Started again. "The chest. The registry he saved. He saved it, Sera. The traitor kept the evidence that could have undone him, and he kept it anyway, and I don't know if that makes it better or a hundred times worse, and I have had ten years to decide and I still don't know, and you—" His hand tightened on mine. "You lost your family to what he did. Whatever your ledger says about me, I wanted you to hear it from me before you read it in the papers."

The lantern behind us guttered. The fiddler changed tunes.

"My ledger," I said finally, "says that a man who signs a bad treaty to save forty-one families, and then spends the rest of his life preserving the proof of what was done to them, is not the man I was hunting." I turned to face him fully. "It says I was hunting a traitor and found a son who's been doing his father's penance without ever once being told what the sin was. It says—"

I stopped, because he was looking at me the way people look at a shoreline after a long crossing, and some things should not be narrated while they're happening. Some things should just be allowed to dock.

We stood at the end of the dark dock for a long time, and I will not transcribe the rest. An archivist is entitled to her sealed records.

* * *

**Juniper**

I told her. Ten years of running, and I told her on a dock in a town called Small Mercies with a fiddler murdering a waltz behind us, and the world did not end. The world did the opposite of ending. The world felt, for about an hour, like a place a man might moor.

I want to be honest about the terror of that, because the terror was the point. I had built my whole life on the arithmetic that stopping costs more than moving. Every port I'd ever loved, I'd loved at departure speed. And here was Sera Quill on the dark end of a dock with her hand in mine, and the arithmetic had quietly failed, and I could hear the new sum working itself out: nine days, eight days, a boat on the blocks, a woman who conjugated a dead country in the present tense, a name that might actually fit.

I almost told her I loved her. I got to the edge of it, the way I'd gotten to the edge of the story all week. But I'd spent my honesty budget for the decade, and besides—there was a chest in a boathouse with a nation's heartbeat in it, an agent named Brannic somewhere behind us with a long memory, and a lord named Vane who had not yet run out of ways to say *mine*. I knew better than to hand anyone a hostage to fortune. I'd seen what Vane did with lists of names.

So instead I said, "The hat really is good," and she laughed—her third betrayal of the week—and we walked back through the lanterns like two people who had forgotten, briefly and forgivably, that they were fugitives.

* * *

**Sera**

The last morning, Marek pronounced the timber honest and the Marguerite sound, and Juniper went down to settle the account, and I stayed at the transcribing table to pack the documents, and that is when I saw it.

I had been through the registry a dozen times—the forty-one families, the evacuation that never came, the counter-seal in the margins. But morning light through the chandlery's cracked window falls at a different angle than lamplight, and watermarks are shy things, and there, ghosted into the paper of the registry's final leaf, faint as breath on glass, was a second map.

Not drawn. *Watermarked*—pressed into the paper at the mill, hidden in the fibers, visible only when the light came from behind. A river. The Vell, unmistakably, but charted past Grey Locks, past the Judgment, past every mark where my memory of the published charts simply stopped—and continuing, up and up, to a symbol at the source. A crown. And beneath the crown, in the old Ostravan cipher I had learned at my father's knee and never once had occasion to use until this moment, a single line of text:

*What was drowned was not lost.*

I sat very still. Outside, I could hear Juniper's voice arguing cheerfully with Marek about an invoice, and the gulls, and the ordinary blessed noise of a town that did not know what was in its harbor.

The registry wasn't just a list of the saved. It was a key. And the lock it fit was waiting at the head of the river, in the one place every chart in the coalition's hands agreed was not there.

I heard Juniper's boots on the stair, and I did not hide the leaf, because I was done with hiding things from him—but I did not have time to decide how to say it, either, because he came through the door with the morning behind him and an expression I had not seen on his face all week, and the expression was wrong. Not the dock, not the lanterns, not the man who had ironed nothing and been ironed anyway.

"Juniper?" I said. "What is it?"

He set a telegram on the table between us, face up, and stood there while I read it.

*YOUR DECKHAND IS OUR GUEST. THE CHEST FOR THE BOY. YOU HAVE UNTIL THE MARGUERITE FLOATS. — B.*
