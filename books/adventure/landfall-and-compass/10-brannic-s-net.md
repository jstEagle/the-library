## 10. Brannic's Net

The trouble with caring about people is that it gives the world a list of doors to knock on.

I'd spent ten years keeping my ledger short. A boat, a boiler, a deckhand I paid too well because he was worth it. Tull was nineteen, all elbows and appetite, with a laugh like a loose valve and a habit of whistling off-key when he thought no one was listening. He called me Captain like it was a joke we were both in on. When I hired him at Casterly Wharf he'd asked what the cargo usually was and I said "whatever fits," which was true of the freight and, I'd assumed, of nothing else.

I should have known better. Everything else had stopped fitting months ago. The chest rode in my hold like a second keel, and Sera Quill rode in my wheelhouse like a second conscience, and somewhere behind us Agent Brannic rode the river like a debt collector who never needed to raise his voice because the interest did the talking for him.

We'd tied up at Marrow Bend, a fuel stop so small it didn't appear on charts so much as apologize for appearing anywhere at all. One dock, one coal shed, one tavern with a cat that owned the establishment and tolerated the clientele. I sent Tull up the bank with the pay chit for coal and a warning to keep his mouth shut about anything heavier than coal.

He was gone forty minutes. That was the first wrong note. Tull could spend an hour arguing over two coppers, but not when the spending was someone else's errand.

When he came back down the ramp, he wasn't alone.

Brannic walked beside him the way weather walks beside a field—unhurried, inevitable, already deciding what it would do to you. Two men in plain gray coats followed, wearing the particular blankness of people paid to be furniture until they're paid to be something else. Tull's hands were cuffed in front of him, which is the polite way, the way you cuff a man you intend to return. His face was doing something complicated: trying to be sorry, trying to be brave, mostly being nineteen.

"Captain Winterbourne." Brannic stopped at the foot of my gangway. He didn't come aboard. That was its own message—he didn't need to. "You're a hard man to find. No, that's not right. You're an easy man to find who insists on making it tedious."

"Agent Brannic." I leaned on the rail. "Still traveling without luggage, I see. Must make packing simple."

"It does." He smiled the way a ledger balances. "Your boy here was overheard at the coal office describing a salvage. A chest, he said. Triple-crown seal, he said. Very proud of it, I think. Loyalty makes men chatty."

Tull opened his mouth. "Captain, I never said—I only told the clerk the *weight*, for the crane ticket—"

"It's all right, Tull." It wasn't, but the sentence cost less than watching him twist. "Go sit on the hatch. Mind the cat."

They let him go up the ramp past them, cuffs and all, and he sat where I pointed, shoulders hunched, staring at his boots like they'd betrayed him too.

"You want the chest," I said.

"I want a conversation," Brannic said. "The chest is a conversational opening. Lord Vane prefers to think of it that way. He's a great one for euphemism. Calls the dissolution of a nation 'administrative realignment.' Calls what happened at Ostrava 'the regrettable necessity.' I've heard him use the phrase 'tidying the archive' three times this year, and each time I found myself wanting to ask him what exactly he was tidying *out*." Something moved behind his eyes then, quick as a fish under ice, and was gone before I could name it. "But that's my problem. Yours is simpler. You have property that doesn't belong to you, and I have your boy."

"He's not my boy."

"No," Brannic agreed pleasantly. "But he thinks he is, and that's worth more. Sentiment is the strongest chain we issue, Captain. Doesn't rust. Doesn't need maintenance. The prisoner maintains it himself."

I looked at Tull on the hatch. He was trying very hard not to look back, which meant he was listening to every word.

"What's the trade?"

"The chest. All of it, unopened by anyone further. In exchange, the boy walks free, no charges, no record, and you get a receipt." He produced a folded paper from his coat and held it up without offering it. "Vane signs personally. You'd be surprised how few things he still signs personally. Most men in his position have learned to sign nothing. It suggests he considers this concluded business. Tidy."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then the boy goes to Grey Locks holding facility pending investigation of smuggling violations, which carry, let me consult my memory—yes. A minimum sentence of four years, and Grey Locks is not gentle to boys who cry." He said it flatly, without relish, which somehow made it worse. Cruelty performed is at least honest about itself. "And then we take the chest anyway, once we've finished demonstrating that your affections have a price and we know how to read the meter."

* * *

Sera was below, in the engine room, pretending to study the registry while actually studying me. She'd learned to read my silences the way I read water—the set of the current before a sandbar shifts, the smell of the air before a storm decides. She came up the ladder before I'd finished climbing down.

"They took Tull," she said. Not a question. She'd been watching from the galley porthole.

"Brannic wants the chest for the boy."

"And you're going to pay."

"I'm going to negotiate."

"Juniper." She used my name rarely enough that it landed like a dropped tool. "That chest is the only proof my family existed. If Brannic takes it, it goes into a furnace in some ministry basement, and forty years from now there will be no record that the Quills ever drew breath, and I'll have helped deliver it myself because I liked the way a deckhand whistled."

"I'm not giving him the chest."

"Then what?"

"That's what I'm working out." I sat on the engine housing, and she sat beside me, close enough that our shoulders touched, which was becoming a habit neither of us remarked on. Below us the boiler ticked as it cooled, patient as a clock. "Brannic said something odd. About tidying the archive. He used the word *archive*, Sera. Not documents. Not papers. Archive."

She went still. Sera going still was louder than most people shouting. "The source," she whispered. "The flooded vault. He knows it exists."

"He knows Vane wants it gone. There's a difference between knowing a thing and understanding it, and I don't think Brannic has understood yet what he's carrying water for." I turned it over in my head while I talked, the way you turn a coin to check both faces. "Think about it. If Vane just wanted the claim dead, he'd burn the chest and be done. Paper burns cheap. But he's spent gunboats and agents chasing us up a river instead of simply waiting for us to drown in the Judgment Rapids, which any competent engineer could arrange. Why? Because the chest isn't the danger. The chest is the *map*. And somewhere upstream there's a room full of paper that says who gave the order, and whose signature sits under it, and Vane needs that room to stay drowned forever."

"So we give him the chest and he still hunts us."

"We give him the chest and he stops needing us alive." I rubbed my eyes. Ten years of running had taught me every trick of staying uncaught, and none of them worked once someone decided you knew too much. Evasion was a currency, and I was approaching the point where I'd spent so much of it that I'd priced myself out of ordinary mercy. "There's a third option."

"There always is, with you."

"I buy the boy."

"With what? Your savings are—" She stopped, recalculated, and her expression did something complicated. "You'd give him everything. The refit money. The winter money. The money that keeps the Marguerite floating."

"The Marguerite floated fine for years on luck and stubbornness. She can manage a little longer."

"Juniper, that's not—"

"It's arithmetic, Sera." I stood. My knees objected; they'd gotten philosophical about stairs lately. "A chest can be copied. I've got a man upstairs who can copy anything, including, I suspect, the handwriting on a treaty. But a boy doesn't come in copies. There's one Tull. The world issued him once and discontinued the line." I paused. "Besides, he whistles off-key. You can't replace that. Believe me, I've tried humming along."

* * *

Negotiating with Brannic was like playing cards with a man who kept the whole deck in his sleeve and wanted you to admire the tailoring.

We met on the dock, in the open, because he preferred witnesses and I preferred daylight. The gray coats waited by the coal shed. Tull stayed on the hatch under the eye of the third one, who had developed a sudden fascination with our rigging.

"My counteroffer," I said. "You release the boy now. In exchange, I pay you the sum of eight hundred crowns—my savings, every copper, in banknotes you can verify at the Marrow Bend exchange—and I give you a certified copy of the chest's contents. Every page. Sealed by a notary here in town, witnessed, dated. You'll have everything Vane needs to know what the documents say, and the originals stay with me."

Brannic considered this for a long moment. "Why would Vane accept copies?"

"Because copies are how he works. He's spent forty years making sure the original record of Ostrava reads the way he wants it to read. Another set of papers saying what he wants them to say—that's not a threat to him, that's a donation." I held his gaze. "And because you're going to recommend it to him. Because you've read those documents, or your people have, and somewhere in that careful bureaucratic skull of yours you've noticed that the chest mentions a second cache. At the source. And you've noticed that Lord Vane's instructions to you conspicuously fail to mention destroying it—which means either he doesn't trust you, or he trusts you to figure it out yourself, and neither option tastes good."

The fish-under-ice look again. Longer this time.

"You presume a great deal, Captain."

"I pilot a river for a living. Presumption is the job description. Every bend is a presumption."

"And if Lord Vane refuses your terms?"

"Then you arrest the boy, and I run the narrows tonight with a lightened purse and a full head of steam, and you explain to your lord why his tidy operation now involves a fugitive with nothing left to lose and a deckhand with a grudge and four years to memorize faces. Men in Grey Locks write letters, Agent. Long ones. To newspapers."

Something almost like respect crossed his face, quickly suppressed, the way a man suppresses a cough in church. "Eight hundred crowns is a great deal of money for a deckhand."

"He's not a deckhand." I heard myself say it before I'd decided to. "He's crew. There's a difference. Deckhands get replaced. Crew get ransomed."

Brannic studied me for a moment the way Sera studied documents—looking for the forgery, finding instead something inconveniently authentic. "You realize," he said quietly, "that you're teaching me something about you that my reports will reflect. A captain who pays ransom establishes a market. Every agent on this river will know your price now."

"My price was always high. Now it's just public."

He almost smiled. Then he unfolded his paper, made a mark, and handed me a pencil stub like a surgeon handing over the knife. "Amended terms. Eight hundred crowns, verified today. The boy released into your custody, charges suspended—not dismissed, Captain, suspended; Vane keeps the drawer open. And in exchange for the generosity of these terms, you'll answer one question honestly, and I'll know if you don't."

"Ask."

"The registry in your chest. The royal registry your father preserved." He watched me carefully as he said it, weighing each word like ballast. "Does it indicate where the source archive lies?"

I thought about lying. I was, by profession and temperament, extremely good at lying; it was practically my native language, and I spoke it with a captain's calm that had fooled customs men on three rivers. But Brannic had just watched me pay eight hundred crowns for a whistling boy. He had calibrated me. A lie now would ring false against the true note he'd just heard, and he'd know it, and worse—he'd know I thought he wouldn't.

"The registry describes the route in fragments," I said. "Landmarks, distances, a riddle about a waterfall. Enough to find it if you're determined and lucky. Not enough to find it if you're merely thorough."

"Which are you?"

"Determined," I said. "Luck and I have an arrangement—we don't discuss each other in public."

He wrote something in a small book. "Then we understand each other. The boy comes down when the money clears. Captain—one piece of professional advice, free of charge, which should tell you how seriously I mean it." He closed the book. "Whatever you find at the source, don't bring it back down this river. Vane's patience ends where the last charted mile begins, and so, I suspect, will yours."

* * *

The money cleared at dusk. I watched the clerk count it twice, and each note that left my hand felt less like currency than like cartilage—part of the structure of my life, pulled out and laid on the counter. Eight hundred crowns was the new paddlewheel bearings. It was the hull survey. It was the winter coal, the mooring fees, the margin that let a man sleep through a storm because he'd already paid for the repair he might need. I'd built that pile coin by coin across ten years of contracts taken and refused, and I emptied it in eleven minutes for a boy who whistled off-key.

Worth it. That was the strange part. Itemize it however you liked—every entry came back marked *paid in full*.

Tull came down the ramp at dark, rubbing his wrists, walking carefully like a man testing whether the ground still belonged to him. He stopped in front of me and couldn't seem to find any words above the size of pebbles.

"Captain, I—"

"You talked to a coal clerk about cargo weight. That's not a crime, it's Tuesday." I put a hand on his shoulder, and he flinched and then didn't. "But from here to wherever we're going, you don't describe the hold to anyone. Not the weight, not the shape, not whether it smells of cedar. As far as you know, we haul grain and disappointment."

"Yes, sir." His voice cracked on *sir*. Nineteen years old and he'd aged a decade in an afternoon. "Captain—they said if you didn't pay, they'd—"

"I know what they said."

"They came for *me* because of what you're carrying. That's not fair to you."

"Fair." I looked out at the river, black and patient, already rehearsing tomorrow's moods. "Tull, the river isn't fair. It's just consistent. It takes payment from whoever's standing closest. Right now that's us." I squeezed his shoulder once and let go. "Get below. Eat something. We cast off at first light."

"For where?" Sera asked from the wheelhouse door. She'd heard everything; she always heard everything. "Grey Locks is downstream. The source is upstream. And we just told Brannic we're going."

"We told Brannic the truth, which is the best lie there is—nobody checks it." I climbed to the wheel and put my hand on it, and the wood felt different tonight, or I did. "The fragments in the registry mention a landmark above the Judgment Rapids. A weir, an old climb, a gorge. Which means the next hundred miles of river are going to be the hardest piloting of my life, done broke, hunted, with a lightened purse and a full heart."

"That's not a plan, Juniper. That's a eulogy with a heading."

"It's a heading," I agreed. "We'll improvise the rest. It's what the river's for."

Below us, Tull started whistling—off-key, defiant, alive. Sera laughed despite herself, and the sound did something to the air in the wheelhouse that eight hundred crowns couldn't have bought.

Above the northern hills, past the coal smoke and the lamplight of Marrow Bend, the clouds had begun to stack. Black, heavy, organized. The monsoon was coming early this year, and it was coming upriver to meet us.
