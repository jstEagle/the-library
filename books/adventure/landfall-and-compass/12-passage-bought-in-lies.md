## 12. Passage Bought in Lies

Grey Locks announced itself an hour before we saw it, the way a dentist announces himself with a drill you can hear through the wall. The river narrowed to a third of its width between cliffs the color of wet slate, and up ahead, strung across the gap like a spider that had gotten ambitions, sat the checkpoint: two gun platforms, a chain boom across the water, and a customs house built of stone so gray it looked like the cliffs had tried to grow a building and given up halfway.

"Cozy," Tull said from the deck, spitting over the rail with the enthusiasm of a boy who'd recently been arrested and ransomed back and had decided the world owed him a certain theatrical bitterness.

"Charming," I said. "You'll note the architecture. That's coalition standard issue. They build ugly on purpose. Pretty things suggest you might want to stay, and staying is the one thing the treaty forbids."

Sera came up from the cabin carrying a leather folio against her chest like a breviary, which in a sense it was. Inside it were our sins, neatly inked. She'd worked on the papers for two days and nights, by lamplight, with a forger's patience I found frankly alarming in an archivist. I'd asked her once, around hour nineteen, where a girl who catalogued drowned kingdoms learned to fake a coalition shipping seal, and she'd looked at me with an expression I can only describe as *kindly disappointed* and said, "Juniper. I'm from Ostrava. We learn to copy things. It's the national art form. It's why they erased us — we kept copies."

I had decided not to pursue the point. There are conversations you have and conversations you survive, and at Grey Locks I needed us all in the second category.

"Run it again," I said.

She opened the folio. "You are Master Corven Ashe, contractor for Vane Riverworks, Upper Reaches Division. You are hauling survey equipment and bonded coal samples to the Vane station above the Locks. Your cargo manifest is genuine — I altered the genuine one, which is the trick, always start with truth and bend it, never build a lie from nothing, lies built from nothing smell like paint." She tapped a page. "Your charter is the *Marguerite*, registered out of Casterly Wharf, which is true. Your crew list is true except for me. I'm your clerk. Quill is a common enough name. Nobody checks a clerk."

"Nobody checks a clerk," I repeated. It had the ring of a famous last sentence.

"And the chest?"

"The chest," she said, "is a survey instrument case. Vane Riverworks ships instrument cases by the dozen. Brannic's people have seen a hundred of them. They'll be looking for a smuggler's chest, not a bureaucrat's crate. The best hiding place in the world is a filing system."

I looked at the chest, which sat on the deck wearing a fresh coat of drab gray paint and a stenciled inventory number, and I will confess it looked bored. It looked like a thing that had never contained a kingdom in its life. Sera had stenciled it herself, freehand, and the lettering was so perfect it made my teeth ache.

"You're wasted on archives," I said.

"I'm *used* on archives," she said. "There's a difference. Archives are where you hide things so well that even you forget them. That's not waste, that's the job." She closed the folio. "Juniper. The man you're about to be — Corven Ashe — he has to be someone. Not a costume. Brannic already smelled you once. If he's at that checkpoint, he'll be watching how you stand, how you answer, whether you flinch at Vane's name. You can't act him. You have to *be* him for twenty minutes."

"And who is Corven Ashe, exactly?"

She considered. "He's a man who took Vane's money and sleeps fine. He's a man who thinks the treaty was a mercy, because it ended a war and gave him work. He's a little bored. He's a little tired of the river. He thinks you, Juniper Winterbourne, are a nobody with a boat."

"That last part will be easy," I said. "I've been saying it for ten years."

"Yes," she said quietly. "I know. That's what worries me."

* * *

The boom came into full view as we rounded the last bend, and I brought the *Marguerite* down to a crawl, wheel easy under my hands, an honest working boat making an honest working approach. There's a speed that reads as guilt and a speed that reads as boredom, and the difference is about a quarter knot. I found the boredom.

The customs house had a loading dock and a chain hoist and a man on the dock with a ledger and a rifle slung with the loose familiarity of someone who'd never once had to use it and resented the weight. Above, on the gun platforms, two more figures, one of whom was studying us through a long glass. I didn't look at the long glass. Looking at a long glass is how you get invited into its opinions.

"Charter and papers," the dock man said.

I handed down the folio. "Corven Ashe, Vane Riverworks. Survey cargo and bonded samples for the station above the Locks. My clerk has the duplicate manifest if your man wants to count crates."

The dock man — Hallow, by his badge, a name that suggested either piety or gallows humor — flipped through the papers with the speed of a man who had long ago stopped actually reading them. That was the plan working. Sera's genius wasn't the forgery; it was the tedium. She'd built us a paper trail so dull that boredom itself became our alibi.

"Vane Riverworks," Hallow said. "You people are up and down this river like fleas on a hound."

"We're thorough," I said, and let a little of Corven Ashe's tiredness into it. "Lord Vane believes in knowing every inch of his investments."

"Lord Vane believes in a lot of things," Hallow said, in the tone of a man who had opinions he was too underpaid to voice, and stamped the charter.

And that was nearly the whole of it. That was how close it came to being nothing — a stamp, a wave, the boom cranking up out of the water on its greased chains, the *Marguerite* sliding through into the upper river with a kingdom in a gray crate on her deck.

Then the long glass came down, and the man holding it started down the platform stairs, and it was Brannic.

* * *

I have a theory about fear, which is that it's just memory arriving too early. Brannic coming down those stairs, I remembered the *Marguerite*'s boiler flue and the lie I'd told him about coal samples, and my body tried to have that whole conversation again in advance, and I had to stand very still on my own deck and let the moment walk past me like a stranger I owed money to.

"Captain." Brannic stopped at the gangway. He had the kind of face that made you feel audited. "Ashe, is it."

"Corven Ashe." I didn't offer a hand. Corven Ashe wouldn't. Corven Ashe had been standing in river damp since dawn and wanted his tea. "You have me at a disadvantage, Inspector."

"Brannic. Coalition Internal." He looked at the papers in Hallow's hand without taking them. "Vane Riverworks. I wasn't aware the Upper Reaches Division was running survey work this far down."

"Neither was I until the orders came," I said. "But I'm a contractor, Inspector, not a strategist. Lord Vane's left hand doesn't explain itself to the right."

Something moved behind his eyes — not suspicion exactly. Recognition of a certain *flavor*. I've spent ten years learning what my face does under questioning, and I know the exact moment a man decides whether I'm lying, which is usually the moment he decides what he wants to believe. Brannic was deciding nothing. That was the problem with Brannic. He was a man who kept decisions open like wounds.

"Your cargo," he said. "Survey instruments."

"And bonded coal samples. Manifest's stamped. Your man Hallow's already been through it."

"Hallow counts crates," Brannic said. "I count other things." He stepped onto the gangway, and every muscle in Tull's back, three feet from me, went to piano wire. "I boarded a boat three weeks ago. Cargo boat, Casterly Wharf registry. A captain with river hands and no river history. He had a chest I didn't get to open." He looked at me. "You have his build."

"Inspector," I said, and here is where ten years of being nobody paid its dividend, because Corven Ashe had to be bored and tired and faintly insulted, and I only had to reach about a half inch down to find all three. "I've been hauling for Vane's people since before you had a badge. If I look like every other captain on this river, that's because this river makes us in one mold and pours us out by the dozen. If you want to open that crate, open it. It's stenciled, it's manifested, and every hour it sits on my dock is an hour Lord Vane's survey crew sits idle, and I'll be writing *that* in my report, with your name in it, spelled correctly, because I'm told he reads."

Silence. The river went on being a river underneath us, patient as arithmetic. On the gun platform, the long glass man had lowered his instrument and was watching with the frank enjoyment of someone at a play.

Brannic smiled. It was not a warming event. "You're very good, Captain Ashe."

"I'm adequately paid," I said.

He stepped back off the gangway. "Hallow. The boom." Then, to me, almost pleasantly: "Safe passage, Captain. The upper river's treacherous this time of year. So many things get lost in it. Chests. Names. Men who used to be other men." He touched his hat brim. "I do hope you find what you're looking for."

"I already know where everything is," I said. "It's on the manifest."

He laughed — a short, dry sound, like a file on iron — and walked back up the stairs, and I stood at the wheel with my heart going like a paddlewheel in flood and my face doing absolutely nothing, because that was the job, that had always been the job, and for the first time in ten years the job felt less like a talent and more like a symptom.

The boom rose. The chains dripped. We went through.

* * *

I didn't speak until Grey Locks was a smudge of gray behind us and the river had widened enough to hold a conversation without the cliffs eavesdropping. Then I tied off the wheel, walked to the rail, and was quietly, thoroughly sick into the Vell. There's a dignity in doing it before anyone asks.

Sera appeared at my elbow with a tin cup of something hot and terrible. She didn't say anything about the vomiting, which is one of perhaps four hundred reasons I was in love with her and had told her none of them.

"He knew," I said, when my voice was fit for company.

"He suspected. There's a difference, and you just spent twenty minutes making it matter." She handed me the cup. "You were good. You were better than good. You gave him a reason not to look, and the reason was paperwork and Vane's own name. He'll chew on it for days. Was it the same captain? Wasn't it? He can't prove either thing, and Brannic is a man who needs proof the rest of us need air."

"He'll write it down," I said. "He'll have my description. Corven Ashe, Vane contractor, such a height, such hands, insolent in the approved manner. And the next checkpoint, or the one after, some clerk will match it to the *Marguerite*'s real registry and the whole costume burns."

"Yes," Sera said. "It burns. That's what I've been trying to tell you since Casterly Wharf. Every identity is a candle. You don't get to keep the candle and light it too. We had three — the *Marguerite* honest, the *Marguerite* contracted, and the papers I haven't shown you. We've spent the second one. We have one left, and after that we're just a boat with a chest on it and our faces, and our faces are known."

I drank the terrible tea and watched the water. Somewhere behind us, Brannic was writing me into existence as a fiction, and somewhere ahead of us was a flooded archive at the top of the world, and between the two of them was a river full of checkpoints, and we had one candle left.

"You know what's funny," I said. "My father was famous for this. The lying. The papers, the seals, the second ledger under the first. He could walk into any room in the coalition and be whoever the room needed, and everyone said it was genius, and I swore when I was fifteen I would never, ever be fluent in that language." I turned the cup in my hands. "And now I've just done it better than he ever taught me, because he never got the chance, because I ran."

Sera was quiet a while. The river made its opinions known in the usual way, which is to say all of them at once.

"Juniper," she said finally. "There's a difference between lying the way he lied and lying the way you just lied. He lied to make a kingdom disappear. You lied to carry one back." She took the empty cup out of my hands, and her fingers stayed on mine a moment longer than the transaction required. "The fluency isn't the sin. It never was. It's what you say in the language."

* * *

We made the upper reaches by dusk, and that was when Tull, up at the bow on watch, started shouting in a voice that cracked in the middle like a boy's, because he was still mostly a boy.

"Smoke. Smoke on the water — no. On the *bank*." He pointed upstream, where the river bent around a bluff, and against the last red of the sky there was a column of smoke rising, black and deliberate, the kind of smoke that isn't an accident but a message. And below it, riding at anchor in the middle of the channel with her running lights lit in a pattern I knew from three weeks ago and had hoped to never see again, lay a patrol boat.

Not Brannic's. Bigger. Newer. Flying no colors at all, which on the Vell meant one thing only: money so high up it didn't need a flag.

At her stern, in letters of gold leaf that caught the last of the light, a name I'd read on a refused contract in a fog-bound bend a lifetime ago.

*Vane's Reach.*

Sera's hand found my sleeve. "Juniper. That's not a checkpoint."

"No," I said, and put my hands on the wheel, because my hands didn't know what else to do, and because whatever came next, the *Marguerite* and I had only one candle left and no more river to run it down. "That's an invitation."
