## 13. Rapids Called the Judgment

I've been a river man for eleven years, and in that time I've learned three things worth passing on. One: never trust a bend you can't see around. Two: never trust a captain who won't tell you what he's afraid of. Three: never, under any circumstances, trust a stretch of water that other pilots have bothered to name.

The Judgment Rapids had a name. Had a whole liturgy of names, if you counted the ones carved into the rocks at the head of them by men who'd run them and lived — and the ones scratched shallow by men who hadn't.

Tull, deckhand, second-class, of the Marguerite. That's me. I keep the log when Juniper's busy at the wheel, I keep the coal when he's not, and I keep my opinions mostly to myself, which is why I'm writing this one down instead of saying it out loud, because out loud it would've been mutiny, and as it turned out we got there anyway, just by a longer road.

* * *

It started with the fog lifting off Grey Locks behind us like a curtain coming down instead of up. We'd bought our passage through that checkpoint with lies — good lies, Sera-made lies, papers so clean they squeaked — but a lie is a candle, not a lamp. It burns down. And when it burns down, what's left is whatever you actually are, standing in the dark holding an empty holder.

Juniper stood at the wheel that morning looking like exactly that. He'd been quiet since Grey Locks, quieter than his usual quiet, which was already a professional-grade silence, the kind most men need years of practice to achieve. But this was different. This was the quiet of a man who'd discovered he was fluent in something he didn't want to speak anymore.

"Judgment's two miles," he said, to nobody, to everybody. "We could put in at Miller's Bend and wait out the season."

Sera looked up from her documents. "The season's six weeks."

"I know how long the season is."

"You're thinking about running the Judgment in monsoon water." She said it flat, the way you'd say *you're thinking about putting your hand in a fire.*

"I'm thinking about a lot of things."

I want it noted, for the record, that I said nothing. I coiled rope. Coiling rope is what you do when the grown-ups are deciding whether to kill you all. I had gotten quite good at it over the past weeks, because Captain Winterbourne had developed a talent for standing in places where decisions about life and death were being made, and someone had to coil rope through all of it or the boat would've looked untidy even as she sank.

But here's the thing I couldn't say then and can say now: I wanted him to run it. Not because I'm brave. Because I'd watched him thread the Narrows at night with no lights and a hull full of borrowed nerve, and I'd seen what his face did afterward — that hungry, horrified look of a man who has just found out he's better than he wanted to be. A man like that doesn't stop. He just picks slower ways of not stopping until somebody makes him choose.

Miller's Bend would be a slower way. The Judgment would be a fast one.

He chose the fast one. Of course he did. Men who spend ten years outrunning their own name will eventually run straight into the one thing named after judgment, because the river knows a joke when it sees one.

* * *

The Judgment Rapids are called that because the Vell narrows to a third of its width between two walls of granite, drops forty feet in half a mile, and then — this is the part the old pilots loved — splits around a midstream shelf of rock shaped, according to everyone who ever described it, exactly like a judge's bench. You go left or right of the Bench. Left is longer, shallower, kinder. Right is shorter, deeper, and eats paddlewheels the way I eat hardtack: without malice, without pause, and completely.

In summer water, with a fresh crew and a captain who slept nights, you took the left channel and you paid the toll in gray hairs.

In monsoon water, with a patched boiler, a skeleton crew, and a cargo hold containing a chest that half the coalition's guns were pointed at, you didn't take either channel. You went home. That was the correct answer. That was the answer every pilot for forty years had given, and the reason the rocks at the head of the rapids had room for more carvings.

Juniper took us to the head of them and stopped the engine, and the Marguerite sat in the current like a held breath.

"Here's where we stand," he said.

And I'll tell you what struck me, because it struck me like a plank across the back: he didn't do the voice. Every captain I'd served under had a voice for moments like this — the gravel-and-glory voice, the one that made dying sound like a pension plan. Juniper didn't use it. He just talked, the way he talked at cards, the way he talked when he was reading water, plain and level, like the truth was just another current and he was going to read it right or die trying.

"The left channel's carrying maybe four feet over the shelf lip. We draw five and a half. If we go left, we grind the hull open on the Bench's shoulder and we do it slow enough to watch." He looked at each of us. Me, and Ovid at the firebox, and Marta on the stern line — yes, Marta, who'd signed on at Port of Small Mercies for wages and stayed on for reasons she claimed involved the pay, which nobody believed, least of all her. "Right channel's deep enough. Right channel's also where the recirculating eddy sits under the drop, and it turns a wheel backward. If we lose steerage in the eddy, we broach, and a broached boat in monsoon water comes apart in about ninety seconds."

"Ninety seconds," Ovid repeated, in the tone of a man doing arithmetic on his own funeral.

"Maybe less. Maybe more. Water's a liar." Juniper wiped his hands on his trousers. "I can't promise you through. I've run worse water, but I've run it in boats that weren't tired and crews that weren't three people and a stowaway archivist. What I can promise is that I'll take the wheel myself, and if she goes, I go with her, and I won't ask any of you to stay aboard past the moment you want off. There's a landing above the rapids on the far bank. Anyone who wants to walk around and meet us below — if there's a below — can walk."

Silence. River noise. Somewhere upstream a bird making an idiot of itself, the way birds do when men are deciding things.

That was the speech. No glory in it. No pension plan. Just a man standing in front of his crew telling them precisely how they might die and offering them the door.

You want to know what mutiny looks like? I'll tell you what it looked like on the Marguerite that morning. It looked like Ovid spitting into the firebox and saying, "If you're steering, I'm firing. Somebody's got to make sure the boat's honest even if the captain isn't." It looked like Marta checking the stern line twice and then saying, "Walk? In this country? I'd rather drown dry." It looked like me opening my mouth to object on principle — because somebody on this boat has to object on principle, it's basically my job description — and finding that the objection had gone out of me somewhere between *ninety seconds* and *I go with her.*

Because here's what I understood, standing there with a coil of rope in my hands like always: every captain before him had lied to us. *Easy water, boys. Quick run, boys.* They'd lied because lying kept the crew cheap and the cargo moving and the captain comfortable. And we'd known they were lying, and we'd worked for them anyway, and we'd hated them politely, the way you hate weather.

This one had told the truth, ugly and itemized, and offered us the door, and in doing so he'd done the one thing no captain had ever done for me.

He'd made staying my decision.

* * *

I'm going to describe the run now, and I want you to understand that everything I'm about to write happened in under four minutes, and that four minutes is plenty long enough for a whole life if you pack it right.

He took us in on the right channel. Of course he did. The left was death by inches and the right was death by chance, and a man who'd spent ten years outrunning chance was finally, fatally, willing to gamble with it — because chance, unlike the coalition, plays fair.

The Marguerite hit the first drop and the whole world went white. Not metaphor-white. Actual white, spray blowing up off the ledges in sheets, so thick I couldn't see the bow from the stern, couldn't see Marta, couldn't see anything but the wheelhouse windows glowing like lanterns with Juniper's silhouette bent inside them, both hands on the wheel, leaning into the current like a man arguing with God and holding his ground.

Ovid fed her coal like a man possessed, shovel ringing, and the boiler screamed, and I was on the bow line because that was my station, and my job was to do nothing unless told otherwise, and doing nothing while the river tried to eat us was the hardest work I've ever done.

The Bench came up on the left like a courthouse rising out of floodwater. I heard Juniper ring the engine — one bell, hard astern — and the Marguerite checked, shuddered, the current grabbing her stern and slewing us sideways, and for one long, glittering second we were broadside to the drop, showing the river our whole flank like a dare.

Then the wheel answered. I don't know how. I've thought about it since, and I don't know. The rudder should have been useless in that water; the physics of it don't work; ask any pilot. But the wheel answered, and the bow came around, and we went down the right side of the drop in a cataract of noise, and the paddlewheel — poor old wheel, older than me, older than Juniper's sins — bit into the recirculating eddy under the fall and turned backward.

I felt it through the deck. A wrongness, a reversal, the heartbeat of the ship stuttering like a startled bird. And then a crack — not a big crack, not a dramatic crack, the kind of crack you'd miss at a party — and the starboard paddle buckets began to come away, one, two, a handful, torn loose by the eddy's grip, spinning off downstream like a hand losing its fingers.

We cleared the tailrace on momentum alone. Juniper brought her against the far bank where the water slackened, and dropped the hook, and the Marguerite swung into the current and hung there, listing slightly to starboard, breathing hard the way boats do when the engines stop.

Four minutes. Nobody dead. Wheel half-eaten.

I looked back upstream at the Judgment, and the Judgment looked back at me, and neither of us said anything, because what was there to say. The river keeps its opinions carved in rock, and it had just added a few lines to ours.

* * *

Stranded is a strong word. Stranded implies you expected rescue. We were marooned, which is stranded plus pride.

The nearest settlement was Corben's Landing, eleven miles downstream by water, three days by foot through country that ate surveyors. Our engine was fine. Our boiler was fine. Our paddlewheel looked like something a giant had used to stir soup and then lost interest in. Without it we were a very expensive houseboat with excellent taste in hiding spots.

Juniper sent me up the mast with the glass, which is how I spotted the smoke.

Not chimney smoke. Funnel smoke. Two funnels, moving upstream against the current, which meant steam power and money, and in this stretch of the Vell, steam power and money meant exactly one thing these days: Vane's patrol boats, working their way toward the source gorge, sweeping the river like a man sweeping a floor he intends to burn.

I came down the mast faster than I'd gone up, which the mast probably appreciated.

"Patrol," I said. "Two of them. Upstream bound. They'll pass this bank within the hour."

And that — that exact word, *patrol*, spoken aloud on a crippled boat with a chest of sedition in her hold — was the match that lit the mutiny.

Marta said we should scuttle the chest and surrender the papers and claim salvage. Ovid said we should scuttle the boat and walk. Sera said nothing, but she put her body between the cabin door and the rest of us in a way that said the chest would go over her drowned body first, and I believed her, because I'd spent enough weeks watching her to know that Sera Quill's family had already been erased once and she had decided, with the particular calm of the genuinely dangerous, that it wasn't going to happen twice.

And Juniper stood there and let us argue. Let it get loud. Let Ovid call him a man who gets crews killed for secrets, let Marta say the word *mutiny* right out loud, in the open air, which on a boat is roughly equivalent to lighting a fuse in the powder store.

Then he held up one hand, and here's the thing — they stopped. I stopped. Not because of the hand. Because of the face above it.

"I'm not going to order you," he said. "Any of you. Here's what I know. Those boats are Brannic's, and Brannic isn't hunting the chest anymore — he's herding us toward the source, because Vane wants the archive destroyed and wants us to lead him to it. If we surrender, we're witnesses, and witnesses to what we've seen don't stay witnesses long. If we run overland, we die in the hills, and the chest dies with us, and Sera's family stays erased, and every name in that registry stays erased, forever, and Lord Aldous Vane gets to be a statue in a square instead of a signature on a massacre." He paused. "If we fix the wheel, we've got maybe a day's head start on them to the last navigable mile, and after that it's boots and rope and climbing, and none of it is safe, and I can't promise any of you through. Again."

"You keep saying that," Marta said. "Can't promise. Can't promise."

"Because it's true. I could promise you easy water and a quick run, and you'd believe me, because captains have been saying it for a hundred years and crews have been drowning for a hundred years believing it." He looked at her, and then at all of us. "I ran the Judgment because stopping cost more than going. That's been my whole life, Marta. Moving. I told myself it was the debts, then the name, then the coalition, and every year the reasons changed and the moving stayed. Well. I'm done pretending the running is wisdom. From here to the source, I'm not asking you to follow a captain. I'm asking you to decide whether what's in that chest is worth what it costs to carry it. Decide that, and then decide whether you want to carry it with me. Either answer, I'll see you paid and put ashore safe as I can manage. Both answers, I'll respect."

Quiet again. River noise. That idiot bird again, still at it.

And I stood there with my rope — I think I may have been born holding a coil of rope; there are worse fates — and I did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding since Grey Locks. Eleven years on the river. Eleven years of captains who lied beautifully and paid promptly and forgot my name by the next port. Eleven years of being crew, which means being cargo that talks.

This fool at the wheel had just told us the truth with the patrol boats in sight, and offered to release us from our own courage, and stood there waiting to be refused.

"Well," I said, because somebody had to break it, and breaking silences is the other half of my job description. "Somebody's got to hold the light while you patch the wheel. Might as well be me. I complain the least."

"You complain constantly," Marta said.

"In writing," I agreed. "Which doesn't count."

Ovid spat into the water, which for Ovid is a sacrament and a signature. Marta swore in three languages and started coiling line, which meant yes in every one of them. Sera looked at Juniper for a long moment — a look I pretended not to see, because some cargo is private — and then went into the cabin to guard her papers.

Juniper looked at me. "Tull. Log entry."

"Aye, Captain." I opened the book. "What am I writing?"

He thought about it while the patrol smoke grew fat on the horizon behind us.

"*Crew informed of all risks,*" he said. "*Crew remained.*"

I wrote it down. Then, underneath, small, where only a future reader with sharp eyes would find it, I added: *First captain I ever served who earned the word.*

* * *

We worked through the afternoon with the patrol smoke hanging on the horizon like a threat written in cursive. Juniper stripped the ruined buckets himself, waist-deep in the cold eddy-water off the stern, hauling timber and iron up onto the deck while Ovid fed the forge and Marta held lines and I held the light, as promised, and complained, as promised, in writing.

By dusk we had a wheel that would turn. By dark we had a wheel that would turn *and bear load*, which is a different animal entirely, and by full dark Juniper called us together and laid out the chart, and that's when I saw it — the place where the ink of the river simply stopped, above the last navigable mile, and beyond it a scatter of fragments, notes in an old hand, and one word repeated in the margins like a prayer or a warning:

*Source. Source. Source.*

"We run tonight," Juniper said quietly, "while their funnels are cold. Above the last mile, we go overland, and the Marguerite waits for us here, and God willing she'll forgive us for leaving her."

"And if the patrols catch us before the last mile?" Marta asked.

Juniper looked at the chart, and then at the dark river, and I swear the man almost smiled.

"Then they'll wish they'd caught us after it."

I wrote that down too. Some entries you keep for the tribunal.
