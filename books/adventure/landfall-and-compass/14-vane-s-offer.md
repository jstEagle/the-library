## 14. Vane's Offer

The Marguerite smelled of tar and river and the particular staleness of a crew that hadn't slept properly in four days, and I was standing at the wheel thinking, for the first time in ten years, about staying. Not staying in a port—staying in a *life*. Staying with the claim, with the registry, with the woman asleep in the chartroom with ink still on her fingers from copying a dead king's ledger. It was a dangerous thought. Thoughts like that get people killed on the Vell. I'd seen it happen to better men than me, usually at exactly the moment they started believing the river owed them something.

The river owed nobody anything. That was the first scripture of piloting, and I'd built a whole religion on it.

Which is why I noticed the smoke before I noticed the boats.

Three columns of it, hanging over the willow bend two miles downstream, too straight and too black to be a landing fire. Coal smoke. Steamers don't burn wood, and nobody burns coal on the upper Vell except the coalition patrol fleet, because coal is expensive and empires are not.

"Tull," I said. "Wake everyone. Quietly."

He was up the ladder before I finished the sentence, which is the thing about Tull—he'd betrayed us at the weir, we'd forgiven him at the weir, and now he moved like a man trying to earn back every second of it. I hadn't decided yet whether that made him the most trustworthy member of my crew or the most exhausting one. Probably both.

Sera came out of the chartroom with her hair half-pinned and her eyes already awake, the way she always woke, like sleep was a document she'd only skimmed. "Smoke?"

"Coal. Three boats, maybe four."

"Vane."

"Vane," I agreed. "Or Brannic. Lately the difference is mostly a matter of which one shoots first."

We came around the willow bend with the current doing half our steering and the other half being a lie I told the wheel, and there they were: three gunboats in a staggered line across the channel, grey hulls, the coalition's crossed-anchor ensign hanging limp in the dead morning air, and a fourth vessel behind them that was not a gunboat at all. She was a paddle-steamer, white above the waterline, brass bright enough to shame a chapel, with an awning aft and a flag at the masthead that I didn't recognize because it wasn't a flag so much as a coat of arms stitched large enough to read from a mile off.

A triple crown. Three crowns, stacked, above a broken key.

Ostrava's seal—worn by the one man alive who'd had a hand in burying it.

"Well," Sera said quietly, beside me. "He's wearing your inheritance."

"He's wearing the corpse," I said. "That's different."

* * *

Lord Aldous Vane came aboard under a white ensign of truce, which on the Vell is a courtesy older than the coalition and honored by everyone, because the river is small and pilots have long memories and even the empire's men know there are places a gunboat can't follow a grudge.

He was smaller than I'd imagined. That's the thing about architects of catastrophe—you build them large in your head, all shadow and brass, and then the gangway delivers up this neat, silver-haired gentleman in a grey coat with kind creases around his eyes, and you have to remind yourself that the creases come from smiling at funerals.

"Captain Winterbourne." He pronounced the name carefully, the way you'd set down something fragile on someone else's table. "I've wanted to make your acquaintance for some time."

"You could have written. Most people do."

"I did write. Through intermediaries. You declined three contracts." He smiled. "I admire a man who knows the price of things."

"The price of things is mostly what I *don't* know about them."

We stood on the afterdeck. My crew had been ordered below, all except Tull at the wheel and Sera, whom Vane acknowledged with a small bow that managed to be both courteous and a threat, the way a cat acknowledging another cat is courteous and a threat. Brannic stood at the rail of the nearest gunboat with his arms folded, watching me with the expression of a man doing arithmetic he already knows the answer to.

"Captain," Vane said, "may I speak plainly?"

"I'd be disappointed otherwise. Plainly is the one language I'm fluent in."

"Forty years ago, a nation called Ostrava ceased to exist. This was necessary. I won't insult you by pretending otherwise, and I won't insult you by pretending it was painless. It was a surgery. The patient was the peace of nine provinces, and the tumor had a crown on it." He folded his hands. "The treaty that dissolved it has held for forty years. Nine provinces, Captain. No war. Do you know how rare that is? Do you know what it cost to buy?"

"I'm starting to get an invoice."

That earned me a real smile, brief and almost human. "Your father was the treaty's quiet hand. He signed the dissolution and he kept the registry, and I have spent forty years being grateful to him and forty years being afraid of him, which is, I find, the standard condition of knowing an honest man." He turned and looked downriver, toward the source, toward the gorge. "The registry names an heir. You. And the registry points, as I'm sure your charming archivist has discovered, to a second cache. The archive at the source."

"You know about that."

"Captain, I have known about that since before you were born. I built the lock at Grey Locks specifically so that no one could reach the source without my knowing. I have spent four decades making sure that one mile of river stayed boring." He looked back at me, and the kindness in his face didn't so much fade as get filed away, like a document returned to a drawer. "And now a cargo pilot with debts in six ports is carrying the deed to a dead kingdom up my river in a boiler flue."

"It was in the flue. We've moved it. You're behind."

Sera made a small sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. Even Vane's mouth twitched. I'll say this for the man: he enjoyed the work.

"Here is my offer," he said. "And I want you to understand that I have made perhaps four offers like this in my life, and three of them were accepted, and the men who accepted them died old and rich and thanked me at the end."

"Riveting salesmanship."

"The claim is renounced. Publicly, before a tribunal, with my counsel present so there's no ambiguity. In exchange: full legitimacy. Papers, citizenship, a pardon for every mile of river you've ever run dirty—and I know about the salt run in '39, Captain, so let's not pretend the pardon is decorative. A berth in the coastal trade, a ship of your own, new, with your name on her. And—" he paused, and here it came, and I felt it before he said it, the way you feel a sandbar before you see it, a wrongness in the water, "—the archivist's family name is restored. Full restoration. Her parents' names back in the civil rolls. Her brother's death record corrected from 'rebel' to 'civilian.' Everything her family lost, returned, with interest."

The deck was very steady under my feet. That was the problem with decks; they're always steady exactly when you need them not to be.

"And the archive?" I said.

"Stays drowned." He said it gently. "The river keeps its secrets, Captain. That's the bargain rivers have always offered. The massacre order, the names, the proof of what was done and who ordered it—all of it sleeps under the falls, and the peace sleeps on top of it. You sail away rich. She sails away with her family's name. And in forty years, some other fool finds some other chest, and it won't matter, because the archive will be silt and the claim will be ash and the nine provinces will still not be at war."

I looked at Sera.

I want to be honest here, because honesty is the one luxury I've never been able to afford until recently and I find I've developed a taste for it. I looked at her and I did not think about the claim. I did not think about thrones or tribunals or my father's signature or the nine provinces or the peace of anyone. I thought: her brother's record says *rebel*. I thought: she has spent her whole life being a daughter of a name that officially never existed, copying other people's histories because her own had been confiscated. I thought: I could give that back to her. This neat little man with the kind creases is standing on my deck offering me the one thing in the world she wants, gift-wrapped, no diving required, no flooded vault, no monsoon, no drowned archive, no body of mine floating down the Vell for her to identify.

And God help me, I wanted to say yes. I stood there wanting it the way you want air. Say yes, Juniper. Say yes and take her hand and take the new ship with the new name and let the dead keep their own dead. Nobody would even blame you. The peace of nine provinces, he said. What's one more drowned truth against that?

That's the thing about Vane's offer that I only understood later: it wasn't a bribe. Bribes are cheap. It was a mirror. He'd found the exact shape of what I wanted and held it up so I could watch myself reach for it.

"Captain," Vane said softly, "you're hesitating. That's not an accusation. Hesitation is the mark of a man who understands the question."

"I'm hesitating because you've made me an offer that's actually good," I said. "That's rare. Most offers are garbage with good manners. Yours is real. That's what makes it filthy."

Sera hadn't spoken. She was watching me with an expression I couldn't read, and I realized with a lurch that she couldn't read mine either, and that we had spent a week learning each other's faces in lamplight and had apparently learned nothing that would survive a man with a coat of arms.

"The archive," I said. "It has names in it. People who were massacred, and the man who ordered it, signed. You want that to stay drowned."

"I want the war to stay unfought."

"It's not a war. It's a *record*. You're asking me to drown a record."

"I'm asking you to drown a match," Vane said, and for the first time the creases around his eyes looked less like smiles and more like strain, and I understood that this neat, courteous man had spent forty years carrying the same weight I'd been carrying for a month, except his had forty years of habit on it. "Captain, I have read the archive's table of contents. I helped write part of it. If it surfaces, there will be tribunals, and the tribunals will be political, and the politics will fracture along exactly the seams the treaty sealed, and within five years there will be men with flags marching in three provinces, and the dead of Ostrava will have company. I have done that arithmetic every day for forty years. It always comes out the same. Some truths are ballast. You cut them loose or everyone drowns."

"Or," I said, "you're the tumor."

The deck went very quiet. Tull stopped pretending to coil a rope. Down on the water, Brannic shifted his weight against the rail.

Vane regarded me for a long moment. "Your father said something like that to me once. Nearly the same words. He signed the treaty anyway." He straightened his coat. "You have until we clear the bend at Hart's Eddy. That's four hours. If the answer is yes, burn a green lamp and my boats will escort you to Casterly Wharf with every courtesy. If the answer is no—" and here the courtesy finally dropped away entirely, and what was underneath was not rage but something worse, something tired and administrative, "—then the archive stays drowned anyway, Captain, because I will put the Marguerite on the bottom of the Vell, and you and the archivist and the boy and the chest, and I will report a boiler explosion, and I will attend the funerals, and I will smile, because that is what I do. I have drowned better ships than yours for less."

"Four hours," I said.

"Four hours." He bowed—to me, then to Sera, that same courteous threat—and went down the gangway, and the white steamer fell back, and the gunboats held their line across the channel like a hand across a doorway.

* * *

We didn't talk for a while. That's the thing nobody tells you about love on a river: there's an enormous amount of standing next to each other not talking, because the talking has to be load-bearing or it isn't worth the breath.

Sera broke first, which surprised me. She stood at the rail watching the gunboats, and she said, "He'd actually do it. Restore my family's name. He has the power; you could see it. It costs him nothing."

"It costs him a witness."

"No. It costs him nothing, because if I take his offer, I stop being a witness. That's the design." She turned. Her eyes were dry and furious and clear. "He didn't offer me safety, Juniper. He offered to buy me. And the terrible thing is I'd sell. If you said yes, I would hate myself for the rest of my life and I would still say yes, and he knows that, and that's why he made the offer to *you* instead of me. Because he knew I'd never get the chance to refuse."

"I know."

"Do you?" She stepped closer. "Because I watched your face up there. I watched you want it. Don't apologize for it—wanting it is the honest part. But I need to know which way you're going to break, and I need to know before Hart's Eddy, because if you say yes, I will not fight you, and if you say no, I need to start copying documents into waterproof oilskin in the next ten minutes."

I looked at her, and I thought about my father, who had signed the treaty and kept the registry, who had spent his whole life being both the traitor and the archivist, both the man who drowned the kingdom and the man who left a map to raise it. I'd spent ten years running from his name. I'd spent a month learning it was two names, and that he'd never gotten to choose between them because he'd chosen both and died carrying them.

I could choose. That was the whole difference between me and my father, the only inheritance I actually wanted: I could choose.

"Say the offer again," I said. "The part about the ship."

"Juniper—"

"The part about the pardon. The salt run. Say it."

"Why?"

"Because I want to hear it out loud one more time before I set it on fire. You don't get to burn things you haven't looked at."

So she said it—legitimacy, the pardon, the ship with my name on her, her family restored, the archive drowned—and her voice cracked on *restored*, and I watched her say the words she wanted more than anything in the world and hand them to me knowing what I'd do with them, and that, I think, was the moment I stopped being a man who runs. Not at the vault. Not at the source. There, at the rail of my own rotten boat, watching a woman give away the thing she wanted most so that I'd have room to give away mine.

"Green lamp," I said, "would get you your brother's name back."

"I know."

"And a tribunal might not. A tribunal is politics, and Vane's right, politics is a flood, and floods don't care whose names they take." I turned to Tull at the wheel. "Tull. Get the oilskin from under my bunk. And the lamp."

He hesitated. "Which lamp, Captain?"

I looked at the gunboats. I looked at the white steamer, brass-bright, wearing a dead nation's crown like a brooch on a hangman's coat. I looked at Sera, who was watching me with her breath held, and I found, to my considerable irritation, that I was smiling.

"The green one's for saying yes," I said. "Light the red."

* * *

The red lamp went up the mast at half past two, and by three the gunboats had their answer, and their answer was steam.

They didn't fire. Vane was too careful for that—a boiler explosion needs a boiler—but they came on in line abreast, closing the channel, herding us toward the north bank where the current runs backward off the bluff and no pilot in his right mind would take a loaded boat. It was textbook. It was, I realized with a cold drop in my stomach, *Brannic's* textbook—I'd watched him board us once with customs papers and a memory like a ledger, and this was the same maneuver, scaled up.

"He's driving us to the bank," Sera said, oilskin-wrapped documents under her arm, hair whipping in the wheelhouse wind.

"He's driving us to the bank," I agreed, and put the wheel over, hard, toward the south channel, where the charts said *impassable, shifting shoals, no survey since the dissolution*, and which the charts said that, I now understood, because Vane had written the charts.

"Juniper." Sera's voice had gone very level. "What's south?"

"Grey Locks," I said. "And below Grey Locks, the old survey channel. The one he built the lock to close." I felt the Marguerite answer the wheel, felt the old girl lean into the current like a horse that has finally been pointed at the fence it's been staring at all day. "He told us himself. He built the lock so no one could reach the source without him knowing. Which means the lock is the only road he thinks exists."

"And the other road?"

I grinned at her, and it wasn't a nice grin. It was my father's grin, probably. "There's always another road. That's the one thing I know that he doesn't: I've spent ten years learning every illegal mile of this river, and he's spent forty years learning the legal ones."

Behind us, a gunboat's whistle blew—one long blast, the river's oldest word, meaning *heave to or be sunk*.

"Tell Tull," I said, "to stand by the boilers. And tell him if anyone asks, we're not running."

Sera, already moving, paused at the door. "Aren't we?"

"No," I said, and rang for full ahead, and the Marguerite gathered herself and ran. "Running's what I did. This is something else."

The gunboats came about behind us, whistles screaming, and ahead the south channel opened up—grey water, brown water, water with no survey and no mercy and, God willing, no Vane—and I put the wheel down and drove us into it.
