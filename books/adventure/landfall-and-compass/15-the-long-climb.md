## 15. The Long Climb

The Marguerite could go no farther, and she told us so in the only language a riverboat speaks: the sound of her hull kissing bottom with the gentle, terminal patience of a woman who has decided to stop arguing.

Juniper killed the engine, and the silence that followed was the loudest thing I'd heard since Grey Locks.

"Last navigable mile," he said, as if announcing a change in the weather rather than the death of everything I understood about how we were supposed to travel. "From here, the Vell gets ideas above its station. Waterfalls. Gorges. The kind of water that eats paddlewheels for breakfast and asks for seconds."

"So we walk."

"So we walk." He said it lightly, the way he said most unbearable things, and began coiling rope with the brisk cheer of a man who had already grieved this particular loss somewhere private and didn't intend to share the funeral with witnesses.

I stood at the rail and looked upriver, and what I saw made my stomach perform a small, dignified revolution. The gorge ahead rose out of the valley like a wound in the earth—sheer walls of grey stone, and between them the Vell running white and furious, no longer the broad brown mother-river I'd known from maps and archives but something young and violent, still furious about being born in the mountains. Above it all, stacked on the southern horizon like cargo nobody wanted to claim, the monsoon clouds had begun to build. Black, blue-black, bruise-colored, taller than anything clouds had a right to be.

"They're early," Tull said beside me. He'd been quiet since the Judgment Rapids, quieter than his usual quiet, which was already considerable. "The rains. My grandmother used to say you could set a calendar by them. This year they're coming like they're late for something."

"Maybe they are," I said. "Everyone else seems to be."

* * *

We spent the morning making the boat safe, because Juniper Winterbourne would not abandon a vessel any more than he'd abandon a person, though he was considerably better at the second now than he'd been two months ago. He lashed the wheel, drained the boiler, covered the engine with tarpaulin weighted against wind, and anchored her to three trees with line thick enough to moor a cathedral.

"She'll hold," he said, patting the rail the way you'd pat a horse's neck.

"And if she doesn't?"

"Then she'll hold anyway, out of spite. She's got plenty."

There was a version of me—a version from Casterly Wharf, from the archive basement where I'd spent eleven years learning to love dead people through their paperwork—who would have found this whole enterprise insane. That Sera Quill had opinions about proper procedure. She catalogued things. She believed documents belonged in climate-controlled rooms with locks on the doors, not hauled over mountains in a chest that had already survived one flood, one customs inspection, one mutiny, and one attempted theft by a boy who was currently carrying half our provisions and refusing to meet anyone's eyes.

But that Sera had also believed her family's name was simply gone—erased cleanly, bureaucratically, the way a bad clerk strikes an error from a ledger. She hadn't yet stood in a cliff cave and read carvings proving the Quills had served the crown of Ostrava for six generations, hadn't traced the letters spelling out her great-grandmother's title while rain hammered the stone outside, hadn't learned that the erasure wasn't history at all but murder with a signature on it. Lord Aldous Vane's signature.

That Sera had burned down along with my old assumptions. What was left was someone who could look at a mountain gorge full of floodwater and think: *fine. Whatever it takes.*

"You're smiling," Juniper observed, hauling the chest onto a travois frame he'd built from a broken spar and two oars. "People don't usually smile at that sky."

"I'm thinking about Vane reading the tribunal summons someday."

"That's a good reason to smile." He cinched the last knot. "Hold onto it. You'll need it around mile four."

Mile four. He said it like a man quoting scripture he'd memorized under duress. Which, I suppose, he had—he'd grown up on this river, before the name Winterbourne became something he fled rather than wore. He knew these hills the way I knew card catalogs: intimately, obsessively, and with a private grief attached to every shelf.

* * *

The first mile was almost pleasant, which should have warned us.

The trail—if trail is the word for a goat's opinion of a path—climbed along the gorge's eastern rim through pine forest gone dark and dripping with the season's earlier rains. The chest rode the travois between Juniper and Tull, who took turns at the drag-ropes without being asked, which was its own kind of apology. I carried the maps wrapped in oilcloth against my chest, inside my coat, against my skin, because after everything we'd lost and nearly lost, I trusted no container more complicated than my own ribs.

Juniper walked point, reading the ground the way he read water. He'd stop sometimes for no visible reason, crouch, touch the dirt, and then wave us left or right around some hazard invisible to the rest of us—a washout under the moss, a spring that turned clay to grease, a stretch of slope that "moves when it rains," delivered with the flat certainty of a man describing his own kitchen.

"How do you know all this?" I asked him once, catching up as the trail switchbacked above a drop that went down and down into white noise.

"My father brought me up here. Before." He said *before* the way other people said a dead relative's name. "He wanted me to know the source country. Said a man ought to understand where his river comes from." A dry note entered his voice. "He never mentioned it came with paperwork."

"He was preserving a kingdom's memory. You can hardly blame him for the filing system."

"I don't blame him for the filing system, Sera. I blame him for signing the treaty in the first place, and then for hiding the evidence instead of using it, and then—" He stopped himself. We'd had this argument in gentler forms four times since the cave. "It doesn't matter. It matters that we get there first."

"It matters both," I said. "That's the whole point of what we're doing. It has to matter both ways, or Vane wins even if we win."

He glanced at me sideways, and there was something in his face—not agreement exactly, but the place where agreement starts, the thaw before the ice breaks. "You always talk like an archivist."

"I am an archivist."

"No. You talk like one. You *are* something else now." He faced forward again and kept climbing, leaving me with that, which was either a compliment or a diagnosis.

* * *

By midday the clouds had eaten the sun entirely, and the light went strange and greenish, the way it does underwater. The air thickened until breathing felt like drinking. My shoulders had discovered new categories of pain, and my boots—good boots, expensive boots, bought back when my biggest worry was grant funding—had revealed themselves to be fair-weather friends.

Tull, meanwhile, carried sixty pounds of provisions like they were a mild inconvenience.

"You're staring," he said, without looking at me.

"I'm wondering what you're made of."

"Mostly guilt." He shifted the pack. "It weighs less than it looks."

I let that sit for a few steps. Then: "Juniper saved your life at the weir. You know he knows what Brannic offered you. And he hasn't said a word about it since."

"I know."

"Most men would have put you off the boat."

"He's not most men." Tull's jaw worked. "That's the problem with him. Makes it hard to hate yourself properly when he keeps being decent at you." He lengthened his stride, conversation concluded, and I understood that this was as much confession as I was going to get, and that it was, in its flinty adolescent way, sincere.

Above us, thunder cleared its throat.

* * *

The second day, the river stopped being scenery and started being adversary.

The trail descended toward a bend where the gorge narrowed to perhaps forty feet across, and the Vell—our Vell, the patient brown road that had carried the Marguerite and every hope we had for a thousand miles—went through it like a fist through paper. The noise was total. It wasn't a sound; it was a condition, like weather or gravity. Spray hung in the air twenty feet above the waterline, and the rocks at the trail's edge were slick black glass.

We roped together. Juniper led, testing each foothold, and I watched him become something I'd only seen in fragments before: not the evasive, charming runner I'd boarded with at Casterly Wharf, but a man doing the one thing in the world he was unambiguously great at. He moved over wet stone the way I move through an index—fast, precise, absolutely certain of where everything was.

Halfway across the exposed ledge, Tull's foot went out from under him.

I felt it through the rope before I heard it—the sudden load, the jerk—and then I was leaning back with all my weight and Juniper had dropped to a knee and braced the line around a granite horn, and for three seconds that lasted approximately a geological age, the whole expedition hung on wet rock and arithmetic. Tull swung, caught the ledge with one hand, scrambled, and was suddenly up and flat against the stone, breathing like a bellows.

Nobody said anything. There was nothing to say that the river wouldn't have drowned out anyway. Juniper just held up two fingers—*two minutes, breathe*—and we breathed, and then we went on, because the alternative to going on was standing on a wet ledge above a roaring gorge while the sky got darker, which was not actually an alternative at all.

That night we camped in a hollow below a leaning slab of rock, and I lay next to Juniper under one blanket, too exhausted for anything but honesty.

"I keep thinking," I said, "that the river carried us all this way. Every mile. Every checkpoint. It was the whole plan—stay on the water, stay moving, stay ahead of them. And now it's the thing between us and the end of it."

"Rivers do that," he said. "They're generous right up until they're not. Same as most things." His arm tightened around my shoulders. "My father used to say the Vell forgives nothing but remembers everything. I thought it was just something old rivermen said." He was quiet a moment. "Now I think he meant it about himself, too."

"The registry. The counter-seal. He remembered everything, and it nearly got us killed."

"It also got us here." He turned his head, and in the last grey light I could see he was smiling, tiredly, crookedly. "For a man who spent ten years running from his father, I've become remarkably good at finishing his work."

"Maybe that's what running was," I said. "Just taking the long way to the same destination."

He laughed—short, surprised, real. "You always talk like an archivist."

"And you always dodge like a Winterbourne."

The first drops hit the tarp then. Fat, deliberate, spaced like artillery ranging targets. Neither of us said anything. We didn't need to. We both did the same math: the monsoon had arrived, the gorge was filling, and whatever window the flooded archive was going to give us, it was closing at the speed of falling water.

* * *

Day three, the climb became a siege.

Rain in the high country isn't weather; it's a policy. It fell with intent, with structure, with the patience of something that had all the time in the world and knew we didn't. The trail dissolved into a stream, then into a series of small waterfalls going the wrong direction. The travois wheels sank to their hubs in mud that sucked at them like it had a grudge. Twice we unloaded the chest entirely and carried it by hand, four corners, four people, stepping in rhythm because rhythm was the only thing keeping anyone upright.

And through all of it, the chest itself. Forty pounds of iron-banded oak that had started this whole catastrophe floating in flood debris, and that now weighed roughly as much as my regrets. Juniper took the heaviest corner without discussion. So did I. Tull and the deckhand—Wick, who had stopped complaining sometime around the Judgment Rapids and entered a state of grim enlightenment—took the others.

At the worst pitch, a slope of red clay steep enough to require hands, the chest slipped. Just a foot, just a lurch, Wick losing his grip with a yelp—and the whole weight of Ostrava's last secret slid toward the drop, and I threw myself across it the way you'd throw yourself across a child, cheek against wet wood, arms hooked under the bands, boots scrabbling for purchase that wasn't there.

Juniper's hand closed on my collar. Tull got the other band. For a moment the four of us were one organism with too many opinions, and then we were a pile at the top of the slope, and the chest was safe, and I was laughing—I couldn't help it—laughing into the rain with my hair in my mouth and my heart trying to exit through my ribs.

"You all right?" Juniper shouted over the water.

"I'm hugging a box!"

"You're hugging a kingdom!"

"Same thing!" I shouted back, and he grinned, soaked and mud-streaked and thirty years older than the man I'd met at Casterly Wharf, and for one ridiculous moment, halfway up a drowning mountain, I was happy. Actually happy. Not the careful, rationed happiness I'd permitted myself for eleven years among the dead files, but the loud, inconvenient kind that arrives exactly where you swore you'd never stand still.

Then the thunder cracked directly overhead, close enough to feel in my teeth, and the sky opened its trapdoor, and happiness went back in its box with everything else.

* * *

We sheltered that night beneath an overhang that Juniper swore by and I swore at, and I sat with the maps by candle stub, checking our position against the fragments for the hundredth time. The registry's clues were maddening—a surveyor's mark here, a bearing there, distances given in an obsolete unit that required me to do arithmetic with numb fingers. But the pieces agreed with each other, and they agreed with the terrain, and by midnight I was certain of something I'd been afraid to be certain of:

The source gorge was less than a day away. And behind its waterfall, swollen now past any charted volume, the vault door would be either findable or suicide, depending on the hour we arrived.

I woke Juniper to tell him. He listened with his eyes closed, which I'd learned was how he listened best, and then he opened them and said, "Then we leave before dawn. Beat the worst of the rise."

"And if the waterfall's impassable?"

"Then we wait for a gap. Rivers always show you a gap eventually. You just have to be willing to stand there looking stupid long enough." He sat up, and his voice dropped into the register he saved for true things. "Sera. Whatever's behind that water—your family's names, my father's seal, Vane's signature—it ends tomorrow. One way or another, the running's done."

I looked at him—at this man who had built a whole life out of never arriving anywhere, sitting in the mud a day's march from the end of every road he'd ever taken—and I felt the exhaustion and the resolve settle into the same bone, the way they must in everyone who ever finished anything hard.

"Yes," I said. "It's done."

Above the overhang, the rain intensified, hammering the stone like a fist on a door, and far up the gorge—so faint I might have imagined it, except that Juniper heard it too and went very still—we heard the sound of the waterfall changing its voice.

Getting louder.

Getting ready for us.
