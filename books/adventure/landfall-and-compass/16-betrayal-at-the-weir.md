## 16. Betrayal at the Weir

I've been robbed before. Twice by strangers, once by a business partner, once — memorably — by a woman in Casterly Wharf who took my heart and my spare anchor chain in the same week. So you'd think I'd have learned to count heads at night. You'd think a man who reads water like scripture would notice when one of his congregation goes missing.

But Tull had been with us since the Judgment Rapids, since I stood on a torn deck and told him the truth about the danger instead of a comfortable lie, and he'd stayed. Staying, I thought, was the same as belonging. That's the mistake. Staying is just staying. Belonging has to be built, and I'd been too busy hauling a chest over mountains to lay a single stone of it.

I woke before dawn in the lee of the gorge wall, Sera's breathing slow beside me, the fire down to a sullen orange eye, and I knew before I opened my eyes all the way. Some part of a riverman's brain never sleeps. It keeps tally the way a purser keeps tally — hull, cargo, crew. And the tally came up short.

The chest was gone. The maps with it.

I didn't wake Sera. I'm not proud of that either. There was a half-second — a long half-second, the kind you can live a whole year inside — where I considered it. Let the boy run. Let Brannic have his paper and his prize, take Sera, take the Marguerite's boat-skiff, and be gone upriver before the monsoon ate the trail. A clean loss. Cheaper than a chase.

Then I remembered my father, who was very good at clean losses, at cutting cable and calling it seamanship, and I got up.

* * *

The trail was easy, which told me everything. Tull wasn't a riverman. He was a dock kid from Grey Locks who'd learned boats the way you learn a foreign language from a phrasebook — well enough to order dinner, not well enough to argue about the weather. He'd dragged the chest, and the chest was oak and iron and weighed as much as a sin you can't stop confessing, and it left a furrow in the wet loam wide enough to steer by.

I followed it up through the birch scrub, the sky going the color of wet slate, the air so thick with coming rain that my shirt stuck to my back before I'd climbed a hundred yards. The monsoon had been stacking itself on the horizon for two days like a debt collector who enjoys the waiting. Now it had stopped waiting. The first drops came down fat and warm and spaced far apart, each one landing with a sound like a knuckle on a door.

The trail bent toward the river — the upper Vell, narrow here, mean-tempered, running fast over shelves of black stone. And then I heard the weir.

Every river has a voice it uses when it's angry, and a weir is where that voice goes hoarse. The old Ostravan engineers had thrown a stone spine across the gorge a century back to pool water for their mills, and the mills were gone and the spine remained, water sheeting over it in a broken white curtain forty feet wide, falling into a churning trough below where the river gathered itself back together and remembered its grudges.

Tull was on the crest of it, hauling the chest along the slick capstones, the maps — my father's registry, the route to the source, everything — wrapped in oilcloth and strapped across his back like a schoolboy's satchel.

"Tull!"

He didn't stop. Of course he didn't stop. I'd have done the same at his age, and had, more or less, which is probably why my legs were already moving before the rest of me voted on it.

The rain found its rhythm then, the spaced knuckle-knocks becoming a drumroll, the drumroll becoming a roar, and the world went gray and loud and sliding. The capstones were filmed with a century of algae and forty years of neglect, and I learned very quickly that running on them was less like running and more like a series of controlled falls that I was not entirely controlling.

"Tull, wait — the stones—"

He turned to look back at me. That was the whole of it. That was the entire tragedy, if you want it in one sentence: he turned to look back at a man he thought was going to hurt him, on wet stone, with forty feet of falling river under his boots.

His heel skated. His arms windmilled. The chest went one way — sliding, grinding, then tipping over the lip of the weir with a boom I felt through my boots — and Tull went the other, off the edge, into the trough.

* * *

People will tell you that in a crisis time slows down. Time does no such thing. Time speeds up, cruelly, and the only mercy is that your body has done its thinking years in advance. I've pulled four people out of rivers in my life and I have never once remembered deciding to. My coat was off before his splash finished, my boots were off after — no, my boots stayed on, that's the thing I remember, the stupid detail, I went into that trough with my boots on because there was no time, and the river took my breath the way it always does, like a tax collected on entry.

The trough below a weir is not water. It's a washing machine full of stone. The falling sheet drives a boil at the surface that holds you under and won't let you up, and below that is a recirculating current that will keep you, cycling you down and back and up and down again until your lungs file for divorce. Tull was in the middle of it, and he could not swim. I could see that instantly — the way he fought the water, every limb at war with every other limb, the way drowning men do, spending their strength on the water instead of the air.

I came up on his blind side, got a fistful of his collar — the oilcloth strap, God bless it, the maps gave me something to hold — and did the thing you do, the only thing: got behind him, locked my arm across his chest, and turned us both downstream, feet up, riding the boil out the way you ride a horse that means to kill you.

He fought me. Of course he fought me. He was drowning and I was another thing drowning him. He drove an elbow into my ribs hard enough to move a tooth, and I took it, because the alternative was letting go, and letting go was arithmetic I didn't care to do.

"Stop — fighting — me," I got out, in the half-second windows when we surfaced, and on the third window he heard me, or his body heard me, or the river knocked the panic out of him, and he went limp the way they have to, and then we were through, spat out the tail of the boil into the fast green water below, and I swam us for the eddy behind the millstone foundation with my boots full of river and my ribs full of elbow and my heart going like a paddlewheel with a bent shaft.

We came out on the stone apron below the weir and lay there gasping like landed fish, the two of us, rain hammering down, and the chest was gone — swept over in the fall, I'd seen it go, a dark shape tumbling in the white — and for one long moment neither of us said anything at all.

Then Tull rolled over and was sick, river water and terror, and after that he started to cry, and it was the crying that made me angry, because it sounded so young.

* * *

"You can't even swim." I was standing over him, and I knew how it looked, and I did it anyway, because anger was easier than the other thing. "You took the chest. You stole the maps. You ran across wet stone above a weir, at night, in the teeth of a monsoon, and you cannot *swim*."

"I could walk around," he said, wretched, hunched, dripping. "The weir narrows at the—"

"It doesn't. I've run this river eleven years. There's no walking around. You'd have known that if you'd ever asked me a single honest question about the water." I crouched, got his chin up, made him look at me. "Who paid you?"

He held out for about four seconds. Then the whole thing came out of him in a flood to match the sky — Brannic, the arrest that had never happened because I'd bought the boy's freedom with my savings, the second approach, quiet, at the wood camp two nights back. Brannic hadn't threatened him. That was the worst part, Tull said. Brannic had *explained*.

"He said Vane's got people in every port from here to the delta. He said the claim dies anyway, the tribunal's bought, the archive's a story old men tell. He said—" Tull's voice cracked. "He said you'd run. That's what you do. He said everyone knows it. Juniper Winterbourne, the man who's never stood still long enough to be caught. He said when you ran, the chest would be sitting there, and all I had to do was be gone before you noticed. He gave me three hundred crowns."

"Three hundred." I sat back on my heels. "I paid two hundred to get you out of his net. So he spent five hundred on you, total, and I've spent a season keeping you fed. And you sold me for three hundred of his."

"It wasn't—" He flinched. "It wasn't about the money. Not all of it. He showed me a letter. From my mother. Said she'd be on the next barge down if I helped, that she'd been asking after me. I haven't seen her in two years. I didn't—" His face folded. "I didn't even know if it was real. I *knew* it probably wasn't real. I went anyway."

The rain came down. The weir roared its hoarse roar. Somewhere up the slope, Sera would be waking to an empty camp and an empty chest, and I'd have to walk back up there and tell her what her proof of a massacre had cost, and I found I couldn't make myself stand yet.

"Tull," I said. "Look at me."

He looked.

"I have run from every true thing that ever claimed me. My name, my family, every debt, every promise, every person who ever loved me — I have run from all of it for ten years, and I am very, very good at it, and I will tell you what it bought me." I held up my hand and counted, wry as a ledger. "One boat. One debt. And a chest full of dead men's paper that people keep trying to kill me for. That's the whole estate. Brannic told you the truth. I run. I've never once stood and fought for anything in my life."

"Then why—" His voice was small. "Why'd you come after me?"

And there it was. The question, sitting in the rain between us like a stone in a streambed, and I understood that everything — the chest, the claim, the whole drowned kingdom — had narrowed down to this one answer, and that whatever I said next was going to be the truest thing about me either of us would ever know.

Because here's what I'd figured out, lying in the trough with my arm around a drowning boy, my boots full of the Vell: mercy isn't soft. Everybody thinks it's soft. Mercy is the hardest currency there is, because you pay it when you can least afford it, to the person who least deserves it, and it buys nothing back — except the man you're going to be. I could beat Tull bloody. I could turn him in at the next lock, trade him to Brannic the way Brannic had traded him to me. Every one of those roads was downhill, and downhill roads are always the easy walking.

Or I could do the expensive thing.

"You came after me," I said, "because I'm tired of being the man Brannic described. That's all. That's the whole of it. I'm tired, and I'm done, and you're the first test of it, and you failed, and I'm going to help you anyway, and I want you to remember, for the rest of your life, how much that cost me."

He stared at me. Rain ran off his chin.

"The chest?" he whispered. "It went over. I saw it—"

"Oak and iron, sealed joints, weighted for ballast." I stood, joints complaining, and looked up at the churning water below the boil, where the river ran fast and green and deep. "My father built that chest to survive a flood that drowned a nation. It'll have sunk in the tailrace, and the tailrace below a weir is the one place on a river where things *stay*. Everything circles. Nothing carries away. It'll be sitting on the bottom not thirty yards from where we're standing, and I'm going to go down and get it, and then you and I are going to have a different conversation about what you owe me."

"You're — you're going to dive? In this?" He gestured at the river, the rain, the apocalypse generally. "The water's up. The current's—"

"Yes," I said. "It is."

* * *

I found it on the second dive, in the green murk below the boil, wedged in the tailrace between two millstone fragments exactly where the recirculating current had been polishing it for the better part of an hour. My lungs were not what they were at twenty-five, and the water was cold under the warm rain, and the current tried its level best to keep me, and I came up the second time with the chest's lifting ring in my fist and a rope Tull had belayed around a birch trunk — the boy worked, I'll give him that, worked like the dock kid he was, hauling hand over hand with his teeth gritted and tears and riverwater running down his face in the same streams — and between the two of us we brought my father's chest up out of the river like a drowned thing given back.

I sat on it, gasping, in the rain, and Tull stood over me, and neither of us said anything for a while.

Then he knelt down in the wet stone, in the mud, in front of the chest and in front of me, and he wasn't performing it — I've seen performed contrition, I've sold some myself — he was just a boy on his knees in the rain who had sold the only people who'd ever kept him fed, and been sold right back, and been fished out of a river by the man he'd sold.

"Brannic's not done," he said. "I have to tell you. He's not working alone anymore. There are gunboats above the source — Vane moved them up last week, before the storm. And Brannic told me the thing he told me to tell no one, in case I got caught, so I'd have something to trade." He swallowed. "He said the archive doesn't matter to them. The documents, the claim, any of it. He said what Vane wants at the source is the *massacre order* — the original. Because it's signed. Because it's in Vane's own hand. And he said if it ever surfaces, it isn't just Vane who hangs. It's every man who signed the treaty after. Half the coalition government. That's why they'll burn the gorge itself before they let it surface. That's why the gunboats."

The rain hammered down. The weir roared. Somewhere above us, black clouds the color of iron were unloading themselves on the gorge, and the river was rising under our feet, inch by visible inch, and I sat on my father's chest in the middle of it and felt — for the first time in ten years of running — not fear.

Fury. Cold, clean, and entirely new.

"Get the rope," I said to Tull, and stood up. "We're going back to camp. And then, boy, we're going upriver — past the gunboats, past the monsoon, past every man Vane can buy — because there's a signature at the end of this river with my father's seal next to it, and the whole coalition government is going to read it."

I shouldered the chest. It weighed what it had always weighed.

"And Tull — you're carrying it."
