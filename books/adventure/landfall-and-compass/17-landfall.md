## 17. Landfall

The monsoon was supposed to be three days out. The river had other opinions.

I felt it before I saw it—a pressure change in my ears like the whole sky leaning in to whisper a secret it had no intention of keeping. Juniper felt it too. I watched him go still at the wheel, one hand flat on the spokes, head tilted the way he did when he read water, and I knew we were in trouble because he didn't say anything. Juniper Winterbourne's silences were more eloquent than most men's speeches, and this one said *we are about to be very wet and very sorry.*

"Talk to me," I said.

"Clouds over the ridge." He nodded upstream, where the gorge narrowed into a throat of black stone. "They're wrong."

"Wrong how?"

"Wrong direction. Wrong color. Wrong *speed*." He said the last word with the particular disgust of a man who has been lied to by weather his entire professional life. "The forecasts said landfall Thursday. That—" he pointed at the horizon, where something that looked like a bruise the size of God's thumbprint was climbing over the mountains— "is Tuesday."

"It is Tuesday," I said.

"I know what day it is, Sera."

I'd learned, over these weeks on the Marguerite, to distinguish between Juniper's varieties of fear. There was checkpoint fear, which made him charming. There was chest fear—the documents, the claim, the father—which made him quiet and cruel to himself. And there was this new thing, which I hadn't seen before the Judgment Rapids tore out his paddlewheel: responsibility fear. It sat differently on him. Like a coat cut for a bigger man.

We were hauling the chest up the last navigable mile of the Vell on a mule track that the maps called a path and the mountain called an insult. Tull walked ahead with the lead rope, favoring the ankle Juniper had saved at the weir—he still didn't know we knew about Brannic, and every mile we carried him deeper into the gorge was a mile of debt none of us could name aloud. Behind us came the Marguerite's crew, six men who had mutinied two days ago and now followed their captain through increasingly vertical terrain for reasons they probably couldn't have articulated under oath.

Then the sky opened, and articulation became irrelevant anyway.

* * *

I have stood in archives where the silence was so complete you could hear your own heartbeat apologize for interrupting. I thought I understood violence of scale. I was wrong. Rain like this isn't weather; it's an argument the sky is having with the earth, and the earth is losing.

It hit the gorge like nails driven sideways. Not drops—*shrapnel*. The temperature dropped ten degrees in as many seconds, and the light went the color of old pewter, and the mule track beneath our boots turned from dirt to grease to running soup in the time it took me to say a word I won't reproduce here but which would have gotten me expelled from three libraries.

"Cave!" Juniper shouted, or I think he shouted—it was hard to tell words from water. He was pointing across the slope to a dark seam in the cliff face, maybe forty yards of treacherous, streaming rock between us and shelter.

Forty yards doesn't sound like much. Forty yards of monsoon-soaked mountainside sounds like a career decision.

We went anyway, because the alternative was standing still while the mountain decided what to do with us. I remember Tull's hand on my pack strap, hauling me past a section of track that simply wasn't there anymore—I watched the river take it, a whole arm's length of path dissolving into white water like sugar in tea—and I remember Juniper at the rear, walking backward, watching all of us and none of the storm, herding us toward the seam in the rock the way he'd once herded cargo barges through fog. Reading disaster like scripture. Finding the verse that says *live.*

Then we were inside, gasping, dripping, alive, and the world outside had become a gray roar with occasional scenery.

* * *

The cave went back farther than its mouth promised. Twenty feet in, the sound of the rain dulled to a drumming, and thirty feet in, Tull found dry ground and declared it sovereign territory by lying down on it. Someone got a tarp strung. Someone else produced a spirit stove and the philosophical question of whether tea was possible under these conditions, which Juniper answered by making tea, because that man's relationship with caffeine is the only commitment he's never flinched from.

I should have rested. My body sent several formal requests. But I've spent my life in rooms full of paper, and when I saw the walls of this cave—really *saw* them, once my eyes adjusted to the stove-light—I forgot how to be tired.

"Juniper." My voice came out strange. "Come look at this."

The walls were carved.

Not graffiti, not the usual sailor's boasts and crude anatomical tributes you find in every riverside cave from the delta to the divide. These were *registers*. Columns of names, cut deep and deliberate, rank upon rank, receding into the dark of the cave like a congregation. Each name had a date beside it, and beside each date, a small symbol: a crown, a quill, an anchor, a flame.

"Do you see it?" I whispered.

"Stone?" Juniper offered, coming to stand beside me. His shoulder touched mine. He didn't move away. He never moved away anymore, and neither did I, and we had both stopped pretending this was accidental.

"It's a roll of service." My fingers found the nearest column, traced the grooves. Cold stone, but I was shaking too much to feel it. "These are oath-days. Crown means sworn to the royal house. Quill means civil service. Anchor—river wardens, like my grandfather. Flame is..." I stopped.

"Is what?"

"Flame is the archive keepers." I swallowed. "My family's mark. Three generations of Quills kept the Ostravan royal registry. Grandfather, then my mother, then—" I couldn't finish. Juniper's hand covered mine on the stone. Warm against cold. He let it stay there.

Because there it was, four columns down, at eye height, cut deep enough to survive forty years of damp and forgetting:

**QUILL, MARGIT — FLAME — SWORN THE 9TH OF HARVEST, YEAR OF THE DOUBLE TIDE**

My mother. Her oath-day. Her mark. Carved into living rock by hands that had expected the record to matter, that had believed the kingdom would stand long enough for someone to come read it.

And below her name, in a different hand, shallower, hurried, scratched rather than carved:

**QUILL, SERA — FLAME — SWORN IN EXILE**

I don't remember sitting down. I was suddenly on the cave floor with my back against ten thousand years of stone, and Juniper was crouched in front of me, and Tull was very carefully looking at the ceiling, and outside the rain hammered the gorge like it was trying to get in and erase us too.

"My mother carved that," I said. "Before she died. She came back here—she must have come back, after the dissolution, after they struck us from the rolls. She climbed up here and she put my name on the wall because the treaty took it off every *other* wall in the country." I laughed, and it came out broken. "She gave me a title in a cave. Archivist of a nation with no address. I thought I was chasing proof, Juniper. I thought I was *guessing*. She already knew. She already wrote it down, and I've spent nine years not knowing to look."

"Sera—"

"She served faithfully." The words came out of me like something being extracted. "That's what this wall says. Every name on it served. We weren't rebels, we weren't traitors, we weren't collateral damage in some necessary political realignment—that's what the coalition histories call it, *realignment*, like we were furniture. We kept the records. We kept the *flame*, literally, there was a lamp in the registry hall that never went out, my mother relit it every dawn for nineteen years, and then Vane's men came and—"

I stopped, because the next part was the part I'd never said aloud, even to myself, in the privacy of my own skull where I keep the things that would bleed if exposed to air.

"And then what?" Juniper asked quietly. He crouched there in the stove-light with rain still beaded in his hair, and he waited, the way he waits at the wheel when the river gets complicated—not rushing the water, just reading it.

"They didn't arrest her." My voice had gone flat. I heard it happen and couldn't stop it. "You don't arrest archivists; that makes martyrs and leaves witnesses. They *erased* her. Warrant of dissolution, Article Twelve. No trial, no charge, no record. One morning the registry hall was sealed and my mother was a woman who had never existed, and I was a girl with no family name, and every document that proved otherwise was burned or drowned or locked behind a waterfall in a vault nobody admits exists." I looked up at him. "Except now I know it wasn't procedure. It wasn't the treaty. Treaties are blunt instruments—they cut wide, they don't cut *specific*. Somebody chose my family. Somebody went down the service rolls and picked the names that knew too much, and the first name on that list was Margit Quill, Keeper of the Flame."

"How do you know it was Vane?"

"Because of the dates." I was on my feet now, and I didn't remember standing, and my voice had stopped being flat and started being something with edges. "I've spent nine years reading the coalition's own paperwork, Juniper, because erased people learn to live in the margins of official documents. The warrant authorizing the registry seizures was signed eleven days *before* the dissolution treaty was ratified. Before. You can't enforce a law that doesn't exist yet—unless you're the man writing the law and the warrant with the same pen." I jabbed a finger at the carved wall, at my mother's name, at the little flame that some loyal hand had cut into stone expecting it to outlive the empire that betrayed it. "Aldous Vane didn't bury Ostrava because the treaty required it. He buried it because of what the registry *proved*. Because somewhere in those flooded stacks is the order he signed—or the order someone signed for him—and my mother knew where it was, and knowing was enough to unmake her."

The cave was silent except for the rain and the river and the sound of six crewmen discovering that the quiet woman with the notebooks had a furnace where her heart should be.

Tull spoke first, which surprised everyone including Tull. "So we're going to get it," he said. Not a question. "The thing in the vault. We're going to get it and take it to the tribunal and nail his signature to the door."

"We're going to try," Juniper said.

"No." Tull sat up, and his ankle must have hurt, and he didn't care. "Captain. I've run cargo with smugglers and liars and one man who claimed to be a deposed duke, and I'm telling you—we're going to *get* it. Whatever's behind that waterfall, whatever the river does to us getting there. Because I looked at that wall, and my gran's from Harrow Bend, and Harrow Bend was Ostravan before the maps changed, and nobody ever told her why she wasn't allowed a surname on the ferry manifests." He pointed at the flame mark, at my mother's name. "That's her they erased too. So we get it."

One by one, the crew nodded. Men who had mutinied in the Judgment Rapids. Men Juniper had won back not with orders but with honesty about the danger. They nodded like a jury returning a verdict, and I stood in front of them with rain-wet hair and nine years of grief transmuting, molecule by molecule, into something with a sharper temper.

Grief, I realized, is just fury that hasn't found its address yet. I had an address now. It was carved on a wall, and it was waiting behind a waterfall at the end of the world.

* * *

Later, when the stove had burned low and the crew slept in a row of exhausted shapes, Juniper and I sat at the cave mouth watching the storm work the gorge. The river below had risen visibly since dusk—you could measure it against the rocks it was swallowing, one landmark at a time. Somewhere up there, at the source, the flooded archive was getting more flooded by the hour. Our deadline was drowning itself.

"The water'll be over the lower shelves by tomorrow," I said. "Maybe higher."

"I know."

"Some of it may already be lost. What we're diving for might not exist anymore."

"I know that too." He was quiet a moment. Then, in the tone of a man setting down something heavy he'd been carrying a long way: "My father's counter-seal is in there. On whatever Vane signed. Sera, if we find it—if it's really there—then everything I've been running from turns out to be the opposite of what I thought. He wasn't the traitor. He was the *witness*. He hid the evidence inside the machine that wanted it dead, and then he died, and I spent ten years ashamed of a man who was braver than I've ever been." He shook his head. "I don't know how to be the son of that."

"You've been practicing," I said. "Since the weir. Since the rapids. Since you paid your savings for Tull instead of trading him." I leaned my shoulder into his. "You're not running anymore, Juniper. You just haven't noticed yet."

He huffed something that was almost a laugh. "The monsoon noticed for me."

Outside, lightning stitched the gorge white, and in that single frame of light I saw the source gorge ahead of us—black stone, white water, and a thread of waterfall falling from a lip of rock half a mile up, swollen fat with the storm, guarding whatever the river had kept for forty years.

Behind us, in the dark of the cave, my mother's name held its place in the stone. Ahead, the water rose.

"First light," Juniper said. "We climb while the rain's thinnest. After that, the gorge belongs to the river."

"And the archive?"

He looked at the waterfall's distant thread, and I watched him do the thing he does—read the water, read the risk, read the man he'd decided to become.

"Tomorrow," he said, "we take it back."
