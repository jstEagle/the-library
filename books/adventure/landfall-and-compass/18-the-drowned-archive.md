## 18. The Drowned Archive

The waterfall had grown opinions overnight.

When we'd first sighted it from the ridge—two days and one ruined map ago—it had been a white thread down the gorge wall, elegant, almost shy. Now it was a fist. The monsoon had arrived early and angry, and the falls came off the cliff in a single unbroken sheet of brown water that hit the pool below hard enough to make the stones flinch. The spray reached us a hundred yards out, cold as a debt collector's handshake.

"That's not a waterfall," Tull said beside me. "That's a verdict."

"It's a door," Sera said.

She was soaked through, hair plastered to her skull, lips faintly blue, and she was smiling. I've known people who smile at funerals and people who smile at storms, but Sera Quill smiled at the exact center of catastrophe, which is either madness or faith, and I'd stopped trying to tell them apart where she was concerned.

"The registry says the vault entrance sits behind the fall," she said, shouting over the roar. "The carvings in the cave confirmed it. The crown seal over the door. It has to be there."

"Has to be," I repeated. "Wonderful word. Has to be. Right up there with probably and just this once."

"You have a better idea?"

I did not. What I had was a boat two miles downstream with a shattered paddlewheel, a crew of four who hadn't mutinied only because I'd finally started telling them the truth, a chest of documents that made every powerful man on the river want me dead or bought, and a gorge filling with rainwater like a bathtub with the tap jammed open. My better ideas had all drowned somewhere around chapter twelve.

So we went to look at the door.

* * *

Getting behind the waterfall meant traversing a ledge no wider than my boot, slick with spray, with the pool churning forty feet below. I went first, because that was the job now. Somewhere between the Judgment Rapids and the weir, between saving Tull and standing still while Vane's gunboats made their offer, the job had changed from *outrun* to *stand in front of*, and I found—to my considerable irritation—that I didn't hate it.

The ledge gave way twice. The second time, Sera's foot skidded off the edge entirely and I caught her wrist and held her against the rock while the falls thundered close enough to taste, and for three full seconds we hung there, her weight pulling at my shoulder, the whole gray weight of the storm pressing down, and neither of us said anything, because there was nothing to say that the water wasn't already saying louder.

Then we were through, into the seam behind the fall, and the world went quiet in the strange underwater way that loud places do when you slip inside them. The rock curved overhead. Daylight came through the falling water in sheets, green-gold, rippling across the walls like light through bottle glass.

And there, cut into the stone, worn but unmistakable: the triple crown of Ostrava.

Sera put her hand flat against it. She didn't say anything. Her shoulders did the talking—a small shudder, then a straightening, like something being set upright after a long time fallen.

"There should be a mechanism," she managed. "A counterweight. The registry fragment mentioned—" She stopped. She was looking at the base of the seal, where the floodwater had done what forty years of secrecy couldn't: torn the old blocking stones loose and left a gap at the bottom of the doorway, black water sliding through it, in and out, breathing with the pulse of the falls.

"It's open," I said.

"It's flooded."

"Well." I shrugged off my coat and handed it to Tull, who had followed us along the ledge with the expression of a man attending his own funeral and hoping the eulogy would be brief. "Nothing in Ostrava ever did anything the easy way."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about diving into dark water in a flooding gorge: your body objects. Every instinct you own lines up at the door of your skull and files complaints. The water behind the fall was cold enough to stop thought, black past arm's length, and moving—not fast, but moving, a slow interior current dragging toward whatever drain the gorge used. Above us, the entrance gap was maybe four feet of clearance between the water surface and the rock. Below us, unknown. Behind us, the pool, the ledge, the world.

I tied a rope around my waist and handed the end to Sera.

"One pull means come after me," I said. "Two pulls means I'm coming up. Three means—" I stopped. "Three won't happen."

"What does three mean?"

"Three means you take the chest and the crew and you go downriver and you don't stop until Casterly Wharf, and you finish this without me."

Her jaw set in that particular way. "Juniper."

"Sera."

"I'm going in with you."

"No."

"Yes." She was already pulling the rope through her hands, checking knots with an archivist's fussy precision, and I realized I was arguing with a woman who had spent ten years cataloguing erased names in borrowed rooms and had walked into every one of our disasters since Casterly Wharf without once asking whose fault it would be if it went wrong. "You dive, I hold the lamp and the line. If you find the archive, you'll need someone to pass things to. You can't carry a waterproof case and manage a rope in current. You know I'm right."

I knew she was right. That was the trouble with Sera. She was right the way the river was right—inevitably, and usually at the moment least convenient.

"One lamp," I conceded. "You stay at the gap. If the level rises more than a hand's width, you pull twice and we're done, documents or no documents."

"And if you don't come up?"

"Then I'm dead, and the rope's wasted on me."

She hit me. Not hard—a flat palm against my shoulder, half anger, half benediction. Then she kissed me, quick and fierce, tasting of rain and iron, and pushed the lamp into my hands, and there was nothing left to negotiate.

I filled my lungs and went under.

* * *

The dark took me like an old friend with bad intentions.

I swam down along the wall, one hand trailing the stone, the lamp burning a small furious bubble of yellow that showed me exactly enough to be frightened by. The passage angled downward. The current pulled at my ankles, patient, insistent, a polite usher showing me to my seat. Ten feet. Fifteen. My ears ached and equalized and ached again. The rope hissed out behind me.

Twenty feet down, the passage opened.

My lamp swept across a room.

It had been a grand room once—I could feel the proportions even through the murk, the high ceiling, the columns spaced with ceremonial regularity. Shelves lined the walls, stone-cut, and on the shelves sat row upon row of lead-sealed cases, hundreds of them, furred with silt, arranged with the terrible tidiness of people who had believed their work would matter forever. A nation's memory, shelved. A country that existed now only as paperwork, filed neatly at the bottom of a flooded gorge behind a waterfall, waiting forty years for somebody to come read it.

The water was higher than it had been designed for. The lowest shelves were already submerged to their lids; the floor of the chamber lay somewhere beneath my fins in six feet of cold black soup. And the water was rising. I could see it—in the time it took my lamp to swing from one column to the next, the highest ripple mark on the stone crept up a finger's width. The monsoon wasn't coming. It had arrived, and it was filling the gorge like a landlord changing the locks.

Somewhere in that room was the case Sera's fragments described. The registry had called it the Black Seal Case—the master record, the one document that mattered, kept apart from the general archive. I swam the perimeter, lamp sweeping, lungs beginning their quiet negotiation with my brain. Third shelf, north wall, set back in its own alcove: a case larger than the others, bound in blackened bronze, the triple crown stamped deep enough to still read through forty years of silt.

I hooked my fingers under it and pulled. It came free in an eruption of sediment that turned my lamp-beam to milk.

Blind, weightless, holding forty pounds of drowned history, I did the arithmetic every diver does: air spent, air remaining, distance to the gap, speed of ascent, speed of current. The numbers came back ugly. The sediment cloud killed my sense of direction entirely. For five seconds—five long, honest seconds—I turned in the black with no wall, no light, no up, hugging a dead kingdom's last testament to my chest, and I thought, with a clarity that felt almost like laughter: *this is what running gets you, Juniper. Eventually every route ends in a room like this.*

Then my hand found the rope.

I climbed it hand over hand, kicking, the case dragging at me like a passenger who'd decided against disembarking, and the yellow glow of Sera's lamp bloomed above me like a sunrise I'd personally ordered. Her arms came down into the water—both of them, defying every instruction I'd given—and hauled, and together we broke the surface inside the entry seam, gasping, the case between us, the gap above us narrowing by the minute.

"Rise?" she choked.

"A hand's width. Maybe two."

"Then we go now. All of it—the chest, everything, before the gap closes."

"Tull!" I shouted back along the seam. "Get ready to receive!"

* * *

We got it out. Of course we got it out—we got all of it out, case after case passed up the seam and along the ledge and stowed in oilcloth in the cave above, Tull hauling with the steady desperation of a man who had learned in the last week that Juniper Winterbourne told the truth about danger and therefore could be believed about safety too. The water rose as we worked. By the last trip, the gap was two feet of thrashing clearance, and I went through it on my back with the Black Seal Case held overhead like an offering, and the falls slapped me down onto the ledge and tried to keep me, and Sera's grip on my collar was the only argument that counted.

Then we were all in the cave, dripping, heaving, alive, and the entrance behind the fall was a brown curtain with no door in it anymore.

Sera knelt over the Black Seal Case with trembling hands. Forty years of seals and locks are no match for an archivist with a knife and a grievance; the bronze lid came up with a groan, and inside, wrapped in waxed linen, dry as the day it was folded, lay a folio perhaps twenty pages thick.

She read. I watched her read, and I will tell you honestly that watching Sera Quill read that document was worse than any checkpoint, any rapid, any gunboat. Her face went through grief like weather—cloud, lightning, the long gray rain—and then it stopped, and settled, and became something I had never seen on it before, which was certainty with a blade in it.

"Read it," she said, and handed it to me. "Out loud. You need to hear it in your own voice."

I read.

The first pages were census. Names. Thousands of names—Ostrava's last honest accounting of itself, taken the spring before the fall. Sera ran her finger down a column and stopped. "*Quill, Maren. Quill, Aldric. Quill, Ives.*" Her voice didn't break. That was somehow worse. "My grandmother. My grandfather. My father, aged six." She looked up at me. "They listed him. They counted him. He existed, Juniper. On paper, in ink, in the crown's own hand—he *existed*."

"He exists now," I said. "You'll make sure of it."

Then the last page. The order itself. I had braced for it through nine chapters of my life, though I didn't know that's what I'd been doing, and when it came it was smaller than I expected—one page, formal, bureaucratic, the language of men who sign massacres the way they sign grain shipments.

*By authority vested under the Coalition Provision Compact, the settlement of Ostrava-on-Vell is declared dissolved. Resistance to relocation shall be met with such measures as field commanders deem necessary. No survivors of the action are to be entered into any register.*

Beneath it, a signature I knew from a hundred contract disputes and shipping manifests and whispered threats on the river: **Aldous Vane**.

And beneath that—my breath went out of me like a hull taking water—beneath that, pressed into the wax, unmistakable, a counter-seal I had seen exactly once before, on a letter my father burned in our stove the winter before he disappeared, telling me it was nothing, telling me to forget it, telling me with his eyes that it was everything:

**Elias Winterbourne, Witness for the Crown.**

Not architect. Not executioner. *Witness.* He had signed the order—but the registry, the whole shelved nation behind that waterfall, bore his other signature too, the secret one, the one that said: *I preserved this. I hid the count. I hid the names. When they come to erase these people completely, let there be one place where they were written down.*

He hadn't saved Ostrava. He hadn't stopped Vane. He had done the only thing a coward with a conscience can do—he had kept the receipts. And then he had run, the way I ran, upriver and downriver and through every fog-bound bend on the Vell, carrying a name he couldn't wear and a truth he couldn't drop, until the river finally gave him back to himself as a chest in a flood and a son who didn't want it.

"Juniper." Sera's hand was on my arm. "Your father signed both."

"I know."

"Do you understand what that means? At the tribunal—Vane's signature orders the massacre. Your father's counter-seal proves the coalition knew, sanctioned it, and then buried the evidence. This isn't just proof of what happened. It's proof of the cover-up. It's Vane's whole machine, in one case, with his name on the crank."

"I know," I said again, because I did, and because saying it twice was easier than saying the third thing, which was: *he was like me, and I am like him, and the only difference between us is that I'm still here, and the water hasn't closed over me yet.*

Tull cleared his throat from the mouth of the cave. He was looking out at the gorge, and his face had gone the color of the water.

"Captain," he said. "You'll want to see this."

I went to the cave mouth. The rain had thickened to a solid gray roaring thing, and the river below—the river that had carried us the whole way, the river I had trusted with my life since I was nineteen years old—was climbing its banks in visible surges, brown and muscular, tearing whole trees off the slopes and swallowing them whole.

And downriver, around the last bend, small with distance but perfectly distinct against the white water: lanterns. A line of them, moving along the gorge trail. Moving toward us.

Brannic had found the overland route. Or Vane had. In this storm, with this river, with a gorge flooding behind a waterfall that no longer had a door—

There was nowhere left to run. There was, as it happened, nowhere left to run to.

I looked back at Sera, kneeling over the open case, holding her family's existence in both hands.

"They're coming," I said. "How fast can you pack?"

"How fast do we need to go?"

"Faster than that," I said, as the first lantern rounded the bend below.
