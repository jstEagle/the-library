## 19. Stand at the Source

The water in the vault came up to my knees and rising, and I was doing arithmetic with my life.

Not the romantic kind—the kind a pilot does when his boat is taking on water faster than the pumps can argue. Inflow versus outflow. Time remaining versus time needed. Sera needed maybe ten minutes to seal the documents into the waterproof case and get them up the rope to Tull waiting at the lip of the falls. The vault had, by my estimate, four.

I've always been good at that math. It's the whole trade, really. Reading water, reading weather, reading the gap between what you have and what you need, and then deciding which cargo goes overboard first. Ten years on the Vell taught me that every disaster is just a schedule you didn't keep.

What the Vell never taught me was what to do when the thing going overboard is yourself.

"Juniper." Sera's voice came through the roar of the waterfall behind us—swollen fat with monsoon, hammering down over the vault entrance like the river's own fist. "The case won't close. The registry's too thick."

"Leave the registry."

"I'm not leaving the registry."

"Sera—"

"It's *names*, Juniper." She was waist-deep now, hair plastered black against her face, holding the great ledger of Ostrava above the waterline like a woman lifting a child out of a flood. Every erased family in it. Hers among them, written in a clerk's careful hand forty years ago, as if ink could be proof against drowning. "It's every name they took. I am not leaving it."

So there went my four minutes. Call it two and a half.

That's when I heard the boats.

* * *

You'd think a gorge at the source of the Vell, in the teeth of an early monsoon, would be the one place in the world nobody would come looking for you. You'd be wrong, because Lord Aldous Vane has never once in his life accepted that somewhere is beyond him. That's practically his coat of arms—a man standing on a map, pointing at the parts he owns.

I heard engines through the waterfall's thunder before I saw anything: the flat, patient drone of coalition patrol craft idling up the last navigable mile, then the wet slap of boots on stone. Torchlight swung across the curtain of falling water and threw our shadows huge and wavering against the vault's far wall—two drowned ghosts dancing for their supper.

Sera looked at me. Water to her chin now. The case still open in her hands.

"How many?" she asked.

"Doesn't matter."

"It matters a little."

"It matters zero," I said, "because the number of them isn't something I can change, and the number of minutes we have is. Get the ledger into the case. Take pages if you have to. I'll go talk to them."

She stared at me. In the torchlight through the falls her eyes were very bright and very angry, which is her natural state, but there was something else under it. "Talk."

"I'm told I'm good at lying."

"Not to men with guns you're not."

"Well," I said, wading toward the entrance, "there's a first time for everything. Apparently today's full of them."

* * *

Here's what I know about running, since I've done it professionally for a decade. Running feels like courage from the inside. You tell yourself a story about freedom, about lightness, about not being tied to ports or people or names. And it works, right up until the moment you realize that everything you were running from got heavier while you weren't watching, so that now it weighs more than any cargo you ever hauled, and your legs are tired, and the road ahead looks exactly like the road behind.

The torches came around the falls' edge. Six men, rifles slung, led by a lieutenant I recognized from Grey Locks—the checkpoint where we'd bought passage with forged papers and burned our last safe identity doing it. He'd been suspicious then. He wasn't suspicious now. Suspicion is a luxury; certainty had arrived by gunboat.

"Winterbourne." He said it like a diagnosis. "Lord Vane wants a word."

"Tell him I'm busy drowning."

"He anticipated that." The lieutenant actually smiled. He had the smile of a man who'd been promised a bonus. "He said you'd make jokes. He said to tell you the vault collapses within the hour, the documents are worthless underwater, and the only choice left is who walks out of this gorge carrying them. He'd prefer it was his man. But he'll settle for knowing where the body floats."

Behind me, splashing, paper rustling—Sera working, fast, trusting me to hold the line. Which was, I realized with a feeling like vertigo, exactly what I was doing. Not evading. Not slipping sideways through some fog-bound bend while the danger passed behind me. Standing in a doorway of rising water with my feet planted like I owned the place.

My father stood in doorways like this. I know because I finally read the ledger, and I know because the guilt in my bones has always had his posture.

"The documents are already secured," I said. "Copies. Three couriers, three routes. Kill me and the tribunal gets everything by Thursday."

The lieutenant considered that. Torchlight flickered on the water between us, which was at my thighs now and climbing, cold as a debt collector's handshake.

"You're lying," he said.

"Probably."

"But you might not be." He shifted his weight. Behind him, one of his men glanced nervously at the falls, where the volume of falling water had audibly increased, the river deciding how much of itself to spend on killing us. "Vane said you were a runner. He said runners always fold."

"Vane's known me for a month. The river's known me for ten years." I spread my hands, palms up, a captain showing empty holds. "Ask the river."

* * *

I want to be honest about what happened inside me in that next minute, because it wasn't the trumpet-blast courage of the ballads. It was smaller and stranger than that.

It was noticing that I wasn't afraid of the guns.

I was afraid of the water—I'm a pilot, I respect water the way priests respect God—but the guns, the men, the whole apparatus of Vane's power: standing in front of it, I felt only a tired sort of recognition. I'd spent ten years treating these people as weather, as things that happened to other boats. But weather doesn't arrest deckhands. Weather doesn't erase nations from maps or families from registries. Weather doesn't sign massacre orders and then build a treaty on top of the graves to hide them.

Men did that. Men with rifles and orders. And men, unlike weather, can be stood in front of.

"Last chance, Winterbourne."

"Funny," I said. "I was about to say the same to you. That vault's coming down, lieutenant, and Vane's going to want to know why his man was standing under it. You want to explain to Lord Aldous that you drowned for a stack of paper? Or do you want to be up on high ground when the tribunal starts asking questions? Because it's going to start asking them soon. Brannic knows."

Something moved behind his eyes at Brannic's name. Good. The machine had a crack in it; I'd seen Brannic look at Vane's signature in the flooded archive below and understand, in real time, whose neck the noose was actually sized for. Machines with cracks are my specialty. I've piloted one for ten years.

"Sir," said one of his men, younger, rain streaming off his helmet, "the water—"

"I heard it," the lieutenant snapped.

And then the mountain answered for all of us.

* * *

I felt it through my boots before I heard it—a deep groan, structural, the sound of forty years of masonry giving up its argument with the river. Above the falls, the old spillway arch that had held back half the source lake cracked along a seam some engineer had warned about before anyone was alive to care.

The waterfall doubled. Then it tripled.

The lieutenant shouted something. His men were already scrambling for the high ledge, training winning out over orders, and I couldn't blame them—I was scrambling too, backward, blind, arms out, until my hand found Sera's wrist in the churning dark.

"Case!" she screamed.

"Gone?"

"Sealed! On my back!"

Then the wall of water hit the vault proper and the world became noise.

* * *

I have nearly died on the Vell more times than I've kept count of, and each time there's a moment—half a heartbeat—where the mind goes perfectly quiet and takes inventory. Hull integrity: failing. Cargo: one archivist, one ledger of names, one heir to a drowned kingdom who never asked for the job. Ballast: ten years of running, jettisoned, finally, thank God, because it was pulling us under.

The vault ceiling came down in sections. The water lifted us both and slammed us against the inner wall, and I got an arm around an iron stanchion—some fixture of the old archive, put there by dead engineers who built better than they knew—and held on. Sera's fist in my collar. Her back to my chest, the case strapped between us like a child carried through a fire.

The lieutenant and his men were gone. Whether up the ledge or under the water, I couldn't say, and I found I couldn't much care, which frightened me a little, that hardening. There'd be a bill for it later. There's always a bill.

"Hold on!" I shouted, though to what, against what, I couldn't have said.

The stanchion held. The vault did not. Piece by piece the roof opened like a box being unwrapped by the flood, and gray monsoon light poured down through the collapsing stone, and the water that had been our enemy became, briefly, our road—lifting us, tumbling us, spitting us out through the broken crown of the archive into open air.

* * *

We came up in the gorge pool below the falls, gasping, the case still lashed between us, and Tull was there—he'd come down the rope when the mountain started talking, the little idiot, brave past all sense—and between the three of us we dragged onto the shingle bank as the source lake finished emptying its throat into the gorge.

I lay on my back on the stones and laughed. I couldn't help it. It came out of me like water out of the vault, ten years of it, all the fog-bound bends and refused contracts and closed doors, all the running, gone in one long ugly laugh at the sky.

Sera rolled over, coughing, and looked at me like I'd lost my mind.

"Maybe," I admitted.

"The case—"

"Is it intact?"

She fumbled the straps, opened it, and went silent. Then she pressed the ledger to her chest and started crying, which she'd deny later, and I pretended not to see, which is marriage practice, though neither of us knew that yet.

Tull sat down heavily beside us, soaked to the soul. "Captain," he said, "the boats. Vane's boats are still up-channel, and the lieutenant—if he made it—he'll report."

"Let him report," I said.

And here's the strange part, the part I keep turning over like a stone in my pocket: I meant it. Let him report. Let Vane know the vault collapsed and the documents survived and the Winterbourne boy stood in the doorway and did not run. For ten years I'd built my whole life on the principle that stopping costs more than moving—that the moment you plant your feet, the world collects its debt.

But standing in that doorway, I'd paid the debt. All of it. The name, the father, the claim, the fear. And what was left, once the water drained away, was embarrassingly little and completely enough: a woman with a ledger, a boy with a rope, a case full of names, and a long climb ahead to anywhere the truth could be read aloud.

I got up. My legs shook. I let them.

"We move before they regroup," I said. "Up-trail, away from the channel. Tull, can you carry the case?"

He could. Of course he could. Everyone carries something on this river; I'd just spent ten years pretending mine weighed nothing.

We started climbing. Behind us the gorge roared where the archive had been, and somewhere down-channel, engines turned, hunting.

They'd find the empty vault. They'd find the story I wanted them to find.

What they wouldn't find—what no gunboat on the Vell could reach now—was already climbing the trail ahead of them, three soaked figures and a kingdom's worth of names, walking toward the tribunal and the end of Lord Aldous Vane, one switchback at a time.
