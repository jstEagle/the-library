## 22. Compass

The Vell in spring is a liar with a beautiful face. That's the first thing I tell every new deckhand, usually while the river is doing something charming—glittering, docile, wearing its morning fog like a borrowed coat—and I say it because by August that same water will try to kill us, and I want them to remember I warned them when it was still being polite.

This particular morning it was being very polite indeed. Fog to the gunwales, the kind you could cut with a spoon, and the Marguerite sliding down from Casterly Wharf with a hold full of mill iron and a wheel turning easy under my hands. Ten years I'd run this river like a man fleeing a debt collector. Now I was running it like a man who lived there.

The difference, if you're wondering, is mostly in how you read the bends.

A fugitive reads water for exits. Where can I cut loose, where can I beach her, where's the channel that doesn't show on the charts because nobody official bothers. A resident reads for depth and mood. And the Vell has moods the way old men have opinions—slow to form, impossible to argue with, liable to change without notice.

"Sandbar's moved," Sera said beside me, not looking up from the ledger she was keeping. She'd been at it since before dawn, cataloging cargo in that small fierce handwriting of hers, the one that looked like it was filing charges against someone. "Left bank, maybe forty yards downstream of where your chart says."

"You can see through fog?"

"I can see *you* seeing through fog. You've been easing her starboard for two minutes and pretending it's habit."

I grinned at the wheel. "Forty yards?"

"Thirty-eight. Don't be dramatic."

That was Sera Quill for you. Archivist, survivor of an erased nation, woman who had once held a pistol on a coalition agent in a flooding vault and talked him into treason with nothing but a signature and a raised eyebrow—and she still kept cargo ledgers like the fate of kingdoms hung on accurate tonnage figures. Maybe for her they did. Numbers were how she'd rebuilt a family out of paper. Every name she restored to a manifest was a headstone she got to un-plant.

We slid past the sandbar with six feet to spare. The Marguerite didn't even grumble. She'd earned her rest the hard way last autumn—a torn paddlewheel in the Judgment Rapids, a season in drydock, more new timber in her than original—and now she ran smooth as a sermon, which I trusted about as far as I could throw her. Boats are like people that way. The ones that have been broken and mended run fine right up until they don't, and the trick is loving them anyway.

Tull came up from the hold wiping his hands, seventeen years old and trying very hard to look like he'd been born knowing what a capstan was. He still flinched at loud noises and wouldn't take payment in coin from anyone wearing a uniform, but he'd stopped calling me *sir*, which in Tull's private currency was roughly a knighthood.

"Fog's lifting off the narrows," he reported. "Might make Grey Locks by midday if we push."

"We're not pushing."

He blinked. A year ago I'd have pushed. A year ago pushing was the whole religion—make up time, make up distance, stay ahead of whatever was behind. Now I looked at the fog thinning over the water like breath off glass and felt absolutely no urgency whatsoever, and it still startled me some mornings, that absence. Grief leaves a hole. So does hurry.

"Midday's fine," I said. "Or after. The iron isn't going anywhere and neither are we."

Tull nodded slowly, filed that away in whatever ledger he kept behind his eyes, and went forward. Sera watched him go, then watched me, which was worse.

"You used to run this stretch at night," she said. "In fog. In flood. Brannic told me you once took her through the Narrows with no running lights and half a chart."

"He exaggerates."

"He said you did it twice."

"The second time was to prove the first wasn't luck." I turned the wheel a spoke and let the current do the arguing. "It wasn't luck. It was also stupid, and I've decided to retire from stupid. It pays terribly."

"It paid in survival."

"It paid in not stopping." I heard my own voice come out flat and made myself soften it. "There's a difference. Took me ten years and one flooded archive to learn it."

Sera closed the ledger and leaned her elbows on the rail beside me, and for a while neither of us said anything, because the river was saying plenty. Spring melt talking in the shallows. A kingfisher doing its blue arithmetic along the willows. Somewhere upstream, the lock horn at Grey Locks, patient as a church bell.

Ten months since Landfall. Since the monsoon came down the gorge like the end of the world and I stood in a drowning vault with a waterproof case against my chest and Vane's men coming through the water behind me, and—for the first time in my life—I planted my feet instead of finding an exit. It had cost me the escape route. It nearly cost me everything else. But standing turned out to be a skill like any other: terrible the first time, less terrible the second, and by the tribunal at Casterly Wharf I could stand in front of a room full of lawyers and lords and renounce a crown without my voice shaking more than a little.

They'd wanted me to be a king. The Ostravan survivors, the old loyalists, the sentimental press—they'd wanted a restoration, banners, a boy on a barge. What they got instead was a pilot who handed them a massacre order bearing Lord Aldous Vane's signature and his father's counter-seal, and told them the throne could drown with the rest of it, but the names couldn't. Every erased family. Every confiscated deed. Every child entered in the royal registry whose existence the treaty had declared fictional.

Sera's family among them. Her father's name read aloud in open session, restored to the rolls, and I will go to my grave remembering the sound she made—not a sob, nothing so simple. More like a keel taking water after a long time dry.

Vane cracked under it. Not all at once—the powerful never shatter, they spall, shedding pieces until the shape underneath is undeniable—but he cracked, and his machine turned on itself, and Brannic carried the evidence to the tribunal with the grim efficiency of a man switching sides the way other men switch coats. Last I heard, Vane was awaiting trial in a cell with a view of the harbor he'd spent forty years pretending didn't exist. I hope the view is good. I hope he looks at it every day.

And me? They gave me a choice of rewards and I took the only one that mattered: citizenship. Not the crown. The *name*. Winterbourne, entered back into the rolls of a nation that officially hadn't existed when I was born, with a line through the word *traitor* that had followed my father's signature and a note in the margin in the registrar's own hand: *preserved the registry at great personal risk.* My father. The man I'd spent a decade hating for signing the dissolution treaty, who had been playing the longest game on the river the whole time—signing away a kingdom to keep its memory alive in ink. I still didn't know how to feel about him most days. Some mornings I woke up furious at him. Most mornings now I just missed him, which was worse and better at the same time.

"You're doing it again," Sera said.

"Doing what?"

"Sailing past your own thoughts without stopping. I can always tell. Your jaw does a thing."

"My jaw does many things. It's a versatile jaw."

"Juniper."

I sighed and gave her the truth, because that was our arrangement now—we'd traded shields for honesty somewhere between a cliff cave and a tribunal, and there was no going back to the old currency. "I was thinking about my father. About whether he ever steered a morning like this one and let himself enjoy it, or whether he spent forty years holding the wheel white-knuckled waiting for the next agent to come aboard."

"What do you think?"

"I think he'd have liked her." I nodded at the rail, the deck, the old girl carrying us. "She's exactly the kind of thing he'd have saved."

* * *

Grey Locks at midday was Grey Locks as I'd never known it: unhurried. The checkpoint stands were empty, the customs shed converted to a ferry office, and the lock keeper—a broad woman named Ostrey who'd once waved us through on forged papers and pretended forever afterward that she hadn't—came down to the wall with two cups of tea and no paperwork at all.

"Master Winterbourne," she said, handing the cups up. She still stumbled slightly on the surname, testing it, the way everyone did. Half the river called me Captain, a stubborn quarter still called me *that smuggler*, and the papers insisted on Master, but Ostrey said it like she was checking whether it fit. It fit better every month.

"Mistress Ostrey. Cargo's iron and flour, nothing interesting, I checked personally."

"Pity. I miss the interesting." She glanced at Sera, then back. "Tribunal clerk came through Tuesday asking after you. Wanted to know your winter berth."

My hand tightened on the cup before I could stop it. Old reflexes die hard; some of them have to be buried at sea. "What for?"

Ostrey shrugged, magnificent and unbothered. "Didn't say. Left a card." She produced it from her apron like a magician. Cream stock, good weight, a crest I didn't recognize—a compass rose, quartered, with a crown floating above it like a balloon someone had let go of.

Sera took it before I could. Turned it over. Read the back, and I watched her face do three things in quick succession: recognition, surprise, and something that might have been hope wearing a disguise.

"Well?" I said.

"It's the Restoration Office," she said. "The historical commission. They've been reconstructing the Ostravan civil rolls—all the families, all the properties." She looked up at me, and the fog had burned off entirely and the sun was on her face and she was almost smiling. "Juniper, they found the Quill estate. Upstream. Above the source gorge—it survived, the house, the orchards, everything. They've been trying to locate surviving heirs since winter."

"And they want...?"

"They want me." She said it carefully, the way you set down something fragile. "And they asked for you by name too. As witness. As—" she hesitated, "—as the man who returned the registry. There's a ceremony. In June. They want names read. They want *someone* to stand for the ones who can't."

I looked at the card. At the compass rose with its runaway crown. Somewhere in my chest, the old machinery stirred—the part of me that had spent a decade converting every invitation into a departure schedule—and I was interested to find that it caught, sputtered, and produced nothing at all.

"When in June?" I asked.

Sera stared at me. "You're asking the date. You're not already calculating the tide window for leaving."

"Don't get used to it."

Too late; she was already smiling like sunrise on open water, and Tull was shouting from the bow that the lock gates were opening, and the Vell ran green and gold and moody toward the high country where a drowned kingdom had left its doors unlocked for anyone willing to come home.

I put the Marguerite's nose to the current, took the wheel, and steered us upstream—toward the source, toward June, toward whatever a man was supposed to do with a name he'd finally decided to keep.

For once, I wasn't running anything but the boat.

And she ran true.
