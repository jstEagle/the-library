## 1. The Weight of Stopping

The thief left footprints like an apology—sincere, but not good enough to fix anything.

I crouched over the third one in a quarter mile and let myself feel a small, professional satisfaction. Sable Vane, if the caravan master's description held, was either very good at his trade or very bad at planning his exits, because he'd crossed the salt pan at dawn with the sun behind him and the crust still soft from last night's dew, and the flats had taken his impression the way wax takes a seal. Long stride. Weight on the balls of the feet. A slight drag on the left heel that said *old injury* or *bad boots*, and I was betting on boots, because men who run for a living tend their injuries and men who steal for a living tend their purses.

Either way, I'd catch him by nightfall.

That was the arithmetic I liked best: distance divided by stride, water divided by days, a problem you could solve with your eyes and your patience and nothing else. Tracking was honest work. The ground didn't lie to you. People lied constantly—the caravan master had sworn Vane robbed the shrine of everything, then couldn't tell me what *everything* was—but ground told the truth in a language anyone could learn if they were willing to shut up long enough to listen.

I stood, brushed the salt from my knees, and walked on.

The flats stretched ahead of me, white as a dead man's palm, cracked into hexagons where the last wet season had come and gone without apology. Heat shimmered off the crust in slow waves. There was no shade within two days' walk in any direction, which is exactly how I like my country: empty enough that nobody asks your name.

Because names are the trouble. Names are where it all went wrong.

My father used to say a Brightwater could stand anywhere in the harbor district and be greeted by name—dockhands, fishwives, the harbormaster himself. *A name is a door, little bird,* he'd say, ruffling my hair with hands that smelled of tar and ledger ink. *Keep yours clean and every door opens.*

He kept his clean. That was the joke of it, the bitter punchline I've been chewing for eleven years. Tammas Brightwater, customs clerk of the Harbor Council, found discrepancies in the tithe ledgers—found them, documented them, brought them to the Council's own magistrates like the dutiful little clerk he was—and inside a season those same magistrates had him charged with the embezzlement instead. Falsified accounts. Confiscated house. Public stripping of office on the Council steps while half the city watched and the other half pretended not to.

They didn't just ruin him. They made him *untrue*. And a man who isn't true can't testify, can't accuse, can't even properly defend himself, because who believes a liar?

I was fourteen when they did it. Old enough to understand, young enough to believe the truth would out. It took me another three years to learn that truth doesn't out on its own; it has to be carried, and carrying it costs more than most people can pay. My father paid with his heart—it gave out two winters after the trial, quietly, in a rented room that smelled of mildew and cheap tallow. My mother paid differently. She simply stopped being findable. One morning her chair was empty and her ring was on the table and there was no note, because a woman with no name left behind doesn't owe anyone an explanation.

So I took the only inheritance available: I became no one. Dropped Brightwater like a hot coal and picked up work that required silence—a bounty tracker, a finder of lost things, mostly lost people who didn't want to be found. I got good at it. Good at moving, good at watching, good at leaving towns before the second cup of tea, before anyone could ask where I was from or, worse, offer to tell me about myself.

Stopping means remembering. So I don't stop.

* * *

By midday the trail turned east, toward the drowned coast, and I allowed myself the luxury of a curse.

Nobody sane walks east this time of year. The monsoon season was coming—I could read it in the sky the way other people read letters, in the bruised green tint at the horizon, in the way the wind had started arriving in shoves instead of whispers. In three days, maybe four, the coast would turn into a drowning machine. The flats would flood, the roads would vanish, and anything caught between here and high ground would have to choose between swimming and praying.

Vane was heading straight into it. Which meant one of two things: he was desperate, or he knew something I didn't.

I drank sparingly from my waterskin and considered turning back. Considered it honestly, the way you're supposed to. The contract was for return of stolen goods, and the caravan shrine's goods—whatever they were—were now four days east and getting farther. My employer, a fat, sweating factor named Odo Quill, had offered thirty silver for recovery, payable on delivery. Thirty silver was food for a month and a bed that wasn't sand. Thirty silver was also thirty silver, and I'd chased smaller sums through worse country.

But there was something else, and I'm honest enough with myself to admit it, at least when no one's listening. Quill had described the theft twice, and both times his eyes had slid sideways, and both times he'd gotten the inventory wrong in the same direction. First it was candlesticks and offering-plate. Then candlesticks, plate, and "a stone, some old relic thing, worthless, sentimental." Nobody pays thirty silver for sentimental. And nobody robs a roadside shrine at midnight, past three sleeping guards, taking only what fits in a coat pocket, unless what fits in the pocket is worth dying for.

Sable Vane hadn't stolen candlesticks. He'd stolen something small and heavy, and he was running east like a man with an appointment.

I wanted to know why. That was the trap, of course. Wanting to know things is how trackers become involved, and involvement is just stopping with better scenery. But my feet had already decided—they do that, my feet, they're traitors with excellent instincts—and so I followed the bootprints east, telling myself it was about the silver.

The salt crunched under me like old snow. Somewhere behind, the horizon had begun assembling clouds.

* * *

I caught up to him at dusk, or rather I caught up to where he'd been, which is the tracking profession's particular cruelty.

His camp sat in the lee of a salt-crust ridge: cold fire-ring, carefully banked ash—he'd buried it, which meant he feared pursuit and knew firelight carries—gnawed jerky wrappers, and a latrine trench dug with more care than most soldiers bother with. Disciplined. I circled wide, reading the ground around the camp the way you'd read a face, and found what I was looking for on the eastern side: a spot where he'd stood still for a long time, weight settled into both heels, facing back the way he'd come.

Facing west. Facing me, though he couldn't have known it. He'd stood there looking back along his own trail, and the ground said he'd stood there a while.

Men look back for two reasons. Fear, or regret. The prints said his weight had been easy, unhurried—not the stance of a rabbit listening for hounds. Regret, then. Or something wearing regret's clothes.

I built no fire myself. I ate cold flatbread and dried plums and watched the stars wheel up out of the east, brilliant and indifferent, and I thought about doors.

There's a moment, every job, somewhere around the second or third day, when I could still turn around. When the trail hasn't yet committed me, when the quarry is still a shape and not a person, when I could hand the problem back to whoever paid for it and walk away clean. I've learned to notice that moment. I almost never take it. Not because of duty—duty is a word I keep in a drawer with my father's name, rarely opened—but because the alternative to following the trail is standing still, and standing still is where the memories live.

Stop moving, and there's my father on the Council steps, hat in his hands, spine held straight through sheer furious dignity while a magistrate reads out lies in a voice like dripping water. Stop, and there's my mother's ring on the table, catching light it had no business catching. Stop, and there's the rented room, and the landlord's awkward kindness, and the terrible specific silence of a body that has recently stopped being a person.

Movement burns all that as fuel. Grief makes surprisingly good kindling, if you never let it settle into coals.

So: east, then. Toward the storm, after a thief who looked back along his trail with regret, carrying something worth thirty silver and a lie.

I slept in shifts, light as a bird on a wire, and dreamed of water—which I never do, which should have told me something. In the dream the water was rising up white steps in a harbor district I haven't seen in eleven years, and someone was calling my full name across it, the whole three syllables, *Lior Brightwater*, and I woke with my hand on my knife and the eastern sky gone the color of a bruise healing.

* * *

The coast announced itself before I saw it, the way big things do: first a change in the air, salt thickening until I could taste it, then gulls—gulls, inland, arguing over something—then a low gray line on the horizon that resolved, slowly, into the drowned coast.

They call it that because it is. Once there were towns down there, real ones, with harbors and customs houses and all the machinery of lawful trade. Then the sea came up—or the land went down; scholars argue, scholars will argue about anything that lets them avoid honest work—and now the old streets lie under a few feet of water at high tide and a few inches of mud at low, and the people who live out here salvage what the tide gives back. Roof tiles. Door hinges. Occasionally worse.

Vane's trail led straight into it.

I found his last camp on a mudflat where a church steeple leaned out of the muck like a drunk leaning on a wall. This fire was warm. This jerky wrapper was fresh. I was less than an hour behind him, maybe less than half, and the tide was going out, peeling back off the flats in long silver sheets, exposing channels and sandbars and, far out, a dark seam in a headland that might have been rock or might have been something else.

I ran. I'm not proud of running—running is what prey does, and a tracker runs only when she's stopped thinking—but the trail was hot and the sky was going green-black in the east and something in my chest had narrowed to a single point, the way it does, the wanting-to-know sharpening into needing-to-know.

The trail crossed a channel, thigh-deep and running fast with the ebb. I forded it, gasping, and came up the far bank with my boots full of silt and my lungs full of salt, and there the trail ended.

Not faded. Ended. Vane's bootprints marched onto a stretch of bare tidal sand and simply stopped, mid-stride, as if the world had misplaced him.

I stood there dripping, doing the arithmetic again, and the arithmetic refused to balance. No tracks out. No tracks back. Soft sand, fresh prints, nowhere to go. Either the man had grown wings, or—

Or the cave.

I saw it then, out on the headland: the dark seam I'd noticed from the ridge, now exposed by the retreating tide, a black mouth in the rock maybe eight feet above the current waterline. High enough to stay dry. Low enough that the sea closed it twice a day like a fist.

He'd known the tide tables. He'd timed his whole flight to arrive at this stretch of coast at this hour of this day, and he'd walked into a hole in the world that would be underwater again by morning.

I should have waited. Any sensible tracker waits—waits for him to come out, waits for daylight, waits for backup that isn't coming because I don't have any. Instead I stood on that sand with the wind picking at my hair and the storm stacking itself up on the horizon like a debt coming due, and I thought about my father's name, and my mother's ring, and thirty pieces of silver, and I thought: *he looked back with regret, and he hid the thing instead of selling it, and none of that adds up either.*

Two sets of arithmetic refusing to balance. That's usually where the story starts.

Behind me, far inland, thunder cleared its throat. Ahead, the cave mouth breathed out air that smelled of stone and deep water and something older, something like memory given a smell.

I checked my knife, tightened my pack straps, and started walking toward the entrance of a place that opens twice a day and takes payment.

I didn't know yet what the payment was. I'd learn. Everything out here gets learned eventually—the hard way, at cost, in weather.

But that, as my father used to say, is a door for tomorrow.
