## 2. Salt on the Wind

The drowned coast announced itself the way bad news does—gradually, then all at once. First the salt flats went soft underfoot, each step squelching instead of crunching, like the land had decided to stop pretending to be solid. Then came the smell: brine and rot and something older underneath, the scent of a place the sea had claimed once and never quite given back.

I'd been tracking Sable Vane for eleven days. I knew his stride the way some people know a favorite song—long, lazy-looking, deceptively quick. He favored his left foot on downhill stretches, not from injury but from habit, some old wound he'd stopped noticing years ago. He camped cold. He doubled back at least once a day, not out of paranoia but out of craft, checking his own trail the way a carpenter checks a joint. And he was heading, with the single-mindedness of a man who'd stopped caring whether anyone followed him, straight for the water.

Which was strange. Thieves who rob caravan shrines don't usually run toward the sea. The sea is where stolen goods go to be fenced or drowned, and this man had carried his prize—whatever it was, wrapped in oilcloth and guarded like a sleeping child—for a week and a half without once trying to sell it. I'd watched him pass three markets, two smuggler camps, and a fence in Halfway Wells who practically waved money at him. He'd bought dried fish and a coil of rope and kept walking.

You don't steal something you won't sell. You steal something you won't give back.

I tried not to think about what that meant for me, standing in the wet sand with a ranger's badge I'd stopped wearing and a ranger's habits I couldn't.

* * *

The tide flats began an hour before noon, and the world went flat and silver. Water lay everywhere in thin sheets, mirroring a sky that had started the day innocent and blue and was now, at its eastern edge, bruising. I'd seen that color before. Every ranger who works the southern reaches has seen it, and every one of us learns the same lesson: when the clouds go the color of an old scar over the water, you find high ground or you dig in, because the monsoon doesn't knock.

Sable Vane was a quarter mile ahead, a dark smudge against all that shine, and he'd stopped moving.

I dropped flat behind a shelf of salt-cured rock and waited. He stood at the edge of the flats where they met the drowned coast proper—a landscape of half-submerged ruins, chimneys standing in water like drowned men, the roofs of old houses just breaking the surface at low tide. The sea had come inland a generation ago and taken a whole town, and nobody had bothered to rebuild, because the sea, unlike most thieves, doesn't take things once.

He was looking at the water. Waiting for it, I realized. Watching the tide the way I'd watch a trail.

I should have waited for dark. I should have circled wide and come at him from the ruins, where there was cover. I should have done the patient thing, the smart thing, the thing that had kept me alive and invisible for six years.

Instead I stood up and said, "Sable Vane. Ranger's office. You're under citation for theft from a consecrated caravan shrine."

His voice carried back across the flats, dry as the salt I'd just left. "Ranger's office is three hundred miles east, last I heard. And last I heard, the rangers stopped coming out here when the Council bought their commissions."

He wasn't wrong. That was the trouble with this whole coast—nobody was entirely wrong, which made everyone impossible.

I came down the flats toward him, boots sucking at the mud, and he watched me come without running. That bothered me more than running would have. A man who runs still thinks he can get away. A man who stands and watches has already decided something.

"Hand over what you took," I said, when I was close enough to see that his eyes were the flat gray of the sky over his shoulder, "and I'll write it up as recovery, not robbery. The shrine at Karst Crossing serves pilgrims. Old people. You took their offerings."

"I took a rock," he said.

"You took a rock they'd prayed over for forty years."

Something moved in his face—not guilt, exactly. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man who'd expected a different argument and found himself briefly unprepared for this one. "And what would the Harbor Council do with it, if you took it back from me? You know where it'd end up."

"I don't take orders from the Council."

"No," he said, and there was no mockery in it, which was somehow worse. "You just do their work for free."

I'll admit—I lunged. Not my finest moment. Eleven days of salt wind and bad sleep and the growing, itching suspicion that this whole chase was wrong-shaped, and I put it all into a grab for his collar, and he twisted away like he'd been waiting for exactly that, and we went into the tide flats together, and that was when the land itself decided to take his side.

The mud gave under my leading foot. Not soft—*hollow*, as if the flats were a crust over something deeper, and I went down to the knee in cold brine while Sable Vane, lighter than me or simply luckier, scrambled up onto a rib of old foundation stone and kept moving. I hauled myself out and lost my boot to the mud and stood there in one boot and one soaked stocking, watching a thief I'd chased across half a desert get smaller against the drowned coast, and I said a word my mother would have washed my mouth out for.

The first thunder answered from the east.

* * *

I found the fisherman an hour later, or he found me—I never settled which. I'd gotten my boot back and was working my way along the ruins, following Sable's trail through water that rose and fell with a rhythm I didn't know yet, when I heard coughing from inside the shell of a house whose roof had become a reef.

He was an old man laid out on what had once been a kitchen table, and he was dying in the unhurried, practiced way of someone who'd been dying for days and had simply decided to get on with it. His boat—what was left of it—lay keeled over in the shallows outside, hull stove in against a submerged wall. Gulls had opinions about the situation and shared them loudly.

"Water," he said, when my shadow fell across him.

I gave him water from my flask. He drank like it was a job he took seriously, and then he looked at me with eyes gone yellow at the edges and said, "Ranger."

"Retired."

"There's no such thing. You just stop getting paid." He coughed, wet and long. "You're after the Vane boy."

I didn't ask how he knew. Dying men get told things, or they see things, or the coast is simply smaller than it looks. "You've seen him?"

"Saw him head for the cave. Low tide opens it." His hand rose, trembling, and pointed—not at the water but at a headland a mile north, a black finger of rock where the old sea cliffs had held against the drowning. "The one under the point. Opens twice a year, when the tide drops far enough. My grandfather saw it open in his day. Took a whole crew in after salvage." He stopped. The gulls screamed. "Takes payment," he said.

"Payment of what?"

He looked at me for a long time, and I got the distinct impression he was deciding whether I'd earned the rest of the sentence or whether I'd have to earn it myself, the way everyone did.

"Whatever you brought in that you'll miss," he said finally. "The cave doesn't want coin. Coin's already been spent, see. It wants—" Another cough bent him double, and when he straightened, his voice had dropped to the register people use for things they're ashamed to believe. "It wants what's yours. The part of you that you'd grieve. My grandfather's crew came out with their cargo and their lives, and one of them sat on the beach that night and couldn't remember his daughter's face. Not couldn't picture it. Couldn't remember *having* one. He kept asking the others whose child it was in the drawing he'd carried for luck."

The wind had come around while he talked. I felt it on the back of my neck, warm and wrong, the breath of the storm still hours out but already leaning on the coast. Somewhere behind the headland, thunder rolled again, longer this time, like something turning over in its sleep.

"That's a story," I said. "Old men tell stories."

"Old men are the only ones who stay alive long enough to tell you what the coast does." He gripped my wrist, and his hand was cold in the warm wind, which was its own kind of wrong. "The Vane boy went in there. Whatever he's carrying, it belongs in there—that's why he came, that's why they all come, sooner or later. Things the sea gave back." His eyes searched my face. "But the cave takes rent, ranger. And the monsoon's coming, and when it comes, the flats you crossed this morning will be ten feet under, and the low road will be gone, and the only dry ground on this whole drowned coast will be that cave and the high rocks, and you'll wish you'd turned around three days ago."

"Can you walk?" I asked. "I can get you to the headland. High ground."

He smiled, and it was a good smile, unhurried, and it told me he'd already settled his accounts. "I've been high ground all week," he said. "It's the low ground I'm done with." He pressed my flask back into my hands. "Go on, then. But you listen to me first. Whatever's in that cave—and there *is* something in that cave, the boy went in empty-handed and I'd wager the harbor's fortune he doesn't come out the same way—don't touch what isn't yours. The cave knows the difference. It always knows."

I stayed with him until the coughing eased, which took a while, and then I stayed a while longer, because leaving a dying man alone on a kitchen table in a drowned house felt like a thing I'd have to remember later, and I was trying very hard to be a person with fewer memories, not more.

"I didn't get your name," I said, standing.

"Doesn't matter." He waved a hand at the ruins, the flats, the bruising sky. "The sea took my name with the rest of the town. Ask it, if you want it back."

* * *

I reached the headland as the light went strange.

That's the only way I can describe it, and I've had years to find a better word: the light goes *strange*, thick and yellow-green, shadows pointing the wrong way, everything looking slightly underwater even before the water comes. The wind had teeth now. It came off the sea in long shoves, and out past the point the swells had stood up and begun to march, gray ranks of them, and the thunder had stopped being weather and started being a voice.

Sable Vane's trail led up the headland and stopped at a shelf of black rock above a hollow where the tide, even now, was pulling back further than any tide I'd seen. It went out and out and out, exposing a seabed of stone and weed and the ribs of something that might have been a ship or might have been architecture, and at the base of the headland, where no entrance had been an hour before, there was a mouth.

A cave mouth, tall as a temple door, breathing cold air.

I stood on the shelf with the wind trying to peel me off the rock and looked at that opening, and I thought about a dying man's yellow eyes, and a crewman asking whose child was in his luck-drawing, and a thief who'd carried a rock for eleven days without selling it, and I thought: *this is where you turn around, Lior. This is the part of the story where the sensible ranger goes back east and files a report nobody reads.*

But here's the thing about turning around. Turning around is all I've done for six years. Turned away from the coast, from the name Brightwater, from the trial my father never got and the shame I never earned and the mother who stopped writing letters I stopped answering. I kept moving because moving was easy, because the horizon never asked questions, because a woman alone on a salt flat is nobody at all, and nobody can't be ruined twice.

And Sable Vane had said one true thing to me on the flats, the kind of true thing that gets under your skin like a splinter: I did the Council's work for free.

The first rain hit while I was still deciding—fat, warm drops the size of coins, spattering the black rock, and behind them the storm came up the coast like a debt collector. I had maybe an hour before the low road drowned. Maybe less. The cave would flood with the tide and the surge both, and whatever was inside it would be sealed away until the sea changed its mind, and I'd spend the monsoon on a rock in the rain, alone, invisible, safe.

I went in.

The passage sloped down and back, dry stone underfoot, the storm's voice cut off behind me as if a door had shut. It was dark, and then it wasn't—far ahead, deep in the rock, something gave off a faint gray glow, like moonlight that had gotten lost. And the air had a taste to it, salt and something else, something like the smell of a house you lived in before you can remember.

My father's compass, in my breast pocket, was trembling.

Not from my hands. I checked. I stood in the dark of a cave that opens twice a year and takes payment, holding a dead man's compass against my chest, and the needle was spinning, slow and steady, around and around, like it was trying to point at something that wasn't a direction at all.

Down in the glow, a voice said—low, wary, unmistakably the man I'd chased across half a desert:

"I know you're there. I heard you on the rocks." A pause. "You might as well come and see it, ranger. Before the tide comes back and we both find out what this place charges."

I put my hand on the wet stone wall and started down, and behind me, far above, the first true thunder of the monsoon cracked the sky open like an egg.
