## 3. What the Tide Gives Back

The tide went out like a man leaving a party without saying goodbye—one moment the sea was everywhere, and the next it had withdrawn three hundred yards of wet gray sand and left everyone standing around pretending they'd meant to do that.

I stood on the flats with a stolen god in my satchel and watched the water pull back from the drowned coast, and I will be honest with you: for about ten seconds, I considered just walking into what remained of the ocean and letting it sort things out.

Ten seconds. Then my sister's face surfaced in my head—Maren, or what was left of Maren, sitting in her chair by the window with her hands folded like a good little hollow thing—and I turned away from the water and started walking toward the cave.

Because that's the trick of guilt. It doesn't weigh anything until you try to put it down. Then it weighs everything.

* * *

Let me back up, because if this ends badly I'd like some record that I wasn't entirely an idiot, just mostly one.

Three days ago I robbed a caravan shrine on the Salt Road. That's how she'll tell it, anyway—the ranger, when she catches me, which she will, because she's been behind me since the second day out from Greywater like a bad debt. She'll say I desecrated holy ground. The Council will say worse. They've got pamphlets.

Here's what actually happened: the shrine at Kiln's Rest wasn't a shrine anymore. It had been converted two years ago into a collection point—you could tell by the iron lattice bolted over the altar, the kind they use to hold people still while the collectors work. The "offerings" in the coffer weren't coins. They were memory-stones, dozens of them, each one dull as river gravel and humming faintly, the way a struck bell hums after everyone's gone home. Somebody's wedding day. Somebody's mother's voice. Somebody's whole childhood, probably, scooped out and boxed up for transport to the Harbor Council's vaults, where they feed the harvested years back into the Tideheart so it stays fat and obedient.

I took all of them. And I took the stone they were meant for—the Tideheart itself, which the caravan was carrying inland under guard because apparently even the Council worries about leaving their favorite weapon lying around where honest people might trip over it.

So yes. Thief. Desecrator. Whatever word makes them feel better about hunting me. I've stopped arguing about vocabulary. When you steal a knife out of a hand that's using it on children, the hand gets a vote too, and the hand always calls it theft.

* * *

The problem with stealing a weapon made of other people's memories is that it doesn't want to be hidden. It wants to be held.

All three days of the run south, the Tideheart sat in my satchel doing something I can only describe as *leaning*. Not heavy, exactly—heavier than its size, but not crushing—but present, the way a stranger standing too close behind you is present. And every time I slept, which was rarely and badly, I dreamed in fragments that weren't mine: a kitchen with blue tiles, a woman laughing at something off to the left, salt bread rising under a cloth. Someone else's kitchen. Someone else's laugh. The stone leaked, and I was downwind.

By the time I reached the drowned coast, I'd stopped sleeping altogether, which is why the fisherman found me sitting on a wrecked hull at dawn, talking to myself, or possibly to the stone, and honestly the distinction had gotten thin.

He was old and half-blind and he mended nets with fingers gone stiff as driftwood, and he looked at me—at the satchel, specifically, because everyone looks at the satchel eventually, the way dogs look at the meat—and he said, "You'll want the cave."

"I don't want anything," I said, which was a lie so obvious we both ignored it.

"Cave opens twice a year," he said, tying off a knot with the patience of a man who had outlived every hurry he'd ever felt. "Tide takes it back twice a year too. Takes payment." He didn't look up. "Everything out here takes payment. Question is only whether you pay before or after you understand the price."

"Is that a riddle?"

"It's a coast," he said. "Riddles are for people with time."

Then he told me where it was, which surprised me until I understood: he wanted the stone off the beach. Whatever he'd seen in his long half-blind life, he'd seen enough of the Council's collectors to know what traveled in iron lattices, and he wanted no part of whatever came looking for it. Kindness and cowardice wear the same coat sometimes. I try not to grade them.

* * *

The tide finished its retreat an hour past noon, and there it was.

The maps call this stretch of coast the Drowned Reach, which is the cartographers being poetic about the fact that the sea took three towns in the great surge forty years ago and never gave them back. What the tide exposes twice a year is the tops of those towns—chimneys, gable-ends, a bell tower leaning like a drunk against nothing—and between two of the drowned rooftops, revealed now as a black seam in the rock, the mouth of a sea cave.

It didn't look like much. A gap maybe eight feet wide, running back into the cliff, its floor ribbed with sand and old shell. Water dripped somewhere inside with the regularity of a patient clock. The air coming out of it was cold and tasted of deep stone and old salt, and underneath that—faintly, unmistakably—it tasted of the same thing the Tideheart tasted of. Memory. The cave and the stone recognized each other. I don't know how else to say it.

"You're not a temple," I told the cave. "Don't get ideas."

The cave declined to answer, which I appreciated. It was the most respectful conversation I'd had in days.

I went in sideways through the gap, satchel clutched to my chest like a baby, which—fine, yes, it had become a baby, three days of carrying a thing that dreams at you will do that. Inside, the passage opened into a chamber tall enough to stand in, lit by a shaft of gray light falling through a crack in the ceiling, and the floor of the chamber was—

Chairs.

Rows of them, carved from the living rock, worn smooth by water and time, arranged facing a low stone table like a congregation. Or a classroom. Or a courtroom. On the table lay a depression exactly the size and shape of the Tideheart, and I want you to understand that I did not put the pieces together slowly. The cave had been built for this. Or grown for this. The drowned towns hadn't just lost their homes to the sea; they'd lost something they'd been keeping here, and the sea had kept it safe the way the sea keeps everything—without opinion, without mercy, waiting.

"Well," I said to the empty chairs. "This is either exactly right or exactly wrong, and I genuinely cannot tell."

I set the satchel on the table. I took out the Tideheart.

Up close, unwrapped from its oilcloth for the first time since Kiln's Rest, it was almost disappointing—a fist-sized stone the color of wet slate, featureless except for a fine spiral groove cut into its surface, like a fingerprint, like a shell cut open. It should have weighed nothing. It weighed a town. I could feel the harvested lives pressing against the inside of it the way minnows press against the inside of a net—not struggling, exactly. Waiting. That was somehow worse.

And beneath the waiting, faint as breath on glass, I heard—or remembered hearing, the border had gone soft—a woman laughing at something off to the left.

"Not yet," I told her, and my voice cracked on it, and I hated that. "Not yet. But soon. I promise you soon."

I set it in the depression. It fit the way a key fits, the way a body fits a grave, and the moment it seated itself the whole chamber exhaled—a pressure change, a sigh in the stone—and the dripping somewhere in the dark fell silent for exactly three heartbeats, then resumed.

Payment, the fisherman had said. I stood there with my palms flat on the cold table and waited to find out what I'd just paid.

What I paid, I think, was this: I forgot the name of my childhood street. Just—gone. Not faded; *gone*, a clean absence, like a tooth pulled. I knew I'd had a street. I knew it had a name. The name itself was a hole shaped like a name.

I stood in the cave and laughed until it turned into something else, because of course. Of course. You want to hide a thing that eats memory inside a place that eats memory, and the place charges admission, and nobody posts the price until you're already through the door.

"Fine," I said, wiping my face. "Fine. I wasn't using that street anymore."

* * *

Here's the part I haven't said yet, the part I've been circling like a gull over a dead fish:

I knew she was behind me.

The ranger. Brightwater—I'd heard the name whispered at a waystation two nights back, a rider asking questions with a ranger's warrant and a ranger's eyes, and the description had fit me so precisely I'd laughed: *tall, dark-haired, carries a green satchel, moves like a man apologizing to the ground.* That last one stung. I don't apologize to the ground. I nod to it. There's a difference.

She was good. Better than good—she read sign I didn't know I was leaving, closed a half-day lead to nothing in two days of hard going across the salt, and would have taken me at the flats this morning if the tide hadn't turned the whole landscape into a maze of channels and mirage. I'd watched her from a drowned rooftop, a small figure in a dust-colored coat moving along the channel edge with the economy of someone who has never once wasted a motion, and I'd thought: *there it is. That's what competence looks like when it's aimed at you.*

And here's the thing—I didn't run.

I could have. The flats go on for miles, and I grew up on water; give me a channel and a fog bank and I'm a rumor. But I stood on that roof with the stone humming against my hip and I thought about Maren again, and about the collector's smooth voice explaining to me, four years ago, with genuine warmth, that the procedure was painless and voluntary and *for the good of harbor stability*, and about the look on Maren's face afterward—that pleasant, empty, waiting look, like a house with all the lamps lit and nobody home—and I thought: *let her come.*

Not because I wanted a fight. I'm not built for fights; I'm built for knots and lies and getting out of rooms. But because a ranger with a warrant is a witness, and a witness who sees what's in this satchel—who understands what the Council does with it—might stop being a ranger with a warrant. Might become something more useful. An ally. Even an accomplice.

Or she might arrest me and drag me and the stone both back to the Harbor Council in chains, in which case I'd have delivered their weapon right to their door with a bow, and Maren would sit by her window forever, and the woman in the stone would laugh at something off to the left for no one, forever.

Fifty-fifty. Maybe sixty-forty the bad way. I've made worse bets with less information, though admittedly not many and not recently.

So I left the Tideheart in the cave, in its cradle, among the drowned congregation's chairs, and I walked back out into the gray afternoon light with my hands empty and my head full of holes, and I climbed onto the highest surviving chimney of the drowned town, and I waited for the ranger to arrive.

* * *

She arrived at dusk, which felt theatrically appropriate.

I watched her work the last mile of flats the way you'd watch a surgeon—she read the channels like text, avoided the two sinkholes I'd have bet money on, and paused once at the fisherman's abandoned net-mending spot, crouching, touching the sand, reading a conversation I'd had there hours ago. Then she straightened, looked directly at the cave mouth—directly, no searching, no doubt—and started toward it.

Which meant she knew. Which meant the fisherman had talked to her too, or she'd read the whole coast the way I read weather, or—which occurred to me with a slow, cold delight—she'd been close enough all day to watch me carry the stone in. She hadn't been chasing me. She'd been *herding* me. Letting me lead her to whatever I was protecting, because a thief runs from what he stole but guards what matters, and she'd known the difference.

I should have been furious. Mostly I was impressed, and a little bit in love, in the strictly professional way you fall a little bit in love with anyone who turns out to be better than you at the thing you pride yourself on.

She stopped at the cave mouth. I saw her silhouette against the gray water, one hand on her knife, and I saw her look at the black seam of the entrance for a long moment—weighing it, the way you weigh a doorway when you suspect the room behind it knows your name.

Then, quietly, in a voice pitched to carry across the flats and no farther, she said: "I know you're watching. Come down. We should talk before the tide comes back."

Not *surrender.* Not *you're under arrest.* *We should talk.*

I sat on my chimney in the dying light with a hole where my childhood street used to be and a stolen god sleeping in the dark below me, and I thought about Maren's empty, lamplit face, and about the woman laughing off to the left, and about how long a person can stay invisible before invisibility stops being safety and starts being the whole of you.

The first storm clouds were stacking up on the southern horizon, purple-black, orderly, patient. Monsoon season, arriving on schedule. In a day, maybe two, this entire coast would be underwater and unfindable, and whatever happened in that cave would happen with no witnesses but the drowned.

I stood up on the chimney, arms spread slightly for balance, and called down:

"Fair warning—it takes payment in there. And I'm fresh out of things worth losing."

The ranger looked up at me across the darkening flats, and even at that distance I could see her decide something.

"Then we'll both owe," she said, and stepped into the cave.
