## 5. Monsoon Warning

### Lior

The storm told me before the sky did.

That's the thing about weather out here—it doesn't ambush you unless you deserve it, and I'd spent eleven years learning to read the small courtesies: the way the wind goes flat and hot an hour before it turns, the smell of salt and rot that rolls in off the drowned coast like a warning from a creditor. By midmorning I knew we were done. Both of us. The thief and me.

I'd had him in sight since dawn, a dark smudge moving along the tide line where the flats gave way to grey water, and I'll admit there was a moment—brief, shameful—when I considered just letting him go. Letting the sea do my work. But a contract is a contract, and more than that, a shrine robbery is a robbery, and I have never been able to stomach the idea of someone taking what isn't theirs and walking away clean. It's the one hypocrisy I've never managed to talk myself out of, given how thoroughly I've walked away from everything else.

So I followed him down onto the flats, and the sky followed both of us.

The first thunder came from the wrong direction—from the landward side, rolling over the salt behind us—and I understood with a cold settling in my gut that the storm wasn't coming toward the coast. It was coming around us. Closing like a hand.

He saw it too. I watched him stop, watch him look back at me across half a mile of wet sand, and even at that distance I could read the arithmetic on his face. Run for open water and drown. Run inland and meet me. Stand still and be buried.

He chose the third option, which told me something about him. Thieves who panic run. This one stood very still, one hand shading his eyes, watching the clouds stack up black over the salt like a wall being built by impatient gods.

I closed the distance because closing distances is what I'm for.

"Bad day to be a criminal," I said when I got close enough to shout.

"Bad day to be anyone." His voice carried better than I expected—low, unhurried, the voice of a man who'd already done his panicking somewhere private. Up close he was younger than the reports suggested and more tired. Salt crusted his sleeves. "You're the ranger."

"I'm nobody."

"You're the ranger," he repeated, with the mild stubbornness of a man stating facts at a funeral. "Brightwater. You've been on me since the shrine. Three days. You walk like you're apologizing to the ground for touching it."

There was no good answer to that, so I didn't give one. The wind picked up, carrying grit, and I turned my face from it and did the thing rangers do instead of talking: I looked at the water.

It was wrong. That's the only word. The tide should have been turning, should have been crawling back up the flats toward us, and instead it was pulling away—fast, farther than any honest tide goes, exposing ribbed sand and dead coral and things I didn't want to name. A tidal bore builds up when a monsoon shoves the whole sea ahead of it like a fist. When it comes ashore, it doesn't come as waves. It comes as a wall.

"We need high ground," I said. "Now."

"There isn't any."

"Then we need to not be here when the argument between the sky and the sea gets settled." I pointed south, where the coast rose into broken cliffs—the drowned district, the old maps called it, streets and seawalls swallowed decades ago and left to rot in the spray. "Caves. Overhangs. Anything above thirty feet."

"And if I just walk the other way?"

"Then you'll die tired," I said. "I'd rather bring you in alive. But I'll settle for bringing you in."

Something flickered across his face—not fear exactly. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man hearing his own death described in reasonable tones and finding the reasoning sound.

"Sable Vane," he said, offering his hand like we were meeting at a market fair.

"I know your name. I've been chasing it for three days."

"Then we're introduced." He didn't take offense at my refusal to shake. He just turned south and started walking, and after a moment of hating myself for it, I followed.

Because here is the truth I don't say out loud: the storm didn't care about my contract. The storm would kill him and me and every living thing on these flats with the same indifferent thoroughness, and all my grim efficiency, all my years of outrunning my own name, amounted to exactly nothing against a wall of water. There are moments when the world strips you down to the simple animal question—*live or die*—and in those moments, pride is just weight you're carrying for no one.

We ran. We found a seam in the cliff face—a collapsed cellar, maybe, some drowned merchant's storage, its stone mouth choked with old netting—and we wedged ourselves into it while the bore came ashore behind us with a sound I will hear until I die. Not thunder. Not surf. A long grinding roar, like the coast itself being chewed.

The water reached twenty feet up the rock and then, grudgingly, fell back.

When it was over, Sable Vane sat in the dark beside me, soaked, shaking, and laughing—a thin, cracked sound with no humor in it.

"Well," he said. "I suppose you've caught me."

### Sable

Here's what they don't tell you about stealing something important: afterward, everyone wants to know why, and none of them will believe the truth.

She believed it faster than most, which unsettled me more than the flood had.

We'd gotten a fire going—her doing, mostly. The woman carries flint and oilcloth and dried kelp tinder like other people carry regrets, everywhere, always, and within ten minutes of the water retreating she had a flame going in a hollow of stone that even the driving rain couldn't find. She moved like someone who'd been hungry and cold enough times to treat survival as a craft rather than luck. I respected it. I also noted, professionally, that respect makes you sloppy around people like her.

"So," she said, feeding the fire a slat of driftwood. "The shrine. The Tideheart. Talk."

"It's not what the Council says it is."

"The Council says it's a relic stolen from public trust."

"The Council says a lot of things." I held my hands to the fire. They were still trembling, and I let them, because hiding it cost energy I needed. "What did they tell you about the job? When they posted the bounty."

"That a thief robbed a caravan shrine on the salt road. That the artifact is Council property. That you're dangerous."

"I am dangerous," I said. "Just not to you." I turned my hands over, watching the firelight move on my palms. "Ask yourself this, ranger. If the Tideheart were worth coin—if it were gold or gems or anything a fence could touch—why would I carry it through open desert for six days instead of selling it in the first port town? Why would I run *toward* the coast the Council controls instead of away from it?"

Her eyes narrowed. She had good eyes, sharp and grey, the kind that made you feel audited. "Because you wanted to be caught."

"No. Because I wanted it somewhere they couldn't reach it, and the only place on this coast they never look is the place they're afraid of." I leaned forward. "You've heard the stories. The cave that opens twice a year. Takes payment."

"Fisherman's nonsense."

"The fisherman wasn't wrong, though. Was he?" I watched her jaw tighten, and I knew I'd scored somewhere. "You talked to someone dying on those flats. I saw the gulls circling. What did he tell you?"

For a long moment she said nothing. Rain hammered the rocks outside our little stone throat, and the fire spat, and I thought she wouldn't answer. Then:

"He said the cave opens twice a year and takes payment. He said his brother went in during a spring tide thirty years ago and came out without his wedding day. Without knowing his own daughter's face." Her voice stayed flat, ranger-flat, but her hands had gone still in her lap, and stillness, in her, meant feeling. "Superstition."

"Is it?" I reached into my coat—slowly, showing her every movement, because I had no interest in dying over a misunderstanding—and drew out the Tideheart.

It looks like nothing. That's the joke of it, the obscenity of it. A dull grey stone the size of a fist, sea-worn, unremarkable. You could kick it down a street and no one would look twice. And inside it—

Inside it are four hundred people's lives.

"Do you know what memory-harvesting actually looks like?" I asked her. "Not the pamphlets. The practice."

"I know the Council taxes 'surplus recollection.' Voluntary. Compensated."

"Voluntary." I laughed, and it came out ugly. "My sister signed a harvest contract because the collectors came to our quarter the winter the fish failed and offered bread credits. She thought she was giving up a bad year. A few hard memories, she said. The hunger, mostly." The fire cracked. I fed it another piece of wood so my hands would have something to do. "They took her wedding. They took our mother's voice. They took eleven years, ranger—eleven years of her, gone into this stone, and what's left walks around our mother's house smiling at strangers and asking me if we've met."

Silence. The rain filled it.

"They keep the memories in the Tideheart," I went on, quieter now. "And they use it. That's the part the pamphlets leave out. A councilman can hold this stone and *borrow*—put a harvested memory into a witness's head, make a man remember a crime he never committed, make a crowd remember a riot that never happened. Why do you think the Harbor Council has never lost an election? Why do you think your father—"

"Don't." The word came out fast and hard, a blade leaving a sheath.

"—was ruined," I finished anyway, gently, because some things need saying even when they get you hit. "Why do you think a respected harbor magistrate with twenty years of clean ledgers suddenly turned up corrupt overnight, with a dozen witnesses who all remembered, clearly, specifically, conveniently, the same bribes?"

She was on her feet. I don't know when she stood; she was simply standing, the way weather stands. The firelight made her shadow enormous on the stone behind her.

"Careful," she said.

"I'm being extremely careful. I'm telling you the reason I stole that stone is that I found out what it was for, and I could not—" My voice cracked and I hated it. "I could not sit in a room with my sister and watch her ask my name again. So yes. I robbed their shrine. Arrest me. But understand what you'd be handing back, and to whom, and what they'll do with it. Understand that every person whose life is in this stone stays hollow as long as the Council holds it."

She stood there a long time. Outside, the storm threw itself against the coast like something trying to get in.

"My father died disgraced," she said finally. "Two years after the trial. Drink, officially." Her thumb moved against her fingers, worrying at nothing. "I was nineteen. I left before the funeral. I've been running contracts on the salt ever since—anything that keeps me moving, anything that means I never have to stand still long enough for anyone to remember the name Brightwater."

"Why tell me that?"

"Because you asked me to understand something," she said, "and it seemed only fair to tell you what I understand." She sat back down across the fire from me, deliberate, controlled, a woman choosing her seat the way other people choose their battles. "I can't verify any of this. You understand that. For all I know you're the finest liar on the coast."

"For all you know."

"But the Council wants you badly enough to post a bounty through three territories, and Councils don't spend that kind of money on thieves who steal candlesticks." She stared into the fire. "And the stone hummed when I touched it earlier. On the flats. When you dropped the pack."

"I didn't drop the pack."

"You dropped the pack," she said, with the serene confidence of someone who has tracked men across deserts for a living, "when the gulls spooked you, and I searched it while you were climbing the dune, and the stone hummed against my palm like a struck bell that hadn't finished ringing. So either it's enchanted, or it's cursed, or you're telling the truth. All three options are terrible."

Despite myself—despite everything—I felt my mouth twitch. "Welcome to my last six days."

"Here's my problem, Vane." She leaned forward, and the firelight moved in her eyes. "I took a contract. I don't break contracts. It's the only rule I've kept in eleven years, and rules are load-bearing when everything else is rubble. But." She held up a hand before I could speak. "*But.* Contracts assume the bounty poster tells the truth, and if you're even half right, this one doesn't. So here's what I can offer, and it's the best you're getting."

"Name it."

"The season's breaking early and it's breaking mean. That was the leading edge today; the real monsoon arrives tomorrow or the day after, and when it does, nothing moves on this coast for weeks. Your boat—"

"You've seen my boat."

"Everyone's seen your boat. It's visible from orbit, presumably, and it's rotten. I checked the hull while you were burying the pack. You've got seams opening along the starboard side that I could put my whole hand into, and I'm not a shipwright, but I know rot when I see it, and that keel is soft as bread. You cannot make open water in that thing. Not tomorrow. Not ever, probably."

"She's a good boat," I said, with dignity.

"She's a coffin with delusions."

"And your alternative, ranger? Walk inland? Through the flooded salt with a monsoon coming? You said it yourself—nothing moves."

"Nothing moves *alone*," she said. "Which brings me to the bargain." She turned her hands to the fire again, and when she spoke, each word came out like something pried loose with tools. "You know this coast. You know where the coves are, which settlements will shelter strangers, which tides are liars. I know how to survive anywhere, how to read weather, how to find food and water and high ground, how to keep two people alive when the world is actively trying to fix that. So. Until the season breaks—you help me survive your coast, and I help you survive generally. And when the storms end—" her jaw worked, "—we settle the question of the contract. Properly. With whatever the truth turns out to be."

"And if the truth is what I've told you?"

"Then I'll decide what to do about it," she said, "and it won't involve dragging you to the Council in chains. That's the farthest I can reach from where I'm sitting. Take it or swim."

Outside, the rain redoubled, as if casting its vote.

I looked at her across the failing fire—at this woman who had chased me across a desert, who had searched my pack and read my boat's bones and spoken her father's disgrace aloud to a stranger for the first time in eleven years, all in the space of one drowning afternoon—and I thought: *she's the most dangerous person I've ever met, and the safest.*

"Take it," I said.

We shook hands across the fire, hers dry and callused, mine still shaking from the flood, and neither of us pretended it was a friendship. It was a truce. Truces are how everything important starts.

She banked the fire low. I spread our wet clothes on the stones. Somewhere above us, the storm gathered its strength for the night, and I lay back against cold rock with the Tideheart's weight against my ribs and thought about my sister, and about whether hollow people feel themselves filling back up when their memories come home—or whether they just wake up grieving.

"Vane." Her voice, from the dark. Half-asleep, almost.

"Mm."

"That fisherman. Before he died. He said the cave takes payment." A pause, long enough that I thought she'd drifted off. Then: "Payment of what?"

I stared at the ceiling of our stone cell, listening to the sea grind against the coast, and told her the truth.

"Memories," I said. "I think it takes memories."

The fire settled. The rain went on. And neither of us slept.
