## 6. The Salvagers of Greywater Cove

The boat that saved us was held together with prayer and tar, which is to say it saved us the way a bad habit saves you from a worse one—barely, and with complaints.

Sable had called it *the Kindly Tide*, which told me everything I needed to know about his relationship with irony. She wallowed through the swells like a drunk aunt at a wedding, shipping water over the gunwales in quantities that suggested she considered the ocean her natural habitat and us merely guests. I bailed. He steered. Neither of us spoke much, because speaking required breath, and breath was better spent on not drowning.

"Left," Sable shouted at one point, and I bailed left. "Right," he shouted later, and I bailed right. It was the most honest conversation we'd had since the cave.

The storm behind us had teeth but no patience; monsoons are bullies that way. They announce themselves for days, arrive in a fury, and then sulk on the horizon deciding whether to commit. We had bought ourselves an afternoon, maybe two. Long enough to die somewhere new, if we were ambitious about it.

"There." He pointed his chin past my shoulder, because his hands were busy pretending the tiller mattered.

I turned. Greywater Cove didn't so much appear as accumulate—a smear of rooftops made from ship planking and regret, stacked up the cliffside like driftwood arranged by someone with more sentiment than skill. Masts of wrecked vessels leaned against houses as rafters. Nets hung between buildings strung with floats, shells, things that glinted. Smoke rose from a dozen chimneys in colors ranging from honest grey to suspicious green.

"It's a salvage camp," I said.

"It's a home," Sable said, with the defensive tone of a man who has been laughed at before.

"I didn't say anything."

"You made your face do something."

"My face does what it wants. It's the only part of me with tenure."

He almost smiled. Then the cove's mouth swallowed us, the swell dropped away like a curtain falling, and there wasn't room in the boat for smiles anymore, because three figures stood on the landing stage with harpoons, and hospitality on this coast apparently began at spearpoint.

* * *

The woman in front was built like an anchor—broad, rusted, and clearly capable of holding something in place or dragging it under, whichever the situation demanded. The two flanking her were younger, twins by the matching scowls, and they held their harpoons with the easy boredom of people who had done this a hundred times and found it dull nine times out of ten.

"Name your wreck," the anchor-woman said.

"We're not a wreck," Sable said.

She looked at the *Kindly Tide*. She looked at it for a long time. Somewhere behind her, one of the twins snorted.

"Fair," she allowed. "State your business, then."

"Fishing."

"Fishing." She said it the way a magistrate says *perjury*. "In a leaking skiff, ahead of a monsoon, with a ranger aboard."

I kept my face still. "You know me?"

"I know the coat. Desert ranger issue doesn't wash up here by accident. Last one wore it eleven years back, and he left in a box." She spat over the side, a gesture of remarkable precision given the wind. "So. Business."

Before either of us could construct a lie elaborate enough to be insulting, a voice came down from the cliff path—old, cracked, carrying the particular authority of a man who had stopped caring whether anyone listened and thereby made everyone listen.

"Maren. Let them up. If the sea wanted them dead it would have managed it without our help, and if the Council sent them, they're cheaper than spies."

Maren's jaw worked. "Brannoc—"

"The Council sends people in good boats," the voice said, closer now, and its owner rounded the path onto the landing stage. Brannoc was a head shorter than me and approximately twice as wide, with a beard gone white in patches like foam on dark water, and eyes the color of a harbor at dusk—nothing you could read, everything you could drown in. He looked at Sable first, then at me, then, last and longest, at the bundle wrapped in oilcloth lashed inside the boat.

I watched him see it. I watched him decide, visibly, in real time, not to have seen it.

"Well," he said. "Come up before the weather changes its mind. And bring your boat. We'll need the nails."

* * *

Greywater Cove, seen up close, was less a settlement than an argument the sea had lost. Every structure declared some previous catastrophe: a hull become a chapel, a keel become a fence, a figurehead mounted above a doorway with fresh flowers tucked into the crook of her carved neck. People moved through the lanes with the unhurried competence of those who knew exactly where every plank in their floor came from, because they'd pulled it out of the water themselves.

They looked at us. That was the thing I noticed first, and it sat wrong in my chest like a swallowed hook. In the desert, being looked at is a prelude to trouble—you learn to move through towns like smoke, present everywhere, seen nowhere. But these people looked and then went back to their work. A boy mending netting gave Sable a nod of professional sympathy regarding his boat. An old woman hauling a crab pot glanced at my ranger's coat, muttered something that might have been a blessing or an inventory of its resale value, and continued hauling.

Nobody asked questions. After years of living where questions were the local currency, the absence of them felt less like kindness than like a snare set too well to see.

"They're staring," I murmured.

"They're working," Sable said. "Different thing."

"Is it, though."

He gave me a look—the one he'd perfected in the cave, patient as tide, irritating as drip-water—and led me up the cliff stairs to a house built half into the rock, its seaward wall made from the stern of some vessel grand enough to have windows. Brannoc sat inside at a table that had clearly once been a hatch cover, eating stew, and he pushed two bowls toward us without asking whether we ate fish, meat, or each other.

"Eat," he said. "Then you'll tell me why the Council's collectors burned the shrine at Saltmere Crossroads, and don't tell me they didn't, because I've had gulls telling me things all week and gulls are terrible liars but excellent couriers."

I put down my spoon. "Gulls."

"Everything on this coast talks, girl. You just have to know which end of the bird to listen to." He leaned back, chair creaking a complaint older than both of us. "Now. The shrine."

Sable and I exchanged the look of two people discovering they're both unarmed at the same negotiation. It was becoming familiar. I hated how quickly it was becoming familiar.

"A caravan shrine," Sable said carefully, "that the Council maintained. Officially, to bless travelers' journeys."

"Unofficially?"

"Unofficially, it was a relay point. For the harvests." His hand drifted toward the oilcloth bundle at his hip and stopped, the way a man's hand stops when it remembers it's in company. "Memories. Taken from people along the coast and the caravan roads. Stored. Used."

Brannoc chewed. Swallowed. Set down his spoon with the care of a man handling something explosive.

"How long," he said, and it wasn't really a question, but Sable answered anyway.

"Longer than anyone's comfortable admitting. My sister—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "There are people who don't remember their own weddings. Their children's names. Whole years, gone smooth as sanded wood. The Council calls it tithing. Says the coast owes the deep, and they collect on the deep's behalf."

"And you stole their collection box." Brannoc's dusk-colored eyes came around to me. "And you chased him. Ranger business?"

"Personal business," I said.

"Those are usually the same thing wearing different boots." He grunted, which I would learn was his version of applause, a eulogy, and a weather report all at once. "You'll stay the night. Storm's coming back tomorrow, meaner. After that we'll see."

"We can pay," Sable started.

"With what? Your boat?" One of the twins—I never did get them straight until much later; this was Pip, I think, the one with the chipped tooth—leaned in the doorway, arms crossed. "We've got three of those already and none of them float."

"Pip," Brannoc said, mildly, in the tone of a man who has said it ten thousand times and expects to say it ten thousand more.

"We can work," I said.

That got attention. Maren, who had followed us up and filled the second doorway the way weather fills a horizon, raised an eyebrow. "A ranger, working."

"I'm between commissions."

"She's a fugitive," Pip offered helpfully.

"I'm *between commissions*," I repeated, with dignity.

Brannoc laughed then—a sound like a hull taking ground, sudden and final—and waved us both quiet. "Work, then. Maren, put the ranger on the winter lines. The thief goes to the boatyard; he clearly owes the sea a debt of craftsmanship." He fixed us both with the look. "And whatever's in that bundle sleeps under my floor tonight, not under yours. Not because I don't trust you. Because I trust everyone, and I've buried the difference."

* * *

I want to record, for honesty's sake, that I planned to leave that same night.

Old habits don't die; they just wait by the door with your boots. I had the layout mapped within an hour—cliff path east, goat track north, salt flats beyond, miserable but passable ahead of the storm if I walked through the dark and didn't mind the odds. Sable had the artifact, the Council had the coast, and I had a name I'd spent eleven years sanding off every ledger and lintel from here to the Glass Reach. Staying meant being counted. Being counted meant being found. Being found—

Well. My father had been found, once. Look how that ended.

I was halfway to convincing myself—it's a short trip; I know the road—when Wren happened to me.

She was maybe fifteen, all elbows and salt-curl, sitting on the seawall below the guest loft with her knees drawn up, watching the water go black. She didn't startle when I came down the stairs, which marked her as either very brave or very foolish, and on this coast I was learning those were often the same trade.

"You're the one who tracked him across the flats," she said.

"Rumor travels fast."

"Gulls," she said solemnly, and I decided I liked her despite myself, which is the most dangerous way to like someone. "Four days, right? Through the Glasswind, alone, in the heat?"

"Three and a half. There was a well."

Her eyes went wide as coins. "Through the Glasswind with *one well*."

"Wren!" a woman called from a doorway. "Lines!"

She went, but she looked back twice, and I stood on the seawall in the failing light feeling something I hadn't felt in years and couldn't immediately name. It took me until the stars came out to identify it, and I didn't like the answer: it was the feeling of being *looked up to*. Of being, horribly, a person someone might want to become.

People flee lots of things. Very few of them flee being admired. But admiration is a leash, and I'd been off-leash so long I'd forgotten collars exist.

I went back up. I didn't pack. I told myself it was because of the storm, and the lie sat comfortably enough that I slept.

* * *

Morning arrived grey and close, the air thick enough to chew, and the whole cove moved with the brisk urgency of people who had raced this storm before and respected it the way you respect a rival: thoroughly, personally, without affection. Lines came in. Boats came out of the water and up onto cradles. Shutters went up, and stores went down into cellars cut into the cliff itself, dry and echoing, lined with generations of salvaged goods.

I worked the winter lines with Maren and learned three things. First, that she was Brannoc's daughter and disapproved of his generosity with the fluency of long practice. Second, that my hands, trained for rope and reins and rifle-stock, took to nets faster than I expected, and Maren noticed, and said nothing, which from her was practically a parade. Third—that one I learned watching the others, the twins hauling together with a rhythm no one had taught them, the old crab-woman laughing at something the boy with the chipped tooth said, the whole cove moving like one body with many stubborn limbs—third, I learned what it looked like when people trusted each other without contracts.

It looked like a trap. That was my professional assessment. Traps always look like abundance. That's how they work.

At midday, Sable found me mending net on the seawall, and sat, and was quiet for a while, which I was beginning to understand was his native language.

"Brannoc knows the Tideheart," he said finally.

I kept my hands moving. "You told him?"

"He told *me*. Last night, after they took it downstairs. He unwrapped it, looked at it, wrapped it back up, and said, 'Huh. Smaller than the last time I saw it.'" Sable turned a shell over in his fingers. "Lior. He's seen it before. He wouldn't say where, or when. Just—" He mimicked the old man's gravel. "'Some things the sea gives back, and some things it keeps, and the trick is knowing which is which.'"

The wind had died entirely, which on a coast is worse than any blow. The water lay flat and gleaming like poured metal. Out past the headland, the sky had begun to organize itself into something with intent.

"Does that worry you?" I asked.

"Everything worries me. I'm a thief. Worry is the tool of the trade." He tossed the shell into the water. "But he fed us. Gave us beds. Put his people between us and the water and asked for nothing."

"That's what I'm saying. Nobody asks for nothing."

"Maybe somebody does." He stood, brushed off his knees. "Maybe that's the thing you've forgotten how to check for."

He went off toward the boatyard, whistling something tuneless, and I sat with my net and my suspicion and the flat dead sea, and I turned his sentence over like a coin I suspected was counterfeit but couldn't find the seam of.

Below, Wren was showing two smaller kids how to tie a bowline, correcting their fingers with a patience I certainly hadn't shown anyone in a decade. She caught me watching and grinned, unashamed, and went back to her knots.

Behind me, from the cellar under Brannoc's house, faint as a pulse through stone, I heard—or thought I heard—a low hum, like a wet finger circling the rim of a glass.

I told myself it was the wind arriving early.

The wind, when it finally came, said nothing at all.
