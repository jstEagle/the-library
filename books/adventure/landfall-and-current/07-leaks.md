## 7. Leaks

The boat was called the *Kindly Tern*, which was either optimism or a joke, and by the look of her hull I'd have bet on the joke.

I'd won her in a card game two years back from a man who'd won her in a divorce, and I think that told you everything you needed to know about her temperament. She leaked from four places I could find and probably six I couldn't. The portside plank near the stern had gone soft as bread left in the rain, and when I pressed my thumb into it, it gave with the small wet sigh of wood that had given up on ambition.

"You're not fixing that," Lior said.

I looked up. She was sitting on an overturned crate at the edge of Greywater Cove's shingle, sharpening a knife she hadn't needed to sharpen, because that seemed to be how she rested. "I'm absolutely fixing that."

"That plank's rotten through. You'd need to replace it, and there's no timber here that'll fit, and even if there were, you'd need to steam-bend it, and—" she gestured at the sky, at the bruised low clouds stacking themselves over the headland like a debt coming due, "—that."

"That," I agreed. "Which is why I'm fixing it badly and calling it fixed."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth. Not a smile. Lior Brightwater didn't smile, or if she did, she did it somewhere I wasn't invited to watch. But the corner of the mouth was progress. I'd been keeping a ledger in my head, and the corner-of-mouth column was up to three entries in four days.

The salvagers had given us a corner of the cove to work in—a lean-to of driftwood and sailcloth wedged between two beached hulls that had been cannibalized so thoroughly they were more idea than boat. Old Brannoc had looked at the *Kindly Tern*, looked at me, and said, "She'll float if you ask her nicely and don't ask for much," which I'd decided to take as encouragement.

The monsoon was coming. Everyone in the cove talked about it the way farmers talk about a calf they know is going to die. Not if. When. The storm signals had gone up on the headland two days ago—three black pennants, snapping in a wind that had teeth in it now—and every boat that could make open water had already gone. Ours couldn't. So we repaired. Or I repaired, and Lior sat nearby being accurate at me.

"Hand me the pitch," I said.

She handed me the pitch. She'd been handing me things for two days with the efficiency of someone who'd decided, provisionally and with reservations, not to let me die. I'd decided to interpret that as friendship. It was either that or loneliness, and I've never been able to tell the difference from the inside.

* * *

Here's the thing about caulking a boat in a dying light with a storm coming: it gives your hands something to do while your mouth decides what it's going to say.

I'd known for two days I was going to tell her about Mara. I'd rehearsed it maybe forty times, and every version came out wrong—too neat, too sad, too much like a bid for sympathy, which I didn't want. Sympathy from Lior Brightwater would have been worse than contempt, because it would have meant she was looking at me, and being looked at by her felt like being read.

But she'd told me things. Small things, in the way of people who think they aren't telling you anything. That she hadn't been home in six years. That her father had a compass he'd carried on every survey, brass, dented, and that she'd left it behind when she left, because taking it would have meant admitting she was leaving something. That she'd tracked me across two hundred miles of salt flat not for the Council's bounty—she'd made that clear enough with a knife at my throat on the second day—but because the thief had robbed a caravan shrine and someone had to answer for it, and she'd apparently appointed herself the someone years ago and never thought to resign.

And I'd sat there with my secret going soft in my chest like that rotten plank, and I'd thought: *you can't ask her to trust you with the Tideheart if you won't trust her with the reason for it.*

"Tell me about the collectors," I said.

Her knife stopped moving. That was the most still I'd ever seen her, and she was already the stillest person I'd ever met. "Why?"

"Because I'm going to tell you about my sister, and I'd like to not be the only one bleeding."

She didn't say anything for a long moment. The wind came down the cove and worried the sailcloth overhead, and somewhere behind us one of the salvagers laughed at something, a bright ordinary sound that felt like it belonged to a different world.

"Harbor Council keeps the peace," she said finally, flat, like reciting. "That's what the proclamations say. Peace costs money. Money comes from tariffs, and from the memory-tithe—voluntary, they call it. Citizens may offer memories to the Tideheart for the common good." Her voice did something strange on *voluntary*, a small bitter bend. "My father was a hydrographer. He charted the coast for them. He found out what *voluntary* meant."

I set down the pitch knife. "What did it mean?"

"It meant the collectors came to your door with a ledger and a polite smile and a list of what the Council required, and if you refused, they came back with soldiers and a different list. He wrote it all down. Names, dates, what was taken. He was going to take it to the capital." She looked out at the water, at the grey line where the sea gave up trying to be a color. "The Council found out. By the time the storm of the scandal passed, he'd lost his commission, his charters, his name. There were people in the harbor town who wouldn't sell him bread. My mother—" She stopped. The knife started moving again, faster. "I was sixteen. I left. It seemed simpler."

"Simpler," I said.

"Running is simple. You put one foot in front of the other and you don't look at anything too long." She glanced at me, and for a second the whole armored face was just a face, tired and young. "You'd know."

"I'd know," I agreed.

* * *

I told it to the plank, mostly. It's easier to confess to something that can't look at you.

"Mara's three years older than me. Raised me, mostly, after our mother went—fever, when I was six. She was the one who taught me knots, and which gulls mean weather, and how to win at cards, though she'd tell you she taught me how to lose at cards, which is the more valuable lesson." I worked pitch into the seam with the blade's flat, pressing it home. "She was a net-mender on the harbor strand. Good at it. You have to be—mending nets is patience wearing your fingers down to nubs."

"What did she give them?" Lior asked. Not gently. Lior didn't do gentle, or did it so rarely it came out sounding like a blade being drawn, but I'd learned by then that the flatness was the gentleness. She was asking the way a surgeon asks.

"Everything, near enough. It started small—she owed harbor fees after a bad season, and the Council had a program, trade memories for debt relief. Everyone did it. It was almost respectable. She gave them the winter she was nineteen. Some festival, I think. Then my mother's funeral, because the grief was *burdensome* and they had a treatment for burdens." My hands had stopped working. I made them start again. "Then it was just—whenever the collectors came by. She'd go with them smiling, because she'd forgotten she had anything to smile about, and she'd come back smiling, and each time there was a little less of her in the smile."

The wind gusted. The sailcloth cracked like a whip.

"The last time, they took her name for us. For me. She knew me the way you know a dog that follows you—not unkindly, but she couldn't have said why she fed me. She'd leave food out and watch me eat it like I was a stray that had turned up." I heard my own voice go dry and light, the way it does when the alternative is not staying dry. "So I stole the thing they were harvesting with. Seemed fair. Seemed *poetic*. I'm told I have a flair."

"You have a flair for getting followed across salt flats by rangers," Lior said.

"That too."

She was quiet a while. When she spoke, her voice had changed—lower, and careful, like she was handling something that might go off. "The Tideheart. When I held it in the cave, it was warm. Like a sleeping animal."

"It does that. It's full of people. That's what warmth is, when you think about it."

"That's a horrible thing to say."

"I've been told I have a flair."

The corner of her mouth again. Fourth entry. I was keeping score because if I didn't keep score of something small, the big things would eat me.

* * *

We worked until the light went from grey to the color of old pewter, and then Brannoc's granddaughter—Wren, all elbows and opinions—brought us a lamp and a pot of fish stew and stayed to watch us eat it with the frank appetite for other people's business that I was starting to suspect was hereditary in the cove.

"Brannoc says your boat's a corpse," Wren announced.

"Brannoc says that about every boat," I said.

"Brannoc's right about every boat." She looked at Lior with the naked hero-worship of a girl who'd watched a stranger gut a fish in four seconds flat and decided she was looking at a legend. "Is it true you tracked him across the Salt Reach without water?"

"I had water," Lior said.

"Is it true you shot a sand-wyrm off a caravan roof at four hundred paces?"

"I've never shot a sand-wyrm in my life."

Wren's face said this was obviously a cover story and she respected the tradecraft. She left eventually, called away by someone bellowing about tarps, and the cove settled into the sounds of a place preparing to be hit—hammers, rope, the low argument of people who'd done this before and would do it again.

"She likes you," I said.

"She's a child."

"She's fifteen and she's decided you're the most interesting thing that's ever washed into this cove. That's a responsibility."

"I'm not responsible for what people decide about me." Lior said it fast and hard, and I heard the thing under it—the same thing that was under everything she said, the flinch from being *seen*, from mattering to anyone, from having a shape in other people's lives that they might notice was gone. I thought about a girl of sixteen putting one foot in front of the other and not looking at anything too long. I thought about Mara leaving food out for a stray.

"Mara used to say," I offered, "that people decide things about you whether you're there for it or not. So you might as well be there."

"Your sister sounds wise."

"She was." I let the tense sit there between us, wrong and honest. "Is. The collectors didn't take all of her. There's a version of her in there that surfaces sometimes—usually when it rains. I don't know why. Maybe water remembers what people can't."

Lior looked at me for a long moment, and I watched her decide something, the way you watch weather change. "The Tideheart," she said slowly. "You said it holds what they took. All of it?"

"Everything that's ever been fed into it. Thirty years of tithe, at least. Whole lifetimes in there, stacked like cargo."

"And it can be—" She stopped. Started again, and her voice had a crack in it she was trying to pass off as gravel. "It can give things back?"

I looked at my hands. They were black to the wrist with pitch, and they'd stopped shaking, which was how I knew how bad the shaking had been.

"I don't know," I said. "I stole it because it was the only weapon I could think of that they couldn't replace. Break it and the harvest's over. That was the whole plan. Break it and run." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "I didn't think about what was *in* it. I didn't think about the fact that breaking it might be—" I gestured vaguely, at the sea, at the storm, at the whole rotten architecture of my cleverness, "—like burning a warehouse full of people to keep it out of slavers' hands."

"Or," Lior said, and there was something in her voice I hadn't heard before, something that had stopped being a ranger tracking a thief and started being a daughter of a ruined man, "or it might be like opening a door."

The lamp flame leaned in the wind. Out past the headland, the clouds had finished stacking themselves and had started, very quietly, to move.

"There's a ledger," she said. "Brannoc keeps a ledger. Wren mentioned it—names, she said. Records." Her eyes had gone bright and fixed, and I recognized the look, because I'd seen it in my own face in the mirror the night I decided to steal the Tideheart. It's the look of a person who has just realized that the thing they lost might not be lost, only *held*, and that held things can be fought for.

"Sable," she said. "Whose name would be in a ledger of the harvested?"

I knew. I knew before she said it, the way you know a wave is coming by the way the water pulls back.

"Whose name," she said again, softer, "would be in it, if the collectors came for a hydrographer's family and took the ones who couldn't run?"

The first rain hit the sailcloth like a thrown handful of gravel, and somewhere out on the darkening water, the sea began to pull back from the shore.
