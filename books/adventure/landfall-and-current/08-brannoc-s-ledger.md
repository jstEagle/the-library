## 8. Brannoc's Ledger

Brannoc's hut smelled of pitch, old rope, and something underneath it all that I couldn't name—salt and iron and time, maybe, the smell of a thing that had been underwater a very long time and never quite dried out. I told myself I'd come looking for a needle. That was true, in the way most lies are true: I did need a needle. The sailcloth on Sable's boat had a tear in it the size of my forearm, and Sable's hands were better suited to pockets than to palm-and-awl work, a fact he'd demonstrated twice that morning by stabbing himself and blaming the awl.

The needle box was on the third shelf, behind a coil of tarred line and a chipped clay jar of fishhooks. And behind the needle box, wedged back where the light from the single window couldn't reach, was a book.

I want to be honest about what happened next, because honesty is the one currency I've never been able to counterfeit. I didn't hesitate. I should have—Brannoc had fed me, sheltered me, asked nothing, and a ranger's first law is that you don't rifle the belongings of the people whose roof you sleep under. But I saw the spine of that book, and something in my chest went off like a snare-trap, all instinct and no thought, and my hands had it open before the better part of me caught up.

It wasn't a book, exactly. It was a ledger. And it wasn't a ledger of salvage, which is what I'd have expected from a man who made his living off the drowned coast.

It was a ledger of names.

Page after page of them, in Brannoc's crabbed, patient hand, the kind of writing that comes from a man who learned his letters late and has never once wasted one. Names, dates, and a third column headed *Taken*, with entries like *harvest, full* or *harvest, partial—childhood only* or, in one case that stopped my breath, *everything but her name. They left her the name. Cruel, that.*

I knew what I was looking at. Sable had told me about the harvests—how the Harbor Council's collectors went door to door in the river towns with their truce flags and their ledgers of their own, and how they took memories the way a tithe-collector takes grain. A tenth, they said. For the good of all. Except the tenth was never a tenth, and the good was never for all, and the people who came out the far side of a collector's visit were quieter, smoother, like stones the river had been at for a century. Sable's sister had been one of them. He'd told me that in the cave, in the dark, in the voice of a man handing over a wound to see if the other person would hand one back.

I had handed one back. I'd told him about my father, about the trial that wasn't a trial, about the name Brightwater going from a byword for honest survey work to a curse you didn't say in front of Council men. What I hadn't told him—what I hadn't told anyone in eleven years—was about my mother.

Because my mother hadn't died. That was the story we told, my father and I, and it was easier than the truth. The truth was that she'd been called to the Council House one autumn morning to answer questions about a shipment discrepancy that didn't exist, and she'd come home three days later walking like a woman on ice, and she'd smiled at me at supper and asked my name.

She'd asked my name.

My father had carried her to the Council House the next day, demanded her back, demanded to know what they'd done, and they'd shown him a document with his wife's own signature on it—her hand, her ink, consenting—and my father had spent the rest of his life and every coin we had trying to prove it was forged, and the proof had destroyed him instead, and I had learned the lesson that has kept me alive ever since: *keep moving, keep your head down, and never, ever be seen.*

I sat down on Brannoc's floor with the ledger in my lap because my legs had decided the matter without consulting me.

The names were organized by town. Greywater first, then the river settlements up the delta, then—my finger stopped on the tab, a scrap of oiled leather glued to the page edge—*Saltmere*. My town. The one I'd been born in and fled from and not gone back to in eleven years, because going back meant being seen, and being seen meant being known, and being known meant—

My mother's name was the fourth one down.

*Merra Brightwater. Harvest, full. Collector: Vess. Witnessed by her husband, who would not leave the steps. Note: the man remembers. They always remember. It is the ones who are taken who cannot.*

I don't know how long I sat there. Long enough that the light through the window moved from the shelf to the floor. Long enough that my eyes stopped stinging and started aching instead, the way they do when you've been holding them open against the wind. Brannoc had written a note under her name, and I read it maybe forty times, because it was the first time in my life anyone outside my family had written my mother down as a person and not a discrepancy. *Merra Brightwater. She used to sing at the net-mending. The whole cove knew the words. Now nobody does. I am writing them down while I still do.*

And below that, in smaller letters, pressed so hard the nib had nearly gone through the page: *If anyone ever finds a way to give these back, I want it known that I kept the list. Somebody has to remember the rememberers.*

* * *

I don't remember deciding to go to the cave. That's the truth of it. One moment I was on Brannoc's floor with the ledger, and the next I was crossing the shingle with the ledger wrapped in oilcloth inside my coat, and the tide was out, and the cave's black mouth stood open like it had been waiting for me, which I suppose in every way that mattered, it had.

The Tideheart sat on its shelf of stone where Sable had left it, and it looked exactly as unremarkable as it had the first time I'd seen it: a fist-sized stone the color of wet slate, dull, dense, the kind of thing you'd skip across a river if you didn't know better. Sable had said it hummed. I'd thought that was a figure of speech. Rangers deal in weather and tracks and the honest physics of distance and thirst; we don't deal in humming stones.

I picked it up.

It was warm. Not sun-warm—there was no sun in the cave—but the warmth of a held hand, of a cheek you'd pressed against someone's, and the hum wasn't a sound so much as a feeling in the bones of my fingers, in the small bones of my ears, a note so low it was less a note than a presence. Like standing near a herd of animals in the dark. Like the sea deciding whether to come in.

And then I was not in the cave.

I was on a beach at dusk, and a woman was singing, and the whole cove was singing with her, and the words were in a working rhythm, the kind of song you sing to keep forty hands moving together, and I knew the tune. I knew the tune the way you know a thing you learned before you learned anything else, before memory proper, down in the place where the body keeps what the mind is never given. And the woman singing it had my chin. She had my chin and my father's crooked smile and hands that moved net-mending twine like they were spinning thread from air.

*Merra,* someone called, and she turned, and I woke on the cave floor with the stone still in my hand and my face wet and no memory of falling.

That's the part I need to write plainly, because everything after depends on it: I fell. I was standing, and then I was on the floor, and the gap between those two facts was full of my mother's face, and I would have crawled back into that gap if crawling had been possible. The hunger that went through me then wasn't like anything I'd felt before, and I have been hungry in the honest way, the way rangers get hungry, three days into a crossing with the salt flats shimmering and nothing but the thought of water to water you. That hunger has an end. This one didn't. This one opened outward. Every fragment the stone gave me—my mother's song, a kitchen, a man's laugh that must have been my father's younger laugh, a door, a door, always a door with someone standing in it—every fragment was a door, and behind every door was another, and I understood, lying on that cold stone with my heart slamming, that I could spend the rest of my life here. That the stone would let me. That it might even want me to.

And I understood the other thing too, the thing the fisherman had meant when he said the cave takes payment. Because when I came back to myself, I couldn't remember the name of the ranger post where I'd wintered two years ago. I could see the bunk, the frost on the window, the dog the post-keeper had—but the name was gone. Not buried. *Gone*, the way a page is gone after it's been torn out. I'd traded it. Two minutes of my mother, one name I'd never needed anyway. That was the exchange rate.

I put the Tideheart back on its shelf the way you put down a snake—slowly, respectfully, without turning your back—and I sat against the cave wall with my knees up and made myself do arithmetic.

Sable had carried the thing for weeks. He'd handled it, slept near it, hidden it, and he was still Sable—sharp, guilty, whole enough. Brannoc had known of it for years and never touched it, or if he had, he'd paid and kept the receipt. The collectors used it—or one like it—to take. The Council used what the collectors gathered. And the people in Brannoc's ledger, the Merra Brightwaters of a dozen towns, were in here. Not dead. *Stored.* Everything but their names.

My mother might be in this stone. Not her body—her body was a question I still couldn't answer, and was afraid to—but her memories, her song, the crooked smile, the hands. And if the stone could give, it could give everything. The collectors took; the stone, in the right hands, might return.

And the price was me. My memories, traded fragment for fragment, my own past spent like coin to buy back hers. I could come out the far side of this with my mother whole in my head and no idea who I'd been before I started digging. There was a version of that that sounded almost fair. There was a version that sounded like the Council with a kinder face: taking from one person to fill another, the same arithmetic, the same ledger, just with my name in the *Taken* column this time, entered in my own hand.

"Thought I might find you here."

Sable stood at the cave mouth with the grey light behind him, and I realized I'd been sitting in the dark long enough that my eyes had given up on the outside world. He looked at the stone on its shelf, then at me, and his face did something complicated.

"You touched it," he said.

"Once."

"Once is how it starts." He came in and sat down across from me, uninvited, which by then was his privilege. "I carried it for six weeks. I told myself I wasn't using it. I was just *holding* it. And then one night I couldn't remember the color of my sister's eyes, and I knew I'd paid for something, and I didn't even know what I'd bought." He rubbed his face. "What did you see?"

I should have lied. Lying to Sable would have been easy; he expected a certain amount of it from me, the way you expect weather. But I was so tired of the weight of it, and he'd handed me his sister in the dark already, and there is only so long you can hold a wound closed with your own hands before it costs more than the opening.

"My mother," I said. "She's in the ledger. Brannoc's ledger. She's in the stone."

Sable was quiet for a long moment. The tide had turned somewhere behind us; I could hear it starting to come for the cave, patient as a debt.

"Brannoc's ledger," he said at last. "The old man's list. I knew he kept one. I didn't know—" He stopped. "Lior. Listen to me. Whatever you saw, whatever it offered you—it offers. That's what it does. It shows you the door and lets you think the door is yours."

"It's my mother."

"It's a memory of your mother. It's the Council's copy of your mother." His voice was gentle and it made me want to break something. "There's a difference, and the difference is the whole war."

The water was coming. I could see it now, a thin silver tongue reaching up the shingle toward the cave mouth, and I knew how this worked—the cave opened twice a day, and it closed, and whatever was inside when it closed stayed until it opened again. Sable got up and held out his hand, and I looked at it, and I thought about how eleven years ago I had walked out of Saltmere alone and had never once regretted it, because regret requires stopping, and stopping requires being seen, and being seen is the thing I have spent my whole life not doing.

I took his hand. It was the second time, and it was getting easier, and that frightened me more than the stone did.

At the cave mouth I stopped and looked back at the Tideheart on its shelf, dull as river slate, humming its one low note to no one. Somewhere in it, a woman was singing at net-mending, and the whole cove knew the words, and I knew the tune and not the words, and Brannoc had written down what he could remember while he still could.

"Brannoc keeps the list," I said. "Someone has to remember the rememberers."

Sable looked at me. "What does that mean?"

I didn't answer, because out on the flats, coming fast across the shingle with the tide at their backs, were three figures in Council grey—and the one in front carried a truce flag, and the one beside him carried a lantern that burned even in daylight, and the one behind them was a girl with a salvage-hook, running toward the cave with Wren's stride and Wren's reckless, flat-out speed, which meant the collector had not come alone, and he had not come to the cove first, and whatever he wanted, he wanted it from *us*, and he had brought a witness to make sure we couldn't pretend otherwise.

The water closed over the cave mouth behind us like a held breath.
