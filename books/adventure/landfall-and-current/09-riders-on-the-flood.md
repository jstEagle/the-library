## 9. Riders on the Flood

The eye of a monsoon is a liar. It gives you blue sky and warm light and half an hour of believing that maybe, this once, the world has decided to be kind—and then it closes its fist around you again.

I was on the rocks above Greywater Cove when I saw the boat come through the gap in the storm clouds' southern edge, running dark, no lanterns, hull riding low with the weight of men and iron. Council make. Council cut. I'd grown up watching those hulls slide out of Harbor City like knives leaving a sheath, and I had hoped—foolishly, I'll admit—to never see one again.

"Brannoc," I said, not taking my eyes off it.

He came up beside me, slow as a tide going out, and squinted south. For a long moment he said nothing at all, which from Brannoc was a whole speech.

"How long?" he asked finally.

"Eye holds maybe two hours. They'll use all of it."

"They always do." He spat over the rocks, thoughtful rather than angry. "That's a collector's boat. Not a patrol skiff. Somebody wants something in my cove badly enough to sail into a monsoon for it."

Somebody wanted me. Or wanted what I'd carried into his cove wrapped in oilcloth and buried under a false floor in the drying shed, which amounted to the same thing now. The Tideheart sat thirty paces below us, dull as river gravel, humming to itself the way it did when nobody was touching it—a sound I felt more than heard, like a word stuck behind your teeth.

"We can take the boat out," I said. "Lior and I. Draw them off down the coast."

Brannoc looked at me the way old men look at young ones who have just suggested something stupid with great sincerity. "In that leaky tub of yours? In the back end of a monsoon?" He shook his head. "Boy, they'd scoop you out of the water before you cleared the point, and then they'd come here anyway, because boats like that don't chase one man. Boats like that sweep." He turned and started down toward the cove, calling over his shoulder. "Get everyone inside. Get the strangers hidden. And get that stone somewhere even I couldn't find it."

* * *

Hiding Lior Brightwater turned out to be less a task than a negotiation.

She stood in the doorway of Brannoc's longhouse with her arms crossed while Wren and two other salvagers dragged a net-mending bench against the wall and pointed at the crawlspace behind it like it was a palace suite.

"No," Lior said.

"It's dry," Wren offered.

"It's a hole."

"It's a hole where the Council won't look," I said, coming in behind her, dripping. "Which is the only qualification that matters today."

She gave me a look I was beginning to know well—the one that weighed whether arguing with me cost more than complying. It usually did, and she usually knew it, and she usually complied anyway with the air of someone granting a great personal favor.

"I don't hide," she said.

"You hid from me across four hundred miles of salt flat," I said. "Very well, as it happens. I only caught you because you let me."

"That was tactical."

"It was Tuesday. Get in the hole."

What I didn't say—what I couldn't say with Wren standing there, thirteen years old and hanging on every word like I was handing out pearls—was that if the collectors found Lior Brightwater in their cove, no amount of hiding would save any of us. The amnesty offer hadn't come yet, but I knew how these things went. First the boat. Then the flags. Then the name, spoken loud in front of everyone whose goodwill you were borrowing, so that sheltering you stopped being charity and started being a crime.

I'd watched them do it to my sister's village. I wasn't going to watch it happen twice.

Lior folded herself into the crawlspace without another word, which somehow made it worse. A woman who could vanish into a salt flat for weeks chose to disappear into a hole in Brannoc's wall because I asked her to, and she hated it, and she did it anyway, and I understood—I understood better than she probably realized—that this was the first time in years anyone had asked her to stay anywhere, even a hole, even for an hour.

Wren pushed the bench back into place and then stood in front of it, arms spread, grinning like she'd personally built the fortress.

"You should be below," I told her. "Storm cellar with the others."

"I'm the fastest climber in the cove."

"You're the smallest person in the cove. That's different."

"Brannoc says small is a skill."

Brannoc said a lot of things. Half of them were wisdom and the other half were excuses to keep Wren within shouting distance, and I suspected this was the second kind. But there was no time to argue, because that was when we heard the oars.

* * *

They came ashore at the landing beach in the flat gray light of the eye, six of them in matching oilskins, moving with the unhurried confidence of people who have never once been told no by a coastline. At their head walked a woman in a gray coat too clean for the weather, and I knew her shape before I knew her face: a collector. Not the one who took my sister—that one was older, softer, gone to fat on other people's memories—but the same breed. Same walk. Same way of looking at a crowd of people like a ledger with the sums not yet done.

Brannoc met them on the sand alone, hands visible, an old man with nothing to declare.

The rest of us scattered to our work, or to what passed for work when your hands were shaking. I took up a position on the fish racks with a knife and a mackerel I had no intention of cleaning, close enough to hear, far enough to be furniture. Wren perched on the net loft above me, because of course she did; telling her to go below had lasted exactly as long as it takes a girl like Wren to climb a ladder.

"—conducting a census," the collector was saying. Her voice carried the way voices carry when they've been trained to. "Routine. The storm displaced many families along this stretch. We're offering assistance."

"On my beach," Brannoc said.

"The Council's beach, Master Brannoc. You lease it."

"I've leased it twenty-two years and never seen a census in a monsoon."

The collector smiled. I knew that smile too. "Times change. May we see your rolls?"

Brannoc kept a ledger—everyone knew it, it was half legend in the cove, a book of names going back further than the Council itself—but I saw his hand drift toward his coat pocket and stop. Whatever was written in that book, it wasn't for gray coats.

"No rolls today," he said. "Paper's wet. Everything's wet. Come back when the season breaks."

"Then we'll count heads ourselves." She turned to her six. "Pair up. Sheds, boats, dwellings. Anyone not of this settlement comes to me."

And that was the moment the enemy stopped being a story I told myself at night and became six pairs of boots on wet sand, and I felt the fear go through the cove like cold water through cloth—not panic, these were salvagers, they didn't panic, but a stillness, a collective holding of breath, everyone suddenly very busy being exactly who they appeared to be.

The searchers worked methodically. Shed by shed. Boat by boat. I cleaned my mackerel and watched the blade shake and pretended it was the wind.

They found nothing in the sheds, because there was nothing to find; the Tideheart was under the floor of the drying shed beneath a layer of salted fish that would have offended any nose into leaving quickly. They found nothing in the boats except rot, which was mostly mine. And then one of them—young, eager, the dangerous kind, the kind trying to be noticed—turned toward the longhouse, toward the net loft, toward the bench against the wall.

Toward the hole where Lior Brightwater was breathing as quietly as a held note.

I moved before I decided to move. That's the truth of it. There was no plan, no clever Sable Vane scheme with three exits and a fallback. I just put down the mackerel and crossed the sand and planted myself between the searcher and the longhouse door with a smile I did not feel.

"Kitchen's through there," I said. "Fair warning, Brannoc's cooking. The census may wish to reconsider."

He looked at me. Young, I saw now. Nineteen, maybe, with the collar of his oilskin turned up and rain beading on a face that hadn't yet learned what it was for. "Step aside."

"Happy to. Soon as you tell me what the Council pays for searching a fish kitchen in a storm. I've been wondering about wages lately. Been thinking of applying."

"Step aside," he said again, and put a hand on my chest, and shoved.

I went down on the wet planks of the landing, palms stinging, dignity in ruins, and heard above me—God help me—Wren's breath catch in the loft. Small sound. Nothing. Except the boy heard it too, because he was young and eager and listening for exactly that, and his head came up, and his hand went to the coil of rope at his belt, the capture line, the thing collectors used instead of chains because chains left marks and marks raised questions.

"No," I said, getting up. "Hey. No—"

He was already climbing.

* * *

Later, everyone would tell the story differently. Brannoc would say the boy slipped. Wren would say she kicked him, and she'd say it with such relish that no one ever checked. What actually happened, as near as I can piece it together, is this:

The boy got one hand over the edge of the loft, and Wren—who had been told to go below, who had been told small was a skill, who had been told a great many things by a great many adults who then failed to protect her—dropped a full net-weight on his fingers.

He screamed. She scrambled back along the loft beams toward the seaward window, quick as a gull, and might have made it clean away except that the loft's old rope rail chose that moment to give, and she went down with it, catching herself on the splintered frame, hanging above the sand with her feet swinging over nothing.

And the boy, cradling his hand, face white with pain and fury, drew the capture line.

I've thought since about how fast everything happened. How there was time for me to shout and no time for the shout to matter. How Brannoc was ten steps away and forty years too slow. How the whole arithmetic of the moment came down to a rope, a girl, and a throw.

The rope never landed.

It stopped mid-air—or nearly mid-air, snagged, yanked sideways, coiling around the loft post instead of around Wren's throat—and the boy staggered, hauled off balance by his own cast, and Lior Brightwater stepped out of the longhouse door as if she had been standing in a doorway her entire life waiting for precisely this.

Where she'd gotten the belaying pin I never learned. Where she'd learned to throw it, I could guess: rangers learn, the way water learns downhill. It caught him across the wrist and the capture line dropped into the sand, and then she was on him—one motion, economical, almost gentle—folding him down onto the planks with his arm behind him and her knee in his back, and the whole thing was over in the time it takes to tell it.

The cove went very quiet.

Six searchers froze. The collector on the beach turned, slowly, and looked up the slope at a stranger holding down her man, and I watched her do the arithmetic I'd been dreading: unknown woman, ranger's economy of movement, wrong place, wrong time, wrong everything.

"Well," the collector said pleasantly. "It seems the census has found someone after all."

* * *

They took their boy and withdrew to the landing, and for a long moment nobody breathed. Then Brannoc's voice rolled out over the cove, steady as bedrock: "Back to work! Storm's coming back and fish won't salt themselves!" And the cove obeyed, because a lie everyone agrees to perform is still a wall, and walls are what keep you alive.

Lior released the boy last, almost courteously, and straightened, and I saw her understand in the same instant I did what she'd done. She'd hidden. She'd stayed hidden, right up until hiding would have cost a child her freedom, and now she was standing in plain view of a collector with her hood back and her face bare, and there was no un-seeing her.

She walked past me without a glance. I caught her arm anyway.

"You shouldn't have—" I started.

"She was going to hang there and be taken," Lior said quietly, "while I listened from a hole in a wall." Her jaw was tight. "I've been invisible a long time, Vane. Today I found out what it costs."

The collector had stopped at the waterline. She turned back once, and though she was too far to read her face, I felt the look land on Lior like a brand—taking her measure, fixing her in place, filing her away. Then the gray coats pushed off from the beach and rowed back into the growing dark, because the eye was closing, and even the Council respects a monsoon.

We stood on the sand and watched them go, the whole cove of us, and nobody cheered, because we all understood what the retreat meant. They hadn't lost interest. They'd gone to fetch more boats.

Brannoc came and stood between us, looking out at the water, and said, in the voice of a man reading his own ledger aloud: "They'll be back with the next calm. And they'll want the girl in the doorway, and the thief who followed her, and likely my ledger too, and I don't intend to give them any of it." He looked at me then, old eyes gone hard as barnacle. "So you'd best tell me everything, boy. Starting with why the Council sails a war boat into a monsoon for a stone."

Behind us, Wren sat on the broken loft frame, cradling a rope-burned palm, watching Lior with an expression I recognized because I'd worn it myself once, years ago, in a crowd, watching someone stand up to a gray coat and survive it.

The first fat drops of the returning storm hit the sand around us like a countdown.

Lior looked at Brannoc, and then at the sea, and I saw her decide something she couldn't take back.

"It starts with a harvest," she said. "And it ends with my mother."
