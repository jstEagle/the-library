## 10. Payment Due

The fisherman's words came back to me on the third night, the way dying men's words always do: inconveniently, and at the worst possible hour.

*The cave opens twice a year and takes payment.*

I'd filed that under superstition at the time, along with his advice about not whistling at gulls and his firm belief that the moon owed him money. But I'd spent three nights now sleeping within twenty feet of the Tideheart, and I was starting to think the old man had been the only honest accountant on this coast.

It started small. I woke in Brannoc's guest hut—the salvagers' version of a guest hut being a roof that leaked with ambition rather than enthusiasm—and I couldn't remember which boot I'd put on first that morning. Not a remarkable thing, you'd say. Nobody remembers their boots. Except I do. I remember everything. That's the whole architecture of me: a woman built out of remembered details, stacked floor to ceiling like a hoarder's house, because if I hold enough small true things, none of the large false ones can get in.

The boot bothered me all through breakfast. Salted fish again. The salvagers had three recipes for fish and one of them was "raw."

"You look terrible," Wren said, watching me poke at my plate. She had that particular talent of the young, which is to say she said things other people thought and then pretended they hadn't.

"I've looked worse."

"True." She grinned. "You looked worse when you arrived. You looked like something the tide rejected twice."

"Wren," Sable said mildly, from across the fire, "let the woman eat."

He'd been doing that lately. Defending me in small, unnecessary ways, as though we were building something out of these little gestures—a wall, maybe, or a debt neither of us wanted to name. I ate my fish and didn't thank him and noticed that I also couldn't quite remember what we'd argued about two nights ago. Something about the boat. Something about whether he was going to do something reckless. It had felt important at the time. Now it was a shape with no contents, like a jar after the pickles are gone.

That's when I started counting.

*

Here is what a ranger knows about memory that most people don't: it's a trail. Every mind leaves sign, the way every animal does, and you learn to read your own spoor as carefully as you read a stranger's. So that afternoon, while the cove went about its business of hauling salvage and arguing about rope, I sat down with a scrap of sailcloth and a stub of charcoal and wrote down everything I could remember of the last six days. Then I compared it against my journal, because I keep a journal, have kept one since I was fourteen years old, since the week my father's name stopped meaning *harbor engineer* and started meaning *traitor*, and writing things down became the only way I trusted the world to stay where I left it.

Six entries missing. Not smudged, not water-damaged. Missing. My own handwriting simply wasn't there, gaps in the record like teeth pulled from a jaw, and the pages around them were intact. Whatever had taken them hadn't touched the paper. It had touched *me*.

I want to tell you I reacted calmly. A ranger's training is supposed to include calm. But there is a difference between the calm you practice on a hillside with a dead man's killer in your sights and the calm you need when the thing being hunted turns out to be your own past, and I did not have that second kind. I sat in the hut with the journal open on my knees and my hands shaking like a drunkard's, and I did the arithmetic.

Three nights near the stone. Six days of memory gone. The fisherman's warning sitting in my chest like a swallowed hook.

*The cave takes payment.*

And I knew—I knew the way you know a storm is coming before the clouds even gather—that the price wasn't coin or blood. It was never going to be coin or blood. What kind of artifact holds ten thousand stolen memories and charges anything so ordinary?

It took memories. It was taking mine. And somewhere in that dull grey heart of stone, my mother might be—

I made myself stop there. Made myself stand up, roll the journal, tuck it away, walk out into the grey afternoon light and breathe salt air until my hands steadied. There would be time to fall apart later. There is always time to fall apart later. That's practically my family motto.

*

Sable found me at the cave mouth an hour before low tide, sitting on the rocks with my boots off and my feet in a tide pool like a girl on holiday, which fooled absolutely no one, least of all me.

"You've figured it out," he said.

"Figured what out? Say it plainly. I'm told I forget things now."

He flinched. Good. Let him. He sat down beside me, close enough that our shoulders almost touched, and watched the water pull back from the flats in long silver sheets, uncovering the cave's throat inch by inch.

"The collectors," he said. "Back when they worked the harbor districts openly—they used to call the process 'admission.' Like you were checking into a hospital. Like it was a kindness." His voice had gone flat, the way voices go when a man is walking carefully around the edge of something bottomless. "My sister came home from her admission and she smiled at me and asked who I was, and then she smiled at the wall and asked who *it* was. The Council called her cured. Said the harvest had removed her grief." He turned to look at me. "They take the memories first, Lior. Before they take anything else. The stone drinks them out of you. That's how it fills. And whoever carries it—whoever spends too long touching it, sleeping beside it, dreaming next to it—"

"Pays admission," I finished. "In reverse."

"In reverse." He laughed, and there was nothing funny anywhere near it. "I should have told you the first night. I told myself the exposure was small. I told myself a lot of things."

"How much have you lost?"

He was quiet long enough that the tide answered for him, hissing over the flats. "My sister's face," he said finally. "I carried it for eleven years and I've been holding this thing for four months, and yesterday I tried to picture her and got a shape. A shape wearing her voice." He rubbed his eyes with the heel of his hand, hard, like he could scrub the stone's fingerprints off his skull. "So now you know why I stole it, and why I have to get rid of it fast, and why I haven't yet. Because every day I carry it, I pay. And every day it sits in some Council vault, a hundred people in Greywater Cove pay instead."

"There's a third option," I said. "Someone else carries it."

"No."

"Sable—"

"*No.*" Sharper than I'd ever heard him. Then, quieter: "You've already paid six days. You don't get to volunteer for more. That's—" He stopped. Started again. "That's not how this works."

"And how does it work, exactly? Because from where I sit, it looks like the stone eats whoever's nearest, and the nearest person is whoever's brave enough or stupid enough to hold it, and both of us qualify."

"That's not how it works either." He stood, brushed grit from his knees, and looked down at me with an expression I couldn't file anywhere—too tender for anger, too grim for kindness. "Come with me. There's something you need to see before you decide anything else. And Lior—" he hesitated, and I braced, because hesitations before revelations are never good news, "—when you see it, I need you to not touch the stone. Whatever happens. Promise me."

I promised. I'm still deciding whether I lied.

*

Inside, the cave was doing its evening trick: the last light through the entrance catching the wet walls, turning the whole chamber the color of tarnished pewter. The Tideheart sat on its natural shelf of rock near the back—not an altar, whatever Sable's dramatic instincts suggested, just a shelf, though I'll admit the stone had arranged itself there with the smugness of a cat on the best cushion.

Sable lit a lamp. Then he reached into his coat and brought out a bundle of oilcloth, unwrapped it, and laid the contents on the flat rock between us.

Brannoc's ledger. Or a copy of part of it—pages torn free, which explained the old man's fury when he'd caught me reading it two nights ago, a fury I'd mistaken for simple secrecy.

"I borrowed these while you were teaching Wren weather signs," Sable said. "Before you ask—yes, without asking. We're even now."

The pages were a list. Names, dates, districts. Some names had notes in Brannoc's crabbed hand: *taken 9th of Stormmonth, harvest-grade three, returned hollow.* Others had a different note, rarer, written in what looked like a steadier ink: *returned whole.*

And there, halfway down the second page, in my mother's handwriting—or what I had spent fifteen years believing was my mother's handwriting, because I'd copied letters of hers until the shapes lived in my fingers—

*Maren Brightwater. Taken 3rd of Deepwinter. Harvest-grade five. No return recorded.*

Grade five. I didn't know the scale. I looked up at Sable and he read the question off my face the way he was getting annoyingly good at doing.

"One through five," he said. "One is surface memories. A face, a name, a grudge. Two is skill. Three is a year or two of a life. Four—" his jaw tightened, "—four is what they took from my sister. Everything that made her *her*. Five is..." He gestured at the page. "Five is people they didn't expect to survive the extraction. People whose whole selves went into the stone. The Council's records call grade-five subjects 'fully admitted.'"

"Which means?"

"It means the person doesn't come back, Lior. It means everything they were is in the stone. All of it. Not echoes. Not fragments. Them."

The cave was very quiet. Somewhere above us, water dripped with the patience of a thing that has all the time in the world.

"My father," I said, and my voice came out steady, which surprised me, "told me she died of fever. Deepwinter, the year I was nine. He buried—" I stopped. Recalculated. Fifteen years of a story, and it came apart in my hands like wet paper. "There was no body. He said the tide took the coffin. I believed him because I was nine and because he cried at the funeral, and my father never cried, so I thought it must be real."

"He wasn't lying to protect himself," Sable said gently. "He was lying to protect you. If the Council knew Brightwater's daughter knew her mother was harvested—"

"They'd have harvested me too. To tie off loose ends." I looked down at the ledger page, at my mother's name sitting there in her own hand, patient as a seed. "He spent the rest of his life trying to expose them. That's what ruined him. That's what the trial was really about. And he never told me because telling me would have painted a target on a child, and so I grew up hating him a little, for the shame of it, for the running—and the whole time he was carrying this alone."

The strange thing, the thing I keep turning over even now, is that I didn't cry. Grief that old has been wrung dry so many times it comes out as something else—as weight, as heat, as a slow bright pressure behind the sternum like a second heart learning to beat. I set the ledger pages down. I looked at the Tideheart on its shelf, dull as riverstone, ordinary as anything, holding my mother inside it the way amber holds a fly.

Grade five meant everything of her was in there. Which meant she wasn't dead. Which meant she wasn't alive either. She was *kept*—every stubborn, laughing, infuriating year of her, folded into a grey stone in a sea cave, waiting for someone with the nerve and the stupidity to open it.

"She's in there," I said.

"Lior." Sable's voice was careful. Too careful. "What's left in the stone isn't—you can't talk to it. You can't wake her up. It's memory, not a soul. I need you to understand that before you—"

"I understand what you need me to understand." I stood. The stone hummed against my awareness, faint as a struck glass, and I swear it leaned toward me, or I leaned toward it, the distinction having stopped mattering. "You want me to treat her like cargo. Evidence. One more injustice to average out against the others."

"I want you to not destroy yourself reaching for something that isn't there anymore."

"And I want my mother back, and neither of us is getting what we want, so let's skip to the part where we decide what to *do*."

He stared at me. Outside, the tide had begun its turn; I could hear it, a long low gathering breath, the sea remembering its claim on the flats. In a few hours the cave mouth would drown and we'd be sealed in with the thing until morning. With my mother. The thought landed strangely—not horror, not comfort. Proximity.

"Every day that stone stays intact," I said slowly, working it through aloud, because saying things aloud is how I know if I believe them, "the Council hunts it, we hide it, someone pays admission, and Maren Brightwater stays locked in a box. Every day. Forever, if we're cowards about it. Unless—" and here the heat behind my sternum sharpened into something with edges, something with a purpose, "—unless there's a way to open it. All the way. Give everyone back what's theirs. Her included. Even if what comes back to me is only—" my voice caught, and I pushed through it, "—even if it's only her memories finding their way home to wherever home is now. Even if I never hear her voice. It wouldn't be hers otherwise. It's not ours to keep. None of it is. That's the whole point, isn't it? That's what the Council never understood. Memory isn't property. It's *current*. It's supposed to move."

Sable was quiet for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice was rough. "Brannoc will say it can't be done. The stone's held together for two hundred years. Breaking it might release everything, or nothing, or kill everyone standing nearby."

"Then we find out which. Carefully. Deliberately. With a plan." I picked up the lamp. "Because I am done paying admission, Sable. Me, your sister, the whole drowned coast—we're done. The Council built a vault out of other people's lives, and I intend to see it opened if I have to—"

The lamp guttered. Not the wind—the cave mouth was behind us, sheltered. The flame bent toward the shelf, toward the stone, and the hum I'd been feeling rose half a tone, and for one clear cold instant I heard it. Not with my ears. Underneath them.

A woman humming. Off-key. A lullaby about a lighthouse keeper's daughter, the one my father used to mangle at bedtime, the one I'd forgotten I knew until this exact moment, and oh, that was the cruelest cut of all—the stone giving back a memory of hers that had been mine first, sold back to me like a thief returning a purse with the coins missing.

The flame straightened. The hum faded. Sable was gripping my arm, saying my name, saying it a third time.

"Lior. What happened. What did you hear?"

I looked at the dull grey stone, and the grief in my chest finished its long slow turn and came out the far side as something I recognized from my work—from manhunts, from the moment a tracker stops following and starts *closing*.

Purpose.

"She's in there," I said. "And I know exactly how we're going to get her out."

Behind us, at the cave mouth, the sea closed over the threshold with a sound like a door locking—and from the direction of the cove, thin across the water, came a whistle. Three notes. Brannoc's alarm.

Lights were moving on the headland. Lanterns, many of them, in a line too orderly for salvagers.

The Council had found us.
