## 11. Wren

I want it on the record that I asked nicely.

That matters, because Brannoc tells the story later like I ambushed the ranger at knifepoint, and Sable tells it like I tripped over my own feet into a lesson, and neither of them was there for the asking, which happened at dawn, in the rain, with my best manners and everything.

Lior Brightwater was mending a net when I found her. Not because anyone asked her to. Because the net was torn and she was awake and apparently that's what she did with idle hands—fixed things nobody assigned her. She sat on an upturned skiff under the lean-to with the net pooled in her lap like something drowned, working a bone needle through the mesh with fingers that never hesitated. Rain came off the roof in a beaded curtain, and she sat just far enough back that none of it touched her, which told me a lot about how she'd survived this long.

"Wren." She didn't look up. "You've been standing there since before the gull."

"I have not."

"You've been there since the second gull. The first one you were behind the fish racks." She pulled the needle through, snugged the knot. "You breathe loud when you're working up to something."

I breathed deliberately quieter, which proved her point, and she almost smiled—the corner of her mouth doing a thing like it remembered smiling from a long time ago and wasn't sure it wanted to renew the acquaintance.

"Teach me," I said.

"Teach you what?"

"Tracking. Real tracking. The way you found Sable across the salt flats. Brannoc says you read ground like a priest reads scripture. Pip says you're a ghost. Tam says you once followed a man three days by the way his boots wore uneven, and then you sold him back his own horse."

"That last one's exaggerated," Lior said. "It was two days."

My heart did a whole drum solo. "So you'll teach me?"

"No."

Just like that. No. The needle kept moving. The rain kept falling. Somewhere out in the cove the water was doing its gray churning thing, and I stood there feeling about two inches tall.

"Why not?"

"Because tracking's not a trick you learn in an afternoon. It's years of being hungry and cold and alone, and learning to notice things because noticing is all you've got." She finally looked up at me, and her eyes were the flat gray of the sea before weather. "You've got a cove full of people who'd notice if you went missing. That's worth more than any skill I could give you. Don't trade it."

"That's not fair," I said. "You don't get to decide what I'm willing to pay."

Something moved behind her eyes then—quick, like a fish under ice—and I knew I'd said a true thing, because true things are the ones that make people flinch.

"Ask me again tomorrow," she said, and went back to her net, and that was that.

* * *

Except it wasn't, because I'm Wren, and "ask again tomorrow" has never once meant no in the history of our family. Brannoc took me off a wreck-beach when I was six, all ribs and elbows and a name I wouldn't tell anybody, and he learned fast that I don't quit. He says it's my best quality. He says it through his teeth, usually.

So the next day I tried again, and got another no. And the day after that, and by the fourth day Lior had taken to mending nets in different spots around the cove like a woman shaking a tail, which would have been flattering if it weren't so insulting. On the fifth day I stopped asking and started following instead.

Not close. I'd watched her work; I knew better than to crowd a tracker. I kept the fish racks between us, kept my steps on stone where they wouldn't print, kept my breathing even. She went up the goat path at the cove's east lip in the gray hour before sunrise, the hour she seemed to like best, when the monsoon clouds hung low and fat and the whole world smelled like iron and salt.

She stopped at the top of the path without turning around.

"The third gull," she said. "You breathe loud when you're pleased with yourself."

I came up beside her, grinning so hard my face hurt. She looked out at the water and didn't grin back, but she also didn't tell me to go away, and in Lior-language that was practically a parade.

Below us the cove lay in its bowl: the salvagers' huts with their patchwork roofs, Brannoc's great hall built from half a ship, the drying racks, the boats hauled up above the tideline. Smoke stood up straight from the cookfire and then bent east, hard, all at once.

"There," Lior said quietly.

"Where? What?"

"Look at the smoke. Then look at the water. Then tell me what tonight looks like."

I squinted. The smoke had gone from standing to streaming, a white banner snapping east. Out past the headland, the sea had changed color overnight—that bruised green-gray it gets, and the swells coming in were longer than yesterday, farther apart, like the water was taking deep breaths before saying something big.

"Wind's backing around," I said slowly. Salvagers' kids learn weather the way farm kids learn dirt; it's the first thing Brannoc teaches, because weather is the only landlord we've ever had who doesn't take bribes. "From the south now. And the swell's coming out of the southeast, which means..."

"Which means?"

"A low. A real one. Off the water somewhere, still building." I frowned at the horizon, where the clouds had organized themselves into bands, stacked and darkening toward the seaward edge. "Two days. Maybe three. We should pull the small boats above the storm line and double-lash the racks."

Lior turned and looked at me properly then, and whatever she saw in my face made her do the almost-smile again, warmer this time.

"How long have you known how to do that?"

"Do what? Everybody here reads weather. It's not special."

"It's the most special thing there is," she said. "Everything else I could teach you—tracks, spoor, sign—it all comes down to the same muscle. Paying attention. Caring what the world is telling you." She looked back at the horizon. "You already have it. You've had it your whole life. You just call it something smaller than it is."

The wind pushed my hair across my face and I didn't fix it, because I was busy trying not to let my expression do anything embarrassing.

"So you'll teach me?" I asked, because hope makes me stupid.

"I'll teach you what I actually know," she said. "Not the romance of it. The reading. Weather, light, water, ground. How to stand still until the land forgets you're there. Whether you ever use it is your business." She held up one finger. "But hear the price first. Once you can see clearly, you can't unsee. You'll know when a friend is lying. You'll know when a storm's coming while everyone else is laughing. You'll carry it everywhere you go, the seeing, and some days it'll feel less like a gift than a weight." Her voice went strange and quiet on that last part, like she was quoting somebody, or herself from a long time ago.

"Brannoc says nothing worth having weighs nothing," I said.

"Brannoc sounds like he's been paying attention too."

"He mostly pays attention to the bottom of a cup."

"And yet he built all this." She gestured at the cove with her chin. "First lesson starts now. Tell me everything the next hour of sky does, before it does it. If you're right twice, I'll teach you tomorrow. If you're wrong twice—" the almost-smile flickered, "—you mend my nets for a month."

"They're already mended."

"Then you'll find more."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about watching the sky for a whole hour: it's the hardest work I've ever done, and I once spent two days gutting eels.

I sat on the headland with my knees hugged to my chest and I watched clouds the way other people watch a card game. Lior sat beside me and said nothing, which I learned was itself a lesson—she was teaching me to not need her talking. The wind backed further south, then southwest, then did a slow confused circle like it couldn't make up its mind, and I wrote down each turn in my head the way Brannoc writes his ledger entries, date and detail and consequence.

The cloud bands lowered. The air went thick enough to chew. The gulls came in off the water an hour before their usual time and sat sullen on the rocks instead of fishing, and I remembered Tam saying once that birds know a blow before barometers do, and I put it in the ledger of my head.

And the light. Nobody warns you about the light. Around mid-morning the sun went the color of a coin at the bottom of a well, yellow-white and watery, and the shadows got sharp as knife cuts even though the day was dimming, and I felt the hair on my arms lift because I'd seen that light exactly twice before, both times before storms that cost the cove boats.

"Rain by nightfall," I said, when the hour was up. My voice cracked on it, and I hated that. "Hard rain, from the south. Wind gusting hard after midnight. Tomorrow worse. The swell says the low's still building offshore—we haven't seen anything yet."

Lior was quiet a long moment.

"Right twice," she said.

I leapt up and punched the air and immediately tripped over a rock, because grace has never been among my payments either, and she laughed—a short, rusty sound, like a door opening in a house that had been shut for years. She caught herself looking surprised at her own laugh. I pretended not to see, because I was learning that lesson too: some things you track, and some things you have the decency to let alone.

"Tomorrow," she said. "Dawn. Bring nothing. Wanting nothing is the first tool."

* * *

The lessons settled into the cove's rhythm like they'd always been there. Dawn on the headland. Reading cloud strata, reading swell intervals, reading the way current lines wrote themselves on the water's surface in long pale seams. She taught me ground-reading too, eventually—not the romantic bloodhound stuff, but the patience of it: how a broken spiderweb tells you which way something passed, how crushed grass greens differently where weight lay, how the tide itself is the biggest tracker on the coast, sorting and arranging everything the sea touches if you know its filing system.

And between lessons, she talked. Not much. But more than before. I learned she liked her tea bitter enough to strip paint. I learned she counted things when she was nervous—gulls, waves, knots in the wood of the lean-to. I learned that when I mentioned my mother, dead before Brannoc found me, Lior went very still in a way that said the word *mother* was a locked room in her, and I didn't pick that lock. Even I have limits. A few.

What I figured out, somewhere in the second week, was the thing she probably thought she was hiding: teaching me was doing something to her. Every morning she came up the goat path a little less like a woman passing through and a little more like a woman who lived somewhere. She started greeting Pip by name. She fixed the hinge on Brannoc's smokehouse door without being asked, and when he tried to pay her she looked genuinely baffled, like payment hadn't occurred to her as a category.

Sable noticed it too. I caught him watching her one evening across the cookfire, wearing an expression I decided privately not to tease him about yet. Saving it. Good teasing keeps.

"She's staying," I told him, low, while Lior argued tide tables with Brannoc across the fire.

"Don't jinx it," Sable said. "She's like a cat. You name it, it leaves."

"She's not leaving."

"You don't know that."

"I know weather," I said. "People are just weather that talks. And her storm's breaking up."

He snorted into his cup, but he glanced at her again anyway, soft around the eyes, and I filed that away too. My ledger was getting full.

* * *

On the morning everything changed, I was first up the goat path, which never happened, which meant something had happened.

Lior was already at the top, standing very straight, looking south along the coast. The sky behind her was the wrong color—not storm-dark, but a hard clean blue with a strange metallic shine to it, the kind of sky that comes in the eye of a big system or right before one decides where it's going. The wind had died completely. Dead calm. The sea lay flat as poured metal, and every salvage instinct in my body screamed at the wrongness of it.

"What is it?" I asked.

"Barometer's falling faster than it should," she said. Her voice was level but her hand was doing the counting thing against her thigh—waves, gulls, knots. "This isn't a normal low anymore. It's organizing. Look at the horizon line—see the haze? That's spray being thrown off swells we can't even see yet. There's a wall of water coming up the coast, Wren, and this calm is it clearing its throat."

"How long?"

"Day. Maybe less." She turned to me, and I saw her make a decision, the way you watch someone tie off a knot. "Get Brannoc. Get everyone. Boats above the storm line, high as they'll go. Double everything. And Wren—" she caught my shoulder, and her grip was strong and certain and it steadied me more than any words, "—you read the sky better than half the rangers I've served with. When the wind turns, you tell me which way it's swinging. Can you do that?"

Can I do that. Me. Sixteen years old, wreck-beach ribs and elbows, the girl who breathes loud when she's pleased with herself.

"Yes," I said, and my voice didn't crack even once.

I was halfway down the goat path, running, when the first distant thunder rolled up out of that metal-blue south—not storm thunder, deeper than that, a long grinding boom like the sea clearing its throat for real—and I heard Lior behind me say, very quietly, to herself or to the horizon:

"Monsoon's come early."

I ran faster.
