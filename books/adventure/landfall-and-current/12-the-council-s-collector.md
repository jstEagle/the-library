## 12. The Council's Collector

I was mending the third seam on the boat's port side when Wren came running down the shingle, all elbows and alarm, shouting that there were sails in the offing.

"Whose sails?" I asked, though my hands had already gone still, which is how I knew the answer before she said it.

"The Council's. White flags up. Brannoc says they're flying truce."

Brannoc would know. Brannoc had been reading the coast longer than I'd been alive, and he'd taught half the cove to read it too. Truce flags meant parley, and parley meant somebody wanted something badly enough to pretend at courtesy.

I set down the pitch brush and looked at my work. Three seams patched, a fourth weeping slow as a confession. The boat would float. It would not outrun anything with a keel and a crew that ate regularly. Not that running had ever been much of a plan for me. Running was Lior's specialty. I'd watched her do it for weeks across the salt flats—beautiful, efficient, joyless, like watching a hawk pretend to be a sparrow.

"They'll want the Tideheart," Wren said. She'd taken to calling it *the* Tideheart now, definite article and all, as if it were a resident of the cove rather than a stone we kept wrapped in oilcloth under Brannoc's floor. "That's what they've come for."

"That's what they've come for," I agreed, and went to find Lior.

She was on the headland, because of course she was. Lior Brightwater spent more time on high ground than a goat with ambitions. She stood with her back to me, watching the white sails come around the point, and I saw her shoulders do the thing they did when she was counting threats—left, right, water, wind—and arriving at an answer she didn't like.

"Sable," she said, without turning. She always knew my footsteps. I'd stopped finding that unnerving around the time I realized she probably knew everyone's footsteps, and filed them away like a miser files coins.

"White flags," I said.

"I see them."

"Truce means they think they can win by talking."

"It means they think we can be bought." She turned then, and her face was doing its usual work of being nothing at all, which from Lior was practically a shout. "How many boats?"

"Two. Maybe thirty men."

"And us?"

"Brannoc, a girl who wants to fight everything, a dozen salvagers with gutting knives, and a leaking boat." I shrugged. "We've had worse odds. We've also had better ones. Statistically, most of our odds have been worse."

Something twitched at the corner of her mouth. It wasn't quite a smile. With Lior it never quite was; smiles were another thing she'd left behind somewhere on the salt flats, along with her name and any reasonable expectation of a good night's sleep.

"Brannoc will hear them out," she said. "He'll want to protect the cove."

"Brannoc will hear them out," I agreed. "And then he'll look at you, and he'll ask what you want to do, and whatever you say, half the cove will decide the opposite, because that's what happens when strangers bring trouble into a place where people have learned to live without it."

"I didn't bring trouble," she said. "Trouble was already here. I'm just the part of it that walks."

---

They came ashore at midday, when the tide was low and the light lay flat on the water like a sheet of hammered tin. Two longboats, oars shipped, white cloth streaming from short staffs at bow and stern. The men rowing them wore grey uniforms gone salt-stiff, and they sat very straight, the way soldiers sit when they want you to notice they're soldiers even in borrowed fishing boats.

And in the prow of the first boat stood a man in a coat too fine for the coast, hands folded, face composed into an expression of gentle patience—the kind of expression worn by people who have spent years being paid to wait.

I knew him before the boats touched gravel.

You don't forget the face of the man who emptied your sister. You might forget your own name, someday, if the drink works on you hard enough, or the sea, or the years. But you don't forget him. He came to our house on a Tuesday in the season of small rains, with papers and two escorts and a voice like warm oil, and he explained to my mother that the Council required a *contribution* from every household of standing, that it was painless, that it was patriotic, that it was temporary. My sister Maren was sixteen. She walked into the back room holding his hat, because he'd asked her to hold his hat, because he was courteous, because courtesy was the knife he used.

She came out three hours later smiling at nothing. She still smiles at nothing. She smiled at nothing this morning, across the fire, while she braided rope she'd already braided twice.

His name was Collector Vess. I had sworn, in the loud private way young men swear things, that if I ever saw him again I would put my hands around his throat and squeeze until the harbor masters had to invent a new word for what was left.

Instead I stood on the shingle with wet pitch on my fingers and watched him step delicately over a tide pool, lifting his coat hem like a man crossing a churchyard, and I discovered that hatred, given enough years to cure, turns into something quieter and far more useful: attention. Every detail of him registered. The ink stains on his right cuff. The way his eyes moved first to Brannoc, then to the crowd, then—patiently, methodically—to me, and lingered, and softened, as if recognizing an old friend.

He remembered me too. Of course he did. Collectors keep ledgers.

"Good people of Greywater Cove," Vess said, and his voice carried the way voices carry when they've been trained in council chambers to reach the cheap seats. "I come under truce, and under truce I shall speak plainly. The Harbor Council extends its amnesty to this settlement. No collector ships will visit these waters for a full year. No levies. No contributions. In exchange, the Council asks the return of certain property recently removed from its custody—a relic, a stone, of no great value except to scholars." He smiled. It was a good smile. It had been used on my mother. "Property," he said, "and one fugitive."

The cove had gathered in its loose, wary way—salvagers with their arms crossed, women with gutting knives still in their belts, old Brannoc planted in front like a breakwater. I felt the crowd's attention swing toward me and Lior like a weathervane in a shifting wind, and I hated it, and understood it. A year without collectors. A year without the levy boats coming up the inlet, taking their tithe of catch and coin and, occasionally, memory. These people had built their lives in the gaps between the Council's fingers. Vess was offering to take his hand off them for twelve months.

All it cost was a stone and a stranger.

"The fugitive," Brannoc said. His voice was gravel over deep water. "Name her."

Vess consulted a paper he did not need to consult. Theater. Everything about the man was theater; the paper was a prop, the pause was a prop, the mild regret on his face was a prop, and underneath all of it worked the machinery of a mind that had hollowed out half the coast and called it civic duty.

"Lior Brightwater," he said. "Daughter of Aldous Brightwater, ranger, condemned. Wanted for theft of Council property, obstruction of lawful collection, and—" he glanced up, savoring it, "—the harboring of stolen goods. The reward is substantial. The amnesty, I should emphasize, covers the entire settlement regardless of whether the fugitive is delivered alive or otherwise."

*Otherwise.* He let the word sit in the salt air like a fish left out too long.

I looked at Lior. She stood at the edge of the crowd, hood back, letting herself be seen—which cost her something, I knew that much by now, knew it the way you know the weight of a pack you've carried so long you've forgotten it isn't part of your body. Her face gave away nothing. Her hands, at her sides, were very still, and Lior's hands were never still unless she was holding them still on purpose.

And I looked at the cove. At Mara the net-mender, whose brother had come back from a contribution visit smiling at walls. At the twins who dove wrecks, whose father had sold his memories of them to pay a debt—or had them taken; the ledger never distinguished. At Wren, fifteen years old and furious, staring at Vess like she could sink his boat with her eyes if she just believed hard enough.

I saw the arithmetic happening behind their faces. I had done the same arithmetic myself, once, in a leaky skiff with a stolen god in my bag: *what is one stranger against all of us?*

It's a fair question. That's the obscenity of it. It's a fair question, and the answer is supposed to be obvious, and it never is, not when the collectors are real and the winter is real and your children are real.

"Amnesty," Brannoc repeated slowly. "A year."

"A year," Vess confirmed warmly. "Perhaps longer, for good faith. The Council remembers its friends."

"The Council remembers its friends," Brannoc said, "right up until it needs their teeth." A few salvagers laughed, short and hard. Vess's smile held, but I saw the ink-stained hand flex slightly at his side, a pianist annoyed at a wrong note. "You'll have my answer at sundown. Truce holds till then. Get off my beach."

Vess bowed—actually bowed, the coat flaring—and turned back to his boats. And as he passed me, close enough that I could smell the cedar oil in his hair, he paused.

"Sable Vane," he said quietly. "You've grown. Your mother was well, last report. Your sister sends her regards—well. Someone sends them for her." His eyes flicked toward the headland, toward the cave above the tideline, then back to me, mild as milk. "The stone is heavy, I imagine. Heavy things wear down the people who carry them. Bring it to me at sundown and your family's account closes clean. All of it. Even hers."

Then he stepped into the boat, and the oars bit, and the white flags drew away across the tin-colored water, and I stood there with pitch drying on my hands and my heart going like a drum in a parade I hadn't volunteered for.

---

Lior found me at the boat, because she always found me; it was becoming a habit I neither encouraged nor could break.

"He knew your sister," she said. Not a question.

"He made her." I kept working the seam, though the pitch had gone cold and useless in my hands. Work was a thing to do with the body when the body wanted to do murder. "Maren. Sixteen. He sat in our kitchen and drank our tea and told my mother it wouldn't hurt. Then he took eleven years of her and left the rest smiling." I scraped at a seam that didn't need scraping. "He offered to sell me her memories back. Just now. Did you hear it? Sundown. The stone for Maren whole. He's done the sums, Lior. He knows exactly what she's worth to me, because knowing what people are worth is the entire trade."

"Yes," Lior said. "I heard it."

"You should run." I said it flatly, because it needed saying and because saying it hurt, and I've always believed in getting wounds open early. "Sundown comes, the cove wavers, and the smart money says you're gone over the headland before the light fails. You're good at gone. I've chased you across half a territory. I can vouch for the quality."

For a moment she said nothing. Out past the point, the storm clouds were stacking themselves again, patient as creditors, and the gulls were doing their nervous arithmetic overhead.

"If I run," she said finally, "they take the cove apart looking for me anyway. Amnesty dies the moment I'm over the ridge. You know that. He knows that. That's why he named me in front of everyone—he's not hunting me, Sable. He's pricing me. He wants to see what these people think I cost." She looked at me then, and there was something in her face I hadn't seen before—not fear, exactly. Recognition. "And he wants to see what you think she costs. Your sister. He's betting the stone against your blood, and he's betting you'll blink."

"He's usually right," I said. "Men like him are usually right. That's what makes them unbearable."

"Not always." She picked up the pitch brush, looked at it, set it down again. "Wren asked me today why I never stay anywhere. I told her the truth—that staying means being known, and being known means being caught." A pause. The wind came off the water and moved her hair, and she let it. "I'm starting to think being caught might not be the worst thing that could happen to me. Ask me again at sundown."

She walked up the shingle toward Brannoc's, and I watched her go, and I thought about my sister braiding the same rope twice, smiling at nothing, and about a dull grey stone under the floorboards that hummed when you touched it, and about a man in a fine coat who had just priced every person I loved and found the numbers agreeable.

At sundown, Brannoc would give his answer. At sundown, Vess expected his stone. And between now and sundown, half the cove would look at Lior Brightwater and see either a neighbor or a price tag, and I would have to stand among them and find out which kind of people we all really were.

The first drops of rain hit the shingle, fat and warm, tasting of the storm to come.

Sundown was going to be interesting.
