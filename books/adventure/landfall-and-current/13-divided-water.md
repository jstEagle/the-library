## 13. Divided Water

The trouble with being seen, I decided on the third morning after the collector's visit, is that people start having opinions about you.

I'd spent six years cultivating invisibility the way other people cultivate gardens—patiently, daily, with an eye toward the seasons. A ranger who keeps her hood up, her voice low, and her name out of her mouth can walk through a market like a draft through a door. Nobody remembers a draft. But one truce-flag boat, one smug collector with a laminated writ, and suddenly I was the most discussed woman in Greywater Cove. Lior Brightwater. Wanted. Reward attached. The word *reward* has a way of traveling through a settlement faster than the tide comes in, and it comes in fast here.

I sat on the seawall above Brannoc's drying racks and watched the cove argue about me.

Not *with* me, mind. About me. That was the part that stung, in the small quiet place behind the sternum where I kept the things that stung. They argued in the low voices people think don't carry across water, but water carries everything; ask any drowned coast. Dell and the two Marsh brothers thought the reward—three hundred crowns, enough to re-rig every leaking hull in the cove twice over—was worth a long walk to the nearest Council station. Old Sefa thought selling me was bad luck. Wren thought it was *evil*, and said so at a volume that defeated the purpose of the low voices. And Brannoc sat in the middle of it like a stone in a stream, saying nothing, which from Brannoc was a sermon in itself.

I'd heard the stone sermon before. It was the same one my father used to preach when the Harbor Council's men came through the district with their ledgers and their polite questions. Silence, he said, is what the powerful harvest first. Everything else grows out of it.

He'd been wrong about that, in the end. Or right, depending on how you counted. The Council had harvested him anyway—his name, his standing, his good right hand of a career—leaving me the crop.

"You're doing the thing," Wren said, dropping onto the seawall beside me with the boneless grace of someone who had grown up climbing wrecks. "The staring-at-nothing thing."

"I'm assessing weather."

"You're staring at nothing."

"The weather is nothing. It's the eye's edge. Look at the cloud line off the point—flat-bottomed, stacked high. We've got a day, maybe two, before the next band comes through."

Wren squinted at the horizon, doing her best to look like she was assessing weather and not assessing me. She'd gotten better at reading the sky in the week since I'd started teaching her. She was still terrible at pretending she wasn't looking at people. It was, I suspected, her best quality.

"Dell's talking about tying you up," she said. "The Marsh brothers are talking about just *telling* the Council where you are and letting them do the tying."

"And you're talking to me, which makes you either brave or stupid."

"Brave," she said firmly. "Probably. Brannoc says stupid is when you know the cost and pay it anyway without counting."

"Brannoc says a lot of things."

"Yeah." She was quiet a moment, and when she spoke again her voice had dropped into the register she used when she was being older than her years, which was most of the time and never enough. "He's counting now, though. You know that, right? He's been in his ledger all morning. Not writing. Just looking at it."

That landed harder than it should have. Brannoc's ledger was the cove's true heart—a record of every name the Council had erased, kept in a cramped hand on salt-warped paper, hidden behind a false back in his strongbox for longer than I'd been alive. My mother's name was in it. That was the thing I kept not thinking about, the way you keep not touching a bruise. *Maren Brightwater, taken 4th of the Drowning Moon.* And beside it, in Brannoc's later hand: *witness unverified.*

Unverified. Which meant Brannoc had written it down from someone's account and never known if it was true, and now the Tideheart sat wrapped in oilcloth under the floor of his hut, humming like a struck bell, full of everyone's stolen years—and possibly, in some form I couldn't name and didn't dare hope for, a piece of her.

"Where is he?" I asked.

"His hut. Sable's with him."

Sable. Of course Sable was with him. Sable Vane had a gift for being exactly where the difficult conversations happened, wearing his guilt like a second coat. I'd have found it irritating if I didn't do the same thing with my restlessness.

I stood. Wren stood with me, hopeful as a dog.

"Stay here," I said.

"You're going to listen at his door."

"I'm going to *approach his door openly* and then listen at it, because I'm a professional."

She grinned. I let her. It was easier than letting her see what the listening actually cost.

* * *

Brannoc's hut smelled of tar, old paper, and the particular staleness of a man who hadn't slept. I didn't bother with the door. I knocked once and came in, because in the cove that passed for manners.

He sat at his table with the ledger open before him and the oilcloth bundle beside it, and Sable stood at the window with his arms crossed, watching the cove the way I'd been watching it. We were a matched set, the two of us. I hated that.

"You heard," Brannoc said. It wasn't a question.

"I heard Dell offering to buy rope at the chandlery this morning. Rope and a gag. He wasn't subtle."

"Dell's not subtle. Dell's also not wrong about the arithmetic." Brannoc rubbed his eyes with a thumb and forefinger, and for the first time I saw how old he was—not the performing oldness of the wise elder, but the real kind, sitting in the joints and the breath. "Three hundred crowns. That's the Marsh brothers' boat re-caulked, that's Sefa's nets replaced, that's medicine through the winter. I can sit here and tell them a name is worth more than money, and I'd even believe it. But it's not my name on the writ."

"It's your roof over my head."

"It's my roof over *everyone's* head, girl, that's the problem." He turned the ledger toward me. Not to my mother's page—to the front, where the names started, forty years of them, a whole drowned parish of the erased. "I've spent my life writing down what the Council took. You know what I've learned? Names are heavy. Carrying one is nothing. Carrying forty of them, that's a life. Carrying three hundred crowns' worth of other people's hunger—" He shook his head. "I don't know if I can hold the roof shut. That's the truth of it. I'm not wavering because I doubt you. I'm wavering because I doubt *them*, and I've earned the right to."

I looked at Sable. He was doing his window thing, jaw tight.

"You have something to add?" I said.

"I have a boat with a patched hull and a bad opinion of open water in monsoon season," he said. "And I have the same arithmetic problem, except mine's worse, because if they take the Tideheart and you both, I've lost everything I came here to protect, and if they take it and *not* you, I've lost it anyway, just slower." He turned from the window. His eyes had the flat shine of a man who hadn't slept either. "The collector comes back when the storm breaks. Truce flags or no. You know that."

"I know that."

"So the question isn't whether the cove sells you. The question is whether you make the cove's choice for them first." He said it gently, which was somehow worse. "You're already packed. I've seen the way you've been sleeping—with your boots on."

I looked down at my boots. Dry, laced, pointed at the door. Six years of habit will do that; my body had decided before my head caught up, the way it always did. Running wasn't a choice for me anymore than breathing was. It was just what happened when the air got thin.

"Maybe that's the answer," I said, and heard how flat my voice came out. "Nobody has to choose. I take the Tideheart, go out through the flats at the next low, and the collector follows me and nobody here is standing between him and his reward. The cove keeps its crowns. Brannoc keeps his roof. Clean arithmetic."

"Clean," Sable said, "except for the part where you're one person with a rock against a patrol boat."

"I've been one person against worse."

"Name it."

I opened my mouth. Closed it. The honest answer was *nothing*, and we both knew it, and the knowing sat in the room with us like a third person.

Brannoc closed the ledger with a soft, terrible finality. "Go on, then," he said. "If you're going. Go before the low turns. And girl—" I stopped at the door. "The name in my book. Maren. If there's any chance the stone holds what I hope it holds, running with it under your arm is just another way of letting the Council keep it. You'd be a thief doing their storage for them."

I didn't answer. There wasn't an answer that wasn't either a lie or a surrender, and I wasn't ready to pick which.

The Tideheart was warm through the oilcloth when I lifted it. It had been warmer every day. I told myself it was the hut's stove.

* * *

The tide was going out, and the flats were coming up dark and glistening, all the way to the cave mouth I knew was opening somewhere in that wet black mirror. My route was simple. Cross at the low, reach the cave, seal myself in with the stone until the next low, move at night along the drowned coast until the Council's patrols lost my scent in the salt. I'd done harder things with worse information. I'd done them alone, which was the part I kept telling myself was the point.

The pack was on my shoulders. The boots were dry. The flats were waiting.

Wren was standing on the path to the shore, arms crossed, planted like a fence post.

I should have gone around her. There was room. There's always room; that's the whole philosophy of running—you never have to push past anyone, you just find the gap. But she was fifteen years old and she'd spent a week learning to read the sky from me, and she was looking at me with the particular ferocity of someone who has decided that love is a thing you can argue a person into.

"Move, Wren."

"No."

"The tide's going out. I don't have time for—"

"You have *hours*." Her voice cracked on it, and she hated that, I could see her hating it, and she planted her feet harder anyway. "You have hours and you're spending them pretending this is about tides. It's not about tides. It's about you deciding that leaving is the same as helping."

"It *is* helping. Every hour I'm here, the cove is in danger. Dell's already—"

"Dell's a coward with a rope. That's not the cove. That's *Dell*." She stepped closer, into my path proper, close enough that I could see the salt drying white at her temples. "Brannoc took you in. Sable told you the truth when he could've fought you. I—" Her jaw worked. "You taught me the clouds. You said the sky doesn't lie, it just takes patience. So I'm asking you to be patient. With *us*. With this."

"The sky doesn't lie," I said, "but it doesn't ask either. It just comes through and takes the coast apart and leaves."

"Then you'd know," she said, "that running from weather doesn't work. You taught me that too. You said you don't outride a monsoon, you *shelter* and you *bail* and you keep your people together till it breaks." Her eyes were bright and furious and wet. "You said that. First lesson. I wrote it in my head where I keep things."

The Tideheart pulsed against my spine, warm as a sleeping animal, and for one unguarded moment I let myself feel what it was offering—the hum at the edge of my mind, the sense of vast water held very still, of voices underneath. *Maren Brightwater, taken 4th of the Drowning Moon.* If I ran, I'd have the stone. I'd have the hum. I'd have the maybe. I'd have all of it, alone, the way I'd had everything alone for six years, and it would keep me exactly as company as it always had: none.

The cost of stopping. That was the lesson I'd been dodging since the caravan shrine, since the salt flats, since my father's name came apart in the harbor wind. Stopping didn't just mean being seen. It meant being *countable*—one more mouth, one more name, one more person whose fate was braided into other people's fates, so that when the storm took someone, it took a piece of you with it. Running was cheaper. Running was always cheaper. That was the whole sales pitch of running, and I'd bought it at full price for six years without once asking what I'd actually purchased.

What I'd purchased was this: a shore at dawn, a fifteen-year-old standing in my way, and the discovery that I would rather stand here being argued with than walk past her.

That was the cost. That was also the *point*. Nobody had told me they were the same thing.

"Three hundred crowns," I said quietly.

"I know."

"Enough to re-rig every boat in this cove."

"I know."

"And I'm worth more than that to you."

"You're worth more than that to *me*," Wren said, with the ruthless precision of the young. "And Brannoc, and Sable, and Sefa. Dell can price you however he wants. A cove isn't one opinion, Lior. It's a tide pool. Everything in it touches everything else." She hesitated, and then, because she was fifteen and hadn't learned to be ashamed of hope: "You said the sky doesn't lie. Well, neither do I. If you walk out on those flats alone, I'm going to follow you. I'm a *salvager*. I can track a gull across open water. You will not lose me, and then there'll be two of us in danger, and it'll be your fault, and I'll say so the entire time."

I laughed. It came out of me sideways, rough, half a sob wearing a laugh's clothes, and I turned my face toward the water until it finished.

The flats lay open and gleaming, the cave mouth a black seam in the world, the whole drowned coast offering me the old bargain: go, be a draft through a door, be safe and weightless and alone. Behind me, smoke rose from Brannoc's chimney. Somewhere in the settlement, Dell was buying rope. The collector was out there past the point with his truce flags and his patrol boat, waiting for the storm to break, patient as arithmetic.

The Tideheart hummed against my back. And underneath the hum—fainter than breath, steadier than a heartbeat—I heard, or imagined I heard, or chose to hear, a woman's voice saying a name. Not mine. My father's.

I took the pack off my shoulders.

Wren watched me do it with her whole body, not moving, not celebrating, just watching, the way you watch weather you've committed to standing in.

"First lesson," she said. "Shelter and bail. Keep your people together till it breaks."

"Second lesson," I said, "is that your people get a vote on whether they're yours. Come on. We've got a day before the next band, and I need to have a conversation with Dell about rope."

We were halfway up the path when the lookout's horn sounded from the point—one long note, then two short. Not the storm signal. The other one.

A sail, standing in past the shoals. Flying truce colors.

The collector hadn't waited for the storm to break after all.

He'd come at the eye's edge, when the cove's boats were beached and its fighters scattered—and he was coming ashore.
