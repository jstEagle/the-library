## 14. Storm Surge

### Lior

The sky had been threatening us for three days the way a debt collector threatens—politely at first, then with increasing specificity.

By dawn of the fourth day it stopped being polite.

I woke in Brannoc's boathouse to a sound I'd only heard described by rangers who'd served on the coast and never quite managed to make it sound real: the sea inhaling. Not waves. A single, sustained pull, like the whole ocean drawing breath to scream. The air pressure dropped so fast my ears popped twice before I got my boots on.

Wren was already awake, standing in the doorway, watching the horizon with an expression I recognized from mirrors. That flat, calculating look of someone measuring how much trouble they're in.

"It's early," she said. "Brannoc said we had until tomorrow."

"Brannoc's been wrong about weather before?"

"He once predicted rain during a drought." She shrugged. "But he says the monsoon doesn't come early. It comes when it wants, and it wants what it wants."

I liked that about Wren—she delivered doom like she was reading a supply manifest. No drama. Just inventory.

Outside, Greywater Cove was performing its morning routine with the desperate cheerfulness of people who knew the performance might be their last. Old Maret was nailing storm shutters over windows that hadn't been whole since before I was born. Two salvagers were hauling nets up past the tideline, well past, another forty feet than any sane person would bother with. Brannoc stood at the center of it all like a lighthouse that had given up on shining and settled for pointing.

"You feel that?" he asked me, without turning around. He'd known I was there. The man heard footsteps on wet stone the way other people heard their own names.

"The sea's breathing in," I said.

"Aye." He finally looked at me, and his eyes were doing something complicated. "That means it means to breathe out hard. Very hard. Girl, where's your thief?"

"Sable's at the boat. He's been caulking her since yesterday."

Brannoc winced. It was a small thing, a flicker, but I'd spent years learning to read faces because my life occasionally depended on it, and Brannoc's face just told me the boat was already dead.

"How bad?"

"She's a salvage hull held together with prayer and pitch. In this?" He shook his head slowly. "There is no *in this*. There's only before and after."

I went down to the waterline anyway. Because that's what I did. Because running toward problems was the one habit deeper than running away from them, and I'd never figured out which one was actually in charge.

* * *

Sable was waist-deep in the shallows, working pitch into the hull seams with the focused fury of a man arguing with wood. His hands were bleeding in the way hands do after two days of this work—raw, split at the knuckles, the blood washing off into water the color of old pewter. The sky behind him had gone a green-black I didn't have a name for, the kind of color that made you understand why ancient peoples invented angry gods.

"Tell me she'll float," I called over the wind.

He didn't stop working. "She'll float."

"Liar."

"I'm a professional liar," he said, still not looking up. "You'd know if I were lying. It would sound better than that."

The truth was sitting right there between us, obvious as a shipwreck. Even if the boat survived the launch—and she wouldn't—even if they cleared the cove mouth—and they couldn't—the open water beyond was already a churning gray wall. Sable's plan had always been thin: ride out the worst of the season somewhere south, come back when the Council lost interest. But plans are things you make when the world gives you options, and the world had just repossessed all of ours.

"Sable." I waded out to him, and the water was warm, which somehow made it worse. Warm like bathwater, like something inviting. "Look at the horizon."

"I've seen the horizon."

"*Look at it.*"

He looked. And I watched the last of his stubbornness drain out of him, watched him become, for one unguarded second, exactly what he was: a tired man in rising water holding a hammer against a dying boat.

"Well," he said. "That's unfortunate."

That was all. That was the eulogy for the escape plan. And then he laughed—a short, cracked sound with no humor in it, the laugh of a man who has just realized the door he's been watching was painted on the wall.

"We could try anyway," he said.

"And drown competently."

"I've drowned incompetently before. There's less dignity in it than you'd think."

I put my hand on the hull. Under my palm I could feel her shivering—not from the work, from the water. From the swell coming in long and low and wrong, each one bigger than the last, each one arriving a little sooner. The rhythm of a sea gathering itself.

"Brannoc's cave," I said.

Sable went very still. "You don't know what you're asking."

"I know it opens twice a year and takes payment. I know the Tideheart's inside it. I know—" I stopped, because saying the rest aloud made it real, and I wasn't ready for it to be real yet. *I know my mother might be in there.* "I know it's the only stone structure on this coast above the surge line."

"The Tideheart eats memories, Lior. Every hour near it costs you something. You don't remember what you forget—that's the trick of it. You just find holes and can't say when they were made."

"Better holes than drowned."

He stared at me through the rain that had started falling now, fat warm drops that felt less like weather and more like the sky sweating. And I saw him weigh it, saw him think about his sister, hollow-eyed in a Council hospice, and understood that for him this had never really been a choice. He'd been walking toward that cave since the day he stole the stone.

"Okay," he said quietly. "Okay. Help me get her high. If anything's left of her after, I want it to be salvageable."

We dragged the boat up the shingle together, both of us leaning into it, the wind trying to peel us off her like bark. It took everything. When we finally got her above the wrack line and anchored under netting and stone, I sat down hard on the rocks and Sable sat next to me, and for a while neither of us said anything, because there was nothing left to pretend about and pretending had been most of our conversation.

"There's no leaving now," he said at last. Not bitter. Almost relieved. Like a man setting down luggage he'd forgotten he was carrying.

"No," I agreed. "Now there's only here."

The sea breathed in again, longer this time, and somewhere out on the horizon, the green-black wall began to move.

### Sable

Here is what nobody tells you about catastrophe: it's mostly waiting.

The storm took four hours to arrive and six seconds to destroy everything worth destroying, and in between those two numbers there was a long gray afternoon of hauling salvagers' belongings into the cave, of Brannoc directing traffic with the calm of a man who had done this dance before, of Wren carrying crates twice her size because nobody could tell her not to. We moved the settlement uphill in a slow, grim procession. Nets. Tools. Water barrels. Brannoc's ledger—I carried that myself, wrapped in oilcloth, tucked against my chest like a heartbeat. I'd seen Lior's face when she found her mother's name in it. Some things you protect without being asked.

The cave swallowed us all by dusk.

I'd expected chaos. What I got instead was order so practiced it looked like ritual. Brannoc had sheltered his people through storms for thirty years, and the cave's main chamber had been fitted for it generations back—stone shelves, a fire pit vented through a crack in the rock, freshwater seeping from a spring in the far wall that tasted of minerals and patience. Forty-odd salvagers arranged themselves by some unwritten law, families in the alcoves, singles nearer the entrance, and Brannoc at the center like a hearth-stone himself.

And at the back, past a bend in the rock, the low chamber where the tide pool lay, and where the Tideheart sat on its natural altar of black stone, dull as river gravel, humming its almost-silent hum.

Everyone pretended not to look at it. Everyone looked at it.

"That thing should go deeper," muttered a salvager named Corwin, a broad man with forearms like mooring posts. "Bury it. Sink it in the pool."

"And wake whatever guards it?" someone else said. "Brannoc says—"

"Brannoc says a lot of things."

"Brannoc," said Brannoc, from across the chamber, without raising his voice, "says burying it in the pool is how you get the pool to start giving things back. Sit down, Corwin."

Corwin sat.

I found Lior near the entrance, watching the storm come. You couldn't see much—the light had failed—but you could hear it, a sound building out on the water like a freight train made of rain, and under it the sea, breathing out now, long and enormous, the exhale we'd been promised.

"Your boat," she said. Not a question.

"Is either safely above the line or currently becoming driftwood." I sat beside her. "I've decided not to check."

"Wise."

"My sister taught me that. Oddly enough, before the Council taught her everything else." I picked at the dried pitch on my knuckles. "She used to say worry is just imagination pointed the wrong direction."

Lior glanced at me. "She sounds like someone worth remembering."

"She was." The word came out flat and final, a door closing, and Lior—being Lior—didn't push. That was the strange courtesy between us. We'd traded our wounds in the dark weeks ago, hers of exile, mine of a hollowed sister, and ever since we'd handled them like smuggled goods: carefully, quietly, never in front of witnesses.

The storm arrived.

I don't have language for it that doesn't sound like lying. The wind hit the cove and the sound was not weather; it was geology happening fast. Rain came sideways, came upward, came in volumes that turned the air itself into something you could drown in standing up. Through the cave mouth we watched the sea stand up—all of it, the whole horizon lifting into a single moving hill—and then the hill came ashore.

The first surge took the lower settlement. I heard it more than saw it: timber splintering, the deep groan of the boathouse folding, Maret shouting something that the wind ate whole. Water came halfway up the shingle, white and furious, reached for the cave mouth, fell short, withdrew, and came again, higher.

"Deeper!" Brannoc roared, and the salvagers moved, everyone shifting back, back, back, until we were all pressed into the inner chambers, and the third surge came all the way in.

It filled the entrance passage thigh-deep and stayed. The cave, which opened twice a year, had decided to open inward. We were sealed in with the storm, forty-three people, one dying fire, one ledger, one girl who wouldn't stop grinning about it, and one artifact that hummed in the dark like it was pleased.

"Told you," Wren said to Lior, elbowing her. "No leaving now."

"I recall resisting this plan," Lior said dryly.

"You resisted everything. It's your best quality."

Somewhere in the press of bodies, someone started singing—an old coast song, half lament, half counting rhyme, the kind meant to keep time with work or grief, whichever arrived first. Others joined. The fire caught properly at last, fed with broken crate-wood, and the light jumped across wet stone and tired faces, and I stood at the edge of it watching Lior Brightwater, the woman who had chased me across a salt flat to arrest me, sit down among strangers and let a child lean against her shoulder.

She looked terrified. Not of the storm. Of the leaning.

* * *

Later—hours later, when the singing had worn down to embers and most of the cove slept in tangled heaps against the cold stone—I couldn't sleep. The Tideheart pulled at me the way it always did, a faint tidal tug behind the eyes. I told myself I was checking on it. Guarding it. Professional diligence.

I walked back to the low chamber alone.

The pool had risen with the surge, black and glassy, and the artifact sat on its stone above the waterline, unchanged, patient. As I approached, the hum sharpened, and—this had happened before, but never this strong—an image surfaced in me unbidden: my sister, age nine, standing in shallow water, holding a crab she'd caught, laughing at me because I was afraid of it.

A memory. Mine. Offered up like tribute.

"That's how it works, then," said a voice behind me, and I nearly went into the pool.

Lior stood at the bend in the rock, arms crossed, watching me with an expression I was beginning to know: the ranger's face, cataloguing evidence.

"It takes," I said. "Tonight it gave back. Make sense of that."

"Nothing about that stone makes sense." She came closer, stopping just outside arm's reach of it, which I noticed she did deliberately. She'd been keeping that distance for days. "But I felt it too. When I touched the ledger earlier. It showed me—" Her jaw tightened. "My mother's hands. Doing something ordinary. Kneading bread. I haven't thought about her hands in ten years, and tonight I can't stop."

The hum rose between us, soft as breathing.

"It knows we're trapped," I said. "It knows it has time."

"Then it's the only one here that isn't afraid of that." Lior looked at the stone a long moment, and the firelight from the main chamber barely reached us, and in that half-dark her face did something I'd never seen it do: it stopped guarding itself.

"Sable," she said. "When the storm breaks—if the boat's gone, if the Council's still hunting, if there's nothing left of the cove but this cave—I need you to promise me something."

"Name it."

"Don't let me go in alone after that stone. Whatever I say. Whatever I *remember* saying." Her voice was steady, which is how I knew it cost her. "Because I think my mother's in there. Or the shape of her. And I think it will offer her to me, and I think I'll say yes, and I don't trust what I'll agree to pay."

The Tideheart hummed. Out beyond the sealed entrance, the storm screamed against the rock like something demanding to be let in—or like something warning us what waited on the other side when the water finally fell.

I gave her my hand. "You won't go in alone," I said. "Whatever happens."

Above the black pool, the dull stone brightened—just slightly, just briefly—like a thing opening one eye.

Like a thing that had heard us, and was pleased.
