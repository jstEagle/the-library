## 15. Inside the Heart

The sea closed the cave's mouth behind us like a door that had been waiting all season to shut.

I want to say I took it calmly. That the ranger in me assessed the situation—forty feet of flooded passage, a rising tide, twenty-three frightened salvagers, one leaking lamp—and began drafting solutions. But the truth is uglier and simpler: when the water sealed us in, my first thought was *I should have left*. My second was that I'd had eleven chances to leave since Greywater Cove, and every single time, something had reached out and hooked me by the collar. Wren's questions. Brannoc's ledger. Sable's stupid, stubborn honesty.

And now the Tideheart, sitting on its salt-crusted altar like it had been expecting me.

"Everyone against the far wall," Brannoc rasped. The old man's arm was bound where the patrol's crossbow bolt had grazed him, and his breathing had a wet rattle in it that none of us wanted to name. "High tide passes in six hours. We've sheltered here before. It holds."

"It held before there were this many of us," muttered Dellin, a salvager with forearms like dock rope. He wasn't wrong. The chamber was a throat of black stone, half of it already ankle-deep in surging water, and the air tasted of salt and lamp oil and fear.

Sable found me in the crowd without looking for me. He'd gotten good at that—finding me. "You're doing the thing," he said quietly.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you stand very still and plan your whole life around an exit that doesn't exist."

"I don't have a whole life."

"You do, actually. It's just been on layaway." He nodded toward the altar. "It's humming again. Has been since you walked in."

He was right. I could feel it through the soles of my boots—a low vibration, like a hive thinking. The Tideheart sat dull as river stone to the eye, but up close, in the lamplight, I could see movement inside it. Not light exactly. Weather. Slow gray shapes turning over each other, the way silt turns in floodwater.

Brannoc had told me what it did to holders. *Takes admission*, the dying fisherman had said, back on the flats, and I'd thought he meant coin. He'd meant memory. Every hour I'd spent touching the stone during the skirmish—carrying it, hiding it, pressing it to my chest when the patrol boat's lanterns swept the cove—I'd paid something out. Small things first. The name of the innkeeper at Dry Fork who'd once given me a free meal. The color of the sky the morning I passed my ranger's trial. Little rooms in the house of myself, going dark.

I should have been afraid of that. Instead, some traitorous part of me leaned toward the hum like a plant toward a window.

Because if it took, it also kept. And somewhere inside that dull gray stone might be a woman named Maren Brightwater, who had walked into the harbor district nineteen years ago and never walked out, whose name her husband never spoke again except once, drunk, weeping into his hands, and whose face I had to reconstruct from a locket because my own memory of her had gone soft as wet paper.

"Lior." Sable's hand on my shoulder. "Whatever you're thinking—"

"I'm not thinking anything."

"You're thinking so loudly the dead are complaining."

* * *

The storm made its opinion known around midnight. Or what I guessed was midnight—the cave kept its own hours, marked only by the water climbing our shins, then our knees. Wind screamed down the flooded passage in gusts that snuffed two of our three lamps. Wren pressed close to my side, seventeen years old and trying very hard not to shake, and I put an arm around her because it was the only order I could give that mattered.

Then, above the wind, I heard it.

Singing.

Not from the salvagers. From the stone.

It was thin, half-drowned, more feeling than melody—but I knew it the way you know a scar. A lullaby about a boat with silver sails, sung off-key in a low smoky voice, the way my mother sang it, the way no one else in the world sang it because she'd made up the third verse herself and never taught it to anyone but me. And I had forgotten the third verse. I had *forgotten* it, years ago, and mourned it without knowing what I'd lost, and now it was pouring out of a rock in a drowned cave like water finding its old channel.

I was moving before I decided to move.

"Lior—" Sable's voice behind me.

"Don't follow me."

"That's the fastest way to make me follow you."

I waded to the altar. Up close the hum became words underneath the singing, hundreds of voices layered like sediment—harvested voices, Council-taken voices, the stolen years of everyone the collectors had hollowed. The ledger Brannoc kept was paper. This was the library itself.

I put both hands on the Tideheart.

* * *

Falling is the closest word. Falling into deep water, where down and up trade places and your body forgets which direction it agreed to drown in.

Gray everywhere. Then not gray—gray resolving into scenes, into weather, into lives. I fell through a fisherman's last morning, the smell of tar and frying onions, his daughter's laugh, and then I was through him and falling through a clerk's terror as masked men held her head between their hands and *drew*, pulling memory out of her like thread from a spool while she screamed silently in a room with no windows. I fell through harvest after harvest. Years of them. Decades. The Harbor Council hadn't taken memories the way I'd imagined—one thief, one crime. They'd farmed people. Whole neighborhoods sheared bare and sent home smiling, docile, grateful, empty.

I tried to grab for control. Rangers are trained for this—ground yourself, name five things real. Salt water on my knees. Wren's breath at my back. Lamp oil. Stone. Wind. But the Tideheart didn't care about my training. It cared about what I wanted, and what I wanted was a lullaby with a made-up third verse, and the stone is generous with want. It has to be. Want is how it feeds.

The gray parted, and there she was.

Not her—her memory of herself, which is a different thing and somehow worse and better. Maren Brightwater, thirty-four years old, standing on the seawall in early spring with her sleeves rolled up, arguing with a man in Council gray. I knew the memory wasn't mine; I'd have been four. But the stone let me stand inside it anyway, a ghost at her shoulder, close enough to see the ink stain on her left knuckle, close enough to hear.

"—and I'm telling you, Aldous, people know. They *know* something's wrong. Half the fishing quarter can't remember their own weddings."

"They remember what they need to remember," the man said. His voice was smooth as oiled rope. I would learn later that his name was Collector Vess, and that I would hate it until my dying day. "The city runs better. Fewer riots. Fewer debts. You've seen the ledgers."

"I've seen the faces." My mother's voice, and oh, it wrecked me, hearing it live instead of remembered—hearing it *angry*. "You didn't take their bad years, you took everything. Tam Wilder's wife looked straight through him yesterday. Their son died last winter and she thanked me for asking about her 'lovely quiet life.' What are you building, Aldous? What needs a city full of people who can't grieve?"

"A stable one."

"And my husband?"

Something shifted in Vess's face. Something almost apologetic, which was somehow the most frightening thing in the whole drowning dark. "Corvin Brightwater filed a petition. Fourteen pages. Witness statements. Shipping manifests showing the collector barges' routes." He sighed. "Maren. He was going to take it to the coastal magistrates. You must see that we couldn't—"

"He's a customs officer. He believes in the law. That's his entire crime."

"Yes," Vess said gently. "That was the problem."

The memory lurched. Now I was in a room I recognized with a sick lurch of my own—the hearing room, the one from my childhood nightmares, though in the dream it had always been empty. It wasn't empty. My father stood at a long table, gaunt, unshaven, fourteen pages of evidence scattered in front of him while three Council members read them with expressions of polite boredom. And my mother was being escorted past him by two guards, and she was fighting them—not screaming, Maren Brightwater didn't scream, she was saying, low and fast and fierce, *"Corvin, look at me, look at me, whatever they say I did, I went willingly, I went to get proof, do you hear me, I went WILLINGLY—"*

And my father looked at her. And I watched—through the stone's merciless clarity—watched the exact moment he believed her, and the exact moment he understood it wouldn't matter, and the moment after that when he decided, right there in front of the tribunal, to stop begging and start burning. He told them what they were. Loudly. On the record. With names.

They ruined him for it, of course. Stripped his post, forged the embezzlement charges, erased him from the customs rolls. But that was cleanup. The ruin was incidental. My father hadn't been destroyed for theft or failure or weakness.

He'd been destroyed for *telling the truth*. And my mother—my mother hadn't abandoned us. She'd gone into the collectors' house on purpose, wearing a petition clerk's badge, to steal proof, and they'd caught her, and instead of killing her they'd done something the Council considered more efficient.

They'd harvested her. All of her. Nineteen years ago. And put what was left of her to work in a Council laundry, folding other people's linens with empty hands, while her husband drank himself toward an early grave under a ruined name and her daughter ran to the desert to become someone—*anyone*—who wasn't a Brightwater.

I had spent nineteen years ashamed of my family.

We had been the only people in the harbor district who ever fought.

* * *

I came back to myself on my knees in rising water, both hands still fused to the stone, sobbing in a way I hadn't sobbed since I was nine years old and too proud to cry at my father's funeral. Someone had hold of my shoulders—Sable, soaked to the waist, shouting my name. Wren was crying too, though I think she'd started before I had. The whole cave had heard the lullaby. The whole cave had heard everything the stone sang out loud, because apparently grief doesn't stay private when it's got a monsoon for an amplifier.

"Maren Brightwater," Brannoc said quietly. In the last lamp's light his face was carved from something older than stone. "Page forty-one of my ledger. *Taken spring tide, year of the Double Harvest.* I wrote her down myself, girl. I write them all down. Somebody has to remember the ones they empty out."

"She went willingly," I said. My voice came out scraped raw. "She went in to get proof. For him. For all of us. And the Council—" I stopped, because the next part wanted to come out as a scream, and there were children in this cave. "They made us the cautionary tale. Everyone in the district knew the Brightwaters as thieves. As traitors. My whole life, every town I rolled into, I braced for someone to spit the name. And it was *their* name that should have been spat. Vess. The whole rotten Council. They farmed an entire coast and called the harvest peace."

Sable crouched in front of me. His sister was somewhere in that stone too—I knew it, he knew it, neither of us said it yet. "Lior. Listen to me. Your father didn't fail. Your mother didn't fall. They were the resistance, and nobody ever told them they'd won anything, because they hadn't yet. But the ledger exists. Brannoc exists. Wren exists. The stone is *full*, Lior—it's so full of them it's singing. That's not defeat. That's a case waiting for a court."

"The Council is coming back with the next tide window," Dellin said grimly from the wall. "Vess doesn't send one boat after a failed one. He'll send everything."

"Then we'd better decide what we're defending," Brannoc said, "before they arrive."

I looked down at the Tideheart, still warm under my palms, still humming my mother's made-up verse. Every instinct I'd built across nineteen years of running said the same thing it had always said: *take it and go, alone, fast, invisible.* Run into the desert. Bury the stone where no one would ever find it. Stay a rumor.

But my mother had walked *into* the collectors' house. My father had stood up in a room designed to break him and burned his own life down rather than swallow the truth. Running wasn't the family business. It was just the thing I'd done instead of grieving.

I lifted my hands off the stone. The lullaby faded, but it didn't vanish—I could still feel it under my skin, patient as tide.

"No," I said, and every head in the cave turned. "Nobody hides it and nobody surrenders it. When Vess comes, he's going to offer a bargain—I've seen how he works now, I've *been* inside how he works. And when he does, I want him to look around this cave and understand exactly what he's up against." I stood, water streaming off me, and found Wren's eyes in the lamplight. "My parents started this fight nineteen years ago. It's past time somebody finished it."

Above us, muffled by forty feet of stone and sea, the storm changed its note—and beneath it, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of engines.

More than one.
