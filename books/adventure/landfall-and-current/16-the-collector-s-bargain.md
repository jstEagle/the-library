## 16. The Collector's Bargain

There is a particular art to arriving somewhere soaked, and I have mastered it.

My name is Ovidian Marsh, and for thirty-one years I have been the Harbor Council's Collector of Surplus Memory. I mention the full title because it matters. Titles are levers. Say *thief* and people bar their doors; say *Collector* and they apologize for making you wait in the rain. I did not invent this trick. I merely perfected it, the way a man perfects anything he does often enough to stop hating it.

The truce flag I carried into Greywater Cove was white linen, properly hemmed, because even deception should be well-made. The storm had paused—the eye of the monsoon sitting over the coast like a cat that has decided not to kill you yet—and in that strange yellow half-light I walked up the shingle with my hands visible and my voice pitched to its gentlest register, and I said the words I have said in a hundred doorways:

"I come under parley. No one needs to be hurt today."

Most of them were lies. Not the no-one-needs-to-be-hurt part—that was true, or true enough, or true in the way that mattered for the next hour. But *I come* implied volition, as though I had chosen this errand, this coast, this miserable salt-rotted cove at the ass-end of the drowned lands. In truth the Council had chosen me the way one chooses an old tool: reluctantly, because nothing newer worked, and with the quiet intention of discarding me after.

They took my weapons at the waterline. Both of them, which flattered me; most settlements only find one. Then old Brannoc—white-bearded, one-eyed, built like something the sea had tried to digest and given up on—walked me through the cove between two salvagers who held their hooks with the casual menace of men who gut fish for a living, and I made my assessments the way I always do.

Fourteen souls, give or take. Two fighters worth worrying about. A girl, young, watching me with the open hatred children haven't yet learned to disguise. A boatwright with burn-scarred hands. And at the fire's edge, apart from everyone, two people who would not look at each other but kept almost looking: the thief Sable Vane, damp and defiant, and the ranger Lior Brightwater, whose name I had carried in my satchel for six days like a stone I intended to throw.

Ah, yes. The Brightwater girl. Let us speak of her, since everyone else in this story insists on doing so.

* * *

I knew her father, once. Corwin Brightwater, harbor surveyor, a decent man with a decent man's fatal flaw: he believed the ledgers were meant to be read. He found the harvest schedules—the extraction quotas, the districts, the names—and instead of doing the sensible thing, which is to say nothing, he began asking questions in rooms where questions are currency debased beyond value.

The Council destroyed him efficiently. Debts fabricated, licenses revoked, a wife who stopped being his wife in every way that could be documented and several that couldn't. I know because I helped draft some of the documentation. I was younger then. I told myself it was clerical work. I have told myself many things over thirty-one years; it is remarkable what a man can carry if he labels the boxes correctly.

What I did not know—what I learned only three days ago, from a patrol report that crossed my desk and then mysteriously failed to be filed—was that Brightwater's wife had been harvested before any of it. Early cohort. District records listed her as *processed, remainder retained*. Remainder retained. That is how we wrote it. As though a woman were soup stock.

And now here was her daughter, standing in a smuggler's cove beside the Tideheart, and here was I, holding the only key to whatever remained of her mother inside that stone.

You will understand, then, why I did not feel entirely like a tool. For the first time in years, I felt like a man holding all the cards, and I want to be honest with you, whoever you are, reading whatever record of this survives: it felt wonderful, and I am ashamed of how wonderful it felt, and I did it anyway.

* * *

Brannoc gave me a stool by the fire, which was either courtesy or theater, and tea that tasted of kelp and iron. Around me the salvagers arranged themselves in a loose crescent—not quite a mob, not quite an audience. Wren, the girl, sat closest, arms crossed, radiating the specific fury of someone who has recently discovered the world is worse than advertised.

Sable Vane stood. "Whatever he's selling," he said, "we're not buying."

"Good," I said. "Because I'm not selling. I'm giving." I let that sit. Silence is a lever too; pull it slowly. "I'm here to offer amnesty. Full amnesty. The theft of Council property from the caravan shrine at Dustmere—forgiven. The flight across the flats—forgiven. The harboring—" I gestured graciously at the entire cove, at Brannoc, at the hooked men, at the furious child. "Forgiven. All of it. The Council is prepared to let the Tideheart matter end quietly."

"And the price?" Brannoc asked. Old men always ask about the price. Young men ask about the prize; old men have learned which question keeps them alive.

"The artifact," I said. "Intact. Unopened. Returned to Council custody."

Vane laughed, short and ugly. "So they can point it at another district. So they can hollow out another town. You want us to hand you the knife and thank you for the amnesty."

"I want you to survive the season," I said mildly. "Whether the Council points anything anywhere after that is, I regret to inform you, profoundly beyond your control. You are fourteen people in a flooded cove with one boat, and the boat, I'm told, is rotten." I turned my palms up—an old negotiator's trick, showing emptiness. "I am not threatening you. Threats are for people who need them. I am simply describing the weather."

It was going exactly as such conversations go, which is to say nowhere, when I turned to Lior Brightwater and played the card I had carried up the shingle.

"Ranger," I said. "Or should I say—Brightwater?"

The cove went still the way water goes still before something rises in it.

"There is a warrant," I continued, gently, regretfully, every syllable upholstered. "Issued six days ago. Desertion of post, obstruction of Council officers, association with known thieves. Your name traveled ahead of you faster than you traveled at all. These good people have been sheltering a fugitive, and I would hate for that fact to complicate the generous terms I've offered them."

I watched it land. I always watch. It is the collector's second nature: watch where the memory goes when you squeeze it. It went through the cove like dye through water—doubt in the boatwright's face, calculation in two of the hook-men, a flicker of something wounded in the girl Wren. Brannoc's single eye narrowed but held. And Vane stepped, quite unconsciously, half in front of Brightwater, which told me more about the state of things than an hour of interrogation would have.

But Brightwater herself—she looked at me the way her father must have looked at ledgers. Not afraid. *Reading.*

"You knew my mother," she said.

Not a question. Damn the woman. Not a question.

* * *

Here is the thing about temptation: it never arrives wearing its own face. It wears the faces of the dead. It comes to you in your mother's voice, your lost love's laugh, the smell of a house that burned down twenty years ago, and it says *I can give this back*, and the terrible truth—the truth I have built a career upon—is that sometimes it can.

I set down my tea. I opened my satchel. And I spoke carefully, because this was the hinge of everything, mine and theirs.

"District Nine, harvest cohort eleven," I said. "A woman named Maren Brightwater. Seamstress. She sang while she worked—badly, according to the intake notes, though the notes also say her children didn't seem to mind." I watched Lior Brightwater's breath change. "The extraction was incomplete. Some memories resist. Hers resisted more than most—they logged it as an anomaly. What remained of her was archived rather than distributed, because the fragments wouldn't hold a new identity cleanly. She has been in Council storage for nineteen years. Or rather—" I corrected myself, precisely, cruelly, because precision is mercy's sharpest knife— "*fragments* of her have. Until recently, those fragments were stored in the Tideheart itself. Which means that everything the stone holds, the stone holds still. Including her."

The fire cracked. Somewhere out in the eye of the storm, water moved against the shingle with a sound like slow applause.

"I cannot restore her," I said. "No one can. Nineteen years is nineteen years; the woman who sang badly is gone, and I won't insult you by pretending otherwise. But the memories—the voice, the songs, the shape of her hands, the day you were born, whatever the collectors caught before she slipped their nets—all of it is in that stone, and all of it can be returned to *you*. Whole. Yours. Every stolen year of hers that you never got to keep." I leaned forward. "That is my offer, Lior Brightwater. Not amnesty. Amnesty is paper. This is blood. Give me the Tideheart intact, and I will see her fragments extracted and delivered to you before the season turns. You will hear your mother again. You will remember her remembering you."

I have made many offers in my life. I have watched strong people crumble at smaller ones. I confess I expected her to waver longer than she did.

She wavered for perhaps four heartbeats. I counted. Counting is what I do instead of praying.

Then Sable Vane said, "Lior. Don't."

* * *

He crossed to her fast, low-voiced, and I did not pretend not to listen. Eavesdropping is simply negotiation conducted with patience.

"It's a lie," Vane said. "Or worse—it's true. That's the trap of it. They'll extract her fragments, sure, and hand you a vial, and the moment the Tideheart's back in Council hands they'll rearm it. Point it at the next district. Hollow out the next town's mothers and fathers and sisters—" his voice caught on *sisters*, and I noted it, filed it, the hollowed sister from the coastal records, Serah Vane, processed remainder distributed— "and your mother's memory becomes the payment that buys it. You'd be handing them the weapon loaded with her."

"They have it anyway if I refuse," Brightwater said quietly. "Eventually."

"Not if it's in pieces."

"And then she's in pieces forever." Brightwater's voice did not rise. That was the frightening thing about her; nothing rose, everything deepened. "You don't get to make this cheap for me, Sable. Don't you dare make it cheap."

"I'm not—" He dragged a hand through his hair. "I watched what they left of my sister. I know exactly what those fragments are. They're not her, Lior. They're the *shape* where she was. You'll spend the rest of your life pouring yourself into a mold that doesn't fit, and they'll have won twice."

"And if I don't take it," she said, "I'll spend the rest of my life wondering. Wondering is its own mold."

Gods, but she was good. I sat on my stool drinking terrible kelp tea and felt something I had not felt in decades, which was the uncomfortable recognition that I was watching two people conduct, in miniature and in earnest, the argument I had been having with myself for thirty-one years. Vane was arguing with the Collector. Brightwater was arguing with the orphan. And I sat there wearing both their opponents' faces.

Wren stood up. The girl. Of course the girl stood up; the young always believe courage is a solution rather than a cost.

"Don't listen to him," Wren said. "He's the one who emptied them. He *did* it—to people, real people, whole towns—he stood in their kitchens with his little ledger and he took their lives out of their heads, and now he's standing there offering to sell one back like—like he's the cure for the disease he spread."

"Wren," Brannoc rumbled.

"No! Everyone's being polite to him because of the stupid flag, and he's *loving* it, look at him, he's loving every second—" Her voice cracked. "My da doesn't know my birthday anymore. He knows the tide tables better than he knows my face. And this man probably wrote the order."

I looked at her. I did not look away, because looking away would have been a kind of lying, and I had decided—I had decided sometime on the walk up the shingle, in the yellow light of the storm's eye—that today, of all days, I would try to stop lying.

"Yes," I said. "I did. District Seven, nine years ago. The order bears my seal. I stood in your kitchen, child. I remember your father's face better than he remembers yours. One of us should."

The cove made a sound—a collective indrawn breath, the sound of a wound being acknowledged. Wren stared at me with tears she refused to let fall, and I thought: *there it is. There is the thing I spent thirty-one years collecting out of other people's heads, trying to make sure no one ever collected it out of mine.* Grief with the lid off.

"Which is why," I said, and my voice, damn it, my voice was not perfectly steady, "you should know that I am not lying to her. The fragments exist. The offer is real. And the Council will rearm the stone whether I deliver it or not—unless it is delivered broken, or unless the woman holding it is cleverer than the Council has ever been." I rose from the stool, joints announcing themselves, thirty-one years settling into my knees. "I have given you the terms. I will give you until the tide turns. That is not generosity. That is the eye of the storm closing, and all of us running out of weather to hide in."

* * *

I walked to the mouth of the cave alone, because a negotiator knows when presence becomes pressure, and pressure curdles offers into threats. Behind me I heard them murmuring—Brannoc's gravel bass, Vane's urgent tenor, the girl's fierce whisper—and above it all, or beneath it, the Tideheart humming on its altar like a struck bell that would not finish ringing.

I stood at the cave mouth and watched the sea begin to rise, and I will tell you the truth, since I appear to have started telling it: I hoped she would refuse.

There. It's said. Thirty-one years of service, and in the last hour of usefulness I found myself hoping the mark would spit in my face. Because I had seen her count those four heartbeats, and I had seen what lived inside them—not greed, not even grief, but *love*, the real article, the kind that measures cost honestly—and I understood that if Lior Brightwater traded her mother's ghost for the Council's leash, then everything I had ever taken from anyone would finally, permanently, have been worth taking. It would mean love could be bought. It would mean the whole filthy ledger balanced.

And if she refused—if she stood in front of her mother's remaining voice and said *no*—then I would have to live with knowing that what I did for a living was impossible to undo, and that some things, at least, could not be purchased at any price.

Either way, I lost. I had known that since the shingle. The truce flag was for them; the surrender was mine.

The tide turned. Water climbed the stones outside, patient as a debt collector. And behind me, boots on wet rock—two sets, coming toward me together, side by side, the thief and the ranger, and Lior Brightwater's voice carrying flat and clear across the cave:

"Collector. We have a counteroffer."

I turned around. Outside, the storm's eye was closing, and the first black wall of the monsoon's return stood up over the horizon like a verdict.
