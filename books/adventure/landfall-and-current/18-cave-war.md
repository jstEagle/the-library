## 18. Cave War

**LIOR**

The tide came in like a debt collector.

I'd heard it from the mouth of the cave an hour before I saw it—a low grinding hush, the sound of a very large thing deciding it was done waiting politely. The storm outside had spent itself down to a snarling wind, and through the ragged seam where the cave met the sea I watched the water climb the flats in long gray sheets, patient and total, until the entrance was a shimmering curtain and we were all of us—salvagers, thieves, disgraced rangers, one hollow-eyed old man with a ledger—sealed inside with the Tideheart like sardines in a jar that somebody had very expensive plans for.

"They'll come at high water," Sable said. He'd said it four times now, each time with the same flat certainty, the way a man repeats a diagnosis he doesn't like. "The collector won't wait for the surge to pass. High water means the patrol boats can put men right at the mouth. No wading. No exposure."

"And we can't leave," Wren said, "because leaving means swimming."

"Leaving means drowning," Brannoc corrected, mildly, the way he corrected everything, as though drowning were a minor clerical error. "Swimming implies a destination."

I loved him for that. I want that on the record, because of what happened after.

We'd spent the hours since the collector's departure doing the arithmetic of a losing fight. The cave's inner chamber was a single wide throat with the Tideheart's altar at the far end—a shelf of black stone where the artifact sat, dull as a river pebble, humming its low wrong note that I felt in my teeth more than heard. One entrance, now underwater. One back fissure too narrow for a grown man, which Wren had measured with her own shoulders and declared "maybe, if you hate yourself."

"So we fight in a bottle," I said.

"So we fight in a bottle," Brannoc agreed, and started handing out knives like a host handing out drinks.

I want to tell you I had a plan. Rangers have plans; it's the whole trade. You read the ground, you pick the ground, you make the ground work for you. But the ground here belonged to the sea and the sea belonged to the Council's coin, and all I had was a bottle, nine people, and a stone that ate memories if you held it too long.

I'd been careful not to touch it since the altar. Mostly. My hand kept drifting toward it anyway, the way a tongue finds a broken tooth. Somewhere inside that dull gray heart was my mother's voice. I'd heard it. Three words, maybe four, before I'd wrenched myself away with my ears ringing and an hour missing from my own head. *Lior, love, run.* That was the message. That had always been the message. My whole life had been one long obedient run, and I was so very tired of being good at it.

"Ranger." Brannoc's voice, low, beside me. He'd come to the mouth where I stood watching the water curtain, and he stood the way old sailors stand, swaying to a swell that wasn't there. "The collector named you. In front of everyone. Five hundred crowns."

"I heard."

"Some of my people looked at each other when he said it. I won't lie to you in a cave."

"I'd be disappointed if you did."

He nodded slowly, working something in his jaw. "I've buried a lot of names in that ledger of mine. Names the Council took. Names I took, when the taking was cheaper than the fighting." He looked at the water. "I'm too old to be ashamed of it and too stubborn not to be. But hear me: whatever the others decide when the boats come—my decision was made the moment that man walked into my cove under a white flag and used it to sell a child."

"Wren."

"Wren." He said her name like setting down something heavy. "So. When it starts, you keep her behind you, and you don't argue with an old man about where he stands. Agreed?"

It was, I noted, not a question. I'd known Brannoc for eleven days and he had never once asked a question he didn't already hold the answer to.

"Agreed," I said, and we shook on it, his hand dry and hard as driftwood, and that was the last bargain we ever made.

* * *

**SABLE**

I've stolen a lot of things. Watches, ledgers, a bishop's horse, once, which I do not recommend. You learn things about people when you take what's theirs. You learn that most people guard their money badly and their secrets worse, and that nearly everyone keeps one thing they'd die before surrendering, and it's never what you'd guess. A cook I knew guarded a chipped cup. A magistrate guarded a lock of his sister's hair, which was awkward, because his sister was the one I was robbing.

I'm telling you this because when the patrol boats came, I finally understood what I guarded, and it wasn't the Tideheart.

The water curtain at the cave mouth shivered, then broke. They came through it in a line, six of them in the first wave, oilskin-cloaked, lantern-shielded, moving with the drilled economy of men who had done this in worse places. Behind them, through the gap, I could see the patrol boat's lanterns swinging at anchor and the collector standing in the bow with his hands folded, watching us the way a man watches a scale settle.

The collector. Pellam Harrow, he'd said his name was, as if giving us a name was a courtesy and not a threat. The man who had stood in my mother's kitchen eleven years ago and taken my sister's memory out of her head with a device like a tuning fork, and left her smiling at the wall. He'd come to the cove under truce and offered amnesty and named Lior a fugitive with a bounty, and he'd smiled the whole time, and I had understood with total clarity that I was going to kill him or die trying, and that neither outcome would fix anything, and that I was going to do it anyway.

But first there were six men with crossbows.

"Ranger!" I shouted it across the chamber because she was at the mouth and I was at the altar and the geometry was wrong. "Left side, the shelf!"

She was already moving. I'll say this for Lior Brightwater: she talks about running like it's a disease she's cured of, but when it counted she moved like water finding a downhill. She took the left shelf with two steps and a slide, put her back to the stone, and started giving orders in the flat ranger cadence that made even Brannoc's people straighten up.

"Crossbows first. They'll loose once in the dark and then it's blades. Wren—behind me. Brannoc—"

"Right shelf," Brannoc said. "I know my own cave, girl."

The first bolt came and went over my head and buried itself in the altar stone with a crack like a knuckle rapping on a coffin. The Tideheart hummed louder. I'd swear the thing *enjoyed* it.

Then it was knives and dark and shouting, and I stopped being a narrator and became a body with a blade, which is the only honest kind of fighting there is.

* * *

**WREN**

I had a boat hook. That was my whole armament, a boat hook with a bent tine, and I stood behind Lior Brightwater like she'd told me to, and I watched her fight, and I want to describe it properly because I've heard the stories about rangers all my life and the stories are wrong.

The stories say rangers are ghosts. That they come out of nowhere and go back into it. But Lior wasn't a ghost in that cave. She was the most *there* person I've ever seen. Every man who came at her, she saw—really saw, the way she'd taught me to see weather, reading what a body was going to do before the body knew. She didn't fight like she was killing anyone. She fought like she was correcting them. A man swung a cutlass and she turned it past her with the flat of her knife and put her boot in his chest and he went into the tide pool and didn't come back for it. She wasn't cruel. She was *exact*, and it was the exactness that was terrifying, because you can't hide from someone who sees you that completely.

I know, because she saw me too. Halfway through, when a second wave came through the water curtain and one of them grabbed my boat hook and hauled, she was there—across the chamber, through two men, impossibly fast—and she took the man's wrist and broke his grip and put herself between me and his knife like it was nothing, like it was *nothing*, and she said, "Down," and I went down, and above me she and Sable Vane closed around me like a door.

That's when I understood what Brannoc had been building all these years. Not a salvage crew. A door. And I was inside it.

I got up. I kept hold of my stupid bent boat hook. And when the third wave came, I didn't stand behind anyone.

* * *

**LIOR**

The third wave was the one with the collector's sergeant in it, a big methodical man with a boarding axe who'd clearly been told to stop being polite. He came through the water curtain and bellowed something about the Council's law, and Brannoc answered him.

I didn't hear all of it. There was a lot of noise. But I heard the shape of it—Brannoc standing on his right shelf with his old salvage knife, telling the sergeant that the Council's law had never reached past the high-tide line and wouldn't tonight, and the sergeant telling him he was an old fool protecting a fugitive worth five hundred crowns, and Brannoc saying—and this I heard clear as a bell through all the chaos—

"Her name's not a price. It's *Brightwater*. Write it down."

Then he stepped off the shelf.

I want to be precise about what Brannoc did, because precision is the only mourning I'm any good at. The sergeant was going for me. I was engaged—two men, blades, the narrow ground doing its work but slowly—and the sergeant had the angle and the weight and the axe, and Brannoc saw it the way he saw everything, in the long slow way of a man who'd spent sixty years reading water. He stepped off his shelf into the sergeant's reach, into the one place an old man with a knife could matter, and he took the boarding axe in his left arm so that it couldn't reach me, and he put his knife into the sergeant's throat with his right, and the sergeant died, and so did Brannoc, some seconds later, on the wet stone, with Wren screaming his name and the tide coming in over all of us like it hadn't noticed.

He never asked a question he didn't know the answer to. He knew exactly where he stood.

We drove the rest of them back to the water. It wasn't skill by then, or not mostly. It was the thing the collector had never priced in, the thing the Council had spent thirty years trying to prove didn't exist: nine people who had decided, in a leaking cave, that they belonged to each other. The patrol broke. They went back through the curtain in a ragged retreat, and the boat's lanterns swung away toward deeper water, and for a moment there was only the storm-wind at the mouth and the sound of Wren holding Brannoc and the Tideheart humming its low, greedy, patient note, as if it had all the time in the world.

It did, I think. It was waiting to see what we'd do with ours.

* * *

**SABLE**

We buried him at the back of the cave, above the tide line, in a niche where the salvagers had laid two others before him, and Wren marked the stone with the bent tine of her boat hook, scratching his name deep, because she said names scratched deep lasted longer and she was right and I wasn't going to argue with her about anything ever again.

Lior stood apart, the way she always did, but not far apart. That was the difference. Eleven days ago she'd have been at the cave mouth, already halfway to gone. Now she stood three steps from Wren, close enough to touch, watching the water curtain with her arms folded and her jaw set, and when Wren's hand found hers in the dark, she let it.

The collector would come back. Men like Pellam Harrow always come back; it's the whole of their religion. The boat's lanterns hadn't gone to deep water to give up. They'd gone to wait for the next high tide, or the tide after, or for reinforcements from the harbor, and somewhere in that arithmetic was the moment when nine exhausted people in a bottle would finally lose.

Unless there wasn't a next tide to wait out. Unless there was nothing left to wait *for*.

I looked at the Tideheart on its black altar, humming, patient, holding my sister's stolen years and Lior's mother's voice and three hundred other people's whole selves, and I looked at Lior looking at it, and I saw her hand drift toward it the way it always drifted, the tongue finding the broken tooth.

"Lior," I said.

"I know," she said.

She picked it up.

* * *

**WREN**

She picked it up and the whole cave changed. The hum went through the stone and through us, and the water at the mouth lit up from underneath, faint and green and wrong, and Lior Brightwater stood there holding the Tideheart with her eyes already going somewhere I couldn't follow.

"Whatever you're about to do," Sable said, "do it fast. They'll be back at the next tide."

"I know," she said again, and turned to me, and her face was the ranger face, the exact one, the one that saw everything. "Wren. When the water goes green—when the memories go out of it—everyone in this cave is going to feel things that aren't theirs. Hold onto each other. That's the whole instruction. Hold on."

"Hold onto what?" I asked, because I was fifteen and scared and the water was glowing.

She smiled. It was the first time I'd ever seen her do it, and it was crooked and tired and it was the most honest thing I'd seen all year.

"Onto each other," she said. "That's what it's for."

Then she walked toward the altar, and the Tideheart began, very softly, to sing.
