## 19. Landfall

The water came for us like a debt collector.

I heard it before I saw it—a sound like the whole ocean clearing its throat—and then it was in the cave mouth, a wall of grey-green fury lit from behind by lightning, and everything Brannoc's people had built to hold the line went under in a single breath. The barricade of salvaged timbers. The netting weighted with anchor stones. Wren's clever little channel dug to divert the surge. All of it, gone, swallowed by a sea that had decided the negotiation was over.

"Down!" Sable's hand closed on my collar and hauled me behind the altar's plinth as the wave broke against the far wall and came back at us as waist-deep chaos. The Tideheart sat on its stone shelf above our heads, dull as river rock, humming that low note I'd been hearing in my teeth for three days now.

The patrol came with the water.

They'd waited for the tide—of course they had. The Collector wasn't a man who took chances; he took opportunities. I counted six shapes wading through the churn, lanterns dead, blades out, moving with the ugly competence of people who did this for wages. Behind them, half-drowned in the flooded entrance, I could see the Collector himself standing on his boat's deck, dry under an oilskin canopy, watching us like a man watching an auction he already knew the outcome of.

"Lior." Sable was beside me, bleeding from somewhere above his hairline, water streaming off him. "Tell me you have a plan."

"I have a stone," I said. "And a wall at my back."

"That's not a plan."

"It's a position."

He almost laughed. In another life, we might have been friends who joked like this over bad coffee instead of strangers who traded wounds in a flooded cave. "Brannoc—" he started, and couldn't finish.

Brannoc. Old Brannoc, who'd taken two strangers in without questions because questions were a luxury and kindness wasn't. Who'd stepped into the Collector's path with nothing but a boat hook and seventy years of stubbornness, buying us the minutes we needed. The water had taken him toward the cave mouth and the patrol had finished what the sea started, and there was no time to grieve him now except in the way that mattered: by making his death cost them something.

Wren appeared out of the murk at my left, hauling one of the younger salvagers—Tally, nine years old, terrified—up onto the high ledge near the altar. Her face was a mask of salt spray and fury, and when she looked at me she didn't look away, and I understood that whatever happened in the next five minutes, this girl had decided I was someone worth standing next to. That was its own kind of weight. Heavier than any I'd carried running.

"The ledge," I said. "Get everyone up there. Behind the altar. Go."

"And you?"

"I'm going to have a conversation with a rock."

She didn't argue. That, more than anything, told me how bad it looked.

* * *

The patrol reached the shallows inside the cave and fanned out, and I did the arithmetic I always did—their numbers, their footing, the current pulling at their knees, the distance to the nearest blade—and the arithmetic said *you lose*. Six trained men against a ranger with a knife and a thief with a boat hook, defending a ledge full of children and grieving salvagers. The math didn't care about righteousness. Math never did.

But then the salvagers stopped retreating.

It started with a woman named Osha, who'd spent the week teaching me which kelp was edible, driving a sharpened oar into the chest of the lead patrolman. Then the fisherman twins, hauling a net across the water and tangling two more. Sable roaring down from the ledge swinging the boat hook like it owed him money. It wasn't a battle so much as a refusal—an entire cove of invisible people deciding, all at once, to be seen, and making the Council's men pay for every inch of seeing.

I got to the altar.

Up close, the Tideheart wasn't dull at all. That had been the trick of it, I realized—like me, it had spent years pretending to be nothing. Now, with the storm pressing in and the water rising around the plinth, light moved beneath its surface like fish under ice. Vast schools of it. Memories, thousands of them, harvested from a coast full of people who'd woken one morning missing years and been told it was fever, or grief, or their own failing minds.

My mother might be in there.

The thought hit me the way the wave had hit the barricade. Somewhere in that stone was the woman who'd taught me to read stars, whose voice I'd found in the deep places of the artifact three nights ago, singing a lullaby I hadn't heard since I was seven. What was left of her. What the Council had left of her after they hollowed her out to punish a father who'd dared to count their stolen ledgers.

"Lior Brightwater!" The Collector's voice cut through the din, amplified by some brass contraption, disgustingly calm. "The amnesty stands! Your mother's memories, restored whole—you have my word!"

His word. A man whose entire profession was taking things people couldn't afford to lose.

"You know what your word is worth!" I shouted back.

"I know what the alternative is worth!" A pause, and then, softer, almost gentle, which was worse: "You could have her back. Every year. Every voice. Don't you want to hear her again?"

God help me, I did. My hands were on the stone and part of me—the part that had been running for fifteen years, the part that kept moving because stopping meant remembering—that part wanted it so badly my vision blurred. One bargain. One intact artifact handed over, and I'd walk out of this cave with my mother's laughter in my ears.

Then I felt it through my palms: the faint erosion, the tide of the thing pulling at *my* edges, the way it had pulled at everyone who held it too long. The price the cave charged. The memories in the stone weren't stored, I understood suddenly—they were *held*, clenched, kept from their owners like ransom. And the Council didn't want to free them. They wanted to rearm them. To keep a coast full of hollowed people easy to rule.

The memories didn't belong to the Council. They didn't belong to me, either. They belonged to the fisherman who couldn't remember his daughter's face. To Sable's sister, drifting somewhere in her haze. To every name in Brannoc's ledger.

They belonged to the people. And the people were right here.

"Sable!" I turned. He was holding the last two patrolmen at the base of the ledge, soaked, exhausted, alive. "Your sister—what would she want? If she could choose?"

He looked at me across the churning water, and I watched him do the same terrible math I'd done, and I watched love win. "Break it," he said. "Lior—*break it!*"

I raised the Tideheart above my head.

The Collector screamed something—I'd remember later that it sounded like *no*, stripped of all his polish, just a man watching money burn—and the first patrolman lunged for the ledge, and Wren stepped into his path with an oar taller than she was, and I brought the stone down on the altar's edge with everything I had. Fifteen years of running. Fifteen years of my father's ruined name. Brannoc's body somewhere in the flood. All of it, in my arms, coming down.

The Tideheart shattered.

* * *

There is no honest way to describe what happened next, so I'll tell it plainly and let you think I'm exaggerating.

Light came out of it. Not a flash—light doesn't flash, light *pours*, and this poured upward through the water and the storm-cracked ceiling of the cave and out into the monsoon sky, and everywhere it touched, rain fell differently. I felt it leave me like a breath I'd been holding since childhood. And in the light were voices. Thousands of them, layered, overlapping—a coast's worth of stolen years singing themselves home.

I heard my mother. Not a fragment this time, not a lullaby worn thin by storage—her, whole, laughing at something my father said, saying *Lior, come see the storm come in.* And then the voice passed through me and past me, out over the water, going where it had always belonged, and I stood in the empty space it left and discovered the space wasn't empty at all. It hurt. It hurt like a wound cleaning itself.

The patrolmen stopped fighting. You could see it happen to them—one moment blades, the next, men standing knee-deep in floodwater with their faces turned up, weeping, remembering things the Council had scraped out of them to make them obedient. Half the Council's muscle, I realized, had been harvested too. They'd been guarding the vault from the inside without knowing it.

The Collector's boat was turning, fleeing, his brass voice cracking orders nobody followed anymore. The storm drove him out past the point and the sea, which keeps its own ledger, took an interest.

The water began to fall.

Not all at once—the monsoon had days left in it—but the surge eased, the cave mouth cleared, and the light kept pouring out of the broken stone long after there should have been nothing left, streaming inland on the wind, and I knew—I *knew*—that in houses all along the drowned coast, people were waking up. Hollow ones sitting up in bed with their own names in their mouths. Fishermen remembering daughters. Sisters surfacing from haze.

Sable reached me first. He didn't say anything. He just put his forehead against mine, both of us shaking, salt and blood and rain between us, and stayed.

Wren climbed up onto the altar ledge, oar still in her white-knuckled grip, and looked at the shards of the Tideheart scattered in the floodwater, dimming now, ordinary, just stone.

"Was it worth it?" she asked. She was nine years old and asking real questions, the way only the young and the grieving can.

I thought about my mother's voice, passing through me and away. About the years I'd never get back, hers or mine. About Brannoc, who'd never see what his stubbornness bought.

"No," I said honestly. "And yes. Both at once. Get used to that—it's most of what staying feels like."

She nodded slowly, like I'd given her something useful, and maybe I had.

Outside, the storm was breaking apart, and through the tearing clouds the first clean light of morning fell across the water—and on the horizon, small and patient, sails were appearing. Not the Council's. Word traveled fast when a thousand minds woke up remembering exactly who had robbed them.

Sable followed my gaze and exhaled. "That's going to be complicated."

"Everything worth keeping is," I said, and picked my way down from the altar to find out who was coming, and whether the coast we'd just set free would need someone to stand watch over it.

For the first time in fifteen years, I hoped the answer was yes.
