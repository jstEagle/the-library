## 20. Aftercurrent

The storm broke the way a fever breaks—not all at once, but in stages, each one leaving you unsure whether you'd survived it or merely been granted a reprieve.

I knew because I was awake for all of it. I had been awake for most of the night, wedged into a niche of the sea cave with Wren asleep against my shoulder and Lior standing watch at the waterline like a woman who had made peace with the ocean and intended to supervise the arrangement personally. The rain had hammered the cave mouth all night, turning the world outside into a gray roar, and then—somewhere past what I guessed was the third hour of morning—the roar had thinned. Softened. Become something almost like weather again instead of a verdict.

"Is it stopping?" Wren mumbled against my arm.

"It's easing," I said. "Don't get sentimental about it yet."

"I'm not sentimental." She yawned enormously. "I'm practical. Practical people know when to be sentimental."

I decided not to argue with that, mostly because she was right and partly because my entire body felt like someone had wrung me out and hung me over a rail to dry. The fight was less than a day behind us. Brannoc's body lay wrapped in sailcloth near the back of the cave where the salvagers had carried him, and every time my eyes drifted that way, the grief arrived on schedule, punctual as tide. We would bury him when the ground allowed it. The ground did not allow much of anything yet.

Lior turned from the waterline. Even exhausted—*especially* exhausted—she had the look of someone cataloguing exits. Old habits. But there was something else under it now, something I'd watched settle into her over the past two days since she'd put her hands on the Tideheart's altar and chosen destruction over surrender. She looked less like a woman running from her name. She looked like a woman deciding whether she wanted it back.

"The surge's receding," she said. "Give it an hour and we can walk out."

"Walk out to what?"

"That," she said, "is the question."

* * *

The answer arrived before the hour did.

It came as light. I want to be precise about this, because afterward people would describe it a dozen different ways—some said dawn, some said lightning, some said the Tideheart itself—and all of them were wrong or incomplete. What happened was this: the last wall of the storm tore open above the cove, and through the gap came a column of pale gold, thin as rope, that dropped out of the clouds and struck the flooded flats below us. And then another. And another. Dozens of them, stitched across the drowned coast like a loom working itself, and where each thread of light touched water, it sank without a ripple.

"What is that?" Wren whispered.

"Memories," Lior said quietly. Beside me, I felt her go very still. "They're going home."

I had seen the Tideheart work only twice—once when the Council used it publicly, years ago, to "restore civic harmony" after the dock riots, which meant they reached into a crowd of three hundred people and took their anger out like pulling a splinter, leaving them smiling and empty; and once in this cave, when Lior shattered it. The shattering had been light too, but light with nowhere to go, swallowed by stone and seawater. This was different. This was light with an address.

We climbed out onto the rocks above the cove to watch. The settlement of Greywater Cove had taken the flood hard—half the stilt-houses were splintered, the drying racks were kindling, Brannoc's great hall had lost its roof entirely—but the people were coming out of the high shelters now, blinking into the strange gold morning, and one by one they began to stop. To stand still. To put their hands over their mouths, or their hearts, or sometimes just over their eyes, the way you do when you're trying to hold something inside your skull that has suddenly, violently, come back.

Old Maren, who had fished the cove for forty years and whom I'd always known as a man of perhaps six complete thoughts, dropped his bucket and sat down right there in the mud and began to weep. Not the weeping of grief. I've heard grief plenty. This was the weeping of a man remembering his daughter's face—a daughter, we learned later, who had died at sea eleven years ago, whose name he hadn't been able to hold on to since the collectors came through, and who had just walked back into his mind whole and laughing and twenty-three years old forever.

"They're getting everything back?" Wren asked. Her voice was small. "All of it? All those years?"

"As much as the stone held," Lior said. "Which was—" She stopped. Recalculated. "Most of it. The Council was greedy. Greed makes good record-keeping."

Down in the cove, the light-threads kept falling. A woman I didn't know screamed once, joyfully, and laughed until she had to sit down. Two brothers who hadn't spoken in years—I knew them by sight only, the ones who ran the net-mending shed—grabbed each other by the shoulders and stood forehead to forehead, saying nothing, saying everything. Somewhere a child was asking questions, rapid-fire, delighted: *Who is that? Why is everyone crying? Is this a holiday?*

And underneath all of it, threading through the gold like a dark seam, was the other thing returning. Because memory doesn't come back clean. It comes back with its edges attached—with the shape of the hand that took it. People were remembering not just their stolen years but the taking. The collectors' boats. The truce flags. The polite men with the ledgers who smiled and said *this will only hurt a moment* and were lying.

By midmorning, someone had dragged the Council's customs skiff—it had washed up hull-cracked on the flats—out to deep water and burned it. Nobody organized it. Nobody stopped it either.

"Well," I said. "That's going to complicate the amnesty negotiations."

"There aren't going to be amnesty negotiations," Lior said.

"No," I agreed. "There are not."

* * *

I left the cove in the early afternoon, when the water had dropped enough to make the causeway passable, and I walked the long spit toward the mainland with my heart going harder than it had during any part of the fight.

My sister lived—or had lived, or existed—in a rooming house in Greywater's mainland quarter, in a narrow bed by a narrow window, attended by a landlady who charged fair rates and asked no questions, which in the Council's coast was the highest form of charity available. Pell. My sister Pell. Four years older than me, sharper than me in every way that mattered, and hollowed out eight years ago by a collector named Vess who wore grey gloves and apologized beautifully while he worked.

I say *hollowed* because that's the honest word. The Council's harvests weren't crude. They didn't leave drooling husks—they left people who could function, who could nod and smile and sweep a floor and answer simple questions, so long as nobody asked them anything that mattered. Ask Pell her name and she'd tell you. Ask her our mother's lullaby, or why she flinched at gull-cry, or what she'd been saving her coin for, and you'd get a pleasant blankness, a smile like a door painted on a wall. I used to visit every month and talk at her for an hour, telling her things that happened to us, hoping some of it snagged somewhere. It never did. She'd see me out at the end with perfect courtesy and close the door, and I'd stand in the hall hating myself for hoping, and then hate myself more for coming back anyway, and then come back anyway.

The rooming house still stood. That was the first miracle. The second was waiting on the stairs.

Pell was sitting on the third-floor landing, wrapped in a blanket, and she was crying—but it was the Maren kind of crying, the *returning* kind, and when she saw me she didn't give me the painted-door smile. She gave me the other one. The real one, crooked on the left side, the one I hadn't seen since I was nineteen years old.

"Sable," she said. "You absolute idiot. You went and got taller without me."

I don't remember crossing the landing. I remember that her arms were around me and she was thinner than she should have been and her hair smelled of salt and cheap soap and she was gripping the back of my shirt like I might be repossessed, and I remember that I was making a sound I didn't know I could make, something between laughing and sobbing that I will deny to my grave if anyone asks.

"You're here," she kept saying. "You're here, you're here—"

"Where else would I be?"

"Don't." She pulled back and hit my chest with the flat of her hand, not hard. "Don't do that. Don't be funny. I have eight years and I can't find most of them and the ones I found are—" Her breath hitched. "Sable, I remember him taking it. I remember the gloves. I remember being *angry*, and I haven't been angry in eight years, I couldn't even find it, and now it's all here at once and I don't know where to put it."

"Put it down for now," I said. "You can pick it up later. Anger keeps."

She laughed wetly and wiped her face with the blanket. "Where did it come from? The landlady says there was light falling out of the sky like the gods are paying out wages."

"A friend of ours broke a rock," I said.

"A rock."

"A very important rock."

She studied me with the old sharpness, the look that used to catch me stealing biscuits off the windowsill before I'd finished the thought of stealing them. "You stole it, didn't you. Whatever it was. You stole it and broke it and that's why you smell like a cave and have a cut over your eye."

"I didn't steal it," I said. "I *liberated* it. There's a legal distinction."

"There is not."

"There is now. I'm writing the law myself."

She laughed again—that laugh, that exact laugh—and then her face changed, and I watched the new memories move through her like weather. "The collectors," she said slowly. "Vess. He'll come back, won't he. They'll send someone. They always—"

"They won't," I said. "Not the way they did. Something happened at the cave. The Council's collector came with terms and Lior Brightwater told him no, and then the patrol came, and—" I paused. "Brannoc died, Pell. Holding the line so the rest of us wouldn't have to."

"Brannoc." She said the name carefully, tasting it. "The salvager. Mother used to trade with him, before. He gave me a carved gull once. I'd forgotten the gull." Her jaw tightened. "I'm forgetting things *again*, but these are mine to forget. That's different. Do you understand? It's different when it's yours."

I understood. I understood so completely that I couldn't speak for a moment, so I just nodded, and she took my hand, and we sat together on the landing while the afternoon light came through the stairwell window, ordinary light now, the gold threads all spent, the sky scrubbed clean and blue as a mended sail.

* * *

I stayed until dusk, and then I walked back along the causeway because there was work, and because Pell had finally fallen asleep in her chair mid-sentence—she was telling me about a boy she'd loved at seventeen, a story I had never heard, a gift I hadn't known I was owed—and because sleeping people deserve to wake up to a world that kept functioning while they rested. That was the whole job, really. That had always been the whole job.

The cove was lit with fires when I returned. Real celebration fires, driftwood and lamp-oil, reckless in the way people are only reckless when they've decided the worst has already happened. Salvagers were hauling wreckage into orderly piles with a ferocity that looked suspiciously like joy. Someone was playing a fiddle badly enough that it had to be sincere. Wren found me before I'd cleared the causeway and fell into step beside me, walking the way she'd learned to walk in the last week—like the ground was worth paying attention to.

"Pell woke up?" she asked.

"She woke up."

"And she knew you?"

"She insulted my height and called me an idiot within ten seconds." I grinned. "Knew me better than I know myself."

Wren nodded solemnly, filing this away. Then: "Lior's at the cave."

My grin faded. "Of course she is."

"She goes every few hours. Sits inside. Comes back out." Wren kicked a stone off the causeway with unnecessary violence. "Wren-the-wise thinks she's checking whether anything's left of the Tideheart. Wren-the-worried thinks she's saying goodbye to her mother."

"Wren-the-fourteen shouldn't carry both those opinions at once. It's heavy freight."

"I'm fifteen in the spring."

"Noted for the record."

We reached the cave mouth as the last light went. Lior was there, exactly where Wren said, sitting on the altar shelf with her knees drawn up, and beside her—this caught me short—was old Maren's daughter's name scratched into the rock, one of dozens, maybe hundreds, that the salvagers had begun carving into the cave walls since morning. A ledger in stone. Every name the Council had taken, written where the sea could keep it. Brannoc's ledger, continued by other hands.

Lior heard me and didn't turn. "Her voice is gone," she said, when I'd settled beside her. "In the stone. I keep reaching for it and there's nothing. Just the shape of where it was." She was quiet a moment. "But everyone else's came back. Everyone in the cove. So hers went somewhere. Or became something. Or—" She exhaled. "I don't have the theology for it yet."

"Maybe you don't need the theology. Maybe you need the burial."

"Those aren't exclusive."

"No," I admitted. "They rarely are."

Below us, the cove fires crackled. The fiddle stumbled onward heroically. Out past the breakwater, the sea lay flat and silvered under a rising moon, and for the first time in the week I'd known this coast, it looked like what it was instead of what it threatened: just water. Enormous, patient, indifferent water, keeping its own counsel.

"Lior," I said. "The Council's leaderless. Half its collectors are going to run and half are going to dig in, and somebody's going to have to decide which is which, and keep the ones who dig in from doing it with fire this time. Somebody with a ranger's warrant and a famous disgraced name that suddenly isn't disgraced anymore."

"I know."

"Somebody who taught a fifteen-year-old to read weather."

"*Fifteen in the spring.*"

"Somebody," I pressed gently, "who shattered a god-stone rather than let it be a weapon. The coast is going to ask her to stay. In fact—" I nudged a pebble off the shelf and listened to it click down into the dark. "In fact, I'd wager the asking has already started."

She was silent long enough that the tide filled the silence for her, breathing in and out of the cave mouth, slow and even, like something enormous finally asleep.

"I buried running," she said at last. "Two days ago, on that altar. I'm fairly sure it stayed buried." Then she stood, and brushed the salt off her clothes, and looked out at the fires with an expression I will carry longer than any scar I earned that week—not peace exactly, nothing so finished as peace, but the honest beginning of it, the first clear inch of ground after a flood.

"Come on," she said. "If we're staying, there's a ledger to finish, and Brannoc's to bury properly, and Wren wants her first lesson at dawn."

*The Long Way Home*, I thought, following her down toward the light. Well. It was about time somebody took it.
