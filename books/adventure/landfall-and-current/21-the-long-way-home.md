## 21. The Long Way Home

The dead don't care about weather, but the living do, and so we buried Brannoc on the first morning in nine days that the sky held still.

Greywater Cove had never seen a funeral like it, mostly because Greywater Cove had never seen a funeral at all — or if it had, no one remembered, which after everything we'd learned about memory was a joke I refused to make out loud. The salvagers turned out in whatever passed for their best: coats patched with sailcloth, boots resoled with tire rubber, one ancient woman wearing what I swear was a wedding dress cut down to fishing waders. They stood on the shingle above the tideline while six of Brannoc's oldest crew carried him down on a door pried off his own quarters, because he'd once said — and Sable quoted him with suspicious accuracy — that he'd be carried out the same way he came in, on somebody else's furniture.

I stood at the back. Old habit. Even now, even here, my feet tried to find the edge of every gathering, the spot where I could watch without being watched. But Wren had planted herself beside me with the immovability of a barnacle, and when I shifted left, she shifted left, and when I shifted right she looked up at me with an expression that said *I know what you're doing and it won't work*, and so I stayed put, in the middle of things, where people could see me.

It's remarkable how much you can endure when a fifteen-year-old refuses to let you skulk.

Sable spoke first. His voice cracked twice and he didn't apologize for either crack. He told the story of how Brannoc had found him three years ago, half-drowned and full of a plan so reckless that Brannoc had laughed until he coughed, then fed him, then asked how he could help. "He never asked me why," Sable said. "That's the thing about him. Everyone else wanted the story first. Brannoc just handed you a bowl of soup and started clearing space."

Then old Maren — the wedding dress woman, who turned out to have been Brannoc's partner in salvage and possibly in life, the records being what they were, which is to say waterlogged — read from his ledger. Not the names. Those were going somewhere else now, back to families, back to towns, back to the hollow-eyed people waking up all along the coast with years returned to them like packages lost in transit. No, she read his accounts. The mundane ones. *Owed to Pell: two hooks, one favor.* *Wren: took bread without asking, docked against future wages, wages forgiven, bread also forgiven.*

People laughed through their tears. It was the right kind of eulogy. Brannoc would have hated anything grander and said so, loudly, probably while eating something.

When they lowered him into the sand above the storm line, the whole cove sang — a low, wordless thing, more current than melody, the sound of people who live with the sea and have stopped pretending they'll ever win. I didn't sing. My voice doesn't do that anymore. But I stood in the middle, and I took off my hat, and I let the wind get at my hair like everyone else, and that felt like its own kind of verse.

* * *

The Council fell faster than anyone expected, which tells you everything about how much it deserved to stand.

The collector's patrol boat had limped back to Harbor with news of the cave, the shattered Tideheart, and a collector who arrived babbling about light coming out of a stone and rain falling upward and a ranger named Brightwater who looked at him like he was already dead. By the time the storm season broke and the first trade boats dared open water, the story had done the rounds of every port from here to the Salt Reach, growing in the telling, as true stories do. The Council members hadn't been elected so much as installed by whoever controlled the harvests, and with the harvests exposed — with hundreds of citizens suddenly remembering exactly who had signed the orders to empty them — there wasn't a chair in the harbor offices that could hold its occupant past sundown.

Some fled. Some were dragged to the same cells where they'd kept the resistors. One, I'm told, tried to swim for a departing freighter and was fished out by fishermen whose memories he'd helped take, which strikes me as either mercy or the cruelest irony the coast has produced, and knowing these people, both.

The coast woke up remembering. That was the miracle nobody had planned for. Farmers who'd spent four years not knowing why they couldn't grieve their drowned son suddenly could. A lighthouse keeper in Vell recovered eleven years and immediately filed a grievance about eleven years of unpaid wages, which made me laugh for the first time since the cave. And everywhere, people began the slow, clumsy work of reassembling themselves — arguing with spouses about missing time, holding funerals delayed by seasons, staring into mirrors trying to find the face they'd had before someone sold pieces of it.

Sable's sister, Dellet, sat on the seawall most mornings, doing nothing, needing to do nothing, just being present in her own head again. He sat with her. He didn't try to fill the silence or explain himself. He'd done enough explaining in the cave to last them both a lifetime.

"You could go back to the flats," he said to me one evening, watching her. "Nobody's hunting you now. Brightwater name's cleared — your father's too. There's a whole coast that owes you."

"I know," I said.

"That's it? *I know?*"

"I'm thinking about it."

He snorted. "You've been thinking about leaving since before the flood. You think I can't see it? You pack your eyes every time a conversation ends."

He wasn't wrong. That was the humiliating part. Some habits are load-bearing, and mine — the running, the edge-of-the-crowd standing, the exit always mapped — had been holding up the whole structure of me for so long that I genuinely didn't know what would happen if I took them out. Probably collapse. Possibly rebuild. The two look identical from inside.

* * *

I buried my father's compass the day after Brannoc's funeral. Not buried — placed. There's a difference, and the difference mattered to me, though I'd have shot anyone who asked me to explain it.

But that comes later. First there was Wren.

She'd been circling me for days the way gulls circle a fish cleaning station — patient, opportunistic, pretending interest in other things. She'd help haul rope nearby. She'd appear at the fire with questions about knots she already knew how to tie. Finally she cornered me at the boatyard, where Sable and three salvagers were trying to decide whether his ruined sloop was worth rebuilding or should be given a Viking funeral, and she planted herself in front of me with her arms crossed and said:

"Teach me."

"Teach you what?"

"Everything. Tracking. Reading weather. How you found Sable across the salt flats when he'd covered his trail." Her chin came up. "Brannoc said you were the best tracker he'd ever heard of, and Brannoc didn't say nice things about anybody except in his ledger."

"Wren—"

"I'm not asking to be a ranger. I know rangers are a southern thing, and the south thinks we're all smugglers. I'm asking to learn. There's nobody else here who knows this stuff, and after—" her voice caught, just slightly, on the shape of everything after, "—after, I want to be useful. Not just salvage-useful. *Know*-useful."

I looked at her and saw, with uncomfortable clarity, myself at fifteen: furious, hungry, certain that knowledge was the only inheritance worth having because everything else got taken. I also saw the trap I'd built for myself, which was the belief that passing anything on meant staying long enough to see whether it took.

Staying. There was that word again, sitting in my chest like a swallowed stone.

"Apprenticeship isn't a favor," I said finally. "It's a contract. You work when I say work, sleep when I say sleep, and when I tell you a storm will kill you, you believe me the first time, not the third. And you don't get to quit in month two because it turns out tracking is ninety percent boredom and ten percent blisters."

Her face did something complicated — hope fighting dignity and winning narrowly. "Deal."

"And you finish mending those nets first. A tracker who leaves loose ends dies of loose ends."

"Yes—" she caught herself grinning, wrestled it down to something professional, failed completely. "Yes, ma'am."

"Don't call me ma'am. Call me Lior."

"No, ma'am."

I let it go. Pick your battles; I'd learned that much from Brannoc's ledger, if nothing else.

We shook on it, her hand small and rope-hardened in mine, and something settled in me that I hadn't known was unsettled — some argument between the part of me that runs and the part, apparently older and deeper, that teaches. My mother had been a teacher. I'd forgotten that, or rather it had been taken, and then given back in a burst of light and rain in a drowning cave, and I was still finding places in myself where the returned memories fit like bones reset after a bad break. She'd taught letters to harbor children who couldn't afford school. She'd said — I could hear her saying it now, clear as gulls — *what you keep to yourself rots; what you give away grows.*

Fine, Mother. Point taken. I'd give it away. Starting with weather signs and ending, apparently, with everything else I had.

* * *

The weeks that followed were the strangest of my life, and I include the week I spent trapped in a sea cave with a sentient rock and a hostile navy.

Rebuilding looks like nothing from the outside. It's hauling timber and arguing about roof angles and standing in line for flour. But underneath, the cove was performing surgery on itself. Families reunited across gaps of stolen time. Grief finally permitted to happen, decades late, in kitchens and on seawalls. Maren organized a memorial wall — slate, not paper, paper being optimistic in this climate — and carved each harvested name as it was reclaimed, and the wall filled faster than anyone could carve, and people brought chalk for the overflow, and soon the whole harbor office exterior was covered in names and dates and, in one case, a very detailed complaint about the quality of Council-issue boots.

I worked. That was the surprise. I'd assumed I'd itch to leave within days, and instead I found myself repairing the breakwater with Sable's crew, teaching Wren to read cloud bases over the western hills, sitting evenings with Dellet while she practiced being a person again and occasionally asked me questions about her brother that I answered honestly, which is harder than it sounds.

"You love him," she said one night, not looking at me, in the way of someone who has recently relearned that directness costs nothing.

"He's tolerable."

"Lior."

"He's—" I turned my cup in my hands. "He stole a stone from powerful people to give strangers back their own minds, and he did it badly, and he nearly got us all killed, and he's the bravest man I've ever met. Does that answer it?"

"It answers it sideways, which I understand is how you answer everything."

Dellet had been hollow for four years and had come back with more insight than most people accumulate in forty. I decided not to hold it against her.

* * *

On the last morning of the month, I climbed to the sea cave.

The tide was out, the way it is twice a year, and the entrance stood open like a mouth that had finished speaking. Inside, the altar chamber was just rock now. Whatever the Tideheart had been — cage, archive, weapon, wound — it was scattered across the whole coast in a million returning memories, and the cave held only salt air and the sound of water counting its way back in.

I'd brought the compass. My father's brass compass, the one thing I'd kept through every year of running, the needle of which had pointed home for a decade after home stopped existing. I'd cleared his name in absentia — the new coastal council, such as it was, had posthumously restored his rank and apologized in language so carefully lawyerly it barely qualified as words — but apologies are paper. This was brass.

I set it on the altar ledge, where the Tideheart had hummed, and stepped back.

"For the next person who comes in here thinking they're alone," I said to the empty chamber. It echoed. Caves are good audiences that way. "So they know somebody found their way out."

Then I walked out ahead of the tide, because I am many things now, but late is not one of them, and Wren was waiting on the flats for her first real lesson, and I had promised her weather by noon.

* * *

The commission arrived by fast courier three days later — a sealed letter, southern wax, official hand. The provisional government of the coast, such as it was, requested the services of Ranger Lior Brightwater to establish lawful order in the drowned territories, with full authority, a salary that made me blink, and a title: Warden of the Greywater Coast.

Sable read it over my shoulder, which is a habit I've decided to permit. "Well?" he said. "Running again?"

I looked at the letter. Then past it, at the harbor — at Maren's wall of names, at Dellet laughing at something Wren said, at the skeleton of Sable's sloop slowly becoming a boat again, at the whole ragged, stubborn, waterlogged family of a place that had taken me in when I was a stranger with a warrant on my head and taught me, without ever once saying it aloud, that invisibility is just loneliness wearing armor.

"No," I said, and found the word weighed nothing at all. "It's the opposite of running. It's the longest assignment of my life."

"How long?"

I folded the letter and tucked it away, and picked up my hat, and turned toward the shore where my apprentice was waiting, impatient, in weather I'd taught her to read.

"The rest of it," I said. "Come on. Storm's building in the west, and I promised her rain by dark."
