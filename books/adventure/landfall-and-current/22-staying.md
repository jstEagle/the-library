## 22. Staying

The commission came on paper the color of old bone, sealed with wax that smelled faintly of fish oil because everything in Greywater Cove smelled faintly of fish oil now, and I suppose it always had.

*To Lior Brightwater, Ranger,* it began, which was a sentence I'd never expected to read in my life, since for eleven years the only documents bearing my name were wanted notices and one very unflattering sketch circulated through three provinces. *By authority of the Provisional Coastal Assembly, you are offered commission to restore lawful order to the drowned coast and its settlements.*

Restore lawful order. As if order were a thing that had ever lived here and merely wandered off. As if you could restore a garden to a salt flat by planting seeds and hoping the sea took pity.

I read it four times on the porch of Brannoc's house—which was my house now, though I still knocked before entering my own front door, some habits being load-bearing—and then I set it on the table under a stone and went to find Wren, because if I was going to make the biggest decision of my adult life, I was going to make it while pretending to do something else.

She was on the flats at low tide, teaching two of the younger salvagers how to read weather, which is to say she was standing with her face tipped up into the wind looking insufferably smug about clouds. She'd gotten good at it. Better than me, maybe, though I'd die before telling her that, because apprentices should earn their compliments and also because her head was already large enough to require its own mooring line.

"Storm coming?" I asked.

"Three days out. Small one." She squinted at me. "You've got your thinking face on."

"I don't have a thinking face."

"You have six faces and one of them is thinking. It's the one where you look like you're being robbed."

I looked out at the water instead of answering, which she correctly interpreted as confirmation. The cove lay flat and silver in the morning light, the wreck of Sable's boat still half-buried in the shallows where the surge had left it, a rib cage of rotten timber that nobody had the heart to clear away. Somewhere past the headland, the sea cave sat behind its wall of collapsed rock, sealed now by our own hands, its entrance choked with stone and rubble and, if the stories were true, whatever ghosts the Tideheart hadn't given back.

"They want me to be a ranger again," I said. "Officially. Here. Building something that lasts."

Wren was quiet for a moment, which for Wren was roughly equivalent to another person's hour of solemn reflection. Then: "Is this the part where you tell me you're leaving?"

"No."

"Because last time you said no, you had a bag packed by sundown."

"That was different."

"It really wasn't."

She wasn't wrong, and we both knew it, and the fact that she could say it to my face without flinching was proof of how much had changed. A year ago, nobody would have dared. A year ago, I'd been a rumor with legs—a ranger who passed through towns like weather, useful and unwelcome and gone by morning. Stopping meant remembering. Remembering meant grieving. Grieving meant admitting that the Brightwater name had weight, and I'd spent a decade convincing myself I could live weightless.

Then a thief with clever hands and a worse poker face had led me across the salt flats into a storm, and a dying fisherman had pointed me at a cave, and an old man with a ledger full of stolen names had handed me my mother's handwriting like it was nothing, like it wasn't the most expensive thing anyone had ever given me.

And I'd stopped. And the world hadn't ended. It had, in fact, gotten considerably better, right up until the part where Brannoc died, and even that—I'm still learning this—even that hadn't been the price of stopping. It had been the price of standing. There's a difference. It took me most of a season to see it.

"So take the commission," Wren said, as if I'd asked. "Obviously."

"It's not that simple."

"It's exactly that simple. You're going to say yes. You just want someone to make you feel bad about hesitating, and I refuse, because feeling bad about hesitating is how you ended up eating alone in caravan camps for eleven years."

I stared at her. "Has Sable been talking to you?"

"Sable talks to everyone. It's his worst quality." She grinned, and it was Brannoc's grin, borrowed and kept, the one he used to wear when he caught us stealing biscuits from the common stores. "Say yes, Lior. Say it out loud. Words are free here. That's the whole point of what we built."

*

I said yes.

Not that day—I'm not a miracle—but three days later, after the small storm came and went and left the cove rinsed clean, I signed the commission at the long table in the assembly hall with a dozen salvagers watching, and Sable Vane leaned against the doorframe with his arms crossed wearing the expression of a man who has won a bet he never officially placed.

"Took you long enough," he said afterward, out on the steps.

"You didn't even bet on me."

"No. I bet against you, actually. Put a week's salvage on you turning it down." He shrugged when I glared at him. "Best money I ever lost. I'd have lost a year's worth."

"That's a terrible financial strategy."

"It's a great one. It only works on things I care about." He fell into step beside me as we walked down toward the water, and for a while neither of us spoke, because that was the other thing that had changed—we could be quiet together now without either of us treating silence like a wound. "You know they'll make it hard," he said eventually. "The Council's gone, but the men who ran it are still breathing. Some of them are on that assembly. They gave you this commission because you're the only person on the coast they can't buy and can't scare."

"Flattery."

"Inventory. I know how collectors think." His sister Maren had taught him that, or rather the thing that had been done to Maren had taught him, and he carried the lesson the way he carried everything—lightly, like it weighed nothing, which was how you knew it weighed everything. "They'll test you. First time you have to enforce a law that protects them instead of us, they'll be watching to see which way you break."

"Then I'll have to not break."

"Mm." He picked up a flat stone and skipped it across the shallows—four skips, showing off. "You know what I keep thinking about? The Tideheart. Everyone got their memories back, all those years the Council harvested, all those hollowed-out people waking up whole. And it was beautiful. Best thing I ever saw." He threw another stone. "But somebody paid for it. Somebody held that thing for months and let it eat her own past so the rest of us could have ours."

"Sable—"

"Not fishing for thanks. Just saying I noticed. Noticing is sort of my job." He glanced sideways at me. "How much did it take? Honestly."

I thought about lying to him, mostly out of habit, and found I'd misplaced the ability somewhere over the winter. "Names, mostly. Little ones. My aunt's face. The name of the town I trained in. The taste of a specific meal I ate when I was nineteen." I turned my hands over, palms up, empty. "It sounds small when I list it. It doesn't feel small. But it's mine to carry, and I'd pay it again."

"I know," he said simply. "That's why I asked. Wanted to hear you say it."

"Why?"

"Because a year ago you'd have lied, and I'd have known you were lying, and we'd both have pretended otherwise forever." He smiled, quick and crooked. "Turns out honesty's just a habit too. Who knew."

*

The cave again, in the spring.

I told myself it was inspection—the commission required me to survey the coast's hazards, and a sealed sea cave containing the shattered remains of an artifact of tremendous power qualified as a hazard by any reasonable definition. This was even true. It was also a lie of convenience, the kind I'd once told daily, and I let myself enjoy the familiarity of it for exactly as long as the walk took.

The collapse had held. We'd done the work properly—Sable and Wren and I and half the cove, hauling stone until the entrance was a memory, until the tide broke against rock that would not open twice a year or ever again. Whatever payment the cave had once demanded, it had collected enough. Let it rest.

I stood on the shelf above the seal with the wind pulling at my coat and took out my father's compass.

It had come back to me, in the end, the way everything had come back. After the collector fled and the patrol boats limped home and the Tideheart burst apart into light and rain, I'd found it in the mud of the flooded cove, facedown, its glass cracked, its needle swinging wild. My father's compass, which he'd pressed into my hands the day I left home, saying *find your way back*, and which I'd carried for eleven years without ever once letting it point anywhere, because pointing anywhere meant choosing somewhere, and choosing somewhere meant staying, and staying—

Well. You know how that story goes now. Everybody does. They sing a version of it in the taverns, and the version they sing has me shattering the Tideheart with one dramatic blow while lightning strikes the sea, which is flattering and almost entirely false. In truth I struck it four times, cried through all of them, and the lightning was just weather. But songs don't need my permission, and neither does the truth, apparently, because both of them go where they like.

The needle still swung wrong. I'd taken it to a repairman in the rebuilt harbor town and he'd shaken his head—cracked housing, drowned works, beyond saving. A dead compass. An heirloom that pointed at nothing.

I'd kept it anyway. Of course I'd kept it. I was my father's daughter, and Brightwaters kept things; that was the whole reason the Council had destroyed him, because he'd kept records, kept proof, kept faith with people who couldn't remember why they should trust him. He'd tried to expose the harvests and been ruined for it, name dragged through every court on the coast, and I'd spent my whole adult life running from that ruin before I understood it was never shame. It was inheritance. It was the family trade: keeping things for people who couldn't keep them themselves.

A ranger keeps things too. That's the job, when you strip away the badge and the authority and the paperwork. You keep the peace. You keep the roads open. You keep watch. You hold what's entrusted to you and hand it back intact.

I knelt at the edge of the seal and set the compass down on the stone, needle spinning its slow useless circles, and I left it there.

Not as an offering. The cave was done taking payments; I'd made sure of that with my own hands. I left it the way you leave flowers at a grave—not because the dead need them, but because the living need somewhere to put the love. My father had wanted to find his way back to this coast his whole life, and the Council had made sure he never could. So I brought him the way back instead, and left him pointing at nothing, at everything, at the whole gray shining reach of the sea he'd fought for.

"There," I said out loud, because some things need air to be real. "We stayed."

The wind answered, the way wind does, with nothing and everything.

*

The commission work was slower than any fight and harder than any flight, and I loved it in a way that embarrassed me.

There was no single enemy now, no tide of armed men to turn back at a cave mouth. There were disputes over salvage rights and water shares, a smuggling ring running medicine past the assembly's taxes that I shut down and then quietly reopened under legal charter because the taxes were stupid and the medicine was needed, and there was Wren, growing into her training the way spring grows into summer—imperceptibly, and then all at once, and then you couldn't remember the landscape without her.

She tracked her first fugitive in autumn, a poacher out of the northern coves, and brought him back walking and unharmed and complaining the entire way, which is the correct outcome for a first capture and I told her so. She didn't smile until we were out of sight of the settlement, and then she smiled so wide I thought she'd strain something.

"Told you," she said.

"Told me what?"

"That you had something to give." She shrugged, suddenly shy, suddenly fifteen again. "Back when you wouldn't teach me anything. I told Sable you would. He bet against me."

"He bets against everyone he cares about. It's his love language. Don't tell him I know."

We walked the last stretch of shore together in the gold light, and the current ran beside us, steady and unhurried, going somewhere without hurrying to arrive.

*

Winter found me on the porch with tea going cold and a letter from the assembly I hadn't opened yet, because even reformed institutions produce paperwork, and some part of me will always believe unread letters can't hurt you.

Sable came up the path with Maren beside him. She walked well now—she'd walked well within weeks of the Tideheart breaking, her memories flooding home like water finding its level—but she still liked the walk up from the harbor, and I still liked watching her take it, this woman who'd been hollowed out to a shell and filled back in, who remembered her brother now, remembered everything, and chose, every day, to spend her recovered hours helping rebuild the records office that would keep it from happening again.

"Lior," Maren said, "the assembly wants you in Harbor Town for the anniversary. They're unveiling a memorial. Your father's name is on it."

I looked at the letter in my hands. Of course it was. Of course they'd written it down and sent it formally, because that was what institutions did, and because some names deserved more than a letter—they deserved stone, and ceremony, and a daughter standing in front of them.

"The Brightwater restoration," Sable said, dropping onto the porch rail. "Full pardon, posthumous honors, the works. You'll have to give a speech."

"I'll have to what."

"A speech. About justice. Memory. The enduring power of—" he waved a hand, "—whatever. Make something up. You're good at making things up; you pretended to be heartless for a decade."

"I was not pretending to be heartless. I was genuinely committed to it."

"And look at you now." He nodded at the cove below—at the mended boats, the new roofs, the children running the tideline who had never known a Council, at Wren down on the flats teaching weather-reading to a new crop of kids with my gestures, my stance, my whole self copied faithfully into a body that wasn't mine and would carry it further than I ever could. "Heartless rangers don't get memorials built for their fathers. Heartless rangers don't get asked to stay."

I looked down at the letter a long moment. Then I set it under the stone on the table—the same stone, the same table, the same porch where a year ago I'd read the commission and gone looking for excuses—and I left it there, unread until morning, certain it would keep.

Because that was the thing I'd finally learned, the thing the salt flats and the storm and the cave and the boy who stole for the right reasons had all conspired to teach me: keeping things safe was the work. Letters kept. Names kept. Compasses kept. People, most of all, kept—not locked away, not hidden, but tended, chosen, returned to.

My father had said *find your way back*, and I'd thought he meant home, some fixed place I'd failed to reach. He'd meant this. The returning itself. The choosing, over and over, morning after morning, to stop running and stand in the current and let it move through you instead of past you.

The tide was coming in. It always was. That was the coast's oldest promise—you cannot hold the water, but you can be here when it arrives.

I stayed on the porch until dark, with my cold tea and my friends and the sound of the sea doing what it had always done, and for the first time in longer than I could measure, nowhere felt like somewhere else I ought to be.

The current carried on. So did I.

Together, this time.
