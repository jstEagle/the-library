## 1. The Sounder's Silence

The *Marta June* had a way of telling Felix Whitlock — though he never said the name aloud, not even to himself in the dark of the wheelhouse — when the river was lying to him. A shudder in the deck plates. A certain hollowness under the bow. This morning she was quiet as a held breath, which meant the Sereno below Port Calloway was running honest, and Felix distrusted that almost as much.

He counted things instead of trusting them. It was a habit he'd built the way other men built houses: plank by plank, until it would hold his weight. Miles logged since dawn: eleven and change. Buoys passed: four, all where they ought to be. Times he'd thought about turning back for no reason he could name: zero, which was itself worth noting.

The fog was burning off the water in long silver rags. Below Calloway the river went wide and lazy, braiding itself around gravel bars like an old man taking his time crossing a room, and Felix ran it the same way — no hurry, no wasted motion, one hand on the wheel and the other around a tin cup of coffee gone bitter from sitting too long on the warm engine housing.

Five years on this stretch of water. Five years since he'd come down out of the hills with a duffel bag, a debt, and a boat with someone else's name painted on her stern, and had made himself into a man who could be described entirely by his schedule. Left Calloway Tuesdays. Resupply run Thursdays. Back downriver before the weekend traffic. He was known at every dock between here and the estuary as *the captain*, which suited him fine. Captains didn't need surnames. Rivers didn't ask for them.

The radio crackled at half past seven, right on time, because Port Calloway's harbormaster believed in schedules the way some men believed in saints.

"*Marta June*, you seeing this fog lift? Over."

"Lifting," Felix said. "River's honest today. Over."

"Then you'll want to come in slow, because there's a surveyor on my dock who's been asking after you since six, and she doesn't strike me as a woman who takes slow for an answer."

Felix looked at the coffee, considered pouring it overboard as a libation to whatever god governed unscheduled conversations, and drank it instead. "Nobody asks after me," he said. "They ask after a boat. Over."

"Well, this one asked for you by boat *and* by reputation, so I'd comb your hair if I were you. Over."

He signed off without answering, which was its own kind of answer, and brought the *Marta June* around the last bend with the particular care of a man approaching something he'd rather approach fast.

* * *

Imogen Reyes was standing at the end of the dock with her boots planted wide, like the planks might try to leave without her. She was younger than Felix expected — thirty, maybe, with sun-cracked lips and the kind of tan that came from months rather than vacations — and she had a leather case strapped across her chest the way soldiers wore ammunition. When the boat bumped the pilings she stepped aboard before he'd finished tying off, which told him most of what he needed to know about her.

"Captain," she said. "Ferry contract. Two weeks' provisions, survey equipment, spare parts, and a radio battery, upriver past the Narrows to the headwater survey camp."

Felix finished the knot first. Then he straightened and looked at her properly. "Past the Narrows is charter water. Different rates. Different risks."

"I know what it is. I drew the map of it."

"You drew a map of the Narrows?"

"My team did. We've been working the headwaters since March." She said it flatly, but there was something underneath the flatness, the way there's something underneath ice on a river in late winter — invisible, and holding everything up.

"Then your team has boats."

"My team has a radio that hasn't answered in nine days." She let that sit. "I flew out two weeks ago for a supply rotation and a meeting with the ministry. The rotation got delayed. The meeting got longer. And then on day five I called camp and got static, and I've been calling every six hours since."

"Radios fail," Felix said. "Antennas fall. Batteries die. Nine days isn't—"

"It's nine days when Dr. Vance checks in twice daily whether she's healthy, flooded, or furious, and she has never once missed a check-in. Not once in fourteen months." Imogen's jaw worked. "She's the most disciplined person I've ever met. Discipline doesn't go silent."

Felix leaned against the rail and did the arithmetic he always did, the arithmetic that kept him alive: fuel, draft, season, risk, payback. The numbers came back the way they always came back when the job smelled wrong — expensive.

"Not my water," he said. "Above the Narrows there's no charts worth the paper. Drowned timber from the old logging days, channels that move after every flood. I run scheduled routes. Scheduled routes don't include going upriver to find out why nobody's answering."

"Then I'll hire someone else."

"You'll have trouble. Most captains won't take charter water in high summer."

"Most captains aren't you." She said it without flattery, which was why it landed. "I asked around. They said if the *Marta June*'s captain won't do it, it can't be done, and also that he doesn't talk, doesn't drink with anyone, and once threw a cargo broker off his dock for touching his lines. I'm hoping the first part's true and the second part means you're exactly the man I need."

"The third part's true too," Felix said. "He touched my lines."

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth — not quite a smile, more like a door opening a crack to check the weather. "Captain. I don't care what your story is. Everybody on this river has one, and half of them are lies and the other half are worse. I care that you're the best pilot on this stretch, and that my people are up there with a dead radio and a food cache that runs out in three weeks. Name a price."

Felix looked upriver, where the Sereno narrowed between its green walls and disappeared toward country he had spent five years not thinking about. The fog was fully burned off now. The water lay flat and bright and full of nothing, and he hated how much he wanted to be on it, moving, doing something, going anywhere that wasn't this dock and this conversation.

Restlessness was the family disease, his father used to say. Better to catch it early and give it a job.

He hadn't thought about his father's voice in weeks. That was the point of the schedule — miles, not memories.

"Double rate," he heard himself say. "Half up front. You ride along, because I don't ferry blind and I don't ferry for people who'll sue me if their expedition turns out to have walked off for fun."

"They haven't walked off for fun."

"Then we agree quickly and get underway. Provisions loaded by noon, on the water by one."

Imogen stuck out her hand. Her grip was dry and hard, a dockworker's grip on a surveyor's hand. "Reyes," she said. "Imogen Reyes."

"Felix," he said, and left it there, the way he always left it there, and watched to see if she'd push.

To her credit, she only raised an eyebrow, filed something away behind her eyes, and turned to shout orders at the harbormaster's boy, who came pelting down the dock with a manifest clipboard and the expression of someone who'd been warned about the captain.

* * *

Loading took until half past twelve, mostly because Imogen's crates were labeled with a precision that bordered on devotion — SURVEY 1 through SURVEY 14, MEDICAL, RADIO SPARE, VANCE'S COFFEE (DO NOT CRUSH) — and Felix checked each one against the manifest twice, because rules were the hull he floated in. Teo, the harbormaster's boy, hauled crates down the gangplank with the tireless drama of a thirteen-year-old being paid by the hour, and narrated the whole operation to himself in a whisper.

"You count everything twice," Imogen observed, watching him tally the medical crate a second time.

"Once for the manifest," Felix said. "Once for the river. The river doesn't read."

"What happens when they disagree?"

"They never disagree. That's how I know I've done it right." He stenciled the last checkmark and closed the ledger. "Cast off in ten minutes. There's a bunk aft if you want to sleep through the first leg. Nothing above water till the Narrows anyway."

But Imogen didn't go aft. She stood at the rail beside him as the *Marta June* backed off the dock and swung her nose upriver, and for a while neither of them spoke, which Felix found less annoying than most conversation.

It was Teo who broke it. The boy had appeared at the top of the gangplank just as they cast off — Felix saw him later on the security of hindsight — but at the time all Felix knew was that Imogen suddenly stiffened beside him and said, quietly:

"Captain. Before we clear the breakwater. There's something you should see in my case. Something about who drew the charts your river authority uses above the Narrows."

"I use my own soundings."

"I know. But the official charts — the ones everyone else runs by — they're reprints of older surveys. Forty years old. From an expedition that mapped the whole upper Sereno and never came back down." She unstrapped the leather case and opened it against the rail, shielding the pages from the wind with her body. "Vance was following those original charts. She said they were better than anything modern. Said whoever drew them had understood the river like it was a living thing."

Felix kept his eyes on the channel ahead. The first green wall of the upper river slid closer, patient as a held door.

"There's a signature on every sheet," Imogen said. "Same hand, every time. Initials and a name."

The wind came off the water and riffled the corner of the chart where her thumb held it down, and Felix looked — because refusing to look would have been worse, would have been the one rule even he couldn't keep — and there it was, in faded iron-gall ink, in a hand he knew better than his own handwriting, because it was the hand that had taught him his:

**E. Whitlock.**

The *Marta June* carried them forward into the current, eleven miles become twelve, and Felix Whitlock — who did not say the name, who had not said it in five years — gripped the wheel and felt the past come up alongside him at anchor, matching his speed, waiting to be taken aboard.
