## 2. Miles, Not Years

The *Kestrel* took on cargo the way old men take on opinions — slowly, grudgingly, and with a great deal of complaint from below decks.

Felix stood at the rail of the Port Calloway freight dock with a manifest in one hand and a cup of dock coffee in the other, watching Imogen Reyes's crates come down the gangplank. Dock coffee was a local institution: burnt, bitter, and served in mugs that had survived more wrecks than most boats. He drank it anyway. It was the kind of honesty he could respect.

"Careful with that one," Imogen called, jogging alongside a crate the size of a coffin. "Instruments. If you hear glass rattling, we're both out of a job."

"If I hear glass rattling," Felix said, "I'll assume it's your radio finally working."

She winced. Good. She'd earned the jab. Nine days of silence from an expedition she'd helped fund, and she'd come to him anyway, cool-eyed and stubborn, asking him to run supplies upriver into country most pilots wouldn't enter with a prayer book and a spare propeller.

He'd said yes before he'd finished his coffee yesterday. That was the part that bothered him.

Felix Whitlock — no. Felix. Just Felix, the way he'd introduced himself for five years, the way his charter papers read, the way the paint on his wheelhouse said *Kestrel* and nothing else. He had rules about this sort of thing. Rule one: know your water. Rule two: never carry what you can't afford to lose. Rule three, unwritten but iron-clad: the past stays at anchor.

The past, apparently, had other ideas.

* * *

By midmorning the deck was stacked and lashed, and Imogen had claimed a spot on the roof of the wheelhouse to inventory her gear, cross-legged like a heron on a piling. Felix went below to sign the final paperwork, which was spread across his chart table in triplicate because lawyers, like rivers, always found the lowest path.

Charter agreement. Cargo declaration. Liability waiver — hers, absolving him; his, absolving her. He signed where the little paper flags pointed, his pen scratching along, until he reached the last page and something slid free of the folder and clattered onto the table.

A compass.

Brass, heavy as a knuckle, its case worn smooth at the edges by forty years of thumbs. The lid fell open on a needle that swung once, twice, and settled north with the confidence of an animal coming home. Scratched into the inside of the cover, small and deliberate, were four letters:

**E.W.**

Felix did not move for a long moment. The river slapped against the hull. Somewhere above, Imogen's boots crossed the roof planks, unhurried.

His father's compass. Elias Whitlock's compass, lost — or left, or planted — and now sitting on his chart table like a guest who knew where the liquor was kept.

He should have put it back in the envelope. He should have walked topside, handed the whole folder to Imogen Reyes, and told her to find another boat. That was the sensible move. That was rule three, executed cleanly, no casualties.

Instead he picked it up.

It fit his palm exactly. Of course it did. His hand was his father's hand reshaped by twenty years of different rope. He remembered the compass from childhood — remembered it hanging around Elias's neck on a leather cord, remembered being told, age six, *the needle doesn't point north, boy, it points true, and those aren't always the same direction.* He'd thought it was a riddle then. Later he'd understood it was a warning.

Someone had known. Someone had packed this into his charter paperwork deliberately, and there was exactly one person aboard who could have.

Felix climbed the ladder two rungs at a time.

Imogen looked up from her inventory list, and whatever she saw in his face made her set down her pencil. "Ah," she said. "You found it."

"You went through my file."

"I went through *everybody's* file." She didn't flinch, didn't apologize, which he grudgingly respected. "I'm funding this expedition out of pocket and my late aunt's estate, and my survey team has been dark for nine days. When I hire a pilot, I research him thoroughly."

"And when you find out he's got a famous name, you decide to spring it on him like a trap?"

"I decided to give you a choice." She stood, brushing off her trousers, and met him eye to eye — she was shorter than him, but she stood like she wasn't. "You could have signed without opening the folder. Most men would have."

"Most men are smarter than me."

"Debatable." She nodded at the compass in his fist. "Do you know why Dr. Vance went up the Sereno?"

"Because someone was paying him to."

"Because he found charts." Imogen's voice dropped, not for drama but for precision — she spoke the way she surveyed, marking every word. "In an archive in Bellhaven. River charts of the upper Sereno. The Gorge of Needles. The headwaters. Water nobody has mapped since — well. Since they were mapped." She paused. "They're signed E. Whitlock."

The wind came off the water and Felix let it move him half a step, as if it had pushed him. Below them the river ran brown and patient, going about its business, keeping its secrets the way it always had.

"My father died on that river," Felix said. "Twenty-two years ago. Survey accident, they called it. Boat lost in the gorge."

"That's what the record says."

"You don't believe the record."

"I believe Dr. Vance believed the charts were real, and I believe he followed them, and I believe nine days ago he stopped answering his radio." She held out her hand, palm up. Not demanding the compass — offering to take the weight of it. "I'm not trying to trap you, Captain. I'm trying to get my people home. And you are the only man alive who grew up reading water that E. Whitlock drew."

Felix looked at the compass. The needle hadn't moved. True north, steady as a sermon.

Five years ago he'd sold everything with the name attached except the clothes he stood in. Changed towns, changed rivers, changed the way he signed his name so the loops didn't match the school records. He'd built a life measured in miles — miles run, miles between himself and the story of Elias Whitlock, the brilliant cartographer who traded a family for a river and lost the wager. Felix had counted every mile and told himself the total added up to distance.

And here was the past, delivered by courier, tucked into his own paperwork, sitting warm in his hand like it had never left.

"I nearly walked away just now," he said. "Up on the dock. Before I even opened the folder, some part of me knew."

"I know. You had the look of a man doing arithmetic."

"What's the answer?"

Imogen almost smiled. It transformed her face from a survey instrument into something human. "That depends on whether you count yourself among the things worth saving up there."

* * *

He made her wait while he checked the lashings. It wasn't stalling — or it wasn't only stalling. A man about to make a bad decision deserves at least the dignity of good knots, and Felix worked the lines down the deck, testing each one, buying his hands time while his head argued with itself.

Stay, said the part of him that had spent five years being reasonable. You've got contracts downriver. You've got a reputation built on showing up, not on chasing ghosts. Elias Whitlock chose the river over you once already; you don't owe his ghost a fare.

Go, said the part of him that had never once said anything out loud. Go, because the charts are real and you know it. Go, because nine days is long enough for people to die and short enough that maybe they haven't. Go, because the needle points true and north isn't always the same direction, and you've been pretending for five years that you don't understand the riddle.

He finished the last lashing, straightened, and found Teo — no, not Teo yet, that came later — found nothing but the empty dock and the gulls arguing over fish guts, and went back to the wheelhouse where Imogen waited with the patience of someone who had already guessed the ending.

Felix laid the charter on the chart table. He picked up his pen. Then he set it down again.

"One condition," he said.

"Name it."

"The compass rides with me. Not in the manifest. Not in your gear. With me." He tapped the brass case. "If we find out what happened to my father, I want to be holding this when we do."

Imogen studied him for a moment, and he watched her decide how much truth to spend. "Fair," she said. "Though I'd have given it to you anyway. It was never mine to carry."

"No," Felix agreed quietly. "It never is. That's the whole trouble with inheritances."

He signed. Felix Almeida — the name on his papers, the name he'd worn like a borrowed coat for five years, the ink flowing smooth and practiced, a signature built specifically to resemble no one. Imogen countersigned. The contract was done, the deal struck, the die cast into moving water.

"Cast off in twenty minutes," he said, all business now, because business was easier than feeling. "Get your kit stowed. And Reyes —"

"Yes?"

"When we pass the last marker buoy, you're going to want to tell me everything else you're not telling me. About Vance. About the charts. About whoever thought a dead man's compass was a polite way to say hello." He turned to the wheel, ran his thumb once over the worn brass in his pocket, and felt the boat beneath him shift on the current like a dog waking up. "I'd save it for open water. The gorge doesn't leave room for surprises."

Imogen gathered her folders. At the wheelhouse door she paused. "For what it's worth, Captain — I'm glad you signed."

"We'll see if you still say that in a hundred miles."

He rang the engine bell, and the *Kestrel*, faithful old bird, answered before he did — turning her nose upriver against the current, toward the drowned timber and the shifting channels and whatever was waiting where the maps ran out.

Above the dock, the gulls scattered. Behind him, Port Calloway began to shrink. Ahead, the Sereno bent north into hills that his father had drawn by hand and died inside of, and Felix Whitlock — who had spent five years insisting he was anyone else — steered straight for them.
