## 3. First Rapids

The Sereno turned mean a mile above the last farm, and Felix Whitlock — though he'd have corrected you on the surname, politely, and then changed the subject to water levels — began doing what he did best: counting.

Drowned timber first. A whole stand of cedars had gone into the river somewhere upstream, and now their gray heads bobbed in the current like a congregation that had drowned mid-sermon. He threaded between them at quarter throttle, one hand on the wheel, one on the throttle lever, his eyes moving in the old rhythm — bank, channel, bank, channel — the way other men checked a watch.

"Timber," he said, because Imogen Reyes was standing beside him pretending she wasn't nervous, and it was kinder to give her something to look at than to let her imagine the boat's hull opening like an envelope.

"I see them."

"You see the ones above water." He nodded at a brown smear of current sliding past the bow. "There's one under there with its roots still hooked into the bottom. River hasn't decided whether it wants it yet. We go around the undecided parts."

"That's not a science."

"No," Felix agreed. "It's a marriage. You learn which moods to steer around."

Imogen laughed despite herself — a short, surprised sound, as if she'd brought laughter upriver in her luggage and hadn't meant to unpack it. She was better company than he'd expected from a surveyor. Most mapmakers he'd known treated rivers like arguments they intended to win. She treated this one like a witness who might talk if you were patient.

The *Maren B* climbed. That was the only honest word for it. The river dropped eleven feet in the next three miles through a staircase of gravel bars and deadfall, and every foot of it had to be won back by engine, winch, and occasionally by Felix standing hip-deep in freezing water with a line over his shoulder, cursing in the flat, unhurried way of a man who had made peace with cold feet years ago.

They camped that first night on a shingle bar with a fire of driftwood that burned green and sullen. Imogen spread her charts on a tarp and weighted the corners with stones, and Felix pretended not to study them while he cleaned the intake screen.

He studied them anyway.

The channels were marked in careful surveyor's ink — except for one stretch, forty miles up, where the ink changed hand entirely. Older lines. Denser. Annotated in a small slanted script that Felix's eyes recognized before his brain could stop them, the way you recognize a voice through a wall.

*E.W.*

He closed the intake cover harder than necessary.

"Those sections," Imogen said quietly, without looking up. "Vance wouldn't say where he got them. Only that they were good."

"They would be." He said it before he could bite it back.

She looked at him then. She had a way of looking that wasn't a question but left room for one. Felix wiped his hands on his trousers and went to check the anchor line instead, because some conversations are like drowned timber: best steered around until you know how deep the roots go.

* * *

The second day the river narrowed and the character of the country changed. The farms fell behind. The banks stood up steep and clay-red, hung with vine maple, and the current came down heavy and brown with silt, the color of strong tea left too long. Felix ran it by feel and memory-of-feel, reading the water's surface the way Imogen read her charts — ridges and tongues and V-shaped seams that told him where the deep channel lay.

Around noon he saw the marker buoy.

It shouldn't have been there. That was his first thought, arriving cold and complete before any other. Nobody marked this stretch. The commercial traffic had died twenty years ago; the few boats that came up here were poachers and prospectors, and neither kind wasted buoys on strangers. This one sat in the mouth of what looked like the main channel — a white float, freshly painted, riding high and confident.

Fresh paint meant recent. Recent meant somebody had been here within weeks.

"Stay off the wheel," he told Imogen, and swung wide.

She frowned. "That's the channel marker, isn't it? It's marking the safe line."

"It's marking *a* line." He brought the *Maren B* around in a long arc, studying the buoy from fifty yards. It rode wrong. He couldn't have said how he knew — a fraction too much list, a hitch in its bob where the current should have carried it clean — but he knew, the way you know a smile that doesn't reach the eyes. "Buoys don't lie about the water. They lie about themselves when someone's anchored them over a snag."

"Someone would do that?"

"Someone did." He cut the engine to idle and drifted. "Question is whether they did it for us specifically or for anybody fool enough to come up this river asking questions after a nine-day silence."

Imogen's face went carefully blank, which was its own answer.

Felix took the skiff and a boathook and went out to look. The water was colder than the morning air suggested, and the current pulled at the little boat like a dog on a leash. Up close the buoy confirmed everything: fresh white paint over older paint, and beneath the float, when he leaned over and hooked it, a chain running down into tea-brown murk — and against the chain, just under the surface, the black skeleton of a cedar trunk, its broken top angled upstream like a spear waiting for a hull.

He didn't touch anything else. He backed away, rowed to the boat, and stood dripping on deck while Imogen watched him with her chart-quiet patience.

"Well?" she said.

"Somebody painted a lie and anchored it where tired pilots trust white things." He looked upriver, at the red clay banks folding closer, at the gorge country beginning beyond. "Your Dr. Vance came through here three weeks ago. Somebody watched him go by. And nine days ago your expedition stopped answering the radio."

"You think—"

"I think I don't believe in coincidences that come with fresh paint." He went forward and hauled in the buoy himself, chain and all, and dumped the lying thing in the skiff. If someone upstream wanted the river dark, they could learn he'd noticed. "From here on, we read the water and nothing else. No markers. No signs. Just the river."

"And if the river lies?"

"The river doesn't lie," Felix said. "It just doesn't explain itself. There's a difference, and it matters."

* * *

They made six more miles before dark, all of them hard-won, and tied off in a cutbank eddy behind a wing dam of stone that some forgotten crew had stacked a generation ago and the river had half forgiven. Supper was beans and smoked fish and Imogen's astonishing hoard of dried peaches, which she rationed out like a woman who understood morale.

It was full dark, the fire banked to coals, when Felix heard the sneeze.

Small. Muffled. Coming, unmistakably, from the cargo crates lashed amidships.

He was on his feet with the lantern before his brain finished the arithmetic. Imogen followed, poker in hand, which Felix thought showed either courage or an optimistic view of pokers.

He unclipped two crates, lifted a tarp, and found a boy.

Fourteen, maybe fifteen, hollow-cheeked, dark-haired, curled into the gap between a crate of flour and a crate of nails with the practiced economy of someone who had done this before. He looked up at the lantern light without fear, which was the strangest part. Most stowaways flinch. This one just blinked, sighed like an old man asked to get up once more, and said:

"Took you two days. I thought river men were supposed to be observant."

Felix lowered the lantern slowly. "We're going to have a conversation about fare."

"We're going to have a conversation about the Gorge of Needles," the boy said, "and you're going to want to hear it before you get there, because everyone who doesn't hear it ends up on the rocks, and I'm the only person south of the escarpment who can tell you where the water actually goes."

"He's serious," Imogen said, and there was something in her voice — recognition, maybe, or dread.

"Course I'm serious." The boy unfolded himself from the crates, joints cracking, and stood swaying slightly with the river's motion under his feet, already steadier than most grown passengers managed. His eyes went past Felix, past Imogen, and fixed on the chart case lashed by the wheel — and then lower, to Felix's belt, where the brass compass sat in its loop.

Something crossed the boy's face. Not surprise. Recognition, old and heavy, like grief that had been waiting a long time for its appointment.

"The finder's mark," he said softly. "You're carrying the finder's mark."

Felix's hand closed over the compass without his permission. "Where did you hear that word?"

"Same place I heard your name before you ever gave it." The boy looked up at him, and in the lantern light his eyes were the exact color of the river at dusk — brown, patient, and hiding all kinds of depth. "Whitlock. You're a Whitlock. And I need to take you home."

The fire popped. Somewhere upstream, miles off in the dark, the river kept talking to itself in its low gravel voice, and Felix Whitlock — who counted miles because miles never asked him who he was — felt the last comfortable lie of his quiet life come loose from its mooring and begin, gently, to drift.
