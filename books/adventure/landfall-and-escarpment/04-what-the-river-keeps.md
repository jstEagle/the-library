## 4. What the River Keeps

Teo had hidden in worse places than a crate of dried beans. He had once spent two days in the smokehouse behind Aunt Sefa's drying racks, breathing through a hollow reed while the searchers walked past close enough to touch. Compared to that, the crate was almost comfortable — except for the beans, which got into everything, and except for the fact that this crate was on a boat, and boats, in Teo's experience, were how people left and never came back.

He had watched them leave. That was the whole trouble.

Three strangers had come up the river in autumn, with their shiny instruments and their loud voices and their leader who smiled too much, the way men smile when they are counting something that isn't theirs yet. Teo had been sent down to the terraces to scare birds, which was a job for someone small and expendable, and so he had seen what he saw: the strangers walking into the council house at dusk, and the elders going in after them, and then — nothing. No supper fires. No voices. In the morning the strangers' camp was empty and so was the council house, and Ada stood on the high step looking downriver with an expression Teo had never seen on her face before, and that was when he understood that even Ada didn't know where everyone had gone.

The valley had rules about leaving. Everyone knew them. You did not go past the sentinel stones, you did not show yourself to the river, and above all you did not *go down* — because the river went down to the world, and the world ate whatever the river brought it. Teo was eleven years old and mostly obedient, but he could not stay in a place where people vanished and the grown-ups pretended it was a matter for councils. Councils took weeks. Teo had four days of courage saved up like coins, and he meant to spend every one.

So he had climbed out through the fern gully where the stones were worn smooth by his own grandfather's boots, and run down to the water, and hidden in a supply boat's cargo like a mouse in a granary, and now here he was, being carried back toward the very thing he'd fled from, because apparently courage spent faster than it saved.

The boat's engine changed its mind about something. The whole hull shivered, and Teo heard the captain's voice through the planking — low, unhurried, the voice of a man talking to a machine he trusted more than most people. Then footsteps on deck, heavy boots passing right over Teo's head, and a woman's voice, sharper: Imogen, the surveyor. Teo had listened to enough of their talk to know the shape of things. They were going upstream. Upstream was home. Home was also, possibly, where the missing strangers had gone, and where the captain would find out exactly what kind of stowaway was sleeping in his beans.

That was the part Teo hadn't planned for: what happened after the courage ran out.

* * *

The crate lid came off in the middle of the afternoon, and light poured in like cold water.

Teo had a speech prepared. It had taken him two days to compose, standing watch over the beans, rehearsing under his breath until the words wore smooth as river glass. It began with an apology and ended with an offer, and in between it made him sound brave and useful and not at all like a boy who had cried the first night because the stars were wrong — too many, too bright, all jumbled, none of them the ones Grandmother had taught him to steer by.

What actually came out of his mouth was: "Don't throw me off."

The captain looked down at him. He was a tall man with river-colored eyes and a beard going gray at the edges, and he did not look surprised, which was somehow worse. Behind him, the surveyor woman put her hand over her mouth in a way that meant she was laughing and trying not to be rude about it.

"You've been in my beans," the captain said, "for three days."

"Two and a half," Teo said, because if you were going to be caught, you might as well be accurate. It was one of Ada's sayings and it arrived in his mouth without permission, and something in his chest pinched at hearing her voice in his head.

The captain crouched down to his level. Up close he smelled of engine oil and coffee, and there was a tiredness around his eyes that Teo recognized, the kind that comes from carrying something heavy for a long time and pretending it's a coat.

"What's your name?"

"Teo."

"Just Teo?"

Teo said nothing. Names were doors, and he wasn't opening any more doors today. The captain seemed to understand this, which unsettled Teo more than shouting would have. Shouting he knew how to survive.

"I should turn this boat around," the captain said.

"If you turn around, you'll wreck." Teo sat up straighter, beans cascading off his shoulders. "There's a marker buoy at the second bend below the gorge. Someone moved it. It's set over the drowned timber, where the channel used to be before the slide. Your boat draws too much to go that way. You'll open your hull like a fish."

The captain and the surveyor exchanged a look. Teo had seen adults exchange that look his whole life — the look that said *the child is right and I hate it.*

"How do you know our draft?" the woman asked.

"I've been living under your floor. You argue about fuel every morning." He brushed beans from his hair with as much dignity as he could gather. "I know other things too. I know the Gorge of Needles. Nobody pilots the Needles from outside. Not once. Not ever. You'll want someone who grew up on that water."

"And why," the captain said slowly, "would you help us get there?"

Here it was. The door. Teo looked at the man's hands — big, scarred, steady on the rail — and made his choice the way you jump into deep water: all at once, before your legs can vote.

"Because I'm going home," he said, "and you're going the same way. And because—" His voice wobbled and he hated it. He steadied it. "Because people from my valley are missing. Strangers came, and then they were gone, and our people went after them, and now they're gone too. If you're looking for your expedition, maybe we're looking for the same answer. And nobody finds anything in the Needles alone."

Silence, except the river slapping the hull and the engine ticking as it cooled.

"The Gorge of Needles," the captain repeated. Something crossed his face — not fear exactly, but the memory of fear, worn smooth like a stone carried in a pocket for years. "My father talked about that place."

Teo's heart stopped, restarted, and started running. "Your father?"

"A long time ago." The captain stood, ending the conversation the way tall people do, and turned to the rail. But Teo had already seen what he needed to see, because when the man had crouched down, something had swung free of his collar on a cord — something brass, round, dented along one edge, with a hinged cover worn nearly blank.

Teo knew that object. Every child in the valley knew it, the way children know a story before they know it's true. The finder's mark. The compass that had come up the river fifty years ago in the pocket of a young mapmaker, the one the old songs still argued about — the one who found the valley and then, instead of selling it, gave it away. The one whose name the elders only said in winter, quietly, like a prayer or a debt.

*Whitlock.* The name rose in Teo's throat and he swallowed it down. Not yet. Doors, opened carefully, one at a time.

* * *

They let him sleep that night in the wheelhouse instead of the beans, which Teo considered a promotion. The captain — Felix, the surveyor called him, though he never offered the rest — spread charts on the chart table and asked questions in a voice that never hurried, and Teo answered, and slowly the boat's future unrolled between them like a ribbon.

"Here." Teo put his finger on the gorge's throat, where the canyon pinched to almost nothing. "The water goes through in three channels, but only the middle one is real. The others are the river's lies. My grandmother taught me the song for it — the depth changes with the season, so you can't trust any map older than the rain."

"Fifty years of maps," Felix said quietly, "and the rain wins anyway."

"That's why the valley stays hidden. The river keeps what it wants." Teo hesitated. "You said your father talked about the Needles. Was he a pilot?"

"He drew them." Felix's finger traced the canyon on the chart, and Teo noticed the drawing was better than the printing around it — surer, more loving, done by someone who had stood on those banks. "A long time ago. Before I was born."

"Did he come through?"

"No." A pause, long enough for the lamp to flicker. "He came *from* there. Going the other way. And he never said much about what he'd left behind." Felix looked at Teo then, really looked, with that river-colored gaze that seemed to weigh things. "You flinched when I mentioned him. Earlier. At the word *father.*"

"I didn't flinch."

"You flinched like a fish takes a hook."

Teo glared at the chart. Above the gorge, faintly, in old faded ink beneath the printed names, someone had written a single word in a careful hand: *home?* — with the question mark pressed hard enough to tear the paper. Teo stared at it. He knew that handwriting. He had seen it on the valley's oldest boundary stones, on the inside cover of the song-book, on the plaque by the spring.

"You knew him," Teo said. It wasn't a question.

Felix went very still. Outside, the river murmured against the hull, patient as always, keeping what it kept.

"Teo," he said at last, and his voice had changed — lower, careful, a man stepping onto ice and testing it. "When you say home. When you say the valley. What do they call the founder? The first one. The mapmaker."

Teo looked at the compass hanging at the man's throat, brass and dented and worn blank by fifty years of a thumb rubbing it, and felt the whole river tilt beneath him.

"We don't say his name in summer," Teo said. "But in winter, the elders call him E. Whitlock. And they say his blood is still in the valley." He swallowed. "And I think some of it just walked in off this boat."

Felix closed his eyes. When he opened them again, he reached up and took the compass cord from around his neck and held it out across the chart table, over the faded ink, over the torn question mark, his hand not quite steady.

"Then you'd better tell me," he said, "everything the winter songs say. Because tomorrow we run the Needles — and I'm starting to think I've been piloting toward this my whole life without knowing it."
