## 5. The Gorge of Needles

The gorge announced itself a full hour before they reached it, the way bad news does — first as a rumor, then as a certainty. The walls of the Sereno's valley began to lean inward, green slopes giving way to gray stone, and the river, which had spent two days ambling and gossiping around bends, went suddenly quiet and quick, like a man who has just remembered an appointment he'd rather not keep.

Felix stood at the wheel of the *Marta June* and did the arithmetic he always did when the country turned hostile. Draft: three feet, four inches with her holds loaded. Beam: too wide for comfort and exactly wide enough for survival. Current ahead: unknown. That last figure was the one that bothered him. He had run rivers where the charts were wrong, but never one where the charts were *silent* — and the silence here had his father's handwriting all over it.

"Teo says there's a slot," Imogen said, coming up beside him with two cups of coffee she'd made strong enough to strip varnish. She handed him one. "He says his people call it the Gorge of Needles because the rock comes down in points. Like teeth."

"Cheerful lot."

"They're survivors." She said it without judgment, which was one of the things Felix was learning to like about her. "Survivors get cheerful names for terrible places. It's how they laugh at them."

The boy himself sat on the crate of rope coils near the stern, knees drawn up, watching the walls close in with an expression Felix couldn't read — not fear exactly. Recognition. Teo had been quiet since the bargain was struck, quiet the way cargo is quiet, as if he were still deciding whether being aboard was a rescue or a second mistake. But when Felix had asked him about the channel ahead, the boy had come alive, drawing shapes in spilled coffee on the deck: the narrows, the winch points, the place where the water dropped through a throat of stone.

"He says the old road through is marked," Imogen translated now, watching the boy's hands. "Iron rings. Drilled into the rock by — " she paused, and Felix watched her decide whether to say it — "by the finder. Your father."

*E. Whitlock.* The initials sat in Felix's chest like a swallowed stone. Five years of leaving that name off manifests and charters and dockside small talk, and here it was, drilled into the very rock ahead of him, waiting.

"Then we'll hope he drilled them deep," Felix said, and rang the engine down to half.

* * *

The entrance to the gorge was a lie told by geology. From fifty yards out it looked impassable — a seam in the cliff face barely wider than the boat's beam, water sliding through it fast and glassy-smooth, which on any river Felix knew meant depth, and depth meant the only mercy this place intended to offer. Above the seam, the rock rose six hundred feet in walls so vertical they seemed to have been cut with a blade and polished out of spite. Needles indeed: spires of stone leaned over the gap from both sides, some of them meeting overhead, so that running the passage would feel like threading a needle while standing inside the eye of it.

Felix took the *Marta June* in at dead slow, hands light on the wheel, breathing the way his first captain had taught him — in through the nose, out through the teeth, never let them see you count.

The walls came close enough to touch. Close enough that he could have scraped frost off the stone with his knuckles. The sound changed instantly — the engine note bouncing back off the rock, the wake slapping against the canyon walls and returning, so the boat seemed to be traveling inside its own noise. Overhead, a stripe of sky no wider than a ribbon.

"Rings," Imogen called from the bow. "Starboard side. I can see them."

They were there, just above the waterline: iron rings, green with age, set into the rock at intervals of perhaps forty feet, marching away up the passage into the gloom. Each one had been set with care, bedded in lead, and each one bore a stamp worn nearly smooth but legible if you pressed your thumb into the cold metal and believed.

*E.W.*

Felix pressed his thumb into the third ring and held it there longer than the work required.

"All right," he said, to the ring, to the rock, to nobody. "You did the hard part."

The plan was simple and hated. Past the fourth ring the current steepened into a chute no engine could fight; beyond that, the passage narrowed further and bent twice around pillars of fallen stone. They would warp the boat through by hand — line out to a ring, take up the slack on the winch, hold her against the current while they moved to the next anchor, forty feet at a time, for a quarter mile. It was the kind of work that took hours and forgave nothing.

It went well for ninety minutes, which on the Sereno constituted a golden age.

* * *

The trouble started with the fifth ring.

Imogen had the bow line fast and Felix was taking up the winch when the cable went slack all at once — not the gradual give of a stretching line but the sickening instant release of something letting go. The *Marta June* swung her nose downstream like a horse shying, and Felix had one clear image of the chute ahead, white water boiling between two black fangs of stone, before his hands were moving on their own.

"Reverse! Hard reverse!" He threw the wheel over and the engine bellowed, and the boat checked, trembled, hung — and slid sideways instead of downstream, grinding along the base of the wall with a sound like a giant filing his teeth, until her stern kissed a ledge and stopped.

Silence. The ribbon of sky. Water talking to itself in the dark below.

"Everyone account for themselves," Felix said, and heard his own voice come out level, which surprised him.

"Fine." Imogen, at the bow, hauling in the failed line. "The ring didn't fail. Look."

She was right. The ring stood in its lead bedding, solid as the day it was set. What had failed was the shackle — someone had backed the pin halfway out and peened the threads flat, a sabotage neat enough to survive inspection until load came on it. The same handiwork, Felix thought, as the marker buoy they'd found cut loose below Port Calloway. Someone upstream was tidying the river of visitors, one careful act at a time.

Teo crouched by the shackle, touched the mangled pin, and said something short and bitter in the valley tongue. When Imogen asked, he looked at Felix with an expression far older than his years.

"He says the wardens do this," she translated. "The ones who seal the passes. He says — " the boy hesitated, choosing words — "he says the finder's rings are holy, and breaking them is a sin, and they do it anyway, because keeping people out matters more than what's holy."

Felix knelt and examined the damage. The pin was ruined; the spare shackles were in the tool locker aft, which meant crossing the deck, which meant trusting the ledge under the stern to hold while the current leaned on the hull. He did the arithmetic again. It came out the same way it always did: somebody has to go.

"I'll get the shackles," he said. "Nobody else moves."

Getting them took eleven minutes and cost him a bootful of river and most of the skin on one palm. Coming back, wedged against the rail with the toolbox hugged to his chest, he passed the fuel drums lashed amidships and noticed what he should have noticed an hour ago: a slow shine of diesel tracking along the deck seams toward the scuppers. One of the drums had worked its chocks during the grind against the wall. A fitting had cracked. Not gushing — weeping. The kind of leak you find either early or too late.

They lost half their fuel to that crack, pumping what they could save into intact drums while the river hissed past a hand's breadth away. Felix sat back on his heels afterward and looked at the gauge on the remaining supply and did the worst arithmetic of the trip: enough to run easy for two days, or hard for one. The return journey, unaccounted for.

"We go forward," Imogen said, reading him. "We're past the point where turning around costs less."

"We were past that point in Port Calloway," Felix said. "I just hadn't done the math yet."

Teo, watching the fuel drum with the others, said something quietly. Imogen listened, then laughed — a surprised, real laugh, the first since the gorge began.

"What?"

"He says his grandmother says a boat with half its fuel travels twice as carefully, and therefore arrives twice as wise."

"Your grandmother," Felix said, "sounds like a woman who has talked captains out of doing stupid things before."

"She has," Teo said — his first English since coming aboard, flat and deliberate, chosen like a stone chosen for skipping. Felix decided not to make anything of it. Some doors you let people open themselves.

* * *

They cleared the last bend near dusk, the walls falling back just enough to permit a camp on a gravel shelf, and it was there — tucked under an overhang, stacked and tarped with expedition discipline — that they found the cache.

Vance's mark was stenciled on every crate: *SERENO HEADWATERS SURVEY*. Food, mostly, and rope, and a wooden case of instruments, and — Felix's heart gave a single hard knock — a radio transceiver, the expedition's spare, lashed inside a waterproof bag.

The cache had been opened and repacked more than once. Someone had lived off it. And someone had left a note, weighted under a stone, written in pencil on the back of a survey form:

*Going ahead up the left-hand channel. Two of us. Vance is gone. Don't follow the main stem — it's watched. If anyone reads this, we're making for the high trail. — R.O.*

Imogen read it twice and had to hand it back before her voice would behave. "Rosa Okafor," she said finally. "Our field medic. And Danny Chen. They're alive."

Felix crouched by the radio. It was a good unit, expedition-grade, and it had been packed with care — but the battery pack was missing, and when he lifted the set, the housing was warm. Not sun-warm. The gravel shelf lay in shadow; the overhang killed the evening light entirely. Warm like something recently working. Warm like a machine that had been transmitting, or receiving, within the hour.

He held it in both hands and felt the warmth drain slowly out of it and thought: *they're close.* Not days close. Hours. Maybe less. Rosa Okafor had carried a radio she couldn't power, or had paused here to listen on some other set, or — and this was the thought that put iron in his spine — someone else had been listening, and Rosa had known it, and had left the set deliberately, a message for whoever came behind: *we were here, we are ahead, hurry.*

Or the watchers had been here. Either way, the warmth said *recent*, and recent said *possible*, and possible was more than Felix Whitlock had allowed himself to believe since Port Calloway.

He stood up. The tiredness was still in his body — the winch work lived in his shoulders and forearms like a debt — but somewhere beneath it, something had shifted gear. For five years he had run miles the way other men ran from debts, counting them because counting kept the larger question quiet. Now the miles had a direction, and the direction had people in it, and fear had burned down overnight into something cleaner and far more dangerous.

Purpose.

"First light," he said, stowing the note in his breast pocket, over the pocket where the brass compass rode. "We take the left-hand channel. We travel quiet, and we travel fast."

Teo was already looking upstream, into the dark where the channel split, and whatever he saw there made him pull his knees in tighter.

"And after the split?" Imogen asked.

Felix looked at the boy, and the boy looked back, and neither of them needed a translator for what passed between them.

"After the split," Felix said, "somebody's going to have to explain to me why my family name is carved into a canyon a thousand miles from anywhere — and why the people who live there shoot at it."
