## 6. Escarpment

*Recovered journal of Imogen Reyes, surveyor, Sereno Headwaters Expedition. Entries dated by river reckoning; the last pages water-stained but legible.*

* * *

**Day 41 — Camp at the Second Shelf**

Dr. Vance says we are eleven miles from the source, by his reckoning, and I say we are eleven miles from the end of the map, which is not the same thing but rhymes with it.

I should write down how it happened, while my hands still remember the cold.

The gorge opened at dawn like a book someone had been keeping shut for a hundred years. We came around the last bend of the Needles — Halloran swearing at the winch, Okafor on the bow line, me in the stern with the theodolite wrapped in my coat because my fingers had gone stupid — and there it was: a wall of rock a thousand feet high, green at the top, white with falling water in three places, and a cleft in the middle where the river simply *stopped being visible*, as if the land had swallowed it out of politeness.

Vance stood on the bow and laughed. Actually laughed, out loud, the way he hasn't since the radio died. "Reyes," he said, "we are the first people in recorded history to see this."

And I remember thinking — I remember it exactly, because it has been bothering me ever since like a splinter — that the cleft was not unmarked. There was a post on the near bank. Old, silvered with weather, but squared by tools. And tied to it, high enough to survive the floods, hung a bundle of river-cane, woven in a pattern I did not recognize and did not like.

Somebody had said *welcome* or *go away* to this place before us. The two signs look identical from a distance. That is the whole trouble with signs.

* * *

**Day 43 — Above the Escarpment**

We are through. I hardly know how to write it.

The cleft was a slot no wider than the boat, and the river inside it ran green and slow, which is the river's version of a lie, because the current underneath was pulling like a team of oxen. Halloran wanted to portage. Vance wanted through. We went through, and for two hours I did nothing but read the water and pray to the god of surveyors, who is the same as the god of gamblers.

And then the gorge opened its hand.

A valley. I have surveyed the coast, the high desert, the cloud forest on the far side of the Cordillera, and I have never — I am setting down the plain fact and not the poetry — I have never seen ground so *tended*. Terraces climbing the valley walls like the rings of a tree. Orchards in rows. A channel cut to turn the river through a mill, and a mill turning. Fields of something golden that Okafor swears is a grain that isn't supposed to grow at this altitude.

And people. Dozens of them. They came down to the banks and stood in a line, and not one of them looked surprised to see us.

That is the part I keep circling back to. Halloran said, "They've seen boats before." Vance said, "Descendants of a wrecked expedition, probably — the 1890s party, the one that vanished." And that is a good theory, a *publishable* theory, and I believed it for about a day.

Then an old woman came down to the water alone, ahead of the others, and looked at me — at *me*, not Vance, not the boat, me — with an expression I can only describe as recognition, and said:

"You have his chin."

I said, "I beg your pardon?"

"Your captain," she said. "The one who is coming up the river behind you. He has his father's chin, and his father's chin came from this valley."

I told her we had no captain coming behind us. I told her we were the expedition.

She smiled at me the way you smile at a child who has announced the sky is made of glass, and she said, "No. You are the *invitation*. He is the answer. He always was."

Her name, the others told us, is Ada. Ada Whitlock.

I have not written that name down until now because I did not want it to be real. There was a chart in our charter papers, signed E. Whitlock, and Vance has been following it like scripture since Port Calloway, and I have watched our captain — Felix, the one who won't say his surname, the one who counts miles the way other men count rosaries — go pale every time it comes up.

I don't know what any of it means. I know that when I slept last night, on dry ground, under a roof someone offered without being asked twice, I dreamed of a brass compass, and woke up homesick for a boat.

* * *

**Day 45 — The Long House**

The valley has a name for itself and it is not on any chart: they call it the Finding. Everyone here is descended from someone who was lost — wrecked boats, lost expeditions, one man who claims a mail plane in 1931. The valley finds them, Ada says. That is its whole purpose and its whole law.

Vance presented his case tonight at their evening meal, and I will record it fairly: he was good. He spoke of science, of contact, of medicine and maps and the dignity of being *known*. He said the outside world would come eventually, and better it come as friends than as loggers.

Ada let him finish. That was the first warning sign, I think. She let him finish.

"Doctor," she said, "we have medicine. We have our dead, and we know their names, all the way back to the wrecks. What we do not have is a road. A road is a wound that never closes. You are not the first man to come up here holding a map like a lantern. You are the first to come up holding one signed by my brother."

Her *brother*. E. Whitlock. Elias, she said, though no one else has said it aloud yet. Vance went very still, the way he goes still before he decides to double down, and I watched it happen in real time, and I thought: *oh no.*

There is a faction here, I should note — younger, restless, led by a man called Rurik who was born of the mail-plane line and has never seen a city and wants to, badly. They like Vance. They like the idea of the world. Ada's people hold the council, though, and the council voted tonight, and the vote was: the passes close. The cleft gets sealed. No one goes down; no one comes up.

And we are on the wrong side of that vote.

* * *

**Day 46**

They are sealing the gorge. I watched forty people carry cut timber and stone to the cleft with the efficiency of people who have drilled for this their whole lives, and I understood that the drill is why they have survived.

Vance is not taking it well. He has stopped eating with the others. He has the charts out on his cot at all hours, and tonight I heard him tell Halloran that the valley "belongs in the record" and that a finding that isn't published "is just a rumor with good scenery." Halloran laughed. I did not.

Okafor came to me after, quiet, and said, "Imogen. The old woman's people found two of our supply crates moved. Vance moved them. Downriver side of camp. He's planning something."

I asked him what.

"I don't know," Okafor said. "But he asked me how much fuel the boat would need to run the gorge at flood stage. And then he asked me what would happen if the logjam at the head of the lake let go."

I have been a surveyor for fourteen years and I have never once prayed for a flood. I am starting.

* * *

**Day 48 — Written badly, hand shaking, ink smeared**

Elias Whitlock's grave is up on the third terrace, under a plum tree, and Vance found it.

I don't know how. A child told him, I think — one of Rurik's faction, wanting to please him. And Vance went up alone at dawn, and came down with a tin box, and the box held the *original* charts. Not copies. The hand-drawn ones, with corrections in two inks and notes in the margins in a hand I now recognize as our captain's father's, because Felix writes the same way, I have seen it on his manifests — that slanted, impatient, beautiful script.

Vance stood in front of the whole settlement holding the box over his head like a trophy and said, "This is the inheritance of the entire world, and I will not let it be buried under a plum tree."

And Ada said, very quietly, "That is my brother's grave you are standing on."

And Vance said, "Your brother understood that."

I have never heard a valley go silent before. It has a sound. It is the sound of a mill stopping.

* * *

**Day 49**

He fell.

I have to write it plainly because everything in me wants to write it any other way. Vance went up the escarpment trail before light, alone, with the charts in a satchel and a rope he had not checked, because the settlers had taken his good rope when the passes closed and pride is a kind of blindness. Okafor found his camp broken on the ledge below the third fall. The satchel was gone — into the river, most likely, or into a crack, or into Ada's keeping, and I honestly do not know which would be worse for him.

He was alive when we reached him. That is the terrible mercy of it. Broken at the hip and the back, cold, and lucid, and the first thing he said — before pain, before water, before anything — was:

"Reyes. The radio. Get the radio working. Someone has to tell them where it is."

And I knelt down in the wet gravel and took his hand, and I lied to him. I said, "The radio's working, Marcus. They know. Rest now."

He smiled. He actually smiled, and said, "Good. Good. Eleven miles," and then he looked up at the escarpment, at the green wall and the three white falls, and his eyes went soft the way they only ever went soft over a contour line, and he said, "It's beautiful, isn't it? It's so *beautiful*," and then he was gone between one breath and the next, like a man stepping off a charted bank into unmarked water.

We buried him this afternoon where he fell, facing the falls. Ada came. She stood with her hands folded and she said, over the grave, "He was wrong about nearly everything, and he loved this valley truly, and both of those things are true at once, and that is the hardest thing about people." Then she put a woven river-cane bundle on the cairn — the same pattern as the one on the post in the gorge — and I finally understood that the pattern is not welcome or go away.

It is *found*. It is what they weave for everyone the valley keeps.

* * *

**Day 51**

The passes are sealed. The cleft is closed with timber and stone, and the settlers have gone back to their terraces, and Rurik's faction has scattered to their fields, and it should be over. It should be quiet.

It is not quiet, because of two things.

The first: Okafor and Halloran are being held at the long house, pending what Ada calls "the river's judgment," which no one will explain to me, and which terrifies me more than any threat I have ever heard, because it sounds like something you wait for rather than something you argue against.

The second: last night, at the edge of camp, I heard the sealed cleft — the closed gorge, the shut book — make a sound.

A low, deep, patient groan, like a ship settling, like a joint popping, like the land clearing its throat. And the river behind the seal was running higher than it was two days ago. I put my hand in it and the cold came up my arm like a message.

Flood season is early this year. Everyone in the valley knows it; I've watched them look at the waterline and say nothing to each other, which is its own kind of saying.

And this morning, on the far bank, a boy stood watching our camp. Fourteen, maybe. Thin, river-barefoot, with the finder's mark woven at his wrist. When he saw me see him, he didn't run. He looked at the sealed cleft, and then at me, and held up one hand — five fingers — then closed them slowly, one by one.

I don't know what it means. But I know the radio in our abandoned cache downriver was warm when we found it, and I know someone has been moving ahead of us on this river since the day we entered the gorge, and I know — the way you know a storm by the smell of it — that whatever is coming up that river is coming fast.

Ada stood on her terrace at dawn, looking downriver, and I swear she was counting miles.
