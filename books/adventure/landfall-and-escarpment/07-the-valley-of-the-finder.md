## 7. The Valley of the Finder

The river stopped being a river an hour before Felix understood what he was looking at.

It thinned, first — braided itself into threads that wandered through a marsh of reeds so tall they brushed the *Sounder's* wheelhouse roof. Then the threads found a single channel again, narrow and green-shaded, and the water went glass-still, and the banks rose on either side like the walls of a held breath.

"Teo," Felix said quietly, "you could have mentioned the river ends."

"It doesn't end," Teo said from the bow, where he'd stationed himself with the gravity of a harbormaster. "It *arrives*. There's a difference. Papa says rivers that arrive are the only ones worth the trip."

Ahead, the channel bent around a shoulder of stone — and then opened.

Felix had spent five years on the Sereno and its daughters. He thought he knew what water could do to country. But nothing in his mile-counting life had prepared him for the valley, because the valley had not been carved so much as *tended*. Terraces climbed the far slopes in long green steps, each one stitched with irrigation channels that caught the light and threw it back like spilled coins. Orchards ran in disciplined rows. Smoke rose from a dozen chimneys, thin and unhurried. A footbridge crossed the channel downstream, and on it, two figures had stopped to watch the boat come through, and neither of them seemed the least bit alarmed, which frightened Felix more than gunfire would have.

They had expected him.

"Imogen," he said over his shoulder, "you seeing this?"

Imogen Reyes stood at the rail with her surveyor's eyes working — measuring, triangulating, cataloguing — and Felix watched the cataloguing fail her. "The maps say this is a box canyon with no outlet," she said. "The maps say there's nothing here but scree and a seasonal creek." She laughed, and it came out unsteady. "The maps have been lying for fifty years, Felix."

"Not lying," Teo said. "Keeping."

* * *

They came ashore at a stone landing where the channel widened into something like a harbor, if a harbor could be carved by patience instead of commerce. Boats were drawn up on skids — flat-bottomed, shallow-drafted, built by people who knew rivers. Nets hung drying. A woman mending one looked up, took in Felix's face, and went very still, the way you go still when you've seen a ghost wearing a stranger's clothes.

"Whitlock," she said. Not a question.

"I don't — " Felix started, the old reflex, the five-year reflex, the *I'm nobody, I'm just the boat, I'm miles not years* — and then he heard himself, heard the absurdity of denying a name on a landing where a mending woman had read it off his jawline like a chart notation, and stopped. "Yes," he said. It came out rusty. "Felix Whitlock."

The woman put down her net and her needle and looked at him for a long moment, and then she did something Felix was entirely unprepared for. She smiled.

"He had your frown," she said. "Exactly your frown. Come on, then. Ada's been waiting on you since before you were born, though she'd never admit it."

* * *

The walk up from the landing went through the terraces, and the terraces went through Felix like a blade finding an old scar.

Children stopped their work to stare. An old man on a ladder in an apple tree called down something in a language Felix didn't know, and Teo answered in it, laughing, and the old man laughed too and threw down an apple that Teo caught without breaking stride. There were faces from everywhere in the crowd that gathered, walking alongside at a respectful distance the way people do with something they're not sure is safe: faces that might have come off wrecked expeditions, stranded surveys, lost patrols. Descendants of everyone the Sereno had ever swallowed. Felix counted four distinct skin tones, three styles of dress, one shared wariness.

And everywhere, worked into fence posts and door lintels and the brass fittings of the drawn-up boats, the same mark. A circle. A needle. A compass rose with the north point replaced by a river's fork.

"The finder's mark," Teo said, watching him look. "You really didn't know?"

"I knew my father drew maps," Felix said. "I knew he died doing it. That was the whole of my education on the subject."

"Then you've got a lot of catching up," Teo said, with the gentle ruthlessness of the very young, "and not a lot of river left to do it in."

* * *

Ada Whitlock received them in a long room with a stone floor and a wall of windows looking down the whole green staircase of the valley. She was smaller than Felix had imagined — he hadn't known he'd imagined her, but he clearly had, and in the imagining she'd been a fortress. In person she was more like a keel: low, essential, holding everything up from underneath. Her hair was white as river foam and cut short and practical, and her hands were a farmer's hands and a boatbuilder's hands and, when she folded them on the table, unmistakably a Whitlock's hands. Felix knew those hands. He shaved around his own every morning.

"Sit," she said. "You'll be Felix. You have Edmund's chin and your mother's stubbornness, and you've been piloting that boat like a man trying to outrun a question. Tea?"

"I — how did you — "

"Teo's pigeon arrived four days ago." She poured without asking again, three cups, and pushed one toward Imogen. "You'll be the surveyor. Reyes. Your Dr. Vance is the reason you're here, and the reason two of your people are sitting in a room down the hill being fed very well and not permitted to leave, and we'll come to all of that. First things first." She looked at Felix, and her eyes were his father's eyes, and it was like being read by a chart. "You were told Edmund Whitlock died mapping the Sereno. Who told you?"

"The company. The expedition sponsors. There was a letter. There was a — a settlement, and a plaque on a wall in Port Calloway, and everyone said it like it was weather. *Lost on the river, mapping the headwaters. Terrible thing. Noble work.*"

"Noble work," Ada repeated, and something in her voice could have stripped paint. "Edmund didn't die mapping, Felix. Edmund died *old*, in that bed by the window, two winters back, holding my hand and worrying about you. He lived here twenty-six years. He built half those terraces. He married a woman from the second wreck — the *Almirante*, out of Calloway, fifty-one souls, eleven survivors — and he raised me, because I was on that boat too, and I was nine." She took a slow sip of tea. "He was my brother. Which makes me your aunt, and makes this valley the only home your father had for the last half of his life, and makes you the only Whitlock who ever left and came back."

The room was very quiet. Down in the valley, someone was calling children to a meal, and the sound came up through the windows like water.

"He sent me away," Felix said finally. "I remember. I was eleven. He put me on a boat to my aunt in Calloway — a different aunt — and he said the city would give me a better life, and he never wrote, and he never came, and I spent twenty years deciding what I'd done wrong."

"Nothing," Ada said. "You did nothing. That's the cruelest part and I'll not soften it. He sent you away because the valley was dying to be found and he knew it. Every year the company pushed further upriver. Every year another survey, another charter, another set of boots. And every year he asked himself what would happen to these people — *your* people, now, whether you like it or not — when a map with a road on it reached a city full of people who need what this valley has and have the numbers to take it. He chose. He chose them over you, and it broke him, and he did it anyway, and he spent the rest of his life making the maps that would keep surveyors *out* — false channels, drowned leads, that gorge of his. The Gorge of Needles is Edmund Whitlock's masterwork, Captain. You winched your way through your father's life's work last week and cursed his name the whole time, I'd wager."

"I cursed it," Felix admitted, "quite thoroughly."

"Good. He'd have laughed." And for one moment the keel of the woman cracked and Felix saw the grief in her, fresh as the day it was poured. "He asked about you every winter. Every single one. *Has the boy found work he loves? Is he careful on the water?* And every winter I said, *Edmund, write to him,* and every winter he said, *a letter is a thread, and a thread is a road, and a road is a map, and I will not hand them my son.*" She set down her cup. "Then he sent you the compass. Did you wonder about that? A dying man, a brass compass tucked into charter paperwork, five years after his death — no. After his *leaving*. Someone wanted you to follow. Someone was tired of you measuring your life in miles."

Felix looked at the compass where it sat in his breast pocket, and it felt suddenly like a hand on his shoulder from a man twenty years gone.

* * *

It was near dusk when Ada walked them down the hill to the room where the survivors were kept — a guest house, really, with a garden and a view and a door that locked from the outside, which was the part Felix objected to.

Inside were two of the expedition: a young geologist named Okafor, wrist splinted and healing, and a radio operator named Brandt, who stood at the window like a man who'd been standing at windows for nine days. They looked at Felix with the desperate arithmetic of the abandoned.

"Captain," Brandt said. "Thank God. Vance is dead — he fell, at the escarpment, before we understood any of it — and the others went home downriver weeks ago, and these people won't let us transmit, won't let us leave, and I don't know if that's hospitality or a prison, and I have been doing *terrible* arithmetic in my head for nine days."

"It's neither," Ada said from the doorway, "or it's both. Your fate isn't mine to decide, Mr. Brandt. It never was. In this valley, when a stranger comes and the question of them can't be settled by talk, we put it to the river's judgment."

"The river's judgment," Imogen said carefully. "Which is?"

Ada looked out the window, down the valley, to where the gorge lake lay flat and pewter-colored in the last light — higher than it had been in anyone's memory, Teo had said, higher than the old logs were built to hold.

"Flood season," she said, "came early this year. And the judgment water is rising."

Behind her, far up the valley, a sound rolled down the terraces like the first word of an argument the whole river intended to finish: a crack, deep and wooden, like a great door being tested by a shoulder.
