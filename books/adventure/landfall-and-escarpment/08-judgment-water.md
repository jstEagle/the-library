## 8. Judgment Water

The river told them first.

Felix woke before dawn to a sound he knew better than his own heartbeat — a low, grinding mutter under the valley's usual hush, like a giant turning over in bed. He was out of the guest house and down to the waterline before the sky had finished deciding on its color, and there he saw it: the Sereno running brown where it should have run green, thick with bark shavings and splinters the size of dinner plates.

Upstream, the gorge lake was letting go of its manners.

"Three weeks early," said Teo at his elbow, materializing the way boys do, as if they'd been standing there all along. His face was pale in the half-light. "Grandmother Ada says the snowpack melted fast this year. She says—" He stopped. Swallowed. "She says she's never seen it like this."

Felix crouched and put two fingers in the current. Cold enough to ache. Cold enough that it had come off ice, high up, and come down fast. He thought about the logjam he'd seen from the escarpment — a thousand years of deadfall packed into the gorge throat, holding back a lake the size of a small sea, and nobody downstream the wiser. Nobody downstream even knowing it existed.

"How many people live below Port Calloway?" he asked.

Teo blinked. "I don't—"

"Ferry towns. Fishing camps. Calloway itself has what, four hundred souls? Five hundred?" He stood, wiped his hand on his trousers. "And every one of them thinks the river is just a river."

Behind them, a bell began to ring from the meeting hall — not the soft supper bell Felix had grown almost fond of these past two days, but a hard, urgent clanging with no rhythm to it at all.

* * *

The meeting hall smelled of woodsmoke and lamp oil and forty people's fear. Ada Whitlock stood at the front the way she seemed to stand everywhere — straight-backed, silver-braided, hands folded as though the world were a document she was reviewing for errors. Behind her, pinned to the great beam, hung the old charts: her father's work, E. Whitlock's work, the maps Felix had spent five years pretending didn't have his name written all over them in another man's hand.

"The lake is rising eight inches a day," Ada said. "The jam will hold through the season if we reinforce the north abutment. It has held for sixty years."

"It hasn't held," Felix said. "It's been *negotiating* for sixty years, and the negotiation just ended."

Murmurs. A woman near the door — Marta, who baked bread dense enough to anchor a boat — crossed herself with a flour-dusted hand.

Ada's eyes found him across the room, and they were his father's eyes, which was the cruelest thing about her. "You've been here two days, Captain."

"And I've run that river eleven years." He stepped forward into the lamplight. "I know what water does when it gets behind something it wants. That jam isn't sixty years old in any way that matters — the logs rot, the anchors shift, and this year the melt came early and came angry. You can patch the north abutment. Maybe you buy a week. And when it goes, it doesn't go politely. It comes down the gorge as a wall, and it flattens the valley first, and then it keeps going."

"To where?" someone called. "There's nothing below us but—"

"Calloway. The ferry landings. The fishing camps." He let that sit. "People who have never heard of you. People whose only crime is living downstream of your secret."

Silence, except for the bell still clanging outside until somebody finally had the sense to cut the rope.

Ada descended one step from the dais. "And your alternative, nephew? We send word downriver? We invite the world up? Surveyors, speculators, men with chains and deeds?" Her voice stayed level, but the room leaned toward her like grass toward sun. "My father watched what the world did to every expedition that found us. He built this valley's silence with his whole life. I will not drown it in daylight because the water is inconvenient."

"He didn't build the silence," Felix said quietly. "He died in it. Alone. Because keeping the secret meant never asking anyone for help — including his own son."

He hadn't meant to say that last part aloud. The room went very still. Somewhere a baby fussed and was hushed.

"You don't know what he—" Ada began.

"I know what he sent me." Felix's voice roughened. "A compass and a silence. Twenty years of both. And I know that right now, while we argue about whether the world deserves to know you exist, the world is about to arrive anyway — as a flood. Secrecy won't stop it. Nothing stops it. The only question left is whether anybody below you gets a warning, and whether this valley is still standing when the water's done."

An old man in the second row — Ferran, who mended the terraces — rose slowly to his feet. "Ada. My grandfather helped cut the first drain channel after the Long Winter. We've always known the jam was a debt." He looked around the room. "Debts get called."

Others stood. Not all — maybe half — but half was an ocean more than none, and Felix watched Ada understand it, watch her shoulders take the weight the way a mast takes wind: without bending, but visibly straining.

"The passes stay sealed," she said at last. "That much is not negotiable. But—" and here her voice did bend, barely, "—the captain may carry word downriver. And the two prisoners go with him."

A roar of protest. She raised one hand and the room obeyed her the way water obeys a wall — for now.

"They are surveyors, not soldiers," she said. "They fell with Vance's madness, not into it. And if the captain is right — if the world is coming regardless — then let it meet our mercy first." Her gaze swung back to Felix, and it was a challenge and a surrender in the same look. "Take them, Whitlock. Take your survivors. And take care, because if you're wrong about that jam, the last thing this family ever does together will be a mistake."

* * *

They released the survivors at noon, in the yard outside the meeting hall, under the eyes of half the settlement.

Imogen Reyes looked terrible and magnificent, the way she always did — sunburned, hollow-cheeked, her surveyor's coat torn at the shoulder and mended with valley thread. Beside her, young Okonkwo from the mapping team stood swaying on legs that had spent nine days eating short rations and longer silences.

"They fed us," Imogen said, low, as Felix checked the rope at her wrists one last time before cutting it. "They argued over us. Half of them wanted us gone and half wanted us dead and Ada kept the vote tied for a week." She flexed her fingers, winced. "You came."

"You went quiet."

"Nine days of radio silence and you come up a river your father died on, into a valley full of your own blood, and your opening line to me is *you went quiet*?"

Felix allowed himself a grin. "Missed you too."

Okonkwo was staring past them both, at the terraces climbing the valley walls, at the orchards, at children peeking from doorways. "It's real," he whispered. "Vance swore it was real and they all called him mad, and it's—" He stopped, because Imogen had put a hand on his arm.

"Not yet," she said. "Not one word, not one sketch, not one coordinate. Not until we're below the escarpment and Felix tells us what we are now."

"What are we now?" Okonkwo asked.

Felix looked at the valley — at the smoke rising gentle from chimneys his great-grandfather would have known, at Ada watching from the hall steps with her arms folded, at Teo sitting on the fence rail pretending not to listen to everything.

"Now," Felix said, "we're the warning."

* * *

They worked the rest of the day like people racing a clock they couldn't see. The *Marta's Blessing* — Felix had rechristened her on the ascent, over Teo's solemn objections that boats needed proper names — took on grain, smoked fish, and every able hand the valley could spare for the first leg to the gorge mouth. Felix walked the jam road with Ferran and three others, reading the water, marking where the abutment could be shored and where shoring was prayer dressed up as engineering.

At dusk, Ada found him at the landing.

For a long moment neither spoke. The river ran brown and loud between them, carrying its splinters south like a message neither of them could read yet.

"My brother wrote to me once a year," she said finally. "Every year, on the same day. Never a return address. Always the same closing line." She drew something from her coat — a letter, soft with handling, decades of creases worn to silk. She didn't offer it. Just held it. "*Keep the boy moving,*" she quoted. "*Moving water doesn't freeze.*"

"That sounds like him."

"He thought exile was a kind of armor. For you. For all of us." She looked out at the brown water. "You think I'm wrong to seal the passes."

"I think you're wrong to think secrecy is the same thing as safety. They're not the same, Aunt Ada. One of them is a choice. The other's just a smaller cage."

Something moved behind her face — grief, or its long-delayed cousin. "If the jam breaks before you're clear of the gorge—"

"Then I'll be exactly where my father's charts say the high water can't reach. Channel six, past the Split Rock." He tapped his temple. "I burned nothing yet. It's all still in here."

"Charts burn," Ada said. "Hands remember. That was always his hope for you — though he never had the courage to say it to your face." She turned to go, then paused. "Felix."

It was the first time she'd used his name. It landed somewhere under his ribs and stayed.

"When you reach Calloway — warn them well. And when they ask who brought the warning up from a river no map admits exists—" the ghost of something that was almost a smile touched her mouth, "—you may give them whatever name you can carry without flinching."

She walked back up the path into the lamplight, straight-backed as ever, and Felix stood alone at the landing a while, listening to the water grind its teeth upstream.

Teo appeared beside him, as usual, holding a lantern.

"She likes you," the boy said. "She yelled less than usual."

"That's grandmother for *warm regards*."

Above the gorge, thunder rolled — or something very like thunder, deep and structural, a sound with weight behind it. The lantern flame leaned. Down in the brown water, a fresh wave of splinters came spinning past the landing, thicker than the last, faster.

Felix put his hand on Teo's shoulder and turned him toward the boat.

"Get Imogen and Okonkwo aboard," he said. "We cast off within the hour. Before the river finishes making up its mind."
