## 9. Landfall

The lake above the gorge had begun to sing.

Felix heard it before he saw it — a low groan rolling down the canyon walls like a ship's hull working in heavy swell. The logjam, that vast tangle of drowned timber the valley's founders had woven across the gorge mouth a hundred years ago, was letting go. Behind it stood a lake the size of a small sea, swollen by an early flood season, and it was pressing its palms against the door.

"Tell me again why we're sailing *toward* that sound," Imogen said, gripping the rail of the *Marta June* as they rounded the last bend above the escarpment.

"Because the water's coming down whether we're in its way or not," Felix said. "Only question is whether it comes down all at once or a piece at a time."

On the shore below, torches were already moving. Ada's people — forty of them, maybe fifty — hauling axes and crosscut saws toward the jam, toward the three breach points they'd argued over half the night while the lake groaned louder. Teo was down there. Felix could pick him out even at this distance, small and quick, running messages between the work crews with the compass on a cord around his neck, bouncing against his chest like a second heart.

Ada had given them the plan at dawn, and it was a plan only a woman who'd spent her whole life holding a river together could love: breach the jam in sequence, upstream first, then the middle, then the toe — spill the lake in three swallows instead of one gulp. Enough to save the valley from drowning and spare the lower river towns a wall of water that would scrape them off the map. It depended on timing. It depended on the breaches holding. And it depended on Felix running the gorge *during* the flood, because the *Marta June* was the only thing that could carry the two expedition survivors, Imogen, and half a valley's worth of irreplaceable seed stock and medicine down to the towns before the last breach cut the river off entirely.

"You could wait," Ada had said, and for once there had been no iron in her voice. "You could wait an hour and run it after."

"An hour after, the channel won't exist," Felix had said. "It'll be a new river. Nobody's ever run a river on its first day of being a river."

"No," Ada had agreed. "But you're a Whitlock. First days are the family trade."

He hadn't known what to do with that. He still didn't. He put it in the same place he'd put his father's name, and his aunt's face, and the word *home* — somewhere below the waterline, where it couldn't sink him before the work was done.

* * *

The survivors came aboard first. Dr. Okonkwo, the expedition's botanist, with her arm in a sling and her eyes too calm — the calm of someone who'd spent two weeks as a guest of people she'd come to study. And young Peralta, the radio man, who wept quietly and steadily the whole time he was being loaded aboard like cargo, and who kept saying *thank you* to everyone including, at one point, the boat.

Imogen counted heads twice, then a third time, and looked at Felix. "We're overloaded."

"We're overloaded," Felix agreed. "That's why we're leaving before the water does the loading for us."

He took the wheel. The *Marta June* sat deep, her gunwales a hand's width above the current, and when Felix opened the throttle she answered with the reluctant grace of a fat woman asked to dance. Below them the gorge opened its throat — that slot of white water he'd winched up through a week ago, when the river had been a river. Now it was something else. The first breach had already been cut; he could see the work crew scrambling back from the upstream section of the jam, and he could see the lake finding the gap, pouring through in a brown rope as thick as a church, and the sound it made was not a sound so much as a fact.

"Teo," Felix said into the deck telephone. "Teo, are you clear of the water?"

A crackle. Then the boy's voice, breathless, thin over the wire. "Clear. We're on the high shelf. Ada says — she says *go now, Captain, go now.*"

Felix went.

* * *

There is a kind of piloting that is skill, and a kind that is prayer, and Felix had always believed the difference was that skill could be taught. What he did in the next forty minutes could not have been taught to anyone. He took the *Marta June* down a channel that was being born around him — banks sloughing, boulders the size of cottages rolling in the brown flood like apples in a barrel, standing waves rising and collapsing where no waves had any right to be. He read the water the way his father had taught him to read it before he'd ever resented the teaching: not as a chart, but as a sentence, and every eddy and boil a word in it, and the sentence said *here, fool, here, not there.*

"Port rock!" Imogen shouted, and he was already past it.

"Riffle line, two hundred yards, then it dog-legs left!" he shouted back, and heard her repeat it to Okonkwo at the bow soundings, and understood that they had become, in the space of an hour, a crew.

The middle breach let go while they were in the worst of it. Felix felt it through the hull before he heard it — a long shudder, as if the gorge itself had swallowed wrong — and then the river behind them stood up. He looked back once, which pilots are not supposed to do, and saw the flood coming down the slot in a moving hill, and did the arithmetic every riverman dreads: it was faster than the boat.

"Hard right!" he bellowed, and threw the wheel over, and took the *Marta June* into the one thing on the river slower than the flood — the old side channel, the dead slough his father's charts had marked with a single word in faded ink: *maybe.* It was choked with timber. It was half the width of the main channel. It was, at this moment, the only water in the gorge that wasn't trying to kill them quickly.

They went through it at a walk, the flood roaring past on their left close enough to wet the deck, the boat's hull grinding over drowned logs with a sound like teeth. Peralta had stopped weeping and started praying, which Felix took as a change for the better. Imogen stood at his shoulder and said nothing at all, which he took as the highest compliment of his life.

They came out of the slough a mile below the gorge mouth, and the river there was already rising, and Felix put the wheel down and *ran*.

* * *

He learned afterward — from Teo, later, in the telling and retelling that grief demands — how the third breach had gone.

The toe of the jam was the old part, the founder's work, timber so dense it had grown into a single woven mass, and the saws were barely marking it when the lake decided it had waited long enough. The jam began to fail all at once, from the middle, in the wrong order. Ada was on the toe section with the blasting charge — the valley's one stick of dynamite, saved for exactly this — and she had it placed and wired when the timber under her feet started to move.

Teo had gone out to her. Of course he had. He was a boy who had stowed away on a strange boat rather than stay safe, and he went out along the heaving logs to help her carry the charge clear, and he got halfway, and the log under him rolled.

Ada caught him. Felix's aunt — E. Whitlock's sister, the keeper of the valley, the woman who had spent seventy years holding a river with her bare hands — caught a boy by the collar with one hand and threw the charge's firing box to the far shore with the other, and the blast went off on schedule, and the toe of the jam opened, and the flood went where it was supposed to go, and Ada Whitlock did not.

The water took her the way the river takes everything it has earned: completely, and without malice. Teo said afterward that she was still holding his collar when the log rolled again, and that she let go — that she *chose* to let go — so that the roll of the timber would throw him shoreward instead of dragging him under. He said she was smiling. He said it in the flat, careful voice of a boy building a story strong enough to carry the rest of his life, and Felix had put his hand on the boy's head and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that the river hadn't already said.

The flood came down in three pulses instead of one wall. The valley flooded its low terraces and no more. The lower towns got a river running high and angry for two days, and lost a dock, and no lives. And the *Marta June*, running ahead of the last of it with her seams weeping and her engine screaming, came around the final bend below the gorge and saw Port Calloway's lights — and made landfall.

* * *

They tied up at midnight, in the rain, to a dock crowded with people who had been told a flood was coming and had come to see it and found instead a battered riverboat full of survivors and seed corn and one very quiet boy.

Felix stood on the deck after everyone had gone ashore, and took out the charts.

His father's charts. E. Whitlock's charts, the ones Ada had pressed into his hands at the valley — the gorge, the slough marked *maybe*, the headwaters, the settlement, everything the world outside would pay in blood and bulldozers to possess. He had carried them lashed under the pilothouse roof through the whole descent, and the spray had gotten at them anyway, and now he stood in the rain and unwrapped the oilcloth and looked at what the river had already begun to do.

The ink had run. The lines had bled into one another, the soundings dissolved, the careful hand of his father gone soft and ghostly, a map becoming a memory becoming weather. In another week it would be blank paper.

He found he was laughing. It came out of him sideways, surprising them both — him and the rain.

"You could dry them," Imogen said from the pilothouse door. She had come back aboard without him noticing. She was holding two cups of coffee and offering one, which was, he was beginning to understand, how she said the things other people needed paragraphs for. "Some of it might be legible."

"Most of it's already gone." He turned a page. The gorge was still there, barely — the slot, the slough, the *maybe*. "You know what's strange? I don't need it. I could run that river tomorrow. Every bend of it. It's not on the paper anymore." He tapped his own chest. "It's here."

Imogen looked at him for a long moment. "That's what Ada wanted, isn't it. Not the burning. The carrying."

Felix thought of her on the jam, one hand on a boy's collar, choosing. He thought of his father, who had sent his son away downriver to keep him out, and of the son who had spent five years running from a name and one week learning what it was for.

"Yeah," he said. "That's what she wanted."

He fed the charts into the pilothouse stove, page by page, and watched the gorge and the valley and the headwaters of the Sereno curl and go bright and go out, and the rain came down on the roof like applause, and the boy Teo appeared in the doorway behind Imogen, hollow-eyed and soaked, holding the brass compass in both hands like something that needed returning.

"Captain," Teo said. "The valley's people — the envoys — they're coming down in the spring. Ada's treaty. Someone has to show them the river." His voice cracked and held. "Someone who knows it."

Felix looked at the boy. He looked at the stove, where the last of his inheritance was becoming heat. He reached out and closed the boy's fingers back over the compass — *keep it, keep it, it was always going to be yours* — and heard himself say, clear and steady, for the first time in five years, without flinching, without the taste of ash:

"My name is Felix Whitlock. And in the spring, Teo, I'll take you home."
