## 10. Years, Not Miles

The river had changed its mind about autumn. Where the lower reaches usually ran brown and sullen by October, this year it ran clear and quick, carrying leaves instead of silt, as if the whole watershed had decided to make a good impression for company.

Felix felt the change in his hands before he saw it anywhere else. Five years of running this water had taught him to read it through the wheel, through the faint tremor of current against the hull, and today the *Marianne* — renamed, repainted, her old scars patched with new timber that still smelled of resin — moved through the channel like a woman walking into a room where she already knew everyone.

He was not alone at the rail, which was itself a novelty.

The envoys stood along the starboard side in their valley-made coats, wool dyed with lichen and madder, watching the lowland towns come up out of the morning fog one chimney at a time. There were four of them. Marta, who had been Ada's second and now spoke for the council; old Benno, whose grandfather had been a shipwrecked cartographer and who carried himself like a man perpetually correcting someone's latitudes; a young woman named Sef who had never seen a town larger than two hundred souls and was pretending very hard not to be overwhelmed; and Teo, who was not an envoy at all but had been granted passage on the grounds that he refused to be left behind, and that some arguments were not worth having twice.

"Is that Port Calloway?" Sef asked, pointing at a smudge of rooftops.

"That's the smoke of Port Calloway," Felix said. "The town's about ten minutes behind its own smoke. Always has been."

Benno chuckled. Marta did not smile, but her shoulders came down half an inch from around her ears, which from Marta was the equivalent of laughter.

They had been three days on the water, and Felix had learned their rhythms: Benno rose before dawn to take readings no one had asked him for; Sef catalogued everything, writing in a small waterproofed book until her fingers went blue; Marta watched the banks like a woman counting exits; and Teo —

Teo had appointed himself first mate, bosun, pilot's apprentice, and chief inspector of the galley, in roughly that order of self-importance. He stood at the wheel now, hands hovering near the spokes without quite touching them, the way Felix had once hovered near his own father's work without daring to touch it.

"Current's lazy here," Teo said, in the tone of a boy reciting scripture he'd only half memorized.

"Current's never lazy," Felix said. "It's just patient. There's a difference."

"What's the difference?"

"Patience means it'll wait for you. Lazy means it doesn't care if you show up." He nodded toward the bend ahead. "That eddy under the willow — what do you see?"

Teo squinted. "A place to tie off."

"A place to lose an hour and most of your dignity. Look again. See how the foam curls back on itself? That's the river telling you it's been around this bend more times than you have. Respect it, don't trust it."

The boy nodded solemnly and wrote something down. Felix suspected it was nonsense. He also suspected that in ten years it would be wisdom, and there was nothing anyone could do about that.

* * *

They made Port Calloway by late afternoon, and Felix braced himself for the dock the way you brace for a wave you know is coming.

There was a crowd. Not a large one — Calloway didn't waste crowds — but enough. Word had traveled downriver ahead of them for weeks: the treaty, the valley, the boat coming with the valley's own people aboard. A newspaperman from the coast had been loitering at the wharf since Tuesday, according to Imogen Reyes, who stood at the head of the gangplank with her survey case under her arm and an expression she probably thought looked neutral.

"You're late," she said.

"The river was being thorough."

"Uh-huh." She looked past him at the envoys, and something in her face softened — the thing that happened when Imogen forgot to be a professional. "Marta. We met at the talks. I'm glad it's you they sent."

"They nearly sent nobody," Marta said. "I argued that sending anyone was a risk. Then I argued that I should be the one to take the risk, so that if it went badly, I would be to blame." She shrugged. "I win most arguments that way. It is a terrible habit."

"It's a very effective one," Imogen said. "Come on. The commission's set up in the customs house, and I promise the chairs are uncomfortable and the tea is terrible, which is how you'll know the government is taking you seriously."

She led them off down the boardwalk, Sef's head turning like a compass needle finding north, south, east, and several directions that hadn't previously existed. Benno paused beside Felix at the rail.

"You're not coming?"

"I'll bring the bags."

"You'll stand here until the boat feels right again," Benno said, with the mild accuracy of old men. "Take your time. Rivers keep."

Felix stood there after they'd gone, because Benno was right, and because the dock itself required a moment.

This was the dock where he'd signed Imogen's charter fourteen months ago. This was the bollard he'd tied off to while telling himself it was just another contract, another run, another hundred miles between him and a name he'd spent five years setting down carefully and walking away from. He could almost see his younger self at the rail of the old *Marianne*, checking the manifest twice, refusing to look at the brass compass tucked in the paperwork like a hook waiting for a fish.

The compass was in his coat pocket now. It had been there all voyage, as it had been every day since the gorge, since the flood, since Ada.

He took it out. The brass had gone warm in his pocket, the way it always did, or the way he always imagined it did. The needle swung lazily and settled, pointing not north but northeast — upriver, up-valley, home, if he was honest about the word, and he was trying lately to be honest about words.

His father had carried this through the Gorge of Needles. His father had carried it into a valley and chosen to stay, chosen to send his son away with a lie rather than let the world find the door. And Ada had carried it after him, and hidden it in charter papers because she knew — she had known before Felix did — that the only person who would ever open that particular lock was the boy who'd grown up measuring his life in miles so he'd never have to measure it in anything else.

"Felix."

He turned. Teo stood at the foot of the gangplank, duffel over one shoulder, hair full of river wind, wearing the expression of a boy who had rehearsed a speech and intended to deliver it regardless of circumstances.

"You're supposed to be with Marta."

"I told her I had captain's business." He came up the plank. "Captain's business," he repeated, as though filing it officially.

"That's a serious charge. What is it?"

Teo set the duffel down. He dug in his jacket and produced a folded piece of paper, soft with handling, and held it out. Felix opened it. It was a drawing — the *Marianne*, rendered in the fierce confident strokes of a thirteen-year-old who had decided the boat was his, done from memory, every line right. In the corner, in careful letters: *F. WHITLOCK, MASTER.*

Felix looked at it for a long moment.

"You spelled Whitlock right," he said, and his voice did something he hadn't authorized.

"I practiced." Teo scuffed a boot against the planks. "Marta says the treaty needs pilots. River pilots, ones who know both ends of the water. She says I'm too young, and I said I'd be less young every year, and she said that was the first argument I'd ever won properly." He looked up. "So I'm staying. Downriver. Training. If — " and here the rehearsal ran out, and the real voice came through, the one from the night in the hold among the crates, scared and certain at once — "if you'll have me."

Felix crouched down to the boy's level, the compass still in his hand.

"When I was your age," he said, "my father gave me a rule. One rule. He said: know where you are. Not where you've been, not where you're going. Where you are. I thought it was about navigation." He turned the compass over in his palm. "Took me thirty years to find out it wasn't."

He held it out.

Teo stared at it. "That's the finder's mark. That's — that belongs to the valley. To your family."

"It belongs to whoever's asking where they are." Felix pressed it into the boy's hands and closed the small fingers around it. "And I know where I am, Teo. First time in my life I can say that and mean it. I'm on a dock in Port Calloway, my boat's tied off proper, my crew is ashore making the government drink bad tea, and my apprentice is standing in front of me holding my father's compass, spelling our name right." He straightened up. "Course you'll have me. You're stuck with me. That's how apprenticeship works — ask anyone."

Teo laughed, wet-eyed and furious about it, clutching the compass like it might bolt. "You said 'our name.'"

"I said what I said."

* * *

They walked up to the customs house together as the lamps were being lit, and the newspaperman fell into step beside them with the shameless ease of his profession.

"Captain Whitlock? Felix Whitlock? Of the *Marianne*? Any comment on the treaty? On the valley? They're saying your family founded it —"

Felix stopped. Teo stopped. Somewhere down the street, Sef could be heard discovering the existence of a bakery.

The old reflex rose on schedule — the flinch, the deflection, the mile-counting instinct that said *keep moving, give nothing, be no one.* Felix let it rise, examined it like a reading on a gauge, and found it reading empty.

"My name is Felix Almeida Whitlock," he said. "My father was Elias. My aunt was Ada, and she drew the treaty these people are carrying, and she was smarter than any of us. The valley's story is theirs to tell, and they'll tell it themselves, starting tomorrow, in that building behind you." He tipped his hat. "Me, I'm just the ferryman. But it's a good name. I'm done pretending otherwise."

The newspaperman wrote it all down at speed, then looked up, slightly disappointed. "That's it? No drama?"

"There was plenty of drama," Felix said, resuming his walk. "You missed it. It was upriver."

Inside, the customs house smelled of lamp oil and bad tea, exactly as promised. Marta stood at the head of a long table spread with the treaty — Ada's handwriting marching across the pages, steady and sure even now, posthumously winning arguments. Imogen sat nearby with her survey case closed for once, doing nothing official at all, just keeping company. Benno raised his cup to Felix. Sef waved a pastry.

Teo slid onto the bench beside Marta and set the brass compass on the table between them, where the lamplight caught it and threw a small bright circle onto the treaty paper — a little coin of light resting on the map of a future.

Marta looked at the compass, then at Felix, and something passed across her face that was grief and welcome sharing a chair.

"He would have liked this," she said quietly. "Elias. He hated maps that ended."

Felix sat down, and poured the terrible tea, and drank it anyway.

Outside, the Sereno went on talking to itself in the dark, patient, unhurried, going wherever it pleased — and for the first time in his life, Felix Whitlock was not counting the miles behind him.

He was counting years ahead.
