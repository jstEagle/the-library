## 1. The River Keeps What It Takes

The client's name was Herr Brandt, and he was afraid of the water.

Not in the way most people are afraid of water—that polite, theoretical fear that evaporates after the first hour and a cold beer. No. Herr Brandt was afraid of the Serran River the way some men are afraid of dogs: with a history behind it, a specific bite remembered. He'd told me about it on the drive to the put-in, unprompted, the way nervous people narrate their own terror like it might ward it off. A lake in Bavaria. A capsized dinghy. An uncle who'd gone under laughing, because that was the family way, apparently, to drown with good manners.

"So you see," he'd finished, "I am not a coward. I am a man of experience."

"Experience is just fear with receipts," I'd said, and he'd laughed too hard at that, which told me everything.

I run people down the Serran in the low season, when the tourist outfits upriver have pulled their rafts out of the water and their signs off the road, when the only clients left are the ones nobody else wants: the frightened, the obsessive, the men with midlife crises who book three-day floats as if a river could be a cure for being fifty-two. Low season pays badly and asks much. It suits me fine. I've never trusted work that comes easily; easy work means someone's watching, and I have spent six years making sure no one watches me.

On the river I'm called Mara Voss. It's not even a good alias—Voss is close enough to Reyes that anyone with half an ear for names would hear the echo—but aliases are like tents. They don't need to be beautiful. They need to hold through the night.

* * *

We pushed off from the gravel ramp below Kessel's Landing at seven in the morning, mist still standing in the willows like something waiting to be introduced. The Serran in October is supposed to be a modest river. Shy, even. The guidebooks—the few that bother—call it "a gentle float through pastoral hill country," which is the kind of sentence written by someone who has never been anywhere gentle in their life. But by late autumn the snowmelt is long gone, the tributaries are down to a trickle, and the Serran runs low and clear over its gravel beds, shallow enough in places that you can count the stones through two feet of green glass water.

Except this year it wasn't running low.

I noticed it before we were a hundred meters downstream, the way you notice a wrong note in a song you know by heart. The current had shoulders. It moved with a low-season confidence it had no business having, shouldering past the bend below the ramp where it usually dawdled, and when I dipped a hand in to check the temperature—habit, calibration, the small rituals that keep a guide honest—it came back colder than the air justified. Cold water in October means high country. High country water in volume means snowpack or reservoir release, and there hadn't been enough snow last winter to fill a teacup.

"You look concerned," Herr Brandt said. He was sitting very straight in the bow, both hands on the gunwale, wearing a life jacket cinched so tight it would have kept him afloat through a cannonball.

"I'm reading the river," I said. "It's what I'm for."

"And? What does it say?"

"That it's had more breakfast than usual." I let him chew on that while I took us through the first riffle, feathering the oars, keeping the boat angled so the chop hit us quarter-on instead of square. He made a sound like a kettle deciding whether to whistle. We came through clean. His hands unclenched one finger at a time.

Here's the thing about reading water: everyone thinks it's about danger, and it isn't, or not mostly. It's about accounting. A river is a ledger, and every stone, every eddy, every change in the color of the current is a line item. Water doesn't lie, but it does keep secrets, and it keeps them the way banks keep money—in plain sight, if you know which columns to read. This river was carrying money it shouldn't have had. Somewhere upstream, something was holding back less than it should, or letting go more.

I filed it where I file things that don't fit: in the back of my skull, in the drawer marked *not your problem*. That drawer is well organized. It has to be. My continued freedom depends on my ability to notice things and then, crucially, to leave them alone.

* * *

Herr Brandt turned out to be better company once he was tired enough to stop performing bravery. By midday he'd stopped gripping the boat and started actually looking at it—the herons stalking the shallows with their serial-killer patience, the limestone bluffs going amber in the slant light, the fish rising in the slow water below the second bend with little kissing sounds. He asked questions. Real ones. Where did the river come from, where did it go, why did the water run milky-green in the deep pools and clear as breath over the gravel.

"Upstream," I said, to the first question, which was also the answer to all of them, and which was as far upriver as I intended to point anyone.

"The maps show it goes into the Verrik Reserve," he said. He'd brought maps. Of course he had. Men like Brandt always bring maps; they want the world to be a document they can annotate. "Closed territory. Do you know why it's closed?"

"Because governments close things," I said. "It's how they prove they're governments."

He laughed, but he kept looking north, up the valley, where the hills stepped up in long green stairs toward a ridgeline that was already wearing its first snow like a collar. I didn't look. Looking north is a habit I gave up years ago, along with staying anywhere longer than a month and answering to my real name.

We camped that night on a gravel bar below an old ferry crossing, one of the dozens of rope-ferry sites that dot the Serran like scars—a reminder that this river used to be a border road, before the borders moved, before whatever happened twenty years ago happened and the maps got redrawn and redrawn again until nobody remembered what the ink was covering. There's a rusted cable anchor still bolted into the rock on the far bank. I set our fire well back from the water, pitched Brandt's tent with the door facing away from the current—he'd sleep better not hearing it—and cooked trout over alder coals because a fed client is a calm client.

He talked. Tired men talk. About his uncle, again, briefly, and then about his daughter, who wasn't speaking to him, and then about his firm, which was being bought out from under him by men whose names he pronounced with the particular disgust Germans reserve for financial innovation. I said mm in the right places. Guiding is ninety percent listening and ten percent rowing, and the listening part is easier if you genuinely don't care, which I don't, which is either a professional asset or a personal indictment depending on the day.

But somewhere in there, between his daughter and his firm, he asked me a question that slipped past my guard because it came dressed as small talk.

"How long have you worked this river?" he said. "You know it like a woman knows her own kitchen."

"Three seasons," I said, which was true, and which was also the longest I'd worked any water since the mountains, and hearing myself say it out loud did something strange to the inside of my chest, like a key turning in a lock I'd forgotten I owned.

Three seasons. Thirty-one months, if I'm counting, and I'm always counting. Before the Serran there was the Ilse, eight months, until a client recognized my face from a news photo and I was on a night bus before dawn. Before the Ilse, the coastal runs in Corvane, five months, until a licensing check went sideways and I learned that Corvane's registry office shares data with two countries that would very much like to ask me questions about a rockfall in the Karst Spires that killed eleven people. Eleven people and one reputation, though only mine was buried. Everyone else got a funeral.

Six years. Six years of thirty-month lives, of learning a town's coffee order and leaving before they learn yours, of renting rooms with cash and furnishing them with things you could carry in one trip, of knowing the exact weight of everything you own because you've weighed it against leaving. You get good at it. That's the trap nobody warns you about—you get *good* at impermanence. You develop a competence so complete, so frictionless, that it starts to feel like a personality. Look at me, pitching a tent in four minutes flat, reading water like scripture, charming frightened German businessmen into forgetting they're terrified. Look how capable I am. Look how nothing sticks.

It was full dark now, the fire down to a red heart, Brandt snoring in his tent with the trusting abandon of a man who believes the person outside it has his survival as a professional obligation. Which she does. Which is the only obligation I've managed to keep.

I sat by the fire and listened to the river, and the river sounded wrong.

Not dangerous-wrong. Not yet. Just—full. A river in low season should whisper over its bars; this one had a voice like a crowd murmuring in the next room. I walked down to the edge with my headlamp off and stood in the black and felt it move past my boots, steady and heavy and cold, and I thought about the ledger again. Snowmelt: negligible. Rain: none for nine days. Tributaries: dry. And yet the Serran ran like it was June.

Somewhere above the Verrik Reserve, something big was leaking, or releasing, or failing. A gate held too long and opened too fast. A dam operator's mistake. A schedule nobody published.

Or a schedule somebody published to exactly no one.

I stood there longer than I needed to, telling myself I was checking the water level for tomorrow's run, which was even true, and all the while the other thing sat in me like a swallowed stone: the old itch, the one I'd spent six years starving. Because here is what they don't tell you about running—they tell you it gets easier, and it does, the way calluses get easier, but underneath the callus the thing that made you run never heals. It just waits. And every so often the world sends you a sign, a wrong note, a river running high when it should run low, and the callus splits and underneath it you're still the same person who can't walk past a locked door without wondering what's inside.

I went back to the fire. I added wood I didn't need. I told myself the story I always tell: *you are Mara Voss, you run tourists down a pastoral river, your problems are leaks in a raft and clients who snore, and whatever is happening upstream is upstream, which is another word for not yours.*

The fire popped. Across the bar, the water kept talking in its crowded murmur, patient as debt.

In the morning we'd run the gorge section, and I'd want every centimeter of margin I could read. I checked the gauge stick I'd driven into the bank at camp—one of my rituals, a nail-and-notch system no licensing board ever taught me—and marked tonight's level against yesterday's.

The river had come up two fingers since noon.

Two fingers, in a dry October, in a land of empty tributaries. I looked at the mark for a long time. Then I did what I always do with things that don't fit.

I put them in the drawer.

But drawers, I would learn soon enough, are only rented. And the river, which keeps what it takes, had already decided what it was going to give back.
