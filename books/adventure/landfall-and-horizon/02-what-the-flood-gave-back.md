## 2. What the Flood Gave Back

The river lied to me all morning.

I'd been reading the Serran for six years under three different names, and I like to think I know its moods the way you know a lover's — which is to say I know when it's about to disappoint me, and this morning it was doing that thing where it runs smooth and glassy and pretends it has no opinions. A river that calm in late spring is either dying or thinking. The Serran was thinking.

"Is it always like this?" my client asked. Her name was Petra Voss — no relation, though she didn't know that, because nobody on this stretch of water knew anything real about me except that I took payment in cash and didn't make conversation before noon. She was a botanist from the lowlands, forty-ish, wearing brand-new waders and the expression of someone who had read about rivers in books.

"No," I said. "It's usually worse."

That got a laugh out of her, which was the point. Keep them laughing and they don't notice you checking the waterline every ninety seconds. There were four of us total: me, Petra, her graduate student Dev — a lanky kid who talked to the birds like they were colleagues — and old Halloran, whom I'd hired off the dock at Kessel's Landing mostly because he weighed enough to be ballast and knew enough to stay quiet. We had two boats, gear for five days, and a schedule that assumed the Serran would behave itself.

Rivers never behave themselves. That's not cynicism; that's the job description.

By midday the water had come up eight inches without a cloud in the sky anywhere above us. Eight inches doesn't sound like much unless you understand what it means: somewhere upstream, past the bend of the world where the maps go vague and the reserve fences start, something was letting go. Snowmelt, maybe. Or a sluice gate opening on a dam that wasn't supposed to exist. I filed that thought away with the others I didn't look at directly, and we made camp early on a gravel bar below the second gorge — high ground by local standards, which is the kind of phrase that gets people killed.

I set the tents back from the edge, staked everything twice, and told myself the unease in my chest was just indigestion from Halloran's cooking. Halloran cooked the way he did everything else: with too much salt and total confidence.

* * *

It came at two in the morning, the way these things do — no warning you could act on, only warnings you could have acted on if you'd been the sort of person who sleeps with one ear open, which I am, which is why I heard it first as a change in the dark.

Water makes a sound when it's rising fast. Not louder exactly. Fuller. Like the river had taken a breath and decided to hold it. I was out of my tent with my boots half-laced before my brain finished the sentence, and I got Petra up, then Dev, then Halloran, and we hauled the boats and what gear mattered up the bank while the bar behind us went from gravel to brown chaos in the time it takes to tell it.

The flood peaked an hour later. It didn't crest so much as arrive, like a guest who'd been expected all along. Our campsite was gone. The fire ring I'd built was gone. A log the size of a bus went through where we'd been sleeping, spinning slowly, dignified, like it had somewhere important to be.

We stood on the slope above the new waterline and watched the Serran carry our week away.

"Well," said Halloran, "that's the dry season started early."

Nobody laughed, but I appreciated the attempt. That's the thing about river people — grief comes out sideways, dressed as a joke, and everyone accepts the delivery because unwrapping it would take all night.

By dawn the water had dropped almost as fast as it rose, which told me what I already suspected: this wasn't weather. Weather lingers. This was a pulse, a single great exhale from somewhere upstream, and now the river was settling back into its lie of calm. We were stranded on the slope with one damaged boat, half our food, and a client who kept saying "incredible" in a voice that meant the opposite.

"We'll walk out along the survey track," I told her. "Two days to Kessel's Landing. I'll get your equipment replaced."

She looked at the wreckage of her expedition and then at me, and whatever she saw in my face made her nod. People trust competence even when they should ask questions. It's how I've stayed employed.

* * *

We broke camp and started downstream along the high bank, and that's when the river started giving things back.

A flash flood is a thief with bad impulse control — it takes everything and then dumps half of it wherever it happens to slow down, which is here, against this willow stand, in this eddy, like the river wanted us to see its pockets. Fence posts. A drowned ewe, bloated and apologetic. Half a rowboat painted the green that fishermen use on the Verrik side. A child's boot, which I looked at for exactly as long as it took to decide not to think about it.

Dev found the interesting debris first, because Dev found everything first; the boy had eyes like a hawk and the self-preservation instincts of a moth.

"Freya! Look at this."

Wedged in the roots of a fallen willow, half-buried in silt the flood had laid down like fresh snow, was a chest.

Not a crate. Not a drum. A chest — brass-bound, iron-strapped, the wood gone black with decades underwater but still sound, still square, still stubbornly itself. It sat in the root-crotch of the willow the way things sit when they've traveled a long way and finally been allowed to stop.

"Salvage," Halloran said, and spat. "River's full of it. Farmers lose chests in cellars all the time."

He was right, technically. But I've pulled a lot of things out of a lot of rivers, and I know the difference between something that fell in the water and something the water carried a long way on purpose. This had traveled. The brass was worn in patterns that spoke of current, not handling. And there was a seal stamped into the lid — a crest I didn't recognize, some kind of tower over crossed lines — pressed deep enough that whoever had made it wanted it to survive.

"Leave it," I said. "We're walking out light."

They left it. Of course they left it. But I marked the spot — a notch in the willow trunk, a stone balanced on a root — with the automatic precision of a woman who marks everything, and then I spent the next six hours pretending I hadn't.

* * *

Here's what you need to understand about me, since you're stuck with my side of this story: I do not pick up strange objects. Picking up strange objects is how people end up in stories, and I have spent six years arranging my life so that I am in no stories at all. No stories, no questions. No questions, no attention. Attention is the thing that kills you out here — not the rapids, not the cold, not the border patrols with their dogs. Attention.

So naturally, at midnight, with Petra and Dev asleep and Halloran snoring like a landslide in progress, I went back for it.

I told myself it was practical. Salvage value; I owed Petra a boat. I told myself I'd just look, confirm it was farmer's junk, and leave it for the next flood to finish burying. I told myself a lot of things while I walked a mile back upstream alone in the dark with a pry bar and a lantern turned down to a coal, and the nice thing about telling yourself things at midnight is that nobody checks your sources.

The chest came free of the roots easier than it should have, like it had been waiting. I dragged it up onto the shingle, knelt over it, and worked the pry bar under the lid. The lock was long gone — rusted to lace, or opened by someone before me — and the lid came up with a groan of swollen wood and a smell that hit me like a hand across the face.

Wet paper. Old ink. And underneath both, faint as a ghost walking over your grave: machine oil and concrete dust. The smell of engineering. The smell of my childhood, if I'm being honest, which I try not to be.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth that had actually done its job, were documents.

Maps first. Survey maps, beautiful ones, the kind drawn by hands that loved their work — contour lines fine as hair, elevations noted to the centimeter, and across the top of each sheet, in a formal engraved script: **VESIA.** Rivers I knew, renamed. Towns I'd guided clients past, given other names. Borders that didn't match any border on any map I owned. I knew every cartographic authority on this continent would tell me this country didn't exist. I also knew, looking at the density of detail, that no one invents a country this thoroughly. You don't fabricate forty years of rainfall records for a place you made up.

Under the maps, sealed orders — actual wax, actual seals, the tower-and-lines crest pressed into blood-red wax — and dispatches in a bureaucratic dialect I half-recognized, full of phrases like *resettlement complete* and *hydrological assets consolidated* and *the question of remaining witnesses*, which last one I read three times and felt in my teeth.

And at the bottom, wrapped separately, as if someone had wanted it protected most of all:

A photograph.

Black and white, water-stained at one corner, but clear. A concrete face of a dam filling the frame, vast and brutal and new, scaffolding still clinging to its flanks. And standing before it, third from the left in a line of engineers, arms crossed, squinting into a sun twenty years dead —

My mother.

I want to tell you I reacted well. I want to say I sat back, breathed, applied the discipline that has kept me alive and anonymous for six years. What I actually did was hold the photograph so hard the edges cut my thumbs, kneel on a gravel bar in the dark, and shake.

Because I knew that squint. I knew those crossed arms. I'd inherited the posture and the squint and, God help me, apparently the habit of standing in front of large dangerous things looking like I own them. My mother, Elena Reyes, who died — officially — eleven years ago in an avalanche I have never been allowed to discuss. Who had no connection, official or otherwise, to any country called Vessia, because no such country existed, because I had checked, once, years ago, carefully, the way you check for a thing you hope isn't there.

The dam behind her wasn't in the maps' index. It wasn't labeled. But the maps were of Vessia, and the dam was enormous, and dams like that don't get built by ghosts.

I sat there until the lantern burned low, going through the papers again and again, building the shape of something I refused to name. A country erased. A dam unrecorded. A photograph of a woman the record insisted had never stood there. And threaded through the dispatches, recurring like a bass note, that phrase — *remaining witnesses* — in contexts that made my skin crawl, because the documents treated witnesses the way engineers treat obstacles. Something to be surveyed. Something to be managed.

Something to be flooded.

The sky was going grey at the edges when I finally rewrapped everything, closed the lid, and carried the chest back downriver to camp, hiding it under the willow brush where I could find it again. I told myself I'd decide what to do in daylight. Daylight is when sensible people make decisions.

Daylight came, and I made no decision at all. I just kept seeing the photograph. Kept seeing her squint. Kept hearing the river all around us, running high for the season, running off something upstream, off some structure, off some gate that somebody, somewhere, still opened and closed.

"What's got into you?" Halloran asked, watching me stare at the water instead of reading it.

"Nothing," I said. "Current's odd today."

It was the truest thing I'd said all morning. The current was odd. Everything was odd. Somewhere upstream, a country that didn't exist had sent me its paperwork, and I had signed for it, and there was no return address except the source of the river itself.

Petra wanted to talk about replacement equipment. Dev wanted to talk about the flood dynamics — the boy had filled a notebook with rise rates, bless him. I nodded at both of them and heard none of it, because part of me had already left, already walked upstream past the gorges, past the reserve fence, past the edge of every map I trusted, following the water home to whatever waited where it was born.

Two days to Kessel's Landing, I'd promised. Fine. Two days. I could be patient for two days.

But I caught myself, that evening, checking the chest's seal against the wax stubs in the dispatches, matching impressions like a forger, learning the crest by heart. Tower over crossed lines. I drew it once in the dirt with a stick, studied it, and scrubbed it out with my boot.

Some things you memorize without meaning to. Some things memorize *you*.
