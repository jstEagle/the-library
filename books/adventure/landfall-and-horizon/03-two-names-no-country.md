## 3. Two Names, No Country

Kessel's Landing smelled of diesel, wet rope, and the particular despair of a town that existed only because two countries couldn't agree on whose problem it was. I'd passed through it maybe a dozen times over the years, enough to know which dockside café watered the beer and which one watered the coffee, and never once had I thought of it as a place where a person might learn anything. Border towns are for forgetting. That's what I'd used them for, anyway.

Now I needed it to remember.

The chest sat in the back of my rented room above the ferry office, wrapped in a tarp, and I'd spent two days telling myself I wasn't going to open it again. Two days of hauling paying clients down the low, sluggish Serran, pointing at herons, making the jokes that made the nervous ones less nervous. Two days of coming back at dusk, washing the river off my hands, and looking at the tarp like it might look back.

On the third morning I gave up pretending and went looking for the town's library instead, because I am, at bottom, a person who believes problems can be solved with research, and it has taken me this long in life to learn how wrong I am.

* * *

The Kessel's Landing municipal library occupied the ground floor of a building that had been, according to the plaque, a customs house, a granary, a customs house again, and finally a library, which told you everything about the town's ambitions. Inside it was cool and dim and smelled of paper going gently to dust, and the librarian was a woman of about sixty with reading glasses on a chain and the posture of someone who had spent forty years watching people come in and lie to her.

"Help you?" she asked, in the tone of someone who already knew the answer would be disappointing.

"I'm looking for regional histories," I said. "The Serran headwaters. The Verrik Reserve. Anything on the countries upriver."

"Verrik Reserve is closed territory. There's nothing to research." She said it the way you'd say *the moon is not available for rent*.

"Historically, though. Before it was closed. What was up there?"

The glasses came off. She looked at me over them, and I watched her do the arithmetic people in border towns do—accent, boots, the particular way I held my shoulders like a woman who expected to be told to leave. "What was up there," she repeated.

"Twenty years ago. Thirty. I'm a guide. I like knowing the ground I walk on."

That was true, which helped. Liars fidget with the truth; I've found the best cover story is mostly true with one load-bearing lie in the middle. The load-bearing lie was that I was merely curious.

"Local history is shelf four," she said finally. "Colonial period, shelf six. Anything else is in the closed stacks, and the closed stacks are closed."

I thanked her and went to shelf four, and shelf four told me a great deal by telling me nothing.

Here is what I mean. The regional histories were all there—fat, earnest volumes about the Serran valley, the timber trade, the great floods of the previous century, the founding of every town from the delta to the gorges. And every single one of them had a hole in it. Not a literal hole, though some had that too, pages razored out with a patience I found more frightening than any fire. A hole in the text. You'd be reading about river commerce in the upper valley, and the narrative would step delicately around a stretch of years, the way a tongue finds the gap where a tooth used to be. *The northern territories, prior to their administrative reorganization.* *The communities formerly of the upper basin, whose resettlement was completed in the spring of—* and then nothing, a blank, a footnote referencing a decree number in an archive that, I was willing to bet, did not have a public reading room.

One book had a map. The map showed the Serran's headwaters as a dotted line trailing off into a shaded region labeled, in the blandest possible typeface, TERRITORY UNDER RESERVE ADMINISTRATION. No towns. No names. The river simply stopped existing, the way a sentence stops when the censor's pen comes down.

I stood there in the dust-smelling quiet and felt the hair rise on my arms, because I had grown up on rivers, and rivers do not stop. Rivers are the one thing in this world that never agreed to be erased. Somebody had gone to a great deal of trouble to make the paper agree anyway.

"Shelf six," said a voice at my elbow, "has the same problem, if you were wondering."

The librarian had appeared beside me without making a sound, which is a professional skill I respected immediately. She was holding a reshelving cart she clearly had no intention of using.

"You've noticed," I said.

"I've worked here nineteen years. I've watched men come in with razors and officially sanctioned good manners and thin out the collection. I've watched them do it twice." She glanced toward the door, then at me, and something in her face shifted from *librarian* to something older and more tired. "You're asking about the upper basin. Why?"

"A chest washed down the river after the flood," I said, and decided, in the space of half a breath, to give her a third of the truth. "It had survey maps in it. Sealed orders. A country called Vessia."

The word landed in her like a stone in still water. She didn't move, but everything in her went very still, the way prey animals go still, and I thought: *she knows that word. She's known it a long time, and she's paid for knowing it.*

"You should take your books," she said quietly, "and your curiosity, and get on a boat."

"I haven't taken any books yet."

"You misunderstand me." She stepped closer. Her voice dropped to almost nothing. "In this town, asking questions about Vessia is how people end up on a barge south. Not arrested—there's no charge, no paper, nothing so useful. They're just *relocated*. The ferry office has a list. I've seen the list. I've had friends on it." She straightened, resumed her librarian posture like a coat. "Shelf four. Take what you like. Return it within two weeks."

Then she walked back to her desk and became a woman with reading glasses and no opinions, and I understood that I had just been given a warning by someone who could not afford to give it, and that this was the closest thing to kindness Kessel's Landing was going to offer me.

* * *

I spent the afternoon doing what the librarian's warning told me not to do, but carefully, the way you cross a rotten bridge—one question at a time, testing each plank.

The chest itself was the best archive I had. I'd gone through it twice more in my room the night before, and this time I'd been systematic instead of shaken. Four sealed orders, each bearing the same wax stamp: a mountain over water, and around the rim, in a script I didn't recognize, a word I now knew was *Vessia*. The survey maps—dozens of them, linen-backed, beautifully drafted—covered the upper Serran basin in obsessive detail. Every gorge, every tributary, every village, every elevation. A country rendered in ink so lovingly it read like a love letter.

And on the dam. On the photograph of my mother, twenty years younger, standing in front of a concrete face taller than anything I'd ever guided a client past, her arms crossed, her mouth set in the expression I remembered from every argument we'd ever had: *I am right, and you will understand eventually.*

The orders were in three languages. Two I could half-read. The third I couldn't read at all, but the stamp was the same, and stamps are a language of their own: *this comes from the center. This comes from the people who decide.*

So I did what a person with no archive does. I went where the river people drink.

* * *

The Ferryman's Rest was the dockside café that watered the beer, which meant it was the one where the working boats drank, which meant it was the one where information moved fastest and was worth the least, which suited me fine. I bought rounds the way you buy them in river towns—slowly, for other people, without asking anything—and I let the evening do the work.

By the third round I'd learned that the flood two weeks back had been the worst in a decade, that the Verrik Reserve authority had closed the upper gorges early this year, that the salvage crews working the debris lines downstream were "not from anywhere," which is river talk for men with too-new equipment and no local accent. And by the fifth round, an old deckhand with hands like braided rope leaned in close enough that I could smell forty years of diesel on him, and said:

"You're the one been asking about the old country."

I kept my face pleasant and stupid. "Old country?"

"Vessia." He said it the way you'd say a dead friend's name—carefully, so as not to wake anything. "There were four of those chests. My cousin worked the salvage run the year the reservoir filled. He said the consortium men came upriver in their clean boats and collected three of the four, and the fourth went into the water, and everyone agreed the water had it now." He turned his glass. "Water gives things back sometimes. That's the trouble with water."

"Collected," I said. "Collected by who?"

"Men whose names aren't their names." He drained the glass. "Collectors, they call them. There's a trade in what got erased. Maps, seals, photographs. Rich men in the capital pay for it, and the consortium pays more for it to disappear." He looked at me then, really looked, and I saw that the drunkenness was a coat too, like the librarian's posture, like my own easy smile. "The chest you're asking about. It washed up on your line, didn't it. I watched you pull something out of that willow snag."

The room had not gotten quieter, but it felt like it had. I was suddenly aware of the door, the two men at the corner table who had come in an hour ago and ordered nothing but coffee and had not drunk it, the particular silence of a room where a conversation had been noted.

"Careful," the deckhand said, almost kindly. "You've got the look of a woman who's been hiding a long time. Hiding's a skill. But you've started *looking* for something, and looking is different. Looking leaves a wake."

I paid my tab and left a tip large enough to buy his silence for a week, which in a border town buys about a day, and walked back to my room in the dark with my shoulders up around my ears.

* * *

Here is the thing about being hidden versus being hunted, and I am an expert on the difference, having practiced one for years and now, apparently, the other.

When you're hidden, the world is a set of habits. You move every month. You use the name that fits the region. You never guide the same client twice, never sleep in the same bed a week running, never tell anyone where you wintered. It's exhausting and lonely, but it's *quiet*, and quiet is a currency you can live on.

When you're hunted, the same habits become a trail. Every person I'd bought a round from, every question I'd asked on shelf four, the librarian's careful warning, the chest sitting under a tarp above a ferry office in a town where the ferry office keeps lists—all of it was a wake behind me now, spreading, readable. I had spent two days in Kessel's Landing being curious in a town where curiosity was a deportable offense, and somewhere in those two days, the shape of my questions had stopped being mine and started being a map for someone else.

I sat on the edge of my bed with the tarp pulled back and the chest open, and I looked at my mother's face on the photograph, and I said out loud, to the empty room, "What did you do?"

She didn't answer. Mothers never do when you finally want them to.

But the chest answered, in its way. I went through the sealed orders again, slower, and this time I noticed what I'd been too rattled to see on the gravel bar: one of the four orders was different. The others were stamped and countersigned, bureaucratic and final. This one had been opened and resealed—badly, in haste, the wax smeared by a thumb—and inside, folded small, was a single sheet in my mother's handwriting.

I knew her handwriting the way you know a voice on the phone after ten years. The hard downstrokes. The way she crossed her t's like she was cutting something.

It was a list. Names—nine of them, Vessian names—and beside each, in her tight hand, either the word *safe* or the word *gone*. At the bottom, underlined twice: *If they find the chests, they find the witnesses. Burn this before it burns us.*

She had hidden it instead. In a chest she'd sent into the river, into the one place she thought the consortium's clean boats couldn't follow.

My hands were not steady. I put the paper down on the bed and stood at the window and looked at the black water of the Serran sliding past the docks, carrying everything it takes toward the delta, and I understood two things with total clarity.

The first was that my mother had not been a victim of the lie. She had been one of its builders, and then, at the end, one of the only people trying to unbuild it—and the mountain disaster, my disaster, my blacklist, my ten years of false names, was very possibly the sound of that unbuilding being crushed.

The second was that there were collectors in this town, or coming to it, and I had left a wake wide enough to land a plane on.

I packed in eleven minutes. I'd had practice. The chest's documents went into a waterproof roll bag; the chest itself I wrapped and weighted and sank off the end of the dock at three in the morning, which hurt more than I expected—twenty years underwater hadn't hurt it, but my own hands sent it down, and the river closed over it without comment. The water keeps what it takes. Let it keep the brass and the wood. I'd keep the truth.

I had a boat. I had a river. I had a name that wasn't mine and a mother's handwriting in my pack and, somewhere between the library and the dock, the growing certainty that the men in clean boots already knew which room I'd slept in.

What I didn't have was a plan past the next bend of the river, and I was standing on the dock working out how much fuel I could buy before dawn when I heard footsteps on the planks behind me—unhurried, unpracticed, the footsteps of someone who had never once had to move quietly in his life.

"You're Freya Reyes," said a young man's voice, too loud for three in the morning, and I swear to God I nearly went into the river on purpose.
