## 4. The Archivist Who Wouldn't Leave

There are three ways to be exiled from the capital, and I have catalogued all of them, because cataloguing is what I do. The first is scandal, which is quick and loud. The second is failure, which is slow and quiet. The third—the one reserved for people like me—is *correctness*. You are exiled not for being wrong but for being right in the wrong direction, at the wrong volume, in front of the wrong people.

My name is Tomas Ilic. I was a junior archivist in the Hall of Records, third floor, west wing, the section where documents go to be forgotten on purpose. I spent four years there alphabetizing the state's amnesia, and then one afternoon I pulled a box marked *Hydrographic Surveys, Obsolete, 19–* and found forty pages of maps of a country that did not exist.

Not blank maps. Not mislabeled maps. Maps of Vessia, with its towns named and its rivers surveyed and its capital marked in a neat clerk's hand, and stamped across all of it in red: WITHDRAWN FROM CIRCULATION. Somebody had gone to enormous trouble to unmake a place, and the trouble itself was the evidence. You don't stamp a mistake. You stamp a decision.

I did what any archivist would do. I made copies. I asked questions. I asked the questions *out loud*, which was my real crime, because the Hall of Records can survive anything except an employee who reads what he files.

The transfer to the provincial depot in Kessel's Landing came with a promotion in title and a demotion in everything else. "Regional conservation duties," my director called it, shaking my hand with the warmth of a man closing a door. Kessel's Landing is a border town built on the Serran's muddy shoulders, all salt rot and river money, where the customs officers are the most honest people because everyone else has a side business. I arrived in the rain with two crates of books and a conviction I could not yet articulate, and I settled in to be forgotten.

I had planned to be patient. I want that on the record. I had a whole strategy of patience, a slow, dignified, archival patience, and I abandoned it in eleven days.

* * *

The trouble started, as trouble does, with a rumor that arrived before its subject. Boatmen talk in this town the way water talks—constantly, and about nothing, until suddenly it's about something. On my ninth evening, drinking the cheap amber brandy the landing calls coffee, I heard two deckhands arguing about a chest.

"A brass-bound chest," the older one said, "fished out of the flood debris up past the bend. Sealed with a crest nobody knew. Old Doss swears it was a double-headed eagle."

"Old Doss swears the river fish sing," said the younger.

"The river *does* sing, you ignorant pup, and I'm telling you there was a crest, and I'm telling you the ferry-woman took it."

"Ferry-woman?"

"The one working the low-season run. The quiet one. Reyes, she goes by."

I put down my cup with a care that fooled no one. "This chest," I said. "What happened to the rest of the debris?"

The older deckhand looked at me the way river people look at men with soft hands and hard questions. "What's it to you?"

"Nothing," I said. "Professional interest. I conserve things."

"Conserve your own business," he said, and they left, and I sat there doing the arithmetic that would ruin my life. A crest nobody recognized. A flood that gave something back. And a ferry-woman named Reyes working a run so small it barely qualified as employment, in a town where nobody stayed longer than a season.

I had spent two years assembling the shape of the erasure from the edges inward—the withdrawn maps, the shipping manifests that rerouted around a stretch of river for no stated reason, the census volumes with pages razored out. I knew what the consortium had done to the country. What I had never found was an artifact. Paper burns; paper is *made* to burn. But a brass-bound chest that spent twenty years in the water and came out the far side of a flood—that was the kind of thing that survives its own history. That was the kind of thing you could hold.

The next morning I went down to the ferry landing and made the first of what would become a series of mistakes.

* * *

She was repairing a rope when I found her, sitting on an overturned skiff with the rope across her knees, working a marlinspike through a frayed section with the unhurried violence of someone who has done ten thousand harder things before breakfast. She was maybe thirty, sun-browned, with a scar through one eyebrow and the kind of stillness that animals and soldiers have—the stillness of a person who notices everything and admits to nothing.

"Ms. Reyes?" I said. "Tomas Ilic. I'm an archivist. I wondered if I might ask you about—"

"No," she said, without looking up.

"You don't know what I was going to ask."

"You were going to ask about the chest. Everyone who says *I wondered if I might ask* is asking about the chest." She cut a strand and let it fall. "There is no chest. Good morning."

"It's the afternoon."

"Then good afternoon. There is still no chest."

I want to be clear about what happened next, because in my memory it plays as a single continuous act of stupidity. I said, "The Vessian crest was a double-headed eagle above a weir, and the chest would have been one of four commissioned by the Office of Public Works in the last year of the survey, and if you have it, you are sitting on the only physical record of a nation that forty million documents have failed to erase."

The marlinspike stopped moving.

She looked up at me then, and I saw something I did not expect, which was fear—not the fear of a guilty person but the fear of a person doing rapid calculations about exits. And beneath the fear, in the half-second before she buried it, something else. Something that made my stomach drop, because I had seen that face before. Not hers. An older version of it. In a photograph, in a personnel file I had photographed with a borrowed camera at two in the morning, in the file of a senior hydraulic engineer named Ilse Reyes-Vess who had resigned from the consortium's river division twenty years ago and vanished from every subsequent record.

The eyes. The set of the jaw. The way she looked at me like she was deciding, right now, whether I was a problem or a nuisance, and how much each would cost to solve.

"Who told you that word?" she said quietly. "Vessia."

"A withdrawn map," I said. "Forty pages of them. And a file on an engineer who helped survey a dam that officially never existed, whose daughter would be—" I did the arithmetic. "About your age."

The rope came off her knees. She stood, and I discovered that she was taller than me, which I feel should be noted for the record, and that she had the particular calm of a person who has decided not to throw me into the river only because the river deserved better.

"You're going to walk away," she said, "and you're going to keep walking, and you're not going to come back to this landing, and you're not going to say that word again in this town. Because the people you'd be asking questions for—" she stopped herself, recalibrated. "The people who *don't* want that word said, they don't send archivists. They send men with clean boots. Do you understand me?"

"I understand that you recognized the crest description instantly," I said, "which means you know what the chest is, which means—"

"I know what it *isn't*. It isn't yours." She shouldered her coil of rope. "Go home, archivist."

"Kessel's Landing is my home," I said, which was true in the way a prison cell is a home, and she gave me a look that said she'd caught the truth of it anyway. That was the thing about her I would learn over the following days: she missed nothing. She just declined to comment on most of it.

She walked off toward the boatyard, and I stood there among the fish scales and tar, feeling like a man who has knocked on a door and had it answered by a drawn bow. Any reasonable person would have stopped.

I have never been accused of being any reasonable person.

* * *

I want to explain the stubbornness, because it wasn't courage. Courage implies a choice between fear and action. What I had was simpler and less flattering: I had spent four years in a basement being told that the most interesting thing in the world was none of my business, and I had swallowed it, and swallowed it, and here at last was a woman with her mother's eyes standing in front of the one object that could turn my forty pages of withdrawn maps into a story with a pulse. I was not brave. I was hungry. It only looks like courage from the outside.

So I did what archivists do. I took notes. For three days I documented Ms. Reyes's movements with a thoroughness that I recognize, in retrospect, reads exactly like surveillance, which explains—and does not excuse—what happened on the third day, when she caught me.

I was at the harbor master's office, reading the flood reports, when I felt her behind me. I turned and she was leaning against the doorframe with her arms crossed, and she did not look angry. She looked tired, in a way that went all the way down, and somehow that was worse.

"You're following me."

"I'm researching the flood," I said. "The flood happens to involve you."

"The flood happens to involve half the river. You're following *me*." She came in and shut the door, and the harbor master, who owed her a favor or feared her, found urgent business elsewhere. "Sit down, Ilic."

I sat. It seemed wise.

"Here's what's going to happen," she said. "You're going to tell me exactly who sent you, exactly what you know, and exactly who else knows it. And if I believe you, I'll decide whether you live in this town or leave it. Those are the terms. I've had men with clean boots ask me about that chest already—no, don't interrupt, I know you were going to ask—and I am not going to be hunted by a consortium and a librarian at the same time. So. Talk."

And here is the thing I have turned over a hundred times since: I told her the truth. All of it. The withdrawn maps, the razored censuses, the personnel file, the two years of edges-without-a-center, the exile dressed up as a promotion. I told her about the director's handshake and the third-floor basement and the way the Hall of Records smells of dust and deliberate forgetting. I told her I had no one behind me, no institution, no protection, nothing but a borrowed camera's worth of photographs and a conviction I couldn't prove.

She listened with her whole body, the way river people listen—still, weight forward, reading you the way they read water. And when I finished, she was quiet for a long moment, and then she said the sentence that I suspect she had been building toward since the first afternoon at the landing.

"You're not working for them."

"No."

"You're just a man who can't leave a locked drawer alone."

"That is," I said, "an unkind but accurate summary."

She almost smiled. It was a narrow thing, gone as fast as it came, but I logged it. I log everything; it's a disease. "My mother used to say the archive was where a country kept its conscience," she said, to the window, not to me. "She said you could burn every map and the paper would still remember. I thought she was being poetic." She turned back. "She was being technical."

"Your mother was Ilse Reyes-Vess."

Something moved behind her face, a door opening onto a room with the lights off. "You said that name like you've read her file."

"I've read every file that touches Vessia. Hers touches all of them. She was the senior survey engineer on the Verrik project. She resigned eleven days before the reservoir authorization was signed. After that, nothing. No departure record, no death record, no relocation. A person doesn't vanish from paper by accident, Ms. Reyes. Vanishing from paper is a *service*. Someone provides it."

"I know someone provides it," she said, and her voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of a woman standing at the edge of her own history and checking the ice. "That's the problem."

* * *

We argued for two more days, and I use *argued* generously, because an argument requires two participants with roughly equal footing, and she held most of the footing. She told me to leave. I explained, with citations, why I wouldn't. She threatened me. I offered to be threatened somewhere useful. She pointed out, correctly, that my presence doubled her risk, that a junior archivist asking questions in Kessel's Landing was a lit lantern in a dry field, that the men with clean boots would find me and then find her, and that everything I touched turned into evidence against the people who'd been kind enough to shelter her.

All true. I conceded every point and changed nothing, and by the fourth day she had stopped telling me to leave and started telling me to be quieter, which is how surrender sounds when it comes from a proud person.

It was on the fourth evening, on the landing with the lamps coming on, that she finally asked the question underneath all the others.

"Why?" she said. "You've lost your career over this. You'll lose whatever's left of it. Nobody reinstates a man who reads the withdrawn boxes. So why are you still here, Tomas Ilic, standing on a cold dock arguing with a woman who's told you five times to go away?"

I thought about lying, and then I thought about how she'd know, and I gave her the true thing, which was this:

"Because when I was nine years old, my grandmother told me about a town on the Serran where she'd bought her wedding linen. She described the street. She described the church with the green door. And when I grew up and went looking, there was no town and no street and no church, and everyone told me she'd been confused, that her memory was soft, that old women invent places. And I believed them for twenty years." I looked at the black water. "Then I pulled a box of withdrawn maps and found her town, named, surveyed, stamped in red. She wasn't confused. She was *right*, and the whole world was organized to make her wrong. Somebody has to be on the side of the woman who was right. That's the whole of it. That's my grand motive. A green door and my grandmother's good name."

Freya Reyes looked at me for a long time in the lamplight. The river worked at the pilings underneath us, patient, keeping its accounts.

"My mother had a coat," she said finally. "Grey wool. She wore it the day she left, and she never came back for it, and I kept it for years like it was going to explain something." She pulled the rope off the bollard and coiled it with quick, angry competence. "You're a liability, Ilic. You're loud, you're obvious, and you ask questions like a man who's never had to run. But you're the first person in two years who's asked the right ones, and I'm tired of being the only one who knows the questions exist." She thrust a finger at me. "Rules. You don't speak to anyone about the chest. You don't write anything down that you can't eat. And when I say run, you run, and you don't ask which direction, because I'll already know."

"That's three rules and an insult."

"The insult's free. The rules cost you." She slung the coil over her shoulder and paused, and for a moment the lamplight caught her face and I saw it again, the older ghost of it, the engineer's face from the file, looking out through her daughter's eyes at a stranger who had just made himself her problem. "Come to the boatyard at dawn. If you're going to be a liability, you can at least be a useful one. There's a name on the back of a photograph you need to see, and after that—" she hesitated, and I watched her decide something she'd been resisting for days. "After that, we need to talk about who else is looking for these chests. Because the men with clean boots found me yesterday, and they knew my real name, and that means we're leaving this town tonight whether you're ready or not."

"Tonight," I said. "It's— I have books. I have two crates of—"

"Leave the crates."

"You don't leave *books*," I said, with genuine horror, and she looked at me the way you look at a dog that has just volunteered to swim a flood channel, half despair and half something warmer, and said:

"Dawn, Ilic. Bring nothing you can't carry. And bring the photographs."

I stood on the dock a while after she'd gone, watching the lamps of Kessel's Landing blur in the river fog, and I understood that I had just crossed a line that does not appear on any map I've ever catalogued. Tomorrow I would be a fugitive. Tomorrow the crates would stay, and the depot would find my empty room, and somewhere in the capital a director would shake his head over my file.

But tonight, for the first time in two years, the withdrawn maps had a pulse, and the woman with her mother's eyes had said *we*, and I found that I was not afraid.

I was, I realized, taking notes on my own life now. And the next entry was going to be written in a boat, at night, running from men with clean boots.

I went home to pack nothing.
