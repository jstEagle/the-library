## 5. Men in Clean Boots

The trouble with clean boots is that they announce themselves before the man inside them does.

I noticed them from across the landing at Kessel's Landing—two pairs of them, standing on the wet planks of the ferry dock like they'd been set there by a stagehand. Everything else in Kessel's Landing was mud, fish scale, rope-end, and river rot. These boots were oiled leather, unscuffed, laced with the kind of precision that suggested the wearer had never once had to kick something loose or wade through anything worse than a puddle he chose.

Their owners matched the boots. Two men in their forties, jackets too new for the town, collars pressed, hands soft around tin cups of coffee they weren't drinking. They stood at the rail of the dock looking out at the Serran the way accountants look at ledgers—not admiring it, appraising it.

I was mending a net I didn't need to mend, because mending things is what people here expected of me, and because my hands needed something to do that wasn't shaking.

Three days since the chest. Three days since I'd pried it open by lantern light in the reed shed behind Marla's boarding house and seen my mother's face staring back at me from a photograph taken in front of a dam that did not exist. Three days of Tomas Ilic orbiting me like an anxious moon, asking questions I mostly refused to answer, and three days of me telling myself that nobody knew. That the flood gave the chest to *me*, specifically, out of all the rivers in the world, and no one else had to know about it.

Then two men in clean boots showed up on my dock, and the universe stopped pretending to be subtle.

* * *

They came to me at midday, when the light was flat and the tourists were gone. The taller one did the talking. He had a face built for trustworthiness—the kind of square-jawed, gray-templed arrangement that casting directors use for senators and fathers—and a smile calibrated to arrive exactly half a second after his words did.

"Ms. Voss."

I kept my eyes on the net. "Wrong name. It's Renner. Mara Renner."

"Of course it is." He said it gently, almost kindly, the way you'd correct a child who insisted the dog ate her homework. "Mara Renner, licensed guide, formerly of the Corvane high routes. Before that, the Serrat range. Before that—" he consulted a small notebook he didn't seem to need, "—well. Before that is why we're here."

I set down the net. My hands were steady now, which surprised me. Fear, it turns out, comes in waves, and between the waves there's a strange clear water where you can see everything.

"You've got the wrong woman," I said.

"I have exactly the right woman." He extended a hand. "Dorin Hale. Salvage and recovery, Meridian Contracting. This is my associate, Pell." The shorter one nodded. His boots were even cleaner. "We're working the flood debris downstream. Recovering property for insurers. You pulled a chest out of the willows above the gravel bar last week. We'd very much like to discuss it."

My heart did something complicated against my ribs. Not just fear—there was a flash of fury in there too, hot and stupid, because I'd carried that chest four miles through the dark thinking I was the only person in the world who knew it existed, and apparently I'd been carrying it through a crowd.

"Flood debris," I said. "There were forty chests' worth of junk in those willows. Farm tools, dead sheep, somebody's piano."

"A brass-bound chest, iron strapping, seal-stamped lid," Hale said. "Approximately this tall." He held his hand at the height. "We understand it's in your possession."

"We?"

"My firm. Our clients." The smile again, right on schedule. "Look, Ms. Voss—I can call you Ms. Voss, or I can call you Renner, whichever keeps this conversation comfortable. What's in the chest is archival material. Paper. Old paper, at that. Worthless to anyone but collectors and institutions, and our clients are collectors. They're prepared to offer you a substantial sum for its return. No questions, no records, no fuss. You walk away richer, and everyone goes back to their lives."

"And if I don't have it?"

"Then this conversation never happened, and we'll keep looking." He said it lightly, but underneath the lightness was the sound of a door closing somewhere far away. "Though I'll be honest with you, because I respect competence when I see it: the chest surfaced near your camp. You're the only party on this stretch of river. And you left Kessel's Landing once already yesterday afternoon and came back without it, which suggests either you've hidden it, or you've moved it, and both suggest you know exactly what we're talking about."

I looked at Pell. Pell looked at the river. Somewhere behind us a gull screamed over the fish market like it was being paid to.

"What's in it?" I asked. "You said old paper. Old paper about what?"

Hale's smile stayed exactly where it was, but something behind his eyes recalculated. "That's not relevant to the transaction."

"It's relevant to whether I sell it to you for a song because I think it's somebody's grandmother's land deeds."

"The price won't be a song." He named a figure. It was more money than I'd earned in the last three years combined, and we both knew it, and the fact that they knew what I was worth made my skin crawl in a way the money itself never could.

"That's a lot for old paper," I said.

"It's a lot for *sealed* paper." He let that sit for a moment, then spread his hands, magnanimous. "You don't need to decide now. Tomorrow evening, the Meridian barge docks at the lower pier. If the chest is aboard, payment is immediate. If it isn't—" the smile finally, briefly, flickered, "—we'll assume it was lost in the flood, and we'll grieve accordingly."

"Grieve," I repeated.

"These are emotional times for everyone, Ms. Voss." He tipped an imaginary hat. "Tomorrow evening. Lower pier."

They walked away up the dock, and I watched their boots stay clean the whole way, and I understood that they hadn't come to buy a chest. They'd come to tell me they knew who I was. The offer was a courtesy. The name was the message: *we found you once, we can find you again.*

* * *

Tomas was waiting in the alley behind the net shed, which was where he'd been waiting, in one form or another, for three days. He had the specific energy of a man who has rehearsed an argument eleven times and is disappointed he might not get to use any of it.

"They found you," he said.

"How do you know that?"

"Because two men in clean boots asked the harbormaster about you this morning, and the harbormaster told me, because the harbormaster tells me everything, because I tip him in cigarettes and flattery." He fell into step beside me as I walked, fast, toward nowhere in particular. "Freya. Freya. We need to talk about—"

"Don't say my name in the street."

He shut up for a full ten seconds, which I later learned was a personal record. Then: "What did they offer?"

"Money. A lot of it. For the chest."

"And you said—"

"I said I'd think about it."

Tomas stopped walking. "You said you'd *think* about it? You're going to give it to them?"

"I'm going to *stall* them, Tomas, which is different, and if you'd spent five minutes outside an archive you'd know the difference." I turned down the lane toward Marla's, checking the windows the way I checked rock faces—automatically, reading for the loose hold. "Stalling means tomorrow evening exists. Tomorrow evening means I have a day and a night. Do you understand what a day and a night is worth right now?"

He caught up. "Where's the chest?"

"Where you can't get to it without me."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only kind I'm giving today."

Marla's boarding house sat at the top of the lane, sagging gently toward the river like everything else in Kessel's Landing that had given up on being vertical. I went in through the kitchen, past Marla herself, who took one look at my face and one look at Tomas behind me and said, "I'll make tea," in the tone of a woman who has lived long enough to know when tea is a stalling tactic and makes it anyway.

Upstairs, in the narrow room with the window over the water, I finally let myself stop moving.

Here's what I knew, laid out on the floor of my mind like gear before a climb:

One. They knew my real name. Which meant the false name was dead, which meant every careful month of Mara Renner was already spent, already worthless, already a costume I couldn't take off fast enough.

Two. They hadn't taken me. Men who find a blacklisted guide under a false name and *don't* take her are men who want something first. They wanted the chest handled quietly. Which meant whatever was in it was dangerous enough that they preferred it gone softly rather than loudly.

Three. Tomorrow evening was not a deadline. It was a courtesy. Men like Hale don't leave loose ends overnight if they can help it; they leave them until the workday ends.

Which meant tonight.

* * *

I told Tomas at the window while the tea went cold downstairs. He listened better than I expected, which is to say he interrupted only six times.

"The night boat," he said. "Old Sefa's barge. She runs cargo downriver after dark to avoid the toll station."

"Not downriver." I watched the water go gold and then bruise-colored as the sun dropped behind the ridge. "Upstream."

He blinked. "Upstream? The Serran goes into the gorges upstream. There's nothing up there but the reserve and—"

"And the headwaters." I said it before I'd fully decided to say it, and once it was out, it was out, the way a piton is out—you don't get to un-place it. "The chest came from upstream, Tomas. Floods don't carry things downstream from nowhere. Something opened up above Verrik. Something the water's been sitting on for twenty years."

"The drowned capital," he whispered. He said it the way people say names of the dead.

"If it exists. If any of it exists. But the maps in that chest are survey maps, and survey maps have a source, and sources have coordinates, and I'm a guide. Finding routes through forbidden country is the one thing I have ever been good at." I turned to look at him. He was twenty-six, maybe, with ink stains on his fingers and the posture of a man who'd been told to sit down his whole life and had recently, badly, stopped. "This isn't your river. You can still walk to the capital, file your reports, keep your little exile job. Nobody in Kessel's Landing will remember you in a week."

"You're trying to get rid of me."

"I'm trying to give you an exit. There's a difference."

"There really isn't." He dug into his satchel and produced a notebook—of course he had a notebook—and flipped it open to a page dense with his cramped handwriting. "Two days ago I found a shipping record. Four chests, commissioned the same year, same brass fittings, dispatched from the same office. One was destroyed in a fire in Corvane—that's documented. One is in a private collection, location unknown. One vanished in transit twenty years ago." He looked up. "And one came out of a willow tree and into your hands. Freya, these weren't lost. They were *sent*. Someone scattered the evidence on purpose, before the erasure finished, hoping someday someone would follow it upstream."

I stared at him. The room got very quiet, the way it does before weather.

"Your mother," Tomas said, gentler now, "was in that photograph. In front of the dam. I saw it before you took the chest away. I've spent three years reading everything written about Vessia, and there is no dam in any of it, and there is no engineer by her name in any of it, and that means someone erased her specifically, which means she mattered specifically." He closed the notebook. "I'm coming upstream. Not because I'm brave. Because I'm stubborn, and because nobody else will ever do this, and because—" he faltered, then steadied, "—because the archive taught me one true thing: paper survives. People just have to carry it."

Outside, the last light went off the water. Down at the lower pier, I knew, the Meridian barge would be tying up in the dark, early, patient, full of men whose boots never got dirty because other people did the wading.

"All right," I said. "Sefa's boat leaves at midnight from the old timber slip. Bring nothing you can't swim with."

"And the chest?"

"Already on the boat." I allowed myself the smallest smile I'd smiled in three days. "I told you. Where you can't get to it without me."

* * *

Sefa was seventy if she was a day, built like a mooring post, and she took my double payment without counting it, which told me she already knew more than I'd paid for. Her barge was a low black shape against the blacker water, stacked with crates that smelled of salt fish and kerosene.

"Upstream," she said, in a voice like gravel settling. "Past the toll station. You want quiet running."

"As quiet as the river allows."

"The river doesn't allow anything." She spat over the side, ceremonially. "It permits. Get aboard."

Tomas came down the timber slip at five minutes to midnight, breathing hard, satchel bouncing, one boot squelching in a way that suggested he'd already found the river whether he meant to or not. He clambered aboard, nearly went over the side, was hauled back by the collar by Sefa without her ever looking at him, and stood beside me at the stern as the poles pushed off and Kessel's Landing began to slide away behind us.

The town shrank. The lamps thinned. And then, at the lower pier, a lantern swung—a signal, or a search—and I saw figures on the dock, small with distance, and one of them raised something to his eyes. Glasses. Or a spyglass. Checking a barge that wasn't ours, or checking all of them, or checking for two passengers who were supposed to be asleep in a boarding house until morning.

"They'll know by dawn," Tomas said quietly.

"They'll know by dawn that the room's empty. They won't know the direction for another day, and by then we'll be past the toll station." I watched the dark water fold under the bow. Upstream. Against the current, the way everything worth doing always seemed to go. "After that, it's gorges, and the reserve, and whatever's waiting at the source."

"You're scared," Tomas said. It wasn't an accusation. He said it like he was noting a fact for the record, because that was how archivists loved.

"I've been scared for six years." I found the chest's outline under the tarp with my boot, solid and real, my mother's ghost packed in brass and iron. "This is the first time I'm scared *toward* something instead of away from it."

Sefa rang the bell twice. The current took us. Behind us, Kessel's Landing put out its little lights one by one, going to sleep on its secrets, and ahead of us the gorge walls rose up black against the stars like the pages of a book someone had tried very hard to burn.

I put my hand flat on the tarp, over the chest, and kept it there the whole way into the dark.
