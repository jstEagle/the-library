## 6. Upstream Rules

Tomas Ilic slept like a man who had never once been woken by anything dangerous. He lay on the floor of the rented room with his arms crossed over his chest and his mouth slightly open, and every few minutes he made a small sound, half snore and half argument, as though even unconscious he was filing objections with someone.

I sat at the window with the shutters cracked and watched Kessel's Landing wake up. Fish carts. Gulls arguing over fish carts. A woman beating a rug with the rhythm of someone settling a personal grudge. Ordinary morning noises in a town where two men in clean boots knew my real name.

Three days since we'd slipped out of the harbor. Three days of lying low in a rooming house that smelled of tallow and old river, while I did the thing I was actually good at, which was not running—running was easy—but planning.

The chest sat under the bed, wrapped in oilcloth, looking for all the world like luggage. Inside it were survey maps of a country that no longer existed, sealed orders signed by men whose names had been scraped out of every archive between here and the capital, and a photograph of my mother standing in front of a dam that officially had never been built. I'd looked at that photograph maybe forty times now. It didn't get easier. It got more specific, which is worse.

"Freya." Tomas's voice, muffled by the floorboards and sleep. "Are we leaving today?"

"We're leaving when I say we're leaving."

"That's what you said yesterday."

"And it remained true all day. Remarkable consistency."

He rolled onto his side and squinted at me through the shutter light, hair flattened on one side, looking approximately twelve years old. "You've been staring at that window since before dawn. Either you're planning something or you're hiding from something, and you don't hide."

"I hide professionally. It's called a cover identity."

"You know what I mean." He sat up, and the boyishness fell off him the way it always did when he got to the part he cared about. "We can't stay here. They found you in three days. In a town this size, they'll find us again in five."

"I know."

"So what's the plan? Because if the plan is 'keep moving downstream until the river runs out,' I'd like to register an objection now, while I'm still rested enough to make it well."

I let the shutter close and turned around. Time to say it out loud. Saying things out loud makes them real, and real things can fail, but unreal things can't succeed either.

* * *

"The plan," I said, drawing on the tabletop with a wet fingertip, "is upstream."

Tomas looked at the water mark like it might resolve into sense if he stared hard enough. "Upstream."

"The Serran doesn't start at the border. That's the trick of it. Down here it's fat and slow and everyone assumes it comes from somewhere boring—the Verrik hills, some glacier nobody's surveyed. But follow it up—" I dragged my finger north along the table's grain, past the knot that stood in for the gorges, "—and it goes up into the Verrik Reserve. Closed territory. Has been for twenty years. And do you know why a consortium that erases countries would want a reserve closed?"

"Because the headwaters are there."

"Because the headwaters are *there*," I agreed, "and above the headwaters is the reservoir, and under the reservoir is Solmere."

The name came out of me before I'd decided to say it. The drowned capital. I'd only learned it from the chest's documents four days ago, and already it sat in my mouth like a stone I'd carried for years.

Tomas had gone very still. He did that—he went still the way other people got loud. "The capital. You're saying we go to the drowned city."

"I'm saying we go to where the water that drowns it comes from. The intake works, the dam, whatever they've built up there to keep it quiet. If your mother's report exists anywhere outside a sealed vault—"

"My *mentor's* notation—"

"If it exists," I said, because I wasn't going to fight him about the handwriting again, not yet, "it'll be up there. Sealed things live near the people who seal them. That's not philosophy, that's just how bureaucracies rot."

He was quiet a moment. Then: "How far?"

"By river, six days if the water cooperates. By foot after that, another three into the high country. Call it ten days, give or take everything going wrong."

"And will the water cooperate?"

And there it was. The question I'd been chewing since the second night, when I'd laid the chest's maps out on the floor and measured them against everything I knew about rivers, which was considerably more than the man who drew these maps had known, or admitted to knowing.

"No," I said. "That's the problem. We have a window, and the window is closing."

* * *

Here is what twenty years of guiding teaches you about water: it has a calendar, and it does not care about yours.

The Serran ran high now because of late snowmelt pushed down from mountains that officially fed nothing important. High water meant the gorges were runnable—fast, violent, runnable. But the dry season was coming early this year; I could read it in the air, in the way the mornings came up clear instead of fogged, in the dust on the fish carts' wheels. When the dry season bit, the river would drop. And where the Serran cut through the lower Verrik gorges, dropping water didn't mean gentler rapids. It meant the river pulled itself apart into braided shallows and boulder fields, and the channels that remained became sieves.

There was a stretch the old boatmen used to call the Throat—not on any chart I'd ever seen printed, but Anselm-types always have their own names for things—where low water exposed a limestone shelf across the entire channel. Above the shelf, deep green pool. Below it, forty feet of whitewater threaded with rocks the size of houses. At high water you ran the shelf with your heart in your teeth and lived. At low water you portaged, if you could find footing, or you stopped, and stopping meant being found.

"After the dry season closes the gorges," I told Tomas, "the only way into the Reserve is overland through the Verrik passes, and those are watched. Checkpoints, patrols, the kind of border that exists specifically so that people like us die politely trying to cross it. So we go now, on the water, ahead of the drop. We have—I'm estimating—twelve days before the levels fall below runnable. Maybe fifteen. Maybe eight, if the weather decides to insult me personally."

"So we're racing a season."

"We're racing a season, two men in clean boots, and whatever patrol boats the consortium keeps on the middle river. And we're doing it in a boat we haven't bought yet, with a boatman we haven't found, using money we barely have."

Tomas counted on his fingers, which should have been funny and somehow wasn't. "How much money do we have?"

"Enough for the boatman. Not enough for bribes, not enough for gear beyond what we're carrying, and not enough for mistakes."

"What happens if we miss the window?"

I looked at the map in my head, the one behind my eyes, the one with the reservoir sitting on top of Solmere like a lid on a pot. "Then we wait months for spring melt, in a country where our faces are worth money, and hope the consortium spends the winter deciding we died in the flood. Which they won't."

"So it's now."

"It's now."

He nodded slowly, and I watched him do the thing he did instead of being afraid, which was to reach for the chest and pull out the maps again, as though understanding them harder would make the river behave. I let him. Everyone needs their version of checking knots twice.

* * *

Finding a boatman in Kessel's Landing took one afternoon and cost me most of my remaining patience.

The harbor master wanted papers. The ferry office wanted references. The fishing fleet wanted nothing to do with anyone heading upstream, because—and this was said to me by a man gutting carp with the tenderness of a surgeon—"nothing up there but dead water and dead men, girl, and the dead water's the friendlier of the two."

Which left the other kind of boatman. The kind who worked the tavern at the end of Coal Wharf, where the lamps burned low and the clientele had the specific silence of men who had all, at some point, decided not to answer a question honestly.

I found him at the corner table, because there is always a corner table, and there is always a man at it who watches the door without seeming to. Big through the shoulders, gone soft through the middle, hands like dock rope. Gray in his beard, black in his eyes. He was drinking something clear and expensive, alone, which in a place like that was either arrogance or grief, and usually both.

"Anselm," said the barman, when I asked, in the tone of a man naming a dog that bites.

"Anselm," I repeated, and walked over, and sat down across from him without being invited, because asking permission from a man like this was how you started a negotiation you'd already lost.

He looked at me the way he probably looked at weather. "You're lost, rivergirl. Ferry dock's the other end."

"I need a boat run upriver. Past the gorges. Into the Verrik approaches."

The tavern didn't go quiet—taverns like this never fully quiet—but the conversations nearest us developed a sudden interest in their drinks. Anselm turned his glass a quarter turn on the wood.

"No."

"You haven't heard the pay."

"Don't need to. Nobody runs up past the Throat this time of year except smugglers and corpses, and the smugglers have better manners than you."

"Double rate. Cash, up front, half now."

Something moved behind his eyes—not greed exactly. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man hearing a number he'd expected. "Double rate for what cargo?"

"Cargo that stays wrapped. Questions cost extra."

"Questions aren't for sale."

"Good," I said. "Neither are answers. We'll get along fine."

He studied me then, properly, the way a man reads water before committing a boat to it. I let him look. There was nothing in my face I needed to hide—tiredness, yes, and the particular flatness that comes from having decided something irreversible. Guides learn to show that on purpose. It reassures clients and unsettles everyone else.

"You've done this before," he said finally. "Run bad water."

"I've done worse."

"Where?"

"Places that aren't on maps anymore."

For a moment—half a second, no more—his face did something complicated. Something old. Then it closed like a door.

"Half now," he said. "Half at the Verrik gate, whether you make it or not. My boat, my rules, my route. You don't touch the tiller unless I'm dead."

"Agreed."

"And if the patrol boats stop us, I talk, you keep your mouth shut and your hands visible. Whatever you're carrying—if it's the kind of thing that gets a man's boat burned, I want to know one thing first."

I waited.

"How many of you?"

"Two. Me and the archivist."

His eyes flicked toward the door, where Tomas was pretending very badly to read a notice board that had nothing on it but tariff schedules. "The boy."

"He's not a boy. He's a liability with a library card. But he's mine to manage."

Anselm almost smiled. Almost. It surfaced and went under like something big in deep water. "Half now," he said again, and put out his hand, and his grip was exactly what I'd expected and exactly as reassuring.

I paid him from the roll Tomas had brought out of the capital in a false-bottomed document case, because archivists apparently have expense accounts even in exile. Anselm counted none of it. He folded it away in a coat pocket and drained his drink and stood, and standing, he filled up the corner of the room like a change in pressure.

"Boat leaves tomorrow, an hour before dawn, from the old slip below the coal yard. Don't be late. The tide doesn't wait and neither do I."

"What tide?" I said. "It's a river."

He paused at the edge of the lamplight. "Everything that moves waits for something, rivergirl. Even rivers. Especially rivers." And he was gone into the dark, and the tavern exhaled, and I realized my hands were shaking slightly, and that it wasn't fear.

It was the feeling of a plan becoming real. Plans are safe in your head. Out loud, in cash, in a handshake—they can fail. But they can also actually happen, and I had spent too many years choosing safety over happening.

* * *

Tomas caught up with me on the wharf, walking fast, breath steaming in the river cold.

"He said yes? He actually said yes? Everyone in there looked at him like he was a ghost with a bar tab."

"He said yes."

"Why? Double pay isn't enough for that run. I did the arithmetic on the crossing fees alone, and the risk premium for the gorges should price us out entirely. He's taking the job at a loss, Freya. Boatmen like that don't take losses."

"No," I agreed. "They don't."

"So why?"

I leaned on the rail and watched the Serran move past beneath us, brown and patient, carrying its cargo of silt and secrets down toward the sea. Somewhere up there, past the gorges, past the Throat, past the checkpoints, the water came out of the mountain that had a city drowned behind it. My mother had stood in front of that mountain once, in a photograph, younger than I was now, with her hair tied back and her jaw set like she was daring the concrete to argue.

Maybe Anselm had lost someone to that water. Half the old families along this river had, if you believed the stories nobody told. Maybe he just hated the consortium the way a scarred man hates the knife. Or maybe he was exactly what he appeared—a greedy old smuggler who liked double rates—and I was reading ghosts into strangers because I'd started seeing my mother everywhere, in every face, in every act of stubbornness.

"There's a reason," I said. "There's always a reason. We don't need to know it. We need to be on his boat before dawn, with the chest wrapped, with our stories straight, and with enough food for ten days, because I promise you the river is going to eat some of our schedule and I intend to arrive at the Verrik gate with days to spare, not hours."

Tomas leaned beside me. For a while neither of us spoke, and the river filled the silence the way it fills everything eventually.

"Twelve days," he said quietly. "Before the water drops."

"Twelve days. Maybe less."

"And if we're still up there when the gorges close?"

I watched the current slide past a piling, smooth on top, churning underneath. "Then we find out what kind of guides we really are."

Below the coal yard, in the dark, I could just make out the shape of a boat at the old slip—broad-beamed, shallow-drafted, ugly as sin and probably tougher than sin too. A lantern swung once at its stern, acknowledging us, or warning us, or both.

An hour before dawn, I thought. The race starts tomorrow.

The calendar had begun counting without us.
