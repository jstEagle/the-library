## 7. Anselm's Price

People assume a boatman's price is about money. It isn't. Money is just the shape the lie takes so nobody has to look at what's underneath.

I told Freya Reyes—no, I didn't know her name then, I knew her as "the woman with the chest and the boy who talks too much"—that my rate was double for no questions. She paid it without blinking, which told me two things. One: she had money or access to it. Two: she understood that double was cheap, because anyone who actually wanted to know what she was carrying would have asked for triple and a cut of whatever came after.

I took her coin and said nothing, which is the oldest trade on the river.

* * *

Let me tell you what a boatman sees, since apparently I'm confessing now.

A boatman sees how people sit. The woman sat like ballast—low, centered, ready to shift weight when the water argued. That's not a passenger. Passengers sit like luggage. She sat like someone who'd spent years reading water the way priests read scripture, and had stopped believing in both.

The boy sat like a question mark that had been fed too much coffee. Tomas. He kept touching the chest. Not opening it—touching it, the way you'd touch a sleeping dog you weren't sure was friendly. Every time he did it, the woman's eyes flicked over without turning her head, and every time, the boy pretended he'd been adjusting his pack.

I've run smugglers, deserters, tax evaders, one very apologetic bigamist, and twice a man I'm fairly sure was a murderer, though he tipped well and wept at hymns, so who am I to judge. None of them touched their cargo like that. You touch cargo like that only when the cargo is a wound.

So yes. I suspected.

And here's the part I'll deny under oath: I suspected before they ever stepped aboard.

* * *

Rumors come down the Serran faster than driftwood in spring. A chest with brass bindings, fished out of a flood near the willow bends. A woman asking after Vessia in Kessel's Landing, which is roughly equivalent to standing in the town square shouting fire in a powder house. Collectors sniffing around, men with clean boots and river-soft hands asking salvage contractors' questions.

I heard all of it in the space of four days, because Kessel's Landing has exactly one tavern and I have exactly one stool, and the stool is positioned—by design, by decades of careful positioning—where everyone tells me everything.

When the woman walked down to my mooring with the boy trailing her, I looked at her face and felt something drop through me like an anchor slipping its chain.

Not because I knew her. Because I almost knew her. There's a face I used to see in the firelight of a different boat, twenty-some years back, a woman leaning over charts with a pencil behind her ear, and this stranger had that face worn smooth by weather and guilt. Younger. Harder. But the same bones. The same jaw set the same way when she thought no one was looking.

I said, "Double rate, no questions," before she'd finished her first sentence, because if I let her talk long enough I might have asked one myself, and I have survived this long on the river precisely by never asking the ones that matter.

* * *

You want to know what I lost? Everyone eventually wants to know what I lost. It's the toll for any conversation longer than a transaction.

My wife's name was Ilka. My daughter was Sefa, six years old, missing two front teeth and proud of it. We lived in Ostrava's Landing, which is not on any map anymore, because there is no map anymore, because there is no Ostrava's Landing. There is forty meters of reservoir water and a consortium sign on the far shore reading RESTRICTED WATERS — VERVIK HYDROGRAPHIC AUTHORITY, spelled correctly, which is more care than they ever showed the people underneath.

I ran cargo for the loyalists. That sounds grander than it was. Mostly it was printing plates, pamphlets, a radio once, disassembled into pieces small enough to hide in pickle barrels. Vessia was dying by then—not dead, dying—and the loyalists were men and women trying to keep a heartbeat going with paperwork. I told Ilka it was honest work. I told myself it was honest work. It was neither, but it was ours, and a country is just a thing a lot of people agree to carry together, and we were carrying.

Then the dam closed.

They called it flood management. They called it regional development. They held meetings in languages none of us spoke and published schedules none of us could read, and one morning the water started coming up the street like a slow gray animal that had all the time in the world.

We were supposed to be relocated. Relocation meant a train east and a resettlement stipend and a form stamped with a consortium seal, and Ilka wouldn't take it. She said: the water will stop. Water always stops. Her grandmother had lived through three floods; her family had a ladder bolted to the attic beam from the flood of her great-grandmother's time. She had a whole genealogy of surviving water.

But this wasn't a flood. Floods end. This was a decision.

I was upstream when it happened. Running cargo, naturally. Always running something—that's the disease of men like me, we mistake motion for purpose. When I came back down, the rooftops were gone. Not collapsed, not burned. Gone under. All except the church steeple at what used to be the village center, poking up out of the new lake like a finger pointing at whoever was responsible.

Sefa's shoes are still in my cabin. Both pairs. The everyday pair and the festival pair with the red stitching. I don't know why I kept them. I know exactly why I kept them. Some things you keep so that forgetting has to climb over them every single day.

* * *

So when the woman with Ilka's jaw hired me to run the Serran upstream toward Verrik, toward the reserve, toward the drowned country itself, I quoted her double and told myself it was greed.

Greed keeps a man simple. Greedy men are predictable, trustworthy in the way a knife is trustworthy—it does one thing and you can see the edge. Grief is worse cargo. Grief makes a man do things like take a paying job for reasons he won't say out loud, and then lie awake listening to the river and hearing, under the water sound, a six-year-old laughing at something a dog did.

I signed on for the money. Write that down. Anselm Voss—no relation to anyone, I made sure of that years ago—signed on for the money.

* * *

The trouble started on the third day, above the Marlowe confluence, where the Serran narrows and the current gets opinions.

I'd been uneasy since dawn, though I couldn't have said why. Too quiet. The fishing boats that usually work that stretch were absent, and fishermen are like birds—they vanish before weather, patrols, and other bad news. Then I saw it: a white hull rounding the lower bend, riding high and clean, twin engines, no wake discipline whatsoever. A consortium patrol boat doesn't try to look like anything else. They figure the sight of them is deterrent enough, and mostly they're right.

"Down," I said. "Both of you. Now."

The woman dropped without argument—ballast instincts again. The boy opened his mouth, and she put a hand on his shoulder and he went down too, which was the first evidence I'd seen that he could learn.

"What do you want to do?" she asked from the bottom boards. Quiet. Not scared. Asking my professional opinion, like I was crew instead of hire.

I ran the arithmetic fast. The patrol boat was coming upriver, which meant it would pass us, maybe hail us, maybe board us. Boarding meant questions, and questions meant the chest, and the chest meant everything ended right there in the middle of the river with my money unspent and my reasons unexplained even to myself.

"Main channel's done," I said. "There's a side gorge, half a mile up. The Serpent's Throat."

The woman lifted her head. Even lying flat, I could see her doing the math, and I could see the moment the math came back ugly.

"I've heard of it," she said. "Nobody runs it."

"Nobody smart runs it." I swung the tiller. "Fortunately, stupidity is a renewable resource."

* * *

Here is what the guidebooks—which don't exist, because the consortium burns guidebooks the way it burns countries—would say about the Serpent's Throat: a limestone gorge barely wider than a loaded boat in places, with a gradient that drops you nine meters in under two kilometers, three standing waves of consequence, one undercut ledge known locally as the Widow's Shelf, and a rock in the middle of the exit chute that appears and disappears depending on the season and God's mood.

Here is what I would say: I've run it four times. Twice drunk, once to prove a point to a man who died anyway, and once carrying medicine to a loyalist cell in the winter of the closing, when the alternative was letting people go without insulin. That last time I swore I'd never run it again, and I've kept oaths before, just never the useful ones.

The patrol boat's engine note changed behind us—they'd seen us turn. A loudspeaker crackled something official and stupid about inspection. I gave the throat's mouth full throttle.

The walls closed in. The light went green and gold, filtered through the overhang, and the noise came up all at once—the throat doesn't announce itself gradually, it swallows you into its own opinion of your choices.

"Strap the chest down!" I shouted. "Everything strapped or everything lost!"

The boy scrambled forward, fumbling, and the woman moved past him and did it herself in ten seconds flat, knots I recognized from mountain work, tight and fast. Then she braced beside me at the stern.

"You should be down," I yelled.

"You should be younger," she yelled back, and despite everything, despite the water and the patrol and the twenty-year weight in that boat, I laughed. First time in months. Maybe the first time since the steeple.

The first rapid hit us like a debt collector. Green water over the bow, the world reduced to noise and spray, and then the throat bent left and the current tried to hand us to the right wall, and I leaned on the tiller with everything my shoulders had left and the boat obeyed the way old boats do—reluctantly, insultingly, at the last possible moment.

The Widow's Shelf came and went. I felt the pull of the undercut as we passed—a soft dragging hunger off the port side, the water there flowing backward into the dark under the stone. Anything that goes in doesn't come out. I've seen what the shelf keeps. Once. From a distance. I don't fish that stretch anymore.

Halfway through, the boy surfaced from behind the cargo, white-faced, gripping a line, and shouted something I couldn't hear over the roar. The woman shouted back, "Later!" and he shut up, and I remember thinking: she's been keeping that boy alive by volume for weeks now, and she's good at it.

The exit chute. The rock. God's mood that day was ambiguous—the thing was showing about a foot of black limestone, dead center where the water wanted us. Left of it, the chute narrowed to a slot I didn't trust. Right of it, a hole the size of a dinner table that would spin us like a leaf.

I went right. Close. Closer than close. I heard the rock scrape the hull along the starboard side, a long groan like a word being said very slowly, and then we were through, spat out into the lower pool, drifting in sudden enormous quiet.

Behind us, muffled by stone, the patrol boat's engines idled somewhere above the throat's entrance. They wouldn't follow. Consortium men are many things, but they're not stupid, and stupid is the entry fee for the Serpent's Throat.

* * *

We bailed in silence for a while. The boy worked the bucket with the grim energy of someone processing fear through labor. The woman checked the chest's straps, then the hull scrape—shallow, thank the indifferent sky—and then sat back on the thwart and looked at me.

"That was the worst water I've ever been on," she said, "and I've guided the Karst Reach in melt season."

"It gets worse above Verrik."

"I know."

She said it simply, no bravado, and that's what convinced me, more than the money ever had. She knew, and she was going anyway. People who know and go anyway are either fools or the only kind of person worth following, and I've spent my life sorting them, and my sortings have gotten more generous with age.

The boy—Tomas—finally found his voice. "How did you know that route? That gorge isn't on any chart."

"No," I agreed, "it isn't."

He waited for more. The woman didn't. She watched me with those eyes that were almost somebody else's eyes, and then she looked away and gave me the gift of not asking, which is rarer than courage on this river.

That night we camped on a gravel bench below the gorge, a fire small enough to hide in cupped hands. The boy slept like the young sleep, instantly and completely, one arm flung over the chest. The woman took first watch without discussing it.

I lay on my back and listened to the river. Upstream, somewhere past the dark bend, past the reserve line, past the checkpoints and the signs with their correct spelling, the water was pooling against concrete that my government-in-exile-of-one holds responsible for everything. Somewhere under that water was a kitchen table. A ladder bolted to an attic beam. Two pairs of shoes.

"Anselm," the woman said quietly, from the fire. Not a question. Just checking I was there.

"Still floating," I said.

She nodded and turned back to the water, and I closed my eyes and told myself, one more time, the story I'd be telling the tribunal if there ever was one: I'm in this for the money. Double rate. No questions.

The river carried the lie downstream, the way it carries everything, and kept what it kept.
