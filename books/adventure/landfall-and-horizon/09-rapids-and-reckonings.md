## 9. Rapids and Reckonings

The gorge below Anselm's side channel had a name on none of our maps, which should have told me something. Anselm called it the Throat. He said it the way a man says the name of an ex-wife who took the house.

"You can portage," he offered, leaning on his sweep oar with the studied casualness of someone who already knew my answer. "Two days around the ridge. Or you can run it."

I looked at the water. The Serran came through the narrows in three steps, each one uglier than the last, and the whole river had that flat, glassy, lying look it gets when it's moving faster than it wants you to know. High for the season. It had been high since the day I first noticed it, weeks ago, and I still hadn't decided whether that was weather or something worse.

"We run it," I said.

"Of course you do," Anselm said, and spat over the side in what I'd come to understand was either a blessing or a refund request.

Tomas sat amidships hugging the document chest like it was a child he'd been left holding at a party. He'd been seasick twice since dawn and was pretending he hadn't been, which I respected more than most acts of courage I've witnessed from people with actual training.

"Is it bad?" he asked me. "Scale of one to ten."

"Rivers don't do numbers," I said. "They do moods."

"And this one?"

"Divorced."

He laughed, which was the point. A scared passenger is a stiff passenger, and a stiff passenger is cargo. You want them loose. So I made jokes while I read the water, because that's the job—half hydrology, half theater—and I've gotten good enough at the theater part that sometimes I forget there's a script underneath.

There were four of us, one boat, and too much weight: the chest, the food barrels, the rope, the tarp, Anselm's mysterious toolbag he wouldn't let anyone touch. We ran the first drop clean. The second one clipped us against the wall and tore off an oarlock, and Anselm swore in three languages, two of which I suspected he'd invented specifically for boats. The third drop—the big one, the one that had made me say *we run it* out of pride rather than arithmetic—was where everything went to hell in the traditional manner, which is to say quickly and then very slowly.

* * *

Here's what nobody tells you about whitewater: it isn't loud when it matters. The roar is background. What you actually hear is small—a strap creaking, your own breath, the particular wet click of a barrel coming loose from its lashing. I heard that click. I turned to grab the barrel, which was the wrong call, because turning means taking your eyes off the line, and the line through the Throat's third drop is a rumor you have to keep believing or it stops existing.

The boat hit the hole sideways. That's the technical term—*hole*, as if the river dug itself a little pit just to be rude—and sideways is the one direction you never want to hit anything. The world went white. Then green. Then upside down.

I came up gasping, found the sweep line, got a hand on it. Anselm surfaced near the stern, coughing, alive, furious in a way that seemed almost professional. The chest was gone. Tomas was gone.

I found him thirty seconds later, which in drowning time is a geological era. He was under the seam where two currents folded together, the place where the river holds things down and worries them like a dog with a bone. His hand broke the surface once, fingers spread, then vanished. No screaming. People who are actually drowning don't scream; they're too busy. Screaming is for people who still believe someone will come.

I dove.

Cold water up here has a flavor—stone and snowmelt and old iron—and I tasted all of it. I caught his jacket on the second pass, hooked him under the arms, and kicked for the light. He fought me. They always fight you at first; the body's last stupid instinct is to climb whatever's nearest, and the nearest thing is the person saving you. I took an elbow across the cheekbone, wrapped him tighter, and waited out the panic the way you wait out any storm: by being more stubborn than it.

We came up in the eddy below the drop, both of us hanging onto a granite shelf with the water pulling at our legs like a creditor. I hauled him onto the rock and he vomited half the Serran River and then lay there shaking.

Anselm appeared downstream ten minutes later, poling the swamped boat with the serenity of a man who had long ago stopped expecting the universe to behave. The chest was lashed to it—he'd grabbed it on the way past, because of course he had. One barrel lost. Half the food gone. Both of my spare ropes gone, which hurt more than the food; you can eat less, but you cannot rope less.

"Everyone alive," Anselm observed, "which is the only statistic that improves."

Tomas couldn't stop shaking. Not dramatic shaking—the deep kind, the kind that lives in the spine. I put my jacket over him even though I was soaked too, because that's what you do, and sat beside him on the granite while the sun did its useless best.

"I couldn't get loose," he said finally. "The current—it held me down and I thought—" He stopped. Started again. "I thought about the chest. Isn't that insane? I'm dying and I'm thinking about paperwork."

"It's not insane," I said. "It's what you love. Rivers take inventory fast."

He looked at me then, really looked, water still running out of his hair, and asked the question I'd been waiting days for him not to ask.

"The mountain," he said. "You knew exactly what to do down there. You've pulled someone out before."

* * *

I could have given him the guide's answer. *Training. It's just training.* Everyone who's ever worked mountains or rivers has a version of that answer and we all deploy it like a smoke grenade. But he was shivering on a rock in a gorge with no name, having nearly died for a country that doesn't exist, and there's only so much debt a person can pretend not to notice.

"There was a climb," I said. "Six years ago. Commercial expedition, twelve clients, the north face of the Karstel. I was lead guide."

The Karstel. Even saying the name felt like pressing on a bruise to check whether it still worked. It did.

"Second day, a serac above the route let go. Not huge—not the kind of thing that makes documentaries. Just enough ice to bury the fixed lines and take out the middle of the roped team." I picked at the granite with a fingernail, needing my hands busy. "Nine people died. Four clients, two guides, and three of the Sherpa team. I was below the break. I dug four people out. Two survived."

Tomas didn't say anything. Good. Most people say something, and every possible something is wrong.

"The inquiry found the route safe. Found the conditions within normal parameters. Found that the serac release was, their word, *unforeseeable*." I laughed, and it came out ugly. "Unforeseeable. Except I'd filed a report six weeks earlier flagging the serac as unstable. Warm autumn, unusual meltwater in the couloir. I recommended rerouting. My company sat on the report. Afterward, it was missing from the file. My copy was missing from my email. And somehow the story became that *I* pushed the pace, that *I* ignored signs, that Freya Reyes guides corpses."

"That's not—"

"It gets better." I told myself to stop. I kept going, because once you crack a seal like that the pressure does the work. "The company settled quietly with the families. Quietly. Which required a villain, and the villain couldn't be the company, so it was me. Blacklisted in two countries inside a year. My name on a list next to men who've actually killed clients through negligence. And the worst part—" I made myself say it. "The worst part is I'll never be sure. Maybe the serac really was unforeseeable. Maybe I did push the pace. When everyone tells you a story about yourself long enough, you start keeping a copy."

The river ran past us, indifferent, taking inventory.

"But you don't believe it," Tomas said. Not a question. He'd seen me read water for a week. He'd watched me make forty correct decisions in a row without hesitation.

"I don't know what I believe," I said, which was true, and also not the whole truth, and we both pretended those were the same thing. "That's the punishment. Not the blacklist. The doubt. They install it in you and it works for free forever."

He was quiet a long moment. Then: "Who was your mother to Vessia? Really. Because Maret talked about her like she was a saint, and the consortium talks about her like she was a criminal, and you talk about her like she's a locked room."

Above us the gorge walls went up and up, red stone stitched with shadow, and somewhere up there was a reservoir with a country at the bottom of it, and somewhere above that was a woman who had built the thing that drowned it and then, allegedly, tried to stop it.

"I don't know yet," I said. "That's honest. Everything I remember about her is from before. She left when I was nine. Before that she was just—my mother. She smelled like drafting ink. She taught me knots. She hummed songs in a language I thought was nonsense until Maret sang one back to me and I realized I've known Vessian lullabies my whole life without knowing what they were."

Tomas pulled the jacket tighter. "And after she left?"

"After she left there were letters. For a few years. Then the letters stopped, and then there was a man who came to ask questions about her, politely, twice, and then there weren't letters anymore." I stood, brushed off the seat of my pants, gave my hands something to do by coiling the salvageable length of rope. "So no, I don't know who she was to Vessia. I know who she was to me, and that woman wouldn't have built a dam to drown a capital city. But people contain multitudes, and mothers most of all, because we spend childhood building them into something load-bearing."

"That's the saddest sentence I've ever heard," Tomas said.

"I'm a guide. We specialize in load-bearing."

He smiled despite himself, and then winced, because smiling apparently pulled on swallowed river. Anselm called from the boat that we should move before the light went, using the tone of a man who had listened to every word and would admit to none of it.

* * *

We made camp that night on a gravel bench above the waterline, cold, damp, and short two meals' worth of food. I redistributed the loads by firelight, doing the arithmetic I always do: calories per person per day, distance remaining, margin of error. The margin had gone thin. Thin margins make guides honest, and honesty was clearly contagious tonight, because Anselm, cleaning his pipe with a twig, said to the fire:

"My wife was from Velika Porodica. Village on the reservoir rim. You passed the steeple yesterday—you slept in it."

I went still. Tomas went stiller.

"She wouldn't leave when they bought the valley out. Said the water would have to come to church to get her." He tamped the pipe, unhurried. "It did. I was running cargo downriver that week. Came back and there was a lake where my life used to be." He lit the pipe. "So when I say run the Throat, I mean it. I don't portage anymore. Portaging is for people with somewhere else to be."

Nobody spoke for a while. The fire cracked. Somewhere upstream, faint as memory, water fell over stone in the dark.

Tomas rolled toward me across the gravel, voice low. "Freya. Tomorrow, maybe the day after—we'll reach water Maret described. The old ferry road, the drowned terraces. And after that, the reserve." He hesitated. "When we get there. Whatever we find about her. Are you going to be able to carry it?"

I looked at the fire and thought about the question honestly, which is a luxury I recommend rationing.

"I carried nine people off that mountain," I said. "Four of them were dead. You learn the difference between carrying weight and being crushed by it. Mostly it's about deciding, in advance, that you're going to arrive."

"And did you decide? About her?"

The fire popped. Downriver, the Throat muttered to itself, still chewing on our barrel.

"I decided I'm going to arrive," I said. "What I find when I get there—that part I'm leaving open."

Tomas nodded slowly, and I saw him file the answer away the way he filed everything, carefully, in a mind that was starting to worry me—not because it was wrong, but because it was quick, and quick minds notice patterns, and I had spent the whole evening handing him pieces of a pattern I hadn't finished looking at myself.

He was asleep within minutes, exhausted past insomnia. Anselm smoked his pipe down to ash. I took first watch, sitting with my back against a boulder, watching the black ribbon of the river slide past.

It was near midnight when I noticed it—or rather, when I admitted I'd been noticing it for an hour. The sound of the river had changed. Deeper. Slower. The Serran was dropping, and dropping fast, the way water does when something upstream has opened to swallow it.

I woke Anselm. He listened for ten seconds, and for the first time since I'd met him, the old man looked genuinely afraid.

"That's not dry season," he said. "That's gates. Somebody just opened the intake gates at Verrik."
