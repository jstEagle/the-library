## 10. The Village Under the Waterline

The river told us before Anselm did.

He'd been quiet all morning—not his usual quiet, which was the silence of a man being paid not to talk, but a different kind, a listening kind. He kept standing at the tiller with his head cocked like a dog hearing a whistle nobody else could hear, and twice he made us pull in close to the bank for no reason I could see, just to let some driftwood pass that wasn't worth avoiding.

Then the current changed.

You learn to read water the way other people learn to read faces, and what the Serran said, in its slow gravel-voiced way, was: *I'm getting shallower.* The eddy lines had gone soft and wide. The boils that had been punching up out of the deep channel all week were gone. And the color—that was the strangest part. The river had run the green of bottle glass since Verrik Gate, and now it was going pale, milky at the edges, like it was embarrassed about something.

"Drop," Anselm said finally, which was two more words than he'd spent on us since breakfast.

"How much?" I asked.

He shrugged, which from Anselm was a full paragraph. But Tomas answered for him, because Tomas had been sitting in the bow with the survey maps spread across his knees for three days straight, and if there was one thing my mother's colleagues had been good at, it was knowing exactly how much water they'd stolen.

"Four meters," Tomas said quietly. "Maybe five. The maps show the drawdown curve." He looked up at me, and there was something in his face I hadn't seen before—something almost frightened. "Freya. At this level, things come up."

I thought he meant rocks. I'd been reading water for fifteen years; shallow meant rocks, and rocks meant work, and work was the one language I spoke fluently.

I was wrong.

* * *

We came around a bend where the valley widened into a drowned bowl, and the village rose to meet us.

Not all at once. First a chimney, black and skeletal, standing in six feet of water like a man wading out to meet his death. Then a roofline, tiles still clinging in places, furred with twenty years of silt and weed so that it looked less like architecture than like the back of some enormous sleeping animal. Then another roof. Then a whole street of them, running down into the green water in a neat row, doorways open, as if the houses were simply holding their breath.

Nobody said anything for a long time. Even the river seemed quieter here, sliding past the rooftops without comment.

"They left the buildings," Tomas said at last. His voice had gone thin. "They didn't even—they just filled the valley."

"They moved people out first," Anselm said. It was the longest sentence he'd given us since we hired him, and he delivered it staring straight ahead at the water. "Mostly."

That word did more damage than anything else could have. *Mostly.* I looked at the doorways under the waterline and made myself stop imagining who might have walked through them last.

The low water had done something else too. Along the near shore, where the reservoir had retreated across a wide shelf of cracked grey mud, the tops of walls stood exposed—a garden wall, the gable end of a barn, and there, maybe four hundred meters upstream, breaking the surface like a raised fist:

A church steeple.

Just the tower. The nave lay somewhere beneath us, but the steeple had been built on high ground, or the ground had been higher then, and now it stood alone in the drained basin with its stone going green and its louvres empty where the bell should have been. Somebody had taken the bell. Or somebody had drowned it with everything else.

"We camp there," I said, before I'd decided to say it.

Anselm gave me a look. "Water's dropping. Boat won't float by morning."

"Then we sleep dry for once." I was already steering for the shore. I told myself it was practical—the boat needed unloading anyway, the mud would be firm enough to walk on by dark, and a stone tower beat a wet tent for shelter. All true. None of it was the reason.

The reason was that I couldn't look away from that steeple, and I have never in my life ignored a thing I couldn't look away from. That's how I ended up blacklisted in two countries. That, and my mother.

* * *

The mud took some convincing. It looked solid and wasn't, and we lost a crate of Anselm's cargo to it entirely—he watched it go down with an expression I couldn't parse and didn't ask about—and by the time we'd hauled the boat up onto the old roadbed, which surfaced out of the muck like a spine, the light was going copper and long.

Walking out across the basin was the worst and best thing I've ever done, and I've done a fair number of both.

The mud held if you stepped where the weed had dried to matting. Around our boots, the exposed lakebed gave up its dead in small ways: a child's shoe, leather gone stiff as bark. A enamel pot, whole, its bottom furred white with mineral stain. Fence posts in a line going nowhere, or going everywhere, depending on how you thought about it. And everywhere the small bright litter of a life interrupted—glass, crockery, a hinge, a doll's arm reaching up out of the silt with its fingers curled.

Tomas stopped walking twice. The second time he crouched and picked up a coin, turned it over, and went very still.

"What is it?"

"The date." He handed it to me without looking at me. The coin was worn nearly smooth, but the reverse showed a bridge over water and around the rim, in letters half-eaten by corrosion: *VESSIA.*

"It's not rare," he said. "It's not even old. They minted these until the year they erased the country. There are millions of them. And every single one is illegal to own."

I weighed the little disc in my palm. It felt warm from his hand, heavier than ten grams of metal had any right to be. Somewhere under our feet was a whole nation's worth of these, scattered through the silt like stars.

"Put it back," Anselm said, without turning around.

"Why? Nobody—"

"Somebody comes here." Anselm's voice was flat. "Somebody always comes here. Leave it where they can find it."

I put the coin back exactly where Tomas had found it, face up, and we walked on.

* * *

The steeple was bigger inside than it looked, the way stone things are. We climbed through the louvre window on the landward side—it had been bricked partway up, someone's afterthought, decades ago—and found ourselves in the belfry chamber, floored in heavy planks that had dried out and shrunk but held. Below us, the stairwell descended into black water. You could smell it down there, cold and mineral and old, the smell of a place holding its breath for twenty years.

We got a fire going in a firebox somebody had built into the corner—somebody, again, some visitor from before, or after, I couldn't tell—and ate cold food while the light died over the rim of the basin. From the louvre windows we could see the whole drowned bowl: rooftops going blue-black in the dusk, the street lines showing as darker seams in the mud, and far off, the glint of the main channel where the river ran on without us, indifferent.

It should have been grim. It was, mostly. But there was also something else, and I'm ashamed of how long it took me to name it.

It felt like home.

Not *my* home—I didn't have one, unless you counted a duffel bag and a rotation of rented rooms, and I don't. But the shape of it. Fire in a stone room. People I'd chosen, eating together. Walls between me and the weather. I sat there watching Tomas annotate the maps by firelight, tongue between his teeth, and Anselm cleaning his boat knife with the concentration of a man performing surgery, and I thought: this is what it's supposed to feel like. This is what everyone else got, all those years I was moving on before the month turned over.

And then I thought: this village had exactly this, once. Fire, family, walls. And men with clean boots came and measured their valley and wrote numbers on paper, and the water came, and that was that. Twenty years of government later, and the whole country agrees this place never existed.

Homesickness for a home you never had is the stupidest grief there is. I recommend against it. I had it anyway, sitting in that belfry, and it sat on my chest like a cat that wouldn't be fed off.

"I'm going up," I said, and climbed the ladder to the top chamber before either of them could ask why.

* * *

The bell chamber was open to the sky through the empty louvres, and the stars over the basin were ferocious, no town glow for a hundred miles to soften them. I stood there letting the wind work on me, doing the thing I do when the thinking gets too loud, which is to stop thinking and count breaths.

That's when I saw the offerings.

They were tucked into the corner behind the ladder head, where the wind couldn't get them. A shelf of flat stone—no, not a shelf, a slab pried loose from the parapet and propped deliberately, a little altar. On it, arranged with care:

Fresh bread, wrapped in cloth. Still soft. Days old at most.

A jar of honey, sealed with wax.

Three candles, two burned down to stubs, one unburned.

And flowers. Dried ones, brown and papery, laid in a fan—except the stems weren't brown. Whoever had picked them had set them here recently enough that the stems were still green at the cut ends.

I crouched there for a long time with my hand hovering, not touching, the way you hover over evidence at a disaster site. Because that's what this was. Evidence.

Somebody climbed up here. Recently. Climbed a rotten ladder into a drowned church in a country that officially doesn't exist, and left bread and honey and candles and flowers on a stone slab, the way you leave things for the dead, or the way you leave things—

—for a house. For a home. The way you visit.

"You're seeing it too." Tomas had come up the ladder behind me, quiet as a man who'd learned to move quietly in archives. He crouched beside me. His face in the starlight was very young suddenly. "Someone still comes."

"Someone still *lives* here," I said. "Or close. Bread doesn't keep. Flowers don't keep. They're coming from upriver, within a day's walk." I looked north, up the black seam of the valley, toward the closed reserve and the source and whatever waited for us there. "Someone kept the faith for twenty years, alone, in a flooded graveyard."

"Or not alone," Tomas said softly. "Maret's cairns. The keeper of stones. If she tends memorials along the shore—" He stopped himself, but the thought was already loose between us, pacing its cage.

Below us, faintly, Anselm's voice drifted up through the floorboards, talking to the fire in his own language, low and rhythmic. Not words I knew. A prayer, maybe. Or a list of names. With Anselm, it could have been either.

I touched the edge of the stone slab. Cold, of course. Everything here was cold except the memory of it.

"My mother grew up in a country," I said, to Tomas or to the wind or to nobody. "She had a childhood with streets in it. A language with songs in it. And I got nothing—not even the name of it, not even permission to know it existed. She never told me. Twenty years, and she never once said the word Vessia to me." My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "I used to think she was protecting me. Now I think maybe she was protecting herself. You can't mourn a country out loud if mourning it is evidence."

Tomas didn't say anything. He was good at that, when it mattered. He just reached over and lit the third candle—the unburned one—with a match cupped against the wind, and set it upright on the slab among the dried flowers, so that whoever came next would find it burned and know that strangers had been here, and had understood.

The flame stood very straight in the still air. Below us the drowned village lay in the dark, and somewhere in it, under forty feet of green water, there was a kitchen where a candle had once stood on a table, and a bed where someone had slept, and a door that had opened onto a morning that belonged to a country that was real, once, whether the maps admitted it or not.

"We leave at first light," I said. "And Tomas—we walk soft. Whoever tends this altar knows this valley better than we do. If they want to find us, they will. If they want to help us—" I looked upriver, where the mountains stood blacker than the sky. "Then maybe for the first time since Kessel's Landing, we're not the only ones hunting the truth."

I started down the ladder. Halfway down, I heard it—a sound from outside, from the mudflats below, faint but unmistakable in the dead-still night.

Footsteps. More than one. Coming toward the steeple, unhurried, through a village that had been underwater for twenty years.
