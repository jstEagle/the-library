## 11. The Keeper of Stones

The woman was waiting for us before we knew we were being waited for.

That's the thing about the lower Verrik shore—there's nowhere to hide and nowhere to arrive unannounced. The river narrows there between walls of gray limestone, and the water goes quiet in a way that made my skin crawl. After six days of rapids and Anselm's muttered curses, silence should have been a gift. Instead it felt like the river holding its breath.

"There," Tomas said, pointing.

She stood on a shelf of rock above the waterline, small and straight-backed as a fence post, wearing a coat that had been mended so many times it had forgotten its original color. Beside her, stacked with a precision that hurt to look at, were stones. Dozens of cairns. Hundreds. They marched along the shore into the haze like a sentence someone had started writing and never finished.

"Old woman on the bank," Anselm said, not slowing the boat. "Probably nothing."

"Nothing doesn't build four hundred cairns," I said.

"Three hundred and sixty-one," the woman called out, her voice carrying across the water with the flat certainty of someone who counted every day. "And you're going to beach that boat on the gravel before the current takes you around the bend, because the bend is where the snags live. I've watched eleven boats die there. Twelve, if you don't listen."

Anselm looked at me. I looked at the bend ahead—the water did something wrong there, a smoothness where there should have been texture—and I nodded once. He swung us in toward the gravel shelf, and we grounded with a scrape that sounded, in all that silence, like an apology.

* * *

Her name was Maret. She didn't offer it; Tomas read it later off a wooden marker she kept among her tools, but at the time she simply looked at the three of us the way a customs officer looks at luggage, deciding what needed opening.

"You're going upriver," she said.

"We're passing through," I said, which was true in the way most lies are true.

"Nobody passes through here." She said it without heat, the way you'd say *water is wet*. "There's nothing through. There's only up or back." Her eyes went past me then, to the chest lashed amidships under the tarp, and everything about her changed. Not dramatically. She didn't gasp or clutch at anything. But her whole body went still in a way that living bodies aren't supposed to go still, and when she spoke again her voice had dropped half an octave, gone private.

"Open it."

"No," I said.

"I'm not asking to take anything." She hadn't moved from her spot, hadn't taken a single step closer, and somehow that was more unnerving than if she'd lunged. "I'm asking you to open it where I can see inside. My father carried one just like it for nine years. Brass corners, iron hasps, the Vessian double-eagle burned into the lid. He carried it until the water came, and then he carried it until he couldn't, and then the water took both of them." A pause. "You'll want to know what was in his. Trust me. You'll want to know."

Tomas made a sound beside me that was half breath, half protest. Anselm had found sudden urgent business with the boat's painter. The river said nothing, which was its way of agreeing with her.

I opened the chest.

I told myself it was tactics. That showing a little bought goodwill, that an old woman on an empty shore was the safest audience we'd ever have. All of that was even true. But underneath the tactics, in the place where I keep the things I don't say out loud, I opened it because I was tired of carrying locked questions, and this stranger with her three hundred and sixty-one stones looked at the lid like she'd been waiting twenty years for someone to lift it.

Maret leaned over the survey maps without touching them. Her lips moved slightly, reading the old notation, and I watched something move across her face like weather.

"North district," she murmured. "These are the north district surveys. My father ran these same sheets." She looked up at me, and her eyes were pale gray, the color of the limestone, the color of the water. "Who gave you this?"

"The river," I said.

"The river gives back what it's done with," she said. "It doesn't give gifts. So I'll ask again, girl, and think carefully, because your face is doing something complicated: who gave you this?"

"My mother," I heard myself say. "I think. I don't—I found it after a flood. There's a photograph inside. Of her. In front of a dam."

Something crossed Maret's face then that I will be unpacking for the rest of my life. It wasn't surprise. It was recognition arriving late, like thunder after lightning—a woman on a gravel bar seeing, all at once, not me but the shape of my face, and behind it another face entirely.

"Sit down," she said.

"I'd rather—"

"Sit down, Freya Reyes."

* * *

I want to tell you I handled it well. That I came up swinging, demanded to know how she knew my name, maintained operational discipline. What actually happened is that my knees decided the matter without consulting me, and I sat down on the cold gravel of a dead country's shoreline while an old woman lowered herself onto a stone with the care of someone whose joints had filed formal complaints decades ago.

"Your mother's photograph was in the papers," Maret said. "Twenty years ago. Before they scrubbed it. Ilse Reyes, chief structural engineer, Vessian Water Authority." She pronounced each word like she was setting it down on glass. "You have her mouth. And her way of standing like she's about to argue with you."

"You knew her."

"Everyone in the capital knew *of* her by the end. Fewer knew her. I knew her." Maret picked up a flat stone from beside her boot and turned it over in her fingers, an unconscious habit, a rosary made of geology. "My father was a courier for the Water Authority. Trusted men carried sealed chests between offices because the consortium's people had infiltrated everything by then—every ministry, every archive. Paper traveled by hand or it didn't travel at all. Four chests were made. Identical. So that no one thief, no one raid, no one fire could end the record." She smiled, and it was the saddest smile I've ever seen on a living face. "It nearly worked, too."

"And your father's chest?"

"Went into the water with him. Deliberately. He weighted it and dropped it off the ferry rather than let the consortium's men take it at the checkpoint." She set the stone down with a small click. "He always said the river keeps what it takes. I used to think it was a comfort. Now I think it was a threat."

Tomas had gone very still beside me, the particular stillness of a man watching his life's work walk around wearing a human face. "The other two chests," he said carefully. "Do you know where—"

"I know what everyone knows, which is nothing, and what everyone hopes, which is less." Maret waved this off. "I'm not telling you stories about chests. You have a chest. I'm going to tell you about your mother, because you've clearly pieced together fragments, and fragments are worse than ignorance. Fragments let you imagine the good version." She looked at me straight on. "You've imagined the good version, haven't you? That she was a victim. That they used her name on documents she never saw."

I said nothing. The river said nothing. Somewhere upstream a bird made a sound like a hinge.

"She designed it," Maret said. "The dam. The Kestrel Dam, they called it, though its real name was Project Horizon, and yes—" her eyes flicked to me, "—I know what your book of maps calls the reservoir now. They named the drowning after the thing they drowned. That's how consortium men apologize. Your mother drew every line of that dam. Spent six years on it. Believed in it, at first—it was flood control, power generation, the future, all the words they use. The capital was going to be relocated. Relocated." She repeated the word with a bitterness that had clearly been aging in a barrel. "Two hundred thousand people. Forty centuries of records. A city older than any of the borders on your modern maps. And the relocation kept being delayed, and the budget kept being cut, and the dates kept moving, and your mother kept asking questions, which was the first mistake anyone made in this story."

"What kind of questions?" My voice came out strange. Thin.

"Where are the resettlement funds. Why is the fill schedule accelerated. Why does the final impoundment date precede the last scheduled evacuation convoy." Maret ticked them off on her fingers. "Good questions. The kind of questions engineers ask, which is why you put an engineer on the project if you're honest, and why you bury an engineer if you're not. By the last year she understood what the rest of us only suspected: there was never going to be a relocation. The flooding *was* the plan. Erase the capital, erase the records, erase the nation's proof that it existed, call it progress, call it hydrology, and twenty years later no map would even carry the name." She paused. "So she tried to stop it."

"How?" Tomas breathed.

"Every way a person can. Petitions, appeals, leaks—she fed documents to foreign journalists, and the journalists were bought or frightened or disappeared, and each attempt cost her protection. Then, in the final month, she did the unforgivable thing." Maret's voice dropped. "She went to the spillway gates herself. With a maintenance crew she'd personally selected—people who owed her, people who believed her. The plan was to jam the gates. Damage them subtly enough that filling would fail inspection, buy years, maybe forever. It might have worked. It would have worked, except one of her crew sold them all for a promise of passage west, and the consortium sealed the gates early. Filled the reservoir ahead of schedule. Ahead of the evacuation convoys."

The word *ahead* landed in my chest like a dropped anchor.

"Ahead of the convoys," I repeated.

"Some got out. Most didn't." Maret said it plainly, because dressing it up would have been a cruelty dressed as kindness. "Your mother was on the last ferry out. She spent the rest of her life—what was left of it, four years—trying to get the truth into a court, any court, anywhere. And the consortium couldn't kill her, because martyrs make noise, so they did something cleverer. They destroyed her credibility. They arranged a disaster with her name written all over it."

The mountain. The collapse. Eleven dead, including the client who'd hired my mother's firm, the inquiry, the findings, the blacklist that had followed me across two borders like a dog that wouldn't stop following. I had built my entire adult life on the foundation of that shame. I had become a ghost with a fake name because of it. And here was a woman on a gravel bar telling me, in the tone of someone reporting the weather, that it had all been architecture—that my mother's ruin had been drafted and engineered as carefully as her dam.

"They staged it," I said. My voice sounded like someone else's. "The collapse. They—"

"They undermined the slope themselves and left her signatures on the falsified reports. She was dead within two years—heart, officially—and you were young enough to be managed. Blacklisting the daughter was housekeeping. Tie up the loose thread before it learns to ask questions." Maret finally looked away from me, out at her cairns, at the long gray sentence of them running into the haze. "They almost finished the job. And then the river washed a chest up against a willow root, and here you sit."

* * *

Afterward there was tea, because there is always tea, even at the end of the world—especially at the end of the world. Maret brewed it in a blackened pot over a driftwood fire, and it tasted like pine and smoke and being sorry for myself, which apparently is a flavor tea can carry if the drinker cooperates.

I sat apart for a while. Not from dramatics. I just needed the river in front of me and no one beside me, and the others, to their credit, let me have it. Anselm checked the boat's seams with excessive devotion. Tomas pretended to catalog the chest's contents and mostly watched me with the careful expression of a man near a structure he suspects is load-bearing and cracking.

Here is what I understood, sitting there: I had spent twenty years being ashamed of the wrong thing. I had carried my mother's disgrace like a genetic condition, organized my whole restless, rootless, false-named existence around it, and it turned out the disgrace wasn't hers. It was manufactured. Stamped with her name by the same hands that had stamped a country off the map. Every border guard who'd turned me away, every employer who'd quietly withdrawn an offer, every night I'd spent calculating escape routes instead of futures—all of it built on a lie with excellent documentation.

Rage is supposed to feel hot. Mine arrived cold, which frightened me more. Cold rage does arithmetic.

"Freya." Maret had come to stand beside me, silent as her stones. "You're planning something. I can see it. You have your mother's face when she was planning, too—like the weather's already happened and everyone else just hasn't noticed."

"I'm going to the source," I said. "The reservoir head. Whatever's up there, whatever they're keeping—"

"The intake station." She said it without hesitation, and my blood went cold all over again. "You mean Station Zero. Yes. Someone tends the machinery of forgetting up there. Has for twenty years." She looked at me, and in the fading light her eyes were exactly the color of the drowned city's rooftops must have been, the ones I wouldn't see for days yet. "My father's chest had a companion document. A routing order. If you're going where I think you're going, you'll want to hear what it said about the tunnels beneath the spring."

"The tunnels," I said.

"Mm." Maret folded her hands. "Ask me properly, girl, and stay the night. The river's too high to run the gorges after dark anyway, and you'll want your mother's full story before you meet what's left of her work." She turned back toward her fire, small and straight-backed against all that dark water. "Because I promise you—whatever you think you know about what your mother built, you do not yet know the half of it."
