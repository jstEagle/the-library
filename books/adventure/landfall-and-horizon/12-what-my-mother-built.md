## 12. What My Mother Built

Maret's house smelled of woodsmoke and dried river mint, and it had exactly one lamp, which she set on the table between us like a referee at a fight.

"Read them here," she said. "Not in your room. Here, where I can watch your face. I carried these letters' twins for thirty years, girl. I want to see what they do to you."

"That's a strange kind of hospitality."

"I'm not being hospitable. I'm being a witness." She sat down across from me and folded her hands, and I understood that this was the price of the tea she'd already poured: she was going to sit there while I found out what my mother was, and she wasn't going to let me do it alone, because alone was how the consortium liked its witnesses.

Tomas hovered by the door with the chest open between us, papers fanned out in the lamplight like a losing hand of cards. He'd been sorting for two hours—survey maps to one side, sealed orders to another, and now, at the bottom of the chest's false floor, a bundle wrapped in oilcloth and tied with surveyor's twine.

"These are personal," he said quietly. "Letters. Dozens of them. All addressed the same way."

"To whom?"

He turned the top one so I could read it. *To E. Reyes, Office of Hydraulic Works, Vessia.*

My mother's initials. My mother's name. In an alphabet of a country that had never existed.

I picked up the bundle and my hands were steady, which surprised me, because everything behind my sternum had gone loose and ringing, like a bell someone had struck and then covered with a palm.

* * *

The first letter was dated eleven years before the mountain disaster. Eleven years before the avalanche report, the inquiry, the finding of gross negligence, the blacklist that followed me across two borders like a dog I couldn't shake. Eleven years before I stopped using my real name.

My mother wrote:

*Elena keeps asking why the reservoir has to be so large. I told her it's because water doesn't negotiate. That was a lie. The truth is the consortium wants the valley drowned past any hope of recovery—not deep enough to hold water, deep enough to hold memory. I have started keeping copies of everything. If you are reading this, I trusted you with them because you were the only person who ever checked my arithmetic.*

There were forty-one letters. I read all of them. Maret watched my face, as promised, and Tomas read over my shoulder when I let him, and somewhere around the twentieth letter the tea went cold and nobody moved to warm it.

Here is what the letters said, stripped of my mother's careful engineer's handwriting and her habit of numbering her arguments like she was defending a thesis:

She designed the Verrik Dam. Not consulted, not contracted—designed, from the intake tunnels to the spillway gates, her name on drawings that were later redrawn without her name. She took the commission believing the official story: flood control, power generation, progress for three nations at once. It took her two years to understand that the reservoir's true purpose was submersion. Vessia wasn't going to be developed. It was going to be deleted.

She tried to stop it. Of course she did—I know the woman those letters describe, or I thought I did; turns out I only knew her absence, the shape a person leaves in a household. She filed objections. They were accepted into the record and then removed from it. She testified before a tribunal that was dissolved before it could rule. She smuggled documents out through loyalists—through people like Maret's father, carrying chests upriver—and when the last legal avenue closed, she became what the consortium feared most: a witness with evidence and nothing left to lose.

So they didn't kill her. Killing engineers creates martyrs, and martyrs have funerals, and funerals have mourners who ask questions. Instead they waited. And when I was nineteen and leading my first solo expedition above the tree line, they gave her a choice that wasn't a choice, and she made it, and I paid the bill.

* * *

Let me tell you about the mountain disaster, since everyone else has told it their way for ten years.

Four climbers dead. A guided party caught above the icefall when a serac collapsed onto the route—a route I had chosen, on a day I had chosen, against conditions I should have read better. That was the official finding. Gross negligence. Failure of judgment. The guide Freya Reyes, barred from professional mountaineering in two countries, her certification revoked, her name entered into registries that outfitters actually check.

What the letters say is this: the serac didn't collapse. It was brought down. Charges placed in the ice the night before, by men whose expenses were buried in a consortium subsidiary's seasonal budget—the same budget line, Tomas found later, cross-referenced against a payment to a tribunal clerk in Vessia twenty years prior. My mother had scheduled her final public testimony for the week after my expedition ended. The consortium needed her discredited before she could speak, and they knew her well enough to know she would trade anything for her daughter's safety. So they put the disaster on my rope team, and they let her believe four people died because of her testimony, and she withdrew everything, and she disappeared, and the world called it conscience-stricken flight.

And I—nineteen years old, alive when four others weren't, holding a piece of broken carabiner I kept for years like a relic of my own guilt—I believed every word of it. I built my whole life inside that verdict. Every false name, every border crossed at night, every month-long tenancy and quick goodbye: all of it load-bearing walls in a house built on a lie I never thought to check, because checking it meant opening the door, and the door opened onto four graves.

I sat there in Maret's kitchen with forty-one letters in front of me and I did the thing I do with numbers, because numbers don't flinch. Four deaths. One staged collapse. One mother erased. One country drowned beneath a reservoir with a power station's nameplate bolted to it. And underneath all of it, the same small elegant mechanism, used twice: you don't destroy the evidence, you destroy the witness, and the evidence becomes a rumor, and rumors drown.

"They didn't erase Vessia," I heard myself say. My voice came out flat, the way it does on rapids when the water goes quiet before the drop. "They erased everyone who could prove Vessia existed. The country was just the first name on the list."

Maret nodded slowly, like I'd finally answered a question she'd been asking since before I was born. "My father used to say the water was the cheapest grave ever dug. No headstones. No dates. Just depth."

"The mountain was the same trick," I said. "Different altitude."

* * *

Rage is a strange thing in a body trained for crisis. I could feel it arrive the way weather arrives in high country—a pressure change, a taste in the air—and every instinct I had said: manage it. Assess exposure. Don't make decisions on a slope in changing conditions. Ten years of discipline sat on that rage like a lid on a boiling pot, and the lid rattled, and I let it rattle, because for once I didn't want the pot calm. I wanted it loud.

Because here's what shame does: it makes you small and grateful. Grateful for false names, grateful for towns that tolerate you, grateful for work under other people's licenses. I had been grateful for ten years. I had treated survival as penance, as if staying hidden long enough might balance some ledger. And the whole time, the debt was never mine. I had been paying interest on a crime committed against my family, diligently, monthly, in the currency of my own life.

"You're doing the thing," Tomas said carefully. "Where you go very quiet and start listing things."

"I'm calculating."

"What?"

"How many people it takes to stage an avalanche." I looked up at him. "Placers. Spotters. Someone to write the report. Someone to bury the report. Someone to brief the inquiry panel. That's not a conspiracy, Tomas, that's a payroll. Payrolls leave records. Somewhere there's a budget line with my mother's ruin on it, itemized."

"And if we find it?"

"Then it's not my word against theirs anymore. It's their own handwriting."

Maret reached across the table and turned the last letter toward me. It was different from the others—shorter, undated, the handwriting less certain, written by a woman who knew time was running out.

*If my daughter ever reads this: I did not abandon you. I abandoned the fight, which is worse, and I have regretted it in the only language I have left, which is silence. They told me you would be safe if I vanished. I have learned since that 'safe' is a word they use the way engineers use 'stable'—it means nothing has fallen yet.*

I read it three times. On the third reading, something in my chest that had been clenched since I was nineteen—some knot I'd mistaken for my own spine—came loose all at once, and I put my face in my hands, and I cried the way you cry at altitude: hard, fast, inefficient, done.

Maret put her hand on the back of my neck, rough and warm, the way you'd steady someone on scree.

"There," she said. "That's the face I wanted to see. Not grief. Grief they can use. That's the other thing."

"It's rage."

"Good. Rage climbs."

* * *

We worked until the lamp burned low. Tomas catalogued everything—dates, names, the subsidiary ledgers, the cross-references he'd been chasing since Kessel's Landing—and I noticed he'd gone quiet in a particular way, folding one sheet of the survey maps away from the others with a care that looked almost protective. I was too full of my own fury to ask about it. That's the thing about rage: it's a bright light with terrible peripheral vision. I saw my mother clearly for the first time in my life, and I didn't see the boy at the table hiding a page behind his elbow.

Near dawn, Maret banked the fire and said what she'd apparently come to say hours ago.

"The letters mention the final report. Her last one. Filed with the intake authority at Verrik, sealed pending review, and the review never came because the reviewers were bought." She tapped the table. "Sealed isn't destroyed. This consortium buries things—it doesn't burn them. Burning leaves ash, and ash can be tested. Burying leaves paperwork, and paperwork can be found."

"Verrik's a closed reserve," Tomas said. "Permits take months."

"We don't have months," I said. "The dry season drops the river in—" I did the arithmetic without wanting to, the way you check a crevasse bridge even when you're hoping it holds. "Twelve days. Maybe fourteen. After that the gorges close and the intake roads are impassable until spring."

"So we go now."

"So we go now." I stood, and my legs were steady, and I noticed that too. Ten years of running had at least taught my body how to stand up under bad news. "Pack light. We take the core evidence and leave the rest cached with Maret's cairn route—if we're taken, the cache outlives us."

Maret snorted. "You won't be taken. You're a Reyes. Stubbornness is genetic in your line. Your mother argued with a dam."

"And the dam won."

"The dam filled," Maret corrected. "It hasn't won yet. Nothing's won until everyone agrees to forget, and look at me. Look at my cairns. I've spent thirty years refusing to agree."

Outside, the Serran ran dark and fast under a sky going grey at the edges, and for the first time since I was nineteen I looked at moving water and didn't feel like a fugitive. I felt like a guide. There's a difference. A fugitive asks the river for mercy. A guide reads it, respects it, and takes her clients through anyway.

Upstream, past the gorges, beneath the mountain, a sealed report was waiting with my mother's name on it and the truth in its belly.

Twelve days of water left.

I went to pack.
