## 13. Tomas's Choice

The note came folded inside a fish wrapped in wax paper, which I have to admit was a touch of theater I hadn't expected from the consortium. I'd imagined their communications arriving by cipher, by dead drop, by some elegant mechanism involving hollowed-out book spines. Instead a boy of perhaps ten handed it to me on the dock, took my coin, and left me standing there with a trout and the distinct feeling I'd just been outmaneuvered by an institution that owned most of the maps in the world.

The note read: *You are a long way from your desk, Mr. Ilic. The Verrin Room misses you. So do we. The ferry landing, tomorrow, an hour past dawn. Come alone. Come curious.*

The Verrin Room. The sealed reading room on the fourth floor of the National Archive, where the documents nobody was supposed to want lived behind a door with two locks and a guard whose salary was, I now understood, considerably more than mine. I had spent three years of my career petitioning for access to that room and been refused eleven times. The refusals were polite. The twelfth refusal had come with a reassignment to a provincial records office in a town whose principal export was turnips, and that refusal had been less polite.

They knew my name. Of course they knew my name. I had been naive to think otherwise, but there is a particular species of naivety that belongs to archivists — we spend our lives believing that paper is power, and we forget that the people who control the paper control everything else.

Freya was on the river with Anselm, running a stretch of channel they'd wanted to scout before committing the boat to it. I had stayed behind to copy the survey maps, which is the kind of sentence that sounds harmless until you remember what the maps were of, and I was alone in the room above the chandlery when the fish arrived. Alone with a trout, a note, and the growing realization that tomorrow morning I was going to have to decide something, and that whatever I decided, I was going to have to live with it in a way that left marks.

I did not sleep. I want that on the record, in whatever archive eventually receives this account. I lay awake on a lumpy mattress in Kessel's Landing doing the arithmetic that men like me are trained to do, weighing one set of documents against another, and the ledger would not close.

* * *

The ferry landing at dawn was beautiful in the way that border towns are beautiful — accidentally, briefly, before the light got high enough to show the rust. Mist on the water. Gulls arguing over something dead. And at the end of the pier, a man in a coat too good for the town, standing with the particular stillness of someone who has never had to wait for anything in his life and finds the experience novel.

He was maybe fifty, gray at the temples, with the kind of face that would photograph well for an obituary. He did not introduce himself, which was its own introduction. Institutions like his didn't have names. They had functions.

"Mr. Ilic." His accent was capital-standard, vowels filed smooth. "Thank you for coming. I expected you'd bring the woman."

"Freya doesn't take orders from me," I said. "Nobody takes orders from me. It's one of my defining qualities."

"Indeed." He smiled the way a ledger balances. "Then perhaps you'll take orders from yourself. Sit."

There was a bench on the pier, weathered gray, and we sat on it like two men waiting for a ferry that neither of us intended to board. He produced a leather folder and set it on his knees but did not open it. I understood the gesture. The folder was the whole conversation, and he was going to make me ask.

"You were a promising archivist," he said. "That's not flattery. I've read your file. Your cataloguing work on the pre-Partition municipal records was genuinely excellent. Your thesis on archival gaps as positive evidence — the idea that what's missing from a record can prove as much as what's present — that was the kind of thinking we don't see often enough."

"I was told the thesis was 'speculative and unhelpful.'"

"I was told to tell you that." He said it without embarrassment, which I grudgingly admired. "Mr. Ilic, I'm going to speak plainly, because you strike me as a man who respects plain speech even when it costs him. The country you're chasing no longer exists. That is not a metaphor. It is a settled matter of law, of treaty, of cartography. Twenty years ago, certain arrangements were made, and those arrangements included the dissolution of Vessia and the redistribution of its territory, and the world signed on because the world was tired, and the arrangements have held for twenty years because everyone with the power to disturb them prefers that they hold."

"And the chest?"

"The chest is paper." He opened the folder at last. Inside were documents — real ones, I could tell even at a glance, with the watermarks and the seals that I had spent my career learning to authenticate. "Your reinstatement. Full access to the Verrin Room. A senior appointment, the Corvin Chair, actually, which has been vacant since your mentor retired. And this—" he tapped a second document, thinner, "—is a research mandate. Official, funded, with your name on it. You could spend the rest of your career in the sealed archives, Mr. Ilic, with a key of your own. Everything you've been begging to read. Everything."

I looked at the documents for a long time. I want to be honest about this, because the temptation in accounts like this one is to make the choosing look easy, and it was not easy. The Verrin Room had been the fixed star of my professional life since I was twenty-three years old. I had built my whole career on the premise that behind that door was the truth, and that truth, once reached, would matter. And here was a man from the institution that owned the door, offering me the key, and the price was a chest and a woman whose face I had recognized on sight because I had spent two years studying photographs of her mother.

"The mandate," I said. "What's its scope?"

"Vessia," he said. "Unrestricted."

"Unrestricted," I repeated. "Except for the parts that aren't."

"Every archive has parts that aren't."

"And the chest?"

"Goes to us. The woman goes her own way. You go yours. Nobody is harmed, nobody is pursued, and the historical record is preserved by professionals instead of amateurs in a leaky boat." He closed the folder but didn't hand it over. "You'd be doing the work you were born to do, Mr. Ilic. Inside the system, where work actually lasts. This—" he gestured at the river, the mist, the town, at me, sitting on a pier at dawn in a coat that had been mended twice, "—this ends one way or another. With us, or in a cell, or at the bottom of a gorge. The river you're following doesn't lead anywhere. It leads to water."

I have thought about that sentence many times since. *It leads to water.* He meant it as dismissal. He was, in his way, more right than he knew.

"May I see the mandate?" I asked.

He handed me the folder, and that was his mistake, or perhaps his courtesy, or perhaps — and I've come to believe this — his genuine belief that the offer was good enough that I'd have no reason to do anything but accept it. He turned to watch a heron working the shallows, giving me the privacy of the moment, and I sat there with my reinstatement and my chair and my key to the room where the truth was kept, and I read the mandate's scope clause twice, and then I read the signature block, and then I did the thing I had actually come to the pier to do, though I hadn't let myself know it until my hands were already doing it.

I had brought the copies. Not the originals — Freya kept those, and would have gutted me if I'd suggested otherwise, and rightly. But I had brought my working copies of the survey maps, the ones with the corrections in my mentor's hand, the ones that proved the archive itself had been a party to the lie. I had brought them in my satchel the way you bring a knife you're not sure you'll use.

I didn't need the knife. I needed something else, and the folder gave it to me. Because there, in the mandate, in the scope clause, in the careful capital-standard prose of the institution I had served and been discarded by, was a line item. A research authorization for "the Vessian cartographic corpus, extant holdings, four (4) units."

Four units.

The librarian in Kessel's Landing had told me there were four chests. The consortium's own mandate confirmed it — they knew the number, they'd been counting, which meant they'd been the ones keeping count, which meant the chests had never been lost. They'd been inventory.

I closed the folder. I stood up. The heron flew off, offended by something.

"You'll want an answer," I said.

"I'll want the chest," he said. "The answer is a formality."

"Then here's the formality." I took the mandate — just the mandate, the thin document with my name on it — and I held it up so he could watch, and I struck a match off the pier railing, which took three tries because my hands were not as steady as my voice, and I lit the corner of my own future and let it burn.

He didn't move. I'll give him that. He watched the paper catch and curl and go to ash over the water with the expression of a man watching a filing error correct itself, and when the last of it was cinders I dropped the folder — reinstatement, appointment, all of it — into the river, where it floated for a moment, ink blooming, and then the current took it the way the current takes everything.

"You understand," he said quietly, "what you've just done."

"I've just done the only thing in this conversation that wasn't already decided," I said. "You decided your side years ago. I've decided mine this morning. It took me longer. I'm an archivist. We deliberate."

"Your mentor signed the corrections himself," he said, and there it was — the knife they'd been saving, deployed now because the folder hadn't worked. "Did you tell her that? Did you tell the woman that the man who taught you everything helped draw the maps that drowned a country? You're not her ally, Mr. Ilic. You're the institution's residue. You're what we left behind when we cleaned house."

And here is the thing I have thought about most since, the thing I will think about at odd hours for the rest of my life: he was not wrong. That was the horror of it. My mentor's notation was on those maps. The archive I loved had been a hand on the wheel. Every argument I had ever made for the sanctity of records — every petition, every thesis, every refusal I'd nursed like a wound — had been made in service of an institution that had used its records to erase a nation and then locked the evidence in a room and posted a guard.

I could have gone back to the chandlery and packed. I could have told Freya the offer had been refused and said nothing more, kept my mentor's corrections in my satchel like a stone in my pocket, carried the secret the way the institution carried all its secrets — quietly, indefinitely, until it was useful.

Instead I walked back through the waking town, past the baker opening his shutters, past the boy who had delivered the fish and was now delivering something else to someone else and would never know what he'd been part of, and I climbed the stairs to the room above the chandlery, and I waited.

* * *

Freya came back at midday, river-damp and sharp-eyed, with Anselm's grumbling audible from the dock. She took the stairs two at a time, saw my face, and stopped in the doorway.

"You met them," she said.

"This morning. A man with an excellent coat and no name."

"And?"

I took the satchel off the bed. I took out the survey maps — my copies, the ones with the corrections — and I laid them on the table between us, and then I did the thing that had been sitting in my chest since the pier like a swallowed hook.

"Before I show you this," I said, "you need to know that I've known for days. Since before the gorge. Since before the rapids. The corrections on these maps — the ones that make them navigable to the source — they're in my mentor's hand. I recognized the notation the first night I studied them. He taught it to me. I learned to read it the way you learned to read water." My voice did something I didn't authorize. I let it. "The archive that trained me helped build the lie. And I hid that from you because I was afraid it would end this — us, the boat, all of it — and because some cowardly part of me wanted to keep the archive innocent. Wanted to keep myself innocent. As if loving a thing excused what it did."

Freya looked at the maps for a long time. Then she looked at me, and her face did something complicated — anger arriving, and grief, and underneath both of them, something that might have been recognition, the look of a woman who had spent twenty years being lied to by institutions and had just watched a man choose not to do it.

"You could have taken the deal," she said.

"I burned it. Literally. There's ash in the river off the north pier if you want to verify."

"That's not what I asked."

"No," I said. "I could have. The Verrin Room. My own key. Everything I've wanted since I was twenty-three." I put my hand flat on the maps, on my mentor's careful, complicit handwriting. "But the deal was four units, Freya. Their own mandate said it. They've known about the chests all along. They've been counting them like spoons. Which means somewhere there's a ledger, and somewhere there's a room, and somewhere in that room is everything your mother tried to stop." I looked up at her. "I don't want access to the archive anymore. I want to burn the lock off the door. And I can't do that from inside, and I apparently can't do it from a provincial records office either. So. Here. With you. On the leaky boat."

Anselm appeared in the doorway behind her, having heard approximately all of it, because Anselm heard everything and pretended otherwise. He looked at the maps, at me, at Freya.

"The coat man," he said. "He'll send more than coats next time."

"I know," I said.

"Good," said Anselm, and went back down to his boat.

Freya was still looking at the corrections, her finger tracing my mentor's hand the way I'd traced it, and I watched her arrive at the same place I'd arrived on the pier — that the past doesn't offer clean hands to anyone, that the only choice on the table is what you do with the ones you've got.

"The gate," she said finally. "The Verrik gate. How far?"

"A day's run, if Anselm's honest about the channel. He isn't, but he's close."

"Then we leave tonight," Freya said, and started packing, and that was the whole of my absolution — no speech, no forgiveness pronounced, just the packing, just the being included, just the road.

I went to the window while she worked. Down on the river, the mist had burned off, and the water ran high and brown and purposeful toward the sea, and I thought about the man on the pier, and the folder going to ash, and the mandate's four units, and I understood with perfect clarity that the coat man had not been the end of the consortium's interest but the beginning of its patience. They had offered me a key. When the key failed, they would offer the river something else.

And far upstream, past the gate, past the gorges, under the mountain where the river was born, the drowned capital waited with its records and its rooftops and whatever was left of the truth — and for the first time since they'd exiled me to the turnip town, I was not afraid of what we'd find there.

I was afraid of what would find us first.
