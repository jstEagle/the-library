## 14. Burned Bridge at Verrik Gate

The notice went up on the Verrik Gate customs post sometime in the night, and by morning it had been copied out in three hands and nailed to every wall between the gate and the river.

I read it upside down first, the way you do when someone holds a paper toward you across a table, which is how Anselm was holding it. Then he turned it around and let me read it properly, and I wished he hadn't.

*By order of the Verrik Reserve Authority: the reserve is closed. All approaches to the headwaters are sealed. Persons entering without authorization will be detained.*

And beneath that, in smaller type, the part that mattered:

*Information leading to the recovery of stolen archival property may be rewarded. The individuals traveling with said property — one woman, one man, one boatman — are sought for questioning.*

"Questioning," Anselm said. "That's generous of them."

There was no photograph. There didn't need to be. The description of the woman was precise enough that whoever wrote it had stood close enough to smell my hair: *mid-thirties, dark hair cut short at the jaw, scar through left eyebrow, walks with the gait of someone who has spent her life on moving water.* They'd gotten the eyebrow wrong — the scar was on the right — but everything else was right, and being described that accurately by people who were supposed to think I was a salvage contractor's widow made my skin feel two sizes too small.

"They know," Tomas said, unnecessarily. He had the paper in both hands like it might tell him something the words didn't. "They've known since Kessel's Landing."

"They've known longer than that." I took the notice from him and folded it into my jacket, because paper burns and I wanted to be the one holding the match. "This is what it looks like when they stop pretending. A closed gate. A price on our heads. It means we're not an annoyance anymore. We're a problem with a deadline."

Anselm spat over the side of the boat, which was his way of annotating anything. "Deadline's the water. Dry season comes early this year. You can see it in the willows — they're yellowing from the top down. Two weeks, maybe less, before the gorges drop below runnable. After that there's no river. Just rocks and a long walk."

"There's always a long walk," I said.

"Not above Verrik Gate there isn't. Above the gate the country goes vertical. Karst and cliff and the kind of scree that eats boots. If we don't run the river past the checkpoint, we walk into the reserve on foot through country where a broken ankle is a death sentence, carrying enough food for five days because that's all we can carry." He looked at me with those flat gray eyes of his, the ones that never gave away whether he was talking about geography or about us. "So. Do we run it?"

I looked upstream. The Verrik Gate wasn't a gate in any sense you'd recognize — no walls, no bars. It was a narrows, a place where the Serran pinched itself down between two limestone shoulders and dropped through a quarter mile of water that guidebooks called *the Mangle*, when guidebooks were allowed to mention it at all. The consortium had built their checkpoint on the shelf of rock above the narrows: a low concrete block, a floodlight rig, a patrol boat moored in the eddy below the drop. In daylight, nothing got through. In daylight, they could see every boat coming for half a mile and board it at their leisure.

At night, the floodlights swept the water on a rotation — I'd watched them from the willow bank for most of an hour, counting. Forty seconds of light, twenty of dark. Forty on, twenty off. Somewhere behind the concrete block there was a man turning a dial, and men who turn dials are men who trust machines, and machines keep time better than people but worse than rivers.

"The patrol boat," I said. "Where does it sit?"

"Below the drop, portside eddy. They keep it there so anything that survives the Mangle runs straight into them." Anselm almost smiled. "Almost clever. Except the eddy dies after midnight when they cut the dam release upriver. Water drops maybe eight inches. Eddy shrinks to nothing. Boat swings out on its anchor line and sits in the current like a dog on a chain."

"And if the chain breaks?"

"Chains don't break."

"Lines chafe," I said. "Especially old ones, especially at night, especially when nobody's aboard watching them."

Anselm looked at me for a long moment, and whatever he saw in my face made him nod slowly, the way he did when he'd already decided something and wanted me to think it had been a discussion.

"I'll need two hours and a knife," he said.

* * *

He wouldn't say what he did out there in the dark, and I didn't ask, because there are questions you're better off not knowing the answers to when someone eventually asks you under oath. He was gone from the willow bank for most of two hours, moving along the shore with the unhurried certainty of a man walking ground he'd walked before, which — I understood then — he had. Anselm had run cargo through Verrik Gate back when there had still been a Vessia to run cargo for. The checkpoint was younger than his grief. He knew where the sentry walked because he'd once been the reason the sentry walked there.

When he came back his sleeves were wet to the shoulder and he smelled of river mud and engine grease, and he said only: "Anchor line's been chafing against the hull all season. Terrible maintenance on that boat. Somebody should report it."

Tomas, meanwhile, had done what Tomas does, which is turn fear into paperwork. He'd spent the evening with the chest open on his knees, re-wrapping the core documents in oilcloth and waxed canvas, sealing the seams with candle wax and pine pitch until each packet was a brick you could drop in a river and lose nothing but the wrapping. Three packets. One for each of us, on the theory that if we got separated, the evidence would survive in triplicate, and the consortium would have to drown all three of us to drown it.

"You realize," he said, working the pitch into a seam with his thumbnail, "that if they catch us, possession of these is the crime. Not crossing the gate. The papers are what they want. The price on our heads is just — logistics."

"I know."

"So we should consider—" He stopped. I watched him decide not to say it, watch the sentence die somewhere behind his teeth, and then say it anyway, because Tomas has never once in his life let a sentence die quietly. "We should consider splitting up. I take the papers south through the hills, on foot, alone. You two run the gate as decoys. Empty-handed, you're just smugglers. They fine you, hold you a week, let you go. I'm the one they hang, but I'm also the one they don't catch, because nobody's watching for a lone archivist with a bad knee."

"No," I said.

"You didn't even think about it."

"I thought about it the whole time you were talking." I sat down next to him on the gravel, close enough that our shoulders touched, which in my language is an argument-ender. "Here's what happens if you go alone. You get lost in the karst, or your knee gives out on day two, or you meet a shepherd who sells you to the first man with money, and the papers end up in a consortium evidence locker labeled *recovered*. Everything we've drowned for, burned for, bled for — filed away again. Sealed again. No. We stay together. Not because together is safer. Because apart, none of us gets to be a witness. Together, somebody always survives to say what happened."

He was quiet for a while. The river talked instead, doing its low endless muttering against the stones.

"That's the second time," he finally said, "you've saved my life by insulting my plan."

"Get used to it. There's a third coming."

* * *

We launched at half past midnight, moonless, the kind of dark where the river is a sound rather than a thing. Anselm ran the shallows by ear — I could hear him listening, head tilted, reading the water the way Tomas read documents, finding the channel in the noise. I sat forward with the spare paddle and did the one job I'm actually good at, which is watching black water and believing it when it tells me things.

The floodlight swept us twice on the approach. Both times we were flat in the bottom of the boat among bundles of brush Anselm had lashed along the gunwales, and both times the light passed over us the way light passes over driftwood — uninterested, incurious. Forty seconds on, twenty off. The machine kept its time. The man behind the dial trusted it.

The Mangle announced itself the way big water always does: not by sight but by pressure, a change in the air, a deep subsonic complaint rising through the hull and up through your boot soles into your spine. Then the sound. Then, last of all, the white.

"Stay low," Anselm said. "Paddle when I say. Pray whenever you like, I won't judge the timing."

We went in.

I have run rapids on four rivers in three countries under six names, and I will tell you honestly: I remember almost nothing of the next ninety seconds, and what I remember is not visual. I remember the boat becoming a thing we were inside of rather than riding on. I remember water hitting my face so hard it felt thrown. I remember Anselm's voice cutting through the roar exactly twice — *left, hard left* — and my arms obeying before my mind caught up, which is the whole secret of whitewater, that the body knows before the brain does, and survival belongs to people who've trained the body to be smarter than their fear.

Then the world went sideways.

Not the boat. The world. A wave stood up where no wave should have been — a rebound off the far wall, a hydraulic folding back on itself, the kind of feature that doesn't exist in daylight and doesn't exist in guidebooks and exists only for people who run drops blind at midnight. It took us amidships. I felt the hull flex, heard something structural give up with a crack like a rifle shot, and then the river reached in and took inventory.

Tomas went first. One moment he was a shape beside me; the next the river had him, and I felt the specific horror of that — the water doesn't throw you out, it *invites* you, it peels you loose with a gentleness that is the cruelest thing about it. I got a fistful of his collar as he went past. The river objected. The river always objects. For three full seconds we were both simply cargo, and then my hand found the brush bundle lashed inside the gunwale and I held on to it and to him and screamed the one word that matters in whitewater, which isn't *help*:

"Hold!"

He held. God help him, he held — one hand fisted in my jacket, one in the brush, his face underwater more often than not, trusting me absolutely in a way that terrified me more than the rapid did, because I have never deserved absolute trust from anyone and here was a man with a bad knee betting his lungs on mine.

Anselm brought us through. Of course he did. The boat came out of the Mangle sideways and half-full and making a new sound — a slow grinding wheeze from below the waterline that told me the hull was opened along a seam and admitting the river at a negotiated rate. The patrol boat swung on its shortened line forty yards downstream, searchlight dead, deck empty, riding a little oddly to starboard. Anselm's knife work had bought us a channel. The Mangle had charged us for it.

We grounded in the willows a mile below the gate, bailed her out by lantern light, and looked at what we had.

What we had was a boat that would float for perhaps another hour of careful handling and would come apart entirely the first time it touched anything harder than foam. The crack ran along the garboard seam for four feet. There was no fixing it. There was no *carrying* it. Anselm knelt in the bilge with his palm flat against the split planking the way you'd touch a wound, and I watched him say goodbye to her without saying anything at all.

"She was a good boat," he said finally. "Twenty-two years. Ran me through the Mangle eleven times before tonight."

"What happens to her now?"

"She stays here. Becomes part of the riverbank. That's what boats want, in the end." He straightened up and started pulling gear out of her with the brisk movements of a man who has decided grief is a luxury scheduled for later. "We walk from here."

* * *

We walked.

Above the gate the Serran stopped pretending to be a gentle border river and became what it had always meant to become: a young thing falling out of mountains, loud, cold, unreasonable. The trail — and I use the word with charity — climbed away from the water through karst country where the limestone had been chewed into ridges and sinkholes, and every step cost something. We carried what we could carry, which is another way of saying we left behind almost everything that makes a person soft. Tent gone. Spare clothes gone. Anselm's tool roll, his spare lines, the iron stove — gone, stacked under a rock cairn with a marker only he could read, a promise to a future self that future selves rarely collect.

Three packets of documents. Five days of food. Two tarps, one rope, one lantern, one knife each, and the clothes we stood in. When I shouldered my pack and felt how light it was, I understood that lightness and loss weigh the same.

Tomas limped. His knee had taken a knock in the Mangle that he insisted was nothing and that I could hear in his breathing on every climb. I set the pace slower than I wanted and faster than he wanted and we both pretended this was a compromise.

Near dawn we stopped in the mouth of a sinkhole cave and ate cold rice and looked back the way we'd come. Far downstream, tiny with distance, the checkpoint floodlights still swept their faithful arc. Forty seconds on, twenty off. Somewhere behind that concrete block, a man was turning a dial, and the man believed the machine, and the machine believed the river was closed.

"They'll find the boat by midmorning," Anselm said. "They'll find the chafed line, and they'll know. And then they'll stop patrolling the water and start patrolling the land, and the land is much bigger, and they'll hate it."

"Is that good for us?"

"It's good for someone," he said. "Out here, that's the same thing."

Tomas was quiet a long time, chewing his rice like it owed him an explanation. Then he said, in the careful voice he uses when he's about to confess to something small in order to hide something large: "The maps in the chest. The survey maps. I've been cross-referencing the contour lines against the geological notes, and some of the elevations don't—"

"Tomas." I didn't look at him. I was watching the floodlights sweep their arc, counting. "Whatever it is, it keeps until daylight. Right now we have five days of food, three days of climbing, and a closed border between us and the truth about my mother. Eat your rice."

He ate his rice. But I felt him looking at me in the dark, and I felt the shape of the thing he wasn't saying, sitting between us like a fourth passenger, and I told myself I was too tired to care.

I wasn't too tired to care. I was too tired to be afraid of it yet. There's a difference, and the difference was going to matter.

Above us, invisible in the pre-dawn dark, the reservoir waited with a country in its throat. And for the first time since the flood gave me that brass-bound chest, I stopped calculating escape routes — stopped, out of pure habit, measuring every bend of river for the way back down.

There was no way back down. The boat was wreckage in the willows, the gate was sealed, our faces were on a wall in three handwriting styles, and the only direction left in the world was up.

I found, to my considerable surprise, that this felt like relief.

"We move at first light," I said. "And Tomas — whatever the maps are hiding, you tell me tomorrow. On the trail. Walking. I work things out better when I'm walking."

He nodded, and Anselm snored, and the floodlights swept their patient arc over an empty river, guarding nothing now but their own mistake.
