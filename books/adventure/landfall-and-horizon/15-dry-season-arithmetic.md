## 15. Dry Season Arithmetic

I woke before dawn with the arithmetic already running in my head, the way some people wake up humming a tune.

Five days of water. Three days of climb. Two people who'd never carried a pack above the treeline. One chest of paper that weighed more than my conscience did.

I lay in the dark under the lean-to Maret had built against her stone hut and listened to the river. It was quieter than it had been two days ago. That was the thing about dry season nobody warned you about unless you'd lived it: the water didn't just drop, it *withdrew*, like a creditor pulling out of a bad loan. Every morning the Serran ran a little thinner, a little lower, a little less interested in pretending it could carry anything anywhere. By the time we reached Verrik Gate, the gorges would be passable on foot for exactly as long as they stayed passable at all. Then the autumn rains would come up from the south, and everything we were trying to reach would close like a fist.

Five days of water. I said it again to myself, because numbers only become real when you say them out loud, and I wasn't ready to say this one out loud yet.

Tomas was still asleep beside the cold fire pit, curled around his satchel the way he curled around every document he loved, which was to say possessively and with an air of grievance that anyone might take it from him. Even asleep he looked like a man arguing with a filing system. I envied him that. My arguments had all gone internal years ago, and they never slept.

Maret was already up. Of course she was. Women who tend cairns along a drowned shoreline don't get the luxury of sleeping past first light; the stones don't stack themselves, and the dead don't wait.

She was at her table when I came in, sorting something small and pale into cloth squares. River glass, I realized. Tumbled white and green, smoothed by twenty years of water over a town that used to have windows.

"You're doing the counting," she said, without looking up. "I can see it in your face. Your mother did the same thing. Sat there moving her lips, working sums nobody else could see."

"I'm told I have her face."

"You have her habit." She set down a piece of green glass, deliberate as a chess move. "The face is different. The face is your father's, maybe, or just yours. But the counting—that's hers."

I didn't know what to do with that, so I did what I always did with things I didn't know how to carry. I put them in a pile and dealt with them later.

"Maret," I said. "I need to talk to you about weight."

* * *

Here is what a guide knows that nobody teaches in the brochures: every expedition dies twice. Once in the planning, where you cut away everything you can't afford, and once in the field, where you find out you cut away the wrong things. The art is in making sure the second death isn't fatal. You do that by being ruthless at the first one. Ruthless about food, about rope, about fuel. And ruthless, above all, about sentiment, because sentiment is the heaviest thing anybody ever packed.

I laid it all out on Maret's floor that morning while Tomas watched with the expression of a man attending his own autopsy.

The documents came out of the chest first. Twenty years of paper—survey maps, sealed orders, requisition ledgers, the consortium's own correspondence proving that Vessia hadn't drowned by accident but by design, that the flood that erased a nation had been scheduled like a train departure. We'd been carrying all of it since Kessel's Landing because it had felt impossible to choose. Because every page was a person. Because leaving any of it behind felt like letting them drown twice.

"We can't take it all," I said.

Tomas's head came up like a hound catching scent. "We're not leaving any of it."

"Three days' climb into karst country. Vertical gain, scree, no resupply. Water for five days if we're careful, four if we're honest. Anselm wrecked the boat, so everything we carry, we carry on our backs." I set a scale pan of stones on the floor between us—I'd borrowed it from Maret's supplies, because even my metaphors needed equipment. "Every kilogram of paper is a kilogram of water we can't carry. You want to do the math, or should I?"

"I've read these documents for three weeks, Freya. I know them better than—"

"Then you know which ones matter."

He opened his mouth. Closed it. This was the thing about Tomas that had stopped annoying me somewhere around the gorge and started being almost endearing: he was stubborn, but he was honest with himself eventually, and eventually came faster the more you respected his intelligence instead of fighting it.

"The core evidence," he said slowly. "Mother's final report. The sealed orders authorizing the reservoir timeline. The correspondence linking the consortium director to the staged disaster—the disaster that got you blacklisted." He touched each item as he named it, laying them apart from the rest. "And the survey maps, corrected. They prove the archive knew the source was guarded. They prove complicity inside the institutions."

"And the letters," I said, and hated how my voice caught on it.

"And the letters," he agreed quietly.

Everything else—the bulk of it, the weight of it, twenty years of bureaucratic murder documented in triplicate—stayed in the pile.

"So we burn it?" Tomas asked, and there was real anguish in it, and I understood. To him those pages weren't paper. They were the whole argument, the complete case, the difference between truth and assertion.

"No," said Maret, from the doorway. "You cache it."

* * *

Her plan was better than mine would have been, and I say that as someone whose entire professional identity rests on plans.

The cairn route. She'd been building it for years—a line of memorial stones along the old shore road, marking the villages, the church, the schoolhouse, the names of the drowned, each cairn placed high enough to survive the seasonal rise and low enough to be found by anyone walking the waterline. Nobody paid attention to an old woman stacking rocks. Nobody had ever thought to look inside them.

"There's a hollow one," she said. "Above the high-water mark, under the third bend. Dry as a sermon. We seal the papers in oilcloth, we pack the core with lime to keep the damp off, and we build the cairn back the way it was. Anyone who wants the rest of the story comes and asks me for it." Her chin came up. "And anyone who wants it will have to explain to me why."

"It won't survive a search party," I said.

"It survived twenty years of people not looking," she said. "That's a better record than most archives can claim. Ask your friend here how many Vessian records made it through the capital's own vaults."

Tomas had the grace not to flinch. Much.

So we spent the morning doing the work that guides actually do, which is ninety percent packing and ten percent everything else. Oilcloth from Maret's stores. Lime from the outhouse crock. The documents sorted into two piles—one that fit inside a waterproof map case the size of a hymnal, and one that went into the earth.

It should have been harder than it was. That's what I kept expecting—to feel the weight of the choice, the moral gravity of it. Instead I felt something colder and more familiar: relief. The pack got lighter. The math got cleaner. Five days of water, three days of climb, and now a load I could actually move at altitude.

That's the part nobody tells you about discipline. Everyone talks about it like it's noble, like it's a virtue you wear. But real discipline—the kind that keeps people alive—is just fear wearing a uniform. It's the part of you that says *we leave the extra rope, we skip the summit push, we turn back at the col* in a voice that doesn't care about your feelings. And the terrible secret underneath it, the one I'd been running from across two borders and five false names, is that discipline feels good. Cutting things away feels good. There's a version of yourself that gets lighter with every loss, and after enough years you can't tell anymore whether you're becoming efficient or becoming nothing.

I stood there in Maret's hut with the map case in my hands—everything that mattered, small enough to hold against my ribs—and I thought: this is what I've been doing to myself for six years. One name abandoned here, one friendship cut loose there, one memory sealed in oilcloth and buried under a cairn. Traveling light. Staying alive. Getting smaller every season until there was barely enough of me left to run the ferry and keep the ledger balanced.

"You're doing it again," Maret said.

"Doing what?"

"The counting. But inward." She took the map case from me, turned it over, checked my knots with fingers that had tied fishing lines since before I was born, and handed it back. "Your mother counted outward. She counted the people. That's why she couldn't stop, in the end. That's why they had to erase her." She looked at me with eyes the color of the river glass. "Don't count yourself out of the story, girl. The story needs you in it."

* * *

We cached the documents at noon.

The walk up the shore road was the first time I'd seen the reservoir properly in daylight since the steeple. Low water had pulled back another meter overnight, and the exposed mud lay cracked and pale as old bone, stitched with the shadows of foundations. Here a doorstep. There a garden wall, and climbing roses gone woody and feral, still blooming anyway, because roses are sentimentalists and don't do arithmetic.

Maret walked ahead of us without hurrying, and Tomas and I carried the wrapped bundle between us like pallbearers, which none of us said out loud.

The hollow cairn sat above the third bend, exactly where she said, a shoulder-high stack of river stones with a caprock that lifted out on a wooden key she'd whittled herself. Inside: a cavity lined with slate, dry despite everything, holding a tin box already half full of things I didn't ask about. Photographs, maybe. Letters. A country's pocket contents, waiting.

We nested the bundle in, packed lime around it, replaced the caprock, and rebuilt the cairn stone by stone under Maret's direction—"No, that one goes flat side out, are you blind, your grandfather would have wept"—until it looked exactly as neglected and eternal as it had before. Then Maret did something I didn't expect. She pressed her palm flat against the top stone and said a few words in a language I didn't know, low and rhythmic, and Tomas went very still beside me, and afterward he whispered, "That's Vessian. Old liturgical Vessian. Where did you—"

"My mother sang it over the cradle," Maret said. "Before there was a reason to whisper it." She turned to me. "Now. The rest."

"The rest?"

"You're taking the letters." It wasn't a question. "The personal ones. Not the report—the letters. I saw you set them aside last night when you thought no one was looking."

I had. Four envelopes, soft-cornered, handled so many times the folds had gone furry. My mother's handwriting, addressed to a woman named Ilse Varn who had apparently been her closest friend and who had died in the flooding along with everyone else, which meant the letters had never been sent, which meant my mother had written them knowing no one would read them, which meant they were the truest documents in the entire chest.

"They're not evidence," I said. "They prove nothing. They're just—"

"They're the reason you're going," Maret said. "And I'm too old to pretend I don't know the difference between a mission and a pilgrimage. Take the letters. Take the weight. Some loads aren't for carrying efficiently. They're for carrying *at all*."

So I took them out of the cache and put them in the map case against my ribs, next to the final report, and the case got heavier by perhaps two hundred grams, and I felt lighter by a measure no scale would ever register.

* * *

We left at three in the afternoon, because I wanted the first hours of the climb in daylight and the first camp established before dark, and because every hour we delayed was an hour the consortium's patrols gained on us. Anselm was gone—he'd taken the ruined boat downstream two days ago with a promise to raise whatever trouble he could among the river towns, and I believed him, though whether his trouble would help us or just add noise, I couldn't say. I missed him the way you miss a wall when the wind picks up. He'd been our wall.

The trail left the shore and climbed immediately, switchbacking through scrub pine onto the shoulders of the karst, and within an hour the reservoir was a silver coin far below us and the air had changed—thinner, drier, smelling of stone dust and juniper instead of water.

Tomas climbed badly. I'll give him this: he climbed badly without complaint, which is rarer than climbing well. He'd bought boots in Kessel's Landing that were wrong for scree, and his pack rode too high, and every hundred meters he stopped to breathe with his hands on his knees while I pretended to check the route. But he kept moving. When I adjusted his pack straps without asking, he let me. When I told him to drink on schedule rather than thirst, he drank. Somewhere between the steeple and the checkpoint rapids he had learned the first rule of the mountains, which is that the mountain does not care about your schedule, your pride, or your thesis, and the sooner you surrender to its timetable, the longer you live.

"How much farther today?" he asked, at the fourth breathing stop.

"Two hours. There's a shelf below the first ridge with wind shelter."

"And tomorrow?"

"Tomorrow we cross the ridge and drop into the dry gorge. Day after, we climb the karst to the intake station." I paused. "To Station Zero."

He nodded, and then, because he was Tomas and silence physically pained him: "Freya. About the maps. There's something I need to—"

"Not tonight."

"It might be important."

"It's always important with you. That's what I like about you and what's going to get us killed." I looked back down the trail. The light was going long and gold across the water, and somewhere down there, a cairn held twenty years of proof, and somewhere behind us, men in clean boots were buying information from people who'd sold cheaper things for less. "Tonight we make camp, we eat, we sleep. Tomorrow, on the ridge, with the whole day ahead of us—you tell me what you found in the maps. All of it. Understood?"

Something crossed his face that I chose, in the failing light, not to identify.

"Understood," he said.

* * *

The shelf camp was everything I'd promised and nothing more: a flat apron of rock under an overhang, room for two bedrolls and a stove, a view straight down the length of the reservoir to where the sun was drowning itself in its own reflection. I cooked the last of the fresh food—there would be no more fresh food—and rationed the water into bottles with the precision of a pharmacist, and Tomas watched me do it and didn't joke, which showed how far he'd come.

"Five days," he said finally.

"Four and a half, if we're honest about the heat on the karst."

"And the climb is three days."

"If nothing goes wrong."

"Nothing has gone right since Kessel's Landing."

"Then we're overdue." I capped the last bottle and sat back against the rock beside him. Below us the reservoir lay still as poured metal, and I let myself look at it for a long moment—not as a guide assessing a crossing, not as a fugitive scanning for patrol boats, but as a daughter looking at the water that held her mother's country. Somewhere under that metal surface: streets, doorsteps, a schoolhouse, a library full of records that men in clean boots had burned before the water ever reached them. My mother had tried to stop it. Had failed. Had written letters to a dead woman because the living were all bought or frightened. And then the consortium had needed her silenced completely, and they had built a disaster on a mountainside with my name attached to it like a price tag.

Six years I'd carried that shame, believing some part of it was mine. Tonight, on a rock shelf above a drowned nation, with two hundred grams of her handwriting against my ribs, I finally understood: the shame was never mine. It was inventory. It was weight they'd loaded onto me so I'd stay small and quiet and easy to lose in a crowd.

Well. I was done traveling light.

"Tomas," I said. "First watch is mine. Sleep."

He was out in minutes, curled around the map case I'd made him carry, because if we went down a slope I wanted the evidence on the man most likely to survive the fall, and because I trusted him now, fully, in the way you only trust people who have had chances to betray you and visibly decided against it.

I sat up with the stove's last warmth fading and did the arithmetic one final time, lips moving, the way Maret said my mother used to.

Water: four and a half days. Climb: three. Distance to Station Zero: thirty-one kilometers as the chough flies, forty-some as the switchbacks lie. Patrols: unknown, but closing. Odds: I'd stopped calculating them honestly somewhere around the gorge, because the honest number would have sent me back down the hill, and I was done going back down hills.

What remained, when you cut away everything else—when you stripped the pack to the frame and the life to the essentials—was this: a hymnal-sized case of paper, a sleeping archivist, and a mountain ahead with something hidden under it that a lot of powerful people would kill to keep buried.

Five days of water. Three days of climb.

I finished the sum, and the answer, for the first time in six years, pointed forward.

Far below, at the edge of the darkening water, a light blinked twice—paused—blinked twice again. Not a fisherman's lamp. Fishermen don't signal in code.

Someone was coming up the shore road behind us.
