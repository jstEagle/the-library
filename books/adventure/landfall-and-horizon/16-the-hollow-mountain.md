## 16. The Hollow Mountain

I want to say I noticed the wrongness of the mountain because I'm an archivist and archivists are trained to notice discrepancies. That would be a flattering version of events, and I have spent enough of this journey learning to distrust flattering versions.

The truth is that Freya noticed nothing because she was ahead of us breaking trail through scree, and Anselm noticed nothing because Anselm had stopped noticing anything that wasn't water or weather years ago, and I noticed it because I sat down on a rock to complain about my boots and the rock hummed.

Not vibrated. Hummed. A low, patient note, like a tuning fork someone had struck a decade ago and never let die.

"Freya," I said, and then, when she didn't turn, "Freya. The mountain is singing."

She came back down the slope with that walk she has—fast but never hurried, as if hurry were a character flaw she'd trained out of herself—and put her palm flat against the stone beside me. We waited. Ten seconds. Twenty. Then it came again, fainter here than under my hand but unmistakable: a deep mechanical breath, drawn in and held somewhere far below our feet.

"That's not a spring," she said.

"No."

"Springs don't keep time." She looked up at the massif above us, the Verrik Reserve's forbidden heart, all grey karst and black pine, the place every map we'd stolen or memorized agreed was simply *end*. The source of the Serran. The reason for everything. "That's machinery."

I should have told her then. I want that on the record, whoever ends up reading whatever record survives me—which, given recent events, may be no one, or may be a tribunal, or may be some future archivist like me, hungry and exiled and digging through other people's sealed boxes. I should have said: *Freya, there's something you need to know about these maps, and I've known since before the rapids, and I've been navigating us with corrections I didn't tell you were corrections.*

Instead I said, "Machinery means people," which was true, and cowardly, and bought me another day.

* * *

The karst country changed how we moved. On the river we'd been three people in a boat; now we were three animals picking across limestone pavement that had been dissolved into blades and pits by ten thousand years of rain. The stone was full of holes. Every few hundred meters the ground simply opened—a sinkhole, a shaft, a slot you could drop a scream into and never hear it land. Freya roped us together without being asked about it, and I tried not to think about what it meant that she'd stopped asking.

Anselm hated it. Not the danger—he'd run checkpoint rapids at night with a patrol boat's searchlight burning his neck, so danger and he were old drinking companions—but the silence. On the water he'd talked constantly, a low muttering commentary on channels and gravel bars and the sins of various governments, mostly addressed to the boat. Here he walked with his jaw set and his eyes on the stone, and twice I caught him stopping at sinkholes and looking down into them too long.

The third time, Freya stopped beside him.

"You know them," she said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I know the shape of them." Anselm's voice was gravel dragged over gravel. "Vessian villages sat on country like this. Limestone under everything. When they filled the reservoir, the water didn't just cover the towns—it went *down*, into the caves, into the cracks. Whole cellars flooding from beneath. My grandmother's village, the water came up out of the well before it ever touched the door." He spat, ceremonially, into the nearest hole. "They drowned from underneath. Nobody writes that part down."

"Somebody did," I said quietly. "It's in the chest. Intake surveys, 1949 through '53. They knew exactly what the water would do underground. There's a memo about—" I stopped, because Anselm was looking at me with an expression I'd last seen on Freya's face in the steeple church, and I understood that I had just recited casualty figures to a survivor and called it scholarship.

"The chest," Anselm said. "Your chest. Full of papers about how my family died." He nodded slowly, as if confirming a ledger entry. "Good. Keep walking."

We kept walking. And under our boots, patient as a heartbeat, the mountain went on humming.

* * *

We camped that night in the mouth of a cave—not by choice, but because the temperature dropped like a trapdoor and Freya refused to risk fire on open stone where the light would carry. The cave breathed warm air at us, which should have been a comfort and instead felt like standing near the mouth of something sleeping.

I couldn't sleep. I sat with the survey maps spread across my knees in the dark, not reading them—I knew them by heart—but holding them, the way you hold a letter you've written and not sent.

Here is what I had done, and I will set it down plainly because plainness is the only penance available to me:

When Tomas Ilic deciphered the Vessian survey maps in Chapter Eight of this disaster—no. When I deciphered them, weeks ago, in the cold after the rapids, while Freya slept off her rescue of my drowning body—I discovered they were lies. Beautiful, official, deliberately wrong lies. The maps drew the Serran's headwaters veering east through a gorge called the Kestrel Throat, a route that would take any seeker days out of their way into country that dead-ended at a cliff. The real course ran west, under the massif, through karst. Someone had redrawn the maps to misdirect anyone hunting the source.

And the corrections—the small, precise annotations restoring the truth—were written in a notation style I knew better than my own handwriting. Double-stroke emphasis on hydrological features. Dates rendered in the old academic fashion, month before year. A tiny hooked crossbar on the numeral seven.

Professor Aldous Wren's notation. My mentor. The man who arranged my exile to Kessel's Landing with one hand while slipping me the location of the fourth chest with the other. The man I had believed was a martyr of the archive, punished for asking questions.

He hadn't been punished for asking questions. He'd been *sent*. Whether by the consortium, or by some faction inside the archive itself, or by his own unfathomable agenda, Wren had placed the chest where I would find it, corrected its maps so I could follow them, and aimed me—aimed *us*—at the source like a round down a barrel. And I had let him. I had followed the corrections faithfully, told Freya we were navigating by her mother's documents, and never once mentioned that the surest hand guiding us belonged to a man in the capital who wore clean boots and answered to someone.

I had done it because I was afraid. Because if the maps were compromised, if Wren was compromised, then maybe the whole expedition was someone else's operation and I was not its author but its instrument, and I could not bear that. So I hid the discrepancy the way archives hide things: carefully, neatly, with a filing label that made concealment look like prudence.

And now the mountain was humming, and the humming sounded exactly like the intake tunnels described in the surveys, and the lie I'd been carrying had grown heavy enough that my knees ached under it.

"Tomas." Freya's voice, low, from across the dying fire. "You've been staring at those maps for two hours. You could recite them in your sleep. What are you actually looking at?"

I looked at her. Firelight on the scarred knuckles of her hands, on the face that strangers said looked like her mother's and that I had once, clumsily, criminally, told her so within five minutes of meeting her. She had saved my life in the rapids. She had told me things about the mountain disaster she'd never told anyone, and I had received them and repaid her with a concealed footnote.

"The maps are wrong," I said.

"I know. You told me. Deliberately wrong, to mislead—"

"No." My mouth was dry. "You don't understand. I need to say all of it, and I need you to not interrupt, because if you interrupt I'll lose my nerve, and I've lost it eleven times already." I turned the map toward her, though she couldn't see it in the dark, though I could have recited it. "There are two layers of wrong. The printed maps lie—they route the source east, into the Kestrel Throat, a dead end. Fine. That's the consortium's lie, expected, documented. But there are handwritten corrections restoring the true route. West. Under this massif. And Freya—the handwriting is Aldous Wren's. My mentor. The man who sent me after the chest. He corrected the consortium's false maps, and he gave them to me, and I have been following his corrections since the rapids without telling you they existed."

The fire popped. Somewhere below us, faint as memory, the mountain hummed.

Freya didn't move for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice was very even, which from Freya is worse than shouting. "Say the last part again. Slowly."

"He corrected the maps. I found it before the rapids. I didn't tell you."

"Why?"

"Because if his corrections were good, we'd make better time—and they were good, they've been right about everything—and because if they were bad, then everything we're doing is a script someone else wrote, and I couldn't stand to be a character in someone else's script. So I chose the version where I'm useful instead of the version where I'm honest. I chose it every single day. I'm choosing to stop now, which is late, and which you're entitled to throw back at me forever."

Anselm, from the shadows near the cave mouth, without opening his eyes: "Throw him off the mountain later. Ask him the important question now."

Freya exhaled through her nose. "Fine. The important question." She leaned forward, and the firelight found her eyes. "If Wren corrected the maps—if he wanted us to reach the source—then either he's helping us, or he's herding us. Which is it?"

"That's the question I can't answer," I said. "But I can answer a different one, and I think it's the same question wearing different clothes." I tapped the map, the western route, the humming massif above it. "Wren's corrections don't just restore the source. They route us along the ridge above the old intake galleries. Look at the elevations. Every waypoint sits directly over tunnel infrastructure from the reservoir project. The source isn't hidden, Freya. It never was. It's *guarded*. The consortium built the reservoir's intakes into the mountain beneath the springs, and they've maintained a station there for twenty years, because the one thing they cannot allow is anyone disturbing the drowned capital. The forbidden source isn't forbidden because it's sacred. It's forbidden because it's load-bearing—for the lie."

"And Wren knew that."

"Wren drew us a map straight to the throat of it."

Silence. Anselm opened one eye. Freya stared into the fire, and I watched her do the arithmetic I'd watched her do a dozen times on the water—margins, distances, the weight of what can be carried versus what must be left behind. Except this time the thing being weighed was me.

"You lied to me for six days," she said finally. "On a river. In gorges. With patrol boats behind us."

"Yes."

"If you'd been wrong about the corrections, we'd be at a dead-end cliff right now with the dry season closing."

"Yes."

"And if you'd been right, and told me, what would I have done differently?"

I opened my mouth. Closed it. Because the honest answer was *nothing*—we had no alternative route, no alternative guide, no time to find either—and we both knew it, and somehow that made it worse rather than better. The lie hadn't endangered us. It had only made me a smaller person, and it had stolen from her the right to know the shape of the trap she was walking into.

"Nothing," I admitted. "You'd have done nothing differently. Except you'd have done it knowing. That was the thing I took from you. Not safety. Knowledge."

Freya was quiet for a long time. Then she reached across the fire, took the map from my hands, folded it with the crisp economy she brought to everything, and handed it back.

"From tonight, you narrate," she said. "Every correction, every suspicion, every thought you have about Wren, out loud, as you have it. Even the stupid ones. *Especially* the stupid ones."

"That will be a great deal of talking."

"I know. I've met you." But the corner of her mouth moved, and something in my chest unclenched for the first time in weeks. "Tomas. Why tell me now? What changed?"

I looked past her, to the cave mouth, to the dark massif beyond it, to the faint vibration I could feel through the stone under my crossed legs even here, even now.

"Because tomorrow we reach the ridge," I said, "and I realized I didn't want to see what's up there for the first time through the filter of my own secret. Whatever the consortium built into this mountain, I want to face it as someone who's already confessed everything else. Clean hands aren't possible anymore. But clean *intentions* might still count for something."

Anselm snorted. "Sentimental academics," he said, and rolled over, and was asleep in ninety seconds, the way only guilty men and boatmen can sleep.

Freya banked the fire. In the ember-light she looked at me for a moment longer, and whatever verdict lived in her face, she kept it filed.

"Sleep, Tomas," she said. "Tomorrow we go over the ridge, and I want you sharp. If Wren herded us here, I want to know it before the mountain does."

* * *

We topped the ridge at midmorning, under a sky the color of unpolished pewter, and the mountain opened its secret to us like a wound.

Below us, in a hanging valley no map admitted to, lay the station.

I had imagined—when I'd finally allowed myself to imagine it—a shack, a hut, a listening post. Something embarrassed and temporary. What lay in the valley was none of those things. It was a complex: low concrete buildings roofed in living sod, a crane, a cableway running down into a shaft mouth cut cleanly into the valley floor, a transformer yard humming with the same deep note we'd heard through the rock, amplified now to something you felt in your teeth. Vehicles stood in neat rows under camouflage netting. A flag flew from a pole by the main building—grey field, no device, the flag of a country that officially did not exist, flown by a consortium that officially did not exist, over a station that officially was not there.

Three layers of denial, stacked like the strata of the karst itself.

And beyond the station, rising behind it, the mountain's summit wall—and set into that wall, half-hidden by pine, the mouths of the intake tunnels. Six of them, great concrete throats banded with rust, descending into the dark. The arteries of the reservoir. The veins along which the water had run backward, down into the caves, into the cellars, into Anselm's grandmother's well, drowning a country from underneath.

"There," Freya said, and her voice had gone quiet and terrible. "That's where the water went in." She raised her mother's battered binoculars. "Shift change. Four men at the cable house. Two on the yard. One walking the perimeter with a rifle slung, not carried. They're not soldiers, Tomas. They're *caretakers*."

"Of course they are," I said, and heard my own voice shake. "You don't garrison a lie. You maintain it. You inspect it. You file reports on its condition." I lowered my own borrowed binoculars and looked at the station, at the sod roofs and the tidy vehicles and the flag with no country on it, and twenty years of archive dust fell away from me at once, and I saw the whole shape of the thing I had been chasing since Kessel's Landing.

"They're not guarding the source from us," I said. "They're guarding it from *everyone*. From memory. As long as the station stands, the capital stays undisturbed, the surveys stay sealed, and Vessia stays erased. It's not a secret, Freya. It's a maintenance schedule."

Beside me, Freya Reyes—blacklisted, renamed, orphaned by paperwork—lowered the binoculars and looked at the drowned country's caretakers with an expression I will carry to my own grave, however soon that proves to be.

"Then we've come to the right place," she said. "Because I'm done letting them keep it."

Below us, at the shaft mouth, the crane began to move.
